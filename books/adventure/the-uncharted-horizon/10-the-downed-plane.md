## 10. The Downed Plane

The map said the wreck lay in a fold of country that had no name on any government chart—just a penciled notation in Elias Vane's cramped hand: *support plane, ridge west of the twin falls, 1989.* Below it, smaller, almost apologetic: *if you're reading this, I never came back for her.*

*Her.* He'd called the plane *her*, and something about that made my chest tighten as we climbed out of the lake basin into the green dark of the interior forest.

Marisol walked ahead of me with a machete she'd found under the longboat's thwart, swinging it in short, economical strokes like she'd been born to it. Maybe she had. She was her father's daughter whether she liked it or not, and watching her cut trail, I kept seeing echoes—the same set of the shoulders, the same way of tilting the head when listening to something distant. Elias had done both of those things in the four hours I'd flown him, dying in my passenger seat, and now his ghost was walking ahead of me wearing his daughter's body.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," Marisol said, without turning around.

"Because the map's route crosses here, and if Halvorsen's men are following it too, I want to know what they know before they know we know it."

"That's not an answer. That's a tongue-twister."

"It's the only answer I've got." I wiped sweat off my brow with the back of my wrist. The air was a wet wool blanket pressed against my face. Somewhere above us, thunder muttered—not the monsoon yet, but its rehearsal. "Your father drew this route for a reason. The cache is at the end of it. But the route passes things along the way. Things he wanted whoever carried the map to see."

"You think he wanted us to see a plane crash."

"I think he wanted someone to understand why he spent forty years hiding in the bush."

She didn't answer that. Her machete kept swinging. But after a while she slowed until I caught up, and we walked side by side through the cathedral gloom, our boots sucking in the peat, and neither of us said anything else for a long time, which was its own kind of agreement.

* * *

We camped that night on a shelf of granite above a creek, and I dreamed about my father for the first time in years.

Not the dream where he walks out of the fog at the end of the strip and I wake up shouting. A different one. In this one he was young—younger than I am now—and he was standing in a hangar with a grease pencil behind his ear, drawing a line on a chart, and he looked up at me and said, *Petra, check your fuel* , and I woke up with my heart hammering and the fire burned down to orange eyes and Marisol sitting up across from me, watching.

"You talk in your sleep," she said.

"What did I say?"

"Something about fuel." She poked the coals with a stick. Sparks climbed. "You said it like it mattered."

"Everything matters when you're a pilot. Fuel, weather, weight. You get sloppy about any one of them and the sky collects." I pulled my knees up. "My father used to say the sky is patient. It doesn't chase you. It just waits for the day you make the mistake you were always going to make."

"Cheerful man."

"He disappeared when I was nine. They never found the plane. My mother waited three years and then stopped waiting, and then she stopped altogether, and I grew up in a mission school learning how to be nobody in particular." I heard myself saying it and couldn't stop; the words came out smooth and worn, like river stones I'd been carrying in my pocket so long I'd forgotten they were there. "Invisibility is a skill. I got good at it. Small debts, small strips, small life. Nobody looks twice at a woman who flies medicine and mail."

"And then a dying man paid you double for silence and handed you a map with your father's initials on it."

"Yeah," I said. "And then that."

The thunder rolled closer. Marisol fed another branch to the fire and didn't look at me when she said, quietly, "I used to invent a version of him. My father. When I was a kid. In the good version he was a spy. In the bad version he was dead. I liked the dead version better, because at least then it wasn't a choice."

I didn't have anything to say to that, so I said nothing, and the fire crackled, and somewhere down in the dark the creek talked to itself all night long.

* * *

We found the twin falls at midday on the second day.

You heard them before you saw them—a white-noise roar building through the trees until conversation became impossible, and then the trail bent and there they were: two ribbons of water pouring off the same escarpment, maybe sixty meters of falling, the spray hanging in the air like breath in cold weather even though the day was furnace-hot. The map's pencil line ran along the base of the ridge to the west, and Elias had drawn a small X there with a note I'd read a dozen times: *wreck.*

We found it twenty minutes later, and I knew before I saw the fuselage that something was wrong, because the forest told me first.

There was a path. Not an overgrown game trail—a path, beaten earth, waist-high ferns bent back and browned along its edges, wide enough for two men abreast carrying loads. Fresh-cut stumps where saplings had been cleared, the wood still pale and wet-looking. Somebody had been maintaining a supply line to a wreck that should have been nothing but rust and orchids.

Marisol stopped at the edge of it and looked at me, and I watched the understanding arrive in her face the same way it arrived in mine.

"Somebody comes here," she said. "Regularly."

"Come on." I took the machete from her, because my hands needed something to do, and led the way down the path like a fool, because that's what fear makes you: a fool who walks toward the thing instead of away.

The Cessna sat in a clearing where the ridge flattened out, half-swallowed by thirty years of jungle. The tail was intact, the registration still legible under a skin of moss—*C-GVKS* —and my whole body went cold when I read it, because I'd grown up on stories with that tail number in them. The fuselage lay nose-down at a shallow angle, wings folded back by the impact, one engine buried in black soil. Lichen had colonized the wing roots. A strangler fig had taken the cockpit, its roots pouring through the broken windshield like poured wax.

It should have been a tomb. Thirty years of rain and rot and silence.

Instead, the engine cowling hung open on a hinge that gleamed.

I crouched beside it. The metal around the fasteners was bright, scratched clean of corrosion, and the oil that had dripped onto the ground beneath was dark but not ancient—it hadn't been rained on enough times to wash away. I touched the ground under the wing root and came away with a fingertip of grease that smeared, still tacky at the center.

"This has been stripped recently," I said. "Not decades ago. Weeks. Maybe days."

Marisol had gone very still, the way she went still when an engine made a sound it shouldn't. "Stripped of what? There's no salvage value in a thirty-year-old Cessna."

"Not unless you're looking for something specific." I moved along the fuselage, checking. The cargo door stood open. Inside, the bay was empty—but empty in a particular way. There were rectangles in the grime on the floor where cases had sat, hard-edged outlines pressed into thirty years of dust, and the dust inside those rectangles had been disturbed. "Something was stored here. Something heavy, kept dry, kept flat. Document cases, maybe. And somebody took them."

"The journals," Marisol said. "The cache. You think—"

"No." I stood up too fast and my head swam. "No, the cache is at the end of the route. Elias was clear about that. This is something else. This is—" I turned in a slow circle, looking at the clearing properly for the first time, and that was when I saw the boot prints.

They were everywhere once I knew to look. Pressed into the soft ground near the cargo door, crossing the old impact furrow, leading away toward the falls and back. Not rotted. Not flooded-out. Crisp-edged prints in mud that hadn't seen rain since the last dry spell broke—two days, maybe three. Two sets. One large, one smaller, the smaller one favoring the left foot.

"Petra." Marisol's voice had dropped low, and she wasn't looking at the prints. She was looking past me, at the tail section, at something written there.

I crossed to her. Someone had taken a knife or a screwdriver and carved into the aluminum below the registration, cutting through the moss and the oxide layer down to bright metal. The letters were crude but deliberate, and they were fresh—the exposed metal hadn't begun to dull.

**WE KNOW YOU HAVE IT**

Below that, smaller:

**LAST CHANCE**

The jungle noise seemed to fall away. The falls, the birds, the insects—all of it receded, and there was just the two of us standing in front of a message meant for eyes that weren't ours, and my mouth had gone dry as paper.

"They were expecting Elias," Marisol said. "Or expecting whoever he sent. They've been coming back to this wreck for—who knows how long. Watching it. And when they found the map gone, when they figured out he'd finally passed it on..." She trailed off.

"Then they'll be looking for who he passed it to." I crouched by the boot prints again, forcing my brain to work past the cold weight in my stomach. "Two men. They came in from the northwest—that's the direction of the Halvorsen survey camp on the Kestrel. They loaded something out of the cargo bay and carried it out. Heavy enough to make two trips, look at the spacing." I pointed. "But here's the thing that doesn't fit."

"What?"

"If they already had what they wanted, why leave the message? Why come back?" I looked up at her. "Because whatever they pulled out of this plane isn't what they were looking for. They're still searching. Which means the real prize is still ahead of us—at the cache—or they think it is."

"Or," Marisol said, "they left the message because they know someone else has been here recently, and they want that someone scared enough to run."

I looked down at my own boots, at the prints we'd already stamped all over their crime scene like signatures. "Well. We've certainly given them something to find."

* * *

We searched the wreck anyway. I don't fully know why—some stubborn pilot's instinct that says you inspect the wreckage before you fly on, that says the machine will tell you how it died if you're willing to listen. Marisol worked the fuselage from the tail forward while I took the cockpit, climbing onto the folded wing and lowering myself through the fig-choked windshield frame into a space that smelled of mold and old fuel and, faintly, impossibly, of cedar—someone had stored something aromatic in here, long ago.

The instrument panel was a garden of rust. The control yoke wore a sleeve of moss. But wedged down between the pilot's seat rails, where thirty years of debris had drifted and settled, my fingers found something that wasn't debris.

A nameplate. Brass, screwed to the seat frame, polished by a thumb again and again across the years. I worked it loose and held it up to the light falling through the broken canopy.

**D. R. STERLING — CAPTAIN**

The clearing tilted. I sat down hard in the dead pilot's seat, in my father's seat, in the wreck he'd supposedly died in except that everything here said he'd walked away, and I held that little brass rectangle in both hands like it was the only solid thing in the world.

"Petra?" Marisol's voice came from somewhere far away. "What did you find?"

I couldn't speak. I passed it back to her through the windshield frame, and heard her breath catch.

"Sterling," she said. "That's—your father flew this plane. Your father was on the '89 expedition." A pause, and then, sharper: "Petra, if he survived the crash—if he stripped this wreck himself, if he's the one who's been coming back—"

"He didn't disappear," I said. My voice sounded strange to me, flattened, like a radio transmission from very far away. "He didn't die out here. He *hid*. He hid the evidence and he hid himself and he let everyone—including me—believe he was dead, because being dead was the only place Halvorsen wouldn't look for him."

The thought landed in me like a stone dropped down a well, falling and falling without hitting bottom. My father might be alive. Forty-nine years old in my memory forever, frozen at the moment he kissed my forehead at the mission school gate and said *back by Friday, Pet* —he could be sixty-eight now, seventy, gray and stooped and living under some other name in some other river town, breathing while my mother wasted away waiting, while I grew up learning to be invisible because visibility felt like the thing that had killed him.

Or he was dead after all, and the boot prints belonged to men who had killed him twice—once in '89, and once again, recently, when they'd finished stripping everything he'd spent a lifetime protecting.

Both possibilities hurt in different places. Neither one would let go.

"Petra." Marisol crouched at the windshield frame, and her face, framed by the fig roots, was more open than I'd ever seen it. "Whatever's true—we can't learn it standing here. The cache is still ahead of us. The journals, the affidavit, whatever Elias died protecting. That's the thread. Everything else hangs off it."

She was right, and I hated her a little for it, the way you hate anyone who's right when you want to fall apart.

I climbed out of my father's cockpit. On the way, my hand brushed the yoke, and the moss came away under my palm, and underneath it the leather was worn smooth in the shape of a grip—worn by years of hands, plural, recent hands, and I chose not to think about whose.

* * *

We made six kilometers before dark, putting distance between ourselves and the clearing, moving fast and quiet along the map's penciled line as it climbed toward a saddle between two forested peaks. The thunder had stopped rehearsing and started performing; the light went the color of old brass, and the air pressure changed in that way every bush pilot knows, the way that says *the sky has decided something.*

We sheltered under a rock overhang and ate the last of the rice cold, and I spread the map across my knees by flashlight, studying the route ahead. Past the saddle, the line descended toward a river valley—toward the confluence, and beyond it, three days' travel if the weather held, the cache site marked with a second X and a single word in Elias's hand: *truth.*

"We're being followed," Marisol said. She said it calmly, the way she'd tell me a fuel pump was running rough. "Have been since midday. Something moving parallel to us on the ridge. Stopping when we stop."

"I know."

"You knew?"

"I've been counting the bird calls that aren't birds." I clicked off the flashlight and sat in the dark, listening to the forest hold its breath around us. "Two men, probably. The ones from the wreck, or others. Halvorsen doesn't send pairs when they mean business—they send pairs to herd, and the real party comes later."

"So what do we do?"

I thought about my father's brass nameplate in my breast pocket, pressing against my ribs like a second heartbeat. I thought about the words carved in the tail: *last chance.*

"We keep moving," I said. "Tonight, in the dark, down the north slope instead of the saddle—they'll expect us to take the high route. And tomorrow we hit the river valley, and we find a boat, and we stop being people who leave footprints."

Marisol was quiet for a moment. Then, in the darkness, almost too soft to hear: "My father wrote *truth* on that map. Just the one word. Like it was the destination itself."

"It is," I said. "That's what scares me."

Far off to the south, lightning stitched the horizon, silent at this distance, patient as the sky my father used to warn me about. And on the ridge above us, something that wasn't a bird began to move.
