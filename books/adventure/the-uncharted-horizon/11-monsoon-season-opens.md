## 11. Monsoon Season Opens

The first drop hit the back of my neck like a thrown stone.

I was crouched at the water's edge, rinsing a pot in the shallows of the lake where we'd made camp after coming down off the ridge, and I remember thinking—absurdly—that it felt warm. Then the second drop landed in the pot itself, and the third on my wrist, and by the fourth I was already moving, because you don't grow up around marine engines and river men without learning that the sky doesn't warn twice.

"Petra!" I shouted it over my shoulder, already scrambling for the tarp we'd strung between two trees. "It's starting!"

She came out of the tent with her boots half-laced and her hair plastered flat, and she looked up at the canopy the way people look at a verdict. Above us the leaves had gone from whispering to roaring, and through the gaps I could see the clouds had turned the color of wet slate—not storm clouds, not the passing kind, but the flat, bottomless gray that means *this is the season now, and the season does not care about your plans*.

"It's early," she said.

"You think?"

"No—I mean it's *early*. The monsoon's supposed to break in another ten days, maybe two weeks. My father—" She stopped herself. I watched her do it, watch the sentence die in her mouth the way it always did when she reached for Elias Vane and found nothing there she wanted to hold. "The old-timers said late October. This is September."

"Well," I said, tying off the last rope, "the old-timers are going to have to update their almanac."

The rain came down like something poured. Within twenty minutes the ground under our feet had gone soft, then slick, then liquid, little runnels forming between the trees and finding each other, joining into streams with opinions of their own. We sat under the tarp and ate cold rice and watched the lake begin to breathe—rising in slow, visible pulses against the shore, swallowing the mud shelf where I'd washed the pot, then the root system of a fallen tree, then the tree itself.

Petra didn't talk much that night. Neither did I. There's a particular silence that falls between two people who've both done the math and gotten the same ugly answer, and we sat inside it until the light went out of the sky.

* * *

By morning the trail back to the lake where the plane sat with its bent propeller was gone.

Not flooded—*gone*. What had been a walking track along a low saddle between two ridges was now a brown torrent wide as a road, carrying whole branches, a dead tapir bloated and turning, and a quantity of red clay that told me the hills upstream were shedding their skin. Petra stood at the edge of it with the map case slung across her chest and her jaw set in a way I was beginning to recognize as her version of panic.

"We could go up," she said. "Climb the ridge, come down the far side, circle north."

"To what? The north side drains into the same valley. And look at that slope." I pointed at the hillside above us, where the rain had loosened a scar of earth maybe forty meters long. It wasn't sliding yet. It was thinking about it. "We'd be crossing three of those. In this."

"So we wait it out."

"For how long?" I heard my own voice come out harder than I meant, and I didn't take it back. "Four days, Petra. Maybe five before the concession lapses. Even if the rain stopped right now—and it's not going to stop—it would take a week for that trail to be passable. Longer for the ground to dry. You know it. You've flown over this country your whole life."

She was quiet for a moment. Rain ran off the brim of her cap in a steady thread.

"The river, then," she said finally, and there was something in her voice I didn't like—something that sounded like a woman talking herself into a room with only one door.

* * *

The longboat lay half-sunk in the flooded timber a kilometer downstream from our camp, exactly where Petra remembered seeing it on the flight in: an old river freighter's tender, maybe nine meters, hull black with age, one end resting on a submerged log like a drunk leaning on a fence. Somebody had abandoned it years ago. Somebody else had stripped it of anything worth carrying. What remained was a shell—thwarts rotted to lace, a rudder hanging by one pintle, and a split in the planking below the waterline on the port side about as long as my forearm and open enough to put two fingers inside.

I put two fingers inside it, because apparently I needed to confirm with my own hands what my eyes were already telling me.

"Well?" Petra stood in the shallows, holding the bow line, rain streaming off her.

"Well," I said, "it floats. Technically. In the sense that a sieve holds soup."

"Marisol."

"I can patch it." I pulled my fingers out and wiped them on my thigh and made myself look at the whole hull again, properly, the way I'd look at an engine someone wanted me to make run. That was the trick, I'd learned it in a hundred boatyards from here to Port Amara: don't see the wreck, see the machine. A machine is just problems arranged in an order. "The split's clean—the wood didn't rot through, it cracked. Grounding damage, probably, years back, and then it sat and the crack worked wider. If I can close it and back it with a patch plate, seal the seams, replace that thwart so the hull doesn't flex itself apart under load—"

"Can you do all that?"

"With what?" I gestured at the landscape, which offered me rain, mud, and trees. "I need resin or pitch. Fiberglass if God loves us, which He doesn't. Canvas, tar, anything. Fasteners. And time, which we also don't have."

Petra was already wading toward the wreck. "There was a mission station on the survey map. Twelve kilometers downriver, on the east bank. Elias marked it—supply point. If it's still standing, they'll have a store, tools, maybe a boat shed."

"And if it's not?"

"Then we build a raft," she said, "and we float down a flooding river on a raft, and we probably die, but we'll die having tried something."

I stared at her. Water dripped off my nose. Somewhere in the drowned forest a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oil.

"That's your plan. That's the actual plan you're proposing."

"It's the only plan where 'probably' is doing less work than 'definitely,'" she said. "Definitely dying is staying here. Probably dying is the boat."

And the worst part—the part I still turn over sometimes—is that she was right, and I knew she was right, and some stubborn mechanic's part of my brain had already started listing the repair sequence before she finished the sentence. Close the crack. Back it. Seal the seams. You don't argue with a job once you've started listing the steps. The list owns you.

"Fine," I said. "Fine. But we do it my way. Nobody touches the hull but me. And when it sinks—and it might sink, Petra, I want that stated clearly for the record—you're swimming next to me and you don't get to say I told you so, because I will have told you so, repeatedly, in advance."

"Noted," she said, and hauled the bow line, and the old boat groaned up out of the floodwater like something being raised from a grave, which in every way that mattered, it was.

* * *

We spent two days on that hull, and I will carry those two days in my hands for the rest of my life.

The mission station existed—a cluster of buildings with a tin roof and a chapel, run by a Guatemalan brother named Anselmo who took one look at us, one look at the sky, and said, "You want to go *downstream*? In *that*?" in the tone of a man confirming a diagnosis he'd seen too many times. But he fed us, and he opened his workshop, and he let me raid it.

What I got: a liter of epoxy resin older than I am, hardened at the edges but alive in the middle; a canvas tarp; a bucket of pine pitch Brother Anselmo rendered himself from the local pines ("old method, better than your chemicals, my father used it"); a coffee can of assorted screws and a hand drill; and four hours of shelter from the rain while I laid out the work.

Here is what patching a split hull actually is. First you close the wound—you clamp the planks together, and since I had no clamps, I used ratchet straps borrowed off Petra's gear bag and a pile of stones for counterweight, rigging the whole thing like a field surgery. Then you back it: a plate on the inside, screwed and bedded in pitch, bridging the crack so the load spreads instead of concentrating. Then the outside: canvas soaked in resin, wrapped tight, lashed with cord until it set hard as shell. Then the seams—every seam in a sixty-year-old boat is a question mark, and I answered them all with pitch, working it hot into the gaps with a spatula while the rain hammered the shed roof loud enough to shout over.

Petra helped. She was clumsy with the tools but tireless with everything else—heating pitch, holding straps, bailing the boat every hour as the floodwater found new ways in through seams I hadn't sealed yet. At one point, deep in the second day, I looked up from inside the hull and saw her silhouetted against the doorway, feeding cord through a lashing pattern I'd shown her once, getting it wrong, starting over, getting it wrong again, starting over again—and something in my chest did a thing I didn't have a name for. My father used to stand like that. Watching me work. Patient with his own uselessness in a boatyard, because he knew, the way good men know, that some jobs belong to one pair of hands.

I looked away before the feeling could become a thought.

"How bad is it?" she asked. "Honestly."

"Honestly?" I ran my palm along the patched plate one more time. The resin had cured to a dull amber. The pitch in the seams had gone glassy. The hull, knocked with a knuckle, answered with a sound somewhere between solid and hopeful. "It'll hold in calm water. It'll probably hold in current. It will absolutely not hold if we hit anything harder than a large fish. So we read the water, we pick our lines, and we never, ever stop bailing."

"That's not a lot of margin."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

Brother Anselmo walked us down to the water at first light on the third day, though first light was mostly a rumor behind the rain. The river had risen another meter overnight. Where the longboat had lain tilted on its log, there was now open brown water, and the log itself was somewhere in the next province. The current moved through the drowned trees with a low, continuous roar, like the river clearing its throat.

He gave Petra a bundle of food and gave me a small tin of pitch and pressed both our hands briefly, one after the other, like a man stamping documents.

"The confluence is four days down if the river is kind," he said. "It will not be kind. Keep to the outside of the bends, away from the cut banks—they fall. Sleep on high ground, never on gravel bars—the water comes up at night faster than you believe. And when you hear the narrows—" he paused, and his face did something careful, "—you will hear them before you see them. Pray then, if you pray. If you don't, hold on."

"What's at the narrows?" Petra asked.

"Water," he said, "in a hurry."

* * *

We loaded the boat in the rain: the map case in a waterproof bag lashed under the midship thwart, the food, the tools, the tarp, the bailer—which was a cut-down jerry can, and which I regarded, privately, as the most important piece of equipment aboard. Petra took the bow. I took the stern, with the ruined rudder tied upright and a spare paddle as my steering oar, because a rudder hanging by one pintle is not a rudder, it's a liability with ambitions.

For a long moment we just sat there, letting the current take the weight, feeling the boat decide whether it forgave us.

"Marisol." Petra had her paddle across her knees. Rain ran down her face and she didn't wipe it away. "Last chance. We can beach it, walk back to the mission, wait out the season, lose the claim. Anselmo would take us in. Nobody would think less—"

"I would think less," I said. "Of myself. Which is the only opinion I answer to."

She almost smiled. "Okay."

"Besides," I said, and shoved off, and felt the river take us the way a hand closes around a wrist, "you promised a dying man. I came along for closure. Turns out closure is downstream."

The current picked us up and the bank slid away and the drowned forest closed around us, gray and dripping and endless, and the old boat settled into the water with a sound I chose to interpret as commitment rather than complaint. Every few minutes I checked the patch plate with my boot heel—a habit, a tic, a prayer. Solid. Solid. Solid.

Behind us, the mission disappeared into the rain. Ahead of us, the river bent away south, toward the confluence, toward Port Amara, toward whatever my father had spent his whole life guarding and I had spent mine refusing to look at.

No going back now. The trail was underwater, the plane was a day's climb away with a bent propeller, and the only road left in the world was the one flowing under my feet, moving at the speed of everything I couldn't undo.

I put the steering oar in the water and leaned into the current, and the longboat, patched with pitch and prayer and forty-year-old epoxy, swung its nose downstream.

That was when we both heard it—far off, low, rising under the rain like a note held on the world's largest organ. Petra turned and looked at me, and I looked at her, and neither of us had to ask.

The narrows. And we were aimed straight at them.
