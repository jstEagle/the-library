## 12. Leaking Toward the Confluence

The river did not care that we were on it.

That was the first lesson of the second day, and Marisol learned it faster than I did. She stood hip-deep in the bilge, one hand on the gunwale, the other working the bail bucket in a rhythm she'd found somewhere between exhaustion and prayer—scoop, heave, scoop, heave—while the longboat drank the brown water through its patched seam like a tired animal at a trough. The patch was holding. Barely. The way a promise holds when both people are afraid to test it.

"Petra." Her voice came flat over the drumming rain. "It's coming in faster."

I had the survey sketch spread across my knees under an oilskin, weighted at the corners with two stones and a tin cup. Sixty years old, maybe older—the paper gone soft as cloth, ink faded to the color of old bruises. Elias Vane had copied it from somewhere, or drawn it from memory, or been given it by someone who'd run this water before any of us were born. It showed the river as a single confident line, annotated in a cramped hand: *shoal L after bend*, *ferry rock mid-channel at low water*, *portage right if flooded*. Whoever wrote those notes had trusted the next reader with their life.

I was beginning to understand that this was how Elias had loved people. In margins. In instructions left behind for strangers.

"Then we bail faster," I said, and took up the second bucket.

* * *

We'd fallen into shifts without discussing it, the way you fall into any survival arithmetic. Forty minutes on the oars, forty minutes bailing, forty minutes resting—which meant lying in the bottom of the boat among the wet duffel and the wrapped map, staring up at a sky the color of wet slate, listening to the hull tick and groan around its seam. The rain came in waves now, monsoon-proper, sheets of it that erased the far bank and turned the whole world into water moving in three directions at once: down from the sky, along the river, into our boat.

Marisol rowed the way she did everything—economically, angrily, as if the oars had personally wronged her. She'd tied her hair back with a strip of tarp cord and her forearms worked under the rain jacket like pistons. Watching her, I kept seeing the floatplane's shadow on the lake, the bent propeller, the four days we'd lost to aluminum and epoxy. Four days we didn't have. Five weeks had become three, and three was becoming something smaller every time I checked Elias's watch, which I'd taken to wearing because someone should.

"You're drifting us," she said without turning around.

"I'm steering."

"You're drifting us toward that—" she flicked her chin at a line of white water ahead where the river pinched between two black ridges. "There's a shoal there. I can hear it."

I looked at the sketch. *Shoal L after bend.* The bend was behind us. Which meant the shoal was ahead, on the left, exactly where the water was hissing.

"Right side," I said, and put the tiller over.

She rowed us wide, then hard right, and we slid past the shoal close enough to see the brown water folding over itself in ropes, close enough to feel the pull of it trying to take the stern. Neither of us spoke until the channel widened again and the roar fell behind us like a door closing.

"Your father drew this," she said. It wasn't a question. We'd been circling it for a day and a half.

"The original crew surveyed this stretch in '89. My father was on that crew." I kept my eyes on the channel markers—or what passed for them, a leaning dead tree here, a split rock there, landmarks that had outlived the men who'd named them. "Elias must have gotten the sketch from him. Or made a copy."

"And he never told you? All those years, flying medicine runs past this river—he never once said, *by the way, Petra, I knew your dad*?"

"Elias Vane wasn't a man who spent words."

"No," she said, and something in her voice went sharp and thin. "He wasn't. He spent everything else, though. Wages, birthdays, funerals. Everything except words."

I let it sit. The rain filled the silence for me.

* * *

We camped that night on a gravel bar that the rising water had already claimed half of, under a lean-to of the tarp and two paddles. Marisol got a fire going with damp wood and pure spite, and we ate cold rice and half a tin of sardines each, and I re-wrapped the map in its oilcloth while she watched me do it with an expression I couldn't read.

"Can I ask you something," she said finally, "and you answer it honestly, or not at all?"

"That's a narrow gate."

"It's the only gate I've got left." She poked the fire. Sparks went up and died young. "Do you actually believe a deathbed promise binds you? Legally, morally—whatever. Because I keep watching you drive yourself like the man's ghost is holding the throttle, and I want to know if that's faith or just another way to not stop moving."

I thought about lying. I was getting better at it since the men in clean boots, but not with her. Not anymore. Something about shared bilge water strips you bare.

"My father disappeared when I was nine," I said. "No body, no wreckage they ever showed us, no letter. Just—gone. And my mother spent eleven years leaving the porch light on before she died still leaving it on, and I spent twenty years flying routes that circled that valley without ever once landing in it. So no, Marisol. I don't know what promises bind. I only know what happens when nobody keeps them."

The fire cracked. Downriver, something big moved in the water—a log, a caiman, the river itself shifting its weight—and neither of us flinched, which told me how far we'd come in three days.

"He wrote about you," she said quietly.

"What?"

"In the margin notes. On the map. I read them while you were sleeping yesterday." She wasn't looking at me; she was looking at the fire like it owed her money. "Little things. *P.S. is afraid of thunder but won't admit it. P.S. lands like she's apologizing to the ground.* He watched you grow up, Petra. Mission letters, whatever that means. He knew your name before you ever knew his."

My hands went still on the oilcloth. Somewhere under my ribs, an old door I'd welded shut rattled once on its hinges.

"That doesn't make it true," I said. "Knowing a name isn't knowing a person. You're proof of that—you knew his name your whole life and it bought you nothing."

It was crueler than I meant it. I saw it land. But Marisol Vane had a spine like the black ridges we'd run between all day, and instead of breaking she leaned forward.

"Exactly," she said. "Exactly. So maybe the question isn't whether his promise binds you. Maybe it's whether you're going to spend the rest of your life being the daughter who never got told anything, or start being the woman who finds out for herself." She stood, dumped her sardine tin into the fire, and lay down facing away from me. "Get some sleep. Your shift starts in four hours."

* * *

Day four, the river rose.

We could see it happening—the banks narrowing hour by hour, driftwood riding past us standing upright like drowned men walking, the current picking up speed until steering became suggestion rather than command. The sketch's notes assumed a river sixty years younger and several feet lower. Every rapid we ran was a guess wearing an old man's handwriting as a disguise.

At midday we hit the confluence stretch, where a tributary came in hard from the east and the main channel braided into three threads around a forested island. The sketch showed one channel. The river offered three, and two of them ended, as far as I could tell, in churning white nothing.

"Left," Marisol said.

"Middle. Left goes shallow—see the color?"

"Middle goes *fast*. Look at the standing waves."

"Fast is fine. Shallow tears the patch off and we swim."

"We might swim anyway!"

"Then we'll swim toward the middle!"

She stared at me, rain streaming off her jaw, and then she laughed—one short, sharp bark of a laugh with no humor in it and all kinds of something else—and dug the oars in. "You are the most infuriating—*middle*, fine, MIDDLE—"

The confluence took us like a hand takes a coin. The water stacked and dropped and shoved, and the patch on the port seam began singing a new note, a high wet whistle of separation, and we bailed and steered and swore through ninety seconds that felt like a season, and then the channels closed back together below the island and the river went smooth as poured metal and we sat there gasping among six inches of bilge, alive, ridiculous, victorious.

Marisol looked at the water sloshing around her boots. Then at me. Then she started laughing again, properly this time, helpless, doubled over the oar, and I found I was laughing too, and it occurred to me that I couldn't remember the last time I'd laughed on the water. Maybe ever. Flying, yes—there's a kind of laughter in surviving weather—but this was different. This was the sound of two women discovering they made a crew.

"We need to stop," she said, wiping her face. "Not sleeping. Stopping. Patching. That seam's going to open tonight and I'd rather be on land when it does."

* * *

We hauled out on a mud bank beneath a stand of fig trees, and while Marisol stripped the old patch and went at the seam with epoxy and canvas scraps and language that would have embarrassed a dockworker, I dried the map by the small fire and studied the next stretch. Below the confluence, the notes changed character. Fewer hazards. More distances, more bearings, and then, in fresher ink, newer handwriting—Elias's own, layered over the old surveyor's like a man talking over his teacher:

*Confluence to the narrows — 2 days' good water. After the narrows, ask at the villages. They remember.*

And beneath that, almost too faint to read:

*I should have brought them both. Should have told them both. A man who leaves a map instead of a letter is a coward with good penmanship.*

I read it three times. Then I folded the map away and carried those words around inside me like a stone in a boot.

When Marisol finished—hands black with epoxy, hair plastered to her skull, triumphant in a battered way—I handed her the last of the coffee, brewed thin as communion wine, and asked the question I'd been avoiding since the gravel bar.

"Why did you really come?"

She drank. Took her time about it.

"You want the real answer or the one I gave myself at the dock?"

"There's a difference?"

"Huge." She turned the tin cup in her fingers. "The answer I gave myself was closure. See where he died, scatter whatever, be done with him forever, go home to engines that don't lie to me. Clean story." She looked up. "The real answer is that when you showed up with that telegram, it was the first time anyone had ever come looking for *me* about *him*. Twenty-nine years of him being a hole in my life, and suddenly there's a stranger on my dock saying, *your father sent me, your father thought of you*—and I hated it, and I couldn't breathe, and I got in the boat anyway. Because the worst thing isn't having a bad father, Petra. The worst thing is never finding out why."

The fire popped. Downstream, the river ran on toward the narrows, patient as debt.

"And if the why is worse than the hole?" I said.

"Then at least it has edges." She shrugged. "A hole you can fall into forever. A truth has a shape. You can walk around it."

I thought about my mother's porch light burning eleven years for a man who was never coming home, and how much of my life had been shaped like that light—circular, faithful, aimed at nothing. Marisol was right, and I hated her a little for it, and I loved her a little for it, and I suspected those two things were going to keep being the same thing for a long time.

"Your shift," she said, nodding at the sky going purple. "Wake me if the patch sings."

* * *

I took first watch alone on the water, because the bank was soft and the mosquitoes were a war crime, and running dark was safer than camping. Marisol slept in the bow under the tarp, one arm draped over the duffel that held the map, even unconscious, even exhausted—guarding it the way I was starting to suspect we were both guarding it. Not for the permits. Not for the claim.

For the shape of the truth inside it.

The river ran silver-black under a tearing cloud deck. I steered by the sketch's bearings and the occasional flash of lightning far upstream, counting seconds, doing the arithmetic of distance. Two days to the narrows, Elias had written. Ask at the villages. They remember.

They remember. Three words that meant Elias had lived a whole life out here that neither of us knew—debts and friendships and favors banked along these banks like driftwood. A man could vanish into the interior and leave more of himself behind than most men leave in their own houses. The thought sat strangely with me. I'd spent twenty years assuming absence was emptiness. But the river was teaching me otherwise: absence leaves a channel. Water remembers where something used to be.

Near midnight, the patch sang—not the high whistle of failure but a low complaint, a seep I could manage with the bucket—and I bailed one-handed and steered one-handed and let myself think about what waited past the narrows. The cache. The claim. Halvorsen's men somewhere behind us on this same water, or ahead of us on it. And underneath all of it, the thing Elias had written in fading ink that I hadn't shown Marisol yet:

*A coward with good penmanship.*

I'd show her tomorrow. Or maybe I wouldn't. Some words you have to earn the right to hand someone.

Toward dawn, the channel began to narrow. The banks climbed. Black rock walls rose on either side, dripping, furred with green, and the river quickened between them with a sound like a crowd gathering, and I recognized the geometry from the sketch before I recognized it with my eyes.

The narrows.

And moored against the far wall, half-hidden under an overhang, riding at anchor in the gray first light—

a boat. Long, low, engine-covered, with two figures aboard who were very deliberately not looking at us, which was the most alarming thing they could have done.

I reached back and shook Marisol's shoulder.

"Marisol. Wake up. We have company."
