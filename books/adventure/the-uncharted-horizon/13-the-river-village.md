## 13. The River Village

The smoke told us we were close before anything else did.

A thin gray thread rising off the north bank, half-hidden by the curtain of rain that had followed us downriver for two days. Then a dog barking—carrying impossibly far over the water—and the shape of stilt houses emerging from the green like something remembered rather than seen: thatched roofs gone soft with moss, dugouts pulled up on the mud, laundry strung between posts and whipping in the wind.

Petra cut the engine and let us drift.

"We should announce ourselves," she said. Her voice was hoarse from days of shouting over water and motor noise. "Not come in quiet. Quiet reads as wrong out here."

"How do you announce yourself to people who don't know you?"

"You don't." She was watching the bank, counting things I couldn't see. "You let them decide what you are."

I thought about that. About how much of my life had been spent deciding what other people were before they got the chance—the father who left, the mother who stayed too long, every man who walked into the shop with a smile and a story about why he couldn't pay his bill yet. I'd made a career of reading people fast because the ones who fooled you cost you parts.

"Then what are we?" I asked.

Petra didn't answer right away. She was looking at the village the way she looked at weather—not at it, but through it, at what it would become.

"Tired," she said finally. "We're tired, and our boat is dying, and we need help. That's honest enough."

She restarted the engine and we came in slow, the longboat wallowing, my patch on the hull holding the way a held breath holds—technically, and not for long. The rain thinned as we approached, and figures gathered under the eaves of the nearest house. No one waved. No one ran. They just watched us the way river people watch everything: patient, unhurried, certain that whatever the water brought them, they'd still be here after it left.

An old man stood at the head of the landing steps. He was small and straight-backed, barefoot on wet planks, with a machete hanging from a woven belt and a face so lined it looked like bark. He didn't speak until we'd tied off.

"You are coming from upriver," he said. Not a question.

"Yes," Petra said.

"The water is high."

"It's high everywhere."

He considered this. His eyes went to me, then to the boat, then—staying there longer than anywhere else—to the oilcloth bundle strapped under the thwart where I'd wedged it three days ago and hadn't let it out of arm's reach since.

"You are carrying something," he said.

I felt Petra go still beside me. "We're carrying supplies," I said. "And ourselves."

The old man almost smiled. Almost. It moved across his face like a fish under ice and was gone.

"Supplies," he repeated. He turned and called something over his shoulder in a language I didn't know, and the watchers on the veranda dispersed—not dismissed, just satisfied that whatever judgment needed making had been made. "You will eat. You will dry. Your boat will be looked at. Come."

His name was Anselm, though it took us an hour to learn it, because he introduced himself only as "the one who talks when talking is needed," which I took at first for modesty and later understood to be a job description.

* * *

They fed us fish stewed with something sharp and green, and cassava, and tea so strong it stripped the exhaustion out of my muscles and replaced it with a fine, humming alertness. We ate on the covered platform of Anselm's house while the rain came back and worked itself into a fury against the thatch. A dozen people drifted through over the course of the meal—a woman named Rosa who owned the nets, her two sons, a girl of maybe ten who stared at me with frank, unblinking curiosity until I smiled and she decided, visibly, that I was acceptable.

Word travels faster than boats on a river. By the time the plates were cleared, everyone knew we'd come from the lake country, that our hull was split, that we were going downstream toward Port Amara, and that we were not, whatever we said, carrying mere supplies.

It was Rosa who broke the seal on the real conversation. She sat down across from me, wrung out her hair, and said, without preamble:

"You knew Elias Vane."

It wasn't a question either. This village specialized in statements shaped like questions, or questions that weren't really questions at all. I set down my cup.

"He was my father."

Something passed around the platform—not surprise, exactly. Confirmation. As if they'd been waiting since we landed for the word *father* to be spoken aloud, and now that it had been, the rest could proceed.

"He came here first in—" Rosa counted on her fingers, frowning, "—many years ago. Before the big flood. He was young then. Loud." She laughed, and the laugh was warm, and it did something strange to my chest to hear this man described as loud, this silent stranger whose letters I'd never answered. "He mapped the river for the company. He lived in that house—" she pointed across the water to a stilt house with a collapsed roof, "—for two seasons. He fixed our outboard motors. He taught Anselm's brother to read English. When the fever came, he carried medicine up from the mission on foot, forty kilometers, in the wet season."

"That doesn't sound like him," I said, before I could stop myself.

Rosa looked at me with no offense at all, only interest. "No? What does he sound like to you?"

"A man who sends a telegram once a year and can't be bothered to write more than four lines in it. *Alive. Working. Don't worry.*" I heard my own voice harden and couldn't soften it. "That's what he sounds like."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Rain drummed. Somewhere a child laughed and was hushed.

"People show different faces to different rivers," Anselm said quietly. He'd settled onto the bench beside me at some point, unnoticed. "To some, he was the surveyor. To some, the company man. To us—" he spread his hands, taking in the platform, the village, the brown floodwater sliding past below us, "—he was the one who came back. Every year, even after the company stopped sending him. Even when he was old and coughing. He always came back."

"When?" I asked. My throat was tight. "When was the last time?"

"Two years ago," Rosa said. "Maybe three. He was thin. He slept a lot. But he sat right there—" she nodded at the spot where I was sitting, and I had to fight the urge to move, "—and he drank this same tea, and he talked about his daughter."

The rain filled the silence.

"What did he say about her?" I asked. I meant it to sound indifferent. It came out sounding like nothing of the kind.

Rosa exchanged a glance with Anselm, and whatever passed between them, it ended with her reaching into the folds of her dress and producing a small tin box, rusted at the corners, which she set on the boards between us but did not open.

"Before that," Anselm said, and his voice changed, took on weight, "there is something you must know. Something that concerns your safety, and maybe your lives."

Petra, who had been quiet through all of this, leaned forward. "The Halvorsen men."

Anselm's eyebrows rose slightly. "You know them already."

"We've met."

"Then you know they do not travel like travelers." He said it flatly, and the flatness was worse than anger. "Four days ago, maybe five, a boat came downriver. Aluminum, new motor, two men. Clean clothes. Boots that had never touched mud." He looked at me. "One of them had a photograph. Of a woman. Dark hair, mechanic's hands." He nodded at me. "He asked if anyone had seen you. He asked if a pilot had come through. He asked about papers—a map, documents, anything old."

"And what did you tell him?" Petra asked.

"I told him we see many boats." Anselm shrugged, a whole philosophy contained in the gesture. "But others were less careful. And these men—they do not ask twice. They make examples." He paused. "The Ferreira family, down at the next bend. They sold fuel to strangers last month without asking questions. Yesterday morning their fuel store burned. Nobody saw who did it. Nobody ever sees."

The cold that went through me had nothing to do with the rain.

"They're ahead of us," Petra said. Her voice was level, but I'd spent enough days on the water with her now to hear the current underneath it. "Or behind us. Or both."

"Both," Anselm confirmed. "They went downstream yesterday. But rivers bend, and men return. You cannot stay here long, and you cannot travel stupidly. The high water helps you—it is fast, and they will expect you to hide, not run. But the narrows at Kestrel Bend—" he shook his head slowly, "—that is a place where a boat can be made to have an accident. And accidents are very convenient for men in clean boots."

* * *

Later, when the rain had softened to a whisper and most of the village had gone dark except for a few oil lamps glowing behind mosquito netting, Rosa opened the tin box.

We were alone on the platform. Petra had gone to check on the boat with two of Rosa's sons, who'd pronounced our patch "a crime" and were re-doing it properly by lamplight, and I'd stayed behind mostly because standing up required a decision and I wasn't sure I had one left in me.

"This belonged to Elias," Rosa said. "He left it with us on his last visit. He said—" she hesitated, and I realized she was choosing her words carefully, translating not just language but meaning, "—he said, *if a girl comes down this river looking for me or for what I left behind, give her this. If no girl comes, burn it.*"

My heart was doing something complicated. "Why burn it?"

"Because some things are only gifts if they arrive," she said simply. "Otherwise they are just weight."

She opened the box. Inside, wrapped in waxed paper gone brittle with age, was an envelope. The paper had yellowed to the color of weak tea. The address on the front was written in a careful, draftsman's hand—the hand of a man who drew maps for a living—and it read:

**Marisol Vane, c/o Mrs. E. Okonkwo, Harbor Road, Port Amara.**

My mother's name. Our apartment, the one above the chandlery, the one I'd lived in until I was nineteen. The letter had been mailed—I could see the postmark, faded but legible—eleven years ago.

"It was sent," Rosa said, following my eyes. "Truly. But the river post is unreliable, and the address service in Port Amara lost packages that year—there was a strike, a flood, I don't remember. It came back to us marked undeliverable. Elias was already gone upstream again. So we kept it. Waiting."

Eleven years. Eleven years this letter had been sitting in a tin box in a village I'd never heard of, sixty kilometers of brown water from a life I'd already dismantled and rebuilt without him. I picked it up and it weighed nothing and it weighed everything.

"Do you want to be alone?" Rosa asked.

"No," I said, and surprised myself by meaning it.

I turned the envelope over. My name in my father's handwriting. I traced the letters once, lightly, the way you'd touch something you suspected might vanish.

I opened it.

* * *

There were two pages. The handwriting started neat and deteriorated as it went, as if the man writing it kept having to stop and gather himself.

*Marisol.*

*By the time you read this you'll be older than I can picture, which is true anyway even without the delay. I've started this letter nine times. Anselm will tell you I'm a coward about paper. He's right.*

*I owe you an explanation and I've owed it so long that explanations have started to feel like excuses, so I'm going to give you facts instead and let you do with them what you will.*

*Fact: I left because staying would have killed you. Not me—you. There are men who follow my work, and I don't mean creditors. I found something in '89 that I was not supposed to find, and the finding of it has shaped every choice I've made since, including the worst one, including leaving you. I told myself distance was protection. Maybe I told myself that because it was easier than the truth, which is that I didn't trust myself to keep you safe and stay.*

*Fact: the thing I found is real, and it is documented, and it is hidden somewhere no one will look because everyone thinks I'm looking for gold. I am not looking for gold. I have never been looking for gold. Gold is what I let people believe so they'd stop asking what else an old prospector might be carrying up and down this river for thirty years.*

*Fact: if you are reading this, then either I am dead, or I finally found the courage I should have had when you were six years old. Either way, I'm sorry. Sorry is a small word for it. I know that. I've had thirty years to find a bigger word and I haven't.*

*Don't come looking for me. That's not protection anymore, it's just pride talking—but listen to me: if you ever learn what I was carrying, don't sell it, don't bury it, and don't keep it. Use it. It belongs to the river people as much as to us. More.*

*Your father,*

*Elias*

I read it twice. On the second read my vision blurred somewhere around *including leaving you*, and I had to press the heel of my hand against my eyes and sit very still until the platform stopped tilting.

He'd known. All those years of telegrams—*alive, working, don't worry*—the four-line cowardice of them, and underneath it this. Underneath it a man who'd left his daughter not because he didn't love her but because he loved her badly, in the only broken way he had available, and had spent three decades hauling a secret up and down a river to keep it away from the people who'd killed for less.

Beside me, I became aware that Petra had returned. She was standing at the edge of the lamplight, dripping, saying nothing, watching me hold the pages. In her hand, I noticed, was an envelope of her own—older, softer, its corners rounded by handling. Rosa must have given it to her while I read. I watched Petra look at it the way you look at a door you've been afraid to open for twenty years.

"Yours too?" I asked. My voice came out scraped.

She nodded once. She didn't open it. She folded it into her jacket, inside pocket, over her heart, and sat down beside me on the bench, and for a long time neither of us said anything, and the river went on below us, carrying everything toward the sea.

* * *

We left before dawn.

Anselm woke us himself, pressing hot tea into our hands and refusing thanks. Rosa's sons had finished the hull overnight—properly, this time, with resin and canvas and a kind of stitched reinforcement I'd never have thought of—and the longboat rode noticeably truer when we loaded it. The village turned out on the landing in the gray half-light, silent, watching, the way they'd watched us arrive.

Rosa embraced me briefly and fiercely. "He talked about you like you hung the moon," she said into my ear. "I wanted you to know that. Whatever else is true."

Then Anselm took my hand in both of his, and held it, and said the thing I would carry the rest of the way down that river and past it:

"The narrows at Kestrel Bend are two hours downstream. Go through them early, before the light is full, and hug the eastern channel—the western one has a rock that sits just under the surface when the water is high, and the Halvorsen men know it." He paused. "And Marisol Vane. Your father waited a long time for you to come down this river. Whatever you find at the end of it—don't make his waiting have been for nothing. But also—" and here the old man's eyes crinkled, and for the first time he fully smiled, "—don't let the dead man's regrets steer your boat. You are alive. Steer."

We pushed off into the mist.

The current took us fast, the patched hull hissing through brown water, the village dissolving behind us into fog and memory. Petra handled the tiller; I sat forward with the map open on my knees and the letter's words still burning in my chest like swallowed coal. Two hours to Kestrel Bend. Two hours to a gorge where boats had accidents and men in clean boots made examples.

I checked the oilcloth bundle. Still there. Still dry.

Then I looked up, at the mist ahead, where the river narrowed and the walls began to rise—

—and heard, faint but unmistakable, carried against the current from somewhere downstream: the sound of an aluminum boat, idling, waiting.
