## 14. The Letter

The elder's name was Ibu Sari, and she held the letter the way you hold something that has been waiting a long time to be put down.

"Your father," she said. "He stayed with us. One season, maybe two. He fixed our generator when no one else would come upriver." Her hands were brown and knuckled like tree roots, and they trembled slightly as she passed the envelope across the lamplight. "He said: if ever a woman comes asking after Elias Vane, and she has his stubbornness in her face—give her this."

Petra didn't take it. Not at first.

The letter sat on the woven mat between them, wrapped in a rectangle of oiled silk gone stiff with age, tied with fishing line that had been knotted and reknotted so many times it looked like a small gray scar. The envelope beneath was soft with damp and years, its corners rounded like river stones. Her father's handwriting crawled across the front in that looping, impatient scrawl she had spent thirty years trying to forget: *For P.S., if she comes.*

P.S. Petra Sterling. He'd written her initials before he wrote anything else. Before Elias Vane's name. Before his own instructions. As if, wherever he'd been when he sealed this envelope, whatever he'd been running from or toward, the first thing on his mind had been a girl of nine years old standing in a hangar doorway watching the rain.

"He never came back for it," Ibu Sari said quietly. "We sent word downriver, year after year. The mail boat stopped coming after the flood of '94. Then the company men came, and we learned not to send anything." She paused. "You understand why we kept it hidden."

Petra understood. Halvorsen men had passed through this village three days ahead of them, asking questions, leaving threats. The elder hadn't handed over a dead man's letter to strangers with clean boots. She'd waited for someone with mud on theirs.

Marisol sat cross-legged near the door of the stilt house, sharpening her knife against a whetstone with slow, even strokes—a habit Petra had noticed, the sound of a woman keeping her hands busy so her mouth wouldn't say something she'd regret. The blade whispered against stone. Outside, the monsoon hammered the tin roof like a fist demanding entry, and the river beyond the village ran high and chocolate-brown, carrying whole trees past the pilings like matchsticks.

"Open it," Marisol said, without looking up. "Or I will."

* * *

Petra untied the fishing line with fingers that refused to be steady.

Inside the silk, besides the envelope, there was a photograph. She knew it before she turned it over—the way you know your own reflection in dark glass. A Beaver floatplane on a gravel bar, tail number half worn away but legible if you'd grown up tracing it with a crayon: C-FSTL. Sterling. Her mother had joked once that the registration was fate, that Tom Sterling had bribed somebody at Transport Canada to get those letters. In the photograph, four men stood in front of the plane, squinting into a sun that had bleached the sky white. Her father stood second from left, one hand resting on the propeller like it was a horse he was gentling. On his right, grinning, gaunt, hat pushed back off a forehead already lined by forty years of sun—Elias Vane, younger than Petra was now.

She had never seen this photograph. It had never hung on any wall, sat in any album. Which meant it had been taken here, upriver, in the country where her father had gone to die—or to disappear—and had never been developed anywhere she could find it.

Her hands were shaking now. She let them.

The envelope's flap came away with a dry tearing whisper. Inside: two pages, folded in thirds, soft as cloth from decades of being handled—Ibu Sari must have taken it out sometimes, over the years, read it again, wondered. And something heavier that slid into Petra's palm and made her breath stop.

A brass survey marker tag. Stamped: HALVORSEN CONCESSIONS LTD. — SURVEY STATION 7 — ELEV. 1,240 M — and beneath that, in smaller type, the date. August 1989. Three weeks before her father vanished.

She unfolded the letter.

*Petra—if you're reading this then either I'm dead or I'm hiding well enough that even Elias couldn't find me, and knowing Elias, that means I'm dead.*

Her vision blurred. She blinked it clear. Around her the room went distant—the lamp hissing, the rain, Marisol's knife going still against the stone.

*I'm writing this at Station 7 because I can't write it anywhere they might look, and I'm leaving it with people Halvorsen has never bothered to learn the names of, which is exactly the kind of blindness that's going to undo them. Listen carefully, sweetheart, because I don't know how much time I have to say it, and I've spent too long saying nothing at all.*

*The survey is fake. All of it. Every elevation, every boundary line, every soil assay in the eastern corridor. I found it because I'm the one who flies the lines and my numbers never matched the ones in the final reports—off by meters, then by hundreds, always in the direction that moved the concession boundaries onto land that isn't ours to move them onto. River land. Village land. Land that belongs to people who signed no paper and were asked to sign none, because the paper was drawn before anyone told them a map existed.*

*Halvorsen isn't buying concessions. They're manufacturing them. They falsify the surveys, file the claims, and when the permits convert to title, the indigenous holdings inside those lines simply cease to exist on paper. No compensation. No consultation. Nothing. Just a line on a map that says this was always empty, and a government office three thousand kilometers away that has no reason to doubt a survey flown by a licensed crew.*

*I confronted Rennick about it. He smiled at me the way a man smiles at weather. Then two days later our fuel cache burned and Elias found a man's boot prints where no man should have been, and we both understood what kind of company we were working for. Elias wanted to go to the press. I told him nobody reads a newspaper story about a line on a map. So we did it the other way. We documented everything. Original field journals. My flight logs with the true coordinates. Photographs of every station marker. And an affidavit—notarized in Port Amara, witnessed by a magistrate who owed Elias his life—signed by both of us, stating plainly what was done and how. If that affidavit ever reaches the land registry attached to a counter-claim, the permits don't just lapse. They die. And everything Halvorsen stole reverts to the people it was taken from.*

*That's the cache, Petra. That's what Elias has spent his life sitting on. Not gold. He could have sold it a hundred times—he told me men offered him more money than either of us would see in ten lifetimes—but he said a thing like that isn't worth anything until it's worth everything, and you only get one chance to spend it right.*

*I'm writing this because I'm going back out tomorrow to recover the last of the journals from the support plane. We hid copies in the wreck because it was the one place Halvorsen would never search—they wrote that plane off as lost with all hands, and a company that lies for a living believes its own lies best of all. But I think they've started wondering. I think someone followed us to Station 7 today. If I don't come back, don't come looking. Promise me. Stay small, stay invisible, stay—*

The ink smeared there, a thumbprint or a raindrop, decades old, blurring the last words of the sentence into a gray ghost. Petra stared at it. *Stay invisible.* He'd written it the way you write a prayer you don't believe in. And she had obeyed it. God help her, she had built her entire life inside that unfinished sentence—small, invisible, three payments behind on a plane she loved, flying medicine runs under a name nobody remembered because nobody needed to. She had thought she was hiding from debt. She had been hiding from a dead man's instruction, following orders from a ghost who had never come home to give them.

There was more. The second page was shorter, the handwriting faster, less careful, as if written by lamplight with someone listening in the dark.

*Elias has a daughter. He never talks about her and I don't ask, but a man who guards a secret that hard is guarding a wound. If you ever meet her, be kinder to her than life has been. And Petra—one more thing. Whatever happens, whatever they tell you: I did not crash. I want that written down somewhere besides this letter. I flew that route forty times blindfolded. If my plane comes apart over the eastern corridor, it came apart because someone made it come apart. Don't let them turn me into an accident. Accidents are how companies say sorry without saying anything at all.*

*You are the best thing I ever did. Fly high, little wing.*

*Dad*

* * *

The lamp hissed. The rain kept up its siege. Somewhere outside a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

Petra became aware, gradually, that Marisol had crossed the room and was crouched beside her, reading over her shoulder—reading upside down, fast, the way mechanics read manuals, and when she reached the end she sat back on her heels and said nothing for a long moment.

"Station 7," Marisol said finally. Her voice was rough. "That's the marker tag. That's where he wrote it." She picked up the brass tag from Petra's palm, turned it in the lamplight. "And the support plane—the wreck we found. The one with fresh boot prints." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious at once, which Petra was learning was simply Marisol's face when the world confirmed something she'd always suspected. "Someone stripped it recently. Someone's been looking for these journals for years, and they're close. That's why the enforcers want the map. Not for gold. For the affidavit."

"It was never treasure," Petra said. Her own voice sounded far away, like radio traffic from another aircraft. "It's testimony. The whole time. Elias wasn't guarding a claim—he was guarding evidence. And he gave it to me. To us." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I thought he was paying me double for silence. He was paying me to finish what my father started."

Marisol set the tag down gently, which for Marisol was almost a sacrament. "My father watched you grow up," she said. "In mission letters. He wrote about 'the pilot's girl' in the margins—I read them, that night at the lake, while you were sleeping. He knew your name before you knew his." She hesitated, and Petra saw her decide to say the rest. "He wasn't delivering you a map, Petra. He was introducing you to your father. The only way he had left."

Petra looked down at the letter. *Fly high, little wing.* Thirty years of grief rearranged itself around those four words, not dissolving—that wasn't how grief worked—but settling, the way sediment settles in floodwater, until you could suddenly see the bottom. Her father hadn't abandoned her. He'd been silenced. And the men who silenced him were the same men whose floatplane had shadowed them across a canyon, whose boot prints were fresh on the banks of a wrecked plane, whose lawyer was at this moment counting down the days until a permit lapsed and erased the last living trace of what Tom Sterling had died trying to expose.

They weren't racing for money anymore. They never had been.

"We have four days," Petra said. "Maybe five, if the storm breaks. Port Amara is six days by road." She folded the letter along its old creases, careful as handling a live flare, and tucked it inside her jacket, against her chest, where she could feel it. "The counter-claim needs the journals, the affidavit, and a filing clerk awake at a desk. We have all of it except the time."

Marisol was already moving, pulling their soaked pack toward the lamp, inventorying with quick brutal efficiency. "Then we leave at first light. Downstream. The longboat holds if I re-patch the seam tonight."

"The Narrows," Petra said quietly. "If Halvorsen knows the map exists, they know the river is the only way out. They'll be waiting."

Marisol paused, knife halfway into the pack strap, and gave her a look that was almost a smile—the fierce, exhausted grin of a woman who had spent a week learning that fear and forward motion could share the same boat. "Then we'd better be faster than they are mean."

Outside, the rain eased for the first time in two days, and through the gap in the stilt-house shutters Petra could see the river running high and fast toward the confluence, brown and patient, carrying everything downstream whether it was ready or not.

Somewhere below the gorge, in the dark, engines were idling.
