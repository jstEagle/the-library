## 15. Ambush at the Narrows

The gorge announced itself before we saw it—a low, grinding hum that came up through the hull of the longboat and settled into my back teeth. Petra stood at the bow with the survey sketch flattened against her thigh, the paper gone soft as cloth from six days of river spray, and I watched her read the water the way other people read letters from home.

"Narrows," she said. "Half a mile of it, maybe less. The old crew marked it with a skull."

"A skull."

"Surveyors have a sense of humor." She didn't smile. "Single channel through the rock. If the map's honest, there's an eddy on the left side about two-thirds down where a boat can pull out."

"And if the map's lying?"

"Then we find out what kind of swimmers we are."

I checked the bail bucket between my feet out of habit more than hope. The patch I'd sewn into the split hull three days ago was holding—holding being a generous word for a rectangle of tarpaulin, pine pitch, and stubbornness that seeped a thin silver thread of river around its edges no matter how many times I re-caulked it. The boat was tired. The river was not.

Above us the walls closed in, black basalt streaked white with bird lime, and the sky shrank to a ragged ribbon of gray. The monsoon had paused sometime before dawn—not ended, just caught its breath—and the air hung so thick with moisture it felt like breathing through a wet towel. Every sound arrived doubled: the river's growl, then its echo off the stone.

Petra shipped the paddle and looked back at me. Rain ran off the brim of her cap in a steady trickle. "I want you on the stern line," she said. "If we take water, you cut loose the supply bundle first. Don't argue with me about it. Supplies float, people don't."

"I know how drowning works, Petra."

"I know you do." Something crossed her face—apology, maybe, or the memory of the argument we'd had two nights ago about fathers and forgiveness, which she'd won by refusing to have it. "I'm not trying to teach you your trade. You keep engines alive; I'm asking you to keep us alive. Same job, different machinery."

That was as close to poetry as she got, so I took the stern line and coiled it around my fist, and we went into the Narrows.

* * *

For the first hundred yards it was almost beautiful. The channel threaded between walls close enough to touch if I'd been fool enough to lean out, and the water ran fast and green-black and smooth as poured glass over the top, all its violence hidden underneath. The boat hissed. My knuckles ached around the line. Somewhere above us a hawk screamed once and gave up, as if even it knew this wasn't country made for anything with wings.

Then the channel bent, and I saw the boat.

It sat in the eddy on the right side—the wrong side, the side without the exit—a long aluminum skiff with a canvas canopy and an outboard big enough to push a barge, and two men in it, and one of them raised a hand in a lazy wave as if we were neighbors meeting on a Sunday morning.

"Petra." My voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's. "Right side."

"I see them." She'd already turned, already pulling for the left, but the current had opinions now, and the Narrows was narrowing. The green water ahead broke into standing waves where the channel pinched between two house-sized blocks of fallen rock, and beyond that the river simply dropped away into noise.

The skiff's engine started. It didn't roar—it was too well-maintained for that—but it rose behind us with the patient certainty of something that had done this before.

"They'll want the journals," I said. "They can't afford to let us reach Port Amara with them."

"They also can't afford to run their own boat through that pinch at this water level." Petra's voice was pure pilot, clipped and calm, every word paid for in advance. "Nobody sane runs the Narrows loaded. So they'll try to stop us before it. Marisol—"

"I know." I was already hauling on the line, dragging our supply bundle toward the center of the boat, because I understood what she wasn't going to say: that we could outrun them or we could keep our gear dry, and the river was only going to let us pick one.

The first rifle shot cracked off the basalt walls and came back twice, so that it sounded like three men shooting instead of one bad shot.

"Down!" Petra threw her weight across the thwart and the second round slapped the water a foot off our port side, throwing up a fan of spray that hit my face cold as a slap. The skiff was coming now, no more Sunday-morning manners, its bow rising and falling in the chop, and I could see the shooter clearly—a heavy man in a yellow rain jacket, braced against the canopy strut, working the bolt with unhurried hands. A man doing a chore.

"We can't fight them and the river both," I shouted.

"We're not fighting anyone!" Petra hauled the paddle around and drove us at the pinch between the fallen rocks. "We're running! Pray the patch holds!"

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about rapids: they are not chaos. They are arithmetic. Every rock, every hole, every curling seam of white water is a number in an equation the river solved long ago, and the only question is whether your small wooden answer belongs anywhere in it.

We hit the pinch at full current, and for one suspended moment the whole world was the sound of the hull groaning against water that wanted it dead. Spray came over the bow in sheets. The patch held—I felt it hold, felt the boat bite and stagger and then slide through the gap with barely a hand's width to spare on either side—and then the river dropped us down the throat of the Narrows and everything became white noise and brown water and Petra screaming steering commands that the river mostly ignored.

The skiff did not follow. I heard its engine fall behind, throttled back, defeated by the same arithmetic. For about four seconds I thought we'd won.

Then the patch tore.

It didn't fail dramatically. There was no explosion of water, no dramatic lurch. It simply opened along the old split like a mouth remembering how to speak, and the river came in the way rivers do—everywhere at once, indifferent, total. One moment I was bailing with the bucket; the next the bucket was floating, and my knees were underwater, and the bundle of supplies was lifting off the thwart, and Petra was shouting something I couldn't hear over the roar.

"The line!" she was shouting. "Cut the bundle loose!"

I found the machete under my boot. The supply bundle—food, the tarp, the spare fuel, half our world—was wedged against the stern post, swelling with water, dragging the tail of the boat down. I swung the blade and the rope parted on the second stroke, and I watched our food and shelter and dry matches tumble away into the brown flood and vanish, and I felt nothing about it at all, which I would understand later was its own kind of shock.

What I felt instead was the boat go over.

It happened sideways, without malice. A standing wave took the gunwale, the flooded hull offered no resistance worth naming, and the Narrows simply reached up and took us the way it had taken the '89 expedition's support plane, the way it took everything eventually. Cold closed over my head. The river filled my ears with a sound like a train passing forever.

I have been underwater before. Everyone who works boats has. But this was different—this was violence with a current in it, tumbling me end over end, my boot catching on something, my lungs burning, the surface a rumor of light somewhere above and to the left and then nowhere at all. I remember thinking, with absurd clarity: *Dad drowned in water like this.* And then: *No. He walked out of water like this and never came back, which is worse.*

My shoulder hit rock. The pain was bright and clean and it saved me, because it told me which way was up—pain always points somewhere—and I clawed toward it and broke the surface gasping, spitting brown water, and the gorge walls spun around me.

The boat was gone. Petra was thirty feet downstream, clinging to a half-submerged boulder, one arm hooked over it, her other arm reaching toward me, and her face was the worst thing I saw that day—not afraid, but *concentrated*, the face of a woman doing math she refused to lose.

"Marisol! Feet up! Feet first—don't fight it—"

I got my feet up. The river took me past her close enough to grab, and I grabbed, and for a moment the two of us were one dumbbell of shared weight threatening to pull us both under the boulder, and then her boots found a ledge and she hauled, swearing words I didn't know she owned, and somehow, impossibly, we were both on the rock, folded together like dropped laundry, shaking.

The Narrows roared on without us. Downstream, maybe two hundred yards, the gorge opened and the river spread wide and slow again, and our longboat—what was left of it—bobbed upside down against the far bank like a dead animal.

* * *

We got off the boulder eventually, working our way along the waterline to a gravel shelf where the current slackened, and sat there dripping while the monsoon remembered itself and began, softly, to rain.

I counted what we had. It didn't take long. The clothes we wore. Petra's knife, which had been in her pocket. The map—oilcloth-wrapped, tied inside her shirt against her chest, because she'd been sleeping with it there since the cache, and when she pulled it out and unwrapped it, dry, intact, Elias Vane's careful ink still sharp, I laughed. It came out of me like a hiccup, half sob, and Petra looked at me like I'd lost my mind.

"It's dry," I said. "Everything else is gone and the stupid map is dry."

"That's because I'm not stupid," she said, and then her voice cracked on the last word, and I understood she was laughing too, or crying, or both, the way people do when the adrenaline drains out and leaves the fear sitting there in plain sight.

We inventoried ourselves like strangers. Her left forearm was gashed where the rock had taken skin in a long ribbon; I cleaned it with river water, which was a bad idea medically and the only idea available, and bound it with a strip of my shirt. My shoulder was already purpling. We had no food. No fire. No shelter but the gorge wall's overhang fifty yards up, and beneath it a floor of dry sand that might as well have been a feather bed, we were so far past caring.

It was evening when I finally said it.

"We lost the journals' duplicates. The photographs. Everything in the bundle."

"No." Petra was staring downstream, at the wide slow water, at the place where the river kept our supplies or didn't. "The affidavit's still here." She touched her chest, where the oilcloth lay. "Elias's original signature. That's the thing that matters. The rest was comfort."

"Comfort would've been nice."

"Yeah." She was quiet a while. The rain ticked on the stones. "Marisol. When you went under. I thought—" She stopped. Started again, in the flat pilot's voice, buying each word in advance. "I've flown alone for eleven years because losing people is a debt I can't pay. Today the river tried to collect. And I hauled you out anyway. I need you to know that was a choice, not a reflex."

I looked at her—soaked, bleeding, wrapped around a sixty-year-old map like it was a child—and something in my chest that had been clenched since Port Amara finally let go a little.

"Noted," I said. "Same goes for me. In case it wasn't obvious."

A sound downstream. Both of us froze.

An engine—low, patient, working upstream against the current. Around the bend below us came a long aluminum skiff with a canvas canopy, riding high and empty-looking, its spotlight sweeping the banks in slow arcs.

Halvorsen's boat. Running the Narrows after all—or portaging around it, which meant they'd landed, which meant men on foot, somewhere in these woods, between us and Port Amara.

The spotlight swept past our overhang, hesitated—

—and moved on, fooled by shadow and rain.

Petra's hand found mine in the dark. Her grip said what neither of us could say aloud: *four days, no boat, no food, and they're between us and the land office now.*

Upstream, deep in the gorge, thunder answered the engine, and the river began, very quietly, to rise.
