## 16. What We Carry

The rock overhang was barely a roof at all—just a shelf of grey limestone jutting from the hillside like the brim of a tired hat—but after six hours in the open rain it might as well have been a cathedral. Petra crawled under it first, dragging the dry bag by its strap, and then turned and hauled Marisol in by the wrist, both of them collapsing onto stone that was, miraculously, only damp instead of drowning.

The river had taken almost everything. The longboat was gone—torn loose in the narrows, swallowed whole somewhere below the white water. The food tin was gone. The tarp was gone. What remained was one dry bag with a soaked-through seam, a box of waterproof matches down to its last eleven sticks, a battered aluminum pot, half a roll of electrical tape, Marisol's knife, and the map.

The map. Oilcloth-wrapped, tied with waxed cord, dry as a sermon. Elias Vane's last testament, riding out the flood in its wrapping like a man in a lifeboat while everything around him went under.

Petra sat with her back against the cold stone and listened to the rain hammer the forest. It came down in sheets, in walls, in a sound so total it erased thought. Somewhere upslope, water found a channel and roared through it. The world had been reduced to weather.

"We need fire," Marisol said. Her teeth were chattering so hard the words came out in pieces. "Hypothermia doesn't care about our schedule."

"I know."

"Four days, Petra."

"I said I know." Petra struck a match against the box. It flared, died. She cupped her hands around the second one and fed it slivers of birch bark she'd stripped from a deadfall on the way up, praying the inner layers had stayed dry against her chest. They hadn't, quite. But the third match caught, and she nursed the flame like something newborn, coaxing it with pencil-thin twigs until it stood on its own, and then they had a fire the size of two cupped hands, and it was everything.

They stripped to their underwear without discussion or embarrassment—modesty had drowned back at the narrows along with the boat—and hung their clothes on sticks near the flames, steam rising off the fabric like the ghosts of the last three days leaving their bodies. Petra wrapped herself in the emergency blanket from the bottom of the bag, silver side in, and passed a corner to Marisol. They sat shoulder to shoulder under a foil sheet, two drowned women watching a fire the size of a fist try to hold back a monsoon.

Marisol's lips had gone from blue toward pink again, which Petra took as progress. There was a scrape along her jaw where the rocks had gotten her, and her hands were torn up from the haul on the rope, but she was alive. Petra kept checking anyway, sideways glances every thirty seconds, as if the river might come back for her.

"You can stop doing that," Marisol said.

"Doing what."

"The headcount. I'm still here."

Petra looked at the fire. "Old habit. Counting heads. Making sure everyone you flew in gets flown out." She fed a twig to the flames. "Doesn't always work out that way."

* * *

They ate nothing. There was nothing to eat. Petra split the last of a chocolate bar she'd forgotten in her jacket pocket—a square each, the size of a postage stamp—and they let it melt on their tongues like communion.

It was Marisol who broke the silence, eventually, in the way people do when the body is warm enough and the mind has nowhere left to hide.

"I read them," she said. "The margin notes."

Petra turned. Rain light came off the wet stone in a pale wash, and Marisol's face was half shadow, half fire, the scrape on her jaw dark against her skin.

"On the map. The edges. You were flying, or bailing, or—" She made a vague gesture that encompassed rapids, ambushes, all of it. "There was time. I read them."

Petra felt something tighten behind her ribs. She'd read those margins too, of course—the coordinates, the route corrections, the surveyor's shorthand. But there had been other writing. Smaller. A different hand pressed hard into the oilcloth, private even in its permanence. She'd told herself she'd get to it later. Later had been easy to postpone while there were rapids to run.

"He wrote about you," Marisol said.

The fire popped. Down the slope, the river talked to itself in the dark, running high and brown and full of everything it had taken from them.

"There's a section—it's not coordinates, it's just... writing. Folded into the border where nobody would look unless they were looking." Marisol pulled her knees up under the blanket, arms wrapped around them, chin down. "He wrote about a daughter he left in Port Amara. Me. He wrote about how she'd be twenty-six now, and how she'd be right to hate him, and how he hoped she'd become someone who fixed things because he couldn't fix anything, least of all himself." Her voice stayed flat, controlled, the voice of a woman reading someone else's mail. "He got that part right. I fix engines. Boats. Things that are broken for understandable reasons."

"Marisol—"

"And then there's the other one." She finally looked over, and her eyes were wet, though whether from the river or this, Petra couldn't tell. "A girl at the mission outpost. The pilot's kid. He wrote about watching her grow up in the letters the missionaries sent—how she'd stand on the strip at six years old and watch the planes come in like they were church bells. How she soloed before she was legal. How her father flew supply runs out of that valley and never came home one day in '89, and how the girl kept flying anyway, because—" Marisol stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "Because what else do you do with a sky that took somebody."

Petra didn't say anything. There was nothing in her mouth. There was a hole in her chest shaped exactly like a runway at dawn, thirty years ago, and a small girl standing at the edge of it waiting for a sound that never came.

"He knew your father," Marisol said. "Elias did. They were on the same expedition. And when your dad went down, Elias was the one who—" Her breath hitched. "He wrote that he searched for nine days. That he found the second plane's last heading but never the plane. That he carried it. Thirty years, he carried it, and he couldn't bring himself to tell the little girl on the airstrip because what do you say? So he put it in the margins of a map instead, and he spent his whole life making sure the map would find its way to someone who could finish what your father started."

The rain filled the silence. Petra became aware that she was shaking—not cold, or not only cold—and that the emergency blanket was doing nothing for it.

"He watched me," she said finally. "From a distance. All those years."

"He wrote that you were the best pilot in the territory and that you'd never believe him if he told you."

A laugh came out of Petra, cracked and involuntary, closer to a sob than she wanted it to be. "I met him twice. Twice. Once when he bought fuel off me at the mission, once when he—when he paid me double to fly him out and didn't say why. He sat in my plane and he knew. He knew who I was, whose daughter I was, and he never said a word until we were at twelve thousand feet and he said 'Sterling' like it was a key turning in a lock."

"That sounds like him," Marisol said bitterly, and then the bitterness collapsed in on itself. "That sounds exactly like him. A man who could write forty pages of margin notes about love and couldn't manage one sentence out loud."

* * *

The fire needed feeding. Petra fed it, mechanically, hands moving while the rest of her drifted somewhere between 1989 and now. When she sat back down, Marisol shifted the blanket so it covered them both properly, and neither of them remarked on it.

"My mother told me he was dead," Marisol said. "For years. Then she told me he was alive and just gone, which was worse, somehow. Dead men don't choose to stay away." She turned her hands toward the fire, palms open, examining the rope burns across them like text. "I built a whole life out of not thinking about him. Engines are good for that. They only ask questions you can answer with tools." A pause. "And then a telegram shows up from some bush pilot I've never met, saying your father is dying and left you something, and the whole structure just—" She snapped her fingers; the sound was lost in the rain. "You know what the stupid thing is? I went. I hate him and I went. Because some part of me was always going to go. I think I hated him most for that—for building an exit into me that only opened toward him."

Petra watched the flames. "My dad's plane went down when I was seven," she said. The words came slowly, each one pried loose like a rusted bolt. "They never found it. My mom lasted four more years and then it was just me and whatever the mission would give me. Everyone said 'at least you have the sky,' like that was consolation and not the disease itself." She pulled the blanket tighter. "I've spent thirty years flying away from that valley. Every charter, every medicine run, every load of diesel—I picked routes that didn't cross it. Do you know how hard that is, in a territory this size? To spend a career avoiding one rectangle of map?"

"Why?"

"Because if I never look, it never happened." Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "If I never find the wreck, he's not dead. He's just late. Some fog-bound flight that'll turn up any season now." She shook her head. "It's insane. I'm a grown woman who navigates by dead reckoning and I can't navigate one true fact."

Marisol was quiet for a long moment. The firelight moved on her face.

"Elias wrote something else," she said. "At the very end of the margins. Last thing, ink different, older hand, shakier." She recited it slowly, the way you carry words you've read too many times: "'Two daughters. One I left, one I watched. Neither knows the other exists. If this map does its work, maybe they'll find each other, and maybe that's worth more than what's buried at the end of it.'"

The rain roared. Petra stared at her.

"He planned this," Marisol said. "Not the cache. Not the claim. Us. This. Two women under a rock in a storm, sharing a blanket, telling the truth for once in their lives. He mapped it like everything else—route, bearing, ETA." Her mouth twisted. "Manipulative old bastard."

"Yeah," Petra said, and heard her own voice break open on the word, and then she was crying, and Marisol was crying, and neither of them made any attempt at dignity. They cried the way exhausted people cry—ugly, gasping, graceless—shoulder to shoulder under silver foil while a fire the size of a fist held its ground against a monsoon. Petra cried for her father, late these thirty years. Marisol cried for hers, dead now three weeks and finally, finally located. They cried for the man who had failed them both in opposite directions and loved them both in the same one, from a distance, in the margins, in ink.

When it was done, they were wrung out and lighter, the way you feel after vomiting—hollowed, but clean.

* * *

Later, the fire banked low, clothes mostly dry, Marisol spoke again from the edge of sleep.

"The cache," she murmured. "Whatever's there. Journals, evidence, whatever Elias was guarding. It's not going to be gold, is it."

"No," Petra said. She'd known since the mission, maybe since the first margin note. "It's going to be worse than gold. Gold you can spend and be done with."

Marisol huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Truth. Nobody ever wants the truth. It doesn't keep, it doesn't shine, and it makes everybody angry."

"It also holds land," Petra said. "In a court. Where gold can't."

She reached over and untied the waxed cord, unrolled the map between them by firelight, and for the first time read the border notes straight through, all of them, top to bottom, letting Elias Vane speak in his cramped, stubborn hand. Route corrections. Cache bearings. And threaded through it all, the two threads he'd braided together at the end: the daughter he'd abandoned, and the daughter he'd adopted from afar, and the expedition that had cost one of them her father and given the other one his guilt.

Halfway down the eastern margin, a line she hadn't registered before:

*Cache is not the prize. Cache is the proof. The prize is the valley. File before the permits lapse. Halvorsen will be watching the registry, not the river. Go by road if the water wins.*

Petra traced it with her finger. *Go by road if the water wins.*

The water had won. The boat was at the bottom of the gorge, the food was in the river, and Port Amara was four days out by a road that might not exist anymore under this rain.

But Elias had drawn them a third option, decades ahead of his own death, the way he seemed to have drawn everything.

"Marisol," Petra said quietly. "Look at the eastern margin."

Marisol leaned in, hair still damp, firelight catching the scrape on her jaw. Her eyes moved down the cramped handwriting, and Petra watched her lips move as she read, watched her finger stop on the same line, watched understanding arrive in her face like weather.

"There's a logging spur," Marisol said slowly. "Marked here. Old survey access road, runs south from the ridge above us toward the highway junction. He marked it." She looked up. "He marked the way out before either of us was born."

Outside, the rain kept falling, patient and total. But for the first time since the narrows, Petra felt the clock in her chest tick forward instead of down.

Four days. One road. Everything to carry.

She folded the map along its old creases, tied the cord, and began repacking the bag by firelight.
