## 17. The Cache

**PETRA**

The map's final leg was a lie of scale.

Elias had drawn the last two miles as a casual squiggle along the base of a ridge, the kind of line a cartographer makes when he's tired and wants to be finished. In truth it was two miles of deadfall and devil's club, the kind of ground that fought you for every yard, and by the time the ridge's shadow stretched long across the valley floor, Petra had stopped counting the times she'd gone down to one knee in the tangle. Her palms were striped with scratches. Marisol walked ahead of her, machete in hand, swinging with a flat, mechanical patience that Petra had learned over the past days was how Marisol did everything she didn't want to be doing.

"There." Marisol stopped. "The twin cedars. That's what the margin says, isn't it? *Two sisters, leaning.*"

Petra checked the map against the world. Two cedars, grown together at the base until their trunks fused into a single fluted column, rising off a shelf of granite at the ridge's foot. Above them the rock climbed in gray steps, seamed with moss, and somewhere up in that gray was a cave mouth that Elias had drawn with a small, careful *x*—the only mark on the whole map that looked like it had been traced over, again and again, over years.

"He came back here," Petra said quietly. "Look at the ink. Different pens. He redrew this line a dozen times."

"Thirty-five years of coming back." Marisol sheathed the machete and stood with her hands on her hips, breathing hard, staring up at the rock as if it might answer for him. "My father spent thirty-five years walking to this hole in the ground, and he couldn't manage one visit to Port Amara."

"Maybe this was the visit," Petra said. "Maybe this was the only way he knew how to be a father. Guarding something."

Marisol didn't answer. She started up the granite.

The cave mouth was exactly where the *x* promised—a black seam behind a curtain of hanging roots, barely shoulder-width, breathing cold air. Petra clicked on her headlamp and the beam fell into a passage that sloped down and narrowed, the walls slick with condensation, and for a moment she hesitated, because the last time she'd followed a dying man's directions into the dark, she'd come out alone with a promise she didn't know how to keep.

"Petra." Marisol's voice behind her, low. "You first or me?"

"Me. Watch my light."

They went in sideways, then crouched, then crawled. The passage smelled of wet stone and old air, air that hadn't moved since before either of them was born, and then it opened—suddenly, like a held breath released—into a chamber the size of a chapel. Petra's lamp swept across it and stopped.

A dry-stone wall. Neatly mortared with mud, old and re-mudded, patched and re-patched. And set into it, a door: a slab of slate, and on the slate, in Elias Vane's careful surveyor's hand, a single line of characters.

**E.V. — 1989 — FOR M. AND THE PILOT'S GIRL**

Marisol made a sound that wasn't quite a word.

*The pilot's girl.* Petra read it twice. Her throat closed. She had spent five weeks telling herself she was a courier, nothing more, a paid pair of hands carrying another family's inheritance to another family's daughter. Elias had known otherwise. Elias had known from the first hour in her plane, coughing blood into a rag, watching her fly.

"He knew my father," she said. Her voice came out strange. "He knew my father, and he never said. Not until the end."

"Petra." Marisol touched her arm. "Open it."

The slate slab came away with a grinding scrape and a breath of air so dry and cold it stung. Behind it, a cavity lined with oilcloth and cedar bark, and inside—

Petra's heart, which had been climbing toward the word *gold* despite every promise she'd made herself, took a slow, falling step.

Not gold.

Ammunition boxes, army surplus, rust-bloomed at the corners. Dozens of them. And when Marisol pried the first lid up, the smell that rose out was paper. Old paper, ink, and the faint chemical ghost of film.

"Journals," Marisol said. Her voice had gone very quiet. "These are survey journals. Originals. Look at the dates—May '89, June, August—there are years of them. Petra, there are *years* of them."

Petra lifted one out. The cover was soft with handling, the spine broken from being read and re-read. The handwriting inside was Elias Vane's, but younger—sharper, more confident, the letters of a man who believed the world rewarded precision. She turned pages. Traverse notes. Bearing readings. Stream sediment samples. And then, on a page dated the fourth of July, 1989, a passage that made the cold of the cave settle into her bones:

*H. has altered the figures. I checked against my own readings twice. The assay values in the report filed with the land office are not what we found in the ground. He is inflating them to float the concession. The valley belongs to the river people and he knows it. He knows what he's doing and he's doing it anyway.*

Below it, in different ink, added years later: *I said nothing. God forgive me, I said nothing for thirty years, and then I stopped saying nothing.*

"Marisol." Petra couldn't lift her eyes from the page. "This is the 1989 concession report. The one Halvorsen's whole land claim is built on. And Elias is saying the numbers were forged."

"I can read." But Marisol's voice shook. She was holding a photograph, a stiff rectangle of black-and-white pulled from a waxed envelope. Petra took it when she offered it.

A survey crew, posed on a river gravel bar, summer of 1989. Six men and one woman, squinting into the sun. And third from the left, one hand resting on the float strut of a Beaver, grinning like the whole world was his—

Petra's knees went.

Her father. Younger than she had ever known him, twenty-six years old, alive in silver gelatin, laughing at something outside the frame. On the back, in pencil: *D. Sterling, pilot. Best stick in the territory. — E.V.*

"He was your father's friend," Marisol said softly. "Not just his employer's employee. Look—there's a letter in here addressed to him."

* * *

**MARISOL**

Marisol watched Petra read the letter, and it was like watching someone take a bullet slowly, over the course of a page.

She had spent her whole life constructing her father out of the materials available: a photograph she'd kept out of spite, a name on a birth certificate, a monthly money order that stopped when she turned eighteen with no note, nothing, as if she'd been a subscription that lapsed. She had built him cold and she had built him small, because a small cold man was easier to hate than a large absent one.

But the man who had written these journals was neither small nor simply cold. He was a coward—she could read that in the margins, in the entries where he wrote and crossed out and wrote again, circling his own guilt like a moth around glass. *I should go back. I should tell them. I should go to the land office. I should.* And then, always, some version of: *But if I do, they'll do to me what they did to Sterling.*

She sat down on the cold stone with the journals spread around her and read entry after entry, and the cave filled up with her father's voice—thirty-five years of it, a voice that had never once spoken to her and had spoken to these boxes constantly, nightly, the way a man talks to a confessor.

*The girl will be nine next month. I sent the money order. I don't know what to write in the letter. Everything I could say would either frighten her or make her hate me, and she'll do enough of that on her own when she's old enough to understand.*

Marisol put the journal down.

"Petra." Her own voice sounded like someone else's. "There's something else. Under the journals."

The last box held the thing Elias had built the whole vault around: a single document, typed on a manual machine, the ribbon fading, but signed—signed in ink, witnessed twice, dated and re-dated in notations along the margin as Elias had apparently re-executed it every few years to keep it fresh, like a man renewing a vow.

**AFFIDAVIT OF ELIAS M. VANE,** it began, **CONCERNING THE FALSIFICATION OF SURVEY DATA IN THE 1989 HALVORSEN CONCESSION APPLICATION.**

He named names. He named the survey chief. He named the numbers—original readings and filed readings, side by side, in a table he'd drawn himself. He named the meeting where he'd been told to keep his mouth shut, and he named the date his friend Daniel Sterling's plane had gone down on a flight Halvorsen's man had ordered him to make, in weather, over terrain that had never been surveyed—because Sterling had been carrying the true field notes, and Halvorsen had wanted them out of the sky.

Marisol heard Petra's breath change and looked up. The pilot was standing very still, the letter hanging from her hand, and her face had gone the color of the cave stone.

"They killed him," Petra said. "It wasn't weather. It wasn't my father's flying. It was—" She stopped. Started again. "He was carrying the evidence, and Halvorsen sent him into that storm to lose it. And everyone let it be weather, because weather is convenient."

"And Elias knew," Marisol said. "And he ran. He took the originals and he ran, and he spent the rest of his life—" She gestured at the chamber, the wall, the slate, the thirty-five years of re-mudded mortar. "He spent the rest of his life being a guard dog for a dead man's truth. And he never once came to see me."

The bitterness came up in her before she could stop it, hot and old, and she hated that it came up *here*, in this place, with this woman, when the affidavit was sitting in her hands like a loaded weapon. But it was true and it was hers: he had guarded everything except the one thing that had needed him.

"Marisol." Petra crouched in front of her. "Read this one. The last journal."

The final box. The final journal. The last entry dated three weeks ago, the handwriting gone thin and shaky, the letters of a dying man working against a deadline he could feel in his own chest.

*I am out of time. I have paid the pilot Sterling's daughter to carry this to my daughter, and I did not tell her why, because I am a coward to the last and I wanted her to say yes before she knew the price. If you are reading this, Marisol: I am sorry. There is no version of sorry that is large enough. I did not come to you because I could not look at you and see what my silence cost. I chose the boxes over you every year, because the boxes could not look back at me. It was not a choice. It was a failure. I leave you both everything I have, which is the truth, and I am ashamed it is not also money, and I am not ashamed that it is not money, and I do not know how to hold both of those at once, which is the story of my whole life.*

*The valley belongs to the river people. See that it goes back to them. See that Sterling's name is cleared. And Marisol—your mother named you for the sea she could see from her window. I never told you that. I should have told you that.*

Marisol read it twice. The second time, her vision blurred so badly she had to hold the page at arm's length, and she heard Petra turn away politely and busy herself with the ammunition boxes, giving her the small mercy of not being watched.

When she could speak, she said: "He re-executed the affidavit every few years. Kept it legally fresh. He was a coward for thirty years and then he spent the last five being the most careful man in the territory." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist. "That's the most him thing I've ever heard, and I never even met him."

Petra laughed—a wet, cracked sound—and then Marisol laughed too, and it broke something loose in the cave, and for a minute the two of them sat in the cold dark among a dead man's thirty-five years of guilt and stubbornness, laughing and crying in the same breath, which Marisol supposed was what grief actually was, when you finally let it have the whole of you.

* * *

**PETRA**

They loaded what they could carry.

That was the arithmetic of it, and Petra did the math with the cold clarity the air had given her: four journals, the affidavit, the photographs, the letter addressed to her father that Elias had apparently never had the heart to send and never the heart to burn. The rest—the bulk of thirty-five years—they wrapped back in oilcloth, sealed behind the slate, and re-mudded the wall with river clay from Marisol's kit, Marisol's hands doing the work with a craftsman's care, as if she were closing a coffin properly this time.

At the cave mouth, Petra stopped and looked back at the slate. *FOR M. AND THE PILOT'S GIRL.*

"He knew," she said. "Back at the strip, when he hired me. He knew who I was the whole time."

"Of course he knew. Sterling. You never thought that was a coincidence?" Marisol ducked out into the evening light. "He picked you. Out of every bush pilot in the territory drowning in debt, he picked the daughter of the man whose truth he was guarding. That wasn't a charter, Petra. That was him trying, at the very end, to do one thing right."

Petra stood in the cave's cold breath a moment longer. She thought about the map in her jacket, and the promise she'd made under a tarp in the rain, and how she had believed she was carrying a treasure map, and how she had spent five weeks half-ashamed of herself for hoping it was gold—gold that would pay the bank, save the Beaver, buy back the life she'd had before everything fell apart.

It was better than gold. It was worse than gold. It was the one cargo that couldn't be sold, only delivered.

Outside, the light was going strange. Not the clean gold of evening but a bruised, greenish yellow, and the air had gone thick and still, the whole valley holding its breath. Marisol stood at the ridge's edge looking north, where the sky had organized itself into a single vast anvil of cloud, black at the base, pulsing silently with buried lightning.

"Petra," she said. "How far to the road head?"

"Two days' walk, if the river crossing at Kettle Bend is passable. Three if it isn't."

Marisol pointed north at the storm. "And if that hits the headwaters tonight?"

Petra followed her finger and felt the old pilot's arithmetic begin to run, unbidden, the way it always did: rainfall, catchment, gradient, timing. The Kettle Bend crossing sat below forty miles of steep drainage. Forty miles of steep drainage about to receive the leading edge of a monsoon system that the mission radio had been warning about for two days.

"We need to move now," she said. "Tonight, in the dark if we have to. Because by tomorrow afternoon, that crossing isn't going to be a crossing. It's going to be a wall of water."

Marisol slung the waterproof bag with the affidavit high on her shoulder, the way you carry something that cannot get wet, cannot be dropped, cannot be lost—and Petra understood, watching her, that the girl had already stopped thinking of it as her father's legacy and started thinking of it as a live round.

They started down off the ridge as the first fat, warm drops came ticking through the cedars, and behind them, somewhere under thirty-five years of stone and clay, a dead man's testimony waited in the dark—while ahead of them, the river they had to cross was already beginning to rise.
