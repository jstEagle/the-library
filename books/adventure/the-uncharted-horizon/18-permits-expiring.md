## 18. Permits Expiring

The rain had followed them out of the cache site like a debt collector, patient and inevitable, and by the time we reached the logging road it had settled in for the season.

We stood at the edge of a clearing where the mud track began—a brown ribbon stitched through green country, running south toward Port Amara—and I watched Petra do arithmetic with her eyes. She was good at that. Bush pilots lived and died by arithmetic: fuel loads against headwinds, weight against runway length, daylight against distance. I'd watched her run those numbers a hundred times since the lake, quick and cold as a card counter. But this time the numbers were worse than any she'd flown through.

"Six days," she said. "If the road holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

She didn't answer right away. Above us the clouds had organized themselves into something with intent, a low gray ceiling pressing down on the ridgeline like a hand flattening dough. The air tasted metallic. Somewhere over the western ranges, thunder spoke in a language I didn't need translated.

"The storm breaks tonight or tomorrow," she said finally. "When it does, that road becomes a river. Culverts wash out. Trucks stop running. Everything between here and Port Amara turns into an island." She looked at me. "Three days if we beat it. Six if we don't."

"And the permits lapse in four."

"Four." She said it the way you say a diagnosis.

I crouched down and pressed my palm flat against the oilcloth bundle strapped to my pack—the journals inside, Elias's careful block letters, the affidavit with its fading ink and its names. Halvorsen's name among them, written as an accusation. Four days of legal life left in a claim my father had spent thirty-five years keeping alive with his body, and here we stood at the wrong end of six days of road.

"We could fly," I said, and heard how thin it sounded even as I said it.

"On what?" Petra's laugh had no humor in it. "My plane's sitting on a lake three days behind us with a propeller I bent and no fuel. Even if it weren't—" she gestured at the sky, at the ceiling of gray pressing lower by the hour, "—I don't fly into that. Not for money. Not for permits. Not for anything. That's not cowardice, Marisol. That's just math."

"I wasn't calling you a coward."

"I know." She rubbed her face with both hands, and for a moment she looked younger than me, which was absurd—she was ten years my senior and had been flying since before I could drive—but grief and exhaustion strip everyone back to childhood. "I know what you were doing. You were looking for the version where this is easy. I look for it too. It doesn't exist."

* * *

We found the truck stop forty minutes' walk down the road—a gravel apron, a corrugated-roof cantina, two diesel pumps that hadn't worked since before I was born, and a scattering of trucks whose drivers waited out the weather the way river people wait: with coffee, cards, and commentary.

The cantina smelled of fry oil and wet dog. A radio behind the counter played something mournful and regional, and every man in the room glanced up when we came in, then away again, performing indifference badly. Two women, muddy to the knee, one carrying a rifle-bag that was obviously not a rifle-bag, arriving from the direction of the interior during monsoon season. We might as well have walked in wearing signs.

Petra ordered coffee for both of us and asked the question casually, leaning on the counter like she did this every week.

"Anyone heading south? Toward Amara?"

The man behind the counter—a heavyset fellow with forearms like dock rope—shook his head slowly. "Roads are closing. Company checkpoint at Kilometer Forty went to full stop this morning. Nobody southbound till the weather clears." He paused, wiping the counter with a rag that had given up long ago. "You got business in Amara?"

"Family," Petra said.

It was smooth. It was almost true. I felt something twist in my chest anyway, because family was exactly the business, and lying about it felt like lying about Elias twice.

The truck drivers drifted back to their cards. But one of them—an older man, gray stubble, hands stained the permanent black of someone who'd spent decades around diesel—didn't pick his hand back up. He watched us over his coffee for a long moment, then rose, unhurried, and came to stand beside us at the counter.

"You're the Vane girl," he said quietly.

I went still. Beside me, Petra's whole body changed—weight shifting to the balls of her feet, the way it did when she thought trouble was in the room.

"Easy," the man said, palms out. "Name's Ovidio Reyes. I ran supply boats on the upper river twenty years. Your father bought fuel off me more times than either of us can count." His eyes crinkled, and there was something in them—grief, maybe, or its long-settled cousin. "Word came upriver ahead of you. The village sent a runner when they heard you'd passed through. Old Tomás figured you'd be needing help getting south."

"The village," I said. My voice came out strange. "They've been—"

"People remember kindness, miss. Your father was kind to a lot of people who had nobody else being kind to them." He set his cup down. "Now. You're going to tell me why two women are trying to reach the land office in Amara four days before the Halvorsen concessions expire, and you're going to tell me fast, because I've got a truck loaded with fuel drums going south tomorrow at first light, and whether it stops at Kilometer Forty or goes around Kilometer Forty depends entirely on your answer."

Petra and I looked at each other. In three weeks I had learned to read her face well enough to know what was happening behind it: the calculation, the risk-weighting, the old instinct toward silence warring with the newer knowledge that silence was how Halvorsen won. Her father had been silent. Elias had been silent. Thirty-five years of men keeping secrets to protect people, and all it had bought was graves and expired claims.

I made the decision before she did. Maybe that was my inheritance after all—not the map, but the willingness to open things.

I unstrapped the oilcloth bundle, laid it on the counter, and unwrapped it under Ovidio Reyes's steady gaze. The journal fell open to a page of Elias's block capitals. The photograph beneath it showed a survey crew, 1989, standing in front of a Beaver floatplane, grinning like men who trusted their instruments. My father was third from the left. And at the edge of the frame, half-turned, laughing at something outside the picture, was a young woman in a flight jacket with STERLING stenciled above the pocket.

Ovidio looked at the photograph for a long time. Then at Petra. Then back at the photograph.

"Halvorsen," he said, "has been stealing this valley for forty years, and everyone on this road knows it and nobody can prove it." He closed the journal gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child. "Tomorrow, first light. My truck goes around Kilometer Forty by the old forestry spur. It adds half a day. It also avoids the checkpoint where Halvorsen keeps two men and a radio." He picked up his coffee. "Sleep here. There's bunks in back. And eat something—you both look like the wrong end of a hard season."

* * *

We couldn't sleep, so we planned instead, hunched over the journal by lamplight in the bunkroom while the storm arrived overhead like a freight train derailing in slow motion.

Rain hit the tin roof in sheets. Thunder rolled down the valley without pause, one long continuous growl, and the wooden walls flexed and groaned. Through the window I could see the road disappearing—not metaphorically; visibly, water sheeting across the gravel, the culvert at the far end already choked and backing up.

Petra spread her hand-drawn copy of the route across the bunk between us—we'd learned to never travel with only one copy of anything—and traced the forestry spur with one finger.

"Half a day extra puts us in Amara late on day five," she said. "Permits lapse end of day four." She tapped the paper. "Unless the registry stays open past hours, which it won't, or unless we find something faster on the last leg, which we won't." She sat back. "So we're short. By hours, maybe a full day. Short is short."

"Then we make the time somewhere else." I pulled the journal toward me. "Elias documented everything. Route surveys, dates, witness statements from the river villages—all of it signed, dated, witnessed. The claim doesn't just need filing, it needs to survive Halvorsen's lawyers contesting it. If we arrive with documentation so complete that contesting it means contesting thirty years of evidence—"

"They'll still try," Petra said. "Men like that don't fold because the paperwork is tidy."

"No. But the land office clerk will have to accept the filing, and once it's filed, it's public record, and public record has a way of becoming newspaper." I looked at her. "You said once that you stay invisible because visible people get shot down. But invisible people also die with their claims expiring. At some point the math changes."

Something moved across her face—recognition, maybe, or resistance to recognition. Outside, the storm hammered the roof, and lightning turned the window white, and in the flash I saw her clearly: a woman who had spent her whole adult life keeping her altitude low, now being asked to climb straight into weather.

"My father," she said quietly, "filed a complaint about Halvorsen in 1990. Did you know that? It's in the journal—Elias mentions it. A formal report of falsified survey data, submitted to the regional office." She laughed, short and bitter. "Halvorsen buried it. Three months later, Daniel Sterling's plane went off the charts." She met my eyes. "That's what visibility bought him."

"That's what being alone bought him," I said. "One pilot, one complaint, no witnesses, no press, no second name on the filing." I put my hand flat on the journal, on the page where Elias had recorded the affidavit's signing—five village elders, a mission priest, a river magistrate, all named, all dated. "This time there's an army of witnesses. This time there's two of us. And this time, Halvorsen isn't burying a complaint—they're contesting a claim filed in broad daylight with the fraud attached. Different animal entirely."

Petra was quiet for a long moment. The storm filled the silence, rain and thunder and the groan of timber.

"When we file," she said at last, "it goes in both names. Sterling and Vane. That was always the plan?"

"That was always the plan."

"Then let's make sure we live long enough to sign it." She rolled the map closed and looked at the window, at the drowning road. "First light. Ovidio's truck. Around the checkpoint." A pause. "And Marisol—if anyone asks what we're carrying, we're carrying family papers. Nothing else. Let them decide what that means."

* * *

First light came gray and drowned, the storm still working itself out over the western ranges but the immediate rain thinned to a steady soak. Ovidio's truck was an ancient flatbed with a canvas top and an engine that sounded like a man clearing his throat before making a bad decision. The fuel drums were lashed and tarped. Ovidio himself climbed into the cab, spat into the mud, and jerked his thumb at the bench seat.

"In," he said. "We lose nothing by starting early except our breakfast, and I've eaten worse mornings than this one."

We rode. The road was worse than I'd imagined—water running brown across the surface, potholes deep enough to hide small animals, the forest crowding in dripping and primeval on both sides. Ovidio drove with the calm of a man who had driven worse, humming along tunelessly with the radio, occasionally pointing out landmarks that no longer existed.

At midmorning we turned off onto the forestry spur, and the world narrowed to a single-lane track cut into a hillside, trees leaning over the cab, mud sucking at the tires. Twice we stopped while Ovidio got out to shift a fallen branch. Once we passed a washed-out section where the road simply ended in a raw brown gash, and he took the truck through the ditch beside it at a speed that rearranged my internal organs.

Petra rode with her eyes on the mirrors the entire time.

"He's watching for the floatplane," I murmured.

"He's watching for everything," Ovidio said, not taking his own eyes off the track. "Smart woman. Halvorsen knows their permit clock better than you do, miss. They've known for months that day four was coming. If they think a counter-claim exists, they'll be moving on every road, every river, every airstrip between here and Amara." He glanced at me sidelong. "Your father understood that too. Last time I saw him, 1991, he told me: 'Ovidio, the day I go public is the day I need to be faster than them.' " He shook his head slowly. "He wasn't faster."

The words hung in the cab like exhaust fumes. Petra stared straight ahead. I put my hand in my pocket and touched the compass—no. The compass was gone, traded on the last leg of some other journey, some other lifetime. I touched the journal instead, wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, and felt its weight like a heartbeat.

We stopped at noon at a junction where the spur met a wider logging road, and Ovidio shared out bread and canned fish and coffee from a dented thermos. While we ate, a logging truck ground past southbound, and the driver leaned out and shouted something to Ovidio that I didn't catch—but I caught the way Ovidio's face changed.

"What?" Petra said. "What is it?"

Ovidio wiped his mouth slowly. "Checkpoint at Kilometer Forty isn't checking vehicles anymore," he said. "Man says there's a Halvorsen crew at the Amara end of this road. Land office district. Asking questions. Showing a photograph." He looked at us both, apologetic and grim. "Two women. One of them matches your description, miss. They're not stopping traffic." He folded the bread wrapper with great care. "They're waiting for you to arrive."

Petra set down her coffee. When she spoke, her voice was level and cold and completely certain, and I understood in that moment that whatever else she had been running from all these years, she had finally stopped running.

"They can wait at the front door," she said. "We'll come in through the side. Every registry office has a side door, and every clerk takes lunch, and every lawyer in the world assumes you'll use the front." She looked at me. "How's your forging hand, Marisol? Because I've got a fuel chit to make and you've got a father's signature to practice, and we've got about sixty kilometers to get both of them perfect."

The truck lurched back into gear. Ahead of us the road ran south through the drowned green country, and somewhere at the end of it, a clock was ticking down the last four days of a promise thirty-five years in the keeping.
