## 19. The Long Road

The logging road out of the river country was less a road than a scar—two ribbons of churned mud stitched through the green, running northwest toward Port Amara like a vein somebody had cut and never bothered to close.

We stood at its edge in the grey light of the fourth morning, four days left on the permits, six days of walking ahead of us if we trusted the map's arithmetic. Which we didn't. The map had been drawn by men who measured distance in optimism.

"We need wheels," Marisol said.

She looked worse than I felt, which was saying something. Her hair had dried into salt-stiff ropes after the Narrows, her forearm wore a rope burn she refused to acknowledge, and her only remaining shirt had been white once, in a former life. But her eyes were doing that thing they did—narrowing at a problem the way she narrowed at an engine that wouldn't turn over, as if it owed her money.

"Trucks come through here," I said. "Hauling timber down to the mill road. They slow for the culvert."

"You know this how?"

"I grew up on roads like this." I didn't elaborate. She didn't ask. We'd passed beyond asking somewhere back on the water, past the point where stories cost more than silence.

* * *

The first truck came at mid-morning, a grumbling mountain of mud-caked timber with a cab the color of rust and a driver who took one look at two women soaked to the soul standing in the middle of nowhere and kept his foot where it was. Marisol stepped out anyway, thumb out, smile crooked, and the truck slowed—not from kindness but from confusion, the way any machine slows when it encounters something it can't classify.

The window cranked down. A face like a fistful of weathered leather peered out.

"You girls lost?"

"We're going to Port Amara," Marisol said. "Land office business."

That word—*business*—did what charm couldn't. Logging country respected paperwork the way it respected weather: as an unavoidable force. He jerked his head at the bench seat. "Up front. Don't touch nothing."

His name was Dov. His hands were enormous and cracked like drought earth, and he drove the way men drive who have made peace with every bad thing a road can do to them. For twenty kilometers he said nothing except a running commentary on the state of the bridge washouts, delivered with the flat grief of a man describing dead friends.

It was me who broke the pattern, because I'd seen the sticker on his dash—a surveyor's association decal, faded, the kind you got free at trade shows twenty years ago.

"You ever work the interior surveys?" I asked. "Eighties, nineties?"

He glanced over. "Everybody worked the surveys. That's how this road got here. Somebody drew a line through the trees and said *timber*, and here we all are." A pause. "My brother ran chainsaw on one crew up the Serrano drainage. '89."

Marisol went very still beside me. I kept my voice level. "What company?"

"Halvorsen, back then. Before they got respectable." He spat out the window without slowing. "Crew came back short-handed that year. Always wondered about that."

So did I. More than he would ever know.

* * *

Dov dropped us at a junction where three roads met and a hand-painted sign pointed toward Port Amara with an arrow someone had corrected twice. From there it was another truck, then the back of a produce lorry riding on sacks of onions until my eyes streamed, then a stretch of walking along a rail spur while the sky above us built towers of cloud that had nothing to do with monsoon and everything to do with altitude—the storm system that had chased us off the lake was swinging around behind us like a dog that had caught the scent.

We made forty kilometers that day. It wasn't enough.

The checkpoint appeared at dusk on the second day, exactly where a checkpoint should be: a narrow place where the road pinched between a rock cut and a drop-off, a sawhorse barrier, a kerosene lantern, and two men in rain jackets that were too clean for the country. Halvorsen colors. I knew them now the way you learn a predator's silhouette—not by study but by the animal part of you going cold.

They were checking papers. Fuel chits, transit passes, anything that proved you belonged on a corporate haul road during a permit dispute. Word traveled fast in these hills; Halvorsen knew two women were moving toward the land office, and they'd thrown a net across every artery leading there.

We watched from the treeline, fifty meters up-slope, rain starting to speckle the leaves around us.

"They'll wave trucks through," Marisol whispered. "Just not us."

"So we don't walk through. We ride through—as cargo."

She turned to look at me. In the dim light her expression did something complicated. "You have a plan or you have a sentence fragment?"

"I have half a plan and your skill set."

"My skill set is engines."

"And paper," I said. "You told me. Your mother kept the books for the marina and taught you to write a fuel chit before she taught you to swim. You forged dock receipts for half the fishermen on the coast when their accounts ran dry."

"That was charity."

"This is survival. Same penmanship."

She stared at me a long moment. Then she laughed—one short, sharp bark, quickly strangled—and dug the stub of pencil and the folded sheets of blank chit paper out of the plastic bag she'd waterproofed against everything including God.

* * *

The truck we chose was a fuel tanker, which was either brilliant or suicidal and we didn't have time to determine which. Its driver stopped at the checkpoint, handed over his own papers, and while the guards bent over them under the lantern, Marisol slipped from the trees on the far side, moved along the tanker's shadow, and tucked our forged chit into the clipboard the driver had left on his seat—an addition to his manifest he wouldn't discover until we were long gone, listing two passengers as contracted labor, transit authorized, stamp and all.

The stamp was the beautiful part. She'd carved it herself, two nights ago, from a piece of cork and a broken knife tip, working by firelight while I pretended to sleep. HALVORSEN CONCESSIONS — TRANSIT AUTHORIZED. Crude, but crude reads true at lantern-distance in the rain.

The problem was getting *us* aboard unseen. The tanker's cab was occupied. The tank was sealed.

"The inspection hatch," Marisol breathed. "Top of the tank. If it's not full—"

"It's a fuel tanker. It's full."

"Then we ride the frame. Behind the rear axle housing. There's a catwalk on the good ones."

There was. Barely. When the truck lurched forward through the barrier, we were already flattened against the cold metal above the rear wheels, wedged between tank and frame rails in a space built for no human purpose, with the whole apparatus swaying beneath us like a ship in weather and diesel exhaust rolling over us in warm, choking tides.

I will not pretend it was dignified. I gripped a bracket and pressed my cheek to wet steel and thought about nothing, absolutely nothing, while the guards' lantern light swept past below us close enough to read the rivets. Marisol's boot found mine and stayed there—not a kick, just contact, an anchor. Neither of us spoke for eleven kilometers.

When the road finally widened and the checkpoint lights sank behind a hill, we dropped off at a culvert and rolled into the ditch like sacks of grain, laughing in the ugly, gasping way people laugh after fear, lying in the wet gravel looking up at a sky the color of a bruise.

"You carved a counterfeit stamp of the company trying to ruin us," I said, "out of a wine cork."

"Best crime I've ever committed." She held the cork up to the last light. "I'm keeping it. Framing it someday."

* * *

The storm broke that night.

We sheltered under a timber skid shelter with a roof of corrugated iron that turned the rain into artillery, and we listened to the whole sky come apart, and I did the math I'd been avoiding. Even if the roads held, even if we found rides, Port Amara was still a full day away and the land office closed its filing window at noon on the final day. Noon. Not midnight. Bureaucracy kept banker's hours even for the fate of valleys.

"We're going to miss it by hours," Marisol said quietly, across the fire we weren't allowed to have. "After everything. Hours."

"No," I said. "We're going to arrive with minutes. There's a difference, and the difference is a truck."

"In this?" She gestured at the deluge hammering the iron overhead. "Nobody drives in this."

"Somebody does. Somebody always does. The question is what they want badly enough to stop."

I reached into my jacket, into the inner pocket where I'd carried it since the mission shack, since before Elias Vane put a map in my hands and rewrote the terms of my life. My father's compass. Brass case gone soft-edged with handling, the needle housing clouded, the lid engraved with initials so worn you needed faith to read them: R.S. Roland Sterling. The one object I'd kept from him. The one object I'd carried through every move, every repo notice, every year of staying small and invisible—because as long as I had it, some part of me could pretend I was still flying his routes, still headed somewhere he'd meant to go.

I set it on the crate between us.

Marisol looked at it, then at me, and I watched her understand—not the object, but the act. What it costs to put the last of a thing on a table.

"Petra—"

"It's just brass," I said, and my voice betrayed me on the second word, so I started over. "It's just brass and glass. It doesn't point anywhere anymore. North hasn't been the answer for a long time." I closed my hand around it. It was warm from my body, the way it had been warm from his, presumably, and his father's before that, warmth handed down a line of people who kept moving. "Tomorrow we find a trucker crazy enough to run the flooded section. They'll want payment. Money we don't have. This buys the last leg."

"To Port Amara."

"To the land office. To both our names on a claim." I met her eyes. "Your father spent thirty years carrying the truth in an oilcloth roll. I've spent thirty years carrying a compass that points at a ghost. One of those things was worth the weight. Tomorrow I find out if I'm brave enough to mean that."

Marisol reached across and put her hand over mine, over the compass, and for a moment neither of us let go.

"He wrote about you," she said. "In the margins. I told you that. He called you 'the pilot girl who watches the horizon like it owes her an apology.'" A crooked almost-smile. "Maybe it does. Maybe tomorrow it pays up."

* * *

Morning came grey and dripping, the storm moved off eastward leaving the world rinsed and shining and the road a brown river of its own. We walked the flooded section until we heard it: an engine in low gear, grinding, patient—the sound of a vehicle being driven by someone who respected the water and feared nothing else.

It was a flatbed hauling a bulldozer, of all things, driven by a woman named Okonkwo who ran equipment contracts up the valley and who took one look at the flood crossing, one look at us, and said, "Fifty each or keep walking," with the serenity of someone quoting scripture.

"A hundred and fifty total," I said, "and we help winch you across if you bog down."

She almost smiled. "Cash?"

"I have something better than cash." I brought out the compass and held it up so the light caught the worn engraving. "Brass. Pre-war. Works fine. Worth ten times what you're asking to anyone who knows what it is."

Okonkwo looked at it for a long time. Then at me. Truckers and pilots speak a common language, and part of that language is knowing when a person is selling something that isn't really for sale.

"That's yours," she said. "I can see it's yours."

"It was my father's. He's been gone thirty years." I put it in her palm and closed her fingers over it, and the weight of it leaving my hand was physical, a small amputation, thirty years of pretending lifted out of me all at once. "Take me to Port Amara and it takes you somewhere better than north."

She drove like the road was a personal enemy and she'd won every previous engagement. The dozer chained down behind us, the floodwater climbing the wheel hubs, Marisol's hand white-knuckled on the dash and mine steady on my knee, empty for the first time in memory and lighter than I knew how to be.

An hour out of the city, cresting the last ridge, Port Amara spread below us in the morning haze—tin roofs, harbor cranes, and there, on the civic square, the land office with its flag and its stone steps, small as a stamp, waiting.

Okonkwo checked her watch. "Nine-forty. You've got time."

"How much?" Marisol asked.

"Office closes at noon for filings." Okonkwo shifted gears for the descent. "But I heard at the depot this morning—Halvorsen's lawyer chartered a car at dawn. He's coming to file today too."

The road unspooled downhill, straight and fast, and I leaned forward against the dash like that could add speed, watching the city grow, watching the clock in my chest run backward toward noon.

Two hours. One lawyer. Everything Elias Vane had died carrying, and everything my father had vanished protecting, riding in a canvas satchel between two women in ruined clothes in the cab of a borrowed bulldozer truck.

"Hold together," I whispered—to the journals, to the affidavit, to whatever was left of both our fathers riding shotgun. "Hold together for one more hour."
