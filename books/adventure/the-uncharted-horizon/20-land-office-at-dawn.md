## 20. Land Office at Dawn

**Petra**

The logging truck dropped them at the edge of Port Amara at four in the morning, in a rain that had given up on being weather and settled into being a condition. Petra stood on the shoulder of the coast road with her pack straps cutting into her shoulders and the oilcloth bundle pressed flat against her chest like a second heartbeat, and she watched the town's lights smear themselves across the flooded street.

Six days by road, Marisol had said back at the cache site. They'd done it in four.

"Trucker said the registry opens at eight," Marisol said, wringing out the hem of her jacket. She'd been wringing things out for two days straight—her hair, her socks, her patience—and it had become a kind of tic, a small war against the wet. "Closes at noon today because of the holiday tomorrow. That gives us eight hours."

"We only need five minutes."

"We needed five minutes three days ago too." Marisol looked down the street toward the harbor, where the masts of fishing boats swayed against a sky the color of wet slate. "You see that floatplane?"

Petra saw it. Moored at the government dock, riding low and arrogant on its pontoons, its white fuselage striped with the blue chevron of Halvorsen Concessions. Her stomach performed a slow, cold revolution.

"They beat us here," she said.

"They beat us *here*." Marisol pointed up the hill, to where a stone building with brass letters over its door caught the first gray light: LAND REGISTRY AND CONCESSIONS OFFICE. "Different question is whether they beat us *there*. Office doesn't open for four hours."

"So we stand in line."

"So we stand in line." Marisol shouldered her own pack—the one holding the field journals wrapped in plastic sheeting scavenged from the river village—and started walking. "In the rain. In front of the door. Like people who have nothing better to do than be first."

"That's exactly what we are."

"No," Marisol said, and there was something in her voice that Petra hadn't heard before—not the guarded dryness of the mechanic, not the grief of the daughter, but something harder and cleaner underneath both. "That's what we've been. What we are now is people with evidence. There's a difference. Evidence makes you patient."

They took up position under the registry's shallow portico, out of the worst of the rain, and Petra discovered that standing still after nine days of motion was its own kind of ordeal. Her body wanted to keep moving, keep bailing, keep paddling, keep running. Stillness let everything catch up—the bruise along her ribs from the rapids, the raw places on her palms, and beneath all of it, the low continuous hum of fear that had been her co-pilot since the day Elias Vane had coughed blood into his handkerchief in her passenger seat.

At six-fifteen, a black sedan pulled up across the street.

A man got out. He was maybe fifty, silver at the temples, wearing a rain coat so new it still had creases from the store fold, and he carried a leather document case the way a priest carries scripture—with the confidence of someone whose institution does the believing for him. He stood under an awning across the street, checked his watch twice, and did not look at them once, which was itself a kind of looking.

"Halvorsen's lawyer," Marisol murmured.

"You can't know that."

"I know it the way you know weather." She didn't take her eyes off him. "Clean boots, clean case, clean conscience he rented by the hour. That's their man."

The lawyer made a phone call from under his awning, spoke briefly, and then—this was the part Petra would remember afterward, the detail that would come back to her in quiet moments for years—he crossed the street, climbed the portico steps, and stopped three feet away from them as though they were furniture.

"Ladies." His voice was pleasant, unhurried, the voice of a man reading terms aloud. "You're waiting for the office?"

"We're waiting for the office," Petra said.

"It opens at eight. You're aware the counter-claim window closes at noon?" He smiled at some private joke. "Concession law is unforgiving about deadlines. So many people arrive with dramatic stories and incomplete documentation. The registrar sees it every cycle. Tears, photographs, affidavits of dubious provenance." He adjusted the clasp on his case. "I'd hate for you to waste a morning."

"We have documentation," Marisol said evenly.

"Mm." He glanced at the oilcloth bundle against Petra's chest, and for half a second the pleasantness thinned and Petra saw the thing underneath it, the actual face of the operation: appraisal. Weighing. Deciding what she was worth and what she'd cost to remove. "Field journals, is it? The Vane material?"

Petra's heart slammed once, hard, and then went very quiet. Nine days of rivers and checkpoints had taught her something about fear: it was loudest right before you decided what to do with it.

"If you know what we have," she said, "then you know why your client sent men with guns after two women in a leaking boat. And if you know *that*, Mr.—"

"Cutter."

"—Mr. Cutter, then you know this isn't going to end at a filing counter. It ends in an inquiry room, with your client's survey data under subpoena. You might want to think about which side of that table you'd rather be sitting on."

For a moment the rain filled the silence, drumming on the portico roof, running off the steps in bright ropes. Cutter's expression didn't change, but something behind it recalculated—a ledger closing, another opening.

"The offer stands until noon," he said. "Halvorsen pays generously for the return of stolen company property. More generously than a bush pilot earns in a decade. Whatever debt brought you to this—" his eyes flicked, briefly, insultingly, over her soaked clothes, "—circumstance, it could be resolved by lunch."

Then he walked back across the street, and Petra realized her hands were shaking, and that Marisol had taken one step forward without seeming to move at all, planting herself between Petra and the lawyer like a hull taking a wave.

"He offered us money," Marisol said when the sedan door closed.

"He offered us a price. Different thing." Petra exhaled slowly. "He thinks everyone has one."

"Do you?"

Petra thought about the compass she'd traded to the trucker two nights ago—her father's compass, the last relic, gone into a stranger's glovebox somewhere on the logging road. She'd thought that was the last thing she had left to sell. But no. There was always one more thing, right up until the moment you refused.

"Yeah," she said. "I do. It's just not for sale anymore."

* * *

**Marisol**

By seven-thirty there were eleven people in the line behind them, and Marisol had learned all their grievances by heart, because people waiting in the rain tell you everything.

There was a fisherman named Okonkwo whose family had worked the same estuary for three generations and whose permit had been swallowed by a Halvorsen subsidiary through a clerical error nobody would correct. There was a widow renewing a mineral claim her late husband had staked in '91. There were two young surveyors with a tripod bag and the nervous energy of men about to be told no.

And there was the river elder's granddaughter, a girl of perhaps nineteen named Adaeze, whom Marisol had last seen serving them cassava porridge in a village that existed on no map except Elias Vane's. She had traveled overnight by bus with a folder of petitions signed by forty families of the valley—families whose land Halvorsen's falsified surveys had folded into a concession block that existed mostly on paper, in offices far from any river.

"You came," Marisol said.

"My grandfather said to tell you: the river remembers who carried it." Adaeze held up the folder, water-spotted but intact. "Forty signatures. Thumbprints where they couldn't write. He said if the papers were true, the names would hold them up."

Marisol looked at the thumbprints—dozens of them, inked in faded violet, each one a person she'd never meet—and felt something shift in her chest that she didn't have a name for yet. Her whole life she had measured her father in absences: the empty chair, the unmailed birthday cards, the smell of engine grease on a stranger's jacket that made her flinch into her twenties. She had come on this journey telling herself it was about closure, and closure, she'd understood even then, was a word people used when they wanted an ending more than they wanted the truth.

But this wasn't closure. This was forty families who had never met Elias Vane, holding up his life's work with their inked thumbs.

The lock clicked at 7:58. A clerk inside opened the door, took in the crowd, and sighed the sigh of a man whose morning had just tripled.

"One at a time," he said. "Form 12-C for claims, 14-B for renewals, 9 for complaints, and no, I don't know when the coffee machine will be fixed."

Cutter moved first. Of course he did—he'd been waiting across the street all along, and he swept past the line with a nod to the clerk that suggested prior acquaintance, prior comfort, prior arrangement. Marisol watched him set his leather case on the counter and begin laying out documents with the crisp economy of a card sharp dealing a winning hand.

"Counter," she breathed to Petra. "We go to the counter now. Not the line. The line is for people asking permission."

Petra understood instantly. They broke from the queue—Okonkwo grumbling, the widow glaring—and crossed the marble floor while Cutter was still arranging his exhibits. When the second clerk, a young woman with reading glasses pushed up into her hair, asked who was next, Marisol put the plastic-wrapped journals on the counter with a sound like a gavel coming down.

"Counter-claim," she said. "Concession block 44-K. Filed jointly in the names of Vane and Sterling, with supporting evidence of fraud in the original survey record."

The clerk blinked. "Evidence of—"

"Fraud." Marisol unwrapped the plastic. "1989 field journals, original photographs, and a signed affidavit from the lead surveyor, Elias Vane, attesting that the concession's foundational data was falsified to exclude indigenous holdings. It's all cross-referenced. Page numbers correspond to the map coordinates. Your registrar can verify the journal paper stock against the ministry's own 1989 supply requisitions—I checked the procurement codes while we were hiding in a swamp."

The clerk stared at her. Then at the journals. Then, with the instinct of small officials everywhere, at the senior clerk, who was still smiling at whatever Cutter was saying.

Behind them, Cutter's voice stopped mid-sentence.

Marisol turned. The lawyer was watching the counter, and his face had finally, fully, changed—the pleasantness gone entirely, leaving something older and more honest: a man watching a fire start in a building he'd insured.

* * *

**Petra**

What happened next happened fast, and Petra experienced it the way she experienced engine failure—time going granular, every second arriving separately and complete.

The senior clerk came over. He was a heavyset man near retirement with ink-stained fingers and the unhurried dignity of someone who had processed ten thousand claims and been lied to by most of them. He put on his glasses. He opened the first journal. He read for a long time.

"This is Vane," he said quietly. "Elias Vane. He filed with this office in '89. I remember his hands." He looked up. "We were told he died in the interior."

"He died three weeks ago," Petra said. "In the interior. On my aircraft. He spent thirty-five years keeping these journals safe, and the last thing he asked me was to file them before the permits lapsed." She set the affidavit on top, and beneath it, the map—oilcloth worn soft as cloth, her father's initials visible among the original crew list, R. STERLING written in a young man's confident pencil. "My father was on that expedition. He disappeared that year. These pages say why."

The senior clerk read the affidavit. Then he read the margin notes. Then he took off his glasses and cleaned them on his tie, which Petra suspected was the bureaucratic equivalent of a moment of prayer.

Across the floor, Cutter had recovered enough to advance, document case open, voice smooth again. "Registrar, I must object to the admission of materials obtained under—unclear circumstances. These items may constitute stolen property. My client has a prior claim on—"

"On what, precisely?" The senior clerk's voice didn't rise, but the room did. Heads turned. Okonkwo stepped closer. The widow. The surveyors. Adaeze lifted the folder of thumbprints above her head like a lantern. "On the journals your client's own surveyor signed, swearing the data was false? On the affidavit naming the falsification? Mr. Cutter, I have read this office's files for thirty-one years. Block 44-K's original survey shows a river where there is a mountain. I flagged the discrepancy in 1994 and was told the matter was resolved." He laid the open journal flat on the counter, turned it to face the room, and tapped a photograph: men standing before a boundary marker, and beside it, in Elias Vane's careful hand, *Marker placed 11 km east of surveyed line. R.S. objected. R.S. overruled by H.C. site manager. R.S. departed camp Oct 3. No further contact.*

R.S. Robert Sterling. Petra's father. Objecting, in ink, three weeks before he vanished.

The photograph went around the room the way news travels in small towns—by hand, by murmur, by the sudden straightening of spines. Okonkwo read it and passed it to the widow. The widow read it and passed it to the surveyors. Adaeze reached the counter and added her folder of forty names to the stack, and the senior clerk received it with both hands, formally, like a court accepting evidence.

Cutter stood alone in the middle of the marble floor with his open case and his neat exhibits, and Petra watched him understand, in real time, that the room had turned—that the clerk's ink-stained hands, the fisherman's grievance, the widow's claim, the girl's thumbprints, and two soaked women who had come down a river to get here had assembled themselves into something no amount of legal polish could dissolve.

He closed the case. "My client will hear about this," he said, which was what defeat sounded like when it wore a good coat.

"I expect so," said the senior clerk. "So will the inquiry commission. Next window, please."

The stamp came down at 11:47. Thirteen minutes to spare. The sound of it—rubber on paper, one clean percussive note—was the smallest sound Petra had ever heard and the largest, and she stood at the counter while the clerk wrote the joint filing into the register, *VANE & STERLING, counter-claim, block 44-K, evidence attached,* and found that her eyes were burning and that she didn't care who saw.

Marisol's hand found her shoulder. Neither of them said anything. There was nothing to say that the stamp hadn't already said.

Outside, the rain had stopped. Actual sunlight was breaking over the harbor, gilding the masts, and somewhere a church bell began counting the hour. Petra walked down the registry steps into it, blinking, hollowed out, lighter than she had been in years.

Then she stopped.

At the curb, Cutter was speaking into his phone, low and urgent—but that wasn't what stopped her. What stopped her was the black sedan idling behind him, rear door open, and the man leaning out of it: broad-shouldered, sunburned, with a week-old scratch down one cheek that Petra recognized, because she'd watched him make it pushing through riverside cane with a machete.

The enforcer looked directly at her. He didn't smile. He touched two fingers to his brow—not quite a salute, not quite a threat—and mouthed something she couldn't hear over the bells.

But she could read lips. She'd grown up reading wind and water and the faces of men at fuel docks.

*Not finished.*
