## 22. The Uncharted Horizon

The morning came up gold and clean, the kind of light that made the whole interior look like it had been freshly invented. Petra stood on the strip at Port Amara with a thermos of coffee going cold in her hand and watched the fog burn off the river, and for the first time in longer than she could measure, she wasn't watching the sky to see what was coming for her. She was just watching the sky.

Three weeks since the land office. Three weeks since she'd stood at that counter with ink-stained fingers and a stack of journals that smelled like 1989, and heard the registrar read both names aloud—Sterling, Vane—into the permanent record. Three weeks since Halvorsen's lawyer had gathered his leather folder and left without meeting anyone's eyes, and the room full of clerks and river people and one very surprised newspaper man had turned toward her like a tide turning.

She still woke some nights expecting the repo agent's knock. But this morning there'd been a letter instead: the lien on the de Havilland formally released, paid in full by the estate of Elias Vane, per instructions found among his papers. Marisol had laughed until she cried when they read it together—the old man had been dead five weeks and he was still settling his accounts, still keeping his promises from beyond whatever country he'd gone to. *He bought back your wings,* Marisol said. *Don't you dare sell them again.*

Petra hadn't sold them. She'd done something harder. She'd filed a flight plan under her own name.

* * *

Marisol came down the strip at seven with two duffel bags and an expression Petra had learned to read as carefully as weather. It was the look of a woman who had decided something overnight and was daring anyone to object.

"We're not just flying today," Marisol said, dropping the bags by the strut. "We're working."

"Working."

"Survey work." She pulled a rolled sheet of drafting paper from inside her jacket and flattened it against the fuselage, weighting the corners with river stones. It was a chart of the valley—the valley, their valley, the one now held in trust—and across it she'd drawn flight lines in red pencil, neat as stitching. "The trust needs current aerial documentation before the inquiry convenes. Boundaries, water levels, the village sites. Nobody else knows that country like we do now. Nobody else has a reason to."

Petra studied the lines. They ran up the same river they'd come down, past the narrows where the hull had torn, over the ridge below which the '89 wreck still sat in its clearing like a held breath. The red pencil went everywhere they had bled.

"You've been busy," she said.

"I don't sleep much." Marisol shrugged, but her eyes stayed on the map. "Turns out when you spend your whole life being angry at a ghost, and then the ghost turns out to have been telling the truth all along, you need somewhere to put your hands." She tapped the paper. "So I'm putting them here."

Petra looked at her for a long moment. Six weeks ago this woman had thrown a telegram back in her face at a marine dock and told her exactly what she could do with her dying father's map. Now she stood over that same map redrawn in her own hand, and the anger hadn't vanished—it had been forged into something with an edge and a purpose, the way ore becomes a blade.

"All right," Petra said. "Survey work."

* * *

They took off into the clean morning and climbed, and the world unrolled beneath them the way it only does from eight hundred feet—the river braiding silver through green, the mist still pooled in the hollows like spilled milk, the mountains standing in ranks to the west with their snowfields catching fire from the sunrise.

Petra flew with the window cracked because she liked the smell of the cold air, and because it reminded her she was actually here, actually doing this, actually aloft in her own airplane with no debt riding on the wings and no one's shadow on her tail.

"There," Marisol said, pointing. "That bend. That's where we bailed."

It was. The confluence looked harmless from above, brown water gone pewter in the morning light, the gravel bars like pale ribs. Petra banked gently so Marisol could see, and neither of them said anything about the night Marisol had gone into the water, or the long minutes after when Petra had hauled her onto the shingle and pressed her forehead against a cold cheek and begged her, wordlessly, to keep breathing. Some things didn't need saying twice. They needed saying never, and meaning them forever.

They followed the river upstream. At the narrows, Petra tipped a wing in salute to the rock overhang where they'd shivered through a soaked night sharing the last chocolate and the last honest words either of them had spoken in years. At the lake, she circled once over the spot where a bent propeller had grounded them and changed everything—where two strangers had run out of reasons to be guarded and found, underneath all that armor, two women who recognized each other.

"The wreck next?" Marisol asked.

"The wreck next."

They crossed the ridge and dropped into the survey country, and Petra felt the old ache rise and then, surprisingly, pass through her and out. Below lay the route her father had flown in 1989, the route Elias Vane had drawn in faded ink on oilcloth, the route that had cost both their fathers everything. She knew every turn of it now better than she knew her own face.

And it didn't hurt the way it used to. That was the miracle nobody warned you about—that grief, carried far enough, set down its weight and became ballast instead. Something that kept you steady in turbulence.

She leveled off over the crash clearing. From the air it was almost peaceful: the scar in the trees grown soft-edged with moss, the creek running bright past the tail section. Somewhere down there were the journals' empty cases, the site of the affidavit, the boot prints of men who had thought truth was a thing you could strip and carry away. The inquiry would go down there next month with cameras and lawyers. Let them. The land would tell them what it told everyone who listened long enough.

"Camera," Marisol said, and lifted it, and shot the frame that would become the first plate in the trust's official record: the valley, whole and green and legally, permanently, unstealable.

* * *

They landed at midday on the long gravel bar downstream of the river village, because there was nowhere else they meant to be. The community came out as they always did—the kids first, running the bank, then the elders walking unhurried, then old Amaia last, as always, leaning on her stick with the patience of a woman who had outlasted three governments and intended to outlast several more.

There was food, of course. There was always food. Fish smoked over alder, and flatbread, and the bitter black tea that Petra had learned to drink without sugar and now preferred without. They sat on the gravel in the sun while the de Havilland cooled behind them, ticking softly, and Amaia told them, as she did every visit, one more small story about the young surveyor named Sterling who had fixed the mission's radio in 1989 and taught the children to whistle through grass blades.

"He would be proud," Amaia said, in her way that was less a statement than a fact being entered into the record. "Both of you. This is what he was trying to build. You are what he was trying to build."

Petra looked out at the river. "I traded his compass," she said quietly. "To a trucker. For the last ride to Port Amara."

Amaia smiled with all her old gums. "A compass tells you where north is," she said. "You already know where north is. You proved it. Things like that are for people still finding their heading." She patted Petra's knee with a hand like weathered rope. "You gave away the tool. You kept the knowing. That is a good trade. Your father would call it a fair one."

Later, walking back to the plane, Petra found a small package tucked under the pilot seat, wrapped in banana leaf and tied with twine. Inside, cushioned in moss, was a compass. Old brass, dented case, the glass scratched nearly opaque.

On a slip of paper in a child's careful hand: *For the pilot who knows her heading. So you have one anyway. — T.*

She stood there a long time with it in her palm, the needle swinging, settling, pointing true north as if it had never doubted. Then she laughed—a real laugh, from the bottom of her, the kind she hadn't managed since she was nineteen years old—and stowed it in the map pocket where her father's used to ride.

Some things you inherit. Some things you're given. And some things, she was learning, you simply agree to carry.

* * *

They flew home into the evening, the sun dropping behind the western ranges and setting the whole sky ablaze, and the de Havilland ran sweet and steady through the golden air like a machine that knew it was forgiven.

"So," Marisol said over the intercom, when the valley had fallen away behind them and the lights of Port Amara were still twenty minutes out. She had the red-pencil chart on her knees, and she'd been making marks on it for most of the leg home. "I've been thinking."

"Dangerous."

"Shut up and listen." She smoothed the paper. "The trust will pay for documentation flights. Ongoing ones. Seasonal surveys, boundary checks, flood monitoring. It's in the filing—we wrote it in ourselves, remember? Provision nine." She looked over at Petra, and even in the dim cockpit Petra could see the careful neutrality of her expression, the effort it was taking to sound casual. "Provision nine needs a licensed operator. With a plane. Who knows the country."

Petra flew on for a moment, letting the words settle like sediment.

"You're asking me to take a contract."

"I'm asking you to stop treating your own life like a fuel stop. You gas up, you leave, nobody knows your name." Marisol's voice softened a degree. "You spent years being invisible because invisible can't be hurt. I know how that works. I invented half of it myself. But we just spent six weeks proving that visible survives. Visible files claims. Visible wins, sometimes, if she shows up before closing time." She tapped the chart. "So. Provision nine. Partners?"

The river slid by below, dark now, holding the last light. Ahead, the horizon was a long band of burning orange fading upward into violet, and beyond it, unseeable but utterly certain, all the rest of the country waiting to be mapped—the unnamed tributaries, the unmapped ridges, the blank spaces on every chart that some stubborn pilot with a camera and a reason might someday fill in.

Her father had died trying to draw that country honestly. Elias had lived fifty years guarding the drawing. And she and Marisol had carried it the last mile, through floodwater and gunfire-adjacent rapids and their own worst histories, to a counter in Port Amara, minutes before the bell.

The inheritance was never money. She'd known that since the rock overhang, since the letter, since the journals came up out of the cache box smelling of oilcloth and patience. The inheritance was the heading. North, and true, and hers to fly.

"Yes," Petra said. "Partners."

Marisol nodded once, businesslike, and then ruined it completely by grinning, wide and unguarded, the grin of a woman who had finally stopped circling a wound and started walking away from it. She uncapped the red pencil and wrote across the top of the chart, in letters that would be photocopied into the trust's records within the week:

**STERLING–VANE AERIAL SURVEY. EST. THIS YEAR. ALL COUNTRY CONSIDERED.**

Below it, smaller, she added a line that made Petra laugh out loud over the engine noise:

*No blank spaces left behind.*

They came into Port Amara at dusk, wheels touching the strip in the last copper light, and Petra taxied in past the fuel shed and the shack that had been her whole world for eleven cramped years, and she saw it now for what it was—not a hiding place but a starting line. Tomorrow there would be permits to copy, contracts to draft, a wreck site to document properly, an inquiry to testify before, a hundred small administrative battles against a company that did not yet know it had lost. Tonight there was coffee, badly made, shared on a wing root with the best partner she'd ever flown with.

But right now, taxiing in under the first stars, Petra Sterling let herself look east, past the strip lights, past the darkening hills, to where the horizon ran unbroken and unknown all the way around the world.

The compass in the map pocket pointed north. She didn't check it. She already knew.
