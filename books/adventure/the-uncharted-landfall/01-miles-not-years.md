## 1. Miles, Not Years

The Cessna 180 came off the gravel at Fort Simpson at 6:40 in the morning, wheels leaving the ground the way they always did—reluctantly, then all at once, like a decision finally made. Sable Winterbourne had made that same decision four thousand times in ten years, and it never got old. The Mackenzie River unspooled beneath him, brown and broad as a highway, carrying ice floes north in May like a convoy of broken plates.

He flew alone. He always flew alone. That was the arrangement he'd made with the world: give me distance and I'll give you nothing else. No wife, no crew, no radio chatter beyond what the job required. His registration said Sable Winterbourne because that was his legal name, but nobody up here used it. He was *the pilot*, or *that fella from Fort Providence*, or, at the outposts where he landed twice a month, just a nod and a hand raised over cargo.

Ten years of medicine runs and mail sacks and groceries in cardboard boxes marked FRAGILE by people who didn't mean it. Ten years of knowing every bend of the river the way you know the lines of your own palm. He could fly the Fort Simpson–Wrigley leg with his eyes closed, which was exactly why he never did. The land forgave nothing. It didn't punish either—it just absorbed you, quietly, and moved on. He'd seen what was left of men who'd treated the basin casually: a wing panel on a sandbar, a boot, a story told for twenty years afterward in the low voice people use for the dead.

The morning was clean. Cold enough to keep the air dense and honest, clear enough to see the Franklin Mountains sixty miles east, blue-white and indifferent. Sable settled into the cruise at four thousand feet and let the plane do what planes do when you stop interfering with them.

His manifest today was ordinary. Insulin and antibiotics for Wrigley. A crate of engine parts for the mission school. Two hundred pounds of flour, sugar, tea. A mail sack. And one small padded envelope, hand-delivered at dawn by a woman who hadn't given her name, addressed to an elder named Antoine Menacho at Wrigley, with instructions from the health centre in Fort Simpson that Sable was to put it directly into the man's hands and not into the general delivery.

Directly into his hands. Which meant the man was dying, or close enough that the distinction no longer mattered.

Sable had been flying medicine to dying people for a decade. It was the part of the work nobody romanticized—the bush pilot myth was all rescues and daring, and sometimes it was that, but mostly it was this: a man with a box of ampoules landing on a strip while somebody's family stood waiting with their hats off, already grieving, wanting the plane to be faster than it could possibly be. You learned to carry it. Or you learned not to look. Sable had done both, in rotation, for years.

The river bent north below him. A moose stood hip-deep in a side channel, unbothered, ancient. Somewhere under that water were the bodies of two prospectors from 1937 that old-timers still pointed out to each other, though the river kept its own records and shared none.

This was the life. Measured in miles, not years. Every season a circuit, every circuit the same, the map in his head worn smooth as a river stone. Sable Winterbourne, thirty-eight years old, owner of one airplane, one debt, and no history anyone could find—which was how he liked it. There had been a before. There was always a before. But the North didn't ask about before, and neither did he.

* * *

Wrigley's strip was a thousand feet of packed dirt beside the river, and the whole town fit inside a view from downwind: forty houses, the church, the band office, the store, smoke rising straight up in the windless cold. He called his position on 126.7 even though half the time nobody answered, and today nobody did, and that was fine too.

Antoine Menacho's house sat at the end closest to the water, a small blue-sided place with a woodpile stacked like fortifications and a dog that had been barking at Sable's plane for nine years without once learning anything from the experience.

The family was waiting. They were always waiting—he'd delivered here often enough—but today there was a stillness to them that told him everything before the envelope did. Three generations on the porch. The old man's daughter, Bernice, a heavyset woman with a face that did its grieving privately, met him at the fence.

"You got it," she said. Not a question.

"Got it." He held up the envelope. "Health centre says direct to him."

"He's inside. He's having a good morning." She said it the way people say such things—as if a good morning were a currency being spent carefully, saved for exactly this. "You'll sit with him a minute? He likes company that isn't worried about him."

Sable looked at the envelope in his hand. Small. Light. Probably cost the system eleven dollars to make and fly and deliver, and would buy the old man weeks, maybe months, maybe only comfort. Hard to say which of those was worth most, and not his call.

"Yeah," he said. "I'll sit."

Inside, the house smelled of woodsmoke and spruce gum and something medicinal simmering on the stove—labrador tea, maybe, or one of the remedies that had been working fine up here for a few thousand years before anybody flew anything anywhere. Antoine Menacho lay propped in a bed by the front window, positioned so he could watch the river and, apparently, the airstrip. His eyes were still sharp. Everything else had gone soft and diminished around them, like a shoreline after flood.

"The plane woke me up," the old man said, in English, his voice a dry rustle. "That sound. I've been hearing it since before you were born, probably. First it was warplanes going to Alaska. Then it was the geologists." He smiled with very few teeth. "Now it's you."

"Sable." He offered his hand. The old man's grip was surprisingly strong, a knot of tendon.

"I know who you are. Everybody knows who you are. That's the joke—you think nobody does." Antoine took the envelope, worked it open with fingers that trembled until they found their purpose, and didn't. He read the label on the vials inside, nodded slowly, and set them on the windowsill in the sun, deliberately, like a man arranging evidence. "Bernice will figure out the needles. She's smarter than the nurses and she knows it, which is the best kind of smart."

"How are you feeling?"

"Like a man asking the river for directions." The old man laughed, which turned into a cough, which he rode out with the patience of long practice. When it passed he studied Sable with those sharp eyes. "You fly the plateau much? Kettle River way?"

"Not much. Fuel's awkward out there. Why?"

"Government fellas were through in the fall. Six of them. Young, mostly. Polite. They camped up on the plateau doing their—" he waved a hand, "—their measuring. Rocks, ground, whatever it is they measure. My nephew drove some of their gear up in September." Antoine's gaze went back to the window, to the river. "They haven't come down."

"They're still up there?"

"That's the question, eh. Radio went quiet. Nine days now, maybe ten. People talk. Somebody's cousin heard from somebody at Tulita that the government's asking pilots if they'll go look." The old man turned back, and there was something in his face Sable couldn't quite name—not fear exactly, more like a man watching weather build on a horizon far off but coming. "You hear anything like that?"

"No. Nobody's asked me."

"Hm." Antoine let it sit. Outside, the dog had given up barking and was asleep in the sun. "Well. If they ask you, you'll go. You'll go because it's money and because it's a search and searching is decent work." He said it without accusation, as fact, the way you'd say *water runs downhill*. "Just remember the plateau is old ground. Older than the maps. Things get lost up there that were never trying to hide."

Sable didn't know what to say to that, so he said, "I'll take your mail bag out when I go."

"Do that." The old man closed his eyes, done with him, or resting for the next round of it. At the door, Bernice caught Sable's sleeve.

"He talks like that lately," she said quietly. "Riddles. The doctor says the sickness is in his blood, not his head, but—" She stopped, gathered herself. "Thank you for sitting. Most of them drop the box and go."

"It's a nice window," Sable said, which wasn't anything, and she let it be nothing, and walked him out.

* * *

He lifted off at 10:15 with the mail and a passenger list of zero, climbing south over the river with Wrigley shrinking in the mirror. The old man's words rode along with him, the way things did up here—there was so little noise that a sentence could stay in the cockpit for hours, turning over.

*Things get lost up there that were never trying to hide.*

It was nothing. Old men talked. Nine days of missed radio checks meant a dead battery or a broken antenna or a party that had pushed deeper into the plateau than planned and gotten comfortable. Survey crews went quiet all the time; half the North's geography had been mapped by people who forgot to phone home. And if the government wanted a search flown, they'd want it flown properly, with contracts and insurance and a pilot whose paperwork was boring—and that described Sable Winterbourne down to the rivets.

He stopped at Tulita for fuel at noon. The tank operator, a laconic Dene man named Peter Yallee who'd known Sable since the beginning, pumped avgas and conversation in equal, measured amounts.

"Heard you went to Wrigley," Peter said.

"Flew right past your question."

"Menacho's bad, eh."

"Bad."

Peter nodded, watched the gauge. Then, casually, the way you mention a hockey score: "Government man was here yesterday. From Yellowknife. Asking about charter rates to the Kettle plateau. Wanted names of pilots who knew the area." The nozzle clicked off. Peter topped it, slow. "Told him about you."

Sable felt it settle somewhere under his sternum—a small cold weight, familiar, the feeling of a door opening onto a room he hadn't agreed to enter. "What's his name?"

"Corliss. Suit too nice for the country. Paid cash for his coffee and counted it twice." Peter coiled the hose. "You'll get a call."

"Maybe."

"You'll get a call." Peter handed him the receipt and, with it, a look that lasted half a second longer than receipts require. "Debt on the plane still eating you?"

"Every month."

"Mm." Peter zipped his jacket against the wind. "Careful what eats you back."

* * *

The last leg home was two hundred miles of empty, gorgeous nothing, the kind of flying that had taught Sable to love the sky in the first place. Late light came slantwise across the Mackenzie lowlands, gilding every lake, laying the shadows of clouds out flat on the tundra like ink spilled on paper. He flew it the way he flew everything—hands light, mind running the checklist of his own life. Fuel: adequate. Money: not. Reputation: clean. History: sealed.

At Fort Providence he tied down, logged 4.7 hours, and walked to the pay phone outside the Northern store because his own line was a party line he didn't trust and the pay phone was worse but anonymous. One message waited with the store clerk, written on the back of a lottery ticket:

**CORLISS — GOV'T SURVEYS, YELLOWKNIFE. FORT SIMPSON LODGE TMRW 8PM. RE: CHARTER INQUIRY. TRIPLE STANDARD RATE.**

Triple rate. Sable read it twice. Triple rate bought a lot of engine overhaul. Triple rate paid off the plane by Christmas and left enough over to stop thinking about money for the first time in a decade, which was its own kind of intoxicant, and he noticed himself noticing that, and didn't like it.

He folded the ticket into his shirt pocket and stood a moment in the blue dusk while ravens argued over the garbage bins and the first stars came out hard and white over the treeline.

Nine days of silence. A suit counting change twice. An old man's riddle about things that were never hiding.

Sable went home to his cabin above the strip, heated beans, cleaned his boots, and told himself the morning would sort itself out. It usually did. Distance sorted everything, eventually—that was the whole religion of his life, and he'd never once had reason to doubt it.

But he slept badly, and in the dark he kept seeing a blue-sided house by a river, and a small envelope on a sunny windowsill, and six young men on a plateau who had stopped answering their radio, and he understood, in the wordless way pilots understand weather, that something out there had already changed direction.

He just didn't know yet that it was him.
