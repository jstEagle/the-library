## 2. The Contract

The man was waiting at the strip when Sable came down from his cabin at seven, which was the first wrong thing. Nobody waited at the Fort Simpson strip at seven in October. The fuel agent didn't arrive until eight, the mail contract until nine, and the float plane crowd had gone south with the geese weeks ago. A man standing alone on frost-cracked gravel in a city overcoat, hands in his pockets, breath steaming like a small private weather system—that wasn't a customer. That was a problem wearing a good coat.

Sable had seen him from above, actually. He'd been up on the ridge behind the cabin with his coffee, doing his morning arithmetic of the sky—wind out of the northeast, ceiling high, visibility forty miles—and he'd noticed a car parked by the hangar that hadn't been there at midnight. A government sedan, dark green, territorial plates. It sat there all night in the cold because whoever drove it had somewhere to be and no intention of being late.

Now the man turned as Sable's boots crunched on the gravel, and he smiled the way men smile when they've rehearsed it.

"Mr. Winterbourne." Not a question. He knew the name before he'd ever seen the face.

"Sable's what people call me." He stopped ten feet off, close enough to talk, far enough to move. Old habit. "You're up early."

"Corliss." The man offered a hand. His grip was dry and brief, a handshake conducted for the record rather than the pleasure of it. He was maybe fifty, silver at the temples, with the kind of tan you don't get this far north in October—which meant he'd flown in from somewhere warmer, recently, and expected to fly back out the same way. Ottawa skin. "Department of Northern Affairs. I'm told you do charter work."

"I do supply runs. Medicine, mail, freight. Whatever fits in a 180."

"And search work?"

"Never been asked."

"Well." Corliss produced a leather folder from under his arm and opened it against his forearm like a waiter presenting a bill. "Then consider this your first request. Six-man survey team, Geological Survey of Canada, working the Kettle River plateau. They missed their scheduled radio check-in nine days ago. We'd like them found."

Sable looked at the folder without taking it. "Nine days is a long time to wait."

"It took time to establish that the silence wasn't equipment failure." Corliss said it smoothly, but something flickered behind his eyes—the small tell of a man choosing his words in advance and hoping they'd hold weight. "There was a storm on the plateau two weeks back. We assumed they'd ridden it out and lost their antenna. When the second check-in also failed, we escalated."

"Escalated to me."

"To whoever knows that country. You've been flying the Mackenzie basin for—" he glanced at the folder, though Sable would have bet money he hadn't needed to— "ten years. You know every creek between here and the plateau. And you're available."

"I'm available," Sable agreed, "at my rate."

"Triple it."

The number landed in the cold air between them and just sat there, too big to be casual. Sable kept his face still, but inside him something leaned forward like a dog hearing its name. Three times his rate covered the arrears on the Cessna in a single week of flying. Covered it and left enough to put new tires on the tundra gear and still eat through winter without counting cans. He thought about the bank notice folded in the drawer under his coffee tin, the one he'd read so many times the creases were going soft.

He thought about how a man who wanted something found out fast what another man needed.

"Triple," he repeated. "Government doesn't pay triple for anything. Government pays half and calls it patriotism."

"This isn't a routine file." Corliss closed the folder partway, then seemed to reconsider and held it out again. "Take a look."

Sable took it. The paper inside was typed, official letterhead, and almost entirely blank where it mattered. The flight authorization had a reference number but no originating office. The insurance rider named him as pilot-in-command but listed the aircraft registration with one digit transposed—a digit that, if he signed it as written, would make the coverage worthless. The operational order described the search area in coordinates only, no landmarks, no names, as if the plateau had been wiped off the map for the purposes of this document. And the section marked *Reporting* instructed him to file his findings not with the local RCMP detachment or the regional office in Yellowknife, both of which were a radio call away, but directly to Corliss himself, at an address in Ottawa, by courier.

"You want me to fly a grid pattern," Sable said slowly, "over country I could draw blindfolded, and report to a post office box four thousand miles south."

"The matter has a degree of sensitivity."

"What kind of sensitivity?"

"The administrative kind." Corliss smiled again, and this time it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Survey teams carry instruments worth more than your airplane, Mr. Winterbourne. If this becomes public knowledge before we understand what happened—if prospectors, say, get wind of where six trained geologists were working—we could have a staking rush on that plateau inside a month. Every claim jumper between here and Great Slave Lake. The department prefers discretion until the situation resolves."

It was a reasonable explanation. That was the trouble. It was reasonable the way a forged cheque is well-written—it had all the right parts in all the right places, and it was still wrong. Sable had spent ten years learning to read the north, and the north taught you that the dangerous things rarely looked dangerous. They looked like weather coming down a valley. They looked like a frozen river in April. They looked like paperwork with too many blanks.

He handed the folder back. "I'll need the correct aircraft registration on the insurance. I'll need the authorization countersigned by the Yellowknife office. And I'll need to know who those six men are. Names, next of kin, last known camp location. I don't search for ghosts."

For the first time, Corliss looked genuinely at him—not at the pilot, not at the asset, but at the man—and whatever he saw there made him recalculate something. "You're suspicious by nature."

"I'm careful by trade. Suspicion is just care with a longer memory."

A sound escaped Corliss that might have been a laugh in a younger throat. He took out a pen, corrected the registration digit on the insurance rider without comment—an admission, small and clean—and wrote the survey team's details on a separate sheet from memory. Voss, R. — team leader. Halloran, D. Quill, M. Okafor, T. Lindqvist, P. Marchand, J. Last known position: a basecamp on the plateau at the head of the Kettle River, coordinates supplied. Last contact: nine days ago, a routine position report, everything nominal.

*Everything nominal.* Sable read the phrase twice. In his experience, nothing in the north was ever nominal. Nominal was a word southerners used about country they didn't understand.

"They're young?" he asked.

"Professionals," Corliss said, which was not an answer.

"Quill. Mara Quill. There was a woman on the university circuit a few years back—bedrock mapping up the Liard. Would that be her?"

Something moved behind Corliss's eyes again, faster this time. "I couldn't say."

"You wrote the list from memory," Sable said mildly. "You know exactly who she is."

The silence lasted three seconds. Somewhere across the strip a raven commented on it, one dry croak, and lifted off the hangar roof into the blue.

"Mr. Winterbourne." Corliss put the pen away and buttoned his coat, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into a register that was almost friendly, which was worse than anything that had come before it. "I'm going to be direct with you, because I'm told bush pilots respect that. This job pays triple because it's worth triple to us, and it's worth triple because it needs doing quietly and quickly. Find the team, confirm their status, report back. That's the whole contract. You're not being asked to investigate anything. You're being asked to look out a window and describe what you see. Can you do that?"

"I can look out a window," Sable said. "What I describe is up to me."

"Of course." Corliss extended his hand again, and this time the grip lingered half a second longer, dry and firm as old paper. "Fuel and expenses billed to the department. Half up front—I'll have it wired to your account by noon. Wheels up tomorrow if the weather holds."

He walked to the green sedan, unhurried, and Sable stood in the frost watching him go, feeling the shape of the thing settle over him like cloud moving down a valley. Half up front. By noon. A man who paid before the work was done wasn't buying a service. He was buying silence in advance, and he wanted the receipt early.

* * *

The money arrived at 11:47. Sable watched the teller at the Northern Bank count it out twice, watched the deposit slip print, and felt the debt on his airplane shrink from a mountain to a hill, and hated himself a little for the relief. That was the trap of needing things: it made other people's blanks easy to overlook. He'd known men who'd signed onto bad boats for less than triple rate. Some of them came back. Some of them were still out there, under the water, keeping their own kind of silence.

He spent the afternoon getting ready, because ready was ready regardless of whose money paid for it. He pulled the cowl off the 180 and checked the oil screen, topped up the hydraulic fluid on the ski rigging, dug out the survival kit and repacked it—stove, tarp, chocolate, the .30-30 he carried more for comfort than use. He filed the sledgehammer, rope, and spare spark plugs where his hands could find them in the dark. Ten years of habit. The plane was the one honest relationship in his life; he kept his side of it scrupulously.

Toward dusk he walked up to the ridge with a thermos and sat on his usual rock, looking east toward the plateau, sixty miles off as the crow flew and considerably farther as the Cessna climbed. The light was going long and amber across the basin, the rivers running steel-grey below their first skim of ice, and out there somewhere were six professionals who had reported *everything nominal* and then folded up like a tent in the wind.

He turned Corliss's sheet over in his mind. Names from memory. Coordinates without landmarks. Reporting by courier to Ottawa. A man who knew Mara Quill's history and wouldn't admit it. And underneath all of it, the detail that bothered him most, the one he kept circling back to like a hawk on a wire:

Nine days.

You didn't wait nine days. Not for a six-man crew in survey country in October, with winter stacking up behind the calendar like freight cars. If the department had truly believed those men were alive and in trouble, the search would have started on day two, with military aircraft, with everything. Nine days meant either incompetence—and Corliss was many things, but sloppy wasn't among them—or it meant the department already knew something about what had happened on that plateau, and the nine days had been spent deciding what to do about it.

And what you did, if you were a department with a sensitive file, was hire a discreet local man. Pay him triple. Keep the reporting line short and private. Give him a window to look out of, and hope he described what he was told to see.

Sable poured the last of the coffee and drank it cooling, and the first star came out over the plateau like a pinhole in black tin.

He wasn't a crusader. He wanted that understood, mostly by himself. He'd spent a decade building a life with no edges—no politics, no debts except the bank's, no entanglements deeper than a fuel slip. Distance was his religion, and distance required neutrality the way fire required air. But there was a difference, he was beginning to understand, between neutral and blind, and Corliss's paperwork had too many blanks for a careful man to sign without reading what wasn't written there.

Tomorrow he'd fly the grid. He'd take the money, because the money was real and the debt was realer, and because refusing it would only mean someone else flew the pattern with fewer questions and colder eyes. But he'd fly it his way—low, slow, thorough, reading the country the way it deserved to be read. And if there was something out there that a department in Ottawa wanted found quietly, or not found at all, then Sable Winterbourne intended to be the one who decided what his window showed.

Below him, the strip lay grey in the last light, and the green sedan was gone. He hadn't heard it leave. He hadn't heard it arrive last night, either, and that fact followed him down the ridge and into the cabin, where he laid out the Corliss sheet on the table beside his own maps and began, methodically, to compare coordinates.

The search area was right. The plateau, the headwaters, the basecamp—all of it matched the survey filings he'd seen posted in Yellowknife years ago. But the operational order drew the grid boundary along the south edge of the plateau, and the south edge, according to every map Sable owned, ran straight along the Kettle River reserve—the treaty land where the community had trapped and fished since before there were maps at all.

Corliss's grid didn't include it. As if the land itself had been left off the list of places worth searching.

As if whatever the surveyors had found, they'd found it there.

Sable sat back in the lamplight, the pen motionless in his hand, and outside the wind came around to the northeast, out of the plateau, carrying the first smell of snow.
