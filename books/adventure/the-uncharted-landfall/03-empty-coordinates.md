## 3. Empty Coordinates

The first morning of the search, I flew the grid like a man mowing a lawn.

That was Corliss's word for it—*mow the grid*—delivered with a little smile over his clipboard at the Fort Providence strip, as if the plateau were a hayfield and I were a hired tractor. North-south lines a mile apart, then east-west to cross-check, the whole pattern stitched over the Kettle River high country where six surveyors had stopped answering their radio nine days ago. He'd drawn the grid on a topographic map with a ruler and a red grease pencil, and the neatness of it bothered me more than I let on. Men who draw grids like that believe the world will hold still while they measure it.

I'd flown supply runs out of Fort Providence for ten years. I knew the plateau the way you know a face you've seen across a room for a decade—not intimately, but well enough to know when something about it had changed. The Kettle River country was muskeg and black spruce, burn scars from a fire twenty years back, eskers running through it like the spine of some buried animal. In August the caribou trails stitched the high ground. In September the fog came off the river valleys in the mornings and sat there until ten, lying to you about what was underneath.

Corliss wanted me flying by first light. I was airborne at 6:40, climbing out of the river mist into air so clean it felt rinsed.

The Cessna 180 is not a fast airplane, but she is an honest one. She'll tell you everything—the weight of your cargo, the temper of the air, the lie in your own fuel planning—and she doesn't care whether you want to hear it. Her name, painted on the cowl by some previous owner and never repainted, was *Josephine*. I hadn't chosen it. It came with the debt. There are days when I think the bank owns more of her than I do, and other days when I think no one owns an airplane; you just keep her flying and she permits you to sit up front.

I flew the first line south to north along the eastern edge of the grid, throttle set to a patient 95 miles per hour, eyes moving in the rhythm every bush pilot learns: wingtip, ground, wingtip, horizon, instruments, repeat. Looking for six men in country like that is looking for six words in a library. What you actually look for is interruption. A straight line where nothing grows straight. Smoke. Color that shouldn't be there. The glint of aluminum in spruce.

By noon I'd covered the eastern third and found nothing but a moose standing hip-deep in a beaver pond, regarding my airplane with the flat indifference of a creature who has never once needed paperwork.

I put down on the lake Corliss had designated as the forward base—a long narrow finger of water with a gravel bar at its north end—and ate beans from a tin in the shadow of the wing. The silence up there has a texture. It isn't empty. It's full of things choosing not to make noise: a jay working the spruce cones, wind worrying the tops of the trees two miles off, the small tick of cooling metal. A country can sound occupied or abandoned, and this one sounded *watched*, which is a thing I couldn't have explained to Corliss and didn't try.

Day one: nothing.

* * *

Day two, the light went flat.

No sun, just a white ceiling pressing down at four thousand feet, and under it the plateau turned the color of old dishwater. Flat light kills depth perception—you lose the hollows, the cutbanks, the whole grammar of the terrain—and every bush pilot has a story about a hillside that wasn't there until he was already committed to it. I flew lower than I liked and slower than was comfortable, and the land unrolled beneath me like a map that had been left out in the rain.

It was on the second east-west line, near a creek the maps called Unnamed Tributary 4, that I saw the flash.

Not metal glint—that's sharp, momentary, directional. This was softer. A pale rectangle against dark spruce, like a page left open on a table. I circled twice, dropped to eight hundred feet, and picked out the shape of tents. Two of them, maybe three, pitched on a bench above the creek among the burn-dead snags.

My heart did the thing hearts do. Six missing men, nine days of silence, and here was color on the plateau. I keyed the radio and gave Corliss's office my position, got static in return, and made the decision every pilot makes alone because there is never anyone else in the cockpit: I'd land and look.

There was no lake close enough. The bench had a muskeg flat beside it, sedge and standing water and the constant threat of a wheel punching through into soup. I found a stretch of gravel bar along the creek half a mile downstream, put her down with the water low and the wind quartering, and walked in carrying the rifle I carry for bears and the optimism I carry for people.

The camp was tidy.

That was the first wrongness, and it arrived before anything else did. Tents pitched square, guy-lines taut, pegs seated properly. A fire pit ringed with stones and swept clean. Gear stacked under a fly: packboards, crates, a canvas food cache hung correctly between two trees, high enough for wolverines and low enough for men. Six men don't keep a camp like this if they're dead, starving, or fleeing. Six men keep a camp like this if they expect to come back—or if they're being careful about what they leave behind.

And they had left things behind. That was the second wrongness.

Two rifles stood leaned in the corner of the larger tent, barrels down, actions closed, slings wrapped around the stocks in that particular military-neat way that says someone was trained and hasn't forgotten. Nobody walks away from a rifle in bear country. Not a hungry man, not a frightened one, not a lost one. You can pry a rifle out of a northerner's hands with a crowbar and a court order. These had been *set down*, deliberately, the way you set down tools at the end of a shift.

Beside them, a radio. A proper field set, the kind the government issues, with a folding antenna and a hand crank. Its antenna was coiled. Not dropped, not tangled, not snapped off in a fall—coiled, in a flat spiral, and tied with a length of parachute cord. Tied. Someone had taken the time to tie off the antenna of the radio that was supposed to call for help, and then walked into the bush without it.

I stood in that tent for a long time with my hat in my hands, feeling the wrongness move through the camp like cold water through a hull.

Disaster leaves a signature. I've seen enough of it from the air—burn patterns, debris fields, the way wreckage scatters along the direction of travel. Panic leaves a signature too: dropped gear, torn packs, a trail of small stupid losses marking the path of people running. Even starvation leaves one, in the end. But this camp had no signature at all. It had been *closed out*. Dishes done. Fire cold and buried. Boots gone from the tent floor—every man had walked out in his own boots, in order, taking whatever they'd decided to take and leaving what they'd decided to leave.

Six men do not agree to vanish together by accident.

I checked the food cache because hunger explains a lot of behavior. It was more than half full. Flour, rice, tinned meat, tea, sugar sealed in plastic bags against the damp. Nine days of silence and they'd walked away from three weeks of food. I counted the sleeping bags: none. They'd taken their bags and their packs and their rifles' ammunition—no, wait. I opened the ammo box. Full. Forty rounds, factory sealed, sitting next to two rifles nobody would need again.

You don't leave ammunition. Ammunition is life in the North. Leaving it is a statement, even if the man leaving it never meant it as one. It says: *we are not going to need to defend ourselves, or feed ourselves, or signal anyone.* It says the decision was made somewhere else, by someone else, and these men were only executing it.

I sat on a crate in the doorway of the big tent and ate a handful of their rice because it seemed important to share the camp's bad luck somehow, and I thought about what I'd tell Corliss. *Found the camp. Intact. No sign of the team.* And Corliss would write it on his clipboard and look pleased, because a tidy camp is a reportable fact, and facts in tidy rows are what men like him eat.

But the camp wasn't a fact. It was a message, and I was pretty sure I was the only one reading it.

* * *

Day three, I flew the western grid, and the wrongness followed me.

It gets in the plane with you, that kind of thing. You find yourself checking mirrors that aren't there, lifting a wing to look behind you, noticing your own reflection in the window and being briefly surprised by it. The plateau kept its own counsel. Fog in the river valleys until mid-morning, then a hard blue afternoon with the kind of visibility that makes you feel like the only living thing under heaven—which, for three hours, I might have been.

On the last line of the day, low over a burn scar where the black snags stood up like the masts of a drowned fleet, I saw the tracks.

Or rather, I saw where tracks should have been and weren't. A game trail crossed a sedge flat below me, and alongside it, plain as print in the wet ground, ran the mark of a heavy boot—single file, spaced evenly, heading northeast toward the high esker country. One man, walking with purpose. I banked around and followed the line of it as far as I could, and here was the thing that finished the job the camp had started: the trail simply ended. Not faded, not scattered, not lost in rock. Ended, at the edge of a patch of moss so undisturbed it might have been ironed. As if the man had walked to that spot, considered his options, and been *removed*—or as if he'd known exactly where the aircraft would be flying and had stepped off the trail precisely there, into ground chosen because it holds no track.

Nobody does that by instinct. That's done by someone who knows how aerial search works.

I climbed back to altitude and flew the last hour in a silence of my own, doing arithmetic I didn't like. Six men. A camp closed out with parade-ground precision. A radio antenna coiled and tied. Food and ammunition abandoned. One set of deliberate tracks that ended where tracks become invisible to the sky. And somewhere in all of it, a leader named Voss who had radioed Ottawa in a panic and then gone quiet—according to Mara Quill, though I didn't know her name yet, and wouldn't for days.

What I knew was simpler and worse: this was not a disappearance. Disappearances are messy. This was a *withdrawal*, planned and executed, by men who understood exactly what the sky could see and had dressed accordingly. Either they were hiding from something that could find them from the air—

—or they were hiding from the search itself. From me. From the man in the Cessna drawing red lines on Corliss's map.

I landed at the forward base as the light went copper, taxied to the gravel bar, and sat in the cockpit with the engine ticking down, watching the shadows walk up the far shore. Three days of grid. Nothing to show but questions wearing the uniforms of answers. Somewhere out in that darkening spruce were six men who had walked away from warmth, food, rifles, and rescue, in boots, in order, on purpose.

I got on the radio and reported the camp's coordinates to Corliss's office, precise to the tenth of a minute, because that was the job. Then I sat a while longer, watching the first star come out over the esker, and admitted the thing I'd been circling since the tent:

Somebody had to go back in there on foot. Somebody had to read the ground the way the ground wanted to be read, not the way a grid reads it. And the plateau, which had spent three days showing me nothing, had shown me exactly one thing worth following: smoke.

Because as I'd climbed away from the burn scar that afternoon, banking south for the base, I'd looked back—one last habit of a decade of supply runs, checking my wake—and seen it. Thin, gray, deliberate. A cookfire, burning in a fold of the high country where no registered trapline ran, no survey grid reached, and no sensible man would light a flame visible to the very aircraft that was hunting him.

Unless he wanted to be found.

Unless he wanted to be found by *me*.
