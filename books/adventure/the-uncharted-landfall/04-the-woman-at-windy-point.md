## 4. The Woman at Windy Point

Mara Quill had been watching the plane for eleven minutes before she let herself believe it was real.

It came up the Kettle River valley from the south, low and unhurried, a Cessna on floats skimming the brown water, and her first thought—absurd, humiliating—was that it was a mirage. Nine days of spruce and silence and cold camp meals eaten standing up, nine days of listening to the plateau breathe, and now here was an engine note she knew the way she knew her own pulse. A bush pilot's plane. Somebody's lifeline, somebody's gossip wagon, somebody's hearse when the winter came early.

She lay flat on the granite of Windy Point with her binoculars pressed to her eyes and did not move.

Below her, maybe four hundred feet down where the ridge dropped to the river, the plane banked once over the old survey camp—the tents still standing, the radio mast still up, everything arranged so neatly it looked like a diorama of itself—and then climbed away northward, following the line of the escarpment, following the grid pattern that meant someone was searching in earnest. Flying a search pattern. Someone had finally been paid enough to come looking for them.

*Us,* she corrected herself. *Looking for us.*

Her hands were shaking. She made them stop.

Nine days ago she'd have run down this ridge waving both arms, screaming herself hoarse, grateful past all dignity for the sight of any aircraft at all. Nine days ago she'd been a geologist with a contract number and a field schedule and a tent she shared with a woman who snored. Now she was a woman lying on a rock watching a rescue plane pass overhead while she willed it not to see her, and the distance between those two women was six core samples and one phone call to Ottawa, and Mara Quill hated every foot of it.

The engine faded. The plane shrank to a speck against the northern haze, then to nothing, and the valley closed back over itself like water.

She got up slowly, knees cracking, and stood in the wind that gave Windy Point its name—a wind that came off the icefields to the west and never stopped, that had worn the stunted pines along the ridgeline into shapes like crouching animals. Below her the Kettle River ran silver-brown through its braided channels, and beyond it, invisible under the forest and the muskeg and the thin green skin of the world, lay the thing they had found.

The thing that had scattered them like flushed birds.

---

She'd gone down to the core shack on the fourth morning of the survey because Danny Okafor had been acting strange for two days—not strange exactly, but *quiet*, and Danny quiet was louder than most men shouting. He was the junior sampler, twenty-three years old, meticulous as a monk, and he'd been re-boxing the core trays at midnight without being asked, which was the kind of behavior that in the bush meant either love or trouble.

"What've you got?" she'd asked him, and he hadn't answered right away. He'd held a length of NQ core out to her in both hands, the way you hand someone something breakable, or holy.

Chalcopyrite. Massive sulfide. Brass-gold mineralization running through the core like veins through marble, and below it, where the drill had punched through into the second zone, bornite—that deep iridescent purple-blue that gets called peacock ore by people who don't know what they're holding, and gets called *money* by people who do.

Mara had done the arithmetic standing there in the core shack with her headlamp on, and the arithmetic had stopped her breath. Grade, width, continuity, depth—the numbers assembled themselves in her head unbidden, the way they always did, and the shape they made was not a good deposit. It was not even a great deposit. It was the kind of deposit that got written about. The kind that got companies built around it. The kind that, in forty years of looking, she had read about in other people's papers and never once expected to touch.

And it sat directly beneath the Kettle River reserve.

Not near it. Not adjacent to it, with a convenient geological boundary that could be argued about in hearings. Beneath it. The ore body's footprint and the community's treaty land were the same shape on the map, more or less, the way a shadow is the same shape as the thing that casts it.

"Does Voss know?" she'd said.

Danny had looked at her with an expression she would carry for the rest of her life. Not fear. Something worse. *Relief.* The relief of a man who has been carrying a secret alone in the wilderness and finally gets to set it down.

"He radioed Ottawa," Danny said. "Last night. Encrypted channel. I heard him."

Voss had been team leader for eleven years. Elias Voss was a careful man, a decent man, a man who kept his boots resoled and his reports honest and had once, famously, refused a promotion because accepting it would have meant signing off on a mine assessment he didn't believe. If Voss had gone to Ottawa on an encrypted channel at midnight, it wasn't to report a discovery. Discoveries got reported in daylight, with champagne.

Two days later the charter floatplane came in with new orders, and Mara watched Voss read them standing beside the dock, and she watched the man change.

He read the pages twice. Then he folded them along their original creases, which was the act of a man handling something dangerous, and put them in his breast pocket, and stood looking at the river for a long time. When he turned around his face had been rearranged into something official, and he called the whole team together at the mess tent, and he told them the survey was concluded ahead of schedule, and that the data would be handled by Ottawa from this point, and that everyone would be flown out on Thursday.

That was the lie. Everyone could feel it was a lie. Surveys didn't conclude ahead of schedule; surveys ran over, always, because the bush ate schedules. And data didn't get "handled by Ottawa." Data got shipped, logged, archived. *Handled* was what you did with problems.

It was Halloran who figured out the shape of it first—Halloran, the senior mapper, the oldest of them, a man Mara had liked moderately and trusted moderately, both of which turned out to be two measures too generous. He took her aside behind the core shack on Tuesday evening, smelling faintly of the rye he'd started hiding in his coffee thermos, and he said, in a voice pitched low and pleasant:

"You're a smart girl, Quill. Smart girls know when a door opens."

"What door?"

"The kind with money behind it." He'd smiled at her then, and it was the smile that told her everything, because it was a recruiting smile, patient and warm, the smile of a man who had already said yes himself. "There's a revision coming down. Boundary adjustment. Technical matter. Some of us are going to be asked to initial some maps, and some of us are going to be looked after, and some of us—" he'd shrugged, delicately, "—are going to find the bush very large and very empty."

Mara had looked at him and felt something in her chest go cold and hard, a stone dropping through the warm water of her body, settling somewhere below her lungs.

"You're describing a bribe," she said.

"I'm describing *career management*," Halloran said. "There's a difference. You'll learn it."

---

They scattered on Thursday—or rather, the plan scattered them, and each of them broke differently along the fault lines of their own character, which is the only way anyone ever breaks.

The charter came in and took three of them: Prentiss and Leduc and the cook, Adeyemi, who cried quietly the whole flight out and signed whatever he was handed in Yellowknife because he had a daughter with a heart condition and medical bills with teeth. Mara didn't blame him. That was the trap of it, the engineering of it—it was built so that no single refusal would ever be clean. Every yes had a hostage attached.

Halloran stayed behind voluntarily, officially to "close out the camp," actually to supervise the burning of the duplicate field maps and the packing of the falsified ones. Two others—Okafor and the driller, Szymanski—walked into the bush the morning before the charter arrived, taking nothing, telling no one, leaving their gear in unnervingly perfect order because Okafor had understood, with his meticulous monk's mind, that disorder would draw searchers and order might not. They were out there now, somewhere south of the river, living off what the land gave and what Mara had cached for them before she left camp herself.

And Voss—

Voss had walked too, but different. Voss had taken the satchel.

She'd seen him go, the last night, in the blue hour when the light doesn't leave so much as retreat. He came to her tent, and he didn't ask her to hide him or help him or anything a man asks when he wants to survive. He set the canvas satchel on her folding table and opened it, and inside were the six original core boxes, the ones drilled before the orders came down, the ones whose assay certificates had been typed in the field lab with no one's initials forged on anything.

"They're going to redraw the boundaries," he said. His voice was flat, exhausted, the voice of a man reading his own autopsy. "The deposit will end at the reserve line. On paper. There's a version of the map where my own signature appears on a boundary I never drew, and by the time anyone checks the originals against it, the claim will be staked and the permits will be issued and it won't matter anymore." He'd closed the satchel. "Except it does matter. Because the land isn't empty. It's Cardinal land. Josephine Cardinal's grandfather trapped that valley before Canada had a name for it, and the treaty says what it says, and these people are going to lose it because six men found gold-colored rock under their graves."

"Why are you telling me?"

"Because you're the only one who hasn't decided yet." He'd almost smiled. "I've watched you for three weeks, Quill. You haven't decided anything since the core shack. That means there's still time."

Then he'd picked up the satchel and walked west toward the old trapper's cabin on the Carcajou trail, and Mara had stood in the blue light and understood that she was watching a man walk toward a broken ankle, or a fever, or worse, because the bush collects its tolls from everyone equally and does not care what you're carrying.

She should have gone with him. She knew that now. She'd stayed instead—stayed hidden, stayed alive, stayed useless—because some animal part of her brain had calculated that a dead witness protects nothing, and she'd hated herself with a clean, steady flame ever since. Guilt, she was learning, was just grief with the serial numbers filed off. It looked identical. It weighed the same.

---

Now she crouched on Windy Point with the wind pulling at her hood, and she made herself think about the plane instead of the past, because the plane was the present tense, and the present tense was the only place a person could still do anything.

A search plane meant the government had noticed the silence. Nine days of missed check-ins bought you a search, standard procedure, and searches ended one of two ways: with survivors walking to a strip, or with a report filed and the file closed. Either way, the clock was now running on the fiction. Once the team was "found"—or officially declared lost—the story would harden into its final form. Tragic accident. Equipment failure. The North takes its toll. And underneath the eulogies, quiet as a mole tunneling, the boundary revision would move forward, because dead or rescued, the survey team could no longer contradict the map.

Unless someone contradicted it first.

She ran the options the way she'd run a traverse, marking each one honestly, including the ugly ones.

Signal the plane, get lifted out, tell the pilot everything, trust the chain of custody from a stranger's cockpit to a Yellowknife office to a minister's desk. Fast. Clean. And it put every piece of evidence she had—her memory, her notes, the cache locations—into the hands of whoever happened to be paying for that airplane. Because she'd watched the charter come and go, and she'd seen the man who stepped off it, and he hadn't dressed like a pilot or a bureaucrat. He'd dressed like a lawyer who owned the courtroom. Corliss, Voss had called him, spitting the name like a pit. The fixer. The one who carried the revisions in his breast pocket and smiles like a handshake.

Or stay hidden. Stay a ghost. Let the search fail, let the file close, keep herself and her knowledge intact until she could move it herself, on her own terms, to the only people who couldn't be bought: the Kettle River elders, and whoever among the outside world still printed things.

Slower. Uglier. And it meant letting the searchers fly home empty, meaning the families of Prentiss and Leduc and Adeyemi would spend another week believing their people were dead in a lake somewhere. She thought of Adeyemi's daughter and the thought went through her like a drill bit.

Both options cost someone. That was the discovery nobody puts in the brochures: out here, every choice is a cut, and you just pick where to bleed.

The wind gusted, flattening the grass along the ridge, and far below her she saw movement at the old camp—a figure, small at this distance, moving between the tents. Her heart slammed. Then she saw the second figure, and the third, and recognized the deliberate slowness of starving men conserving calories.

Okafor and Szymanski had come back to camp. Drawn back, maybe, by the same hope she'd crushed—the hope of rescue—or maybe just unable to stay away from the only structure in a hundred miles. And the search plane had already passed them by, already banked away north, and if it flew the grid in order it wouldn't return to this sector until tomorrow, and tomorrow the men might be too weak to signal, or the weather might be down, or—

Or nothing. Or she could stop watching and start deciding.

Mara took out her notebook—the real one, the field notebook she'd kept honest through all of it, the one document in the whole rotten survey that no one had touched—and she wrote, in pencil, in her small square handwriting:

*Search plane, Cessna on floats, 1400 hrs, flew grid north. Did not signal. Decision: hold.*

And then, underneath, because honesty was the one discipline she had left:

*Cost: two days of Okafor and Szymanski's strength. Mine too.*

She looked north, to where the plane had gone, and she made herself study what she'd seen of it. Floats, not wheels—which meant lakes, which meant a pilot who worked the small strips, the medicine runs, the outpost routes. Not a company charter. Company charters flew wheels and schedules. This had been a working man's plane, dented and patched, flying like it was being driven rather than piloted.

An independent. Maybe the only kind of pilot in the territory who hadn't already been reached—or who could still be reached, before Corliss's people got to him. Every contract had a price, but independents priced themselves, and sometimes the price wasn't money. Sometimes it was whether you could look at yourself in the mirror of your own windshield.

She'd need to find him. Not today—today she needed to get down off this ridge and warn Okafor and Szymanski that the search had missed them, and get them fed, and decide which of them could still walk. But soon. Before the file closed. Before the map hardened.

Because somewhere out there was a man with a dented Cessna who thought he was looking for six missing surveyors, and Mara Quill was going to have to teach him that what he was really looking for was a story—and that once he heard it, he would never again be able to land anywhere neutral.

She folded the notebook away and started down the ridge, into the trees, toward the smoke of a cookfire she'd have to explain, carrying the weight of a choice she'd already made and hadn't yet said out loud:

The plane would come back. And next time, she was going to be standing in the open when it did.
