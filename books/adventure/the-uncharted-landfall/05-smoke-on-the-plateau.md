## 5. Smoke on the Plateau

The smoke showed up at 4:17 in the afternoon, on the third day of grid searches, and Sable Winterbourne saw it before he understood it.

He was flying leg seven of a pattern he'd flown so many times his hands had stopped needing him—climb, turn, scan, note, descend to the next line. The plateau unrolled beneath the Cessna's wheels in its usual gray-green boredom: muskeg, black spruce, the Kettle River braiding itself into silver threads through the valley. His notebook lay open on his knee with three days of nothing written in it. *Grid 4, clear. Grid 5, clear. Grid 6, caribou, twelve head, moving north.* He'd started logging wildlife just to keep the pen moving.

Then, off his left wingtip, where the plateau shouldered up against a stand of birch that hadn't learned yet to drop its leaves, there was a smudge in the air. Thin. Gray-white. Rising straight up in windless air the way only one kind of fire rises.

A cookfire.

Sable banked without deciding to. That was the thing about ten years of bush flying—it moved your body faster than your judgment. By the time his brain caught up and said *careful*, he was already in a slow orbit around the smoke, throttled back to something just above a stall, peering down through the glass.

The fire sat in a shallow draw between two ridges, maybe four hundred meters from the tree line. Someone had built it small and disciplined—a hunter's fire, not a lost man's fire. Lost men built bonfires. Lost men burned green boughs and half the forest trying to be seen. This fire was the size of two cupped hands, tucked under an overhang of rock, nearly invisible from anything but directly above.

Which meant whoever tended it did not want to be found.

Or wanted very much to be found by exactly one person, and wasn't sure yet who that would be.

Sable circled twice more. No movement below. No figures waving jackets. Just the fire, and beside it—and this was what finally made his stomach tighten—the glint of aluminum. A tent. Pitched low, guyed tight, camouflaged with cut boughs laid over the fly like a bird's nest someone had tried to hide.

He pulled out the survey team's last known coordinates and checked them against his own position. Eleven kilometers southwest. Close enough. Nine days missing, six men, and now: one fire, one tent, and no signal fire, no ground-to-air code stomped into the muskeg, no mirror flashes.

He should log it and radio it in. That was the job. Corliss had been explicit: *observe, confirm, report coordinates, do not land unless survival is at stake.* Corliss had been explicit about a lot of things, in that flat government voice that made every instruction sound like it had already been approved by someone whose name you weren't cleared to know.

But survival *was* at stake—that was the argument Sable's hands were already making for him, easing the yoke over, lining up the long meadow east of the draw. Two men or six men had been out here nine days in September cold. If they were hurt, if they were starving, then the difference between reporting coordinates tonight and landing now was measured in toes and fingers, maybe lives. He'd hauled medicine to dying elders across this whole basin for a decade. He knew what distance cost.

And underneath that decent excuse, quieter, there was the other thing. The thing he didn't say to himself. Three days of empty grids had gotten under his skin. Gear caches left in perfect order. Antennas coiled like someone expected to come back. A disappearance shaped like intention. He wanted to look at these men with his own eyes and see whether they looked like victims or like something else.

He ran his pre-landing checks slower than usual, as if thoroughness could make the decision respectable.

The meadow was soft but honest—no stumps, no hidden creek, just tussock grass going gold at the tips. He came in over the birches with a touch of power, flared, and set the wheels down with the particular tenderness he saved for ground he didn't trust. Rolled out long. Stopped.

Silence rushed in the way it does when the engine quits—the world suddenly enormous and listening.

Sable sat for a moment with his hands on the yoke. Somewhere behind him, eleven kilometers northeast, was the camp that had gone silent. Somewhere beyond that was Fort Providence, and Corliss, and paperwork with too many blanks. And here, forty meters ahead, was a cookfire burning small and disciplined under a rock overhang, and nobody coming out to meet him.

"Right," he said to the windshield, because saying things out loud was a habit of men who flew alone, and got out.

* * *

He approached the camp the way you approach a strange dog. Open hands, angled path, talking the whole time—not words so much as a tone, the low even murmur that says *I'm not a threat, I'm just a fact walking toward you.*

No answer. But as he got closer he saw details that rearranged the picture: two bedrolls inside the tent's shadow, not six. One rifle leaned against the rock, barrel down, muzzle clean. A pot of water just off the boil. And food—or the ghost of food. A tin of pemmican scraped to the metal. A foil wrapper folded into a neat triangle. Nothing wasted. Nothing thrown away.

Nine days of careful hunger.

"Hello the camp," he called, using the old trapper's greeting, the one that meant *I see you, so let's skip the part where we pretend otherwise.*

A pause long enough to count heartbeats in. Then a voice from behind the rock overhang—flat, wary, educated: "How many of you?"

"One."

Another pause. Then two men stood up out of the rocks as if the rocks had decided to give them back.

They were bad. Not wounded-bad, exactly—no blood, no improvised bandages—but hollowed-out bad, the kind of thin that comes from eating every other day so the food lasts. The taller one had a beard going gray at the chin though he couldn't have been past thirty-five, and eyes that did a full inventory of Sable in about two seconds: hands, hip, aircraft, horizon. The shorter one swayed slightly on his feet, gripping the rock, and Sable registered the tremor of someone running on nothing but will and caffeine.

"You're the pilot," the tall one said. Not a question.

"I'm the pilot." Sable stopped at the edge of the fire ring, keeping the distance polite. "Sable Winterbourne. I've been flying search lines over this plateau for three days. You people are nine days overdue on a radio check." He let that sit. "You want to tell me why your gear cache looked like a museum exhibit, and why your fire's built to be invisible?"

The two men exchanged a glance. It was quick, but Sable had spent ten years reading faces across a cargo deck—elders deciding how much truth a stranger could carry, traders deciding how much lie a price could hold. The glance said: *he doesn't know.*

"We got separated," the tall one said. "From the rest of the team."

"When."

"A while ago."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting," the tall one said, and there was no apology in it. Just exhaustion holding up a wall.

The shorter one laughed—a single dry bark with no humor in it, the laugh of a man whose nerves had been stripped to wire. "Christ, Denholm, he landed. He flew three days of grids and then he landed. Doesn't that buy anything?"

"It buys a ride out," Denholm said. "That's all it buys."

Sable crouched by the fire, unhurried, and took the kettle off the heat. He had two thermoses of coffee in the plane and a bag of bannock Josephine Cardinal's granddaughter had pressed on him at Kettle River two weeks ago, still good. He fetched them without asking permission, because feeding people was also a language, and sometimes it opened doors that questions welded shut.

They ate like it was a religious act. He watched them and did his own inventory. Boots worn but maintained. Hands steady enough after the food went down. And both of them, he noticed, kept orienting themselves the same way—glancing northeast, toward the main camp coordinates, every few minutes, like men checking on a sick relative in another room.

"You're not lost," Sable said quietly. "Lost men don't ration. Lost men don't watch the direction they came from. You walked away from that camp on purpose."

Denholm set down the bannock. For a moment Sable thought he'd get the wall again. Instead the man just looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with his body.

"Pilot. What did they tell you? The man who hired you."

"That six surveyors missed a check-in. That I'm to find them and report their location."

"And then what? What happens when you report us?"

"Rescue, I'd assume."

Denholm smiled, and it was the worst thing Sable had seen in three days. "Yeah. Rescue." He looked at his partner. "Tell him, Okafor."

"Don't—" Okafor started.

"He landed, Danny. He fed us. Either he's the best actor the government ever hired or he genuinely has no idea what he's sitting in the middle of." Denholm turned back. "Here's what I can tell you without getting anyone killed, including you. We found something out here. Something big. And the day we found it, our orders changed—from Ottawa, not from our own chain of command—and the orders stopped being about geology. Four of our team accepted new terms. We didn't. We walked."

"What kind of terms?"

"The kind with money attached." Denholm's jaw worked. "I'll say this much because you'll figure it out anyway: whatever you're being paid to find us, multiply it. Then ask yourself why finding six men with rifles requires that much money."

Sable felt the first crack go through him—not a break, just a hairline, a fault line across the neat strata of his neutrality. Ten years of carrying other people's cargo without opening the boxes. Ten years of *not my business* worn smooth as river stone. And now a starving man in the woods was telling him the box was full of something that made government men change their voices.

"I'm not paid enough to care," Sable said. "I'm paid to fly."

"Good," Denholm said. "Stay that way. Fly us out, take us wherever you were told to take survivors, collect your money, and forget the Kettle River plateau exists. Can you do that?"

"I can do that," Sable said. And discovered, hearing himself say it, that he meant it—which frightened him more than the alternative, because meaning it required not thinking about the words *Kettle River* spoken with that particular weight.

* * *

They broke camp fast, professionals even in collapse—fire drowned, ash scattered, boughs stripped off the tent fly, the site returned to wilderness in twenty minutes. Sable carried the tent roll to the plane and loaded it while Okafor walked the perimeter one last time, erasing them.

It was on the second trip that Sable noticed the third set of tracks.

Faint, days old, boot prints heading south from the campsite into the trees—toward the ridge country, the high ground. Not leaving. Going deeper. He crouched over them and said nothing, and filed them away in the place where he kept things he wasn't ready to think about.

Then they were loading, and the moment arrived the way such moments always do—all at once, after building for hours.

Okafor climbed in first. Settled into the rear seat among the tent rolls, buckled the harness with shaking hands, and almost laughed with relief. Denholm stood at the door with one foot on the strut, and Sable was already running the mental math—weight and balance, fuel, the route south to Fort Providence, Corliss waiting on the strip with his blank paperwork—

Denholm didn't get in.

"Pilot." His voice had changed. Quieter. "There's someone else out here. Our geologist. Mara Quill. She went up onto the ridge the day we split, and she hasn't come down, and she won't—because she's carrying everything. All of it. The samples, the records, the reason." He looked south, toward the tracks Sable had found. "If I fly out of here, she's alone out here with those men, and they know she exists, and eventually they'll come looking. I can't—" His hand tightened on the strut until the knuckles went white. "I can't be the one who leaves her."

"Denholm." Okafor's voice cracked. "Get in the plane. Get in the *plane*. She made her choice, we made ours, we're *starving*, man—"

"I know." Denholm stepped back from the aircraft. Both feet on the ground. "I know what I'm doing."

"You don't! Nobody knows what they're doing out here!"

Sable sat in the pilot's seat with the engine ticking warm and watched a starving man choose to stay starving, and understood—with the clarity that comes right before a life changes shape—that whatever was buried on this plateau, it was heavier than ore. Heavier than money. Heavy enough that a man would rather starve in sight of a rescue than abandon it.

"Denholm," Sable said. "Last chance. Wheels up in sixty seconds."

Denholm put his hand flat on the fuselage, once, like a man touching a coffin or a christening. "Fly him out. Tell them you found two. Tell them the others are dead if you have to—I'd rather be mourned than hunted." He stepped back. "And pilot—if a woman comes down off that ridge someday, standing in the open, waiting for you—"

He stopped. Shook his head.

"Just remember we fed you the truth first. Before anyone else did."

Sable closed the door. Ran up the engine. And sat there for five full seconds with his hand on the throttle, hating himself in advance for what he was about to do, which was exactly what he'd promised to do.

He took off into the gold evening light, climbing away from the draw, and in the mirror of his mind he could still see it: one man walking back toward the trees, and the smoke of a dead fire rising straight up in windless air, marking the spot where his neutrality used to live.

Behind him, Okafor wept silently the whole way south, and Sable flew into the sun, and neither of them spoke, and somewhere on a ridge eleven kilometers northeast a woman named Mara Quill watched his plane dwindle and did not yet know that he was already turning the conversation over in his mind, word by word, like a man counting coins he suspected were counterfeit:

*Multiply it. Then ask yourself why.*

By the time the lights of Fort Providence rose out of the dark ahead, Sable had his answer, and it was the wrong one, and he knew it—and on the strip below, a government truck waited with its headlights on, and a man named Corliss stood beside it with his hands in his pockets, smiling like a man who already knew what Sable was going to say before Sable said it.
