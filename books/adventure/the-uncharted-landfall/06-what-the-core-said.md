## 6. What the Core Said

The ridge at Windy Point had a name older than the survey, older than the treaty, maybe older than the river itself. Mara Quill had been living on it for six days now, in a hollow behind a wind-thrown spruce, eating cold beans and watching the sky the way other people watched a door.

On the seventh morning, the plane came back.

She knew its sound before she saw it—a Cessna 180 with a slightly rough left magneto, a sound she'd catalogued in three days of grid searches before everything went wrong. The pilot flew his lines like a man reading a page: methodical, thorough, incurious. He crossed the plateau at two thousand feet, banked over the empty camp, and turned south without ever looking toward Windy Point.

Mara let out a breath she'd been holding since dawn.

*Not yet,* she thought. *But soon.*

She opened her notebook—the real one, not the one in the official kit—and read the entry she'd written so many times it had worn a groove in her mind.

---

It had started, as these things always did, with a number.

Core sample K-14, pulled from ninety-one metres below the muskeg on the east bank of the Kettle River. Voss had held the split core up to the light in the field tent and gone very quiet, the way he did when he was doing arithmetic he didn't want to share. Chalcopyrite veining through quartzite, thick as a man's wrist. Then K-17. Then K-22. By the fourth day of drilling, the numbers on Mara's assay sheets stopped looking like geology and started looking like a mistake.

They weren't a mistake. They were one of the largest undeveloped copper deposits in the territory, sitting under land that had been walked, trapped, fished, and buried upon by the same families for six generations. Treaty land. Surveyed, signed, and sworn to in 1921, in a hall that smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool, by men who kept their word even when the government didn't keep its.

Voss had radioed Ottawa from the camp on a Tuesday evening. Mara remembered because she'd been washing core boxes in the river and heard only his half of it—short sentences, longer silences, and then a single word repeated twice: *understood. Understood.*

He came out of the tent grey-faced and told them all to sit down.

"The deposit extends beneath the reserve boundary," he said. "Significantly. The eastern third of the ore body is under Kettle River land."

Halloran had whistled low. Priya had asked the obvious question: "So we redraw the line?"

And Voss had looked at her—young, brilliant, twenty-six years old, already thinking like the men who paid them—and said, "That's what they're going to ask us to do."

The order came by floatplane four days later. Not a letter, not a telegram—a man named Pell, carrying a leather folder and a smile you could have landed a Beaver on. He stayed forty minutes. He drank their coffee. He explained, in the gentle tone of a man discussing weather, that the geological interpretation was "preliminary," that boundary determinations involved "multiple datasets," and that the department had every confidence the team would produce a report reflecting "the full complexity of the situation."

Then he laid three envelopes on the folding table. Cash. Enough to buy a house in Yellowknife, or a silence, whichever came cheaper.

"Consider it an honorarium," Pell said, "for the additional interpretive work."

Nobody touched the envelopes while he was in the tent. Nobody moved until the floatplane's drone faded down the valley. Then Halloran picked his up, weighed it in his hand like a man judging a fish, and put it inside his jacket without a word. Priya stared at the table. The Delaneys—brothers, inseparable, hired as drillers—exchanged a look Mara would think about for years afterward: not greed, exactly. Fear wearing greed's clothing.

Voss swept the last envelope off the table and threw it into the cookfire.

"We file what we found," he said. "All of it. Every metre."

"You'll be blacklisted," Halloran said quietly. "All of us. You know how this works, Tom. They don't fire you. They just never call again."

"Then I'll guide fishermen," Voss said. "I've done worse for better money."

Mara hadn't spoken. She'd been doing what she always did—running the data, checking her own work, hunting for the error that would make the world simple again. There wasn't one. She'd run the assays three times. The copper didn't care about anyone's career. It sat under the traplines and the graves and the fish camps like a fact, patient and enormous, waiting to see what kind of people they were.

That night she wrote in her private notebook: *We are nine people and a decision. Watch which way we break.*

---

They broke fast.

Halloran took his envelope into Fort Providence and came back with new boots and a story about "interpretive uncertainty." The Delaneys took theirs and stopped meeting anyone's eyes. Priya refused hers outright, then panicked—Mara watched it happen over two days, the girl going from righteous to terrified as she understood what refusing actually cost—and on the ninth night Priya packed a duffel and walked into the bush toward the river, leaving a note that said only *I'm sorry. I can't be brave enough for this.*

Two others simply vanished between watches, taking the canoe and half the food. Which left Voss, Mara, and a camp full of men pretending the last week hadn't happened.

On the eleventh day, Voss burned the falsified draft maps in the cookfire, told no one where he was going, and disappeared with the satchel of original cores and his field notebooks. That same evening, the team's official radio transmitted its scheduled check-in to Yellowknife—one final, normal-sounding transmission—and then went silent forever. Mara made that call herself. It was the hardest lie she'd ever told, and she'd told it well, and she hated herself with a cleanliness that surprised her.

Because here was the thing she'd understood, alone on her ridge with her cold beans: silence wasn't neutral. Silence was a choice with a price tag, and someone else always paid it. Somewhere downriver there were elders who had signed their names in good faith in 1921, whose grandchildren would wake up one morning inside a mining lease and never know how it happened. Never know that nine people with clipboards had measured their inheritance and sold it by the metre.

She had signaled nothing, told nothing, done nothing—for six days. Watching the little Cessna fly its grid, she'd caught herself hoping the pilot would find nothing, because if he found nothing, she could keep hiding, and hiding was so much easier than the next thing.

The next thing was this: the truth needed a vehicle. And the only vehicle on this plateau had a rough left magneto and a pilot who flew like a man with something to lose.

---

Now, on the seventh morning, Mara packed her notebook, shouldered the pack she'd kept ready since day one, and stood up out of her hollow for the first time in a week.

Below her, smoke rose from a cookfire near the old game trail—two of the others, she guessed, the ones too scared to run far and too ashamed to go home. Let the pilot find them. Let him start asking questions. Men like that, careful men, anonymous men—they carried questions the way other people carried knives.

She started down the ridge, following the drainage toward the plateau, walking at the pace of a woman who had finally decided where she stood.

Behind her, Windy Point kept its wind. Ahead, somewhere in the muskeg, a bush pilot was about to learn that some cargo doesn't fit in a plane unless the pilot changes shape around it first.

She smiled, grimly, at the thought.

He didn't know it yet, but she'd been one step ahead of him since the day he took the contract.
