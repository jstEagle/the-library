## 7. Nine Days of Silence

The tracks told me two things before I ever saw the man who made them.

First: he was dragging his right leg. The left boot punched clean into the muskeg crust; the right one scored a furrow behind it, like someone pulling a plow with half a horse. Second: he was heading nowhere. Every direction from that ridge led to a lake, a river, or a cliff face, and he'd chosen the one line through all three that no living creature would pick unless it had stopped caring whether it arrived.

I'd been following him since dawn. Not on purpose — at first it was just the trail, and then it was the trail plus the things dropped along it. A pencil, snapped in half. A page torn from a notebook, gone soft with dew, the graphite bled into grey ghosts. A tin cup, set upright on a stump, which bothered me more than anything else because you don't set a cup upright when you're dying. You do it when you're leaving something for someone.

Nine days. That's what Corliss had said. Nine days since six surveyors missed their radio check-in, and here was evidence that at least one of them had walked out of the plateau under his own power, alone, injured, and in no hurry to be found by anyone official.

I stood at the edge of an old burn — black spruce skeletons, fireweed coming up purple through the ash — and looked at the cup until the wind moved the fireweed and I got tired of my own thinking.

*You're a supply pilot,* I told myself. *You deliver flour and penicillin and gossip. You don't follow drag-marks into burns.*

But I'd already followed them for four hours, and the Cessna was tied down at the lake shore behind me like a dog that knew better than to bark, and the truth was simpler than my hesitation: I wanted to know what a man carries that makes him walk on a broken ankle instead of waiting beside a radio for rescue.

* * *

The cabin sat in a fold of the burn where the fire had jumped and spared a stand of birch, which is how these places survive — luck, not logic. It was a trapper's cabin, forty years old if it was a day, sod roof sagging, door hanging by one hinge. Smoke had come out of it recently. Not smoke now. Just the smell of it, cold and sour, and a scatter of spruce boughs pulled inside for bedding.

I stood in the doorway with the sun at my back and let my eyes adjust.

He was against the far wall, under the window, wrapped in a surveyor's parka with the government crest half-torn off. Big man gone small — the way fever does, boiling a person down to essentials. Grey beard maybe ten days grown over a jaw that hadn't seen a razor in longer. His right ankle was the size of a football, propped on a rolled sleeping bag, and above it his hands were doing something I'll remember as long as I fly: they were holding a canvas satchel against his chest the way a woman holds a baby. Both arms. Chin tucked over it. Asleep, or close to it, and still guarding.

"Voss," I said.

His eyes opened and there was a bad second where nothing in them recognized the world — fever looking out through a man's face — and then they focused and went flat and hard, and I understood I was looking at the leader of the Kettle River survey team, nine days missing, presumed scattered or dead.

"Who sent you." Not a question. A wall going up.

"Nobody sent me."

"Corliss."

"I don't know any Corliss." Which was true in the way that mattered and false in every way that didn't, but lying is a bush skill like any other and I've kept mine sharp. "Name's Winterbourne. I fly supply runs out of Fort Providence. I found your team's cache three days ago — rifles, tents, radio with the antenna coiled up neat as a Christmas turkey. No tracks out. Then I found your tracks."

"Coiled antenna." He almost laughed. It turned into coughing, deep and wet, and when it passed he was greyer. "Halloran's work. He liked everything tidy."

"You walked out on a broken ankle."

"I crawled first. Walking came later." He shifted and I heard the satchel's contents shift with him — a dense, glassy clink, metal tubes knocking against each other. "How many people know where this cabin is?"

"Nobody. You left a trail a blind man could read, but nobody's on it but me."

"Corliss will find it."

"Corliss doesn't strike me as a man who reads trails himself. He sends people."

Voss looked at me for a long time. Fever makes some men chatty and some men silent, and this one went silent, and in the silence I did what I always do in strange cabins: I read the room. There was a candle stub burned to a puddle. A pot with the ghost of spruce tea. A hatchet by the door with fresh chips around it — he'd dragged wood in, fed the stove, done it crawling. And on the wall, pinned by a knife, a map. Topographic, government issue, the Kettle River plateau quadrant, and across it someone had drawn lines in red grease pencil. Not survey lines. Boundaries. One boundary ran along the river itself, and another ran a mile east of where any map I'd ever flown over put it, and between the two lines someone had written, in a hand pressed hard enough to tear paper:

*THEY LIVE HERE.*

* * *

I got water boiling. He watched me do it without offering to help, which told me he'd spent nine days learning exactly what his body would and wouldn't do, and lifting a pot wasn't on the list. I found willow bark in his kit — he'd prepared it himself, cut and dried, which meant he'd known the ankle was bad from the start and had planned to be useless for a while — and I steeped it strong and gave it to him and he drank like a man who'd stopped tasting things.

"The others," I said. "Your team."

"Gone."

"Gone where?"

"Different directions." He closed his eyes. "We found something in the cores. Something big. And Ottawa didn't want it where we found it, so they drew new lines around it, and the lines went through the Kettle River people's land like it was empty. Like nobody had trapped that river for a hundred years." His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Six of us saw the order. Six of us did six different things with it. That's the whole story. That's all there is."

"And you took the samples and walked into the bush."

"Somebody had to keep them out of Corliss's hands long enough for—" He stopped. Looked at the satchel. Looked at me. "Long enough for what, I don't know anymore. I've had nine days to think about it and I don't know what I thought a bush full of rock cores was going to accomplish."

"It accomplished keeping them out of Corliss's hands."

"For now. He has a charter plane and I have a broken ankle and a trapper's stove." He laughed again, properly this time, short and bitter. "You know what the funny part is? The samples aren't even necessary. The data's in the notebooks. The notebooks are filed. Sealed, official, sitting in a cabinet in Yellowknife with a government stamp on them, because I'm a good little civil servant and I did the paperwork before I ran." His eyes were bright now, fever-bright, and fixed on me. "The samples are just proof. Physical, hold-it-in-your-hand proof. For people who don't read reports."

"Which people?"

"The elders." He said it simply, and it landed simply, the way heavy things do. "Josephine Cardinal. Her grandfather trapped the east bank before the treaty was worth the paper. If the cores reach her, she can hold them up in front of whatever inquiry eventually happens and say: this is what's under our land, and this is what they tried to pretend wasn't. But if the cores reach Corliss—"

"They never reach anyone. Because there's no inquiry without them."

"Now you're learning the shape of it." He let his head back against the log wall. "Pilot. Winterbourne. Why are you here? Really. Nobody follows drag-marks for free."

I crouched by the stove and fed it a stick of birch and thought about the honest answer, which was that I didn't have one yet. I'd spent ten years building a life with no hooks in it — no debts but the plane, no loyalties but the run, no opinions loud enough to cost me anything. And here was a man offering me the biggest hook in the Northwest Territories, baited with a broken ankle and a bag of rocks.

"I flew a search pattern for nine days," I said. "Grid squares. Empty coordinates. And every night I landed wondering why a team of six trained surveyors vanished so cleanly. Today I found out they didn't vanish. They scattered. On purpose. Because of what they found." I looked at him. "I hate not knowing things. It's the only honest flaw I've got."

"That's not a reason. That's a curiosity."

"It's the only kind of reason I've ever had. Curiosity, then consequences."

He studied me the way you study weather — not deciding whether to trust it, just deciding what it was going to do to you. Then the fever took him again. I watched it happen: the focus sliding off his eyes, the shiver starting in his shoulders and working down. He fought it for a minute, talking through it, words coming apart at the seams.

"Satchel," he said. "Listen. Listen to me."

"I'm listening."

"If I die here — and men die of ankles, pilot, septicemia, it takes three days, I've had nine — if I die, the satchel goes to Josephine Cardinal. Kettle River. Not the RCMP. Not Yellowknife. Not the journalist you're already thinking about — yes, I can see you thinking about it, everyone thinks about journalists, journalists are the easy answer." His hand came off the satchel and closed on my wrist, and even sick he was strong, grip like a winch. "The elders. First. Before anyone. Swear it."

"Voss—"

"*Swear it.*" And then, quieter, the fever winning, almost gentle: "They're the only ones it belongs to. Everything else is theft wearing a lanyard."

Here's the thing about oaths. I'd given maybe three in my life — a marriage, a lease, a promise to a dying elder up the Mackenzie that I'd bring her granddaughter home from school, which I kept, because it was easy. Oaths are easy when you give them in a warm cabin to a feverish man who might not live to collect. They cost nothing at the moment of giving. All the cost comes later, itemized, with interest, and nobody shows you the invoice up front.

I could have said *sure, old man, rest easy*, the way you humor drunks and children, meaning nothing. I felt the temptation of it, smooth and cheap as a bribe.

Instead I said: "If you die, the satchel goes to Josephine Cardinal at Kettle River, first, before anyone else. I swear it."

Something went out of him when I said it. Not tension — something deeper, structural, like a keel finally settling. He let go of my wrist and slid down the wall and was asleep inside a minute, breathing fast and shallow, the satchel rising and falling on his chest.

* * *

I worked until dark.

Splinted the ankle properly — his field splint was a heroic piece of improvisation built from a tent pole and duct tape, but it had rubbed through skin at the shin and the smell coming off it wasn't willow bark. I cleaned it with boiled water and the last of his iodine, and he woke once during it and didn't make a sound, just gripped the sleeping bag and sweated, and I decided that whatever else Voss was — idealist, fool, traitor to his paymasters, take your pick — he wasn't a coward.

While he slept I read the map on the wall. All of it. The red boundaries, the hard-pressed handwriting, and underneath, smaller, a list in pencil: dates, core numbers, depths. Copper figures. I don't know ore from gravel, but I know numbers, and these numbers repeated in a way that meant *a lot* — the same rich figure again and again down a line a mile wide, running straight through ground that some clerk in Ottawa had recently declared unoccupied.

Unoccupied. I'd flown over that valley a hundred times. I'd waved at fish camps on its banks. I'd hauled a crib frame to the Cardinal place two springs ago and gotten paid in smoked whitefish and a story about a moose.

*They live here,* the map said, in a dead man's handwriting, if it came to that.

I banked the stove, wedged the door shut with the hatchet handle, and stepped outside into the blue cold. Stars in the burn-gap, hard and close. My breath hanging. Somewhere south, a charter plane with Corliss's money in it, and somewhere north, a community that didn't know yet what was being drawn around it in grease pencil.

I'd sworn an oath today. Given too easily, in a smoky cabin, to a stranger. I could feel it already, sitting in my chest like ballast — weight I hadn't carried yesterday, weight that changed how the whole aircraft handled.

Behind me, inside, Voss started talking in his sleep. Names, mostly. Halloran. Mara Quill. Coordinates, half-formed. And once, clearly, twice:

*"Don't tell the pilot. Don't tell the pilot anything."*

I stood in the dark a long moment, listening to a man warn me, in his delirium, against himself.

Then I went back in, added wood to the stove, and started planning a flight to Kettle River — because whatever Voss feared I'd do with what I knew, I'd already decided the opposite, and the only thing worse than an oath given too easily is one you break while the man who holds it is still breathing.
