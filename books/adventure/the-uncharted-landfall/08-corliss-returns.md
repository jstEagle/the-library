## 8. Corliss Returns

The strip at Fort Providence was empty when I came in low over the river, which should have been my first warning. Empty strips make pilots nervous the way empty streets make women walking home nervous — nothing wrong, technically, just an absence where presence belongs.

I flared the 180 onto the gravel and rolled out long, letting her bleed speed at her own pace, because there was no reason to hurry and every reason to keep my hands busy. Three days of grid search behind me. Two half-starved surveyors who wouldn't board. A cookfire on a plateau where no one should be. And somewhere in the back of my skull, the image of that radio with its antenna coiled neat as a Sunday napkin.

Nobody coils an antenna after a disaster.

I taxied to my usual spot by the fuel drums and shut her down, and that's when I saw the truck. Black, government-plate, parked in the shade of the equipment shed with the patience of something that had been there a while. Corliss stepped out of it before my prop stopped turning, which told me he'd been watching me land. He was wearing the same grey suit from Yellowknife, pressed despite nine days of northern dust, and he had two paper cups of coffee balanced in one hand like this was a chance meeting at a hockey rink.

"Sable Winterbourne." He said my name the way a man reads a serial number off an engine block. "You look like hell."

"Three days of flying grids." I climbed down and didn't take the coffee. "Your surveyors are still missing."

"Mm." He set both cups on the wing strut anyway, a small offering I was meant to refuse, and I understood the move for what it was. Everything Corliss did was a small offering you were meant to refuse, so that refusing felt like your idea. "Walk with me."

"I've got cargo to unload."

"It can wait five minutes." He started toward the riverbank without checking whether I followed, which was either confidence or contempt, and with Corliss the two were hard to tell apart. I followed. That was the trap working exactly as designed.

We stood at the edge of the strip where the gravel gave way to sedge grass going gold at the tips, and below us the Mackenzie ran wide and brown and indifferent, carrying everything north whether it wanted to go or not.

"Nine days," Corliss said. "Six men miss their check-in, and the department sends one bush pilot in a twenty-year-old Cessna instead of a proper search-and-rescue operation. You ever wonder about that?"

"Figured budget season."

"Budget season." He smiled at the river. "Sure. Or maybe nobody upstairs is in a hurry to find six men who might have found something they weren't supposed to be looking for yet."

My hands went still on the fence rail. Not much. Just still. But I'd spent ten years learning to read weather and men, and I knew what a man looked like when he was watching you decide something.

"You want to tell me what that means?"

"It means the contract's changed." He turned and looked at me straight, and his eyes were pale and pleasant and completely empty, like windows in a house where nobody lived. "New orders. There's a charter coming through Thursday — mining company, private outfit, very well funded. They need someone who knows the plateau to ferry their people to specific coordinates. Your coordinates. The ones you logged during your search pattern."

"I haven't filed a search log."

"Everyone files a search log, Sable. It's regulation." He said it gently, like correcting a child on the spelling of a word the child had invented. "Grid four, sector seven. You flew it twice. Low enough to see the cookfire smoke."

The river made its noise underneath us. Somewhere behind us a raven said something ugly to another raven and got no answer.

"Those coordinates are part of a government search," I said. "They're not for sale to some mining charter."

"Everything's for sale. That's not cynicism, that's bookkeeping." He reached into his jacket and produced an envelope, thick, unsealed, and laid it on the fence post between us without ceremony. "That's the first installment. There's more where it came from, and here's the better part." He tapped the post once. "Your plane. The debt on it. Forty-one thousand dollars to the Northern Air Finance Corporation, am I close? Don't answer, I'm always close. As of Thursday morning, if you fly the charter, that debt doesn't exist anymore. Paid in full, quietly, no paperwork trail anyone will ever pull. You'll own that Cessna outright for the first time since you bought her."

I looked at the envelope. I looked at the river. I did the arithmetic every bush pilot does at three in the morning when the engine coughs — parts, fuel, insurance, the winter overhaul coming due like a tax collector — and then I did the other arithmetic, the kind that doesn't fit in columns.

"What happened to finding them?" I asked. "Six men. Families waiting in Yellowknife. Wives calling the department every day."

"The families are being handled."

"Handled."

"Informed. Counselled. Given appropriate updates." Each word placed like a stone in a wall. "The search phase is concluding. What comes next is a resource matter, and resource matters require discretion. You're a discreet man, Sable. Ten years flying medicine and mail across half the territory and not one story sold, not one name dropped, not one photograph. You're famous for being unknown. It's practically a professional credential."

He picked up one of the coffee cups and held it out again, and this time I took it, mostly so my hands would have something to do besides shake him or hit him. It was good coffee. That annoyed me.

"There's a condition on the new contract," Corliss said, and here his voice changed — not louder, not harder, just closer, the way a hand moves closer before it closes. "Complete the old one first. File your final search report. Standard closure documentation. And in that report, you'll note that your grid searches covered the full assigned area and found no evidence of the survey team."

"I found gear. Rifles, tents, a radio—"

"You found scattered debris consistent with a party dispersing under unknown circumstances." He was watching me over the rim of his cup. "Write it that way and it costs you nothing. Write it any other way and—" He paused, and the pause was the whole message. "Well. Contracts get complicated. Planes get impounded. Pilots get audited. You'd be amazed how much trouble a man can have with a perfectly legal aircraft when the right office decides to look at it sideways."

There it was. Not a threat — Corliss hadn't threatened me once, not with a single word, and somehow that was worse than if he had. A threat you could fight. This was a door being shown to you, and behind the door, a room, and in the room, everything you owned arranged neatly, waiting to be taken apart bolt by bolt.

"And if I just fly home and forget the whole thing?" I asked. "No charter, no report, no envelope?"

"Then you're a man who walked away from paid work mid-contract, and the finance corporation hears about it from a concerned government partner, and your plane gets called in for inspection, and inspections take months, and months cost money you don't have." He finished his coffee and set the cup upside down on the fence post, precise as a surgeon. "Sable. Look at me."

I looked at him.

"I'm not the villain of this piece. I know how it looks from where you stand, but I'm a middleman. Somebody found something on that plateau, and somebody above me wants it handled, and the handling requires aircraft and pilots and quiet. If it isn't me, it'll be someone less patient. I'm offering you the soft version. Take the envelope, fly Thursday, file the report, and in a month you'll be running your medicine runs with a clean title in your pocket and this whole summer will be a bad dream you got paid to have."

For a moment — I'll say it plainly, because lying to myself is how men like Halloran end up — for a moment I wanted it. Forty-one thousand dollars. The 180 mine free and clear. Ten more years of the life I'd built, measured in miles, safe and anonymous, the map unrolling under me every morning with nothing on it but weather and distance. All I had to do was write six men into a report as scattered debris and hand a mining crew a map to whatever it was they'd walked away from.

All I had to do was stop caring.

"That's a lot of money," I said.

"It's a fair price for a fair service."

"And the surveyors? The ones who are alive?"

Corliss looked at me for a long moment, and something moved behind the empty windows — not warmth, but interest, the way a man looks at a lock he hasn't decided whether to pick or break.

"You know," he said, "most pilots would have taken the envelope by now. Most pilots would have taken it at Yellowknife. You keep asking questions like a man who's already seen something." He let that sit. Then, lightly, tossing it away like it was nothing, like it was a remark about the weather: "Speaking of which — the satchel. Voss carried it everywhere, didn't he? Even into the bush, we hear. Funny thing for a geologist, hauling rock samples around like a baby. Heavy little thing, a satchel like that. Hard to hide. Easy to find, if a person knew where to look."

The coffee went cold in my hand all at once, though the morning was warm.

I hadn't mentioned Voss. I hadn't mentioned a satchel. The two surveyors on the plateau hadn't given me a name, and even if they had, I'd said nothing to anyone but the wind between here and there. Which left exactly one place Corliss could have gotten the shape of it: whoever was watching the plateau had watched me land there, watched me walk into the trees toward that cabin, watched me come out.

Or was still watching.

"How do you know about a satchel?" I asked, and I kept my voice flat, pilot-flat, the voice that doesn't carry past the cockpit.

"Know about what?" Corliss smiled, and it was the friendliest thing I'd seen all day, and it scared me more than anything else he'd done. "I'm describing hypotheticals, Sable. IF a satchel existed, and IF a certain pilot had stumbled across it, then that pilot should understand that samples are department property. Recovering them would be a civic duty. Keeping them would be theft of government assets, and theft changes the whole flavour of a man's situation." He pushed off the fence post and straightened his jacket. "But there's no satchel. You haven't seen one. Neither have I. Isn't that cleaner?"

"Cleaner," I said.

"Much." He walked two steps toward the truck, then stopped, half-turned, and gave me the smile again over his shoulder. "Thursday. Coordinates to the charter. Report filed by Friday. Debt gone by Monday. And Sable —" he tapped his temple, twice, a little salute — "whatever you think you know, remember that knowing things is only valuable if you're the only one who knows them. The moment knowledge gets shared, it becomes evidence. And evidence has a way of getting people hurt. Ask Voss."

He got in the truck. It pulled away down the strip in a long rooster tail of dust, and I stood there holding a cold cup of coffee in one hand and looking at an envelope full of hush money sitting on a fence post in the sun, and I did not touch it.

* * *

I unloaded the medicine crates alone, which was normal, and checked my tie-downs, which was habit, and then sat in the 180's pilot seat with the door open and my boots on the gravel and thought about Voss.

Feverish. Broken ankle wrapped in birch bark and duct tape. Nine days alone in a trapper's cabin with a satchel of core samples hugged to his chest like the last book off a burning ship, and when the fever broke enough for words, he'd grabbed my sleeve with fingers like bird claws and made me swear — swear, out loud, hand on the satchel — that if he died, the samples went to the Kettle River elders. Not Ottawa. Not the department. The elders.

I'd sworn easily. That was the thing about oaths given in a cabin that smelled of mouse droppings and kerosene — they come cheap in the moment and cost everything later. I'd hidden the satchel under the tarp in the aft compartment, under the spare oil and the emergency kit, and flown him to the nursing station at Providence, and told the nurse he was a prospector with a bad ankle and no name worth writing down, and she'd written *unnamed male, forties* in her ledger and asked no questions, because northern nurses learn early which questions don't help anybody.

And now Corliss knew the satchel existed. Knew it casually, hypothetically, the way a man knows a card is in your hand without seeing it — because someone told him, or someone's been reading my tracks since the plateau.

Which meant the cabin wasn't safe. Which meant the nursing station ledger wasn't safe. Which meant Voss himself, feverish and unnamed in a cot by the window, was a loose end that men like Corliss tied off, and I'd just spent forty minutes letting the man who'd do the tying watch me do arithmetic on my face.

I got out of the plane and walked to the pay phone bolted to the side of the Hudson's Bay post, fed it a quarter, and called the nursing station.

"This is Sable. Is the prospector still there?"

"Flew out yesterday," the nurse said. "His brother came for him. Big fellow, quiet. Signed him out around noon."

"Did he now."

"Family's family," she said, and hung up, and I stood there with the receiver warm against my ear doing the math I didn't want to do. Voss couldn't walk on that ankle. Couldn't drive. Whoever took him had carried him, and there weren't many big quiet fellows in the territory who collected feverish geologists from nursing stations within a day of Corliss saying the word *satchel* out loud.

Either Voss had a brother I'd never heard of, or the loose end was already being tied, and the satchel — my oath, my cargo, the whole rotten weight of it — had gone wherever big quiet fellows take things they've been sent to collect.

I hung up the phone. Down the strip, the black truck was gone, but the envelope still sat on the fence post where Corliss had left it, fat and patient, catching the light.

Behind me, from the treeline at the far end of the field, where the sedge met the spruce and the shadows went deep — a movement. Small. Deliberate. Someone standing at the edge of the trees, not hiding anymore, watching me the way you'd watch a man decide whether to pick up money or burn it.

A woman. Slim, sunburned, wearing the ruined remains of government-issue field clothes, standing in the treeline with her arms crossed like she'd been there the whole time.

Like she'd heard every word.

She lifted one hand — not a wave, exactly. More like an opening bid.

And I understood, with the particular clarity of a man whose engine has just quit over open water, that I had never once been ahead of anything that happened on this plateau.
