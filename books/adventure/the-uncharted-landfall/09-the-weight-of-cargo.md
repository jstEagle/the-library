## 9. The Weight of Cargo

Mara had watched him for three days before she decided to let him see her.

That was the arithmetic of it, anyway. Three days of Sable Winterbourne flying his grid over the plateau in that battered Cessna 180, patient as a man mowing a lawn the size of a country. She'd watched from Windy Point with binoculars salvaged from Voss's kit, wrapped in the tarp she'd cut down from their abandoned mess tent, eating cold beans and learning the rhythm of him. He flew the same pattern every morning. He drank coffee from a dented thermos on the strut between legs. He never once looked at the ridge where she crouched—which was either carelessness or discipline, and by the second day she'd bet her last matches on discipline.

Nobody stumbled into three days of not looking at something. You had to *not look* on purpose.

So this morning, when he lifted off from the lake at the bottom of the valley and banked into his first leg, Mara came down off the ridge.

She'd planned it like a core sample: layers, each one revealing only what the layer above it allowed. First the smoke—she'd built it small and green, birch boughs on coals, a thread of white that would read as a signal to anyone trained to read one and as nothing to anyone else. Then herself, standing in the open at the edge of the burn, arms loose, no rifle. A woman alone in eleven thousand square kilometres of empty-looking country was either a corpse walking or a message, and Sable Winterbourne would have to land to find out which.

He circled twice. She counted. On the third pass he waggled his wings—the bush pilot's shrug, *I see you, I'm thinking*—and then he turned downwind and set up an approach to the gravel bar below her, long and flat and textbook, except for the way he flared early, keeping his tailwheel light, reading the surface through the tires before committing weight to it. A careful man's landing. A man who'd been burned before, or expected to be.

She walked down to meet him while the prop was still turning. Never let a pilot shut down; a running engine is a running option, and she wanted him feeling like he had one.

"You're a long way from your camp," he said through the window. His voice was flat, unhurried, the voice of a man who asked questions he already knew answers to—or thought he did.

"My camp is nine days' walk northeast," she said. "Or it was."

"And now?"

"Now it's wherever you're going."

Something moved behind his eyes—not surprise exactly. Recalculation. She watched him do it, watch him take her apart and reassemble her as a different problem than the one he'd flown in expecting. Good. Let him. The whole point was to be more complicated than the version Corliss had sold him.

"Six surveyors went quiet," he said. "Ottawa's paying me to find them. I've found gear. A radio with its antenna coiled up neat as sewing. No tracks out." He looked at her hands, her boots, the tarp bundle on her shoulder. "And now you."

"Congratulations," Mara said. "You've found one."

"One what?"

"One surveyor who can walk and talk. There were six of us. Do the math on your own time—I'm not doing it standing in your prop wash."

He killed the engine then, which surprised her. She'd expected him to keep it idling, keep his exit warm. Instead he swung out of the plane, unhurried, and stood there in his oil-stained canvas jacket looking at her with the particular patience of a man who has spent ten years listening to people lie to him in remote places and has developed a taste for it.

"Sable Winterbourne," he said, and didn't offer a hand.

"Mara Quill. Geologist." She let that sit. "You're the pilot they hired because you know this basin better than the government maps do. Which is funny, if you think about it."

"Why's that?"

"Because the government maps are wrong. On purpose."

* * *

She made him build the fire up under the bluff, out of sight of the sky, and she watched how he did it—small, fast, smokeless, a man who'd spent a decade not being seen—and while he worked she laid out her half-truths like cards she intended to lose on purpose.

"We found copper," she said. "A lot of it. Porphyry-style, the kind that makes companies weep into their prospectuses. Under the plateau." She fed a stick to the fire. "Voss radioed Ottawa the day we confirmed grades. Nine days later our resupply flight didn't come, our radio check-ins started getting answered by someone who wasn't our project manager, and Halloran found a second set of field maps in the supply crate that nobody had ordered."

"What kind of wrong?"

"The kind where the deposit sits on Crown land instead of treaty land. The kind where the Kettle River community's traplines get redrawn into 'unoccupied.'" She glanced at him. "You fly medicine to those people. Don't tell me you don't know whose ground that is."

"I know whose ground it is," Sable said. Careful. Neutral. A man holding a coin he hadn't flipped yet.

"So does Ottawa. That's the point."

He was quiet for a while. The fire ticked. Somewhere above the bluff a raven said something rude and moved on.

"Corliss," he said finally. "The man who hired me. His paperwork had holes in it big enough to fly the plane through. Triple rate, cash advance, no company letterhead worth trusting." He looked at her across the flames. "You knew he'd hire a local pilot. You knew he'd need one."

"I guessed," Mara said. "Corliss doesn't do anything subtle, but he does everything thorough. A search needs a plane. A plane needs a pilot who won't ask questions, or one who will and can be handled." She held his gaze. "Which are you?"

"That depends on the question."

"The question is whether you'll fly me to Yellowknife with a satchel of core samples and a set of original field notebooks, and whether you'll keep your mouth shut about it until they're somewhere Corliss can't burn them."

There it was. Laid on the table between them like a rock sample: heavy, ugly, worth more than it looked.

Sable didn't answer right away. He got up, walked back to the plane, and came back with his thermos and two tin cups, and poured coffee into both, and handed her one, and that small domestic gesture told her more than anything he'd said. A man shares his coffee when he's decided to listen longer. Or when he's deciding how much you're worth.

"Here's my problem," he said. "Nine days ago I was a supply pilot. Medicine, mail, gossip. I mind my own business because minding my own business is how I've stayed alive and free up here. Yesterday I found two of your colleagues starving next to a cookfire, and they begged me to fly them out and then refused to get in the plane. Today you walk out of the bush asking me to commit a crime against whoever owns that satchel you haven't shown me yet." He sipped. "Every direction I turn, somebody wants me to stop being neutral. I liked neutral. Neutral had good fuel economy."

"And yet," Mara said, "you landed."

He looked at her.

"You saw my fire. You could have logged the coordinates, radioed it in, gone home to Fort Providence and collected your fee for finding one survivor. Instead you put a careful man's landing on a gravel bar and walked up to a stranger with no rifle." She leaned forward. "You want to know why I'm one step ahead of you, Mr. Winterbourne? Because I've been watching you since chapter one. Since your first grid run. And in three days you never once looked at Windy Point—but you also never once reported the gear cache at coordinates you'd already photographed. Your camera pod was mounted for those runs. I checked the film logic. You've got pictures of that cache sitting in that plane right now, and Corliss doesn't have them."

The silence stretched long enough that she heard her own pulse in it.

"You went through my plane?" he said, and there was something underneath the flatness—not anger. Interest.

"I went through your *flight plan*," she said. "Your plane I'm respecting. For now."

He almost smiled. It was a small thing, quickly folded away, but she caught it, and she understood then that she'd made her real mistake—not showing him the maps, not naming Corliss, but making him curious. Curiosity was the one cargo a man like Sable Winterbourne couldn't refuse to carry.

"Show me," he said.

* * *

She unrolled the maps on the flat top of his supply crate, weighting the corners with stones, and watched his face while he read them. He read like a pilot reads weather—fast, skeptical, hunting the lie in it. His finger stopped on the boundary line where it jogged east around the river bend, cutting out the Cardinal family's trapline, and stayed there.

"This line," he said. "It's drawn different on the official survey. I've seen the published version posted at the trading post. It runs straight through here."

"It runs straight through here in the original field books too. This—" she tapped the falsified overlay, "—was drafted in Yellowknife two weeks after Voss radioed Ottawa. Same drafting office, same sheet numbers, different truth. Six men saw the originals. Two took money to forget them. One ran. Voss kept the samples. I kept these." She rolled the map closed. "And you, Mr. Winterbourne, are the only licensed pilot within four hundred miles who isn't already bought."

"That's a hell of a job description."

"It's the only honest one on offer."

He stood and walked to the edge of the bluff and looked north, toward the plateau, toward the strip at Fort Providence where a government man with clean fingernails was presumably waiting with paperwork full of blanks. She gave him the time. Men like him didn't decide in conversation; they decided in silence, looking at distance.

When he turned around, his face had settled into something she recognized from mirrors—nothing given away, everything calculated.

"Here's what I can't figure," he said. "Why come to me at all? You've had nine days. You could have walked out to the highway, flagged a truck, been in Yellowknife inside a week. Instead you sat on a ridge watching me fly circles, waiting. That's not desperation. That's selection." He came back to the fire and crouched, forearms on knees, close enough that she could smell woodsmoke and avgas on him. "So tell me the part you're still holding back. Because there's a part. There's always a part."

Mara considered lying. Considered it seriously, the way you consider jumping a crevasse—measuring, respectful, aware of the cost of getting it wrong.

Then she told him the true thing instead.

"They're not looking for us to bring us home," she said. "Halloran figured it out before he took the money—that's partly why he took it, I think. Cowards buy insurance. The charter Corliss is arranging isn't a mining company. It's enforcement. Our disappearance was supposed to become permanent once the search officially failed. You weren't hired to find us, Sable. You were hired to *not find us*, publicly, so the file could close." She let that land. "And the moment you report finding me, you stop being useful and start being a witness. Witnesses go in lakes up here. Ask anybody."

He didn't flinch. But his hand, resting on his knee, went very still, the way a man's hand goes still when the ground under him has quietly changed category.

"So I'm already in it," he said.

"You were in it the day you signed Corliss's contract. You just hadn't read the fine print yet." She stood, brushing grit from her knees, and offered him the last card, face up. "I need your plane and your silence. Not your heroics. Not your politics. Just fuel, hours, and a locked mouth. In exchange you get the truth, which is worthless, and the chance to not spend the rest of your life knowing you flew for the other side. I know what that trade costs. I made it myself, in reverse, nine days ago."

Sable looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed—one short, dry bark, no humor in it, all recognition.

"You know what I did when Corliss offered me triple rate?" he said. "I hesitated. Out loud. Because I wanted him to think I could be bought slow. Bought-slow pilots get handled gently." He shook his head. "And you sat on a ridge for three days watching me not-look at you, timing my coffee breaks. We're the same animal, Ms. Quill. You just den in rocks and I den in aluminum."

"Two liars," she said. "Recognizing each other."

"Is that what we're doing?"

"That's what we're doing."

He looked at the plane, then at the north, then at her, and she watched him arrive at it—not the decision itself, which would come later, in the dark, with the engine cold and the choice sharp—but the understanding that neutrality had died somewhere between the gravel bar and the fire, and he'd attended the funeral without noticing.

"Yellowknife's eight hundred miles," he said. "Weather window's closing by Thursday. I've got maybe enough fuel for the first leg if I don't tell anyone where I'm going, which means I can't file a flight plan, which means if we go down, nobody looks."

"Nobody would have looked anyway," Mara said. "That was the whole design."

"Yeah." He kicked dirt over the fire, methodical, thorough, a man erasing himself. "One condition. The satchel, the notebooks, whatever else you're carrying—they ride in the back, strapped, and I hold the keys to nothing. If Corliss's people stop us, you tell them I was flying you out under protest and you pulled a gun. Can you pull a gun convincingly?"

"I grew up with three brothers."

"Good answer." He was already moving toward the plane, checking the tie-downs, and over his shoulder he said it, casual as a fuel check: "Oh—and the two you sent to that cookfire? The ones who wouldn't board? They told me you'd come. Said you'd find me before I found you." He paused, prop blade under his palm. "They also said to tell you Voss is hurt. Broken ankle, fever, holed up in an old trapper's cabin up the Grayling. Guarding something like it's the ark."

Mara felt the ground tilt, very slightly, the way it does when a map turns out to be drawn wrong.

"He's alive," she said.

"He's alive and losing the race," Sable said. "So here's my question, geologist. Do we fly to Yellowknife with what we've got—or do we go get the rest of it first?"

Above the bluff, the raven again, louder this time, like the country itself demanding an answer.

Mara looked north, where the Grayling bent through the black spruce toward a cabin she'd passed a hundred times on maps and never once on foot, and understood that the man beside her had just done to her what she'd spent three days doing to him: laid a fork in the road and waited, smiling inside, to see which way she'd choose.

"Get the rest of it first," she said. "We were always going to."

Sable nodded slowly, and for the first time since she'd come down off the ridge, he looked like a man flying toward something instead of away from it.

"Grayling cabin, then," he said. "Hope you don't mind a rough strip. I don't land pretty twice in one day."

He was already loading the crate. Mara stood a moment longer, looking at the flattened grass where the fire had been, at the stones that had weighted down the truth, and thought: *he erased the fire before he agreed to anything.* A careful man. A liar. Hers, now, God help them both.

Somewhere south, a charter was fueling. Somewhere north, a feverish man was guarding a satchel like a relic. And between them, eight hundred miles of bad weather and worse intentions, with her and a bush pilot in the middle of it, carrying the only cargo in the territory that mattered.

She picked up her tarp bundle and walked to the plane.
