## 10. Fuel and Fire

The smell woke me before the light did.

I'd been sleeping in the plane again—seats folded flat, sleeping bag zipped to my chin against a night that had turned mean in September's first week—and I came up out of a dream about nothing important with my nose full of it: aviation fuel and smoke and something underneath both, something sweeter and wrong, like burnt sugar. For one stupid second I thought I was dreaming still. Then I saw the orange glow through the windshield, reflected off the underside of the clouds, and I was moving before my boots were on.

The strip at Fort Providence is a gravel scar through black spruce, half a mile of nothing with a windsock that hangs dead more often than not. My cache sat two hundred yards down from where I'd parked the Cessna—a government-surplus steel drum shack I'd built myself, corrugated roof, padlock I'd never trusted. It was burning like a church.

Not just burning. *Roaring.* Fifty-five-gallon drums of avgas don't burn quiet. They cook until the seams let go and then they jump, and I could hear them going off inside the fireball like artillery, one, two, a third, each detonation slapping heat across my face from a hundred feet away. The spruce at the edge of the clearing had caught. Embers were climbing into the dark in slow spirals, beautiful and obscene, and somewhere behind me a dog started up, and then another, and the whole settlement would be awake soon.

I stood there doing arithmetic I didn't want to do.

Two drums of 100LL. A drum of stove oil. My spare parts, my tools, the tarp-covered crate of survival gear I'd carried for ten years and never once needed—the flares, the rations, the .30-06 wrapped in wax paper. All of it going up in a column of light you could probably read by in Yellowknife.

And then the other number, the one that mattered: without that fuel, I wasn't a pilot. I was a man sitting in an expensive aluminum lawn chair.

* * *

By dawn the fire had burned itself down to a black wound in the gravel and the settlement had done what settlements do, which is gather and look. Old Marcel Toussaint brought coffee in a dented percolator and stood beside me saying nothing, which from Marcel was a eulogy. The RCMP detachment for this stretch of the Mackenzie was a corporal named Dyson who flew in twice a month, and he wouldn't be here for three days, so the investigation consisted of me walking the ashes while the sun came up red through the smoke haze.

Fires tell stories if you know how to listen. I'd put out enough engine blazes and seen enough lightning strikes to read the language. This one had been started deliberately and started *well*—accelerant poured along the windward side, ignition point low, timed for a night with no moon. Professional, or close to it. And here was the detail that kept snagging in my head like a burr: whoever did it had left the Cessna alone.

Think about that. Two hundred yards away sat an aircraft worth more than everything in the cache combined, parked in the open, unguarded, with a man asleep in it who hadn't stirred when they walked past carrying jerry cans. If Corliss wanted me grounded, the clean move was the plane. Slash the tires—no. Drain the oil. Put a wrench through the cowling, sugar in the tanks, cut the control cables where nobody'd find it until four thousand feet. Any mechanic's kid knows a dozen ways to kill an airplane quietly. Instead they'd torched the fuel and left the machine intact, and they'd done it loud enough to wake the whole river.

That wasn't sabotage. That was a message with a signature missing.

Or so I thought, until I found the boot prints.

They'd come in from the bush side, away from the settlement road—two sets, one heavy, one lighter, pressed deep in the soft ground where the firefighting bucket-line had trampled everything else. But back near the tree line, where the ground stayed dry, the lighter set had stepped in a patch of grey ash from somebody's old campfire, months old, and left a print sharp as a photograph. Eleven inches. Vibram sole, the heel worn on the outside edge. A man who walked toe-out, rolling off his heels.

I knew that walk. I'd watched it for three days on the plateau, coming toward me out of the willows with his hands raised and his ribs showing through his shirt. The younger of the two surveyors. The one who'd begged me to fly him out and then, standing at the door of my plane with the engine running, changed his mind like a man stepping back from a cliff.

Halloran. That was the name Mara Quill had finally given me, two nights ago, in the low voice she used for things that cost her. One of the bought ones. One of the men who'd taken Ottawa's money and signed their names to a lie about where the copper ended and whose land it sat under.

I crouched over that print for a long time while Marcel's coffee went cold in my hand.

Why? Why would Halloran burn my fuel? He'd had his chance to leave on my airplane and turned it down, and now he was sneaking around the strip at night destroying the only thing keeping me airborne? Unless—

Unless he wasn't trying to ground me. Unless he was trying to keep me *from flying somewhere.* Somewhere specific. Somewhere I'd talked about going.

And there was only one place I'd talked about going. I'd said it out loud, at the cookfire on the plateau, and I'd said it again to Mara at Windy Point, and I'd said it a third time to Voss in the fever-dark of the trapper's cabin with the man's fingers locked around my wrist: *Kettle River. The samples go to the elders at Kettle River.*

Halloran had been listening. Or someone had been listening through Halloran.

* * *

Mara found me at noon.

I heard her before I saw her, because that was Mara—she moved through brush like weather, all patience, no hurry, but she always let you hear her eventually. A courtesy, or a test. She came out of the spruce on the far side of the burned cache and stood looking at the wreckage with her hands jammed in the pockets of a canvas coat too thin for the season, and her face did something complicated and then settled on nothing at all.

"Corliss?" she said.

"That's what I thought at three this morning." I kicked a fused lump of drum steel. It rang dully. "It's not."

She waited. She was good at waiting. I'd learned that about her the way you learn the shape of a current—by being pulled.

"Boot print," I said. "Size eleven, heel worn outside. Walks toe-out." I looked at her. "Your friend Halloran."

Something moved behind her eyes—not surprise. Confirmation. That bothered me more than the fire had.

"You're not shocked," I said.

"I'm not surprised." She crouched, studied the ash print herself, touched its edge without disturbing it. "Sable. When the team split, we didn't all split the same way. Voss took the samples and ran north. I took the field notes and went to ground. And Halloran—" she straightened, brushed ash off her fingers like she was brushing off the memory too, "—Halloran took the money and then couldn't live with it. He's been circling ever since. Drunk most of the time. Terrified all of the time. He thinks Corliss's people are going to tidy up loose ends, and he knows he's a loose end."

"So he burns my fuel."

"He burns your fuel because he heard you say Kettle River, and he panicked. Because if you fly those samples to Josephine Cardinal, and the elders go public, then Corliss's employers stop paying hush money and start paying for silence of the permanent kind. Every man who signed that falsified survey becomes a liability instead of an investment." She met my eyes. "Including him. Especially him. He's not smart enough to see that burning your cache doesn't protect him. He's just scared enough to try."

I turned that over. It fit the evidence—the amateurishness of it, the noise, the leaving of the plane. Fear does sloppy work. Corliss would have done it clean and quiet and I'd have found out at altitude, when the engine quit.

"Where is he now?"

"The old Hudson's Bay post downstream. There's a squatter's cabin." Her mouth twisted. "He drinks with the ghosts there. I've been keeping an eye on him since the plateau. He's been getting worse. Talking to himself. Talking to God. Last week he started talking about confession."

"Confession to who?"

"To anyone who'll carry it." She looked at me straight. "He asked about you. Whether you were the kind of man who keeps oaths."

The question landed harder than she probably meant it to, because Voss had asked me the same thing, fever-bright and dying by inches in that cabin, and I'd said yes the way you say yes to a drowning man—easily, cheaply, meaning every word and having no idea yet what the words would weigh.

"What aren't you telling me, Mara?"

For a moment I thought she'd give me one of her half-truths, the currency she dealt in. Instead she pulled something from her coat pocket: a folded sheet of paper, soft with handling, and held it out. A photocopy, third or fourth generation, greyscale gone muddy. A map. The Kettle River plateau, the survey grid, the deposit boundary drawn in confident red ink—and beneath it, in smaller type, the legend: *Land status: vacant Crown land, no registered interests.*

No registered interests. Four words that erased a hundred and forty years of traplines, graves, fish camps, a community that had watched the river freeze and break since before Canada had a name.

"This is the version that went to Ottawa," she said. "Voss signed it. I signed it. Halloran signed it. We all signed it, Sable, and the ones who took the money sleep fine, and the rest of us have been walking around out here trying to figure out how to unsign our names." She folded the map away. "That's why I need your plane. Not the fuel—you've got no fuel. I need the plane and I need you, because you're the only pilot within five hundred miles who isn't on somebody's payroll, and as of last night you're not even mobile."

"Thanks for the reminder."

"Don't take it personally. Take it as arithmetic." She glanced at the blackened cache, the scorched spruce, the settlement kids already poking at the ashes down the strip despite their grandmothers' shouts. "You're grounded, broke, and the only person alive who knows the whole shape of this story. Do you know what that makes you?"

"Tired," I said.

"It makes you the story. Which means Corliss can't ignore you anymore, and neither can I, and neither can you." She started backing toward the treeline, the way she did, always leaving before conversations finished with her. "The Hudson's Bay post. Tonight, if you're coming. He won't stay sober much longer, and sober is the only version of Halloran that tells the truth."

Then she was gone, and I stood alone in the ruins of my fuel supply with the wind picking up out of the northwest, smelling of cold and coming snow.

* * *

I spent the afternoon doing what pilots do when they can't fly, which is work on airplanes, which is to say I spent it lying under my own Cessna pretending the problem was mechanical.

The plane itself was fine. That was the joke of it. Tires intact, tanks full—I'd topped off before landing, forty-four gallons usable, maybe six hours of flying. Six hours. Whitehorse was nine from here in still air, longer in the weather that was building. Yellowknife was closer, but Yellowknife was where Corliss's office was, and Kettle River was southeast across country I knew like the lines of my own palm, and six hours would get me there and back with margin to spare—if I could get fuel, and if the weather held, and if nobody shot at me.

Broke. Grounded. And the only pilot who knew the whole shape of it.

I lay there on the gravel with grease on my hands and let that sentence sit in my chest until it stopped feeling like a complaint and started feeling like a fact. Ten years I'd built a life out of not mattering. Ferry the medicine, carry the gossip, log the hours, keep the nose clean, owe nobody nothing except the bank, and even the bank I paid on time because debt was a leash and I'd seen what leashes did. Anonymity wasn't loneliness. Anonymity was armor. You can't be made an example of if nobody knows your name.

But the armor had a hole in it now, and the hole was shaped like a satchel of core samples sitting in a trapper's cabin guarded by a man with a broken ankle, and shaped like an elder named Josephine Cardinal asking me a question I hadn't answered yet, and shaped like a boot print in old campfire ash belonging to a coward who might, if I got to him before the drink did, hand me the whole rotten chain end to end.

Marcel came by at dusk with bread and a look. "Insurance?" he asked.

"No papers," I said. "Cash economy."

He nodded slowly, the way old men nod when they've already decided what they think. Then he set a twenty-dollar bill on the wheel strut and walked away before I could refuse it, and after him came others, quieter—a kid with a five, the Co-op manager with ten, the nurse with a rolled fistful of small bills she pushed into my jacket pocket without breaking stride. Nobody said what it was for. Everybody knew what it was for. By dark there was sixty-one dollars on the strut and a promise from the Co-op man that he could get stove oil and drum gasoline from the barge shipment next week, aviation fuel being another matter entirely.

Sixty-one dollars and a community's spare change. Against a government man with a budget and no conscience.

I should have felt crushed. Instead—and I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the one cargo I've got left—I felt something I hadn't felt in years. The opposite of anonymous. Seen. Pinned to the map like a specimen, yes, but also *held*, the way the river holds a boat, whether you consent to it or not.

I gathered the money, buttoned it into my breast pocket, and looked south down the darkening strip toward the old Hudson's Bay post, where a drunk coward sat alone with the worst secret in the territory, waiting for somebody brave enough or broke enough to come collect it.

Both, as it happened. Both, tonight.

I checked the loads in the rifle out of habit, though I prayed I wouldn't need it, and started walking.
