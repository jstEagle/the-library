## 11. The Kettle River Elders

**Sable**

Halloran was where the dark had left him, slumped against the wall of the old Hudson's Bay post with a bottle between his knees and his collar turned up against the cold. He heard my boots on the gravel and didn't look up.

"Come to arrest me?" he said.

"Come to ask you to fly."

That got his head up. His eyes were red-rimmed and swimming, but underneath the drink there was something quick and frightened, an animal that hadn't entirely stopped listening. "I don't fly anymore. I don't do anything anymore. That's sort of my whole situation."

"You navigated for Voss for three seasons. You know the Kettle River country better than any map Ottawa ever printed."

"Knowing it is the problem." He laughed, a wet sound. "You know what they paid me? You know what a man's signature goes for these days? I could have bought a house in Yellowknife. I bought this instead." He raised the bottle like a toast to it.

"I'm not asking you to sign anything. I'm asking you to sit in the back of a plane and point."

He looked at me for a long time. The wind came down the strip and moved the gravel around our feet, patient as water.

"They'll kill you," he said finally. "You understand that. Corliss doesn't fire people. He un-people's them. There'll be a file somewhere by Friday that says you never existed, and then there'll be a man with a rifle who makes it true."

"Then I'd best be hard to find by Friday."

He got up slowly, swaying, and I saw him decide—not to be brave, because he wasn't, but to be useful, which is sometimes braver. He tucked the bottle into his coat like a man hiding evidence from himself.

"Voss," he said. "How bad?"

"Fever's down. Ankle's a mess. He talks to people who aren't there."

"He always did." Halloran walked past me toward the strip, then stopped. "Winterbourne. Why are you doing this? You've been out here ten years without taking a side. I've read your file. Everybody's read your file. You're the most neutral man in the North."

"That file's out of date."

* * *

We went at first light, or what passes for it in October—a grey seep of dawn through cloud so low it felt like weather pressing on the roof of the world. The fuel drum Mara had borrowed from the mission at Providence sat lashed in the back beside Voss, who lay wrapped in tarps and moaning softly at the bumps, talking in his fever to a wife he hadn't seen in years and a river he couldn't name. Halloran sat wedged against the drum with his eyes shut, navigating by memory, calling turns before I needed them: "Bear left at the burn scar. The river bends north after the second ridge—trust me, the charts lie there."

The charts did lie there. I'd flown over that bend a hundred times and never once wondered why the line on paper didn't match the ground beneath it. Now I knew why, and knowing made the whole country look different, the way a face looks different once you've learned it was lying to you.

Kettle River came up out of the murk an hour later: a scatter of log houses along a wide brown sweep of water, woodsmoke standing straight up in the still air, fish racks on the bank, a church with a leaning steeple that some missionary had built a century ago and the river had been trying to reclaim ever since. A dirt strip mowed into the muskeg at the edge of town. Kids running out to watch us land, because kids everywhere run when a plane comes, and a knot of adults behind them moving slower, the way people move when aircraft have brought them nothing good lately.

Josephine Cardinal stood at the front of them.

I'd met her twice before, years back, delivering insulin and mail. She was seventy-some and built like the shoreline—weathered, unmoving, worn smooth in places and sharp in others. She watched me cut the engine and climb out, and she didn't smile, and she didn't ask about the weather, which is how bush conversations start, which meant she already knew this one wasn't going to be about weather.

"You brought sick men," she said.

"One sick man. One drunk one." I opened the door and the smell of fever came out with the cold. "And something else."

Two young men helped me carry Voss to the health station, a one-room building with a cot and a cabinet of supplies I'd personally delivered over the years, bandage by bandage, run by run. Halloran drifted after us like a man following his own funeral. And through all of it I kept one hand on the satchel, and Josephine Cardinal watched me keep that hand there, and said nothing, which was worse than asking.

* * *

**Mara**

Mara Quill had walked out of the bush two days behind Sable Winterbourne, following his fuel spills and his campfire ash the way she'd followed ore bodies for fifteen years—one trace at a time, patiently, until the pattern gave itself up. She came into Kettle River on foot along the fish-drying trail, mud to her knees, and found the whole settlement gathered outside the health station like a jury waiting for its case.

She should have felt afraid. What she felt instead was relief so sharp it embarrassed her—nine days of ridge-watching, of eating cold rice and rehearsing arguments to no one, and now here was the moment, arriving whether she was ready or not.

Josephine Cardinal stood apart from the others, arms folded, looking at the stranger walking out of the treeline with a canvas roll under her arm.

"You're one of the survey team," Josephine said. Not a question.

"The geologist. Mara Quill." She stopped three paces off. Up close the old woman's eyes were pale as river ice and just as hard to see the bottom of. "I need to show you something. And then I need to ask you for help, and I don't have any right to either."

"Nobody does. Come in anyway."

Inside the health station, Voss slept under blankets, breathing in long ragged pulls. Sable stood by the window with his arms crossed, watching her come in like a man watching weather. Halloran sat in the corner with his head in his hands, and when he saw her he flinched as if she'd struck him.

"Mara." His voice cracked on it.

"Hello, Danny." She let him sit in it a moment. Then she unrolled the canvas on the table and weighted the corners with four tin cups, and the room went quiet around the maps the way a room goes quiet around a body.

They were the survey sheets—the real ones, the field originals, not the clean copies that had gone south in the report. Mara had carried them out of the camp the night the team scattered, sewn inside her parka, sleeping on them, dreaming on them. Six weeks of core samples translated into lines and numbers, and every line told the same story: copper, and lots of it, a deposit so rich it made the men in Ottawa stop thinking like scientists and start thinking like owners.

And the boundary line—the treaty line, surveyed and agreed and signed in 1921, the line that had held for a hundred years—ran straight through the middle of it.

Except on these sheets, it didn't. On these sheets someone had redrawn it, a few hundred metres east, then a few hundred more, until the whole deposit sat in territory marked *unoccupied*, and the Kettle River community's land had been shaved away like fat trimmed from a bone.

Sable leaned over the table. She watched him read it, watched the pilot's mind work—contours, distances, the country rendered in ink—and saw the moment he understood that the blank spaces on Corliss's paperwork and the shifted line on this map were the same blankness, the same lie, drawn at different scales.

"My grandfather trapped that," Josephine said.

Her voice was very quiet. Everyone in the room leaned toward it.

"This bend here." Her finger came down on the river's eastern loop, inside the redrawn line. "He ran marten there sixty winters. My father after him. My brother after my father. There are cabins on that ground—my uncle's cabin, still standing, still used every spring. And your government has written it empty." She looked up at Mara, and her eyes weren't angry; they were worse than angry. They were certain. "They didn't make a mistake. Mistakes get corrected. This was corrected *toward* them."

"Yes," Mara said. "By order. We were told the deposit fell outside the reserve boundary and to draft accordingly. Four of us signed. Voss wouldn't. He radioed Ottawa and they cut his funding within the week, and then the orders started coming through channels I can't even describe, and the team—" She stopped. Started again. "The team broke. Some took money. Some ran. I hid and hated myself, and then I decided hating myself was a luxury, and I started carrying these instead."

Silence. Outside, a dog barked twice and quit, as if even it understood the weight in the room.

Josephine rolled the maps closed with both hands, carefully, the way you handle something that might go off. Then she looked at Sable.

"And you," she said. "The pilot. Ten years you've flown over us. Delivered our medicine, our mail, our coffins. Never landed unless invited. Never asked a question you didn't want answered. I've always thought: there is a careful man."

Sable said nothing. Mara watched his jaw tighten.

"Now you've landed," Josephine went on, "with a dying surveyor and a drunk one and a bag of rocks, and you're standing in my health station looking like a man who's lost his footing. So I'll ask you one thing, and think hard before you answer, because I will remember it."

She stepped closer. Up close she smelled of woodsmoke and spruce gum and old wool, and her voice dropped to almost nothing, so that only he could hear it, though everyone did.

"When it costs you—when it takes your plane, your licence, the sky you love, everything you actually care about—whose side are you on then? Not today, when it's easy. Then."

The question went into him like a splinter. Mara saw it land, saw the flinch he tried not to make, the way his eyes went to the window, to the strip, to his Cessna sitting out there in the grey light with its debt and its dents and its ten years of careful anonymity parked in its seats.

"I don't know yet," he said.

It was, Mara thought, the first honest thing anyone had said in this whole rotten affair. Josephine Cardinal studied him for a long moment, then nodded once, as if honesty were at least a down payment.

"Good," she said. "A man who says *I know* is lying. A man who says *I don't know yet* might find out." She turned to Mara. "Now. Tell me exactly what the company expects to happen next, and don't leave out the parts that frighten you."

"There aren't any other kinds," Mara said, and began to tell her.

* * *

**Sable**

They talked for two hours. I mostly listened, which is what pilots are for, and what I heard rearranged the country outside the window into enemy terrain. Corliss's charter wasn't a mining outfit—it was enforcement dressed as one, authorized to make loose ends permanent. The sealed report in Yellowknife. The notebooks. The names Halloran finally started giving up, one at a time, like teeth coming out, each one costing him a pull from the bottle he no longer bothered to hide.

Near dusk, Josephine put the word out quietly: no one flew anywhere, no one spoke to strangers, the community would stand together and say nothing to nobody. She'd done it before, I realized. Against the fur companies, against the Indian Agent, against a hundred years of men with paperwork. This was simply the newest shape of an old fight.

At dark I walked out to check the plane, because checking the plane is how I pray. The strip was empty, the river running black and quiet, stars starting through a tear in the cloud.

Footsteps behind me. Mara, parka over her shoulders, breath steaming.

"She gutted you," she said.

"She asked a fair question."

"It's not fair. It's worse than fair." She stood beside me, not touching, looking at the plane. "I spent nine days on a ridge deciding whether to signal you. Nine days. And you know what tipped it? Watching you search. You flew those grids like you actually wanted to find us. Most hired men fly like they want to get home."

I didn't answer. Downriver, far off, a light was moving along the bank where no light should be—not a house light, not a boat. Moving fast. Moving toward us.

"Mara," I said. "Go wake Josephine."

She followed my gaze and went still. "Is that—"

"That's a vehicle on a road that ended in 1974. Which means somebody drove a long way tonight to be here by morning." I was already pulling the tie-downs loose, hands working ahead of my thoughts. "And I don't intend to be on the ground when they arrive."
