## 12. Halloran's Confession

The bottle had been my friend for eleven days, and like all my friends it was lying to me.

I was sitting on the loading dock behind the Northern Trading post in Fort Providence, because it was the only place in town where a man could drink without being watched, and I needed to not be watched so badly that the wanting of it had become its own kind of work. The Mackenzie went by out there, brown and patient, carrying ice floes north like it was delivering them somewhere. I used to think rivers knew where they were going. Now I knew better. Rivers just go downhill. Same as men.

I heard the Cessna come in an hour before I saw him — you hear everything in this country if you're sober enough to listen, and even when you're not, some sounds cut through. That engine had a rasp in it, a little unevenness on the left cylinder, and I'd have known it anywhere by then because for nine days I'd been listening for it the way a guilty man listens for footsteps.

Sable Winterbourne. The pilot. He came around the corner of the trading post with a coil of rope over his shoulder and a canvas bag in his hand, and he stopped when he saw me, the way a man stops when he steps on something soft in the dark.

"Halloran." He said it flat. Not surprised. That was the thing about Sable — nothing surprised him, or if it did, he'd buried the surprise so deep under that prairie-flat face of his that you'd need a core drill to find it.

"Winterbourne." My voice came out wrong. Too loud, then too quiet, like a radio with a bad connection. "Come to arrest me?"

"For what?"

"That's the question, isn't it." I laughed, and the laugh turned into something else halfway up my throat, and I took a pull from the bottle to cover it. Cheap rye. It tasted like punishment, which was appropriate. "For what. Eleven days I've been asking myself that."

He didn't sit down right away. He stood there at the edge of the dock with the light going amber behind him, the river smell coming off the water, and I could see him deciding — deciding whether I was worth the time, whether I was a trap, whether I was anything at all but a drunk on a dock. Then he set down the rope and the bag and lowered himself onto the planking across from me, forearms on his knees, and waited. He was good at waiting. Bush pilots are. You spend your life circling weather; you learn patience or you learn the ground.

"They offered me forty thousand," I said.

He said nothing.

"Forty thousand dollars to initial a map." I held up my hands and looked at them like they belonged to somebody else, which in a way they did now, because the hands that had signed that map couldn't possibly be mine. Mine had spent twenty years learning rock. These were stranger's hands. "Forty grand. Do you know what I made in a season of contract survey work before this? Eighteen, if the weather cooperated. I've got a daughter in Edmonton I haven't seen in three years because I can't afford to be the man who shows up broke again. Forty thousand dollars, Winterbourne. They knew exactly what it was worth. They'd done the arithmetic on every one of us."

"What did you initial?"

I told him. God help me, I told him all of it, and once I started I couldn't stop, the words coming up like floodwater through a floor drain. The Kettle River deposit — copper, and more than copper, there were values in those cores that Voss got quiet about, the kind of quiet that means a man is recalculating his whole life. Sixty-one meters of continuous mineralization under the east bench. And the east bench, that was the problem, wasn't it. The east bench was treaty land. Had been since before there was a Department of Indian Affairs, since Josephine Cardinal's grandfather ran traplines through country the maps called empty because nobody with a pen had bothered to look.

"Voss radioed Ottawa the day we pulled the last core," I said. "You should have seen his face. He wasn't excited. That's what I keep coming back to. A man finds the biggest strike of his career and he looks like he's at a funeral. Because he already knew. He'd read the boundary lines and he knew what Ottawa would do with them, and three days later the orders came down and proved him right." I drank. My hand was shaking hard enough that the bottle chattered against my teeth. "Redraw the eastern boundary. Move it west. Two kilometers, maybe less — just enough that the deposit falls inside 'unoccupied Crown land.' Just a line on paper. Nobody moves a fence. Nobody has to. There's no fence out there, Winterbourne. There never was. There's just what the paper says, and the paper is whatever they tell us to write."

"And you wrote it."

"I initialed it." The distinction felt enormous and worthless, both at once. "Me and Deslauriers and Chen. Three signatures. Voss wouldn't do it. Quill wouldn't do it. Okafor — Okafor just walked into the bush one morning and we didn't see him again until—" I stopped. I'd been stopping at that part for eleven days too.

"Until you scattered," Sable said. "Six surveyors, one camp, gear left in good order, radio coiled up neat as a church collection. I found it. Took me three days of flying grids to find it, and when I did, it looked like people who meant to come back."

"We weren't supposed to look like we scattered." I heard my own voice drop, and something in it made Sable go very still, that stillness animals have. "That's the part I have to tell you, and I need another drink first, and I'm ashamed of needing another drink first, but there it is."

I drank. The river went by. Somewhere across the water a loon started up, that crazy laughing call, like the whole world was a joke only it got.

"The charter that Corliss is arranging," I said. "The one he wants you to fly coordinates for. You think it's a mining company. Assessment crew, security consultants, whatever the letterhead says. It isn't." I looked at him straight, which cost me more than the confession had. "I saw the correspondence. Chen showed me by accident — he was drunker than me, if you can believe it, and he wanted somebody else to know so it wasn't only his sin. The company is called Meridian Resource Group on paper. On paper it has offices in Vancouver and a board of directors and a reclamation fund. Underneath the paper it answers to the same deputy minister who signed the boundary order, and its function isn't mining. Its function is *disposition*." 

"Disposition of what?"

"Of loose ends." The word came out of me small and cold. "Us. The survey team. Anyone who won't sign, anyone who talks, anyone holding samples that don't match the official record. The charter isn't coming to assess the deposit, Winterbourne. It's coming to make sure the story ends the way Ottawa wrote it. Six men walked away from their camp and were never seen again — that's the ending they want. Bears, exposure, bad judgment, take your pick, the North eats people, everyone knows that, nobody asks questions. And the man flying them in, the man who knows the plateau, who knows where we cached our gear—" I gestured at him with the bottle, and my hand betrayed me, slopping rye across the planks. "That man gets written into the story too. As the one who found nothing. Or as the one who found them too late. Or as the one who wasn't careful with his fuel and burned. However they need it."

Sable hadn't moved. I don't think he'd blinked. When he finally spoke his voice was as level as the horizon. "Corliss offered to clear my debt yesterday. Twenty-two hundred dollars, gone, in exchange for one flight."

"Twenty-two hundred." I almost laughed. "They cleared mine for forty thousand and I still ended up here, so you might want to renegotiate."

"Why are you telling me this?"

And there it was. The question I'd been drinking toward for eleven days, the one underneath all the others. Why me. Why now. Why a bush pilot I'd known for two weeks, instead of the RCMP detachment in Fort Simpson, instead of a newspaper, instead of a priest, instead of anybody whose job it was to catch men like me.

I thought about my daughter. I thought about her face at the airport in Edmonton three years ago, watching me walk backward toward security with my duffel over my shoulder, promising her next summer, promising her next summer the way I'd promised her mother things I never delivered. I thought about how forty thousand dollars was supposed to fix that — buy me a landing, buy me a way to stand in front of her without flinching — and how instead it had bought me a dock and a bottle and a view of a river that goes downhill forever.

"Because I initialed a map," I said, "and a line moved two kilometers, and there's a woman out there — Cardinal, the elder — whose grandfather's grave is going to end up inside a pit wall, and she'll never even get a headstone to visit, because the paper will say nobody ever lived there. Because I have a daughter who is going to grow up and ask me what I did, and I would rather she ask why I was poor than why I was bought." I wiped my mouth with the back of my hand. "Because cowardice is just courage that arrived late, Winterbourne, and mine is arriving very, very late, and I'll take it anyway. Late courage. Drunk courage. Whatever kind I've got left."

He studied me for a long moment. I let him. A man confessing doesn't get to control how he's seen; that's part of the price.

"You understand what you're handing me," he said. "If I act on this — if I fly anywhere near that charter, if I talk to anyone — you're not a loose end anymore, Halloran. You're the loose end that talked. They'll come for you first, because you're the easiest."

"I know."

"And you're drunk, and half-frozen, and you've got nowhere to run except deeper into a bottle."

"I know that too." I set the bottle down between us on the planks, deliberately, the way you'd put down a weapon. "So here's what I've got that isn't drink. Names. Dates. Amounts. I kept notes — not because I planned to talk, because I planned to *not* talk, and some animal part of me knew I'd need proof someday that I hadn't imagined the whole thing. Chen's transfer record. The deputy minister's initials on the boundary memo. The Meridian charter number and the manifest codes — those aren't mining codes, Winterbourne, I worked resource logistics for six years, I know what a drill mobilization manifest looks like and that isn't one. It's got armament line items on it. Armament. For a geology camp." I fumbled in my coat and brought out a folded sheaf of pages gone soft with sweat, the pencil writing smeared but legible, and held them out. "It's not much. It's what I am."

He took the pages. Read them slowly, there on the dock, while the light died and the loon quit laughing and the river kept on doing the only honest thing in the territory, which was going where gravity sent it.

When he finished, he folded the pages along their existing creases and put them inside his jacket, against his chest, and I understood that I had just stopped existing as a free man. Whatever happened next, I was cargo now. Evidence in motion.

"There's a journalist in Whitehorse," Sable said. "Name of Adele Fournier. She's covered northern land claims for eight years. If these names check out—"

"They'll check out."

"—then this goes further than a dock in Fort Providence. Further than you or me. And the people who wrote that boundary order will burn every document they can reach, including the ones that mention your name." He looked at me, and for the first time there was something in his face besides that flat watchfulness — something almost like pity, or its tougher cousin, respect. "You could still take the money and disappear. Forty thousand buys a lot of disappearing."

"My daughter wouldn't recognize a disappeared man," I said. "She'd recognize a father who showed up. Even late. Even broke." I laughed, and this time it stayed a laugh, mostly. "Besides. I've been disappearing for three years. I'm very good at it. It hasn't worked yet."

He stood, picked up his rope and his canvas bag, and paused at the edge of the dock with the last grey light behind him.

"Halloran. The charter — when does it move?"

"Thursday," I said. "Out of Hay River. Two aircraft, twelve men, and a flight plan filed as mineral assessment." I swallowed. "But the manifest lists night-vision equipment, Winterbourne. Geologists don't work nights."

He nodded once, the way pilots nod at weather they've decided to fly through anyway, and walked off toward the strip, and I sat alone on the dock with the empty bottle and the strangest feeling I'd had in eleven days, which was that the shaking in my hands had stopped.

Then I heard it — his engine turning over out at the strip, that little rasp in the left cylinder — and I realized with a cold drop in my stomach that Thursday wasn't the deadline I should have been counting.

Because Corliss's men had landed twenty minutes after Sable did. I'd watched their Beaver circle the town twice before putting down at the far end of the strip, and I'd been too drunk, too relieved, too busy with my confession to wonder what a company enforcement team was doing touching down in Fort Providence the same evening its last loose end started talking.

They'd find him at the fuel drums. They'd find him before he ever got airborne.

I got up off the dock faster than I had in years, and I ran.
