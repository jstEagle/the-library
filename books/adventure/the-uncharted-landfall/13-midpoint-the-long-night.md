## 13. Midpoint: The Long Night

I woke to the sound of nothing.

That's the thing about the north that no one who hasn't wintered there understands. Silence isn't empty up here. It's full — of wind deciding where to go, of ice settling on the lake, of an animal working the treeline for mice under the snow. You learn its vocabulary the way you learn a language, and after ten years I could read it like a chart.

This silence was wrong. It had a hole in it.

I lay in my bag in the trapper's cabin and listened for the hole's edges. Voss's breathing from the corner by the stove — gone. Mara's even rhythm from the far bunk — gone too, but that one didn't surprise me; she'd been restless all evening, feeding the fire twice, checking the window every time the aurora shifted. But Voss was feverish, ankle swollen to the size of a grapefruit, dosed on willow bark and half a bottle of aspirin I'd been saving for exactly this kind of emergency. He hadn't walked anywhere on his own since I'd carried him in.

The stove was still warm. That meant they hadn't been gone long.

I got up quietly, which is a skill you develop when you share country with things that would rather not be found, and crossed to the door. The snow outside held two sets of prints going out and one set coming back — Mara's, out and in again — and over those, pressed deep and recent, the wide flat tread of packer boots. At least three men. They'd come up the creek bed where the drifts gave them cover, taken Voss off the bunk he couldn't defend, and walked him out the same way.

There was blood on the doorframe. Not much. A smear at head height, already dulling in the cold. Someone had hit him, or dragged him past something sharp, or both.

The satchel was gone with him.

I stood there in the doorway with the cold pouring over me like water and let myself feel, for about ten seconds, the full weight of what I'd done. I'd made an oath to a delirious man. Then I'd slept eight feet away from him while the people he'd sworn it against walked into camp and took him like taking a rifle off a wall.

Ten seconds. Then I started thinking like a pilot, because that was the only way I knew how to think when everything else failed.

* * *

They'd have taken him to Corliss. There was nowhere else to take him. Corliss's operation had a spine that ran from Fort Providence down to Yellowknife, and everything — the fuel cache, the charter planes, the men with packer boots — hung off that spine like organs. Which meant Voss and the satchel were either at the strip or moving toward it tonight, and either way they'd pass through the one place in five hundred square miles where I could still reach them.

The problem was that reaching them required an airplane, and my airplane was sitting on Corliss's impound line at Fort Providence with a guard on it and a lien on its soul.

Here is what I knew about my own heart as I stood in that doorway: it wanted, more than anything, to walk away. Not fly away — walk. Leave the plane, leave the debt, leave the whole rotten story to rot where it lay. Head south down the Mackenzie on foot if I had to, catch a barge at Wrigley, disappear into the anonymous current of the world the way I'd been disappearing my whole adult life. Ten years of careful smallness, of being nobody from nowhere with a clean logbook and no opinions. All of it was still available to me. All I had to do was stop caring.

I tested the feeling honestly, the way you test ice before you commit the plane to it. Put your weight on it slowly. Listen for the crack.

It cracked. Because underneath the wanting-out was a different wanting, older and less convenient: the memory of Josephine Cardinal's face when she'd asked me whose side I was on when it cost me. And underneath *that*, the image of Voss on the bunk, burning with fever, pressing the satchel strap into my hand like a man handing off a child. *If I don't make it,* he'd said. Not *if I change my mind.* Not *if it gets hard.* If I don't make it.

He wasn't dead yet. So neither was the oath.

I went back inside, dressed properly, packed the cabin's supplies into a duffel — rope, axe, the rest of the aspirin, three days of food — and then I did the thing I'd been circling around all night without admitting it.

I sat down on the bunk Voss had been carried out of, and I burned my escape route.

Not literally. Not yet. But I made the decision the way you make a go/no-go call on a marginal day: out loud, alone, with the facts laid out in front of you so you can't pretend later that you never saw them. If I went after Corliss, I was done as a bush pilot. Not grounded — worse. Marked. A man who steals his own aircraft back from a government impound doesn't get his license suspended; he gets remembered. And the people Corliss worked for remembered faces the way banks remember debts.

Every other option was a version of the same lie. Fly out, report nothing, survive — survive doing what? Hauling gas and groceries over country where I'd know, every time I crossed the Kettle River plateau, exactly what was buried under it and who had been paid to bury the truth. Every mile would be a receipt. I'd spent ten years building a life measured in distance because distance was honest. This would poison it. There is no altitude high enough to fly above your own knowledge.

So. No-go on walking away.

What remained was theft, deception, and a plan thin enough to see light through. I spread it out anyway, because a bad plan executed beats a good plan admired.

* * *

By first grey light I was back at the lake where I'd left the canoe cached — Mara's idea originally, one of her many ideas I'd resented at the time and was now grateful for — and paddling the fourteen miles down to the old trading post landing where I kept a second cache nobody knew about, not even Corliss's people, because I'd established it years ago for reasons that had seemed paranoid then and merely prudent now. Inside the oil drum: a spare set of coveralls, a mechanic's tool roll, and forty feet of good rope.

Then I did the strangest work of my life. I walked openly up the road to Fort Providence strip, in daylight, carrying tools, looking like exactly what I appeared to be: a bush pilot with a broken airplane and nothing better to do than fix it.

Corliss's man on the impound line was named Dreever. Big, patient, bored — the dangerous kind of guard, the kind who isn't looking for trouble because trouble interrupts his coffee. He watched me come across the field with my tool roll and didn't reach for anything except his mug.

"Plane's impounded," he said.

"I know. I'm not flying it." I held up the tool roll. "Spark plugs are fouled, oil's been sitting cold for a week, battery's probably flat as a claim staked on rock. Whoever ends up with this aircraft next, it'll be a wreck if it sits. I'm doing maintenance. It's in everyone's interest."

He considered that. Considered me. I gave him the look I'd perfected over ten years — the one that says *I am a tired man thinking about carburetors and nothing else in the world* — and it worked, mostly because it happened to be true. I was tired. I was thinking about carburetors. Just not only.

"Stay on this side of the rope," he said finally.

I stayed on this side of the rope. Mostly.

Working on the 180 with a guard watching is a strange kind of theatre. Every motion has to be legible. Pull a plug, show it to the air, frown at it, gap it, reinstall. Drain a cup of oil, hold it to the light like tea. Meanwhile my hands were doing a second job underneath the first, the way a pianist's left hand carries a melody the right hand seems to ignore. I checked the tie-downs — two ropes and a chock, and the chock loose enough to kick free. I noted that Dreever's patrol route took him to the fuel drums and back on a loop of about four minutes, and that for perhaps ninety seconds of each loop his back was fully to my tail. I found that the magneto switch cover could be reached through the open inspection port if a man's arm were long and his intentions poor. And I counted, twice, the aircraft on the line: Corliss's charter Beaver, a company Cessna 206 I didn't recognize, and mine, sitting third in the row like a hostage with a haircut.

Voss wasn't there. Neither was the satchel. But the Beaver's cargo pod was lashed shut with new rope, and there were boot prints around it, and when Dreever wandered off on his loop I put my ear against the pod's skin and heard, faintly, a man breathing. Shallow. Feverish. Alive.

Alive was enough. Alive was everything. I straightened up, wiped my hands, and felt something settle in my chest that I recognized from takeoffs in weather I shouldn't have flown: fear, converted at pressure into purpose.

* * *

Mara found me at dusk.

I was on my knees at the engine cowling, pretending to torque an exhaust clamp, when a shadow fell across the cowl and I looked up into her face — dirt-streaked, wind-burned, eyes red-rimmed from a day of following men through the bush faster than any human should follow anyone.

"You lost him," she said.

"They lost *him*," I said. "I lost everything else."

She crouched beside the wheel, keeping her voice under the wind. "Three of them. I tracked them to the river trail before dark. They've got a boat waiting at the lower crossing — they're moving him down to Yellowknife tomorrow, once the charter comes in." She glanced at the Beaver. "That one. It goes south at first light with Voss in the pod and the samples in the baggage compartment. After that he's in a federal building in Ottawa and whatever he knows becomes whatever they say he knows."

"So we take him tonight."

"We?" She almost smiled, and it wasn't a kind smile. "Since when is it we?"

"Since you followed three armed men through twenty miles of bush alone instead of running south, which is what a smart person would have done. Since you're crouching in the mud telling me their schedule instead of selling me out to Corliss, which would have paid better." I sat back on my heels. "You've been ahead of me since the day I met you, Quill. I'm just trying to catch up."

For a moment she said nothing, and the wind filled the space between us with the smell of snow coming. Then she nodded, once, the smallest possible contract.

"Dreever does his loop every four minutes," I told her. "Ninety seconds of blind spot. Chock's loose. Mags are reachable through the inspection port. If we go at three a.m., moonset, he'll be on his fourth coffee and slowest on the turn."

"And after?"

"After," I said, "we find out what kind of thieves we are."

She stood, pulled her collar up, and melted toward the tree line the way she did everything — without wasted motion, without looking back. I watched the space where she'd been until the dark closed over it.

Then I turned back to my engine, pulled the exhaust clamp I'd been pretending to tighten, and began, carefully, methodically, to prepare my own airplane for a crime.

Somewhere under the cowling, in the cold and the coming snow, the last honest version of Sable Winterbourne finished his final inspection and signed off. What rolled out onto that strip at three o'clock the next morning wouldn't be a supply plane anymore. It would be a getaway vehicle, a rescue craft, and a confession with wings — and every mile it flew from that moment on would be a mile I could never log.

Dreever's boots crunched gravel behind me, right on schedule. Four minutes. Ninety seconds.

I picked up my wrench and waited for the blind spot.
