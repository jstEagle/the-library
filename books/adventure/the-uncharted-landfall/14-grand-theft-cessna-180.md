## 14. Grand Theft, Cessna 180

The blind spot lasted ninety seconds. I'd counted it three nights running from the shadow of Dreever's hangar: the guard walked the north fence at 3:07, disappeared behind the fuel drums at 3:09, and reappeared at 3:11 with a cigarette going and his flashlight pointed anywhere but the impound row. Nine days of watching a man smoke had taught me his whole liturgy. He never varied. Men who never vary are gifts.

I rolled the Cessna out on foot, nose first, one hand on the propeller spinner, boots silent in the fresh dusting of snow. A 180 is heavy when you're polite to it and light when you're not. I was not being polite tonight.

The engine cover came off and went under the wing. Pre-flight by feel — I'd done the full inspection the night before, but you check twice when the stakes are your neck. Fuel: full, thanks to my own siphon hose and Dreever's unlocked drum, which made this technically a second theft of the same man's property, and I decided that made us even for the fuel he'd sold me before selling me out. Oil: checked. Controls: free and correct. Magneto switches: off, because I wanted the first sound of this night to happen exactly when I chose it.

Halloran was supposed to be at the south end of the strip, sober enough to keep watch. What I got instead was a shape rising out of the ditch grass like a scarecrow that had given up on the profession, swaying gently, holding a bottle by the neck the way you'd hold evidence.

"You're late," he said, too loud.

"I'm early. You're just loud." I grabbed his sleeve. "Can you walk?"

"Walk? I can *navigate*." He patted his breast pocket, hit his own chin instead of the pocket, tried again, and produced a folded chart with the reverence of a priest producing relics. "Dead reckoning, Winterbourne. Fort Providence to Kettle River, heading one-six-seven, wind out of the northwest, correction angle—"

"It's midnight and there's no wind."

"There's always wind," Halloran said gravely. "That's the first thing they teach you."

"That's the first thing they teach *pilots*. You're a geologist."

"A geologist," he said, climbing into the back seat unassisted, which surprised both of us, "who has been drinking to forget coordinates, and has failed, because I remember all of them." He buckled himself in upside down, thought about it, and fixed it. Mostly. "Also I have the satchel's manifest memorized. Sample numbers, depths, intervals. Ask me anything."

"Ask you later. When you can say words with consonants."

"They'll hear us," he whispered suddenly, gripping the seat back, all the swagger gone. "Winterbourne, they'll hear us. Corliss said — he told me what happens to people who—"

"He told you what happens to people who get caught. So don't get caught." I swung up onto the wing root and dropped into the pilot's seat. My seat. The vinyl worn smooth in the shape of ten years of me. It felt strange to sit in it as a criminal. It felt, if I'm honest, correct.

I cracked the window two inches, primed her, and turned the mags on.

Here's the thing about starting an aircraft engine at three in the morning in a town of forty people: there is no quiet way. There is only the way where you commit. I pulled the prop through by hand once — habit, superstition, apology — then climbed back in and hit the starter.

The Cessna coughed, caught, and roared into the silence like a confession shouted in church. Every dog in Fort Providence started barking at once. Somewhere behind me a door banged open.

"Go," said Halloran, lucid as a surgeon. "Go go go."

I shoved the throttle forward and we lurched across the snow-dusted gravel, tail swinging, the strip lights — such as they were, two kerosene lamps Dreever kept for emergencies — sliding past. Behind us a flashlight beam swung wildly across the field, found the moving plane, and stuck there.

Then the searchlight.

It came off the company truck mounted high and white and absolute, sweeping the strip like God looking for His keys. It caught the tail first, then the wing, then us, pinned in a circle of noon at three a.m., and I did the thing every bush pilot knows and no flight instructor teaches: I flew the airplane while it was still on the ground. Full throttle, tail up, wheels bouncing over frozen ruts, aiming straight down the dark at nothing, trusting the strip was longer than my doubt.

"Hold on," I said.

"To what?" Halloran asked, and then we left the earth and he answered his own question by making a sound I'd only previously heard from drowning men.

The searchlight fell away beneath us. In the mirror I saw the truck fishtailing after us down the strip, gaining nothing, its beam carving useless arcs through the falling snow. Someone fired a rifle — one shot, more argument than aim, the bullet going wherever bullets go when angry men shoot at the sky over their own town.

"They're shooting," Halloran reported helpfully.

"They're shooting *behind* us. There's a difference." I banked southwest, low, into the river valley where radar was a rumor and the company's reach ended at the tree line. "Heading?"

"One-six-seven," Halloran recited instantly, eyes closed, hand flat on the chart like he was taking its pulse. "Corrected for drift, four degrees right. Kettle River in ninety minutes if the weather holds and forty if it doesn't."

"That's not how time works."

"Neither is stealing your own airplane," he said, opening one eye, "and yet here we are, doing it beautifully."

* * *

We flew through the falling snow and I want to tell you I was afraid the whole way, but that would be a lie, and I'd promised myself somewhere over the Mackenzie that the lying stopped at the cockpit door.

For ten years I had flown away from things. Away from a marriage that ended in a letter I still carried. Away from a debt collector in Hay River. Away from every conversation that required me to stay in a room longer than an hour. Flying away is easy. The plane wants to go; you just point it at the horizon and let the miles do the forgiving.

This was different. Every mile southwest was a mile toward something — toward Voss in whatever hole Corliss's men had put him in, toward Mara somewhere ahead of us in the bush, toward Josephine Cardinal and a question she'd asked me that I hadn't been able to answer until tonight. *Whose side are you on when it costs you?*

It cost me the plane. It cost me the strip, the runs, the name I'd built one careful delivery at a time. It cost me the anonymous life, which I realized now hadn't been safety. It had just been smallness with good sightlines.

And underneath the fear — the real fear, the kind that sits in your hands and makes them sweat on the yoke — was something I hadn't felt since I was nineteen years old and solo for the first time over Great Slave with the whole day ahead of me.

Joy. Stupid, illegal, magnificent joy.

"Winterbourne." Halloran leaned forward, breath like a distillery fire, voice suddenly steady. "There's something you should know. About the charter. About what Corliss really—"

"Not now."

"Now. Because if we go down, someone should know besides me." He fumbled the chart open against the seatback. "The company isn't a mining company. The charter's enforcement. They've got authorization to make our disappearance permanent — Voss's, mine, yours. I signed papers, Winterbourne. I took the money and I signed papers and I need you to know that if I don't say it drunk I'll never say it sober."

I looked at him in the mirror. Snow strobed past the windows. Somewhere below us the Kettle River country waited, armed and awake.

"Say it again tomorrow," I told him. "Sober. On paper. With your name on it."

"And if there's no tomorrow?"

"Then I'll write it down myself, and I'll swear I heard it from a dying man who finally found his courage." I leveled the wings. "But there's a tomorrow, Halloran. We stole it fair and square."

He laughed — a broken, wet, genuine laugh — and settled back, and within a minute he was asleep, chart still clutched to his chest like a child with a security blanket, muttering headings in his dreams.

Ahead, the snow thickened from flakes to a wall. The airframe started to shudder. I checked the clock, checked the compass, checked the mirror one last time for searchlights, and found none.

What I found instead, far below and off our corrected heading, was a glow in the trees that wasn't dawn, wasn't a town, and wasn't going to wait for us.

Fire. Where no fire should be.

I pushed the nose down toward it, and the storm closed behind us like a door.
