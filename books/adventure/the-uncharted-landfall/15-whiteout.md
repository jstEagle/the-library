## 15. Whiteout

The storm didn't announce itself. That was the thing about weather up here that no forecast ever captured—it simply changed its mind, and you were either ahead of the change or inside it.

Sable had been reading the sky for ten years. He'd read it that morning over Great Slave and seen nothing but the usual hard blue, the kind of cold that made the altimeter honest. By noon, crossing the low country north of Lac la Martre, the western horizon had gone the color of dirty wool, and by the time he understood what he was looking at, the wool had eaten half the world.

"Is that bad?" Halloran asked from the back seat. His voice had a new quality to it—thinner, more careful. Two days of forced sobriety at forty below will do that to a man.

"It's bad."

The Cessna 180 bucked once, twice, like something testing a fence. Sable's hands moved without consulting him: throttle trimmed, mixture rich, carb heat on. Below them the ice of the lake stretched white to every edge, featureless as paper. He needed features. Features were how you landed on ice—you picked a shoreline, a point of rock, anything the wind hadn't scoured clean of shadow.

There. A dark seam along the eastern shore, black spruce leaning away from the prevailing wind, and a long open sweep of lake ice running up to meet it. Old wind-scoured ice, thick as a church floor this time of year.

"Hold on," he said, and put her down.

The skis touched with a sound like a door closing. Then they were sliding, hissing across the frozen lake while the storm came down the valley behind them like something poured from a jug, and Sable taxied into the lee of the trees with maybe ninety seconds to spare before the world went white.

* * *

They got the tent up in the teeth of it—Halloran holding poles with hands that shook from more than cold now, Sable anchoring the corners with snow blocks cut from the drift against the shore. The plane sat thirty feet out, tied down to a deadfall, already becoming a pale shape in the murk.

Inside the tent, with the stove lit and the two of them wedged among fuel drums and the satchel of core samples Voss had sworn him to protect, Sable did what pilots do after an unscheduled landing: he walked his machine.

He found it on the left main gear. A crack in the strut, running diagonal, fine as a hair but unmistakable when he flexed the gear and watched it open and close like a slow wound breathing. Metal fatigue, probably—the same fatigue that lived in all of them, in the airframe, in the man. She'd take no more landings until it was welded or sleeved, and there was no welding out here, and no sleeve, and no one coming.

"How long?" Halloran asked, when Sable told him.

"Depends on the storm. Depends on whether I can rig something." Sable crouched by the stove, working warmth back into his fingers. "Could be two days. Could be five."

Halloran sat with that. Outside, the wind found a voice and began using it—a long, rising complaint through the spruce, like the land itself clearing its throat to say something.

"I've been thinking," Halloran said.

"Don't hurt yourself."

But there was no bite left in Sable's voice and they both knew it. Something had happened between Fort Providence and here, in the hours of engine drone and shared silence. Halloran had watched Sable steal his own airplane. Sable had watched Halloran drink his last flask dry somewhere over the Marian River and then sit with his hands empty in his lap like a man who'd set down something heavy and didn't know what to do with his arms.

"I keep seeing the map," Halloran said. "The one we filed. I drew part of it myself. My own hand." He held the hand up, turned it in the stove light as if it belonged to someone else. "You know what a line on a map is? It's just ink. It doesn't weigh anything. But that line—" his voice caught, steadied— "that line moves a river. Moves a whole community off their own ground, and the ink still doesn't weigh anything. That's what I can't get past. How light it was. How easy."

Sable said nothing. He'd learned long ago that men like Halloran, men drowning in a truth they'd been paid to swallow, didn't need commentary. They needed a witness.

"The names," Halloran said. "You want the names?"

Sable reached for his flight log.

It was the one honest thing he owned. Ten years of entries—dates, hours, destinations, loads, weather—in his own cramped hand, kept because keeping it was the law and because, in a life built on careful anonymity, the log was the one place where everything he did was written down exactly as it happened. No blanks, like Corliss's paperwork. No convenient erasures. Every mile accounted for.

He opened it to a fresh page and uncapped his pen.

"Start wherever you want," he said. "Go slow. Dates matter."

* * *

Halloran talked for three hours.

He talked while the storm screamed and the stove ticked and the candle guttered in its jar, and Sable wrote, and what came out of Halloran's mouth was a chain, link by link, each name attached to a date and a sum.

It started eleven months ago, in Yellowknife, in a hotel bar where a man named Devereux—company man, smooth, expensive coat—bought the survey team dinner and then bought their silence, course by course. Voss walked out first. Halloran stayed for dessert. That was the whole story of both men, really, told in the grammar of a restaurant bill.

Devereux answered to a director whose name Halloran only knew as "Mr. Sands"—a name so flat it had to be false, and both of them knew it, and Sable wrote it anyway because even a false name is a thread you can pull. Sands answered to Ottawa, to a deputy minister whose signature appeared on a memo authorizing the boundary revision—memo dated March 4th, Halloran remembered the date because it was his daughter's birthday and he'd been drunk enough to think that was funny at the time.

He gave Sable the amounts. Fourteen thousand for Halloran himself, wired in two installments, the account numbers still burned into his memory like brands. Eighteen for the team's cartographer, Pell, who'd redrawn the Kettle River traplines onto Crown land with a steady hand and then stopped sleeping. Twelve apiece for the two who'd scattered south and hadn't been heard from since.

And Corliss. Corliss wasn't company security; he was the company's guarantee. His charter existed so that the story would never have witnesses, only survivors, and only as many survivors as were useful.

"He told us at the start," Halloran said, staring into the stove. "Not in those words. He said, 'Gentlemen, the north is full of accidents.' And we all laughed. God help me, we laughed."

Sable wrote until his hand ached. Pages of it. When Halloran finally ran dry—sitting back against the duffel, wrung out, ten years lighter and ten years older—Sable read the entry back to him aloud, every name, every date, every figure, and made him confirm each one.

"You understand what this is now?" Sable said, tapping the log. "This isn't a diary anymore. This is testimony. If we make it out, this book goes to people who can use it. If we don't make it out—" he looked at the stove, at the small orange heart of fire that was the only warm thing for a hundred miles— "then it burns with us, and the land keeps the secret another hundred years."

Halloran nodded slowly. Then: "Voss. Is he alive? Did they—"

"They took him. Mara went after them." Sable fed a stick into the stove. "Which means she's out there alone, on foot, chasing men who fly."

"She's done worse with less," Halloran said, and something in his tone made Sable look at him. "That woman doesn't quit. She's the reason any of us know the truth at all. When Voss radioed Ottawa and got that order back—falsify the boundaries—she's the one who copied the original field notes before they could be 'corrected.' Hid them. If there's a way to beat these people, it runs through her."

Outside, the wind shifted and dropped a register, the way storms do when they're reloading. Sable crawled out into the blue-black dark and checked the tie-downs, and while he was out there he looked west, toward Fort Providence, toward the impound yard where his own aircraft had spent one guilty night, toward the country where Corliss's men were moving Voss and the satchel toward whatever road or strip would carry them south.

Every hour the storm grounded him was an hour they gained.

Or an hour Mara lost, alone in it.

He stood in the wind a long time, letting it decide nothing for him, and then went back inside.

* * *

They slept in shifts. Near dawn the storm exhausted itself the way northern storms do—all at once, like a held breath released—and Sable stepped out into a world remade: sky scrubbed to a hard violet, the lake smoking with ground mist, the spruce wearing a foot of fresh white.

He walked to the plane and knelt at the cracked strut and studied it in the clean light, and the crack looked back at him, patient, final.

Then he saw it. Down the shoreline, half a mile east, where the mist was thinning—a shape. Dark against the white. A figure, walking. Small with distance, but moving with purpose, following the shore straight toward them, and even before he raised the binoculars Sable knew the stride, knew the set of the shoulders, knew that nobody else in the world walked out of a storm like they owned the ground under it.

Mara Quill. Alone, frost-rimed, empty-handed—or so it seemed until she got close enough for the binoculars to find the canvas field case strapped across her back, and Sable lowered the glasses slowly, heart hammering, because he knew what Voss had hidden and where, and if Mara was carrying what he thought she was carrying—

She raised one hand. Not a wave. A signal: hurry.

Behind her, far to the south, faint as a scratch on glass, he heard it—engines. Not his.
