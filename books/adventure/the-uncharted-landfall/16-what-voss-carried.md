## 16. What Voss Carried

Mara had been walking since before dawn, and by the time the sun cleared the ridgeline she had stopped counting the hours and started counting her own bones. Left ankle complaining. Right hip a hot wire. The pack straps cutting grooves into shoulders that hadn't carried this much weight since field school. She was thirty-four years old and she felt like a core sample herself: drilled through, pulled up from depth, everything she was laid out in a column for the world to read.

She kept moving anyway. That was the whole trick of it, she'd learned on the plateau—keep moving until moving is all you are.

The convoy's dust told her where they were long before she saw them. It hung over the treeline like a dirty thought, a brown smear against the blue of the morning, and Mara dropped off the game trail into the spruce shadow and worked her way parallel, closing the distance in the way she'd learned to close distances: slowly, laterally, never straight at anything you wanted.

There were three vehicles. A pickup with a box rack, an older Suburban with tinted glass that had no business being this far north, and a flatbed carrying drums of fuel and what looked like survey equipment—the company's own gear, brought along to make the theft look like recovery. Two men rode in the pickup bed with rifles across their knees, riding loose and bored, the posture of men who believed the hard part was over.

They'd stopped at a creek crossing to let the trucks cool, and that was when Mara saw Voss.

He was lying in the shade of the Suburban on a bedroll that wasn't there for his comfort. His hands were zip-tied in front of him. One eye was swollen shut and his face had gone the color of old tallow, and even at two hundred meters Mara could see the way he held himself—not curled around the pain but braced against it, spine straight as a drill rod, the way he'd sat through every budget meeting Ottawa ever inflicted on them. Alive. Beaten. Still Voss.

The satchel lay open on the tailgate of the pickup.

Mara's stomach dropped through the permafrost of her body. She could see the core trays from here, the aluminum boxes with their foam liners, the sample tubes racked in their numbered slots like ammunition. One of Corliss's men was photographing them with a phone, turning each tube to catch the label, and another stood over the open satchel going through its pockets with the thoroughness of a customs officer.

They had the samples. They had the physical evidence, the copper-bearing core pulled straight out of the ground beneath Kettle River land, and there was nothing Mara could do about it from two hundred meters away with a blistered heel and a rifle she didn't know how to use well enough to matter.

She should have felt despair. Instead she felt something worse: clarity. Because she knew what the samples proved and what they didn't, and she knew what Voss had understood in the fever-dark of that trapper's cabin while his ankle swelled to the size of a grapefruit and his mind raced ahead of his body the way it always had.

*He hid them,* she thought. *Of course he did.*

* * *

She'd known Voss for six years, and in six years she had never once seen him lose an argument to anyone but himself. He was the kind of man who read regulations for pleasure, who kept carbon copies of carbon copies, who had once delayed a helicopter charter for forty minutes because the weight manifest was signed in the wrong ink. Everyone on the team had mocked him for it. Halloran used to do impressions—*Excuse me, gentlemen, I'll need that in triplicate*—and they'd all laughed, because bureaucracy was the enemy, paperwork was the tax you paid so you could do the real work of putting drills into rock.

But Mara had spent enough nights in the cook tent listening to Voss talk about why he'd joined the Geological Survey to know the truth underneath the pedantry. He believed in the record. Not in government, not in companies, not in any flag or ministry—he believed in the *record*, in the idea that if you wrote something down honestly, in the right place, in the right format, witnessed and filed and sealed, then it existed outside the reach of any single man's greed. The record was the one thing power couldn't burn fast enough. Power always came for the person eventually. It rarely came for the filing system.

So when the order had come down from Ottawa—when the deputy minister's letter arrived by satellite uplink asking them, in language so polite it took a full day to understand it was an instruction, to *reconsider the boundary interpretation in light of anticipated economic interests*—Voss had done exactly what everyone expected him to do. He'd raged. He'd radioed the directorate and gotten a voice smooth as river ice telling him the matter was above his pay grade. He'd watched half his team take the buyout envelopes and start packing.

And then, quietly, without telling anyone, he had gone to work.

Mara remembered watching him those last days at camp, hunched over the folding table with the official report forms spread out, filling in boxes in his small precise hand, and she'd assumed he was capitulating—that he was compiling the falsified report the ministry wanted, redrawing the boundaries with his own pen, surrendering in the only currency he had left. She'd been ashamed of him. She'd said as much, once, standing at the edge of the firelight, and he'd looked up at her with an expression she couldn't read and said only: *"Check the annexes, Quill."*

She hadn't checked the annexes. There hadn't been time. The team scattered within the week, and Voss went into the bush with a broken ankle and a satchel, and Mara went to the ridge to watch the sky, and none of them ever saw the report again.

Until now, crouched in the spruce shadow watching Corliss's men paw through the satchel, she finally understood what she'd been looking at across that cookfire.

The official report was already filed. It had been filed in Yellowknife eleven days before the team missed its first radio check-in—filed, logged, stamped, and sealed under the standard ninety-day review embargo, sitting in the records office of the Territorial Survey Annex in a grey archival box with a barcode on it. And in its annexes, in tables formatted exactly to specification, in the precise bureaucratic language of geological assessment, were the *real* numbers. The real boundaries. The core logs, unaltered, cross-referenced to the sample registry, describing a deposit that ran directly beneath treaty land that the cover pages declared vacant and unoccupied.

Voss had hidden the truth inside the lie. He'd built the falsified report exactly as ordered—and then buried the genuine evidence in its appendix, where no one would look, because everyone would believe the cover page. The forgery was the vault. The compliance was the camouflage.

Corliss's men had the core samples now, and the samples meant nothing they could safely use. Every tube was registered—logged into the national sample registry under chain-of-custody numbers that pointed, irrevocably, back to the original field notebooks. Destroy the samples and the registry entries became anomalies someone would eventually investigate. Keep them and they remained evidence. Either way, the samples alone proved nothing about the boundaries. Boundaries lived in documents. And documents lived in Yellowknife, in a grey box, behind a seal, waiting.

*Check the annexes, Quill.*

She checked them now, in memory, and her breath went thin. She'd seen the annex tables once, briefly, the night before the scattering—she'd assumed they were more of Voss's pedantry. But she remembered the figures. She remembered thinking the numbers looked *right*, truer than anything else in the file, and she'd been too angry and too frightened to ask why.

Hope hit her like altitude sickness—dizzying, nauseating, real.

* * *

The problem, of course, was getting to Yellowknife before Corliss did.

Because Corliss wasn't stupid. Cruel men often weren't; cruelty was just efficiency aimed at people instead of problems. He had the samples, which told him the deposit was real, and he had Voss, who would tell him—was perhaps telling him right now, down there in the shade, in exchange for the beating to stop—everything he knew about where the documentation lived. The ninety-day embargo cut both ways. It protected the report from casual eyes, but a man with ministerial signatures could break a seal in an afternoon. If Corliss got to the annex first, the notebooks would be pulled, "reviewed," and quietly replaced with clean copies, and the record Voss believed in would be edited by the very people it was meant to restrain.

Mara watched the convoy through the afternoon, tracking its rhythm the way she'd once tracked drill crews. They were heading northeast—toward the winter road junction, toward Fort Providence, toward the highway corridor that led, eventually, to Yellowknife. They were in no hurry. That was the thing that frightened her most. They moved like men who had already won, stopping to eat, to refuel, to let Voss suffer in the heat of the truck bed without urgency, because the last piece was a piece of paper and paper could be collected whenever convenient.

She counted bodies again. Seven she could see, possibly nine. Rifles, radios, vehicles, fuel. Against all that she had: her legs, a dead satellite phone in her pack, and whatever Sable Winterbourne had decided to be while nobody was watching.

That was the other problem. The pilot.

She'd been running the calculus on Sable since the day his Cessna first crossed the plateau and she'd lain flat on Windy Point with her heart hammering, deciding whether to signal. She'd chosen not to, then. Too early, too exposed, too many things unknown. But she'd watched him work since—watched him grid-search with a discipline that wasn't mercenary, watched him find the cookfire and land anyway, watched him carry Voss's oath out of the bush like a man carrying something fragile he hadn't agreed to hold. He burned his fuel cache rather than let it be used—or someone had burned it for him, and either way he was grounded now, broke, and angrier than he probably knew.

He was also, she suspected, the only pilot in the territory Corliss couldn't simply buy, because Corliss had already tried buying him and Sable had made whatever face pilots make when they're swallowing a no.

She needed him. Not his plane—his plane was impounded or wrecked or both—but his knowledge of the corridors, the strips, the weather windows, the ways a small aircraft moves through the North without being seen. She needed a smuggler's instincts applied to a librarian's errand: get two people to a records office in Yellowknife before a seal gets broken.

It was, she admitted to herself, a ridiculous plan. It was also the only plan. Hope hidden in plain sight deserved nothing less than a rescue conducted the same way.

* * *

Night came down cold and total, and the convoy made camp in a gravel pit with a fire ring some earlier travelers had left. Mara lay in the willows two hundred meters out, frozen to the ground in fact and nearly in spirit, and watched the sentry rotate twice, and waited for the thing she needed: the moment when men who believe they've won stop watching their prisoner.

It came near midnight. The fires burned low. The sentry walked the perimeter with his collar up and his hands in his armpits, and Voss lay where they'd dumped him beside the Suburban, tied, unmoving, apparently asleep.

Mara moved.

She'd planned the approach for four hours, and it went wrong immediately—a dog she hadn't seen, chained near the flatbed, lifted its head and gave a single questioning woof—and she froze in the open with her heart slamming, and the sentry turned, and she pressed herself flat against the frozen gravel and became geology. Ten seconds. Twenty. The sentry shrugged and walked on, and the dog, having expressed its professional opinion, put its head back down.

She reached Voss.

His good eye found her in the dark and widened, and she put a finger to her lips and set to work on the zip ties with the multi-tool she'd carried for ten years and never once used on anything but equipment. The ties were industrial grade; the tool's saw blade was not. It took forever. It took a year. Voss lay perfectly still throughout, and when the plastic finally parted he exhaled through his teeth, a sound like a slow leak.

"Quill," he breathed. "You absolute idiot."

"Save it." She pressed water into his hands and watched him drink like a man remembering how. "Can you walk?"

"Can I—" His laugh was a ruin. "I can crawl. Crawling I can do."

"Then listen. We don't run tonight. Running gets us both shot in the trees and they find your body and my body and the story ends with 'tragic accident, case closed.'" She kept her voice below the fire-crackle. "We do it my way. Tell me if I'm wrong."

His good eye studied her, and behind the swelling she watched the geologist come back online, the man who assessed everything, who trusted data over hope. "Tell me what you have."

"I have nothing. That's why I'm here. Tell me about the report, Voss. Tell me I'm right about the annexes."

Something moved through his ruined face—relief, maybe, or grief, or the particular exhaustion of a man who has been guarding a secret alone in the dark for weeks. "You're right," he whispered. "Filed and sealed. Yellowknife, Territorial Survey Annex, accession number 88-C-1141. Ninety-day embargo. The cover pages say what Corliss paid for. The annexes say what actually happened. Field notebooks one through fourteen, cross-referenced, initialed, witnessed by Halloran before he—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Before he chose differently."

"Halloran signed the witness lines?"

"All of them. Every page. He was drunk when he took the money and sober enough, afterward, to realize what his signature was worth." Voss's mouth twisted. "There's a version of Halloran that hates himself accurately. It might be useful."

"It might be more than useful." Mara told him, quickly, quietly, about the burned fuel cache, about the night Halloran had stumbled into Sable's camp smelling of paraffin and self-loathing, about the confession she'd pieced together secondhand. "If his name is on those witness lines, then the man who took the bribe also certified the evidence against it. Corliss can't forge around that without leaving Halloran's signature sitting on the true documents like a brand."

Voss closed his eye. For a moment he was silent, and Mara watched his chest rise and fall and understood that she was looking at a man doing arithmetic with his life.

"They'll move me tomorrow," he said finally. "Northeast. They'll want me present when the seal gets broken—it looks legitimate, a directorate retrieval, the team leader authorizing access to his own report. My signature on the request slip makes it legal." He opened the eye again. "Which means I know where they're going and when. And you know what's in the box. Between us we're worth more free than they can afford."

"So we get word to Sable."

"Sable." Voss said the name carefully, tasting it. "The pilot. The one who swore my oath for me while I was delirious and probably doesn't remember doing it."

"He remembers."

"Then here's what he needs to know." Voss's voice dropped further, and Mara leaned in close enough to smell blood and woodsmoke on him. "Accession 88-C-1141. Embargo breaks in nineteen days—but a signature can break it sooner, and Corliss has signatures. So it's a race, and the race is to Yellowknife, and the only way to win it is to get there first, break the seal ourselves, legally or otherwise, and get photographs of the annex tables into hands that matter before Corliss's people can sanitize the file. Photographs, Quill. Not the originals. Originals can be stolen back. Photographs multiply."

"And the hands that matter?"

"That," Voss said, "is the question I've been lying here answering for nine days. There's a federal auditor named Descharme who's been sniffing around the Survey's contracting practices since spring. Independent mandate. Reports to Ottawa directly, not through the ministry. If the annex evidence reached *him*—" He grimaced. "But first it has to reach Yellowknife. And for that we need a plane, a pilot, and a miracle with a fuel gauge."

Mara looked back across the gravel pit at the dying fires, at the sentry making his round, at the flatbed with its drums of fuel, and felt the plan assemble itself in her head with the click of a well-seated drill bit.

"You're going back on that bedroll," she said. "Ties and all. You're going to lie there and be their prisoner for one more day, because the moment you're missing, they go to alert, and alert men don't drive predictable routes."

"Mara—"

"One day. I'll be ahead of them. Find me at the winter road junction, or don't find me at all, and trust that I'll be coming back with a Cessna and a lunatic."

Voss caught her wrist with his freed hands—faster than she expected, stronger—and held it for a moment. His grip trembled with cold or fear or six years of friendship, and his voice came out stripped of everything except the truth.

"The annexes, Quill. Whatever happens. Check the annexes."

"I checked," she said. "I believe you. Now believe me."

She re-tied his wrists herself, loosely, with a slipknot he could shed in ten seconds, and she pressed the multi-tool into his palm and folded his fingers over it, and she crawled backward out of the firelight into the willows while the dog slept and the sentry hummed and the convoy dreamed its victorious dreams.

Then she stood up, shouldered her pack, and turned northeast, toward Fort Providence, toward a grounded pilot and an impounded airplane, and began to run.

Behind her the fires sank. Ahead of her the stars wheeled over the barrens, indifferent and enormous, and somewhere out under them, in a grey archival box in a climate-controlled room, fourteen notebooks waited inside a lie—patient as ore, dense as proof, hoping, in the way documents hope, to be found before they were destroyed.

Nineteen days. Maybe fewer.

Mara ran.
