## 17. The Sealed Report

**Sable**

The weather over Great Slave was the kind that made old pilots religious and young ones dead. Sable stood at the fuel dock at Hay River with a paper chart spread across the wing of his Cessna 180, tracing a corridor of green and red with one finger while Mara Quill watched him with the patience of someone who had already decided he'd say yes.

"That's a wall," she said. "Not weather. A wall."

"It's a seam." He tapped the line where two fronts met. "There's a gap running northwest out of Fort Resolution, maybe forty miles wide, maybe twenty. It'll be moving. We move faster."

"And if it closes?"

"Then we land on whatever's under us and wait for it to open."

She looked at the sky, then at him. "You've done this before."

"I've done everything before. That's the problem with me." He folded the chart. "Get in. And Mara—whatever you're carrying in that satchel of yours, if we go down, you let it burn. You swim. Promise me."

"I don't make promises I intend to keep."

"Good," he said. "Neither do I."

They lifted off at 0640 into air that smelled like snow deciding whether to fall. The lake country slid beneath them, black water stitched with white ice, and for the first hour the seam held exactly as he'd drawn it—a canyon of grey light between two ceilings of cloud, turbulence rattling the coffee thermos against the floorboards but nothing worse.

The charter found them an hour in.

Sable saw it first as a smudge on the northern horizon, a twin-engine shape holding their altitude and their heading, close enough to read the registration if you cared to. He didn't need to. He knew the silhouette. Corliss's people flew like they did everything else—expensively, visibly, daring you to notice.

"Don't look," he said.

Mara looked. Of course she looked.

"Twin Otter," she said. "Company markings filed off. They've been based at Yellowknife since Tuesday. I counted them landing."

"You counted them landing from where?"

"A ridge above the strip, with binoculars, eating cold beans." She said it without embarrassment. "I told you. I've been ahead of you since the beginning."

He banked gently south, casual, a pilot adjusting for wind. The Twin Otter adjusted too.

"They're not trying to catch us," Sable said. "They're herding. If they wanted us down, they'd have come down. They want to know where we land."

"Then don't land where they expect."

"There's only one place this plane can land in Yellowknife, and it's a float base with one road in and out." He chewed on it. The lake below was freezing over in patches now, the season turning mean. "Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless we land somewhere else first, and let someone else be interesting." He keyed the radio, tuned to the common frequency, and put on his best bored-bush-pilot voice. "Yellowknife traffic, Cessna One-Eight-Zero, ten miles south, descending for the float base, watch for canoe traffic near the river mouth."

Then he climbed.

It was a small lie and a large risk—the Twin Otter would settle over the float base expecting them, and instead Sable hauled the 180 up through the seam's ragged western edge, into cloud that shook the aircraft like a dog shaking a rat, and crossed the bay blind, on instruments he half-trusted, toward a logging strip on the far shore that hadn't seen regular traffic since the eighties.

Mara didn't speak the whole crossing. When they broke out low over black spruce and he set it down on a gravel strip soft enough to swallow the wheels to the hubs, she exhaled for the first time in twenty minutes.

"You lied on the radio," she said.

"I did."

"To buy us time."

"To buy us an afternoon. They'll sit on that float base watching the water until dark." He shut the engine down and the silence came in huge. "After dark, we walk in."

* * *

**Mara**

The survey office sat on Franklin Avenue behind a façade of yellow brick, three storeys of government indifference with a brass plate by the door: NORTHERN GEODETIC SURVEY — RECORDS AND ARCHIVE. Mara had walked past it eleven times in her life and never once thought about what slept in its basement.

Now it was all she could think about.

They came in from the alley side at 2200, when Yellowknife had gone quiet in that particular northern way—not asleep, just listening. Sable carried a pry bar wrapped in a sock; Mara carried the thing that mattered more, which was knowledge. Three weeks ago, hungover and furious, Voss had told her where he'd hidden the original field notebooks, and she had spent every day since making sure she remembered it exactly.

"The seal is the whole trick," she whispered as they crouched by the service door. "When a survey report is filed, the supporting notebooks get sealed in an archive envelope. Officially nobody opens it without ministerial sign-off. Unofficially, nobody ever opens it at all. It goes into a drawer and dies."

"So Voss hid the real notebooks inside the official seal."

"He swapped them. The sealed envelope contains his originals—the ones showing the true boundaries, the ones Ottawa ordered him to falsify. What everyone thinks are the notebooks—the copies Halloran helped forge—are sitting in Corliss's briefcase right now, worth less than the paper." She felt something loosen in her chest saying it aloud, something she'd been carrying clenched since Windy Point. "Voss knew they'd search him. He knew they'd take the satchel. So he put the truth somewhere no one would ever look because looking would require paperwork."

Sable almost smiled. In the streetlight he looked older than his years and younger than his eyes. "A man after my own heart. Hiding things in plain sight inside other things."

"Can you get us in?"

"I can get us in. Whether we get out depends on how bored the night guard is."

The answer was: not bored enough. There was a guard, a round man with a radio and a thermos, doing a slow circuit of the building every forty minutes. Sable timed two full circuits with his watch, lips moving, and then said, "Thirty-eight minutes. That gives us six to work with once he rounds the corner onto Franklin. The service door lock is a five-pin. I've opened worse with colder hands."

"You've broken into government buildings?"

"I've broken into everything. Debt makes a man versatile."

The pick work took four minutes and cost him two broken tension wrenches and a word he apologized for. Then the door swung, and they were inside, in a corridor that smelled of toner and floor wax, emergency lights painting everything the colour of weak tea.

Down one flight. Past a door marked RECORDS — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL. Into the basement archive, where the air was dry as a bone in a desert and the shelves ran off into the dark like rows of coffins waiting patiently to be needed.

Mara had memorized the accession number. KS-1147. Kettle River Survey, 1971 series, supplementary materials. She walked the aisle with her penlight cupped in her fingers, reading labels, and her heart went from steady to hammering when she found the drawer—and empty.

The slot where KS-1147 should have been held nothing but a retrieval card, dated yesterday.

*Requested for review. Held at front desk.*

"No," she said, out loud, and heard Sable's hand close on her shoulder.

"Breathe. Who requested it?"

The card gave a name. R. Corliss. Status: pending escort pickup, 0800 tomorrow.

"He knows," Mara whispered. "Somehow he knows, and he's going to collect it legally, in daylight, with a signature, and once it's in his hands the seal means nothing—he can burn it and file a loss report and there's not a court in the country that—" Her voice cracked and she hated it.

Sable took the card out of her hand and looked at it for a long moment. Then he turned and started walking back up the stairs, fast.

"Sable—"

"Front desk," he said. "Held at front desk. Which means it's in a pigeonhole ten feet from a night guard who thinks the most exciting thing tonight will be his sandwich."

"That's insane."

"Yes," he agreed. "It's my best quality. Stay behind me and look helpless."

* * *

**Sable**

The night guard's name, according to the lanyard, was Dwayne, and Dwayne was exactly the kind of problem Sable liked: a good man having a slow night. He sat behind the lobby desk with a paperback western and a radio murmuring hockey scores, and when the elevator opened and a dishevelled woman hurried out followed by a man in a bush pilot's jacket moving with urgent purpose, Dwayne's hand went to his radio and stopped.

"We need help," Mara said, and God bless her, she sounded genuinely wrecked, because she was. "There's been an accident at the mine site, my brother—I need the incident file, they said it was pulled for review, please, I've been driving since Fort Resolution—"

Dwayne looked at Sable. Sable did the hardest acting of his life, which was none at all: he simply stood there looking like a man whose night had gone to hell, hat in his hands, jaw tight.

"What site?" Dwayne asked, already half-rising.

"Giant," Mara said. "The old section. Please, the file—it should be in your hold area, they said someone requested it—"

And here was the beautiful, terrible thing about bureaucracy, the thing Sable had spent ten years flying around and was only now learning to fly through: Dwayne did not check any list. Dwayne turned to the pigeonholes behind him, ran a finger along the tabs, and pulled a fat brown envelope with KS-1147 scrawled across it, because a distressed sister asking for an accident file was, in his experience, a thing you helped.

"This one? This isn't an accident file, miss, this is—"

"My brother's name is in it," Mara said, and put her hand flat on the counter, and her voice did something small and breaking that Sable would think about for years afterward, wondering how much was performance and how much was the truth leaking through.

Dwayne set the envelope on the counter. "You can't take it out of the building. But you can look at it here, if you sign the sheet."

"We'll sign anything," Sable said.

They took it to the lobby table under the fluorescent hum, and Dwayne, being decent, went back to his western and gave them the fiction of privacy. Mara broke the archival seal with her thumbnail—destroying it, deliberately, so that no one could reseal and quietly swap the contents later—and lifted out the field notebooks, three of them, canvas-bound, water-stained, Voss's cramped handwriting marching across every page.

Mara photographed. Sable watched Dwayne.

She worked fast and clean, phone flat, flash off, page by page: the boundary readings, the core logs, and there—halfway through the second book—a memo in different ink, initialled at the bottom with letters that made Mara's breath go shallow. Orders. Dated nine days before the team went silent. *Reclassify parcels 4 through 9 per attached. Historical occupancy to be noted as 'transient.' Confirm receipt.*

Voss had written *Received under protest* beneath it, and then, smaller, in the margin, in pencil: *Originals stay with me. Let them forge their own.*

"Got it," Mara breathed. "Every page. It's done, it's—"

The lobby doors opened.

Two men came in out of the cold, and Sable knew them the way you know weather coming—one was the driver from the impound at Fort Providence, the other he'd never seen, but the cut of the coat and the way his eyes went straight to the counter and then to the envelope told Sable everything. Corliss hadn't sent them to collect at 0800. He'd sent them early. Or they'd never left.

"Evening," the coat said pleasantly, to Dwayne. "We're here for a retrieval. Reference KS-1147."

Dwayne frowned. "That's—there's a family looking at it right now, actually—"

The coat looked at the table. At Mara. At Sable. His expression didn't change, which was itself a message, sent and received.

"Well," the coat said. "Small world."

Sable's mind went very quiet and very fast, the way it did on short final in crosswinds. Envelope on the table. Photographs already taken—all of them, thank God, every page—phone in Mara's pocket. Notebooks irreplaceable. Seal already broken, which meant the moment these men touched it, the chain of custody died and became a story anyone could tell.

"Mara," he said mildly, "go start the truck."

"We don't have a truck."

"Go start the truck."

She understood. She swept the notebooks back into the envelope, tucked it under her arm, and walked—not ran, walked, magnificent—toward the service corridor while the coat took one step forward and Dwayne said, "Sir, you can't just—"

"She's taking government property," the coat said.

"She's a grieving sister reading a file she signed for," Sable said, stepping sideways into the coat's sightline, big and slow and harmless-looking, buying seconds the way he bought everything: with debt. "Sign-in sheet's right there. Check the names. Take it up with whoever runs your mornings."

The coat hesitated, and in the hesitation Mara was gone through the service door, and Sable heard her boots on the stairwell, and he smiled at the coat with all the warmth of a frozen lake.

"You should hurry," he said. "She drives fast."

Then he walked out the front doors into the cold, unhurried, a man with nowhere to be, and turned the first corner, and ran.

* * *

**Mara**

She was at the alley door with the envelope crushed against her chest when Sable came around the corner at a dead sprint, breath smoking, and for one absurd second they just looked at each other—two people who had spent their whole lives trusting no one, standing in a Yellowknife alley at midnight holding the same stolen truth.

"Truck," she said.

"You started it?"

"I started a very convincing argument with a door handle. Run."

They ran. Four blocks through back lanes to the logging strip where the 180 sat tied down under frost, and Sable did his preflight in ninety seconds flat, hands moving on faith and muscle, while Mara buckled herself in and discovered she was shaking—not from fear, she realized, but from the strange new weight of not being alone with it anymore. For three weeks the truth had been hers alone to carry, and now it was in a stranger's phone camera and a stranger's stubborn hands, and the weight had not gotten lighter, exactly. It had gotten shared.

The engine caught. Sable taxied out onto the ice-glazed strip without lights, and as they lifted into the dark Mara looked back at the town and saw, on the road to the float base, headlights moving fast. Too fast. Plural.

"They'll guess the strip eventually," she said.

"Not tonight. Tonight they'll guess wrong, because tonight I'm not flying the obvious line." He banked north, away from Hay River, away from anywhere sensible, into the open dark east of the lake. "We'll put down at a fishing camp I know on Nonacho, sleep four hours, and run the last leg at first light."

"To where? The photos have to reach someone who can't be bought or frightened. Do you know such a person? Because I don't. I've spent a month learning that everyone can be one or the other."

Sable was quiet for a long moment, flying by the stars and the shape of the land below, and when he spoke his voice had something in it she hadn't heard before—something almost like hope, worn carefully, like a tool he wasn't sure still worked.

"Halloran gave me a name," he said. "Before the whiteout. A journalist in Whitehorse. Swore on whatever's left of his soul that she doesn't scare and she doesn't sell." He glanced over. "Halloran also swore he could navigate drunk, so weigh it accordingly."

"Weighing it," Mara said. But she was already scrolling through the photographs, checking each frame, and Voss's pencilled margin stared up at her from the little screen—*originals stay with me*—and she thought: he trusted no one either, and he trusted the record, and maybe that was the whole secret. Not trust in people. Trust in what people write down when they refuse to lie.

Below them, the lakes were freezing one by one, white seams closing over black water, the whole country locking itself down for winter. Somewhere behind them, Corliss's men were discovering an empty strip and a broken seal and a night guard named Dwayne who would swear until his dying day that the sister had signed the sheet properly.

Ahead: Whitehorse. A name. A story that would not stay sealed.

"Hold on," Sable said, and pushed north into the cold.
