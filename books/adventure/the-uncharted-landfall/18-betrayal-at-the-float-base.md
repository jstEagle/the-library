## 18. Betrayal at the Float Base

The mechanic's name was Denny Foss, and he had a face like a dropped wrench and a handshake that could crack walnuts. He'd lent Sable forty gallons of avgas out of his own stock two days ago, no paperwork, no questions, just a grunt and a pump handle. That was the north. That was how it worked. You didn't sign for things between friends; you remembered them.

Sable should have remembered that memory could be bought.

He was at the float base before dawn, loading the Cessna by lamplight, when the first wrongness arrived: the smell of coffee where there shouldn't be any. Denny kept a thermos in his shack, but this was fresh, percolated, and it was coming from the office window of the charter hangar at the far end of the dock — a hangar that had been locked since spring.

The second wrongness was the boat.

It sat low in the water at the end of the pier, a freighter canoe with a tarp over its cargo, and there were three men in it who were doing an excellent job of pretending to fish. Nobody fishes at four in the morning on Great Slave Lake with the water like black glass and the temperature sitting at minus twelve. They held their rods anyway, patient as decoys.

Sable stood in the shallows with a fuel drum under each arm and did the arithmetic. Mara was due at the dock within the hour with the photographs from Yellowknife — thirty-six frames of original survey notebooks, the whole rotten architecture of the falsified boundaries, shot by flashlight in a sealed records room while Corliss's men slept off their own confidence. The film was in her coat. Her coat was currently walking toward him along the shore road, alone, in the dark, carrying everything.

"Denny." Sable set down the drums and crossed to the mechanic's shack. The door was open. Denny sat inside with his big hands wrapped around a mug he wasn't drinking, staring at the wall the way men stare at walls when they've already made the decision they're ashamed of.

"Don't," Denny said, before Sable could open his mouth.

"Forty gallons," Sable said. "No paper."

"I know what I did." Denny's voice came out flat, scraped clean. "They came yesterday. Not Corliss himself — his people. Showed me a paper with my name on it. Fuel tax violations going back six years. Enough to take my licence, my shop, probably the house in Hay River." He finally looked up. "I told them you'd be here before dawn. I told them about the girl coming in by the shore road." His jaw worked. "I'm sorry, Sable. I want you to hear me say it plain. I'm sorry. But sorry doesn't pay a mortgage."

"No," Sable said. "It just pays the man holding the paper."

He left Denny sitting in his shame and walked back out onto the dock, and by then it didn't matter anymore, because the shore road had produced its headlights.

* * *

They took Mara twenty feet from the end of the pier.

Sable watched it happen from the dock with his hands empty and his heart going like a runaway engine. A truck rolled up behind her without lights until the last second, then flooded the road white, and two men stepped out with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once been told no by anyone who mattered. One of them spoke to her. She didn't run. That was the thing that would stay with him — she didn't run, because running would have meant admitting fear, and Mara Quill would have chewed off her own arm first. She stood in the headlights and let them search her coat, and when the man pulled out the film canister and held it up like a trophy, she said something short and contemptuous that Sable couldn't hear but could perfectly imagine.

Then they put her in the truck, and the truck drove down to the water, and the fishermen stopped fishing.

Corliss got out of the canoe last. He always got out last; Sable understood now that it was choreography, that the man arranged every room he entered so that he was the final reveal. He wore a parka worth more than Sable's annual fuel bill and he climbed onto the dock rubbing his hands like a man arriving at a lodge he owned.

"Pilot Winterbourne." He smiled. "You look terrible. Have you been sleeping in the plane again?"

"Where is she going?"

"Wherever we need her to be, which depends entirely on you." Corliss nodded at the Cessna, tied down at the float base, gulls muttering overhead like a jury. "Beautiful morning for a flight. Here's what happens next, and I'll only say it once, because unlike some people in this story, I respect your time."

He laid it out the way a man lays out cards he already knows will win. One flight. Sable flies a charter run south — documents, equipment, nothing dangerous, nothing illegal on paper, because nothing Corliss ever touched was illegal on paper. In exchange: Mara released by nightfall, unharmed, annoyed but intact. Voss's medical bills handled. The stolen-aircraft business in Fort Providence evaporates from every file it currently lived in. And the debt — the debt Sable had been drowning in since the engine rebuild two winters ago — gone. Signed, stamped, forgotten.

"And the photographs?" Sable said.

"Developed and destroyed. Along with the negatives, the notebooks' existence becomes a rumor, and the rumor dies of old age." Corliss tucked his hands into his pockets, almost gentle. "And the Kettle River community goes on exactly as it has for a hundred years, which is to say: never knowing. That's the part you keep tripping over, pilot. You think I'm taking something from them. I'm not. I'm leaving them exactly where they are. Ignorance isn't theft. It's a mercy most people pay good money for."

"They signed a treaty."

"Treaties are written by governments, and governments are written by companies, and companies are written by men like me." Corliss said it without heat, without pride, the way you'd recite a weather report. "There's no villain in this, Sable. There's just the way things get done. You've spent ten years flying medicine to people who are going to die anyway. Don't tell me you haven't wondered why the map never changes for them."

The lake lay flat and silent around them. Somewhere across the water a loon called, absurdly, beautifully, indifferent to all of it.

"You're asking me to fly for you," Sable said.

"I'm offering you a job. There's a difference, and the difference is that one of them comes with a future." Corliss checked his watch. "You have until the sun clears that ridge. Then the offer expires, the girl goes somewhere inconvenient, and I find another pilot — younger, hungrier, and considerably less sentimental. Though honestly?" He looked Sable up and down. "I hope you say yes. Good pilots who can keep quiet are rarer than copper."

He turned and walked back down the dock toward the waiting truck, and Sable stood alone on the planks with the fuel drums at his feet and the Cessna behind him and the sun beginning to bleed red along the ridge line like something opening.

* * *

He thought about it. That was the shameful part, and he let himself feel the full weight of it: he thought about it. Ten years of careful anonymity, of debts and distance, of a life measured in miles because miles couldn't betray you — and here was a door back to all of it, held open by the worst man he'd ever shaken hands with. Fly one run. Save Mara. Erase himself cleanly off the board and go back to being nobody, which had been the whole point of being anybody for a decade.

The sun cleared the ridge.

Sable looked at it for a long moment. Then he untied the Cessna, hauled the fuel drums aboard, and started the engine — not south, the way Corliss's men expected, but north, lifting off the black water into the red light with the whole shape of the story finally clear in his head.

Corliss had the photographs. But Voss had hidden the originals somewhere no one would think to look, and Mara — Mara had told Sable where, three nights ago, in the low voice she used for truths she wasn't sure she could afford. The sealed report in Yellowknife was already photographed. What mattered now was who else could be made to see it.

There was a federal investigator in Whitehorse who owed Halloran a phone call. There was a hearing that hadn't happened yet. And there was one thing Corliss's entire machine could not buy, forge, or fly away from:

Witnesses who refused to stay silent.

Sable banked north, alone in the cockpit, and began to plan the only flight left that mattered.
