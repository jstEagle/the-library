## 19. The Answer Is No

The charter came out of the south at nine hundred feet, fat and silver, a de Havilland Otter with the company's name painted small on the tail so you'd have to want to see it. Sable saw it. He'd been watching the southern sky for an hour, standing in the shallows of Kettle River with the water pulling at his boot soles, waiting for exactly this.

Behind him the community was moving with a purpose he'd never seen in ten years of bush flying. Not panic. Something older than panic. Men were carrying rifles the way other men carry tools—unhurried, familiar, the way you carry a thing you've owned since before you needed it. Josephine Cardinal stood at the edge of the strip with a shotgun broken over her arm like a question she hadn't decided to answer yet.

"You could still be gone before they land," she said, not looking at him.

"I could."

"But you won't."

"No."

She nodded once, the way she'd nodded at the falsified maps two days ago, and went back to watching the sky. That was the thing about Josephine. She didn't argue with you. She just made sure you'd heard yourself say it.

The Otter turned onto a long base leg for the strip. Sable walked to his Cessna 180, the one he'd stolen out of Corliss's impound at Fort Providence with a searchlight sweeping the field and Halloran singing badly in the back seat, and he did not climb in. He put his hand on the door instead, and he waited, and he let the Otter come to him.

* * *

Corliss climbed out first. That was always the tell with Corliss—he came down the ladder himself, no pilot's dignity about it, because dignity was a currency he'd never needed. He was wearing the same canvas jacket from Fort Providence, the one that made him look like every government man in every northern office, and he was smiling the way he smiled when the paperwork was already signed.

"Sable." He spread his hands. "You made good time."

"I had a tailwind."

"Everybody has a tailwind eventually." Corliss came closer, stopping at a respectful distance, hands still open. Behind him, the Otter's pilot stayed in his seat, and two men in the back who weren't surveyors and weren't miners and weren't anything with a job title stayed in theirs. "Here's where we are. The company is prepared to be generous. You fly one last run—Yellowknife, back by Thursday. You keep the Cessna. The debt's gone, the stolen-aircraft business evaporates, and nobody remembers your name, which I understand is the thing you've valued most for ten years."

"And the community?"

Corliss's smile didn't move but something behind it did, a small recalibration, the look of a man who'd hoped he wouldn't have to say the quiet part. "There is no community issue. There's a mineral claim on unoccupied Crown land, properly surveyed, properly filed. That's the whole story. That's the only story."

"Josephine's grandfather trapped that land before either of us was born."

"Trapping isn't title." Corliss said it gently, like a doctor delivering a diagnosis he'd given a hundred times. "Sable. Look at them." He tilted his head toward the strip, toward the line of men and women standing between him and the river, rifles held low and easy. "You think this ends well for them? You think Ottawa rewrites a treaty because a bush pilot found a satchel? I'm offering you the only version of this where you walk away clean. Take it."

Sable looked at the Otter. He looked at his own Cessna, fuel tanks full, borrowed drum drained into them at dawn. He thought about Voss in the community's care, ankle splinted, fever finally broken, sleeping in a house that had taken him in without asking what he'd cost them. He thought about Mara somewhere south with the photographs of the original notebooks, and Halloran's sworn statement in a Yellowknife envelope, and the flight log in his jacket pocket with names and dates written in the honest hand he'd kept for ten years.

He thought about the box canyon.

It was forty miles southwest. He'd found it his first summer, hunting a shortcut that didn't exist, and he'd spent an afternoon learning it the way you learn a scar on your own hand. The Kettle River ran into it through a slot no wider than the Cessna's wingspan, turned ninety degrees inside a wall of granite, and dead-ended in a bowl with one way in and no way out except straight up, in a column of rising air that only worked when the morning sun hit the east face and cooked a thermal off the rock. He'd ridden that thermal out once at seven in the morning, sweating, laughing, alone.

The Otter was a beautiful airplane and it weighed four times what his did.

"Corliss," Sable said. "I'm going to say no now, and I want you to hear all of it."

Corliss's hands finally came down.

"The answer is no."

* * *

Sable was moving before Corliss finished inhaling. He was in the Cessna, door shut, mags on, and the engine caught on the second blade because ten years of cold mornings had taught the engine and the man the same lesson: don't hesitate. He taxied with the tail swinging wide, deliberately, blocking Corliss's view of the strip for three full seconds, and then he was lined up and rolling and the wheels left the gravel and the river came up under him, brown and patient.

Behind him, he heard the Otter start. He'd counted on it. A man like Corliss didn't chase you because he was angry; he chased you because the math still worked. One last run, one last chance to close the story before Thursday, before the investigator, before whatever Mara was doing with those photographs. The math worked. That was the tragedy of men like Corliss. The math always worked right up until the country itself disagreed with it.

Sable flew low. Not show-low—working low, the way he flew every day, the river as his road, willows combing the air a hundred feet below his wheels. He didn't head south. He turned southwest, into the morning sun, and let the Otter see him do it.

The radio crackled. Corliss's voice, flat now, the smile gone out of it. "Sable. Turn around. Last offer."

Sable keyed the mic. "You should know something about this canyon I'm flying into."

Silence. Then, carefully: "What canyon."

"The one you're about to follow me into."

He throttled up and put the Cessna into the slot.

* * *

The walls came up on both sides like a book closing. Granite, lichen-stained, close enough that he could have counted the cracks if he'd had the attention to spare, which he didn't, because the slot was ninety feet of wingspan-width and the Cessna's wingspan was thirty-six and the margin was the rest of his life. The river flashed beneath him. A raven lifted off a ledge and folded away into a crack and was gone, and Sable thought, absurdly, *even the birds know the exits.*

Behind him, the Otter's engine note changed. Deeper. Committed. Corliss had told his pilot to follow.

The canyon turned ninety degrees and Sable turned with it, banking inside the turn, right wingtip kissing the shadow line, and came out into the bowl.

It was a cathedral with no roof. Granite on three sides, eight hundred feet, streaked with old water stains. The river came in over a gravel fan and sank into a spring and disappeared. The morning sun was on the east face now, full on it, and the rock was breathing—Sable could feel the thermal as a shudder in the airframe, the column of warm air rising off the sunlit stone like a hand held out.

He pulled up into it. Full power, nose high, the stall horn singing its thin warning and him letting it sing, riding the edge the way you ride anything that can kill you—by listening to it. The bowl turned beneath him. Once. Twice. On the third circle he looked down and saw the Otter come out of the slot.

It was magnificent and it was doomed and he felt no triumph, only a terrible clarity, watching it. The pilot was good. He could tell from the way the big plane flared out of the turn, wings level, energy managed, a professional doing everything right. The pilot banked and climbed and the bowl swallowed him and the Otter's wings, built for weight and endurance, not for this, and the granite stood on every side, and there was no thermal for a machine that heavy, not this morning, not in this shadow.

The Otter climbed four hundred feet. Five. The pilot turned for the slot—he was good, he was cutting his losses—and Sable watched the big plane's wing pass through the edge of his own thermal, rob it, wobble, recover, and line up on the slot at an airspeed that was honest and insufficient.

The Otter made the slot. Sable heard, more than saw, the wingtip brush the wall—a crack of aluminum on stone, small as a branch snapping—and then the big plane was through, descending the river corridor with a new sound in its engine, a ragged edge, and trailing a thread of smoke from the left wing.

Sable climbed out of the bowl on the thermal, gentle now, the column carrying him up the east face and over the rim into the flat morning light, and he leveled off and looked back once.

The Otter was going to make the river flats. He could see it in the way the pilot held the nose—level, patient, trading altitude for distance, a man flying a damaged airplane to a landing instead of a crash. The smoke thickened, then thinned. The plane settled toward a long gravel bar eight miles downriver, and Sable watched until it touched, gear up or gear down he couldn't tell, sliding, stopping, whole enough.

He keyed the radio, though he knew they might not answer. "Your pilot flew that well. Tell him I said so."

Static. Then Corliss, and his voice was different—not angry, not smiling. Small. A man discovering the size of the country. "You knew. You knew that canyon."

"I knew it the way you'll never know anything out here, Corliss. You rented it. I learned it."

He turned north and flew home.

* * *

Kettle River came up out of the morning like something from a map that hadn't been drawn yet. Smoke from the woodstoves, standing straight in the still air. The river bright with sun. And the strip—Corliss's strip, the government's strip, the strip on the falsified maps marked *unoccupied*—full of people.

They hadn't scattered when the engines started. They'd moved to the edges and waited, and now they stood along both sides of the gravel, and Josephine Cardinal stood at the head of it with her shotgun still broken over her arm, and beside her, leaning on a crutch, his ankle wrapped, his face gray and stubborn, stood Voss.

Sable landed long and slow and rolled out the length of the strip between the lines of people, and it was the strangest landing of his life, because it felt like a landing in both directions—wheels on gravel, and something else touching down too, something that had been airborne in him for ten years.

He shut down. He sat in the cockpit for a moment, hands on the yoke, listening to the engine tick.

Then he climbed out with the satchel of core samples in one hand and the flight log in his jacket, and he walked down the strip between the people of Kettle River, and nobody moved, and nobody had to.

Josephine looked at him, then past him, at the empty southern sky where the Otter had vanished. "They'll walk out," she said. "Or someone will come for them. Either way, they'll be here by tonight."

"Then we should be ready by tonight."

"We were ready before you were born, Sable Winterbourne." She finally broke the shotgun closed, and the sound of it went down the strip like a sentence being finished. "Now tell me what's coming, and this time don't leave anything out."

He opened his mouth to answer—and stopped. Because down the river, around the bend, small and silver and wrong, a floatplane was coming. It wasn't Corliss's Otter, and it wasn't any plane Sable knew, and it was flying the river low with its wheels—its floats—already down for a landing.

Mara had gone south with the photographs. Halloran's statement was in a Yellowknife envelope. And the only people who came up the Kettle River by air, unannounced, on the morning after a chase, were either the company's second wave—

—or the law.

Josephine saw it too. She squinted into the sun off the water, and Sable watched her decide, in real time, which it was.

"Josephine," he said quietly, "whoever that is—when they step out, I'd like Voss standing next to me."
