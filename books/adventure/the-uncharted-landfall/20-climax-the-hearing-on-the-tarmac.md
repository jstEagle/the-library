## 20. Climax: The Hearing on the Tarmac

**Sable**

The wind off the plateau came down the valley in long grey sheets, and by nine in the morning the whole Kettle River strip was rimmed with people who had no business standing in it.

They'd come out of the bush and down from the ridge and upriver by boat—Josephine Cardinal's people, maybe sixty of them, spread along the tree line with the unhurried patience of folks who had decided something and were now simply waiting for the world to catch up. Some carried rifles. Most didn't. Josephine stood at the front with her hands folded, wearing a wool coat that had outlived three governments, and she did not look at Sable once. He understood. Looking would have been a question, and there was no time left for questions.

His Cessna sat at the north end of the strip where he'd shut her down an hour before dawn, cold-soaked and fuel-light, everything he had left in the world parked on a gravel bar in the middle of somebody else's fight.

Mara crouched beside the wheel strut, checking the tie-down for the fourth time. "Company charter lifted from Fort Providence twenty minutes ago," she said. "Halloran's radio call went through clean. Investigator's plane is behind them by half an hour."

"Then we've got half an hour of weather," Sable said.

"That's about right."

He looked south down the valley, where the cloud deck hung low and ragged over the river bends. Somewhere under it, a heavier, faster aircraft was coming to take things away from people, and somewhere behind *that*, a federal investigator named Adeyemi was flying toward a hearing nobody had scheduled, on a tarmac nobody owned, because Halloran—a drunk, a coward, a man Sable had personally hauled out of a snowbank twice—had sobered up long enough to put his name on paper.

It occurred to Sable, not for the first time, that history in the North rarely arrived the way the textbooks said. It arrived by floatplane, in bad light, smelling of avgas.

* * *

**Mara**

She watched the southern sky and thought about Voss.

They had him at the Fort Providence infirmary now—two cracked ribs, a face like a dropped plate, and a voice that had come back overnight with a clarity that frightened her more than his silence ever had. The Mounties had him under guard, technically, but Corliss's men had been eased out of the building at gunpoint-suggestion, and this morning a constable had radioed ahead: *Voss insists on being flown to Kettle River. Says he'll identify the maps himself or die trying. Doctor says he can travel if he doesn't move.*

Which meant they were bringing him on a stretcher, into the middle of this.

Mara had spent eleven days on a ridge learning how to be still, and stillness had taught her one thing above all others: you do not get to choose the ground where your truth gets told. You only get to choose whether you're standing on it when it happens.

"There," Sable said.

The company charter came out of the murk over the river bend like a shark under ice—twin-engine, corporate white, the kind of aircraft that existed to make other aircraft feel temporary. It flared over the strip, touched down hard enough to bounce, and rolled to a stop fifty yards short of the Cessna, props winding down with a sound like a held breath releasing.

The door opened. Corliss stepped out first, which told Mara everything. A man who expected to win walked second; a man who needed to *perform* winning walked first, into the wind, coat buttoned, smiling at sixty armed and unarmed people as though they were a delegation come to welcome him.

Behind him came four of his men, and behind *them*, hesitating on the airstair, the charter pilot—a lean man in a company jacket, holding a leather document case against his chest like a life vest.

Mara felt the moment tilt. She'd flown enough charters to know what a pilot looked like when he was done being loyal.

* * *

**Sable**

Corliss crossed the gravel like a man crossing his own front yard.

"Winterbourne." He stopped an arm's length away, close enough that Sable could smell the coffee on him, the city on him. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other guy."

"I have. Repeatedly." Corliss glanced past him—at Mara, at the tree line, at Josephine—and his smile thinned by a degree. "Here's how today goes. I take possession of the survey materials—the photographs, the notebooks, whatever copies exist. I file a routine report. In six weeks the boundary amendment passes quietly through two committees and nobody up here notices anything except a new road. Everyone flies home. Including you."

"And if I say no?"

Corliss sighed, genuinely, the sigh of a man burdened by other people's stubbornness. "Then this becomes a matter for enforcement rather than administration, and I promise you, Winterbourne, you don't want to learn the difference."

"The last fellow who worked for you learned it," Sable said. "Name of Voss. He's coming here on a stretcher to tell everyone exactly what you had done to him."

Something moved behind Corliss's eyes—not fear, but recalculation, the fast arithmetic of a man discovering his ledger has a page missing. And in that half-second of arithmetic, the charter pilot stepped forward.

"Mr. Corliss." His voice was steady, flat, northern. "Before anything else happens, these are yours."

He held out the document case.

Corliss reached for it—and the pilot opened it himself, turned it around, and pulled out a sheaf of radio logs, carbon copies, timestamped and initialed. Sable, standing close enough, read the top sheet upside down: *TRANSMISSION LOG — COMPANY CHARTER 4-1-1 — AUTHORIZATION RELAYED: C. CORLISS — SUBJECT: PERMANENT DISPOSITION OF SURVEY PERSONNEL.*

The words *permanent disposition* sat there in typed block letters like a body in the snow.

"Every flight you've ordered since June," the pilot said. "Every relay through our Whitehorse station. My name's on those logs too, Mr. Corliss. I signed them because you told me they were administrative. Then I flew a man to a lake to interrogate a surveyor, and I decided my name wasn't going on any more of your paperwork without witnesses." He nodded toward the tree line. "There appear to be quite a few witnesses."

Corliss turned slowly. For the first time since Fort Providence, since the fuel cache, since the impound and the smile and the offer that was never really an offer—he looked like a man standing on ice and hearing it talk.

"Gunnar," he said softly. "Think about your pension."

"I have," said the pilot. "I've thought about very little else for three weeks. That's the trouble with pensions, sir. They assume there's going to be a later."

* * *

**Mara**

The second aircraft came in while Corliss was still recalculating.

It dropped out of the low ceiling unannounced, a battered government Otter with RCMP markings, and landed long and fast and slightly sideways in the gusts, which told Mara its pilot had been pushing weather to get here. It rolled to a stop beyond the company charter, and a woman stepped down onto the gravel—tall, dark-skinned, wearing a parka over a suit jacket, carrying nothing but a folder and the particular calm of someone who had read every document in it twice.

Adeyemi. Federal inquiry. Halloran's sworn statement folded inside that folder like a lit match.

She surveyed the tarmac—the company plane, the Cessna, the sixty people at the tree line, the pilot holding open a case full of confessions—with the unhurried attention of a woman taking attendance.

"Mr. Corliss," she said. "You're a difficult man to find at convenient moments. Fortunately, inconvenient ones seem to work just as well."

"This is private property," Corliss began.

"It's Crown land adjacent to treaty land," Adeyemi said, "on which you have landed an unlicensed charter carrying personnel implicated—" she tapped the folder "—in bribery, falsification of federal survey records, and what one sworn witness describes, in his own handwriting, as *authorized removal of obstacles*. So let's not start with property. Let's start with the truth, since so much of it appears to be standing here voluntarily."

Her gaze found the charter pilot. "You called it in yourself?"

"Halloran made the statement, ma'am. I brought the receipts."

"Good. Both of you stay." She looked at Corliss's four men, lined up behind their employer like furniture. "Gentlemen, you may walk to the tree line and stand with everyone else, or you may stay within arm's reach of Mr. Corliss and be arrested in alphabetical order. Your choice. I'd think about which side of this photograph you want to be on."

Two of them moved immediately. The other two looked at Corliss, and Corliss looked at the sky, and whatever he saw there made him stop giving orders.

That was when the Otter's second passenger came down the airstair—or was brought down—carried on a stretcher by two constables, blankets to his chin, one ankle boxed in plaster, his face the color of old candle wax.

Voss.

* * *

**Sable**

They set the stretcher down on the gravel between the two aircraft, and the wind pushed at the blankets, and for a moment nobody spoke. Sixty people at the tree line leaned forward without moving. Josephine Cardinal took one step onto the open strip—the first step anyone from the community had taken onto it all morning—and stopped, waiting.

Voss's eyes found Sable first.

"You kept the oath," he rasped.

"Barely."

"Good. Barely is how you know it mattered." Then he turned his head, with visible cost, toward Adeyemi. "Who has the maps?"

Mara stepped forward with the tube. She'd carried it since Yellowknife, slept with it, lied for it, nearly died for it, and now she knelt on the frozen gravel beside a broken man and unrolled the falsified survey sheets flat, weighting the corners with stones.

"Original field boundaries," she said, loud enough to carry. "Drawn by hand, in the field, by this team. And underneath—" she drew out the second set, the Ottawa version, crisp and official "—the amended boundaries. Filed six days after the discovery. Note the Kettle River trapline. Note what happened to it."

Josephine Cardinal came forward at last, slow across the gravel, and stood over the maps the way you stand over a grave.

She looked for a long time. Then she crouched, stiff-kneed, and laid one finger on the redrawn line where it cut across the river bend, and her voice, when it came, was quiet and perfectly level and carried further than any shout.

"This is my grandfather's line. He ran this trapline for forty-one years. He taught my mother the bend where the marten run, and she taught me, and I taught my son, and your government drew a pencil through it and wrote *unoccupied*." She looked up—not at Adeyemi, not at Corliss, but at the space between them, at the whole machinery the two of them represented. "We were never unoccupied. We were *unasked*."

Voss raised one hand off the stretcher, trembling, and pointed at the amended map.

"That signature," he said. "Bottom corner. That's mine. I signed it because they told me the deposit estimate would be void without a compliant boundary report, because they told me the community would be *compensated*, because they told me—"

He stopped. Breathed. The wind filled the pause.

"They told me lies in the passive voice," he said, "so that no one would ever have to be guilty of anything. I am telling you in the active voice. I falsified that map. On instruction. For money I never collected, because the moment I understood what it purchased, I tried to burn it, and this—" he gestured at the stretcher, the plaster, the ruin of his face "—is what burning it cost. Every word I have said since is in the sealed field notebooks, photographed in Yellowknife, witnessed by these two pilots and a drunk man who found his spine at the bottom of a bottle. Ask us. All of us. We will not be quiet anymore."

Silence, then—except for wind, and the tick of cooling engines, and somewhere in the tree line a child asking her father a question in a whisper that carried.

Adeyemi opened her folder and began, methodically, to write.

* * *

**Mara**

Corliss made his last move then, and it was smaller than anyone expected, and that was what finally finished him.

He walked to the amended map, bent as if to examine it, and picked it up—one smooth motion, folding it under his arm, turning back toward his charter with the expression of a man retrieving a misfiled document. Two of his remaining men shifted to cover him.

"Mr. Corliss." Adeyemi hadn't raised her voice. "Put it down."

"It's government property."

"So is the truth. Funny how rarely the two travel together." She snapped her fingers once, and one of the constables from the Otter stepped between Corliss and his aircraft, hand resting on his holster without gripping it. "Down. Now."

And Corliss—who had bought men, burned caches, arranged disappearances, and smiled through all of it—looked at the constable, and at the tree line, and at the pilot Gunnar standing in the open door of the charter with the radio logs held high like a lantern, and Mara watched the exact moment the arithmetic failed him. Not fear. Just the end of the calculation. Nowhere left on the ledger where the numbers came out in his favor.

He put the map down on the gravel. The wind took the corner and fluttered it, and Josephine Cardinal put her boot on it, gently, the way you'd steady a page for a child to read.

"Charles Corliss," Adeyemi said, "you are detained pending formal proceedings under sections fourteen and nineteen of the Inquiry Act, along with every individual whose name appears in these logs—which, I note, includes two deputy ministers, one director of surveys, and a mining consortium whose lawyers are about to have a very bad season." She closed the folder. "Your aircraft is seized. Your pilot, fortunately for all of us, defected with the evidence before you could destroy it, which means that for once in this entire affair, the paperwork survived the powerful man. Imagine that."

The constables took his arms. Corliss went without struggling, which was somehow worse than struggling, and as they walked him past Sable he paused, just briefly, and looked at the Cessna, and at Sable, and said nothing at all.

Some defeats don't need dialogue.

* * *

**Sable**

Afterward, the strip emptied slowly, the way a wound stops bleeding—not all at once, but in stages, each stage leaving less behind.

The Otter lifted first, carrying Corliss and two of his men and a folder that weighed more than its paper. Then the company charter, Gunnar alone at the controls, flying his own surrender documents south to Whitehorse. The constables' vehicle ground away up the winter road with the rest.

That left the gravel, the wind, the Cessna, and the people.

Voss, before they carried him to Josephine's house, caught Sable's sleeve. "The oath's paid," he said. "Both directions."

"Don't thank me. I stole my own airplane."

"Best theft I ever heard of." His eyes drifted to Mara, standing a few feet off with the empty map tube over her shoulder like a rifle. "Take care of the ridge-walker. She sees further than any of us."

Then he was gone up the trail, and the community was drifting back to their houses and their nets and their ordinary extraordinary lives, and Josephine Cardinal stopped in front of Sable on her way past.

She studied him for a long moment. The same question from the night by her stove was still in her face—but changed now, answered and replaced by a new one.

"You could fly out of here anonymous," she said. "By tonight. Corliss's employers will forget your face eventually, if you're far enough away when they try to remember it."

"I know."

"But?"

Sable looked at his Cessna—fuel-light, debt-ridden, the only home he'd had for ten years, sitting on a gravel bar at the center of the biggest story the North would tell for a generation. Behind it, Mara was watching him with the patient, level gaze of a woman who had already guessed his answer and was letting him arrive at it on his own schedule.

"But I'm done measuring my life in miles," he said.

Josephine smiled—small, dry, entirely unsentimental. "Then you'll want to know," she said, "that the inquiry investigator asked to see you in Whitehorse next week. Something about your flight logs." Her eyes glinted. "And something about a certain mechanic at the float base who sold you out, and a fuel bill nobody can account for. Apparently, Mr. Winterbourne, the paperwork isn't finished with you yet."
