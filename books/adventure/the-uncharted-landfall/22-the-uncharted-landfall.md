## 22. The Uncharted Landfall

The last medicine run began the way ten years of them had begun: before dawn, in the blue-black cold, with a flashlight held in my teeth and my breath fogging the hangar door. The Cessna 180 sat on its skis like something sleeping, and I ran my hand along her cowling the way you'd touch a horse's neck, feeling for heat that wasn't there yet.

"Last one," I said to her, though she'd heard me say it before.

I'd said it in November, after the hearing. I'd said it at Christmas, when the inquiry lawyers finally stopped calling. But this time I meant it, because this time I had already sold nothing, signed nothing, promised nothing—and still the plane was going to a new owner, a young Dene pilot named Alfred Tanoh who'd learned to fly on flight simulators in Fort Providence and was hungry for the routes I'd worn smooth as river stones.

I loaded the cargo by feel: insulin for Colville Lake, penicillin for Déline, the small heavy boxes of a life measured in pounds and ounces. Ten years of knowing which community needed what in which season. Ten years of being the man who arrived when the ice was right and left before anyone could ask him to stay.

The engine caught on the third try. It always caught on the third try. Some things you don't fix even when you can.

* * *

I flew the Mackenzie basin one more time the way a man reads a letter he's written to himself.

The river was frozen into a white road running north, and the low sun turned every drift pink, and the outposts came up under my wings like stations of a rosary: Fort Good Hope, its church steeple black against the snow; Norman Wells, where the oil tanks glowed like brass; the unnamed bends where I'd once set down in a whiteout and waited two days for the world to reappear.

At Colville Lake, Alfred would take over the run tomorrow. Today it was mine, and I flew it slow, banking over each strip so I could see the people come out of the warm buildings and stand in the cold waiting for the sound of an engine. They always heard me before they saw me. That was the arrangement—the North heard you coming long before it let you arrive.

Old Elsie Jacobson met me at the Colville dock with her granddaughter's hand in hers. She was ninety-one now, and the insulin had bought her three winters she wasn't supposed to have.

"You flying somewhere better?" she asked, squinting up at me against the glare.

"Nowhere better," I told her. "Just somewhere closer."

She laughed, and her granddaughter laughed, and I took the empty vials she handed me—she always saved them for me, a superstition or a joke, I never asked which—and I stood there a moment longer than the schedule allowed.

That was the thing about a life measured in miles. You could always leave. Nobody ever asked you to stay, so nobody ever got the chance to be disappointed when you went. I had built myself a country made entirely of departures, and I'd called it freedom, and I'd believed it right up until a government man with too-blank paperwork offered me triple rate to find six men who hadn't vanished at all.

* * *

Déline at noon. Fort Franklin's old name, Great Bear Lake shining beyond it like hammered tin. I drank tea in the band office with a man who'd been a boy when I first landed here, and he told me the caribou were late this year, and I said they're always late now, and we both looked out the window at the white silence and didn't say anything else, because some conversations are just two people agreeing not to lie.

Then south, then west, the sun falling behind me, the light going long and amber across the plateau. And there it was ahead, the way it had been there since October, waiting:

Kettle River.

* * *

I came in low over the community the way I always did now, and they came out the way they always did—not because a plane landing is remarkable, but because this plane landing was mine, and everyone knew what today was. Josephine Cardinal stood at the edge of the strip in her parka, hands folded, watching me set down with the particular patience of a woman who has buried a husband and raised four children and outlasted two governments and does not hurry for weather or men.

Alfred Tanoh climbed out of the truck to meet me, grinning, his breath steaming.

"She handles good," he said, meaning the Cessna. "You kept her honest."

"I kept her mostly honest."

"That's all any of us get."

We unloaded together. He'd fly her back to Fort Providence in the morning, start the run the day after, and the medicine would keep moving whether I was in the cockpit or not. That was the whole point. That was the thing Corliss never understood, the thing the company never understood: the route isn't the man. The route is older than the man. The river was a supply line before there were engines, and it will be one after.

Josephine walked me to the house as the light died. Mara was already there, boots drying by the stove, maps spread across the kitchen table—always maps, always the treaty lines and the traplines and the ore body drawn in red beneath them both, the deposit that had started everything, still underground, still theirs, still waiting for a court decision that had finally, in February, gone the right way.

"You're really doing it," Mara said. Not a question. She'd been asking it in different forms since November, testing, the way she tested everything, and tonight she'd finally gotten the answer she wanted and didn't quite believe.

"I sold the plane."

"I know. I watched you sign."

"Then stop asking."

She smiled, that dry half-smile that had undone me slowly over a winter, and Josephine put tea in front of us and said grace in Slavey, short and practical, thanking the Creator for food and for pilots, and I thought: there are worse things than being thanked in the same breath as bread.

* * *

Here is what I gave up, and I want to be exact about it, because the North keeps exact accounts.

I gave up the morning engine check. I gave up the third-try catch. I gave up the view from eight thousand feet where the rivers braid like hair and every problem shrinks to the size of a map dot. I gave up being unreachable, unaccountable, unasked. I gave up the anonymity I'd spent a decade constructing, plank by plank, logbook entry by logbook entry—a man who existed only between places, who belonged nowhere enough that nowhere could never hold him.

Corliss's employers remember my face. A man from Yellowknife told me in January, over coffee I didn't drink, that certain doors would stay closed to me in aviation for a long time, maybe forever. He meant it as a threat. I heard it as a description. There are worse fates than being known, I've decided. Being unknown nearly cost the Kettle River people their land, and it cost Voss nine days of fever in a trapper's cabin guarding a satchel like a relic, and it cost Halloran everything he had except the confession he finally made.

Voss walks with a cane now. He came up from Yellowknife last month to see the ground he'd mapped honestly, the second time, the real numbers. He stood at the ridge above Windy Point where Mara had once watched my plane and torn herself between signaling and staying hidden, and he said, "From up here you can see why they falsified it." And then he said, "And you can see why it wouldn't have mattered." Because the land doesn't care what a report says. The copper has been under the Kettle River since before there were reports, and it will be there after, and the people were there before both.

* * *

Winter loosened its grip in April the way it always does—reluctantly, in stages, the river groaning and cracking all night like something waking up angry. By May the ice was rotten and by June the water was open, and I started work.

The work is boats and trails. I haul freight from the barge landing at the mouth of the river up to the community: fuel drums, lumber, flour and lard, a new furnace for the school, a used snowmobile for the health station. I run the supply loop that my plane used to serve from the air, except slower, heavier, and on the ground—which means I see the country at walking speed for the first time in ten years. I know how far it is between the rapids and the narrows now not in minutes but in portages. I know which hillsides hold berries in August and which muskeg will swallow a quad to its axles.

It turns out I love it. That surprised me more than anything else that happened this year, including the hearing, including Halloran crying into his beer, including the federal investigator arriving on the tarmac like a stagehand stepping out of the wings.

Mara runs the claim defense office out of a room behind the band office, and some mornings she comes down to the dock while I'm loading and stands with her coffee and watches the river and says nothing, and I say nothing, and it is the best conversation I have most days. Two liars, we recognized each other once, on a ridge, in a chapter I won't retell. What we are now is two people who ran out of reasons to hide, standing in full daylight, finding it survivable.

In July she showed me something. We hiked up past Windy Point to the outcrop where the core samples first came from, and she pulled a folded paper from her jacket—the original field sketch, Voss's own hand, the ore body drawn true, with the Kettle River traplines marked on top of it in red pencil. "He wanted you to have this," she said. "He said you carried it farther than it deserved to be carried."

I keep it in the cabin now, tacked above the table where I plan my runs. Not framed. Framed would make it finished. Tacked means ongoing.

* * *

August, and the first frost already silvering the sedge at dawn. I was hauling gravel for the new foundation at the elder's complex, running the loader back and forth, when Josephine came out and stood in my path until I shut the machine down.

"You measure your life in miles no more," she said. It wasn't a question either. Nothing with Josephine ever was.

"No," I admitted. "Years now."

"And what do the years hold?"

I looked at her. She was seventy-eight and had asked me, eleven months ago on this same ground, the question that had gutted me: whose side are you on when it costs you? I'd answered it with an airplane and a stolen takeoff and a box canyon and a burned logbook, and I'd thought the answering was done.

"The years hold this," I said. "This river. This work. These people."

"That is not an answer. That is a place."

"It's the same thing, out here."

She considered that for a long moment, longer than the schedule allowed, longer than I deserved. Then she nodded once, the way a judge nods, and walked on, and I understood that I had passed something, though I couldn't have said what, and that the passing was itself the answer.

* * *

September. The swans went through overhead in great wavering V's, heading south, and I stood on the dock and watched them and felt nothing that needed fixing. Mara found me there.

"Alfred radioed," she said. "He's picking up the winter medical charter next week. First season solo." A pause. "He asked if you'd ride along on the checkout run. Just the one trip. Just to show him the ice reading on Great Bear."

I laughed. "They'll never let me near a cockpit again."

"They don't have to let you. It's his plane. He can carry whoever he wants."

I looked south, down the river, toward Fort Providence, toward the strips and the freight docks and the doors that were closed to me. Then north, at the swans, going where swans go without needing permission.

"One trip," I said.

"One trip," she agreed. "Then home."

Home. I rolled the word around like a core sample, checking it for fractures. It held.

* * *

October, and the ice was forming on the shallows in long white feathers, and I made the last boat run of the season with a load of stove oil and a crate containing the community's new satellite phone, and halfway up the narrows I stopped the engine and just drifted for a while, because the light was doing something to the water that deserved witnesses.

This is what I know now, at the end of the story and the beginning of whatever comes after it.

Justice doesn't arrive. It gets carried—by feverish men guarding satchels, by drunk men finding their last shred of courage, by women on ridges deciding to signal, by elders who ask questions that gut you, by pilots who burn their own escape routes. It gets carried the way medicine gets carried: slowly, in small containers, at cost, by whoever happens to be passing through. And sometimes the carrying changes the carrier. Sometimes the courier lands.

I used to think the uncharted landfall was a place you escaped to—some blank space on the map where a man could disappear and be free. I had it backwards. The blank spaces aren't escapes. They're assignments. Every white space on every map is somebody's home, somebody's trapline, somebody's grandfather's grave, and the whole corrupt machinery of the world runs on pretending otherwise. The surveyors pretended otherwise for money. Ottawa pretended otherwise for ore. Corliss pretended otherwise for his employers, and would have pretended otherwise to the end, if his own pilot hadn't handed over the radio records.

I stopped pretending in a trapper's cabin, holding a dying man's oath I'd given too easily. I've been paying for it ever since, gladly, in the only currency that matters out here: presence. Showing up. Staying.

The plane is gone. The logs are ash. The name Sable Winterbourne is known now in rooms I'll never enter, attached to a story I helped carry to daylight. Some nights I miss the anonymity like a phantom limb. Most nights I sleep like a man who paid his debts.

* * *

On the last day of October, Alfred's Cessna—my Cessna, though ownership is a fiction the North tolerates and forgets—came down through the first real snow of the year and touched the strip at Kettle River, and I was waiting for it, and I got to ride in the right seat one more time, up the valley, over Great Bear, reading the ice for a younger man's eyes while he flew and I remembered.

"There," I said, pointing to the dark patch off the point. "Current under the ice. Never trust it before December."

He wrote it down. In ten years someone will write it down for someone else, and the route will go on being true long after both our names wear off the page.

We landed at dusk. I shook his hand on the strip, in the falling snow, and walked away from the aircraft without looking back, which is the hardest thing a pilot ever learns, and I walked up the hill to the lit windows where Mara was keeping supper warm, and Josephine was keeping the kettle hot, and the community was keeping the land, and the land was keeping all of us.

Behind me the plane sat quiet in the snow, going white, becoming part of the country it had crossed a thousand times.

Ahead of me the trail ran down to the dock, and the dock ran out onto the black ice, and beyond the ice the river went on into the dark toward spring, toward the open water, toward the barges that would come again in June with their loads of flour and fuel and news.

I measured my life in miles for ten years, and in those miles I was free the way a bird is free—free to leave, free to vanish, free to matter to no one. I measure it in years now, and in years I am free the other way: bound to a place, bound to people, bound to the slow work of justice that gets carried and carried and carried until it arrives.

The map still has blank spaces. I live in one. But I am not lost in it, and I am not hiding in it, and I am not passing through it.

I landed here.
