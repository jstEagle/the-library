## 1. The Wreck Nobody Names

The sea keeps better records than any government I've ever worked for. It remembers every hull that ever broke against it, files it away in silt and salt, and waits — patient as a creditor — for someone to come asking.

I was the someone, forty-one meters down, hanging in the green dark off a wreck the navy swore didn't exist.

My dive computer said the water was eleven degrees. My body said it was colder than that, because bodies are honest and computers only do arithmetic. The wreck sat on its starboard side like a drunk sleeping off a bender, keel toward the surface, propeller still turning slowly in the current as if it hadn't gotten the memo about being dead. Corals had colonized the railings. A school of fusiliers poured through the bridge windows like smoke through a broken house.

I counted my way down the hull. That's what I do. That's what I've always done. Miles are the only currency I've ever trusted — you can counterfeit money, love, loyalty, even grief if you practice enough, but a mile is a mile is a mile. You swim it or you don't. My father taught me that before he taught me to swim, back when we were counting kilometers between fishing villages instead of dollars. He used to say: *Lena, everything worth having is measured in distance, not in things.* Then he died owing money to people who measured in neither, which I've decided not to read as a lesson.

The liaison — Commander Osei, though he'd introduced himself as "Mr. Osei from the Maritime Registry," which was already a lie wearing a uniform's posture — had given me coordinates, a depth, and a story. Cargo vessel, he said. *Meridian Star*, Panamanian registry, lost with all hands in a storm nine years ago. Salvage rights pending litigation. My job: recover the voyage data recorder from the wheelhouse, place it in the pressure case he'd provided, bring it up, hand it over, sign a nondisclosure, cash a check, forget.

Simple. Clean. The kind of job that pays three months of fuel and Dev's medical debt and the new impeller the *Kestrel* has been begging for since the monsoon.

Except cargo vessels don't have sonar domes.

I ran my glove along the bulge under the bow, felt the smooth composite where barnacles should have been fighting for real estate. Sonar dome. And further aft, where the superstructure narrowed, two mounts that had once held something heavier than cargo cranes. The navy doesn't lose cargo vessels with weapons mounts. The navy loses warships and then calls them something else, the way you'd call a funeral a "celebration of life" so nobody has to say the word.

I hung there in the cold and let the current push at me, and I thought about the check. Three months of fuel. Dev's lungs, which the clinic in Port Callas wanted four thousand for and I owed them six already. The impeller. Arithmetic is a tyrant, but it's an honest one, and right now the arithmetic said: *swim into the wheelhouse, take the box, don't look at anything else.*

Arithmetic also said the *Meridian Star* was a lie, because I'd done salvage long enough to know tonnage when I saw it, and this ship displaced twice what the paperwork claimed. You can't fake displacement. Water doesn't negotiate.

* * *

The wheelhouse was a cathedral of soft rot. Silt lay over the deck like gray snow, and my lights carved tunnels through the murk. The VDR housing was where it always is — aft bulkhead, armored capsule, bright orange even under nine years of grime, because the people who build those things assume somebody will come looking. Somebody always comes looking. It's the whole point of them.

I cut the retaining bolts, worked the capsule free, and that's when my light caught the second box.

It sat bolted to the deck beside the VDR mount, smaller, matte black, no markings except a stenciled code half-eaten by corrosion: **SC-114 / DO NOT SURFACE**. Which is a funny thing to stencil on equipment, because equipment doesn't care where it surfaces. Only people do. Only people need to be told.

Here's the thing about me and instructions. I follow them beautifully when they make sense. When they don't — when someone tells me *don't look* — some gear in my chest strips and starts grinding. My father called it stubbornness. My ex called it a death wish. Dev calls it Tuesday.

I didn't touch the black box. I want that on the record, wherever records go when nobody names them. I took the orange capsule, sealed it in the pressure case, checked the seals twice, and started my ascent. And then, at fifteen meters, doing my safety stop like a good little professional, I looked down at the wreck spread out below me in the dark like a sentence waiting for its period, and I thought: *nine years.*

Nine years, and nobody had come looking until now. Nine years, and the navy needed a freelance salvager with a bad credit rating instead of its own divers. Nine years, and a man calling himself Mr. Osei had lied to my face about a registry I could verify in ten minutes on any port terminal — and had known, the way liars know, that I'd verify it later rather than sooner, because sooner would cost him a diver and later would cost me nothing but sleep.

I surfaced into gray afternoon light and the smell of diesel and rain coming. The *Kestrel* rocked at her mooring, our whole world in thirty meters of rusted steel — Dev in the wheelhouse wrapped in a blanket despite the heat, running our books on a cracked tablet; Priya on deck coiling line with the economy of motion of someone who grew up hauling nets; the tender lashed down; the fuel gauge lying to us the way gauges do when they're embarrassed.

Osei stood on the dock in civilian clothes that fit him like a disguise. He watched me climb the ladder with the particular attention of a man watching his money come out of the water.

"You found it," he said. Not a question.

"Voyage data recorder." I set the pressure case on the dock between us. "Housing intact. Seals intact. Should give you whatever you're after."

"We're after closure, Ms. Zhou. For the families."

There were no families. I'd checked. The *Meridian Star*'s crew manifest listed fourteen men with names that traced back to nothing — ghost entries, payroll ghosts, the kind of paper trail that exists specifically so it can end. But I said, "Of course. Closure," because the check was already written and sitting in his breast pocket, and because I am not a fool, whatever else I am.

He handed it over. I looked at the number longer than politeness required, because the number was the trap's cheese and I wanted him to see me smell it.

"One question," I said. "Registry says Panama. Tonnage says otherwise. Hull configuration says otherwise. So either the *Meridian Star* was the world's largest outrigger canoe, or she was never Panamanian at all."

Something moved behind Osei's eyes — not guilt, exactly. Guilt implies a conscience. It was more like recalculation. A man deciding how much truth a woman at the bottom of her finances could safely be told.

"She was re-flagged," he said. "After acquisition. Common practice."

"Re-flagged from what?"

"A defunct line." He smiled, and the smile had been assembled from parts. "Ms. Zhou. You recovered a data recorder. That was the scope of your contract. Curiosity is a wonderful quality in a person and a terrible one in a contractor."

"There's another box down there," I said. "Bolted next to the recorder. Stenciled *do not surface*. Care to tell me what I wasn't supposed to find?"

And there it was — the crack, hairline, running through the whole polished surface of him. He didn't answer fast enough. He answered maybe half a second too late, and in that half-second I watched him decide to lie to me again, deliberately, with full knowledge that I'd catch it, because catching it wouldn't matter. Because I was a tool with a boat and he was a man with a budget, and tools get lied to.

"No other box," he said. "You're mistaken. Dives at that depth produce nitrogen narcosis. Confusion is common."

"I did a hundred and twelve minutes bottom time with a twenty-minute margin," I said. "I don't confuse."

"Then perhaps you imagined it." He stepped back from the pressure case, and just before he turned to go, he said it — lightly, almost kindly, which is what made it land like a hook behind the eye:

"For your own sake, Ms. Zhou, stop asking about this ship. Some wrecks are cheaper left unnamed."

Then he was gone, walking down the dock with the pressure case, past the fuel depot, past the chandler, out of my life — except that he wasn't, because that night I sat in the *Kestrel*'s wheelhouse with the check propped against the compass and couldn't make myself feel paid.

* * *

Dev found me there at midnight. He does that — materializes with tea he's burned and questions he hasn't. He lowered himself onto the bench across from me, tablet balanced on his knees, and looked at the check, and then at me, and then at the check again.

"That's three months," he said. "Fuel, my clinic bill, the impeller, and Priya's cut with change left over. That's us breathing until spring."

"I know what it is."

"So why do you look like a woman doing math she hates?"

Because I was. Because the numbers kept refusing to close. Because somewhere between the sonar dome and Osei's smile, the job had stopped being a job and become a question, and questions are the one cargo I've never figured out how to jettison.

"The ship was armed," I said. "Warship, re-flagged, sunk with a crew of ghosts, and the navy hired a broke salvager to pull the recorder instead of sending its own people. And there's a second box down there that says *do not surface* in letters big enough to read through nine years of algae."

Dev was quiet for a while. Rain started on the wheelhouse roof, soft as fingers.

"And the second box," he said slowly, "is still down there."

"It's still down there."

"At forty-one meters. On a classified wreck. That a commander from naval intelligence told you to forget."

"He told me to stop asking. Different verb. Asking implies I already started."

Dev put the tablet down. He rubbed his face with both hands, the gesture of a man aging in fast-forward, and when he looked at me again his eyes had that terrible light they get — the one that means his caution and my curiosity have agreed, against both their wills, on a course of action.

"Lena," he said. "We are six hundred dollars from eating rice and apologies. If you go back down on that wreck without a contract, without a client, without cover —"

"If I go back down," I said, "and that box says what I think it says, then we're not six hundred dollars from anything anymore. We're on the wrong side of something. And everything changes."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not supposed to be. Nothing true is."

He sighed the sigh of a man who has followed me into worse for less. Outside, the rain thickened, and the harbor lights smeared across the black water, and somewhere out past the breakwater, forty-one meters down, a ship with no name kept its secrets in the cold.

I counted the miles in my head — Port Callas to the wreck site, eleven nautical. Eleven miles of water between me and a stenciled warning I couldn't stop hearing.

Eleven miles. Close enough to reach by morning.

Close enough that I already knew I would.
