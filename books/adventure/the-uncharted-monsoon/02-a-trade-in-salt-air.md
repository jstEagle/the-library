## 2. A Trade in Salt Air

Three days after I handed the black box to a man who lied to my face about a ship's registry, I was drinking cheap rum in a port bar called the Drowned Compass, counting the money I didn't have.

I do this thing — maybe you've met people like me — where I measure everything in miles. Not dollars. Dollars are slippery; they evaporate through engine gaskets and harbor fees and Dev's habit of betting on losing dogs. But miles are honest. A mile is a mile whether you're rich or broke, and you always know exactly how many you have left in the tank. That night at the Drowned Compass, the *Pelican's Wake* had fuel for perhaps ninety river miles, a crew of four owed six weeks' pay, and a hull survey due that would cost more than both combined. In mileage terms, we were running on fumes and pride, and pride burns hotter but gets you nowhere.

So when the old man at the end of the bar started coughing like his lungs were filing for divorce, I did what any sensible captain does: I ignored him and ordered another rum.

He ignored my ignoring him.

"You're Zhou," he said. "The diver."

"Depends who's asking and what it costs me."

"Ansel Kade." He dragged his stool closer with a sound like a bone setting wrong. Up close he was all weathered leather and wire, the kind of thin that comes from inside out, from something eating you rather than anything as innocent as poverty. His hands were prospector's hands, scarred and stained, and one of them rested flat on the bar like it was holding down a map only he could see. "You pulled something off that wreck last week. Off the *Corvane*."

The name went through me like cold water through a wetsuit. Nobody said that name. The navy liaison had called it "the vessel in question," twice, and then changed the subject to payment schedules both times.

"I don't know what you're talking about," I said, which was true in the way that mattered legally and false in every way that counted.

Kade smiled. It wasn't a nice smile; it was the smile of a man who'd spent forty years finding things other people buried and had run out of patience for pretense somewhere around year twelve. "Good answer. Wrong one, but good." He coughed again, wet and long, and when he came up for air there was blood on his lip that he wiped away with the casualness of a man brushing off crumbs. "I'm dying, Captain. Doctor gave me a season. Maybe two if I cheat, and I've never been much good at cheating."

"Everyone in this bar is dying of something. Mostly the rum."

"The rum's the least of it." He signaled the bartender with two fingers, and two fresh glasses appeared, which meant he'd bought ahead, which meant he'd planned this conversation before I walked in. That should have worried me more than it did. "I want to make you an offer."

"I'm expensive."

"You're broke," he said pleasantly. "I asked around. Your engineer's been pawning tools. Your cook is feeding four people on credit from three different vendors. And your boat hasn't had bottom paint since the last administration." He slid one of the glasses toward me. "Drink. This goes better if you drink."

I drank. It was terrible. It was also free, and I've never pretended to have standards above my bank balance.

* * *

Here's what Ansel Kade wanted, and I want you to appreciate how insane it was, because at the time I certainly didn't:

He wanted me to take a sealed tin upriver. The Serein River — the whole length of it, past the checkpoints, past the consortium territory, past the rapids they don't put on charts because boats that go in don't come out — to a river town called Vessel, and find a woman named Mara, and put the tin in her hands, and tell her nothing else except that it came from Ansel Kade.

That was it. That was the whole job. No questions about contents. No questions about Mara. No questions about why a dying prospector couldn't mail a package like a normal human being.

In exchange: five thousand now, and a hand-drawn map of the Serein headwaters.

I need to explain the second part, because the first part was just money and money is simple. The Serein headwaters are forbidden. Not dangerous-forbidden, not private-property-forbidden — *forbidden*-forbidden, the kind of forbidden where the consortium pays the local government so well that maps get quietly withdrawn from circulation and pilots who ask about the upper river develop sudden amnesia. Salvage divers trade rumors about it the way sailors used to trade rumors about sea serpents. There's supposed to be something up there worth more than everything downstream combined, and nobody who's gone looking has come back with proof or pictures or their deposit money.

And this dying man had drawn it. By hand. From memory, or from having been there, which amounted to the same crime.

"How accurate is it?" I heard myself ask, which is how you know you're already lost. A smart woman asks about the money. Only a woman planning a betrayal asks about the map.

"Every bend from Vessel to the source," Kade said. "Depths, hazards, the safe channels through Devil's Throat. Forty years of walking that country while men with guns weren't looking." He tapped his temple. "It's not written down anywhere else. When I go, it goes with me unless it goes with you."

"And the tin?"

"Is not your business."

"Everything on my boat is my business. That's what 'my boat' means."

"Then it's cargo," he said. "Sealed cargo. You've carried sealed cargo before, Captain. You carried something sealed off the *Corvane* and never opened it, I'd wager, because you're still breathing." His eyes were pale gray and entirely without mercy. "Consider this the same job, going the other direction. Something hidden coming back into the light instead of into a drawer."

I should mention that I hadn't told anyone about the data core. Not Dev, not Priya, not even in the encrypted log I keep under a fake name. I'd turned over the black box to the navy liaison exactly as contracted, and the only reason I knew the registry number on the wreck didn't match the registry number in my briefing file was that I read things I'm not supposed to read, professionally speaking, because a diver who doesn't read the hull she's diving on ends up as part of the salvage.

But Kade knew the ship's name. The real name. Which meant either he'd been watching me, or he'd known about the *Corvane* long before I ever got wet.

Both options were bad. I drank more rum.

* * *

Let me be honest about my thought process, because honesty is cheap now and expensive later, and I like to spend it early.

My plan, sitting on that barstool, was to take his money and renege. Take the five thousand, take the map, sell the map to someone with a gunboat and a grudge against the consortium, use the proceeds to fix the *Wake* and pay my crew, and let the tin gather dust in a locker until it rusted shut. A promise made to a dying stranger in a port bar isn't a contract; it's a story you tell yourself so the taking feels like trading. I've watched better people than me rationalize worse. I had it all worked out before he finished his second sentence — the moral arithmetic of it, the clean ledger. He was dead in a season anyway. The tin would mean nothing to a dead man. Mara would never know what she hadn't received. The world would turn.

Except.

Except that he coughed blood onto the bar and wiped it up himself, embarrassed, like a man apologizing for spilling a drink, and something in my chest did a thing I didn't authorize.

Except that when he unrolled the map across the sticky wood between us — careful, reverent, the way you handle a thing that took decades — I saw the headwaters rendered in a draftsman's hand crossed with a lover's. Little annotations in the margins. *Cold spring here, sweetest water on the river.* *Eagles nest on the third bluff, March.* A sketch of a waterfall with a note: *M. would have liked this.* The M was underlined twice.

Except that he looked at me when he finished talking, and said, very quietly, "I know you'll think about keeping it. Everyone thinks about keeping it. I'm not asking you to be good, Captain. I'm asking you to be the kind of person who can't stand owing a debt." A pause. "Are you that kind?"

I am, God help me. I hate owing. Owing is just another unit of measurement, and I measure everything.

"Five thousand," I said. "Half now."

"Four now, one on delivery." He counted bills from an envelope that had clearly been waiting in his coat for days. "And the map is yours regardless. Consider it payment for the miles."

"The miles are the expensive part."

"They usually are," Ansel Kade said, and coughed, and laughed at his own coughing, which is either bravery or the rum, and in port bars the two are indistinguishable.

* * *

I walked back to the dock at midnight with the tin in my rucksack and the map folded against my chest like a second heartbeat, and I told myself the lie I'd prepared: *you'll sell it, you'll sell it, you'll sell it.*

Dev was up, naturally, because Dev treats sleep as a rumor he refuses to confirm. He was sitting in the galley rebuilding a water pump that didn't need rebuilding, which is what Dev does instead of talking about money.

"Well?" he said.

"We have work."

His face did the complicated thing — relief and suspicion fighting for the same real estate. "Legal work?"

"Define legal."

"Lena."

"It's a delivery job. Upriver." I set the rucksack on the table between us, and the tin made a sound against the metal, a small dense clunk, like a secret settling. "Vessel. Then possibly farther."

"Farther how far?"

I didn't answer, and Dev looked at the rucksack, and then at me, and being Dev, he understood immediately and completely and hated every second of it. "That's consortium water," he said. "Past Vessel, that's all consortium water."

"I know."

"And we have fuel for—"

"Ninety miles. I know."

He wiped his hands on a rag very slowly, buying time the way mechanics do. "Who's paying?"

"A dying man."

"That's never once been a good sign." But he was already looking at the pump, calculating, because Dev's loyalty works like his engines: you don't ask it to justify itself, you just feed it and stay out of its way. "When do we leave?"

"Tomorrow. Tide's at seven."

He nodded and went back to the pump he'd already fixed, and I stood there in the galley of my beautiful, insolvent boat, listening to the harbor slap against the hull, and I took out the map and looked at it again under the bad light.

*M. would have liked this.*

I told myself, one more time, firmly, the way you tell a dog to stay: *sell it.*

Then I folded it away, and went to bed, and dreamed about a river I'd never seen, running cold and silver through country that belonged to no one, and woke at dawn with salt air in the rigging and the certain, sickening knowledge that I was going to keep the promise after all — that some part of me had signed for it the moment the old man bled on the bar and apologized for the mess.

The tide came in at seven. We cast off at half past.

We were eleven miles upriver, past the last buoy and into water no chart bothered to name, when I noticed the boat behind us. Small, dark, running without lights, holding our wake at exactly the distance of something that didn't want to be seen.

It had been there since the harbor.

It was still there when the sun went down.
