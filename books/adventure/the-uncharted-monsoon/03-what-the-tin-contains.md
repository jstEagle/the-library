## 3. What the Tin Contains

I lasted eleven hours.

That's a personal record for restraint, and I want it noted somewhere that I'm proud of it, because everything else about that night I am not proud of. Eleven hours of running the *Magpie*'s engine checks, re-splicing a frayed winch line that didn't need it, and inventorying a fuel cache I'd counted twice already. Dev watched me do all of it with the particular patience of a man who has known me long enough to read my tells like weather.

"You're going to open it," he said around hour nine, not looking up from the carburetor he was rebuilding with hands too big for the work.

"I made a promise."

"You made a promise to *deliver* it. Nobody said anything about not reading the mail." He set a valve down with a click. "Besides, you've been circling that locker like a shark since we left port. It's exhausting to watch."

He wasn't wrong. The tin sat in the forward locker where I'd stowed it after Ansel Kade died three days later in a hospice bed that smelled of salt and antiseptic, his hand still warm around mine when he made me swear. A sealed ammunition tin, army green, heavy for its size, wrapped twice in oilcloth and tied with wire that had been twisted by someone who expected it to matter. Ansel had weighed maybe a hundred and ten pounds by then, most of it cancer, but his grip on my wrist had been iron. He'd spent his last coherent currency — a map drawn in ballpoint on the back of a tide chart, headwaters of the Serein River, territory marked RESTRICTED in letters pressed hard enough to tear paper — and all he'd asked was that I carry the tin to a woman named Mara in Vessel.

I'd said yes because dying men deserve easy lies, and because the money he'd offered was real, and because I told myself I'd find Mara, hand it over, collect, and never think about any of it again.

Eleven hours. Then I waited until Dev turned in, and I opened it anyway.

* * *

The wire fought me. Of course it did. Ansel had twisted it with pliers and then flattened the ends against the tin so you'd have to cut or mangle it — a man's way of saying *think first*. I thought first for about four seconds, then got the bolt cutters.

Inside: oilcloth again, and beneath it a bundle of papers in plastic sleeves, a fat envelope of photographs, and a small notebook with a rubber band gone brittle around it. No letter. Ansel had been a prospector, not a novelist. The documents would speak for themselves or not at all.

They spoke.

The top sleeve held survey sheets — proper ones, stamped with a firm name I recognized from salvage contracts, dated fourteen years back. Bathymetric charts of the Serein's upper reaches, core sample logs, mineral assays. Gold, mostly, but also something rarer in the trace columns, some rare-earth signature that explained why anyone would bother fighting a river that wanted them dead. The surveys were thorough. They were also, every one of them, marked with a stamp I had to look at three times before my brain agreed to process it: **HYDROGRAPHIC SURVEY — ROYAL NAVY HYDROGRAPHIC OFFICE**.

Navy charts. Navy equipment. Navy personnel listed as survey leads, their service numbers printed neat as hymnals.

I sat in the galley with the cabin lamp turned low and read for two hours, and the longer I read, the colder the boat felt around me, though the night was warm enough that sweat stood on my forearms.

Here is what the papers said, in the flat language of paperwork, which is always worse than anyone saying it out loud:

Fourteen years ago, a joint survey team — navy hydrographers and contracted geologists — mapped the Serein headwaters and found a deposit worth more than the town downstream, the fishery, and probably the province. The mining consortium that eventually moved in didn't discover anything. They were handed the maps. And the price of those maps was written into a memorandum of understanding, initialed by two naval officers whose names meant nothing to me and one line that meant everything: *survey vessel support to be rendered discreet; no public registry of operations.*

No public registry. Which is why, when I dove the wreck off the coast two weeks ago, my liaison Commander Voss had told me the hull was a decommissioned coastal freighter, registry unknown, records lost in an administrative shuffle. I'd asked him point-blank which registry, and he'd smiled his government smile and said *the kind that doesn't exist anymore, Ms. Zhou*, and I'd laughed because it was a good line.

Except now I was holding the registry. Or rather, holding its absence, documented. The wreck I'd dived — the one the navy paid me quietly, in cash, to crack open and retrieve a data core from — was the *SNS Meridian*, the survey vessel itself. Not lost. Scuttled, by the looks of the cutting charges I'd found folded into her keel like a confession, once the operation went fully private and the navy's fingerprints needed wiping off the crime scene. The data core I'd pulled from her server room contained the original digital surveys. The papers in this tin were the paper originals, smuggled out fourteen years ago by someone who'd understood what "no public registry" really meant.

And the notebook — Ansel's, the handwriting matched the map — filled in the human arithmetic. Dated entries across four years. Water samples from below the mine site, upstream, where the Serein ran orange with tailings nobody official had ever admitted existed. Fish counts, dropping. A list of names under a heading he'd written as *PAID TO LOOK AWAY*: harbor officials, a provincial minister, two journalists who'd started asking questions and then stopped very suddenly, one of them permanently according to a newspaper clipping Ansel had kept like a wound.

The last entry was six months old. Just coordinates, the mine site, and one sentence: *They know I have these. If anything happens to me, Mara Voss knows where everything is buried.*

Mara Voss. Not just Mara. I stared at that name for a long time, because Voss was also the surname of the commander who'd hired me for the wreck job, and I don't believe in coincidences that convenient. Either Ansel hadn't known, or he'd known exactly what he was doing when he put that tin in my hands — putting it in the current, letting the river decide.

I repacked everything exactly as I'd found it, down to the fold of the oilcloth, and wired the tin shut with new wire bent to mimic the old twists. Then I sat in the dark and listened to the water slap the hull and did the math I always do, the only math I trust: miles. Miles to Vessel, upriver. Miles of river between me and a consortium that had killed a journalist and scuttled a warship's little cousin to keep a secret. Miles between me and whatever Commander Voss would do when he learned his quiet salvage diver had come up holding the whole story in a biscuit tin.

Every mile said run. Take the cash from the navy job, already spent on engine parts and debt, and disappear south where nobody asks questions.

But I'd read the notebook. Ansel had spent years wading that poisoned water alone, counting dead fish, writing down names, believing paper could outlive men. He'd bet his last mile on me.

I hated him a little for it. I put the tin back in the locker and lay awake until dawn doing arithmetic that wouldn't balance.

* * *

Dev took the midwatch, which is how I ended up hearing the story secondhand, from his own mouth, the next morning over coffee strong enough to strip paint. But this part is his, so I'll let him tell it:

---

Dev here. Lena says I get to talk, so I'll be quick, because she's giving me That Look.

Around 0300 I heard the dinghy. No engine — oars, muffled, which is already wrong, because nobody rows at 0300 unless they don't want to be heard. I killed the cabin lamp and looked through the port and there were two of them coming alongside, dark clothing, no lights, moving like they'd done boardings before. One stayed with the dinghy. One came over the rail quiet as fog.

I did the smart thing, which is nothing. I sat in the dark with the wrench I keep by the bunk and I watched him search our boat. He knew boats, I'll give him that. Checked the lockers in order, checked under the bunks, checked the bilge access, spent a long minute at the forward locker — the one with the tin — and I'll admit my heart was going like a drum line. But Lena had moved it. She'd moved it at dawn, after her little midnight library session, into the spare parts bin in the engine room, wrapped in a rag among the impellers, which is the single ugliest hiding spot on this boat and therefore the best one. The searcher opened the forward locker, found dive gear and rope, and moved on.

He was aboard maybe six minutes. Then he tapped the rail twice, dropped back into the dinghy, and they rowed off toward the harbor mouth without a word between them. Professionals. Nobody screamed, nothing torn up, nothing taken. A message search. *We can reach you.* 

When Lena came up at 0600 I told her all of it, calm as I could, and I watched her face do something I've seen maybe twice in nine years of crewing together — go still, all the way down, like water freezing from the bottom up.

"They didn't take anything," she said.

"No."

"Because they don't know what to take. Yet." She poured coffee she didn't drink. "Which means they know the tin exists, and they don't know if I have it. Someone talked at the hospice. Or the hospital. Or—" She stopped, because we both arrived at the same name at the same time.

Voss.

"So," Dev said, because somebody had to. "What's the play, skipper?"

Lena looked at the harbor, at the patrol boat idling way too casually near the channel marker, and I saw her make the decision the way she makes all of them — fast, and then forever.

"We run the Serein tonight," she said. "By dark. Get ahead of whoever they send next."

"That's consortium water, Lena. Patrols, checkpoints—"

"It's also the only road to Vessel." She finally drank the coffee, grimaced. "Ansel drew me a map, Dev. Time to find out if it's worth the paper."

---

Back to me. We spent the day making the *Magpie* boring — dive gear stowed deep, the tin riding in the parts bin like a passenger with a false passport, Dev's shotgun broken down and distributed into places a casual inspector wouldn't look. I burned Ansel's hospice admission slip and my copy of the navy work order in the galley stove and watched the ashes, feeling like I was burning my own exit.

Because here's the thing I understood standing there: I couldn't put it down now. Not the tin — the knowledge. You can hand back an object. You can't hand back knowing that a navy scuttled its own ship and called it weather, that a river runs orange while ministers sign memos, that a man died counting fish because the truth needed one witness with clean hands. The map in my chart drawer had stopped being a favor to a dead man sometime around 0300 that morning. It was a weapon now, and weapons pointed at other people have a way of pointing back.

At dusk we slipped the mooring without running lights, and the Serein opened ahead of us, black and wide and patient, carrying its poison down to a sea full of people who'd been paid not to taste it.

Two miles upriver, a light came on behind us. Then another.
