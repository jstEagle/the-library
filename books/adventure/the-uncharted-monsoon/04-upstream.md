## 4. Upstream

I learned the Serein the way you learn a person who lies to you — slowly, and then all at once.

My father used to say the river had two voices. The one it sang at noon, wide and warm and honest as a handshake. And the one it sang after dark, when the current dropped its manners and spoke in undertones, telling you where the snags waited, where the banks went soft, where men with guns kept their boats tied under tarps so the satellites wouldn't count them. Ansel Kade sang both voices back to himself for forty years, humming them into charts he drew by hand on butcher paper, because no surveyor was ever going to be paid enough — or brave enough — to map what the consortium wanted unmapped.

He died on a Tuesday, in a port town that smelled of diesel and salt, holding a tin he'd carried three thousand miles and a promise he'd extracted from a stranger. I didn't know any of this yet. On the Tuesday my father died, I was in Vessel, mending nets I couldn't afford to replace, waiting for a man who had promised me since spring that he was coming home.

* * *

The first I knew of Lena Zhou's boat was the sound of it — an engine run low and quiet, the way poachers run engines, the way my father taught me to listen for before he taught me to read. It came upriver in the black hours, hugging the eastern bank where the channel deepens against the bluff, running without lights past the patrol moorings at Kilometer Forty like a thief who knows exactly which houses have dogs.

Vessel is not much of a town. A fuel dock, a church whose bell hasn't rung since the consortium bought the bell-ringer, forty roofs, and a riverfront that changes its story depending on who's asking. The consortium's men ask often. They come down from the mine in fastboats with registration numbers filed off, and they buy silence the way other people buy cigarettes — by the carton, standing at the counter of Ovid's store, cash on the wood. Half the town takes the money. The other half takes the money too, they just pretend it's wages.

I don't take it. I want that said plainly, because everything else about me is about to become complicated. My father raised me to read water, not ledgers, but even I can do the arithmetic on what a kilo of ore buys versus what a kilo of discretion costs, and the whole river has decided discretion is the better trade. Vessel sells silence the way upstream towns sell gravel. I am, as far as I know, the last unsold thing on the lower Serein.

So when the quiet boat came past at three in the morning, I did what my father trained me to do without ever once saying he was training me: I watched. From the reed bank below the ruined customs house, where the current runs slack and a skiff can sit invisible if you know which shadow to borrow. I watched a woman stand at the wheelhouse rail with a chart spread on her knee and a red-lens torch pinning it flat, and I thought — there. That's how he held his maps. That angle of the wrist. That hunger in the way she checked the bank twice before trusting it.

She anchored in the cut behind Gull Island, where the dredge spoils make a lagoon no patrol boat bothers with because the charts call it shoal water and the charts are wrong. Everything on this river is wrong. That's rather the point of it.

* * *

They came ashore at dawn looking for fuel, which is the polite fiction every boat tells when what it wants is information. Dev — big shoulders, kind hands, a limp he'd earned somewhere he wouldn't name — walked up the dock path first, and I let him find me the way you let a stranger find you: by being exactly where the obvious answer is.

"You're the net-mender," he said. "Mara."

"I'm the net-mender." I am also, though he didn't know it yet, the daughter of the man whose map was folded inside the chart case on their deck. But inheritances announce themselves in order. First the face, then the name, then the blood.

Lena came up after him, and I'll grant her this: she looked at me the way nobody on this river looks at anybody anymore, which is directly, without first calculating what I could be made to cost. She asked about fuel prices. She asked about the patrol schedule. She asked, carefully, in the tone of someone testing ice, whether anyone in Vessel had come through lately asking after strangers — and I told her the truth, which is that everyone asks after strangers here, because strangers are either money or trouble, and usually both.

Then she asked the question that changed the shape of my week. She said: does the name Kade mean anything to you?

I stood very still. There is a moment — everyone gets one or two in a life, if they're lucky, or unlucky, which may be the same inventory — when the ground tilts and you understand that everything you were bracing against is arriving at once. My father had been gone nine days. Nine days of not knowing where, or how, or whether the tin had burned with him in whatever fever took him, or been sold to some fixer for the price of a funeral. Nine days of rehearsing grief in an empty house with the shutters open to the river because he'd hated closed shutters, said they made a man dishonest about what he could see.

And now a salvage diver with salt still crusted on her collar was standing on my dock saying his name out loud, and I heard it the way you hear a bell you'd stopped believing would ring.

"Ansel Kade was my father," I said. And then, because grief makes liars of us all and I refused to be one in front of a stranger: "He was coming home. He wrote me every month. The last letter said the tin was safe and the map was finished and he'd see me before the rains."

Lena went quiet in a way that told me more than talking would have. Dev went quiet too, and then not quiet at all — he laughed, a short helpless sound, and I understood later that he was laughing at the arithmetic of it, the absurd coincidence, the river folding four lives into one eddy. But at that moment I only saw a woman flinch.

Because she had opened the tin. Of course she had. I knew it before she admitted it; you can always tell, with people, whether they've looked inside a sealed thing, the same way you can tell whether a door's been forced. Her hands moved differently around it. Careful hands, guilty hands.

"He died in Port Meridian," she said. "A bar called the Salt Air. He traded me the map for a promise — deliver the tin to Mara, in Vessel. I was going to renege. I want you to know that. I planned to sell it, or sink it, or forget it, whichever paid best." She paused. "Then I opened it."

I made her say what was inside. All of it. Standing on my own dock, in front of my own house, with the river I'd grown up on running brown and heavy behind her. Survey records. Coordinates. Dates. A chain of custody that started at a wreck off the coast — a wreck the navy pretended not to own — and ended at an open pit in the headwaters that appeared on no map, paid no royalty, poisoned the water my father had drunk for forty years, and was guarded by men whose salaries were drawn from an account with no name.

My family's stolen inheritance. That's what it was. Not metaphorically — literally, in the way that matters to people who live by water. The mine at the source was killing the fish runs. It was silting the channels my father had spent his life learning. Every dry season the catches got smaller and the consortium's boats got bigger, and the town explained it to itself as weather, as cycles, as bad luck, because explaining it truthfully would have meant admitting we'd all been bought and the price had been our own river.

Ansel hadn't written to tell me he was dying. He'd written to tell me he'd found the proof. He'd carried it alone because he trusted no one to carry it with him, and the loneliness of that — I felt it land in me like a stone dropped down a well, the long delayed echo of a man who'd spent decades being right in a country that paid him to be wrong.

* * *

That night we sat in my kitchen, which is also my father's kitchen, which will be somebody's kitchen long after the consortium has finished strip-mining the word *somebody*. Lena laid the tin's contents across my table next to his hand-drawn charts, and I watched the two documents recognize each other. His ink lines matched their survey coordinates within meters. His annotations — *soft bank, keep east*, *patrol mooring, unlit*, *the narrows lie about their depth* — explained precisely why the consortium's surveys avoided the reaches his charts covered. He hadn't just mapped the river. He'd mapped the gaps in everyone else's map, and the gaps were the crime.

"They searched our boat last night," Lena said, in Port Meridian. Strangers. Professional strangers, moving like men paid by the hour to be thorough. "After I opened the tin. Someone knew it existed."

Of course someone knew. My father had been watched for years — I'd found the evidence in small doses and never assembled it, because assembly felt like accusation and accusation felt like suicide. The letter carrier who delivered his mail had a consortium stipend. The fuel dock logged every jerrican he bought. Twice a year a fastboat with no registration idled past our house at dusk, close enough that I could hear the engine idle down, the crew scanning the bank with optics. They weren't hunting him. They were *herding* him — keeping him alive and moving and predictable, the way you manage an asset you intend to collect later. A dying prospector with proof in a tin is only dangerous until someone finds out who he means to give it to.

They'd found out. And now the search party that had swept Lena's hull in Port Meridian would be sweeping upriver behind her, buying information at every dock between the mouth and the falls, and every dock would sell, because selling was the local industry and honesty was a hobby for people who could afford hobbies.

Which meant one thing, arithmetically speaking, and I have never in my life enjoyed doing arithmetic until that night: we had to move faster than money could.

* * *

I gave them what my father left me besides the grief. The charts. All of them — the butcher-paper originals, curling and fox-eared, annotated in a hand I could read the way other daughters read their fathers' faces. I sat with Lena while she copied them, line by line, and I narrated what each mark meant, and somewhere in the third hour of that I stopped narrating and started testifying, though neither of us named it that yet. *This X is where the patrol boat grounds at low water if it takes the western channel. This dotted line is a portage my father cut in '09 and the consortium never found. This circle is a fuel cache, and no, I won't say where, not yet, not until I know your crew better than I know my own neighbors.*

Because here is the thing I understood, sitting across from a woman who had planned to betray a dying man and then hadn't: trust on this river isn't given or earned. It's *spent*, and you spend it in denominations small enough to survive being stolen. I gave her the channels first. The caches would come later, if she held. The names of the three people in Vessel who weren't bought — that I gave her immediately, because those were cheap and true, and cheap truths are how you open an account.

Dev helped me haul the skiff down the bank path in the dark, and I remember his hands on the gunwale, careful, apologetic, as if the boat were something alive he might bruise. Lena stood in the shallows checking the draft marks against my father's notes, and for a moment — one moment, brief as a strike of lightning, which is to say unforgettable — the three of us were doing exactly what my father had done his whole life alone: reading the river together, cross-checking, trusting the ink over the official story.

Two inheritances. Mine was paper and poison and a dead man's stubbornness. Hers was a data core fished out of a wreck she'd been ordered to forget, and a liaison who lied about registries, and a crew whose fuel bill exceeded their courage by a margin she was still calculating. Neither inheritance was comfortable. Both fit in a tin box. And the river ran through both of them like a spine, connecting a drowned ship off the coast to a bleeding pit at the source, and every kilometer of that connection was owned by people who would kill to keep it unconnected.

I lit the lamp in my father's room that night. First time since the news. I put his last letter on the table under it, the one that said *see you before the rains*, and I crossed out the words *see you* and wrote above them, in his style of hand because I'd practiced it since I was eight: *sent instead.*

Grief becoming resolve doesn't feel like transformation. It feels like bookkeeping. You take the weight of what you lost and you enter it in a ledger marked *owed*, and then you go out and collect. My father was owed forty years of a river's honesty. I intended to collect it in full, with interest, and I intended to spend every cent of it on the truth.

* * *

We cast off before dawn, running dark, taking the eastern channel past the customs ruin where I'd first seen her. As the boat swung into the current I stood on my dock and watched the wake fold away downstream-turned-upstream, and I felt the strangest sensation of my life, which was watching my own grief leave home. It went with them. Wrapped in oilcloth, packed beside the tin, annotated in two hands now instead of one.

Three days later, at Kilometer One-Ninety, where the river narrows against a bluff the charts mark as *unremarkable terrain*, a gray-hulled launch cut across our bow flying nothing at all — no flag, no numbers, no lights — and a man stepped aboard carrying himself like someone who had once mattered and been reminded, expensively, that he didn't anymore.

He introduced himself as Elias Rook, formerly of Naval Intelligence, currently of nowhere in particular. He said he wanted what we had. He said he wanted the truth exposed. He said these two things in the same breath, which is either sincerity or the oldest confidence trick on the river, and he demanded the records as collateral, which is the gesture of a man who has been burned before or is planning to burn you.

Lena bargained. Lena always bargains; it's her native language. But I watched his hands, not his words, and his hands told me something his words hadn't: he handled my father's charts like a believer handles scripture. He read the annotations. He recognized the portage line. He said, quietly, almost involuntarily, *"Kade."*

Not *the prospector.* Not *your source.* Just — Kade. A name spoken with the weight of history behind it, the way you speak a name you've chased across years of files and dead ends and orders you were made to sign.

Whether that makes him a lifeline or the longest arm of the thing we're running from, none of us — least of all Rook — has decided yet.

But the river's already deciding for us. Devil's Throat is thirty kilometers ahead, and the water level gauge at the mission station reads the highest it's read in eleven years.
