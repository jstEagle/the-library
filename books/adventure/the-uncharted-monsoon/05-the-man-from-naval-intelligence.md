## 5. The Man from Naval Intelligence

There are two ways to intercept a boat on the Serein. The first is the way the consortium does it: a gunboat, a loudspeaker, and the arithmetic of fear. The second is the way I did it — alone, in a rented skiff with a dying outboard, holding up a thermos of coffee like a man offering a truce at a picnic.

I'd like to say I chose the second method out of principle. The truth is less flattering: I spent my last credible money on fuel, my last credible contacts stopped answering six months ago, and a man who has been formally relieved of his commission learns quickly that intimidation is a luxury purchased by institutions. I had nothing left to purchase with except the one commodity nobody in this river's economy deals in.

The truth. Or something shaped enough like it that people keep mistaking us.

They saw me before I saw them — of course they did; Lena Zhou runs her boat the way she dives, quiet and low and always reading the current for what's underneath. By the time I pulled alongside the *Corvina*, she was standing at the rail with a machete in one hand and an expression that suggested the machete was the polite option.

"You're a long way from your paperwork," she said.

"Paperwork and I are divorced," I said. "It kept the house."

Dev was behind her with something long-barreled he hadn't bothered to hide, and Mara was nowhere visible, which meant she was somewhere better — probably aft, probably armed, probably already deciding whether I was worth the bullet. A family business, then. Ansel Kade's daughter aboard, which meant the tin had been opened, which meant they knew what they were carrying, which meant every hour I delayed was another hour the consortium's fixers closed the gap.

I held up the thermos again. "Coffee. Real coffee. I know how that sounds coming from a stranger on a river where trust is measured in hull integrity, but I've been drinking reconstituted navy sludge for eleven years and I'm prepared to beg."

Lena didn't lower the machete. "You knew Ansel."

"I knew his file." I said it plainly because the alternative — some warm story about shared campfires — would have been a lie, and lies are the currency I'm trying to bankrupt. "I signed the order that redacted him. That's the closest thing to knowing a man my old job allowed."

Something moved behind her eyes. Not softening. Recalculation. She was doing arithmetic — I could see it, the same arithmetic I'd watched her do in the file footage from the inquiry hearings, when she sat in the public gallery taking notes no one asked her to take. What does this cost me? What does it buy? Is the exchange rate honest?

"Come aboard," she said finally. "You drink first."

* * *

I drank first. It was good coffee, which annoyed me, because I'd been counting on it being terrible so I could stop wanting it.

We sat in the wheelhouse while the river ran brown and fast beneath us, carrying the whole watershed's secrets toward the sea without comment. Lena poured herself a cup and didn't drink it — just held it, warming her hands, watching me over the rim. Dev stood by the door. Mara appeared from aft as if summoned by the word *Kade* and took up position near the chart table, arms folded, wearing her father's stubbornness like a coat that almost fit.

"So," Lena said. "Tell me why a disgraced naval investigator is alone on the Serein in a skiff with a bad engine and a thermos."

"Because I was right," I said, "and being right got me fired, and I haven't finished being right yet."

I told them the shape of it. Not everything — I'm not a fool, and neither are they — but the skeleton. Three years ago I ran an investigation into a hull loss off the coast. Registry discrepancies. Cargo manifests that didn't match the wreck. A recovery operation that happened too fast and too quietly, by contractors who weren't navy and whose invoices went through three shell companies before landing somewhere upstream. I wrote it up. My superior thanked me, sealed it, and reassigned me to logistics. When I pushed, I was pushed back — medically retired, honor intact, career dead, pension contingent on discretion.

"The wreck you dove," I said to Lena. "The black box. You were hired through a broker called Meridian Salvage Group."

Her face didn't change, but her hands tightened around the cup. Confirmation enough.

"Meridian is a consortium cutout. The salvage wasn't recovery. It was retrieval. They needed the core destroyed or disappeared, and they needed a diver deniable enough that if it surfaced, the trail died with her." I looked at her steadily. "You were supposed to be expendable, Ms. Zhou. The fact that you're sitting here means somebody's paperwork failed, and paperwork failures make institutions nervous. Nervous institutions send people."

"We've met the people," Dev said dryly. "They shot at us."

"That was the cheap kind. The expensive kind is still upstream."

Mara spoke for the first time. Her voice was quiet and had iron in it, the way her father's letters had iron in them — I'd read all forty-one pages during the worst year of my life, when the file was the only thing keeping the question alive. "Ansel wrote about a naval man. In his last letter. He said, *if a man named Rook finds you, listen carefully and believe half.*"

"And which half have you chosen?" I asked.

"The half where you're here, alone, with nothing to offer but words." She unfolded her arms. "Men with backing don't travel light. Men with nothing to lose travel exactly like you."

* * *

Here is what I did not tell them, and what I will set down now because a record should be honest even when its keeper isn't:

I did not come upriver alone by accident. I came alone because I don't know anymore whose orders I'd follow. For eleven years I was a man who believed the institution was the truth's delivery mechanism — clumsy, bureaucratic, but ultimately pointed at daylight. Then I watched the institution eat my investigation, my career, and Ansel Kade's name, and I understood that I had been not a delivery mechanism but a filing cabinet. Things went into me. Nothing came out.

So yes, I want the records. And yes, I demanded collateral, and yes, Lena Zhou looked at me across her wheelhouse with the flat patience of a woman who has been lied to by professionals and said:

"No."

Just that. No hedging, no negotiation theater. "No. You don't get the originals. You don't get custody. If you're what you say you are, you don't need them — you need what's *in* them, and I'll show you what's in them. On my boat. With my people. And copies stay with me, in places you'll never find, whether you're alive or dead when this ends."

It was the most honest sentence anyone had spoken to me in three years, and it stung like honesty does.

"Copies," I said. "Of course you made copies."

"I count miles," she said. "Counting is a habit. It spreads."

So we struck terms there in the wheelhouse, over cooling coffee, while the Serein shoved us both toward Devil's Throat whether we liked it or not. I get access — full access, here, now, witnessed. I get to verify the survey records against what I know of the official files, cross-check dates, confirm the chain from the wreck to the mine to the navy's silence. What I don't get is possession. The originals and the surviving copies belong to her crew and to Mara Kade, and if I turn out to be the consortium's longest reach, they lose nothing but time and whatever small warmth they'd extended me.

"If I'm your trap," I said, "you've lost nothing."

"If you're our trap," Lena said, "we've lost the hours. And the hours are the only thing we're rich in."

She spread the documents on the chart table and let me read, and I read, and God help me, it was all there. Every date I remembered. Every invoice trail I'd followed into the dark. The mine at the headwaters — the flooded pit, the tailings seeping into the Serein's source, the water table poisoned for a hundred kilometers downstream — operating under permits that existed in no registry, guarded by men who appeared on no payroll, protected by a silence that had been purchased at the highest level and maintained by people like the man I used to be.

I put the papers down. My hands weren't steady. I'm recording that too.

"Well?" Dev said.

"It's worse than my file," I said. "My file ended at the cover-up. This starts at the crime."

* * *

That night we anchored in a bend below the Throat's roar, and I couldn't sleep, so I sat on deck and listened to the river work. Lena came out around midnight and sat at the other end of the bench, close enough to talk, far enough to move.

"Ask me," she said.

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever you've been chewing since sundown. You've got a face like a man doing long division."

I considered lying, decided against it on principle, and then considered that principle itself and found it wanting. "Why keep me aboard? You could have taken my statements, copied my knowledge, and put me ashore at the mission station. Safer. Cleaner. One fewer variable in a boat full of variables."

She was quiet for a while. The river filled the silence the way rivers do — completely, without effort.

"Because Ansel trusted you enough to write your name down," she said finally. "A dying man with every reason to trust no one wrote *Rook* in the margin of his map, and underlined it twice. I never met him. But Mara did, and Mara says her father didn't underline things he wasn't sure of." She turned her cup in her hands. "And because if you're real — if any part of you is real — then I want the version of this story where someone from inside walked out. I'm tired of stories where they only ever walk in."

I wanted to tell her she was making a mistake. I wanted to tell her about the years between my dismissal and this river, the months I'd spent unable to distinguish my own motives, the possibility — real, unkillable — that the consortium had turned me long ago in ways I couldn't see, that I was a message walking uphill with no knowledge of its contents. That's what a truly honest man would have said.

What I said was: "Get some sleep. Tomorrow's water won't wait."

She nodded and went below, and I stayed on deck, and I will not pretend I don't know which answer she deserved.

Dawn came gray and early. From the bow, Devil's Throat announced itself as a sound before a sight — a long rolling thunder, the river compressing itself through a gorge it hated, white water stacked on white water like the whole watershed had decided to stand up and fight. Dev was checking lines. Mara was checking ammunition. Lena stood at the wheel with the throttle in her hand, looking at the throat of the river the way she looks at everything: measuring.

I took the forward line without being asked. Nobody objected, which on this boat is the closest thing to a welcome I'm going to get.

Then Dev, squinting downstream past the first falls, said quietly: "Smoke. Two klicks. Engine smoke."

And the river, which had been deciding for us all along, made up its mind.
