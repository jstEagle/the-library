## 6. Devil's Throat

I have run rapids on four rivers in three countries, and I can tell you the exact moment a rapid stops being water and becomes a decision. It's not when you hear it. Sound lies. It's not even when you see it, not really — white water photographs well and reads badly. It's when you feel the boat stop answering you. When the river takes the wheel out of your hands and hands you back your own arrogance, gift-wrapped.

Devil's Throat announced itself as a sound first: a low, continuous roar like something enormous breathing under the jungle. Then the smell — wet stone and churned spray — and then the sight of it, which I will be honest about now because I wasn't honest about it then: a quarter mile of river compressed into a chute no wider than our hull is long, boulders stacked like teeth, the whole mess dropping maybe thirty feet in a series of ledges that no sane pilot would attempt loaded, at night, with a gunboat behind them.

"Tell me we're going around," Odile said from the engine hatch. She had grease to her elbows and didn't bother wiping it before she said it, which meant she already knew the answer.

"There's no around," Mara said. She was at the chart table with Ansel's map flattened under her palms, her father's pencil lines gone soft and brown with age. "There's a portage trail on the left bank, but Ansel wrote here—" she tapped a note in a cramped hand, "—'trail washed out '09. Take the throat or take the long walk.'"

"The long walk being through consortium territory on foot, carrying everything," Dev said. He was at the bow with the spotlight off, reading the water by starlight and memory, because light would draw the gunboat faster than any radio call. He'd been quiet since Vessel. Quiet Dev was worse than loud Dev. Loud Dev argued with me; quiet Dev was doing arithmetic he didn't want to share.

"And how far behind us are they?" I asked.

Rook answered from the stern, where he'd stationed himself with a pair of night glasses he'd produced from somewhere, along with a competence that made me trust him less, not more. "Twelve minutes if they're cautious. Six if they're not." He lowered the glasses. "They won't be cautious. We burned their patrol boat's tender at the last bend. That was personal."

"That was *necessary*," Odile said.

"It's both," Rook said. "That's usually what necessary means."

I stood at the helm and did my own arithmetic, the kind I've done my whole life, the kind my father taught me over tide tables: distance against fuel, fuel against time, time against the thing chasing you. Every life is measured in miles. You just don't find out what the exchange rate is until the bill comes.

The exchange rate tonight was Devil's Throat.

"We run it," I said.

Nobody cheered. That's how you know a crew trusts you — they don't waste breath arguing when the math is already done. Odile fed the engines. Dev took his post at the bow rail with a boathook he wouldn't need and a line coiled at his feet that he might. Mara strapped herself into the chart seat and put both hands flat on her father's map, like touching it could keep him close enough to help.

Rook leaned in beside me at the helm. Close enough that I could smell river water and old cigarettes on him. "You've shot this?"

"I've shot things like this."

"That's not an answer."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

The first ledge took us before I finished the sentence.

* * *

There is a version of running rapids that people imagine, all spray and glory, teeth gritted against the wind. The reality is smaller and stranger: you spend most of it inside your own skull, narrating. *Left of the black rock. Don't fight the boil. Let the eddy turn us.* The boat becomes an extension of your hands and then stops being yours at all, and you're just a passenger in a machine made of your own decisions, some of them seconds old, all of them irreversible.

We took the first two ledges clean. The third one had a hole in it the size of a chapel, and the river put us in it.

I felt the stern lift and the bow drop and knew, in that weightless half-second, that we were going in nose-first. "Dev — high side!" I screamed it, and he moved, God help him, he moved exactly right, throwing his weight to the high gunwale while the bow punched down and the world went green and roaring and then white, spray everywhere, the engines screaming as they clawed for water that kept turning to air.

We came out of the hole sideways. The boat rolled hard enough to throw the chart table open and Ansel's map into the bilge water, and I heard Mara make a sound I'll hear for the rest of my life — not a scream, something worse, the sound of someone watching their inheritance drown in six inches of river.

Then the fourth ledge hit us like a closed fist, and after that I stopped keeping records.

What I know comes in pieces. Odile's voice through the engine hatch, flat and profane, keeping the motors alive. Rook's hand on my shoulder, steadying me at the wheel without asking permission. The black rock sliding past close enough to read its grain. And Dev — Dev at the bow, still at his post, still doing his job, right up until the fifth ledge threw a boulder's worth of standing wave across the deck and took him off the rail like the river had simply decided it wanted him.

He caught the safety line. One-handed. I saw it happen — saw him catch it, saw the line go taut, saw the cleat holding it begin, very slowly, to lift out of the deck the way a tooth lifts out of a gum.

"CLEAT!" Odile roared, and Rook was moving before she finished the word, crossing the deck on his belly in the wash, hand over hand down the jackline. The cleat tore free. Dev swung out over the water on nothing but Rook's grip and the physics of a man too stubborn to let go of the line, and the sixth and final ledge took us all down its throat together, blind, everyone screaming or praying or both, and spat us out into the long calm pool below the falls where the river goes suddenly, obscenely quiet, like a liar after telling the truth.

We got Dev aboard. That part I remember clearly, because it was slow and terrible and everyone did it right. His leg was broken — femur, Odile said, by the angle of it, and Odile had set enough bones in dockside brawls to know. Worse than the leg was the way he lay there afterward, gray-faced and shivering, trying to joke. "Told you," he wheezed at me. "Should've taken the long walk."

"Shut up, Dev."

"Yes, boss."

* * *

The gunboat came through the Throat eleven minutes later.

We heard it before we saw it — the same roar that had nearly killed us, but mechanical now, arrogant, an engine that didn't respect the river because it had never needed to. They ran the ledges with searchlights blazing and armor plating and no fear, because fear is a luxury of people who can be hurt.

They also ran it wrong. I don't know whether it was haste or contempt, but they came into the third ledge a half-point wide of the line Dev had read by starlight, and the chapel-sized hole took their bow and held it, and we watched from the treeline — we'd slipped into a side channel in the dark, engines dead, drifting — as the gunboat broached, rolled, and began to break itself against the rocks with a noise like a cathedral falling down stairs.

One man made it out. We watched him cling to a rock mid-river, searchlight still burning underwater below him like a drowned star, and the current worked at him, patient, professional, and after a while the rock was empty.

Nobody on our boat said anything. There was nothing to say that the river hadn't already said better.

Then Odile, who had been below, came up the companionway with a face like a struck match and said, "Lena. The fuel."

I went down. The main tank had held. The reserve cache — the drums we'd lashed in the aft hold, the fuel that was supposed to carry us the last hundred miles to the headwaters and, more importantly, back out — had shifted in the rollover. One drum had ruptured against a fitting. Vapor had found the bilge pump's arcing motor. The fire was small when I opened the hatch and it was entirely out twenty minutes later, and what it left behind was four drums of slagged, scorched, worthless metal and a hold that smelled like a refinery accident.

Odile did the math out loud because someone had to and because saying numbers makes them real, which is either cruelty or courage depending on the day. "Ninety miles to the source. We've got maybe forty miles of burn in the main tank, less if we push the engines. No reserves. No way to get reserves — every cache between here and the headwaters is consortium-controlled."

"So we're forty miles short," Mara said quietly.

"We're fifty-eight miles short," Odile said. "I'm not rounding down on fuel. Round down on fuel and you die optimistic."

* * *

We anchored in the deadwater below a clay bank and held what was left of a crew meeting around Dev, who lay propped in the dinette with his leg splinted and strapped and his color bad, taking sips of water like each one cost him money.

Here is the arithmetic, laid out plain, the way I lay out all my arithmetic eventually:

Dev couldn't travel well and couldn't travel fast. Turning back meant sixty miles downriver through territory where the consortium now knew exactly who we were and exactly what we carried — and the gunboat's loss would make them angry, and anger makes men thorough. Pushing on meant forty miles of fuel for ninety miles of river, which meant finding fuel or finding a miracle, and the only difference between those two words is whether it works.

And underneath all of it, the real question, the one nobody said out loud: one injured man against five able bodies, and every mile forward spent on his behalf was a mile taken from the proof in the tin, from Mara's inheritance, from whatever it was I'd decided this was about back when it was still abstract.

My father used to say the river always collects. I used to think he meant drowning. He didn't. He meant this — the moment when you count what a person costs and understand that the counting itself is the drowning.

"Turn back," Dev said.

Everyone looked at him.

"You heard the numbers." He was smiling, which was the worst part. Dev smiles when he's scared; I've known him nine years and he has never once smiled when he was safe. "Forty for ninety. Even Lena's math can't fix that, and her math is a religion. Drop me at the mission station upbank — Brother Anselm owes me cards debts, he'll patch me up. You four go on light."

"No," Mara said immediately.

"Kid—"

"I'm not a kid, and my father walked this river alone for twenty years so that somebody would finish this. You don't get to opt out of being the reason it gets finished." Her voice cracked on *finished*, and she hated it, and she kept talking anyway, which told me more about her than anything she'd said since Vessel.

Rook said nothing. He sat in the corner with his arms crossed, watching me, and I realized he was watching me the way a man watches a scale settle — waiting to see which way I'd tip, filing it away, and I understood that whatever I decided next would tell Elias Rook more about me than everything I'd done since the wreck.

Fine. Let it.

"Here's what happens," I said. "Nobody gets dropped anywhere. We go on. Forty miles of fuel means we run the river by day now — no more hiding, hiding burns patience and patience burns fuel. We make for the old survey camp Ansel marked at mile sixty-two. Mara, does the map show anything there?"

Mare smoothed the map — dried now, warped, one corner permanently curled like the river had signed it. Her finger found a penciled notation. "Fuel dump. Ansel wrote: 'left it with the sisters at the chapel. Tell them Kade's daughter came.'" A pause. "If the sisters are still alive. If the fuel is still there. If nobody sold it."

"Three ifs," Odile said.

"My whole life is three ifs," Mara said. "It's still mine."

I looked at Dev, laid out and gray and furious at his own body. "You ride. You don't argue, you don't stand watch, you don't touch a line. You're ballast with opinions until that leg knits enough to hate me properly. Agreed?"

His jaw worked. "Agreed," he said finally, and then, quieter, because Dev is incapable of leaving a kindness unpoisoned: "Your funeral, boss."

"No," I said, taking us off the anchor and turning the bow upstream into the dark. "Somebody else's. That's the deal I'm trying to make."

We ran through the night with the engines at a whisper, forty miles of fuel ticking down like a clock with a grudge, and near dawn, when the mist came off the water in sheets, Mara touched my arm and pointed at the eastern bank.

Smoke was rising there. Thin, domestic, deliberate — the smoke of people who cook breakfast every morning and expect to keep doing it.

A chapel, or a trap. On this river, I was learning, the two were often the same building.
