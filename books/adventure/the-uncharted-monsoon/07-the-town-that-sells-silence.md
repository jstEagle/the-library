## 7. The Town That Sells Silence

I have been paid to lie by governments, by corporations, and once — briefly, embarrassingly — by a man who sold counterfeit fishing lures to tourists and wanted a character reference for his parole hearing. So believe me when I tell you I know corruption in all its costumes. And the town of Graywater was not corruption wearing a costume. It was corruption in its bathrobe, drinking its morning coffee, entirely comfortable with who it was.

Graywater sat on a bend of the Serein where the river widened and slowed and seemed, like everything else in this valley, to be waiting for instructions. It was a waystation town — fuel, food, repairs, and the particular silence you could buy by the hour. The buildings were corrugated tin and concrete block. The church had been converted into a warehouse. The church's bell had been converted into a cash register, or so the joke went, though I never heard anyone actually make the joke. That was the thing about Graywater. Nobody joked. Jokes require an audience you trust.

We came in at dusk with a wounded man, a burned fuel cache, and a boat held together by Dev's welding and Lena's stubbornness. Dev had a fever by then, the wound in his side angry and red, and Lena carried him up the dock herself, which told me something about her I filed away next to everything else I'd filed and still couldn't make add up.

"We need a doctor," she said.

"Graywater has a medic," I said. "He's also the consortium's bookkeeper. He'll patch your man and bill the company for the bandages."

"Then we'll pay him ourselves."

"That's not how it works here."

She looked at me with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't enjoy. "Then how does it work?"

I'd been asking myself that for eleven years, since the inquiry that ended my career, and I still didn't have a clean answer. "Come on," I said. "I'll show you."

* * *

The way it worked was this: everyone.

I mean that with precision. Not most people. Not the weak ones or the greedy ones. Everyone. The dockmaster logged arrivals for the consortium and was paid in fuel credits. The medic kept two ledgers, one for wounds and one for names. The woman who ran the cookhouse — a broad, cheerful, terrifying woman named Oyelaran who made the best river fish I'd eaten in a decade — reported conversation the way other people reported weather. The fuel depot, the radio shack, the boy who sold cold drinks on the corner: each of them drew a stipend from Meridian Extraction, each of them knew their neighbors drew one too, and the knowing was the mechanism. You couldn't inform on the system without informing on yourself. It wasn't fear that ran Graywater. Fear is cheap and burns fast. It was something more durable. It was a payroll.

"They're not hostages," I told Lena that first night, on the porch of the medic's clinic while Dev slept off a fever inside. "That's what I spent years getting wrong. I kept looking for the villain, the one man at the top of the chain, and the truth is the chain has no top. Or the top is so far up it might as well be a weather system. Down here there's just links. People who take the money because the money is the only economy for two hundred miles, and because everyone they love is on the same list."

Lena watched the street. A man was painting the consortium's logo — a stylized river delta, blue and gold — onto the side of the fuel depot, freshening it, the way you'd paint your own fence.

"And if you take them off the list?" she said.

"Then you've taken their livelihood, their neighbors' goodwill, and their children's school supplies, and given them a scandal in return. People don't thank you for that. People shoot you for that." I lit a cigarette, an old habit I'd resumed the day my career ended and never found a reason to quit. "You wanted the truth about the river. Here it is. The truth is that silence has a unit price, and it's very reasonable, and everyone can afford it."

She was quiet a while. Then: "The liaison. On the navy side. Voss."

"What about him?"

"He lied to me about the wreck's registry. Small lie. Practiced." She turned to look at me, and in the porch light her face was all angles and exhaustion. "Was he on the list too?"

I thought about it honestly, which is more than I did for most of my career. "Everyone's on someone's list, Zhou. The question is whose, and what it pays."

* * *

The face she trusted turned out to belong to a woman named Petra Ives.

I learned this the way I learned most things in Graywater: by watching who watched us. Ives ran the chandlery two doors from the dock, sold rope and chain and diesel filters, and when our boat came in she'd waved at Lena like a sister. First name terms. A history. I clocked it on day one and said nothing, because I was still deciding what I was to these people — rescuer, parasite, or the longest arm of the thing we were running from — and a man in my position learns to hold his observations like unspent cash.

On the second night, I spent some.

Ives closed her shop late. She walked to the radio shack, stayed eleven minutes, and walked home by a route that passed the medic's clinic, where she slowed without stopping. The next morning, a consortium patrol skiff that had been idle at the dock for two days took on fuel.

I told Lena at the rail of the boat, quietly, while Dev slept and Mara kept watch with a shotgun she held like she'd been born to it, which I suspected she had.

"Ives," I said. "She's reporting."

Lena didn't argue. That was the thing about her — she didn't waste motion on disbelief. She'd been lied to by her own navy; she'd stopped being surprised by betrayal somewhere back downriver. "How long?"

"Since we docked. Maybe before. She may have called ahead when she saw you coming."

Mara spat over the rail, an old prospector's daughter's habit. "Ansel said half the river was bought. He said the other half was for sale."

"He was an optimist," I said.

Lena stared down the street at Ives's shop, at the rope and chain in the window, at whatever memory was moving behind her eyes. "She taught Dev to weld," she said finally. "Three years ago, when our old deckhand quit. She didn't charge us. She fed us for a week when the boat was laid up." Her jaw worked. "Was she feeding us then, or was she already on the list?"

"Probably both," I said. "That's the trick of it. Nobody here is one thing. The payroll doesn't ask you to stop being a person. It just asks you to be a person with a second job."

I expected her to get angry. Instead she did something worse. She nodded, slow, like a woman confirming a diagnosis.

"Then we don't confront her," she said. "We feed her something."

I looked at her — this salvage diver who measured her life in miles, who trusted machinery more than people, who had taken me onto her boat knowing I was either a lifeline or a noose — and I thought, with a feeling I didn't have a name for: they never taught me this at the academy. They taught me to catch corrupt men. Nobody taught me what to do when the corruption was a town, and the town was also, in its crooked, compromised, payroll-collecting heart, full of people who'd once fed you fish stew for free.

* * *

The fracture came on the third night, and it came through me.

The message came over the radio shack's secondary frequency — the one I still had the cipher for, because disgraced doesn't mean disconnected, a distinction I'd spent years being bitter about and had recently begun to find useful. It was from a man named Corben, Meridian's regional security chief, whom I'd known in another life when we were both on the government's side of the ledger. The message was short. It always is, when the content is murder.

*Assets confirmed at Graywater. Recover the records. Eliminate personnel. Rook to handle it — deniable, motivated, has history with the woman.*

*Has history with the woman.* As if what I had was a grudge, as if eleven years ago I hadn't testified and lost everything because I couldn't stomach what the navy did to whistleblowers, and now they'd decided my ruin had made me pliable. There's a particular insult in being recruited by the people who destroyed you. They weren't even offering redemption. They were offering employment.

I sat in my bunk on the boat with the message sheet in my hand and heard, through the bulkhead, Dev coughing in his fever, and Mara humming something low to him, and Lena on deck checking the mooring lines she'd already checked twice.

Here is what I want on the record, since I am apparently the only one keeping one: I did not deliberate long. Whatever else eleven years cost me, they'd clarified something. I had spent my career believing the system had a top — a Corben, a director, a name you could arrest — and that if you reached it, the machine would stop. Graywater had cured me of that. There was no top. There was just the payroll, all the way up, and the only thing that had ever frightened the people who signed it was the payroll becoming public.

So I did the smallest thing available to me. I walked to the radio shack, woke the operator — who took my money without meeting my eyes, because even the smallest coin spends here — and sent a reply on Corben's frequency:

*Records not yet located. Personnel under surveillance. Will report with recovery plan in 48 hours.*

A lie. My first lie on their behalf, and my last, and I made it count by making it time. Forty-eight hours. Long enough for Dev to stand. Long enough for the boat to refuel. Long enough, if Lena's plan worked, for Ives to carry a false trail upriver while we went down.

Then I walked back to the boat and did the second smallest thing. I put the original message on the chart table in front of Lena Zhou and said, "You should know what I was asked to do to you."

She read it. She read it again. Mara read it over her shoulder and her hand went to the shotgun, and I didn't blame her.

"You're telling me this why?" Lena said.

"Because you trusted a woman who reported you, and it nearly got you killed, and you deserve one person in this valley who isn't on the list." I tapped the message. "And because I need you to know the clock. Forty-eight hours before they realize I'm stalling. After that, Corben stops sending messages and starts sending men."

Lena looked at me for a long time. I have been looked at by interrogators, by lawyers, by my own reflection, and none of it prepared me for the way this woman audited a face.

"The medic," she said at last. "He's on the payroll. The dockmaster, the cookhouse, the radio operator — all of it."

"All of it."

"Then we don't buy silence." She rolled the message sheet closed and held it out to me — the evidence of my own recruitment, my own near-betrayal, mine to keep. "We buy one hour at a time, from one person at a time, and we pay in something they're not getting from Meridian."

"Which is?"

She almost smiled. It was a tired, river-worn thing, but it was real. "The truth," she said. "Turns out it's the only currency here nobody's minted yet."

* * *

We left Graywater before dawn on the second day, which by my count gave us thirty-one hours of borrowed time.

Ives stood on her dock as we pulled away. She didn't wave. She watched us go with an expression I couldn't read — grief, or relief, or the particular blankness of a woman who has just realized which side of her ledger she'd rather be on — and then she turned back to her shop and began, methodically, to take down the consortium's calendar from her wall.

Mara saw it too. "She'll report we went upriver," Mara said. "Toward the source. Like you planned."

"Or she'll report the truth," I said, "and spend the rest of her life wondering if she did the right thing. Either way, we're gone."

Behind us, Graywater settled back into its comfortable morning, its bought quiet, its reasonable prices. Ahead, the river narrowed and the hills closed in and the air began to smell of sulfur, faint and wrong, like something upstream was bleeding.

Lena stood at the bow with Ansel's map open in her hands, and she didn't look back once.

I checked my watch. Thirty hours. Somewhere behind us, Corben was reading my lie, and somewhere ahead of us, at the river's forbidden source, the thing that had bought an entire valley was waiting to find out what its silence was worth when somebody finally refused to sell.

The current took us. We didn't fight it. For the first time since I'd boarded this boat, that felt less like surrender and more like aim.
