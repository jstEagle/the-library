## 8. The Source

The river told us we were close before the map did.

For three days the water had been running wrong — too clear where it should have carried silt, then suddenly stained a pale, milky green that no healthy tributary produces. Dev would have called it ghost milk. Dev wasn't with us. That was still an arithmetic I couldn't finish: two fit divers, one half-healed engineer sulking in the boat at the last cache, and me. Mara poled the shallows like she'd been born to it, which in a sense she had — her father's map folded open on the thwart between us, soft as cloth now from handling.

"Ansel marked this bend," she said, tapping a smudge of graphite. "He wrote *the river forgets itself here.* I used to think that was poetry."

"It's not?"

She didn't answer. Around the next bend, the Serein simply stopped being a river.

The valley opened like a held breath finally let out, and what filled it was a lake the color of old copper and new poison, wide as a harbor, ringed by terraced walls of exposed rock that stepped down into the water in perfect unnatural geometry. No shoreline — just benches, cut clean, descending into flooded dark. A pit mine, drowned. Somewhere under that green surface were the galleries Ansel had surveyed, the tunnels, the machinery, the whole swallowed anatomy of an operation that did not exist on any chart I'd ever seen. And from its far edge, a discharge channel fed the Serein its first miles, dosing every drop of the river's childhood with whatever sat dissolved in that lake.

I've spent my life underwater. I know what drowned things look like — wrecks, planes, towns, the occasional car full of regret. I thought I was past being able to be horrified by submerged geography.

I was wrong. This wasn't something that had drowned. This was something that had been *drowned* — deliberately, administratively, with paperwork. The water hadn't risen over the mine. The mine had been handed to the water like a body to an undertaker who'd been paid to lose the file.

"Mara," I said, and my voice came out smaller than I wanted. "How deep is it?"

Her jaw was tight. "Dad's soundings go past forty meters near the north wall. He stopped marking depth after that. Wrote *bottom unknown* instead."

Bottom unknown. On a hand-drawn map by a man who'd sounded rivers for thirty years. Ansel Kade had looked into that water and decided some questions weren't worth the ink.

We beached the boat in a stand of drowned snags on the southern rim, where dead trees stood knee-deep in the flood like a congregation that had been told to wait. Above us, maybe four kilometers across the lake, the processing works clung to the eastern bench — corrugated sheds, a crane gantry, tailings pipes crawling down the rock face like varicose veins. Lights on in daylight. Generators humming through the ground. A place officially nonexistent, running three shifts.

"They're pumping," Mara said. "Right now. Look at the outflow."

I looked. The discharge channel ran fast and steady, and along its lip the stain was freshest, brightest, that sickly green-white. Everything downstream — Vessel, the fishing camps, the delta, the coast where I made my living off a navy that pretended this valley wasn't there — everything drank from this pipe.

The black box in my dry bag suddenly felt like a minor document. This was the headline. The wreck, the registry lies, the navy's silence — all of it was downstream plumbing for this.

"We photograph everything," I said. "Then we're gone before first light."

Mara was already unpacking the camera case with hands that shook only slightly, which given what she was looking at — her family's stolen inheritance, drowning in plain sight — seemed almost heroic. "Ansel surveyed the north wall galleries in 'nineteen," she said. "Before they sealed them. If the records show what he logged, the tonnage alone—"

"Tonnage later. Proof of existence first." I checked the camera batteries twice because checking things is how I pray. "We shoot the works, the outflow, the coordinates against landmarks. We shoot your father's map held up against the pit so anyone with eyes can see they match. Then we leave."

"And if someone sees us leave?"

"Then we run the river faster than they do."

She almost smiled. "You always this cheerful?"

"I count miles, Mara. Cheerful doesn't factor in."

* * *

We worked the southern rim through the afternoon, moving snag to snag, shooting the lake in overlapping frames the way you'd survey a wreck — systematically, without sentiment, because sentiment gets divers killed. The light cooperated, flat and gray, which flattened the horror and somehow made it worse: the photographs would look almost innocent, a pretty alpine tarn, until you noticed the bench cuts, the pipe, the lights.

By late afternoon we'd crossed half the rim on foot, staying below the ridgeline, and reached a vantage directly opposite the works. From there I could see the detail that turned dread into certainty: a barge moored at the north wall, small cranes swinging, lowering sealed drums into the flooded galleries. They weren't mining anymore. They were *burying*. Whatever those drums held, the plan was for the lake to keep it forever, in a hole that appeared on no map, guarded by men whose salaries came from a company with no name.

Mara photographed the barge until the memory card was full, and I swapped in the spare, and neither of us said what we were both thinking, which was that Ansel Kade had died carrying proof of this in a biscuit tin, and his daughter was finishing his sounding.

That's when the dogs started.

Not close. Far — a thin, carrying chorus from the eastern ridge, the way sound travels across water. Mara froze mid-frame. I counted seconds between the baying, the way I count everything, and did the arithmetic I hated: the sound was moving south along the ridge. Toward our boat. Or toward us. With dogs, the distinction collapses fast.

"Patrol route?" I whispered.

"There shouldn't *be* patrols." Her voice had gone flat, controlled, her father's stubbornness arriving in her spine. "This place isn't supposed to exist."

"Neither does the wreck I dove in March. Existence is negotiable here. Movement isn't." I scanned the ridge. Through the trees, maybe eight hundred meters off, a light swung — a flashlight beam walking the slope with the unhurried confidence of someone being paid by the hour. Then a second beam, farther north. Converging, or sweeping toward convergence. Behind us, across the lake, a door banged on the processing works and voices carried over the water, distorted, languageless, but unmistakably *organized*.

They'd found the boat. It took me another ten seconds to accept that, because I'd hidden it well, and another five to understand it didn't matter — a boat on a nonexistent lake is a signature, not a hiding place. We had perhaps twenty minutes of darkness left, and the beams were already stitching the southern rim.

"The gallery entrance," Mara said suddenly.

"What?"

"North wall. Dad marked it — a maintenance adit above the waterline, behind the barge mooring. He said the company never posted guards on it because nobody was supposed to know it existed." She was repacking the camera with fast, certain movements. "It faces away from the works. If we can get around the rim to the north side in the dark—"

"You want to cross toward the searchlights."

"I want to cross toward the one door they don't watch. Yes."

Every instinct I own, instincts calibrated by years of diving classified wrecks for clients who lie, said *no*. Go down, go quiet, go back the way we came and take our chances with the dogs. But the dogs owned the way back, and the beams were closing, and the arithmetic had changed on me. Sometimes the safe exit is the one shaped like a risk.

"Okay," I said. "But we move on the generator noise. When it cycles loud, we run. When it drops, we freeze. Understood?"

She nodded once, and we went.

* * *

We crossed the rim in the last gray hour, running tree to tree when the generators surged, pressed flat against cold rock when they fell silent, while the flashlight beams walked their patient grid behind us and the dogs sang their narrowing song. Twice the beams passed within fifty meters of us, close enough that I could hear men talking, bored and professional, discussing overtime. The economy of silence, paying its wages.

We reached the north wall as full dark settled, and there it was, exactly as Ansel had drawn it: a square-cut adit in the rock face, maybe three meters above the current waterline, timber lintel rotted soft, half-veiled by hanging roots. A rusted chain and padlock hung from iron staples — locked, decades ago, by people who trusted the world to forget.

Mara put her father's map against the stone beside it and I shot the frame: map, adit, barge, lake, works, all in one composition. The photograph that would end careers, possibly including ours.

Inside the adit, the air was cold and mineral and utterly still, the breath of a place holding its secrets. Our headlamps found rails vanishing into black water, gauge boards with faded numbers, and stenciled on the rock beside the entrance, in paint older than either of us:

**SEREIN HYDROGRAPHIC SURVEY — STATION 0.**

Station zero. The source. The navy charted this. Someone had stood exactly here with official paint and official authority, and then the charts had been burned and the station renamed nothing.

Mara touched the letters. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to. I photographed her hand on the stencil, because grief is also evidence.

And that's when the alarm began — a flat electronic pulse rolling across the lake from the works, echoing off the pit walls, doubling and redoubling until the whole drowned valley rang with it. Floodlights snapped on along the eastern benches, one after another, marching toward the north wall. Through the adit mouth I watched boats launch from the barge, three of them, running lights cutting the copper water.

"They found the cameras' flash residue on the south rim," I said. "Or the dogs got lucky. Doesn't matter." I killed my lamp. In the sudden black, Mara's breathing was fast and shallow beside me, and somewhere below our feet, forty meters down, the bottom stayed unknown.

"Lena," she whispered. "Which way?"

Boats converging on the north wall. Dogs on the south rim. One flooded mine, one rotted door, and a river behind us that belonged to the men in the boats.

I put my hand on the rail that ran down into the dark water and felt how solidly it was bolted — built to last, by people who'd expected to come back.

"Down," I said. "There's a second way out of every mine. Ansel drew it. Find me the line."
