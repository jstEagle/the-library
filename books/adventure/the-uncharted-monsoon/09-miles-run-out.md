## 9. Miles Run Out

I have spent my life measuring things in miles, and I can tell you exactly how many of them stood between me and the edge of that pit when the floodlights came on: forty. Forty yards of churned red mud and tailings gravel, with Mara's hand crushing mine and Dev bleeding through his bandage somewhere behind the pump house.

The flooded mine opened below us like a wound that had been cauterized shut — a mile-wide pit of green-black water, terraced walls descending into nothing, the whole thing ringed by chain-link and floodlights and men with rifles who were not supposed to exist at a site that did not officially exist. The water was beautiful in the way that poison is beautiful. Ansel's map had called it the Source. It looked more like an ending.

"They're coming up the east ramp," Mara said. She had binoculars she'd stolen off a dead man three days ago, and she used them like a woman reading her own obituary. "Six. Maybe eight."

"Rook?"

"Gone since the alarm." She lowered the binoculars. "Lena. We should run."

Running was the plan. Running had always been the plan — get the photographs, get out, let the records do their slow work from a safe distance. But the alarm had killed the safe distance, and the gunboat we'd left burning at Devil's Throat had apparently radioed ahead, and now there were trucks on the access road and a helicopter thumping somewhere south of us like a heart that wouldn't quit.

And Dev couldn't run. Dev, who'd taken a shard of hull through his side at Devil's Throat and refused to stay behind at Vessel because, as he put it, "somebody has to count your miles for you, boss, you always lose track past two hundred." He was propped against the pump house wall with his color wrong and his hands steady, which was worse than if they'd been shaking.

So it came down to arithmetic again. My whole life, arithmetic. What a dive pays against what air costs. What a crew eats against what a boat burns. And now: what eight lives weigh against what one transmission is worth.

Because here's what I hadn't told anyone, not even Mara, not even Rook — especially not Rook. The night after I opened Ansel's tin, while strangers were searching my boat down in the harbor, I'd sat in the engine room with a soldering iron and a salvaged satellite modem and built myself a dead man's switch. A copy of everything — the survey records, the shipping manifests linking the wreck to the consortium, the navy correspondence Rook had pretended not to notice me photographing — keyed to broadcast unless I checked in every twelve hours.

I'd told myself it was insurance. That was a lie I could live with. Insurance implies you expect to survive.

"Lena." Mara grabbed my arm. "They're fanning out. We have minutes."

I looked at the pit. All those miles of river behind us, poisoned from this one hole in the world, and every town downstream drinking it without knowing. Vessel. The waystations. The kids Dev had taught to skip stones off the lower Serein while we waited out a patrol.

"Get Dev to the drainage culvert," I said. "North rim. There's a maintenance road on the other side — Ansel marked it."

"And you?"

"I'm going to make a phone call."

* * *

Elias Rook had been many things in his life, most of them recently disgraced, but he had never been a man who enjoyed standing still while other people made history. So when the alarms went and Lena Zhou went over the north fence line like a woman who'd done harder things before breakfast, he went with her — at a distance, the way he did everything, including lying.

He found her at the pit's rim, crouched behind a transformer housing, holding a battered satellite unit like it was a grenade with the pin half out.

"You didn't tell me about the dead man's switch," he said.

She didn't flinch, which he respected. "You didn't tell me about the order they gave you in Vessel. We're even."

"We are nowhere near even." He dropped beside her. Below, flashlight beams were converging on the pump house where Dev and Mara had gone to ground. "Listen to me. Okafor's fixers don't take prisoners when there's evidence involved. If you transmit, they will burn this ridge to find you, and they'll start with your people."

"That's why I'm transmitting first."

"The originals—"

"Are already uploaded. Encrypted, mirrored, three copies. The tin was never the weapon, Rook. The tin was just the invitation." She almost smiled. "Ansel knew. Dying men don't draw maps for sentimental reasons."

Rook felt something shift in his chest that he recognized, reluctantly, as hope — an emotion he'd filed away years ago along with his pension and his reputation. "Once it goes live, you can't unsend it. Consortium lawyers, navy lawyers, they'll bury it in injunctions by morning."

"By morning it'll be on six continents. Injunctions travel slower than light." She held the unit up. "Last chance to talk me out of it."

"I've been talking people out of things my whole career," he said. "It never once worked. Send it."

She sent it.

* * *

The progress bar took eleven seconds. I watched all of them. Somewhere in those eleven seconds I stopped being a salvage diver with a side business in deniability and became a woman the consortium would hunt until one of us died of old age or lead poisoning.

Then the bar filled, and the little screen said SENT, and I laughed — actually laughed, once, ugly — because it was done, and because I was terrified, and because both of those things can be true in the same breath if you breathe fast enough.

The radio on Rook's shoulder crackled. Okafor's voice, silk over rebar: *"Rook. Report."*

Rook looked at me. I looked at him. This was the moment Chapter Seven had been building toward, the coin he'd refused in the waystation, and I understood suddenly that everything hinged on whether a disgraced man could spend his last shred of credibility on strangers.

"Report," Okafor repeated.

Rook keyed the mic. "Zhou's transmitting from the north rim. I'm engaging." Then he stood up, walked ten feet into the open, and started shooting at the floodlights — which, I will grant him, was engaging something.

Chaos is a currency too, and Rook spent it like a drunk. Every beam on the ridge swung toward his muzzle flashes. Every fixer forgot about the pump house. I ran for the culvert with the satellite unit hugged to my chest like a child, sliding down the tailings slope on my backside, mud filling my boots, thinking: *this is what it costs, this is the exchange rate, everything you measured yourself in, spent in ninety seconds.*

I reached the culvert. Mara was there. Dev wasn't.

"He went back," Mara said, and her face told me the rest before her mouth caught up. "Okafor's men circled the pump house. He had the flare gun. He said—" Her voice broke clean in half. "He said somebody had to cover your last two hundred yards. You always lose track."

* * *

Dev Okonkwo had counted a great many miles in his life, and he had always known that the final ones would be short. You don't take a hull fragment through the liver and outrun professionals; he'd done the arithmetic in the pump house while Mara argued, and arithmetic had never lied to him yet.

The flare gun was a joke against rifles. He fired it anyway, twice, into the dry brush between himself and the advancing flashlights, and the brush — being consortium-adjacent, and therefore full of diesel drums nobody had bothered to secure — went up like a second sunrise.

It bought thirty seconds. He used twenty-nine of them walking, not running, because running hurt and he wanted his last miles to be his own. He thought about the river. He thought about stones skipping. He thought: *boss, you owe me,* and then he thought, generously: *no. You don't. I paid my own way.*

The third rifle shot found him at the top of the maintenance road, facing north, toward water.

* * *

We went down the maintenance road with the fire lighting our backs and the helicopter overhead screaming into a radio jam it didn't understand yet. By dawn we were in Ansel's old prospecting skiff, hidden in the reeds four miles downstream, watching the smoke column rise off the headwaters like a flag nobody would claim.

Mara didn't speak. I didn't either. Some debts are too large for language; you just carry them.

Rook found us at noon, soaked, singed, and carrying Dev's hat, which he set down between us with a formality that was its own kind of grief. "Transmission hit," he said quietly. "Three wire services, two environmental tribunals, and a naval archivist who owes me a favor. By tonight it's everywhere."

"By tonight we're nowhere," I said. "That was the deal."

He nodded. Then, because he was still Elias Rook, he added: "For what it's worth, Zhou — I'd have taken the money. Ten years ago, I'd have sold the originals and slept fine." He looked at the river. "I'm glad one of us grew."

I didn't answer. I was counting miles — the ones behind us, the ones Dev had covered, the long unmapped distance between the woman who'd planned to renege on a dying man and the woman floating away from the only proof she'd ever owned, having given it away free.

Somewhere upstream, the wound kept bleeding green into the water. But the river had witnesses now.

And so, it turned out, did I.
