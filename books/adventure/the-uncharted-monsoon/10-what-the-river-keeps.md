## 10. What the River Keeps

The scandal broke on a Tuesday, which felt right. Scandals should break on ordinary days, the way teeth do — no warning, just a crunch and then everything's different.

I was in Vessel, of all places, drinking coffee that tasted like boiled rope at Mara's table, when the first feed came through. The survey records. Our survey records, or what was left of them after the consortium's lawyers got done redacting half the frames. Even gutted, they were damning: the mine at the headwaters, the flooded pit bleeding tailings into the Serein, the navy hydrographic charts with the wreck's true registry inked over in someone's careful hand. A ship that never existed, sunk by a company that didn't exist, guarding a mine that officially wasn't there.

The anchor on the feed used the word *allegedly* eleven times in four minutes. I counted. Counting is what I do instead of praying.

"They'll deny it," Mara said. She wasn't watching the screen. She was watching me watch it, the way she'd been watching me for six weeks, patient as water finding its level. "The navy will say the charts are old. The consortium will say the pit is a natural formation. Someone will resign quietly and get a better job."

"Probably," I said.

"And you'll be blamed anyway. For the transmission. For Dev." She said his name gently, but she didn't flinch from it, and I loved her a little for that. Everyone else in Vessel had started saying *the diver* or *your crewmate* around me, as if grief were a contagion you caught by naming it.

"Dev made his own choice," I said. It was true and it was insufficient and it was all there was.

Mara turned off the feed. Outside her window, the Serein went about its business, brown-green and indifferent, carrying its cargo of silt and secrets down to the coast where I'd found them, or they'd found me, hard to say anymore which.

"You keep waiting for it to feel like losing," she said. "I can see it. You keep checking yourself for the wound."

"I gave away the only thing worth anything I've ever held."

"You gave away something that was never yours to sell." She poured more coffee, unasked. "That's different. That took me years to learn, and my tuition was worse than yours."

Maybe. Her father had died alone upriver with a tin under his arm and a daughter who thought he'd abandoned her. Mine — well. My inheritance had been a philosophy: measure your life in miles, because miles don't lie to you the way people do. Miles behind you can't be taken back. Miles ahead can't promise you anything. It was a good system. It kept me alive through wrecks and rapids and one gunboat whose name I still won't say out loud.

It had not prepared me for a Tuesday morning in Vessel, being told by a woman I'd met running from gunfire that I'd come out ahead.

*

The navy's statement came three days later, and it was a masterpiece of the genre. *The vessel in question does not appear in current naval registries. Any claims regarding classified operations are unfounded. The Department remains committed to transparency.* Committed to transparency the way a drowned man is committed to breathing.

Elias Rook sent me a copy with a single line attached: *Eleven allegedlys. You're getting sloppy.*

He was in Vessel too, or near enough — holed up at the waystation, technically a fugitive now himself, since refusing an order to kill us had consequences even in an economy where everyone was on somebody's payroll. He came by the dock most evenings and sat on a bollard like a man visiting a grave he wasn't sure he had rights to.

"They're going to offer you money," he said one evening. "Officially unofficial. Hush compensation. Enough to refit the boat twice over."

"Will they?"

"Of course. It's cheaper than a tribunal. And you'll take it or you won't, and either way I'll have an opinion I keep to myself." He turned a coin over his knuckles — an old habit, some naval academy tic he'd never managed to drown. "What I actually came to say is that the fixers pulled out of the valley last week. All of them. Consortium's cutting losses upstream."

"Because of the scandal?"

"Because of the witnesses." He looked at the river. "You can buy silence, Zhou. You can't buy testimony that's already broadcast. The river has people on it now — journalists, inspectors, two very earnest documentary filmmakers who are going to die of dysentery and make it everyone's problem. But they're there. That's the thing you did. Not the transmission. The *there*."

I thought about that. About how a wound heals — not by being hidden but by being seen often enough that hiding becomes the harder work.

"Ansel knew," I said. "That's why the map. Why the tin. He couldn't get anyone to go look. So he made sure someone would carry the looking upstream whether they wanted to or not."

"He traded you a map for a promise," Rook said. "And you planned to renege."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I've read your file, and because you told me, once, drunk, on the Devil's Throat portage, and because it's the kind of thing you'd do." The coin stopped moving. "The point is you didn't. Whatever else the river keeps, it keeps that."

*

We went upriver in the last week of the season, Mara and I, in a borrowed skiff with an engine that complained like an old man asked to dance. No cargo, no crew, no plan beyond the one I'd been carrying since the pit's rim, since Dev.

The headwaters were quieter now. The guards were gone; the pumps had been shut down by order of a ministry that existed mostly on paper but apparently could occasionally bite. The flooded pit sat in its scarred bowl of earth, green water still, holding the sky the way a held breath holds words. It looked almost peaceful. That was the horror of it, still — that ruin this size can wear calm like a mask, that you can stand at the edge and think *lake* before you think *wound*.

Mara stood beside me and didn't say anything for a long time. Then she took out the tin — empty now, its contents living in a thousand servers and a parliamentary inquiry — and set it on the ground between us.

"I brought it to bury," she said. "But I think it should stay open. Let the rain get in. Let it rust. Let it be a thing the mountain has to look at."

"That's not how graves work."

"No," she agreed. "But it's how evidence works. Evidence shouldn't rest."

So we left the tin open at the pit's edge, lid back, filling slowly with green water and leaf-fall, becoming part of the landscape it had spent thirty years describing.

Then I did the thing I'd actually come to do.

Ansel's map. Hand-drawn, grease-stained, annotated in a dying man's cramped hand — *shoal here at low water, liars at the waystation, tell Mara the river forgives nothing but remembers everything*. I'd carried it through everything. Through the searches and the rapids and the town that sold silence. I'd told myself I kept it because it was useful. Then, when it stopped being useful, because it was proof. Then, when it stopped being proof, because it was his.

None of those reasons survived contact with the actual ground.

I dug at the edge of the source, where the first clean water comes down off the rock and meets the green, in the seam between what the mountain gives and what we took. I wrapped the map in oilcloth the way Ansel must have wrapped the tin, and I put it in the earth, and I covered it, and I pressed the soil down with my palms until my hands smelled like the whole watershed.

"There," I said, to him, to her father, to whatever part of a man persists in the habits of strangers. "Delivered."

Mara crouched beside me. "You know what he'd say?"

"Something insufferable."

"*You owe the river a mile for every one it gave you.*" She smiled, and it was his smile, or close enough that my chest hurt. "He said that to everyone. It's why nobody liked him."

"It's why I liked him."

"I know," she said. "Me too. Eventually."

*

On the way down, we stopped at the shoal where Dev went into the water. There's a marker now — Mara's doing, weeks ago, a length of pipe driven into the bank with his name punched into it, letters already softening with rust. The river forgives nothing but remembers everything. Rust is remembering.

I stood on the bank and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding.

Here's what I owed: a life, to a man who spent his covering ours. A trade, to a dead prospector, paid in full at the source. A debt I couldn't itemize to Elias Rook, who'd held the line at the rim and then vanished into the administrative purgatory of men who chose wrong early and right late. And something larger and less countable to a river that had carried me, tried to kill me, and shown me its wound — all three, sometimes in the same hour.

Here's what I had: a boat with a patched hull and a crew of three fit divers, a reputation that would get us work and lose us other work, and a name that appeared, briefly, in a public inquiry as *source unverified*.

Source unverified. I'd been called worse, by better.

"You're smiling," Mara said, poling us off the shoal.

"I'm counting."

"Miles?"

"No." I watched the headwaters fall away behind us, the green stain thinning as the clean tributaries joined in, the river doing what rivers do — diluting, carrying, forgetting nothing but forgiving a little more each mile. "Years. I'm counting years. Turns out I've got some."

She laughed, and the sound went downstream ahead of us, and I let it.

Back in Vessel, there'd be offers waiting — hush money, salvage contracts, a navy liaison with a new face and the same old eyes wanting a quiet conversation. Back in Vessel, Rook would be on his bollard, and I'd finally have to decide what I owed him, if anything, and whether owing someone is the same as trusting them. Back in Vessel, the world would resume trying to price everything I'd refused to sell.

But that evening, standing at the rail while the skiff ran the easy water, I understood the thing Ansel had known and Dev had paid for and Mara had learned the hard way: survival isn't a possession. It's a loan. And every loan comes due.

The river gave me miles when I needed them measured. Now it was handing me years, and asking what I meant to do with them.

I didn't have an answer yet. But I had a boat, a crew, a promise-shaped hole in my chest that was slowly filling with something sturdier than guilt —

and, moored at the Vessel dock when we came around the last bend, a navy cutter flying no colors, with a woman on its deck who wore my face twenty years older, and who raised a hand in greeting like we were family.

The river, apparently, wasn't finished with me.
