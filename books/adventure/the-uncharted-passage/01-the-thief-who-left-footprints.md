## 1. The Thief Who Left Footprints

The thief left footprints.

That was the first thing Juniper Halloran noticed about them, three days out of Fort Calloway, and it was the thing that kept the chase alive when every other thread had frayed. Nobody left footprints. Not out here, not on the Saltreach, where a man could walk for a week and leave less trace than the wind itself. The salt crust held a print for an hour at most before the heat breathed it smooth again, and anyone with sense walked the drainage lines, the old cart ruts, the ribs of dead riverbed where the ground was baked hard as kiln brick and gave up nothing to the eye.

This one walked the open flats. Deliberately. In a straight line that bent only to avoid the worst of the brine pools, as if they'd read a survey map of the Saltreach and decided to treat it like a road.

Juniper crouched over the latest print — narrow boot, deep heel, a slight drag on the left side that said either an old injury or a habit of walking tired — and felt the familiar two-headed animal in their chest turn over. One head was pure professional admiration. The other wanted to put a bullet through that beautiful tradecraft just to watch it stop working.

*Left side drag,* they noted, and moved on.

The Saltreach didn't forgive. It was forty miles of white nothing between the last reliable water at Dyer's Well and the canyon country to the south, a place where the horizon went soft and liquid around midday and the air itself seemed to have opinions about you. Juniper had crossed it eleven times in nine years of ranger service. They knew its moods the way other people knew their mothers': the morning clarity that made distance lie short, the afternoon shimmer that made it lie long, the way sound carried flat and strange so that a rider two miles off sounded like one standing behind your shoulder.

They also knew what it did to people who crossed it in a hurry.

The thief was crossing it in a hurry. That was the second thing Juniper had figured out, and it was the crack in the whole operation. A careful person would have taken the northern route through the rock gardens, slow and shaded, doubling back twice to shake pursuit. This one ran the flats straight south, burning daylight and water both, which meant they weren't running from something so much as *toward* something. A deadline. A rendezvous. A buyer.

Or a border. The thought arrived uninvited, the way the bad thoughts always did, and Juniper pushed it down where it belonged.

*Not your problem what they're carrying. Your problem is that they stole it.*

Except it wasn't even that, not really. The theft itself had been almost gentle — a survey depot outside Calloway, hit clean sometime in the hour between the night watchman's rounds and dawn. No blood, no broken locks worth mentioning, no mess. Just gone: one sealed strongbox from the Vessian recovery expedition's field cache, along with the expedition's own manifest page describing it. The manifest theft was what bothered Command. The box they could replace. The paper trail, apparently, they couldn't.

*Recovered government property of exceptional historical sensitivity.* That was the phrase in the dispatch, underlined twice by somebody whose handwriting shook slightly, which told Juniper more than the words did. Rangers were used to chasing horse thieves and water poachers and the occasional smuggler running tallow and gun parts across the line. Nobody underlined *historical sensitivity* twice unless they were frightened.

So here was Juniper Halloran, alone, because Command had decided one ranger traveling light would run this thief down faster than a patrol, and because Juniper had volunteered before the sentence finished leaving the captain's mouth. They'd told themselves it was duty. They'd told themselves it was competence being recognized. They had not examined too closely the small hot coal underneath those reasons, the one that glowed brighter with every mile: that this was the first time in three years anything had asked everything of them, and they had discovered, somewhere out on the white flats with their throat full of salt dust, that they had been starving for exactly this.

Nine years of patrols. Nine years of counting cattle and settling boundary disputes and writing reports nobody read. And now a single set of footprints across the end of the world, and Juniper's whole body awake for the first time since —

Since. They didn't finish the thought. They never finished the thought.

* * *

By late afternoon the prints led into the beginnings of canyon country, where the Saltreach broke apart into red stone fingers and dry washes that only looked empty. Juniper stopped on the rim of the first real gorge and read the ground like a letter from home.

The thief had descended the eastern ramp — smart, it stayed in shadow until midafternoon — and then, at the bottom, had done something odd. They'd stopped. Stood a while. The prints clustered, turned, overlapped. Then they'd continued south along the wash at a slower pace, the left-side drag suddenly pronounced.

Tired. Or hurt. Or waiting for someone who hadn't come.

Juniper took out their field glass and swept the wash ahead. Heat shimmer turned the far walls to watercolor. Nothing moved. But half a mile on, where the canyon narrowed between two fallen slabs, there was shade, and there was a seep — Juniper remembered it from a survey six years back, a thin green stain on the rock face where groundwater wept out and died in the sun. Anyone who'd studied the maps would know it. Anyone desperate enough would go there.

Which meant Juniper had a choice. Go down now, push hard, and come up on the seep by dark, possibly catching the thief drinking, possibly catching a knife in the dark instead. Or hold the rim, let the cold come, and take them at first light when the prints would be fresh and the advantage all Juniper's.

The coal in their chest voted for going down now. Everything else voted for patience.

Juniper was still weighing it when the light changed.

Not sunset — sunset came slow and gold to the Saltreach, an hour of amber before the quick violet dark. This was different. A bruise-colored wall building up over the southern mesas, tall as weather gets, silent at this distance but moving fast, and Juniper's stomach dropped through the canyon floor because they knew exactly what it meant and exactly what it meant for anyone standing in a slot wash below.

Flash flood. The desert's oldest murder. Rain falling thirty miles away, in country that couldn't hold a drop of it, all of it funneled into these canyons at once, arriving as a brown wall moving faster than any horse.

And the thief was in the bottom of the wash, half a mile from the narrows, dragging their left leg.

Juniper didn't decide anything. Their body decided — hat cinched, rifle slung tight, boots finding the descent ramp at a controlled slide that wasn't controlled at all. The rational part of them, the part that had kept them alive nine years, filed a formal objection: *You are descending into a flash-flood channel to save the life of the person you are hunting. There is no regulation covering this. There is no report form for this.*

The rest of them was already at the bottom.

The wash floor was sand and rounded stone, and the air had changed — cooler, wetter, a smell like iron and green things that had no business existing out here. Somewhere upstream, faint as a whisper through a wall, came the sound Juniper had heard twice in their life and never forgotten: a low, gathering roar, like the desert clearing its throat.

"Hey!" The shout bounced off the canyon walls and came back doubled. "Flood! Get to high ground!"

No answer. The prints led on toward the seep, toward the narrows, toward the noise.

Juniper ran.

The roar grew teeth. Around the next bend the canyon pinched hard, walls leaning in until a rider couldn't pass, and the light in the gap had gone wrong — greenish, submarine — and the first water reached Juniper's boots before they saw it coming, a cold tongue licking around the bend, ankle-deep, then knee-deep in the space between two heartbeats.

Through the pinch, maybe sixty yards, a figure stood on a low shelf of rock beside the seep. Small. Hooded against the sun earlier, hood thrown back now, staring upstream at the sound with the particular stillness of a person doing arithmetic they don't like.

"Rope!" Juniper screamed, and didn't wait, hauling the coil off their shoulder, looking for an anchor, finding nothing — no tree, no piton, nothing but slick red stone — and the water hit their thighs and shoved, and the arithmetic got very simple very fast.

The figure on the shelf looked at Juniper. Even at sixty yards, even in the failing green light, Juniper saw the moment the thief made their own calculation: the ranger in the flood, the rope with no anchor, the choice between watching a lawman drown and doing something about it.

Then the wall came around the bend.

It wasn't water so much as the idea of water given weight — brown, churning, taller than a horse, carrying whole trees and a fence post and what might have been a door. It took the narrows like a fist closing. Juniper got one arm around a stone spur the size of a grave marker and held on while the desert tried to pry them loose, and through the roar, impossibly, they heard a voice — high, furious, cutting through the flood like a blade:

"The ledge! Move, you idiot, MOVE—"

A rope arced out of the chaos. Actually arced, a proper throw, landing across Juniper's shoulder in the dark. Juniper's hands found it before their mind agreed to. The line snapped taut, nearly tearing free, and then it was steady — someone on the high shelf paying out and holding, someone with strong arms and better sense, hauling Juniper hand over hand up through the flood while the spur they'd been clinging to vanished under the brown wall.

Juniper came over the lip of the shelf coughing, soaked, alive, and rolled onto their back on warm stone, staring up at a sky going violet at the edges.

Above them stood the thief.

Small, wiry, dark-haired, plastered with spray, breathing hard. Holding Juniper's own rope coil — Juniper realized distantly that the throw had been made with the line off Juniper's own shoulder, snatched or offered, some blur of motion in the flood he'd never reconstruct. The thief looked down at them with an expression that was not kindness. It was closer to annoyance, the way a person looks at a stray dog that followed them home in the rain.

"You," the thief said, "are the worst-timed lawman I have ever met."

The flood roared past below, eating the wash, erasing the footprints, erasing everything — the trail, the route, the whole clean record of the chase, forty miles of evidence gone in twenty minutes of brown water. Juniper lay on the stone and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like grief, that the desert had just closed the book on the hunt. No tracks. No route. Two people on a shelf of rock above a drowning canyon, and whatever happened next would happen with no witnesses but the rain falling thirty miles away.

The thief crouched, forearms on knees, studying Juniper the way Juniper had spent three days studying their prints. Up close there was a fresh cut above one eyebrow, and eyes the color of wet slate, and no fear in them at all — which was its own kind of information.

"So," the thief said. "Fort Calloway sent one ranger. Just you?"

"Just me."

"Huh." A glance upstream, at the flood, at the drowned trail. Something shifted behind the slate eyes — recalculation, decision, the same quick arithmetic as the rope throw. "Well. Since we're stuck on this rock together until the water drops, Ranger, I suppose I should tell you the thing you've been chasing."

Juniper sat up slowly, water streaming off their coat, one hand resting easy and entirely deliberate on the empty loop where their rifle sling should have been — the rifle lost somewhere downstream, another entry in the flood's ledger.

"What thing?"

The thief nodded downstream, toward the churning brown torrent, toward whatever the water had carried and whatever it had uncovered on its way — and there was something in her face now that hadn't been there in three days of footprints. Not fear exactly. Dread, worn thin from carrying.

"The reason," she said, "nobody underlined 'historical sensitivity' twice."

Below the shelf, the flood struck the narrows and boomed like a struck bell, and out of the churning brown water, tumbling end over end through the roar, something bronze caught the last of the light.
