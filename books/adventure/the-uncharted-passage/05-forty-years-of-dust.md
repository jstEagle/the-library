## 5. Forty Years of Dust

The ghost town had a name once — Sable had read it on a surveyor's map she'd lifted from a dead man's saddlebag three years ago. Kettlewash, it said, in faded ink, population forty-one. The map didn't say what the town died of, but towns in the Saltreach usually died of the same thing: the river deciding, one spring, that it loved them no longer.

She led Juniper into Kettlewash at dusk, moving low between the collapsed walls, and the ranger followed without argument, which told Sable more about the state of the chase than anything else. Juniper Halloran was too tired to be proud. Good. Tired people listened.

The cistern was behind what had been a granary, a round stone throat sunk into the earth, dry for decades, its mouth half-hidden under a slab of fallen roof. Sable went down first, boots finding the iron rungs someone had hammered into the wall before her grandmother was born. The bottom was sand and old bird bones and the smell of cold stone. It was, she thought, the safest room in fifty miles, because nobody who mattered knew it existed.

Juniper dropped down after her and stood there in the dark, chest still slung across their back like a coffin they'd agreed to carry, breathing hard.

"You can put it down," Sable said. "It's not going to bite."

"It weighs as much as a body."

"It is a body, sort of." She heard Juniper set it down carefully anyway, the bronze edging scraping stone. "A body with paperwork inside."

Above them, through the cistern mouth, the sky went from copper to violet to nothing. Somewhere out on the flats, the river was still muttering to itself, working through whatever the flood had shaken loose. Sable counted to two hundred in her head, listening for hooves, for voices, for the particular silence that meant riders had stopped moving nearby. Nothing came. She let her shoulders down an inch.

"Talk," Juniper said. Not unkindly. Just flat, the way rangers talked when they'd decided a thing was going to happen whether they liked it or not. "You said whoever else wants this will kill for it. I've been carrying it for six hours wondering if you meant it literally. So talk."

Sable sat down against the cistern wall, drew her knees up, and considered the shape of the story. She'd told it before — twice, both times to people who hadn't lived long enough to repeat it — and each time she'd trimmed it differently. To the smuggler she'd left out the treaty. To the archivist in Marrow's Landing she'd left out the deaths. But Juniper had waded into floodwater for this thing without knowing what it was, and had then spent six hours not opening it, which was either remarkable discipline or remarkable stupidity, and Sable was beginning to suspect it was discipline.

"All right," she said. "But you're not going to like the part where I tell you your government has been lying to you since before you were born."

"I don't have a government," Juniper said. "I have a commission."

"That's worse." She pulled the stopper from her water skin and drank, then passed it over. Juniper took it without hesitation, which was its own small miracle — a ranger sharing water with a thief, in the dark, in a hole in the ground. "Forty years ago there was a country here. Vessia. You've heard the name."

"Everyone's heard the name. Ruins upriver. Dead language on old boundary stones. My oath mentions them — *the borders hold where Vessia fell.*"

"They wrote your oath," Sable said, "and they never told you why Vessia fell, or how fast, or who signed the papers that made it legal." She could feel Juniper go still across the dark. "Vessia wasn't conquered, ranger. It was *dissolved*. There's a difference. A conquest leaves ruins and grief. A dissolution leaves paperwork. And paperwork, unlike grief, can be picked back up."

The silence stretched. Above, a nightbird crossed the cistern mouth and its shadow swept the walls like a slow hand.

"My mother was Vessian," Sable said. "Not heritage-Vessian, not grandmother-stories-Vessian. Born there, in the capital, eleven months before the end. Her family ran the last ferry out during the evacuation, and they carried nothing with them but what they could hold, and what my grandfather chose to hold was a sealed case he wouldn't let anyone else touch, even when the boats were overloaded, even when people were offering him their children to carry instead." She paused. "He drowned four years later in a border skirmish that officially never happened. My mother grew up in camps along the Reach, stateless, telling me stories about a country made of paper."

"And the chest?"

"The chest is the country. Or the deed to it." Sable reached out in the dark and laid her palm flat on the bronze binding. Cold metal, colder than the stone around it. "Inside is a treaty. The Founding Compact — the original instrument by which the borderlands were granted to Vessia, signed by every power that mattered at the time, sealed with sovereign wax, witnessed and countersigned and legally *binding*, because a signature doesn't expire just because the signer does. Whoever holds that document holds a recognized, lawful claim to everything from the Saltreach to the far valley. Not a memory. A claim. The kind lawyers and armies both have to respect."

Juniper's voice came out careful. "That document is forty years dead."

"So are treaties that get dug up," Sable said. "Ask any nation that ever lost a war and kept its archives. Paper sleeps. Paper waits. There are men right now — well-dressed men with official colors and government seals — who would burn down every town on the Reach to be holding that paper when the right court convenes."

"The survey party."

"You've met them."

"I've met Corvane." Something moved through Juniper's voice then, something Sable couldn't name yet — a hairline crack under load. "He signed my commission."

"Then you already know he lies beautifully." She leaned her head back against the stone. "They found the chest three weeks ago. An expedition dressed up as a hydrographic survey, dredging the old channels, 'mapping flood patterns.' Except hydrographic surveys don't bring private guards, and they don't dig in the ruins of the Vessian archive-house, and they don't send their findings south under triple seal. I know because I was hired to guard the dig. Then I was hired again, quietly, by someone inside it, to steal what they found. Then the person who hired me tried to have me killed so they'd be the only one who knew where it went." She shrugged, though Juniper couldn't see it. "The desert's full of people who want to be the only one who knows something."

"Who hired you?"

"A woman named Ilvane. Second chair to Corvane. She wants the treaty delivered to the border delegates at the autumn conclave — under *her* control, with her holding the leash of a resurrected Vessia and everyone in it grateful to her for the resurrection." Sable smiled in the dark, without humor. "And Corvane wants it for himself, and whatever committee stands behind him wants it for the state, and none of them want the same thing, which is the only reason I'm still alive. Three sets of hands reaching for one chest means nobody's grip is firm."

"And you?" Juniper asked. "What do you want?"

The question landed harder than she expected. Sable turned it over, looking for the honest answer, and found it where she always found it: in the image of her mother, in the last year, coughing dust in a rented room in Marrow's Landing, saying *don't ever let them hand you that country, little fox, it isn't a country, it's a hook.*

"What I want," Sable said slowly, "is for the chest to reach the last living heir. Odile Vessane. She's alive, she's real, and she's the only person on this earth with the blood-right to decide what happens to that claim — keep it, sell it, or bury it. That was the arrangement. That was always the arrangement. The heir decides. That's the whole point of a blood-right; it cuts out the middlemen, the committees, the directors, the thieves." She heard her own voice roughen and hated it. "Everyone else who touches this chest wants to *use* it. She's the only one who might choose to end it."

"End it?"

"Some inheritances aren't gifts, ranger. Some inheritances are landmines. You don't get to choose whether you step on one — only whether you pass it to your children." Sable pressed her palm harder against the bronze, as if she could feel the paper sleeping inside, patient as arsenic. "My mother carried that weight her whole life and it killed her slower than the fever did. Forty years of dust, and it's all still in there, waiting for somebody ambitious enough to call it destiny."

Juniper was quiet for a long time. When they finally spoke, the flatness was gone from their voice, replaced by something more dangerous — thought.

"Corvane said recovered government property. He said the survey was acting under charter."

"He did say that."

"He also lied about which fork we'd take at the canyon mouth. I checked his map against the terrain while you were sleeping yesterday. The charter route runs east. His riders went west. Toward the archive-house." A breath. "He told me the truth about everything except the one thing that mattered, and he told it to my face while handing me bread."

"Now you understand the disease," Sable said softly. "Welcome to the borderlands. We've all got it. It's called knowing exactly enough to be useful and not enough to be safe."

Above them, faint but unmistakable, came the sound Sable had been dreading since sundown: hooves. Not one rider. Many, moving in formation, unhurried, the way people move when they own the night and know it.

Juniper rose without a word and climbed two rungs until they could see out through the gap in the rubble. Sable watched the starlight catch the line of their shoulders and waited.

"Survey colors," Juniper breathed, dropping back down. "A dozen riders, maybe more. They're making camp at the wells." A pause, and then, quieter, in a voice that had stopped being a ranger's voice and started being something rawer: "They're not searching for us, Sable. They're sweeping. Grid pattern. That's not a recovery operation."

"No," Sable agreed, her stomach turning cold and calm, the way it did right before things went wrong in interesting ways. "That's a cordon. They're not trying to find the chest anymore."

"Then what—"

"They're making sure nobody else finds it first." She pulled her pack close and began, silently, to repack. "Which means somewhere between here and Marrow's Landing, someone gave the order to stop recovering and start *containing* — and orders like that only get given when the person giving them already knows exactly what's in the box."

Juniper stood very still in the dark. Then, in a voice stripped down to bedrock:

"How long do we have?"

Sable looked up at the coin of starlight above them, at the riders settling into camp around the only water for twenty miles, and did the arithmetic she'd hoped not to do.

"Until dawn," she said. "Maybe less. I hope you slept well, ranger — because the only way out of Kettlewash is past twelve armed men who think you're already theirs."

* * *

Outside, the fires of the survey camp bloomed one by one along the wells of Kettlewash, throwing long shadows across a town that had died waiting for something to come back.
