## 7. Night Crossing

They waited out the daylight in the ribs of a dead orchard, and Juniper hated every hour of it.

Lying flat under a wind-bent apple tree gone to gray wood, watching heat shimmer turn the Saltreach into a mirage of itself, Juniper counted the reasons waiting was wrong. The survey party had horses, fresh ones. They had numbers — Juniper had counted eleven riders on the ridge before the dust took them. They had Corvane, who had signed the commission pinned inside Juniper's coat and who did not strike Juniper as a man who forgave a stolen chest.

Waiting was wrong. But moving by day through open salt country with two tired horses and a thief for a companion was worse, so they waited, and Juniper learned something about Sable in the waiting: she could sleep anywhere, instantly, like an animal folding itself away. She slept through the worst heat with her hat over her face and one hand resting on the chest strap, and when she woke she was awake all at once, no fog, no grogginess, eyes going straight to the horizon.

"Riders?" she asked.

"Not yet."

"They'll wait for dark too." She sat up, worked her shoulders, winced. "Corvane doesn't like chasing things he can see. He likes knowing where they'll be tomorrow."

"You know him?"

"I know his type." She pulled a strip of dried meat from her pack and offered half without looking, which was its own kind of arithmetic — enough for two meant planning for two. "The kind that gets other people to do his dying."

Juniper took the meat. It tasted like salt and patience. "There's a marsh," they said. "South of here, where the river used to braid before they cut it for the ferry road. Salt pans on either side, reed channels through the middle. By starlight you can move right past their camp if their fires are on the ridge."

"And if their fires aren't on the ridge?"

"Then we're already dead and just haven't been told."

Sable grinned around the jerky. "Ranger optimism. Eleven years of it, I bet. No wonder you're fun at parties."

* * *

Full dark came down like a lid. No moon — the moon had died three nights ago and wouldn't be reborn till week's end — and the stars came out in the brutal profusion the desert only allowed when everything else went black. The Milky Way spilled across the sky like something overturned.

They walked the horses the first mile, boots wrapped in sacking against the crunch of salt crust, and even so the ground complained under them, small treacherous sounds that sounded enormous in the silence. Ahead, maybe two miles east, the survey camp glowed: four fires in a neat line along the ridge, lanterns between the tents, the murmur of men carrying on water. Exactly where Juniper had told Sable they'd be, and exactly where a smart commander would put them while sending pickets out into the dark.

"There'll be watchers," Juniper murmured as they reached the first reeds.

"There always are. Watchers get cold and bored and start thinking about the fire they're missing." Sable handed over her horse's lead rope. "Which is why we go through the marsh instead of around it. Nobody watches the middle of a place that eats people."

"That's not reassuring."

"It's not supposed to be. It's supposed to be true."

The marsh took them the way Juniper remembered: channels of brackish water shin-deep, then knee-deep, hemmed in by reeds tall as a rider, the salt crust breaking underfoot into plates that tilted and sank. The horses hated it, ears flat, breath loud. Juniper went first because it was their country, their map drawn from years of patrol reports and one bad winter spent mapping drainage nobody cared about, and Sable came behind leading both remounts, and for a long time there was nothing in the world but starlight on black water and the sound of their own movement.

It should have been miserable. It was, mostly — cold, wet, slow, the reeds cutting little lines across the backs of their hands. But underneath the misery Juniper felt something unfamiliar loosening, some knot behind the sternum that had been tied for eleven years. Out here there was no command structure. No ledger. No oath except the one being broken step by step through stinking water. There was only the next channel bend, and the person behind them holding the horses, and the strange fact that Juniper trusted her to be there.

They didn't examine that fact. Examining it felt dangerous in a way Corvane didn't.

* * *

Sable heard the camp change before she saw anything.

A shift in the fire-glow on the low clouds — one light moving where lights shouldn't move. A voice carrying flat across the water, words unreadable but tone unmistakable: someone giving orders, someone else objecting. She stopped the column with a hand on the last horse's flank and stood listening, and the marsh listened with her, frogs going quiet in a spreading ring.

"Pickets," she breathed, close to Juniper's ear. "Moving. Two of them, along the near bank, walking our way."

Juniper's head turned a fraction. "How can you possibly—"

"Voice carries over water. Boots on salt crust carry better. And they're talking, which means bored, which means we have maybe four minutes before they reach the channel mouth ahead." Her hand found Juniper's sleeve in the dark. "Left. There's a pan crossing — dry ground, flat, exposed as sin, but fast. We take it and drop back into the reeds beyond."

"The pan's a trap. That whole flat is underlain with brine channels. You can't tell crust from hollow in the dark."

"So we don't walk it. We run it."

"Sable—"

"Four minutes, ranger."

And because the alternative was standing still while lantern-light bloomed through the reeds behind them, they ran it.

Sable went first this time — she'd crossed this marsh once before, two years ago, running from a different set of men, and her body remembered the safe line even if her mind had tried to bury it. Three hundred yards of white salt glowing faintly under the stars, hooves throwing up haloes of dust, her own heartbeat counting the distance. Behind her Juniper followed, and she could hear the difference in their footfalls — heavier, more deliberate, a person reading the ground instead of trusting it.

She was twenty yards from the far reeds when the sound behind her changed.

Not a footfall. A soft collapse, like a sack of grain set down too fast, and then a sound she'd heard once before in her life and never wanted to hear again: the wet suck of brine taking weight, and a human voice cut off mid-breath.

She spun. Juniper was gone. Where they'd been there was only a dimple in the crust, a hole widening in slow pulses, and one arm above the surface, fingers spread on the salt plate, and the horses screaming somewhere off to her left, tearing free or trying to.

Later she would not remember deciding. She remembered the cold. She remembered dropping flat, distributing her weight the way you learn when the whole world is a lie about what will hold you, and worming forward on elbows and knees across crust that flexed like skin over water. She remembered catching Juniper's wrist — bare skin, freezing, slick with brine — and the arm pulling down as the body below thrashed, and her saying, in a voice she didn't recognize as her own:

"*Stop. Stop moving. Juniper. STOP.*"

And the arm going still.

That was the whole trick, the terrible counterintuitive truth of brine sinkholes: struggle fed them. Every kick liquefied the slurry deeper, dragged the victim further into the cold dense muck below the crust where the salt water sat poisoned and airless. Stillness was life. Stillness was also drowning slowly in the dark while someone you'd known four days held your wrist and figured out what to do next.

"Hold," she said again, quieter. "I've got you. Don't pull. Let me do the work."

She got her legs wide, found a crust plate that held, and began — slowly, an inch at a time, hating every second of the flex beneath her — to lean back. Brine ran into her collar. Her shoulder screamed. Below her grip, Juniper's breathing came in short controlled pulls, the breathing of someone managing panic with technique, and she thought: *of course. Even drowning, they do it properly.*

The crust groaned. Somewhere out in the dark a horse whinnied and got no answer.

"Don't talk," she said. "Save it. I'm going to count, and on three you push with everything you've got, straight back, toward my voice. Not up. Back. Up keeps you in the hole. Back puts you on the plate with me."

A pause. Then, muffled, bubbling, furious and calm at once: "You don't have to—"

"Three."

Juniper pushed.

It was ugly and it worked. The crust broke behind them in a long rippling crack like lake ice, and then they were both sliding on bellies across solid salt, Sable hauling, Juniper clawing, until the reeds closed around them and they lay gasping in black mud with the stars wheeling overhead and the pickets' voices — closer now, confused, circling the hole — thin on the wind.

Sable started laughing. She couldn't help it. Silent, shaking laughter, brine drying white in streaks on her face, and beside her Juniper lay on their back doing the same thing, or something adjacent to it, breath hitching in a rhythm that might have been laughter or might have been the other thing, finally arriving now that the body had permission.

"We lost a horse," Juniper managed.

"We lost a horse."

"And half the supplies were on that horse."

"Yep."

"And you saved my life."

Sable turned her head. In the starlight she couldn't read Juniper's face — it was just a pale shape, wet hair plastered down, eyes catching the sky. That was the thing about the dark, she thought. It made confessions cheap and truths expensive.

"Seems like," she said.

"I don't—" Juniper's voice caught, restarted. "I keep accounts. It's how I was raised into this work. Debts logged, favors weighed. And I don't know what column this goes in. I don't know what a life weighs."

Sable lay back in the mud and looked at the Milky Way and let the silence sit there, because she knew — the way you know things about people you've decided to trust, which is to say without evidence — that rushing it would break it.

"A life weighs a life," she said finally. "But here's the trick nobody tells you. You don't pay it back to me. You pay it forward. To somebody who needs it worse. That's the only interest rate that doesn't rot."

Juniper was quiet a long time. The picket voices faded east, called back by a whistle from the ridge. One of their horses — the bay, sensible animal — came crashing through the reeds trailing its lead rope, and stood over them dripping and indignant, and neither of them moved to catch it.

"When I was in the water," Juniper said at last, very quietly, "under the crust. Before you grabbed me. There was a moment where the honest calculation was: this is fine. This is what the ledger says I owe. Eleven years of service, paid in full, one hole in a marsh." A breath. "And then your hand closed on my wrist and I reached for it anyway. Without deciding to. That's the part I can't log."

Sable rolled onto her side, propped on an elbow, studying the shape of them against the stars.

"Welcome to being a person," she said. "It gets worse."

* * *

They came out of the marsh an hour before dawn, on the far side, soaked to the waist and one horse poorer, and climbed the low shale rise where the ferry road began its long descent toward the river country. Behind them the survey camp still burned its orderly fires. Ahead, miles south, the river waited, and Marrow's Landing somewhere along it, and a woman named Odile Vessane who didn't yet know she was about to inherit a war.

Sable wrung out her hat and settled it on her head. Juniper checked the chest straps twice, then a third time, and Sable watched them do it and said nothing, because she understood: the checking wasn't about the chest anymore. It was about the hands doing the checking. Giving them work. Keeping them from thinking about the wrist-grab, the reach, the unlogged debt.

They walked. The sky went gray, then rose-colored, then the particular bruised gold the desert wore when it had decided, provisionally, to let you live another day.

It was Juniper who saw it first — a smudge of dust on the southern road, too deliberate for weather, too organized for travelers. They stopped together on the rise and watched it resolve, and Juniper's stomach dropped through the salt crust of the whole world.

Riders. Not eleven. Not coming from behind.

Coming from ahead, flying survey colors, moving fast — between them and the river.

Sable squinted, swore softly, and looked at Juniper with the ghost of last night's laughter still somewhere in her face, gone now.

"So," she said. "Which are we doing?"
