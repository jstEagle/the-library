## 8. The Last Heir

Marrow's Landing smelled of tar, wet rope, and fish guts, and Sable loved it instantly.

Any town that smelled that bad was a town that didn't ask questions. The river had widened here into a slow brown ribbon, and the town clung to its banks the way barnacles cling to a hull — crooked stairways, sagging piers, a market strung along the waterline under canvas that had faded from every color to the same exhausted gray. Flatboats loaded with melons. A woman selling ice from a cart packed in sawdust, charging triple because she could. Two boys fishing off a broken barge with more patience than skill.

And at the end of the longest pier, a ferry dock with a hand-painted sign: **ODILE'S CROSSING — FAIR RATES, NO SERMONS.**

"There," Sable said.

Juniper followed her gaze and said nothing, which by now Sable had learned to read as agreement. Three days of hard riding and one night of marsh-crawling had worn them both down to the bone, but Juniper's silence had stopped feeling like suspicion somewhere back in the salt marsh. It had started feeling like company.

The woman on the dock was coiling rope.

She was maybe forty, broad-shouldered, sun-browned, with gray coming in at her temples and hands that looked like they'd spent thirty years arguing with wood and winning. She coiled rope the way other people prayed — fast, precise, without looking at it. A flatboat was tied up behind her, its hull freshly patched, its name scraped off the stern and painted over with something illegible.

Sable had imagined this moment a hundred times across a thousand miles. She'd rehearsed the words in her head during the worst of the saltpan crossing, during the nights when Juniper slept and she kept watch with the chest between her boots: *I've come a long way. I have something of yours. You're going to hate it.*

She hadn't rehearsed what she'd do if the woman just kept coiling rope.

"Odile Vessane?" Sable said.

The rope stopped. For a moment the whole dock seemed to stop with it — the water slapping the pilings, the gulls, the market noise — and then it all started up again and the woman hadn't moved at all except for her eyes, which came up slow and flat as a snake's.

"Never heard of her," she said. "You want the ferry, it's two bits a head, four with a horse. You want trouble, walk back up the hill."

"We walked out of a desert," Sable said. "We crossed the Saltreach. We carried—"

"I know what you carried." The woman stood. Up close she was taller than she'd looked, and there was a knife on her belt worn smooth from use, and her eyes went to the wrapped bundle on Juniper's back with an expression Sable had seen before on people looking at graves. "Word travels downriver faster than you do. A bronze chest with a dead king's stamp on it, pulled out of a flood like the river was sorry for something. Half the town's been talking about it since yesterday." She spat over the rail into the water. "I told them it was a story."

"It's not a story."

"No," the woman said. "It's worse. Stories end."

* * *

Her name, according to the sign and everyone in town, was Doss. Doss the ferrywoman. Doss who'd bought the dock nine years back from a dead man's estate and never gave anyone cause to look twice at her. She made them sit on the flatboat while she worked — patching, caulking, pointedly not looking at them — and Sable sat on a crate with the chest at her feet and felt the whole mission tilt sideways under her.

"You don't understand what it is," Sable said, for the third time.

"I understand exactly what it is." Doss drove a caulking iron into a seam with a short, vicious tap. "I've understood what it was since I was six years old and my mother burned our papers in the stove and told me my name was a debt I'd spend my whole life paying. That's what's in your box, girl. A debt. Forty years of interest."

"The treaty—"

"Is a piece of vellum that got forty thousand people killed once already." She sat back on her heels and looked at Sable directly for the first time. Her eyes were dark, level, and very tired. "You want to know what Vessia was? Vessia was a border drawn by men who never crossed it. Vessia was my father standing in a doorway telling my mother to take me and go, and the door burning behind him. Vessia is a word that gets people like me killed so people like whoever sent you can feel important."

"Nobody sent me," Sable said. "I stole it."

That got a short laugh, dry as salt. "Even better. So it's not a mission, it's a delivery. And you came a thousand miles to hand a landmine to a woman running a ferry, because why?"

"Because you're the heir."

"I'm a ferrywoman." Doss stood, wiped her hands on her thighs, and looked out at the river with an expression Sable couldn't read — something between longing and contempt, aimed at the water. "You know what this boat is? My father's. He ran cargo up and down this river before Vessia fell, back when this bank was Vessian soil and nobody cared. When it fell, the new government confiscated everything with a Vessian name on the deed. He got it back by dying and leaving it to a daughter with a false name. That's my inheritance. A boat I had to orphan to own." She turned. "And you're asking me to trade that for a crown made of ash."

"Yes," Sable said. "Because if you don't, someone worse will use it."

* * *

Juniper spoke then, from where they'd been leaning against the rail, quiet the whole time. Sable had almost forgotten they could talk.

"Director Corvane is behind us," Juniper said. "Survey colors. Maybe four days back, maybe less. He signed my commission. I know how he works. He doesn't want the chest for the government — the government doesn't know it exists. He wants it for himself, and he'll burn this town down to a foundation to get it, and then he'll have the claim, and there'll be no one left alive who could contradict it. Except you."

Doss — Odile — looked at Juniper for a long moment. "You're a ranger."

"I was."

"That's a strange way to say it."

"That's a strange week."

Odile almost smiled. It didn't survive. She walked to the stern, to the place where the old name had been painted over, and stood there with her back to them. Sable watched her shoulders. They weren't still.

"My mother's name was Vessane," she said, to the river. "She died when I was nineteen, in a town two hundred miles from here, under a different false name, and her last words to me were *never let them find you.* Not *never let them catch you.* *Find* you. Because being found was the danger. Being known." She turned around. "And you two walked onto my dock and said my name out loud in front of the fish market."

Sable's stomach dropped. She glanced up the pier. The market was still going, the ice woman, the melon boats, the boys fishing — but now she was counting eyes. Three had turned their way. Maybe four. Nothing yet. Nothing, probably.

"Probably," she told herself, and didn't believe it.

"Nobody heard," she said. "It was—"

"Everyone heard." Odile came back up the deck, and now there was something new in her face under the anger, and Sable recognized it because she'd seen it in mirrors: fear, wearing anger's clothes. "You want to know the worst part? Some small stupid part of me has been waiting forty years for someone to walk up that pier and say that name. I hate that part of me. I've spent my whole life starving that part of me. And you fed it in one sentence." She stopped in front of Sable, close enough that Sable could see the fine lines around her eyes, the sun damage, the resemblance to a face on old coins Sable had only ever seen in books — the Vessian royal line, the strong jaw, the level dark eyes. "So here's what happens now. You take your chest, you get back on your horses, and you ride until the river forgets you. And I'm going to stand on this dock and watch you go, and tomorrow I'm going to paint over that sign and be Doss again, and in ten years I'll be dead and buried under a stone that says DOSS, and that will be the happiest ending available to anyone in this conversation."

"No," Sable said.

Odile blinked. "Excuse me?"

"No." Sable stood up. She was so tired her knees were shaking, and she'd been shot at, flooded, sunk, and hunted across a desert, and she found that all of it had boiled her down to something very simple and very hard. "I didn't steal this chest to make your life easier. I stole it because the people who had it were going to use it to own the borderlands, and the only person on earth with a better right to it than them is you. I don't care if you burn it. I don't care if you sink it in the river. I care that *you* decide, not Corvane, not some survey director with a family crest and a grudge older than me. You want to be Doss? Fine. Be Doss. But be Doss *holding it*, for one hour, and then throw it in the river yourself. Because if you send us away and Corvane takes it off our corpses four miles up the road, then your father burned in that doorway for nothing, and so did mine."

The dock was very quiet.

"My father?" Odile said.

Sable hadn't meant to say it. It came out anyway, the way truth does when you're too tired to hold it. "Vessian border family. Farmers. When Vessia fell, the new charter voided every land grant on the frontier. Soldiers came to clear the 'disputed' parcels. My parents argued they had a treaty right. The treaty was the problem, see — it was the treaty that made our land worth stealing." She heard her own voice go flat, the way it did. "I was four. I don't remember faces. I remember the treaty being blamed. For thirty years I thought if I could just find the thing that ruined us, I'd understand it. I found it. It's a box. It's just a box with a dead man's stamp on it, and it ruins everything it touches, and I am *begging* you to be the one person who decides what it ruins instead of just standing in the way."

Odile looked at her for a long time. Then at Juniper, who said nothing, which somehow said plenty.

"The river," Odile said slowly, "took this chest out of whatever grave it was in and set it in your path. Rivers don't do that for nothing. My father used to say the Marrow keeps what matters and coughs up what it's done with." She looked down at the chest, at the bronze binding, at the seal stamped in the lid like a wound. "I should make you leave."

"Yes," Sable said.

"I should make you leave right now, tonight, and never think of you again."

"You should," Sable agreed. "You won't."

"And why won't I?"

"Because you're still standing here," Sable said. "You could've walked away ten minutes ago. Rope to coil. Boat to patch. You're still standing here."

Odile closed her eyes. When she opened them, the fear was still there, but it had stopped wearing the anger, and what was underneath was worse and better: a woman doing arithmetic she'd spent forty years refusing to do.

"Sunset," she said. "You'll stay on the boat. You'll touch nothing, you'll speak to no one, and at sunset I decide. And if I say go—"

"We go," Juniper said.

"And if I say sink it?"

"Then we sink it," Sable said. "That was always the second-best ending."

Odile almost laughed — a single breath through the nose, gone as fast as it came. She turned back to her caulking, and the conversation was over, and Sable sat back down on her crate with her heart hammering and realized her hands were shaking.

Juniper settled beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. Neither of them said anything. Out on the water, the light was going long and gold, and the boys on the barge had given up fishing, and the ice woman was pushing her cart home, and everything looked exactly like a town where nothing was about to happen.

Except.

Sable had spent her whole life watching doors and windows, exits and approaches, the way other people watched weather. And so she saw it before Juniper did, before Odile, before anyone: at the top of the hill road, where the market thinned out into scrub and the town stopped pretending to be more than it was, a rider had appeared. Just one, sitting his horse in the last of the light, not coming down. Not leaving, either.

Watching the dock.

Watching the boat.

Watching the chest.

"Juniper," Sable said quietly, without moving her lips much. "Don't look up yet."

Juniper looked up, of course, because they were Juniper. But they were fast about it, a flick, and then their hand found the crate edge and went very still.

"One rider," Sable breathed. "Scout. He's seen us."

Below them, Odile drove the caulking iron home, tap, tap, tap, steady as a heartbeat, and the sun went down on Marrow's Landing like a coin slipping under water.
