## 10. Burning the Ferry

Odile Vessane had been pouring lamp oil into the bilge of her father's boat for ten minutes before she let herself feel anything about it.

That was the trick. She'd learned it young, on the dock at Marrow's Landing, when the fish came up rotten and the buyers walked away and her father put his hand on her shoulder and said, *Feel it later, little bird. Feel it now and you'll drop the net.* So she worked. She worked the pump handle of the oil drum, she worked the rags down into the seams, she worked the wicks loose from three lanterns and laid them in a trail from bow to stern like she was setting a table for guests.

The ferry was called the *Kindly Current*. Her father had named it the week Odile was born, which meant the boat was thirty-one years old and so was the lie she lived under. Thirty-one years of hauling grain and goats and gossips across the Marrow River for copper coins, thirty-one years of being nobody, of being Tilda Crane, ferrywoman, widow of no one, daughter of no one worth naming. Thirty-one years since Vessia burned and the Vessanes scattered and her mother put a false name in her mouth like a coin under the tongue of the dead.

And now these two — the ranger with the sun-cracked face and the thief with the clever hands — had rowed up to her dock in the dark and set all of it on her lap like a cat that expected to be fed.

"The lantern's lit," Sable said from the gangplank. "Juniper's got the far bank watched. Corvane's outriders will see the smoke inside the hour."

"I know how fire works," Odile said.

She knelt in the bilge one last time. The wood down here was black and soft with thirty years of river water. Her father had re-caulked every seam himself, on his knees, humming. He'd taught her the caulking too. He'd taught her everything except who she was, because knowing that, he said, would get her killed, and he'd rather she be alive and nameless than dead and royal.

He'd been dead nine years now. The boat was the last thing left that knew his hands.

*Feel it later.*

She climbed out.

* * *

The argument had happened an hour ago, on the dock, and Odile could still hear every word of it the way you hear a bell after it stops ringing.

"You're asking me to burn my own boat," she'd said.

"We're asking you to help us stop Corvane," Juniper said. The ranger stood very straight, hat in hand, the way honest people did when they were about to say something dishonest-adjacent and knew it. "There are forty soldiers downstream at Miller's Bend. I counted their cookfires. When Corvane finishes charming you tomorrow, they cross, they take the chest, and they take you — because a living Vessian heir is worth more to him than any treaty. Dead, you're a martyr he can use. Alive, you're a puppet. Either way, the *Kindly Current* is how his whole column gets over this river tonight or tomorrow night, and we can't sink it without burning it."

"Then swim."

"With the chest? With him hunting us?" Juniper's jaw tightened. "I've read the water. It's low and fast and there's a strainer at the bend that would take a strong swimmer apart. We'd lose everything."

"So lose it." Odile heard her own voice go flat and cold, the voice she used on drunks who wouldn't pay. "You know what you're actually asking? You're not asking me to burn a boat. You're asking me to burn my father. That boat is the only thing in this world he built that's still floating. Everything else he made is ash already — his country, his name, his family, me, what I was supposed to be. And you two come up my dock with your chest full of dead kings' paper and you want to make the ash complete."

Sable hadn't said anything during that part. She'd sat on the mooring post with her boots dangling, watching Odile with those dark patient eyes, and when the silence got long enough she'd said, quietly:

"My mother kept a candle burning for Vessia every night for twenty years. Little clay thing, cost half a copper. She lit it until the day she died, and I'll tell you what it ever bought her. Nothing. Not one thing. Vessia didn't come back, didn't save her, didn't even notice. But you know what the candle did do?" She hopped off the post. "It kept her looking backward. Twenty years facing a door that wasn't going to open. My father used to say grief is a room, and you can live in it if you have to, but don't furnish it."

"That's very pretty," Odile said. "Did you make it up before or after you stole the chest?"

"After," Sable said, unbothered. "Stealing clarifies the mind."

And then Juniper had done the thing that Odile still couldn't decide whether to forgive them for. They'd taken off the ranger badge — the actual brass badge, worn smooth at the edges — and set it down on the dock planks between them, face up, catching the lamplight.

"I took an oath on that," Juniper said. "Twelve years ago. Protect the frontier, serve the compact, obey lawful authority. Every year since, lawful authority has asked me for a little more obedience and given a little less law, and I've paid it, because paying was easier than looking at the bill. Corvane signed my commission. Corvane is camped at Miller's Bend with forty men and a speech prepared about restoring your house, and I promise you, Odile Vessane, there is not one word in that speech that isn't a hook. I've seen him bait hooks my whole career." They picked the badge back up, slowly, and turned it over in their fingers like something they were deciding whether to keep. "I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to look at the arithmetic. A boat against a border. Your father's ghost against everyone else's children."

"And if I say no?"

"Then we take the chest and swim the strainer and probably die, and Corvane crosses anyway, and burns your boat as a courtesy." Juniper met her eyes. "But I wanted you to choose it. That matters to me. Choosing things matters. Nobody let me choose much."

Odile had looked at the badge, and the river, and the dark shape of the *Kindly Current* riding at her mooring, patient as a dog.

Then she'd gone to get the lamp oil.

* * *

Now she stood on her own dock with oil-black hands and watched the first gray light come up over the water, and felt nothing, which was its own kind of feeling.

Juniper appeared out of the reeds on the path from Miller's Bend, moving quick and low. "Outriders are saddling. An hour, maybe less, before someone rides ahead to arrange the crossing. If we're doing it—"

"We're doing it." Odile surprised herself with how steady it came out. "But we do it my way."

Juniper stopped. Sable, coiling rope by the gangplank, went still too, the way thieves went still — total attention, weight forward.

"He'll expect sabotage," Odile said. "A burned ferry looks like defiance, and defiance tells him exactly where we are and what we think. So we don't give him defiance. We give him an accident." She pointed at the old boathouse, the tar shed, the stacked cordwood. "The fire starts here, in the shed. Wind's coming downriver off the flats, it takes the shed, takes the boathouse, takes the boat at her mooring. By the time anyone asks questions, poor Tilda Crane lost everything to a stray spark, tragic, happens every dry summer. She's devastated. She's cooperative. She might even, God pity her, accept the Director's generous offer of passage and protection."

Sable started to grin. Then she looked at Odile properly. "You'd stay. Play the grieving ferrywoman while we run."

"For exactly as long as it takes you two to get clear, and not one minute longer, because I am not staying in a town where Corvane's soldiers are drinking and asking questions about a woman with Vessian cheekbones." Odile wiped her hands on her apron, uselessly; the oil had soaked through to her skin. "After that, I'm coming with you."

Juniper frowned. "The saltpan crossing—"

"I know about the saltpan. I've ferried salt-carriers for fifteen years, ranger. I know where the water seeps are, I know which stars sit over the safe ground, and I know that the map you're carrying has the pan drawn as a blank space with a dragon on it, because cartographers are cowards." She untied her apron and dropped it on the dock. Underneath she was already dressed for travel — she'd been dressed for travel since midnight, since before she agreed to anything, which she supposed told her something about herself she wasn't ready to examine. "You want to burn my father's boat? Fine. But you don't get to burn it and leave me standing in the ashes being grateful. Whatever this costs, it costs all three of us. That's the price. Take it or put the lantern out."

For a long moment nobody spoke. Downriver, faintly, a horse nickered.

"It's a fair price," Sable said.

"It's a steep one," Juniper said, but they were unbuckling their pack as they said it, pulling out the spare waterskin, holding it out to her. Offering provisions. Which was, Odile was learning, how this ranger said yes.

* * *

The fire, when it caught, was beautiful, and Odile hated herself for thinking so.

It started small, the way she'd built it — a shingle of flame in the tar shed, licking up the stacked cordwood, finding the pitch. For a full minute it just breathed, orange light pulsing in the shed doorway like something waking up. Then the wind came down off the flats right on schedule, the way it did every morning, and the fire stood up.

It crossed the yard between the shed and the boathouse in a single leap, a ribbon of flame unrolling, and then the boathouse went with a sound like a held breath released, and the heat hit Odile's face from thirty feet away, and the *Kindly Current* began to die.

She'd thought she would look away. She found she couldn't. The fire ran the length of the hull the way floodwater runs a channel, finding every seam her father had caulked, every joint his hands had fitted, and the old cedar went up bright and greedy, resin crackling, and the mast caught like a torch, and the whole boat stood there in the river burning upright for a long, impossible moment — a ship of fire riding calm water, reflected double below itself, two burning ships, one going up and one going down.

"There's the river keeping it," Sable said softly beside her. "Everything you give the river, it keeps."

"Shut up," Odile said, without heat, and Sable, to her credit, shut up.

Embers lifted off the mast and streamed downriver on the wind, dozens of them, hundreds, a slow orange snowfall drifting toward Miller's Bend, and Odile understood suddenly that the wind was doing their work for them — that sparks would land on Corvane's picket lines, on the dry grass, on the tarred ropes of whatever crossing rig his engineers had staged, and his soldiers would spend their first hour awake fighting fire instead of arranging a ferry. She almost laughed. Her father's boat, going out not as a sacrifice but as a weapon. He'd have hated it. He'd also, she suspected, have insisted on aiming it.

"Tilda Crane," Juniper said beside her, and Odile turned.

The ranger was watching her, not the fire, and their face in the firelight was hard to read — sympathy and wariness and something older, some private grief of their own banked down behind the eyes.

"What about her?"

"When they come ask — and they'll come within the hour — you need to be crying. Can you do that?"

Odile looked back at the boat. At the place where the mooring line had burned through and the blackened hull was settling, taking on the river, her father's thirty-one years of work folding down into the water that had carried it.

"Yes," she said, and found, testing it, that she barely had to pretend. "That part's easy. The rest of the lie is easy. What's not easy—" She stopped. Started again. "What's not easy is that I spent thirty-one years building a life specifically so that no one could ever take it from me by force. Small life. Dull life. Nothing worth stealing. And it turns out the only way to keep it was to destroy it myself, first, on my own terms." She laughed, short and ugly. "Somebody should have told me that was the job."

"Somebody should have told all of us," Juniper said.

* * *

They went out the back way, through the reed beds, single file, Juniper leading with Sable in the middle and Odile last, carrying the chest strapped to her own back because she had refused, absolutely refused, to let either of them carry her father's country behind their shoulders. Behind them the glow of the fire lit the reeds orange, and voices were already rising from the direction of Miller's Bend — shouts, a whistle, the clang of a mess kettle beaten like an alarm. Sparks landing. Good.

At the tree line Juniper paused and looked back, and Odile looked too, one last time.

The *Kindly Current* was nearly gone now, just a smoking skeleton at the waterline, and on the dock stood three figures in survey uniforms, and even at this distance she recognized the center one by his posture alone — the unhurried stillness of a man who owned every room he entered. Director Corvane, arrived early, arriving always early, standing in the light of her father's funeral pyre with his hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring a sunset.

As they watched, he turned his head — slowly, deliberately — and looked directly at the tree line. At them. At the exact spot where they stood, though there was no way, no possible way, he could see three figures in the shadow of the reeds at eighty yards before dawn.

He smiled. Odile felt it land on her skin like a coal.

"He knows," she whispered.

"No," Juniper said, but their hand had gone to their knife. "He suspects. There's a difference."

"Is there?" Sable murmured. "From where I'm standing, both of them mean run."

They ran.
