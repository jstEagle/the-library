## 11. The Ranger's Net

They heard the rangers before they saw them — the particular music of a sweep line moving through canyon country: a whistle note passed hand to hand down the ridge, the click of shod stone, once, far off, a dog.

Juniper went flat against the rock and pulled Sable down beside her by the sleeve.

"Don't move," she breathed. "Don't even breathe loud."

Below them, the canyon road bent along the dry wash like a rope someone had dropped. On it, small with distance but unmistakable, moved six riders in ranger gray, spread wide across the road in the pattern Juniper herself had drilled a hundred green recruits in. Anchor the flanks on high ground. Push the center. Let the terrain do the work.

Her terrain. Her drills. Her people.

Sable's mouth was at her ear. "Friends of yours?"

"That's Wren Okafor on the left flank," Juniper said, before she could stop herself. "And that's Halvorsen's horse — he rides a paint nobody else can sit." She swallowed. "That's a textbook line."

"You'd be proud."

"I taught it to them."

The riders came on at a walk, patient as weather. Somewhere behind them, further up the canyon system, another whistle answered — a second line, maybe a third. The net wasn't a net yet. It was being woven, strand by strand, across every road out of the Saltreach, and somewhere in the middle of it, on a list with two thieves, was her own name.

*Deserter.* The word from the way-station notice board still sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. *Juniper Halloran, badge suspended, considered unreliable. Two thieves and a deserter. Report all sightings.*

Three weeks ago she had been the one reading those boards.

* * *

They moved at night after that, and not well.

The chest rode wrapped in saddle canvas on Odile's mule — Odile had insisted the mule come, and the mule had opinions about canyon switchbacks that it shared at length — and the weight of it changed how they traveled. Slow. Careful. Every hour they spent picking a safe line down a scree slope was an hour the sweep lines gained.

"They're not chasing us," Sable said on the second night, crouched over a map scratched into the dirt by firelight no bigger than a cupped hand. "Understand that. They're herding us. Corvane doesn't want a fight in open country — he wants us funneled somewhere he chooses. Look." Her finger traced the canyon roads. "Every pass east has a patrol. South is the saltpan, and he's betting we won't try it. That leaves the river roads north. Toward Marrow's Landing. Toward him."

"Then we go south."

"Across the pan? Without water caches?"

"We'll carry water."

"Two days' carry for three days' crossing, if nothing goes wrong." Sable looked up. "Something always goes wrong."

Juniper fed a twist of dry sagebrush to the fire and watched the light climb Sable's face — the sharp cheekbones, the eyes that never quite stopped calculating even when they were tired. Three weeks ago this woman had been a name on a warrant. Now she was the person Juniper checked for first when she woke.

"Something always goes wrong," Juniper agreed. "But I know these canyons, and I know the rangers working them. North is where they're thickest. And there's one stretch of road—" She stopped.

Sable waited. She was good at waiting. It was one of the first things Juniper had learned about her, back when learning things about her had been tactical and later when it had become something else entirely.

"There's a choke point called the Kettle," Juniper said finally. "Narrow road, walls on both sides, one way through. Any sweep coming south has to pass it or go around by half a day. It's also the worst possible place to be caught, because there's no cover and no exit."

"So we avoid it."

"We avoid it. But everyone else uses it. Which means if we watch it, we'll see exactly what's moving and where." She drew the shape in the dirt. "We tuck into the side draw above it tonight. We let the net tell us its own shape."

"And if someone comes up the side draw?"

Juniper thought of who would think of the side draw. There were maybe four rangers in the whole service who would. One of them was retired. One of them was dead.

One of them had taught her everything she knew.

"Then I'll handle it," she said.

* * *

It was near dawn when the hoofbeats came up the draw — a single horse, walking, unhurried, which was itself an answer, because no one hunting walked their horse except someone who already knew what they'd find.

Juniper rose out of the rocks with her hands visible and her heart going like a drum in a parade.

Marta Vey dismounted before Juniper could decide whether to run.

She was sixty now, silver-cropped, built like something the desert had tried to erode and given up on. Twenty-two years she'd run the western districts before her knees quit. She'd signed off on Juniper's field certification. She'd taught her to read sign so fine you could tell which foot a fox favored. She'd stood at Juniper's oath ceremony and said, afterward, gripping her shoulder: *You'll be better than me. Don't let them make you smaller than me.*

Now she looked at Juniper across ten feet of canyon dirt, and her face did something complicated and settled into grim.

"Halloran."

"Marta."

"You look like hell."

"You should see the other guy."

"There is no other guy. There's a notice board with your face on it and a Director who's very interested in your whereabouts." Marta's eyes flicked past her, to the shadow under the overhang where Sable sat very still with a knife she wasn't hiding well enough, and to the mule, and to the long bundle lashed flat. Something in her expression tightened. "So it's true. You have it."

"Marta—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "I've been doing this job since before you could ride. I know what a sweep line looks like from both sides. I know what Corvane's orders look like too, because I read them last week and then I requested transfer to a district so remote they'd have to send me supplies by balloon." She stepped closer. Her voice dropped. "Six lines, Halloran. Six. For two thieves. I've hunted smuggler kings with less. Either he thinks you're carrying a war, or he knows you are."

Juniper's mouth was dry. "He wants the treaty. What's inside the chest. A claim on the borderlands, forty years buried."

"I figured. Old men don't spend gold like water on a thief hunt unless there's a crown at the bottom of it." Marta glanced at the sky, measuring the light. Then she did something that made Juniper's chest crack open: she unsaddled her horse, right there, and sat down on a rock like a woman taking a lunch break.

"Eat something," she said. "You both look like you've been living on adrenaline and lies."

"We have."

"Good. Means you're doing it right."

* * *

They ate hardtack and dried apricots while the sun came up over the rim and turned the canyon walls the color of old blood. Marta asked questions the way she'd always asked them — short, flat, no decoration. Where had they crossed the pan fork. Had they seen the second line on the eastern mesa. Did they know the Kettle had a new picket post, four men, rotating at dusk.

Sable answered half of them before Juniper could, and Marta watched her do it with an expression Juniper couldn't read — until she could. It was the same look Marta had given her at the oath ceremony. Recognition. The appraising kind. The kind that meant *this one is worth the trouble.*

"You trust her?" Marta asked quietly, when Sable had gone to check the mule's foot.

"With my life."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the only answer I've got."

Marta chewed on that, and on an apricot pit, and spat it into the dust. "When you took your oath, you swore to protect the frontier from all threats, foreign and domestic, and to hold the law above your own interest. You remember?"

"Every word."

"Do you remember what I told you after?"

Juniper did. *The law is a tool, Halloran, not a god. Tools get picked up by the wrong hands. Your job is knowing whose hand is holding yours.*

"The law's been picked up," Marta said now. "By a man who signs commissions with one hand and moves soldiers with the other. I've seen his survey manifests. Grain shipments that don't need grain. Remounts triple what a survey needs. He's not cataloguing ruins out here. He's staging." She looked at Juniper steadily. "I'm telling you this so you understand what you're running from isn't just a man. It's a machine, and machines don't tire, and they don't forgive, and they especially don't forgive their own."

"I know."

"Do you? Because there's a version of this where you walk into Marrow's Landing tomorrow, put the chest on his table, take your badge back, and sleep indoors for the rest of your life. I'd be lying if I said I hadn't thought about which version of you wins." She paused. "The girl I certified wouldn't have hesitated. But the girl I certified didn't have anyone waiting for her at camp."

From the mule, Sable looked up, and for once didn't pretend she hadn't been listening.

* * *

Marta stayed until midmorning, drawing the sweep lines from memory in the dirt — six strands, their rotation times, the gaps between them measured in hours. She gave them water from her own skins. She gave them a paper packet of fever powder and a tin of salt tablets that Juniper pocketed without letting herself think about why her hands shook.

At the edge of the draw, saddling up, she stopped.

"One more thing." She didn't turn around. "The notice board says deserter. Suspended badge. Unreliable." Now she turned, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely steady. "I've known you eight years, Halloran. You are the most reliable person I ever trained. So whatever that board thinks you deserted from — it wasn't the frontier. Remember that. Whatever you end up doing, whoever you end up being, you never deserted the actual thing. The actual thing is standing right in front of me, and it's still trying to keep people alive."

"Marta—"

"South fork of the Kettle has a gap at the third bend, scree chute, ugly but passable with a mule if you wrap its hooves. Take it at dusk when the picket changes. You'll have ninety minutes." She swung up into the saddle. "After that, I never saw you. Officially, I'm reporting contact with unknown parties in the southern draw, engagement declined, subject armed and hostile."

"Hostile," Juniper repeated.

"It's the kindest lie I can tell. Hostile means they'll come in force and slow. Friendly means they'll come in fast and trusting, and I can't protect you from a friendly approach." Marta gathered her reins. Her voice roughened. "You want to survive this? Be hostile, Halloran. Be the worst rumor they've ever chased. Give them a ghost that bites."

She turned the horse. Then, over her shoulder, one last thing, thrown back casual as a coin:

"And Halloran — the girl I certified is dead now. Grieve her quick and keep moving. Dead girls make excellent cover."

* * *

They watched her go until the draw swallowed her, and then they watched the empty space where she'd been a while longer.

Sable spoke first. "She lied for you."

"She bent a report."

"She committed treason." Sable's voice was quiet. "For you. A woman she trained, who broke her oath, who's carrying stolen state property through her district with a wanted criminal." She shook her head slowly. "I've spent my whole life assuming loyalty was a currency. You pay it, you get paid back. Nobody spends it for free."

"She didn't do it for free." Juniper folded Marta's map scrap into her coat, over her heart, where the badge used to sit. "She did it because she thinks the frontier matters more than the service. And she's right. I just took eight years to catch up."

They broke camp in silence, wrapping the mule's hooves in sacking as Marta had told them, checking water, checking the sky. At the mouth of the draw, the southern fork of the Kettle waited below — a brown ribbon of road between walls the color of rust, and on its rim, tiny with distance, the picket post's smoke rising straight in the windless air.

Four men. Ninety minutes. One chance.

Sable touched her arm. "Hey. Ranger."

"I'm not a ranger anymore."

"No," Sable agreed. "You're something better. Come on — dusk won't wait, and neither will the net."

Below them, faint on the wind, a whistle note passed down the ridge, hand to hand, strand to strand. The net was closing everywhere but here, for ninety minutes, on a scree chute an old woman had given them out of love and treason both.

Juniper wrapped the mule's lead twice around her fist and started down.
