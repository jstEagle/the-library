## 12. Salt Fever

The saltpan began where the map ended.

That was the first thing Juniper noticed about it — the way the survey charts in their saddlebag simply stopped at the pan's western edge, the cartographers having drawn a neat border line and a small notation: *uninhabitable; no route recommended.* Forty years of rangers had agreed with them. The Saltreach flats were bad enough, cracked white clay that threw heat back up at you like an insult, but the pan proper was something else. Sixty miles of salt crust over black mud, flat as a struck bell from horizon to horizon, and not one feature on it that could cast shade.

"We go around," Sable said, when they'd watered the horses at the last seep and stood looking at it.

"There's no around. North is Corvane's patrol road. South is the canyon country, and after the Narrows they'll have every slot and draw watched." Juniper swung back into the saddle. "We go across."

Sable looked at the pan the way most people looked at open graves. "How long?"

"Three days if we push. Four if we're honest about it." They checked the water skins — five, plus the canvas cask lashed behind Sable's saddle. Maybe eleven gallons between them and the far rim, where the maps resumed and, if Odile's directions held, a ruined Vessian observatory sat in the hills like a broken tooth. "We ride at night, hole up through the heat under what cover we can make. Horses get half rations. We get less."

"And if we run short?"

Juniper didn't answer that, and Sable didn't ask again. That was one of the things Juniper had come to respect about her — she asked a question once, filed the answer or the silence away, and moved. She'd been doing it since the canyon, since the chest, since the ambush that had cost them their horses' pack train and half their supplies and very nearly their lives. Three days out from the Narrows now, and she still flinched when a hawk crossed overhead, still checked the skyline every few minutes with those quick dark eyes. But she'd stopped checking whether *Juniper* was behind her. Somewhere in the last week, without either of them deciding it, they'd become the kind of people who rode single file because the trail was narrow, not because one of them might bolt.

They started onto the pan an hour before dusk, while the salt still glowed faintly pink, and by full dark it had turned to bone.

* * *

The first night went well, which should have warned them.

The crust held the horses' weight — mostly. Twice it crackled alarmingly and they dismounted and led the animals wide, feeling for the soft spots where brine seeped close beneath the surface. The stars came out so thick and close that Juniper found themselves navigating more by sky than compass, holding a bearing on a low star cluster Sable called the Fisherman's Net, names for constellations that no ranger instructor had ever taught. They made good distance. The cold came down hard after midnight, as it does on the pans, and they rode wrapped in their blankets with their breath smoking, and for a few hours it almost felt like freedom instead of flight.

Near dawn they found a salt hummock — a low dome of crusted earth where the mud underneath had pushed up over centuries — and camped in its lee, rigging the oilcloth and saddle blankets into a lean-to that would give them maybe three feet of shadow come midday. The horses drank a gallon each. Juniper allowed themselves four swallows and gave Sable six, and pretended not to notice that Sable did the arithmetic too and said nothing.

"You've done this before," Sable said, settling against the hummock's flank. "Crossed open ground like this."

"Twice. Once running contraband intercepts along the southern reach, once carrying dispatches when the relay stations burned." Juniper chewed a strip of dried meat without tasting it. "Both times I knew where my next water was."

"And this time?"

"This time I know where it isn't."

Sable laughed — a real laugh, brief and low, gone almost before it arrived. Then she pulled her scarf up over her face and slept, sitting upright, the way she did everything: efficiently, guarded, ready to move. Juniper took first watch and watched the sun come up over sixty miles of nothing, and thought about how strange it was that the quietest company they'd ever kept belonged to a thief.

* * *

The second day was the furnace.

No wind. The sun climbed and stuck there like a brand pressed to the sky, and the salt threw it back doubled, and by noon the air above the pan shimmered so hard that the horizon dissolved entirely — no line between white ground and white sky, just heat in every direction, and the two of them and the horses crouched under a lean-to that shaded perhaps a quarter of what it needed to. The horses stood hipshot and miserable, ears pinned. One of them, Juniper's bay, had stopped sweating, and that was a fact Juniper made themselves look at directly, because looking away from facts was how people died out here.

They didn't move until dusk. It was the right call and it cost them anyway, because the horses wouldn't drink enough — the brackish taste of the cask water, or simple exhaustion — and every hour an animal stands still in that heat is an hour it's dying slowly. When they finally saddled up, the bay stumbled twice in the first mile.

"Give him the rest of the cask," Sable said.

"That's our margin."

"Give him the rest of the cask, Juniper." Her voice wasn't sharp. It was tired, and certain. "A dead horse is eighty gallons we can't carry and a rider who walks. Spend the water where it buys the most."

It was ranger arithmetic. She'd learned it somewhere, in whatever hard school had made her, and hearing it in her voice did something odd to Juniper's chest — a small ache, like a knuckle cracking. They poured the cask into the bay's nosebag and let him drink his fill, and the mare got what was left, and the skins went nearly empty, and they rode on into the second night with a margin thin enough to see light through.

They made better distance that night. The stars were kind, the crust was firm, and twice Juniper caught themselves riding closer to Sable than the trail required, close enough that their stirrups nearly touched, and each time they eased away again without knowing why.

* * *

It was the third dawn that broke everything.

They'd planned to cross the final twenty miles in the pre-dawn cool and reach the western rim by mid-morning, before the worst heat. But the bay went lame an hour out — a stone bruise, or a crack in the hoof, impossible to tell in the dark — and walking him slowed them to a crawl, and then the mare picked up a gait that told Juniper she was failing too, and the sun rose on them still eight miles from the rim with perhaps a gallon and a half of water left in the world.

Eight miles. On foot, leading failing horses, in rising heat.

"We can make it," Sable said.

"I know," Juniper said, and did not say *you won't.*

Because Juniper had seen heat sickness take people before — green recruits on desert patrols, a smuggler once, dragged into a waystation half-dead — and it didn't take everyone equally. It took the ones who'd been running on empty for weeks, the ones whose bodies had been spending reserves they didn't have since long before the sun got its teeth in them. Sable had been shot at, starved, soaked in a flood, marched across a saltpan, and sleeping four hours a night for eleven days. Her body had been writing checks since the canyon. The pan was simply where the account came due.

She lasted four miles.

Juniper heard it before they saw it — the rhythm of her footsteps beside them going wrong, dragging, syncopated — and turned to find her standing still in the blinding white, swaying slightly, her scarf fallen around her neck and her face gray under the sunburn.

"Sable."

"I'm fine." She took a step. The step went sideways. Then her knees unhinged and Juniper caught her, barely, arms hooking under hers as she folded, and lowered her down onto the salt crust as gently as the urgency allowed.

Her skin was hot and dry. Not sunburn-hot — *furnace*-hot, heat radiating off her like off a stone at dusk. Her pulse hammered fast and shallow. Her eyes were open but tracking badly, fixing on Juniper's face and sliding off it.

"Heat stroke," Juniper said aloud, because naming it made it a problem instead of a terror. "Not exhaustion. Stroke."

"M'fine," Sable said. "Jus' need a minute."

"You don't get a minute." Juniper was already moving — dragging the horses into what little shade their bodies cast, unrolling the oilcloth, propping it on the rifle and a folded blanket into a scrap of shelter maybe four feet square. Getting Sable into it. Stripping off her own outer shirt, wetting it with water they could not afford, laying it over Sable's neck and armpits and wrists. Fanning her with a saddle blanket in slow, steady strokes.

The math was brutal and Juniper ran it anyway, because someone had to. A gallon and a third of water. Eight miles to the rim, then unknown miles beyond it to the observatory. Two horses, one lame. A patient who couldn't walk, couldn't ride unassisted, couldn't sweat — and cooling a body that can't sweat takes water, takes it constantly, takes it like a debt collector.

There was no version of the math where both of them and the horses made it comfortably. There was only the version where Juniper spent everything, immediately, on the one thing that mattered.

So that's what they did.

* * *

The first day was the longest of Juniper's life, and they had already lived through the flood and the ambush and the night their command had ordered them to betray everything they understood.

They stayed put. Moving Sable in that state would kill her faster than thirst would. Juniper rigged the shelter as best the flat white world allowed, hobbled the horses with the last of the grain to keep them nearby, and settled into the work of keeping a human being alive with a gallon of water and two hands.

It was mostly waiting and small corrections. Wet cloth, wrung from a cup of water, laid on skin. Fan. Wait. Check the pulse. Wet cloth again. Sable drifted in and out, mumbling sometimes — fragments, a name that might have been a sister's, a phrase in some border dialect Juniper half-recognized, once, clearly and terribly: *"I didn't take anything that wasn't already taken."* Juniper fanned her and did not pretend they hadn't heard, and did not mention it later either.

Around what Juniper guessed was midafternoon, the seizures started — small ones, a shuddering in the hands and jaw, the body's wiring misfiring as its temperature spiked. Every manual Juniper had memorized said the same thing: cool the core, protect the head, do not let them aspirate. There was no ice. There was no river. There was a cup, a shirt, a blanket, and a person shaking on the salt like something trying to come apart.

Juniper put their hands on either side of Sable's face and held it steady and talked to her, low and constant, nonsense mostly — the names of rivers, the layout of a waystation, the color of the sky before a flash flood — anything with rhythm, anything to anchor her to a voice while the fever tried to carry her off. And somewhere in the middle of it, in the space between one seizure and the next, Juniper understood something they had been carefully not understanding for days, maybe weeks, and the understanding landed without drama, the way a load-bearing beam settles:

*I cannot do this without you.*

Not *the mission* fails without her. Not *the chest* goes undelivered. Just — plainly, stupidly, in the middle of a saltpan with the sun trying to cook them both — *I don't want a life where you're not in it.* And there was no one to say it to, and no time, and no water to spare for tears, so Juniper said it silently, to the white glare and the ringing heat, and then went back to work.

Dusk brought the temperature down and the seizures with it. Sable slept — real sleep, deep and slack — and Juniper sat beside her through the whole of it, one hand resting lightly on her shoulder, counting breaths. The horses stood dark against the stars. The pan was absolutely silent, in a way no place on earth should be silent, and Juniper found they didn't mind it. Silence, for once, felt like company.

* * *

The second day, the water ran out.

Not dwindled — *ran out*, decisively, the last skin drained at dawn splitting between the horses and a final cupful for Sable, who woke lucid enough to argue about it and too weak to win. Her fever had broken in the night but left her hollowed out, dizzy, unable to stand without help. The horses were in worse shape than the arithmetic admitted. The bay could barely bear weight on the lame forefoot. The rim of the pan, eight miles away, glittered on the horizon like a promise neither of them believed anymore.

"We wait for dark and I carry you," Juniper said.

"You'll die doing it."

"Probably not."

"Juniper." Sable's voice was sandpaper. She gripped their wrist with a hand that had almost no strength in it and still managed, somehow, to hold on. "Listen to me. If it comes to it — you take the chest and you go. That was always the deal. The chest matters more than—"

"The chest doesn't matter at all," Juniper said, and heard their own voice come out flat and certain and true. "The chest is paper. You're the only reason any of this is happening. So shut up and save your strength, because I'm not leaving you on this pan, and I'm strong enough to be stubborn about it all day."

Sable stared at them. Something moved behind her eyes — surprise, or the beginning of an argument, or the same understanding Juniper had swallowed yesterday, arriving in her too. She didn't name it. Neither of them named it. She just closed her eyes and leaned her weight, deliberately, trustingly, against Juniper's shoulder, and let herself be cared for.

They waited out the day in the scrap of shade, mouths dry as the ground, and Juniper discovered that thirst has stages past desperation — a kind of burning clarity, a quiet. They dozed in shifts. Once, surfacing from a half-sleep, Juniper found Sable watching them with an expression they'd never seen on her: unguarded, wondering, as if Juniper were a page she hadn't expected to find in a book she thought she knew. Neither of them said anything. The silence said plenty.

At dusk, Juniper got her upright, got her arm across their shoulders, and started walking.

Eight miles. It took the whole night. Juniper carried her piggyback for stretches when her legs quit, walked her leaning on their side when pride or stubbornness rallied her, and led the horses in a slow string behind, the bay limping, the mare's head hanging low. The stars wheeled. The Fisherman's Net sank toward the horizon ahead of them, and Sable murmured bearings against Juniper's ear in a voice worn down to thread, and Juniper steered by her voice alone for the last hour, because her eyes worked better in the dark than theirs and because — though neither of them would ever say it — being steered by her felt like the least frightening thing left in the world.

The salt crust ended an hour before dawn. Real ground rose under their boots — gravel, then dirt, then the first stunted greasewood bushes, then the black shoulders of hills climbing out of the pan's edge. Juniper walked until the stars paled, walked until their legs shook, walked until the ruins showed gray against the fading east: a broken tower on the ridge above, walls tumbled, a dome split open like an eggshell. The observatory. Odile's landmark, exactly where she'd sworn it would be.

Sable slid off Juniper's back at the base of the hill, found her feet, and stood swaying, looking up at the ruin.

"There's a cistern," she rasped. "Vessian observatories all had them. Rain-catchers. If forty years hasn't cracked it—" She didn't finish. She started climbing.

They found the cistern behind the collapsed eastern wall, a round stone mouth half-choked with grit, and Juniper dropped the bucket — the survey kit's tin bucket, carried sixty miles for exactly this — and heard it strike water. Actual water. Cold, mineral, tasting of stone and old rain and survival.

Juniper drank first only because Sable shoved the cup at them and refused to take no, and then they drank together, kneeling at the cistern's edge in the first gray light, passing the cup back and forth in silence, and it was the finest water Juniper Halloran had ever tasted in their life.

Then Sable, still gray-faced, still trembling, nodded toward the tower.

"If we're here," she said, "we might as well open it."

And Juniper looked at the bronze-bound chest strapped behind the mare's saddle — the chest they had carried across a flood, an ambush, and a dead white sea — and felt the weight of what was inside settle onto their shoulders like a hand.
