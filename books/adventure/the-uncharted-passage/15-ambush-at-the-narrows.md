## 15. Ambush at the Narrows

The canyon announced itself the way a knife announces itself: by the light going out of the day.

One moment they were riding through open country, red rock and sage, Odile's mare picking her way along a dry wash. The next, the walls closed in on both sides — sheer sandstone rising sixty feet, close enough that Juniper could have touched them with a bowstave held crosswise. The slot narrowed to barely eight feet. Water-smoothed stone underfoot, polished by flash floods that came maybe twice a decade and left nothing alive behind.

Juniper reined up. Every instinct they had — every one honed across eleven years of ranger patrols, every one that had kept them breathing through drought years and border raids and the long ugly winter of the Salt Fever outbreak — stood up and screamed at once.

"What is it?" Sable asked, riding close. Her voice was low. She'd learned to read Juniper's silences the way other people read maps.

"Too quiet." Juniper turned in the saddle, scanning the rim above. "No birds. No lizards on the rocks. It's midday and this canyon should be crawling with things sunning themselves."

Odile pulled up beside them, squinting upward against the glare. "Maybe they know something we don't."

"Maybe they do."

The horses knew first. Juniper's bay threw its head and stamped; Odile's mare sidled hard against the canyon wall, ears flat. And then the sound came — not a shout, not a warning, but the flat crack of a rifle from somewhere high on the left rim, and the bullet sparked off stone two feet from Juniper's head and whined away down the slot like an angry hornet.

"DOWN!" Juniper was already moving, hauling the reins low, driving the horses forward because there was no cover behind and standing still was dying. Another shot cracked. Rock dust geysered off the wall above Odile's head. Ahead, the canyon bent sharply left, and around that bend —

Around that bend was either escape or a wall of guns, and there was no way to know which without riding into it.

"They're on the rim!" Sable shouted over the drumming hooves. "They'll have the whole length of the narrows covered!"

"Then we don't stop for the whole length!" Juniper put their heels to the bay.

* * *

Sable had been in ambushes before. She'd sprung enough of them herself, back when she ran with the Saltreach smugglers, before she'd learned that the only person you could trust past arm's length was yourself. She knew the arithmetic: shooters on the rim, quarry in the slot, gravity and geometry on the side of the men above. You didn't win that fight. You survived it, if you were lucky, and luck was a currency she'd never had much of.

But she knew this canyon.

She'd spent three days in it once, fourteen years old and starving, hiding from a toll-crew that wanted payment her family couldn't make. Three days learning every bend, every choke point, every place where the floodwaters had undercut the walls and left hollows big enough to hide a body. Three days learning that the Narrows didn't run straight — that a quarter mile in, the slot split around a fallen slab of rock the size of a chapel, and the right-hand channel pinched down to something a man could squeeze through but a horse could not.

"Hold left at the split!" she screamed at Juniper. "There's a boulder field — the passage forks!"

Juniper didn't ask questions. That was the thing about them, Sable thought, ducking as another rifle round chewed the stone overhead. They trusted her in the places where trust cost most. The canyon bent, the walls leaned in until she could see individual grains of sandstone, and then the split opened up ahead — the great slab tumbled from the rim some ancient flood, its face dark with desert varnish, two channels diverging around it like water parting around a ship's prow.

"The right! Take the right!"

"The right's too narrow—"

"It's narrow for *them*, not for us! Horses can't follow but neither can their shots reach — the walls close up! GO!"

Juniper hauled the bay right. For one horrible moment Sable thought the horse would balk — the gap was barely wider than the animal's shoulders, walls scraping on both sides — but the bay was a ranger's horse, trained for bad country, and it squeezed through with Juniper's knee cracking against stone. Odile followed. Sable went last, pressing herself flat as another shot rang out and struck the slab's face in a burst of orange sparks.

And then the noise changed. The rifles' cracks went flat and strange, echoing off stone instead of flying free, and then muffled entirely as the walls swallowed them. The shooting stopped.

For ten heartbeats there was only hoof-falls and breathing and the scrape of tack on rock.

Then the canyon opened again — a slot within the slot, a hidden corridor no survey map had ever marked — and Sable led them out into a chamber where the ceiling soared fifty feet overhead and gray light fell through a crack in the stone like light through a keyhole.

They stopped. The horses blew and trembled. Somewhere far behind, faint as memory, voices shouted on the rim.

"We can't stay," Juniper said. Not a question. A fact.

"No," Sable agreed. "But now we choose where we go next, instead of them choosing for us." She swung down, legs shaking harder than she wanted to admit, and pressed her palm flat against the cool stone. "I know a way out of here that puts us behind them. Above the rim, on the mesa. But it costs us."

"Costs us what?"

She looked at the horses. Juniper looked too. And nobody said anything for a long moment, because all three of them understood what the canyon was asking.

* * *

Juniper had buried animals before. Rangers always did — horses died of snakebite, of bad water, of the stupid accidents that bad country dealt out. You dug the hole, you said whatever words your tradition gave you, and you moved on. It never got easy. It was never supposed to get easy. The oath said a ranger's horse carried them through everything, and the unspoken half of the oath said you owed it everything back.

This was different. This wasn't death. This was leaving them.

The chamber had a second exit — a scramble up broken stone to a ledge, and from the ledge a route Sable swore led to the mesa top. Horses couldn't make it. Neither could half the supplies. So they made camp in the mouth of the hidden corridor, cold camp, no fire, and stripped the packs down to what three people could carry: water, the chest, ammunition, the medical kit, four days of food stretched thin.

Juniper did the math twice and hated it both times. Half their supplies, gone. Their mobility, gone. From here on, they were walkers in a country built to kill walkers.

"You could send them loose," Odile offered quietly. She was crouched by the bay, hand on its neck. "Cut them free. Maybe they find their way back to the flats."

"And lead Corvane's trackers straight to us by following the trail they leave? Or get picked up by his men and remounted?" Juniper shook their head. "No. We hobble them in the deep channel, off the main line. If Corvane's party comes through the fork, they'll find them or they won't. Either way it buys us time."

It was a lie of kindness and they all knew it. The odds those horses ever saw the flats again were poor. Juniper hobbled them anyway, rubbed the bay's forehead one last time, felt the animal's breath warm against their palm, and walked away without looking back, because looking back was a luxury the living couldn't afford.

Sable fell into step beside them on the climb. "You okay?"

"No."

"Good. Lying about it is worse."

They climbed.

* * *

The route out was Sable's alone, and she felt the weight of that with every handhold. If she was wrong, they died on a ledge. If she was right, they came out above Corvane's gun line, behind his position, with the whole mesa between him and the valley beyond.

Fourteen-year-old her had found this route by accident, fleeing the toll-crew, and had sworn never to tell a soul. Now she was handing it to two people she'd known less than a season. The old Sable — the one who'd survived the Saltreach by trusting no one — kept waiting for the fear to come. It didn't. What came instead was something stranger: the certainty that if she fell, these two would try to catch her. And that she'd catch them. That was new. That was terrifying in an entirely different way.

Halfway up the chimney of broken stone, the rock shifted under her boot and she dropped, sliding, and Juniper's hand shot out and locked around her wrist and held — held with the whole body behind it, braced against the wall, until she got her feet back under her.

Neither of them said anything about it. There wasn't time, and also there was too much time — the kind of moment that would keep, that would sit in the chest like a coal and burn slowly for days.

They came out on the mesa top at dusk.

Below them, in the mouth of the canyon they'd escaped, torchlight bloomed. Corvane's men, entering the narrows in force. Sable counted the fires: eight, nine, twelve. Too many. And among them, moving with the unhurried authority of a man inspecting his own property, a figure in a long coat who could only be the Director himself.

"He brought the whole survey party," Odile breathed. "That's not a pursuit. That's an occupation."

"He thinks he has us trapped in the slot," Juniper murmured. "By morning he'll know better. Then he'll read our trail backward and figure out the fork." They settled lower behind a shelf of rock, voice dropping. "Which means tonight is ours. He's looking down into a hole. We're up here, behind him, with the whole night to move."

Sable lay on her stomach beside them, watching the fires below, feeling the exhaustion of the climb settle into her bones — and underneath the exhaustion, something bright and dangerous. Fear, yes. Her hands hadn't fully stopped shaking since the first rifle shot. But threaded through the fear, inseparable from it, was the fierce joy of having slipped the trap. Of having turned an ambush inside out.

"We lost the horses," she said quietly. "Half our food. We're three days' walk from Marrow's Landing territory, Corvane's got a dozen guns, and my legs feel like wet rope."

"I know."

"So why am I smiling?"

Juniper glanced at her. In the last gray light their face was unreadable as always — but their eyes weren't. Their eyes had the same look she felt in her own chest. "Because we're still moving," Juniper said. "And he isn't."

Below, a torch flared. Above, the first stars came out over the mesa, indifferent and vast.

Then Sable went still. "Juniper. Look at the eastern fire."

Juniper looked. Among the survey camp's lights, one signal lamp began to blink — a pattern, repeated, patient. Ranger code. Juniper knew it in their bones; they'd tapped it a thousand times across a thousand dark camps. Their blood went cold as they translated.

**THREE FUGITIVES. CANYON MOUTH. HOLD AND SEARCH AT FIRST LIGHT. REINFORCEMENTS EN ROUTE FROM GARRISON SEVEN.**

Reinforcements. Not Corvane's hired survey guards — rangers. Juniper's own. Coming down the eastern road by dawn.

Sable watched Juniper's face change and understood before a word was spoken. "How many?"

"At garrison strength?" Juniper's voice was flat, careful, the voice of someone holding a door shut from the inside. "Twenty riders. Maybe more."

Odile exhaled slowly. "Then we don't take the eastern road."

"No," Juniper said, and reached for the map in their pack, spreading it against the stone as the campfires burned below. "We take the one place twenty rangers won't follow us. The place I've spent my whole career telling people never to go."

Sable looked at the map, at the smudged ink marking the dead riverbed and the cave country beyond it, and felt the fear and the exhilaration fuse into something new.

"The Uncharted Passage," she said.

Juniper nodded once. "We go underground."
