## 16. What Duty Costs

The horses were gone. Half the supplies with them, and Sable's left arm bound in a strip of shirt that had been clean that morning.

They made camp in a hollow above the Narrows, where the canyon walls folded together like a cupped hand and the wind couldn't reach them. Juniper built a small fire — small enough to hide, large enough to matter — and set water boiling over it while Sable dozed against a rock, feverish but stable, her breathing finally evening out.

Juniper should have slept too. They'd been awake for thirty hours, and their body knew it, every muscle reporting in like a ledger of debts. But sleep wouldn't come. It hadn't really come since the ambush, since the moment a crossbow bolt had taken Sable's horse out from under her and Juniper had turned back — turned *back*, into the killing ground, because the alternative was watching her die on the salt.

That was the thing they couldn't stop chewing on. Not the fight. The turning back.

The fire popped. Sable opened one eye.

"You're staring at me like I owe you money."

"You almost died."

"People say that like it's rare." She shifted, wincing, and propped herself up enough to see over the flames. "It's not. Out here it's practically a hobby."

Juniper didn't laugh. They fed another branch to the fire and watched it catch, and heard themselves say, before they could decide not to: "I had a family once. Before the rangers."

Sable went still. Not dramatically — she was too good for dramatic — but Juniper had spent three weeks learning to read her, and stillness was what she did when something landed.

"Halloran," she said. "Border name."

"Border family." Juniper kept their eyes on the fire. "Little farmstead up past Coldwater Ford, back when there was a Coldwater Ford. My father grew sorghum and pretended he wasn't bored. My mother ran the ferry crossing. Two brothers. One sister. I was the youngest, which meant I was mostly outside, mostly in trouble, mostly happy about both."

"And then?"

"You know the history better than most people alive now. You stole a chest full of it." Juniper turned the stick in the coals. "Forty years ago Vessia fell. And the treaty — the real one, the one in your chest — didn't just give whoever held it a claim to the borderlands. It drew lines. Lines through farms. Through towns. Through families who'd lived on both sides of a river for six generations and never once thought about which nation they belonged to, because the river didn't care."

Sable said nothing. She had the particular silence of someone who already knows how a story ends and is letting you get there yourself.

"The fighting started two years later. Not a war — nobody ever called it a war, because wars have names and treaties and people who admit they happened. This was just... raids. Reprisals. Militias on both sides of a line somebody else had drawn, burning each other's granaries, and then each other's houses, and then each other." Juniper's voice came out flat, which was the only way it could come out; they'd practiced this flatness for twenty years without knowing what they were practicing for. "Coldwater Ford was on the line. One night in autumn, riders came through. Doesn't matter whose. That's the part I used to rage about — that it doesn't even matter whose. By morning it was my mother, my father, my sister. My brothers had been at market. I'd been sleeping in the hay barn because I'd fought with my father over a girl he thought was wrong for me."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, a night bird called once and gave up.

"I was fourteen," Juniper said. "I stood in the yard and looked at what was left, and I made myself a promise. Not revenge — I want to be clear about that, because I've met men who made that promise and none of them are worth knowing. I promised *order*. Somebody would draw the lines and somebody would hold them and nobody would ever again come through a ford at night and unmake a family because a piece of paper said the ground under their feet belonged to someone else. That's why I joined the rangers. That's why I was good at it. Every thief I caught, every smuggler, every claim-jumper — I told myself I was keeping the lines clean. Keeping the night crossings honest."

"That's why you chased me," Sable said quietly.

"That's why I chased you." Juniper finally looked at her. "Three weeks across the Saltreach, through flood country, half-starved, and I never once asked myself if the chest mattered. Only that it was stolen, and stolen things go back where they belong, and if I caught you I'd have done my job right for one more day. You left footprints in the salt flats, you know. First time in fifteen years anyone's been sloppy enough to leave footprints for me. I think—" They stopped. Started again, because stopping was cowardice and they'd spent enough of this life being brave about the wrong things. "I think I needed the chase more than I needed you caught. Because as long as I was chasing, I was still the person with the clean lines. Still the ranger. Still the boy from the hay barn who'd found an answer."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting in a hole in the ground with a thief, hiding from my own command, carrying a treaty that started the thing that killed my family." Juniper laughed, short and joyless. "And the worst part — the genuinely obscene part — is that I read those letters in the map room and felt *relief*. Corvane's family sold Vessia to its enemies and wrote themselves into the settlement. All these years I thought the lines were drawn by fate, by geography, by the great stupid machinery of nations. No. A man named Aldous Corvane sat at a table and moved a river sixty yards east because it made his holdings more defensible. Coldwater Ford died because of a *survey note*."

Sable reached over and took the stick out of Juniper's hands. Set it down. Her fingers stayed on theirs — light, calloused, steady.

"My turn," she said.

"You don't have to—"

"You gave me the ugly version. You don't get to skip mine." She settled back against the rock, arm cradled, face half in shadow. "You know I'm from the borderlands. You guessed it in the cistern, when I knew the ghost-town wells. What you don't know is that my mother was Vessian. Not descended — *was*. Born in Vessia two years before the fall. She was a refugee at four years old, which means she spent her whole life being from a country that didn't exist. People would ask where she was from and she'd say a name and they'd look at her blankly, or worse, they'd look at her like she'd said something dirty. So she stopped saying it. She raised me speaking the border dialect, no Vessian, no songs, nothing. She buried it so deep I didn't find out until I was sixteen, going through her things after she died, and found a bundle of letters wrapped in oilcloth under a floorboard."

"What kind of letters?"

"Letters from her grandmother. Written during the fall. The last year." Sable's voice didn't change much — that was the thing about her, everything cost her and nothing showed unless you watched for it — but her hand tightened slightly around Juniper's. "My mother carried a dead nation in a floorboard for forty years and never once told her own daughter it existed. That's what Vessia did to people. It didn't just fall. It taught everyone who loved it to be ashamed of the love."

"So the chest—"

"The chest isn't the first thing I ever stole." She smiled, thin. "First thing I ever stole was that bundle of letters. From my own house. From my own dead mother's floor. Because reading them felt like theft — like I was taking something she'd decided I shouldn't have. I sat in her kitchen all night with candles burning down and read a language I didn't speak, sounding out words like a child, and by dawn I was fluent in grief in two languages." She exhaled. "Everything after that followed. The smuggling, the stealing, the whole disreputable career. I got good at finding things people buried. Because somebody had to dig them up. Somebody has to, or the buried things rot in the dark and poison the groundwater, and everyone downstream drinks it without knowing why they're sick."

Juniper was quiet a long moment. "Your mother. Was she—"

"On the borderlands when the reprisals started? Yes." Sable looked into the fire. "She survived it. That was its own kind of wound. She used to say the ones who died got to be a tragedy, and the ones who lived got to be a cautionary tale, and she hadn't asked to be either." A pause. "So when I tell you the treaty is a landmine, Halloran, I'm not being clever. I'm telling you what my mother knew in her bones and never let herself say out loud: some inheritances aren't gifts. They're debts other people signed for you."

The fire burned down. Neither of them fed it.

"I keep doing the arithmetic," Juniper said at last. "Every way I run it, the answer comes out the same. If we burn that treaty, nothing comes back. Coldwater Ford stays dead. Your mother stays a woman from nowhere. Odile stays a ferrywoman. Forty years of dust stays dust." They rubbed a hand over their face. "And I still want to burn it. Not because it fixes anything. Because Corvane wants it, and everything Corvane wants is a line drawn through somebody's life, and I have spent twenty years enforcing lines and I am *done*. Do you understand? I'm done. Whatever I do next, it won't be holding lines for men who draw them from horseback."

"That's not duty talking anymore."

"No. Duty's what I buried my family under. Twenty years of service, and I told myself the grave was a foundation." Juniper shook their head slowly. "Turns out it was just a grave."

Sable was silent for a moment. Then she said, "You know what I figured out, somewhere around week two of you hunting me? Watching you work. You track like a man reading a letter from home. Careful. Hungry. Like the trail might say something you'd been waiting to hear." She tilted her head. "I thought you were obsessed with catching me. Now I know you were obsessed with catching *yourself*. And I'll tell you something for free, ranger: you did. Back at the river, when you pulled me out of the flood instead of taking the chest and running. That was the day. Everything since has just been paperwork."

Juniper huffed — almost a laugh. "You're insufferable."

"I'm concussed and insufferable. There's a difference. The first one's temporary."

But her eyes stayed on Juniper's, warm in the firelight, and neither of them looked away, and the silence that settled between them wasn't the old silence — not the one Juniper had spent a lifetime filling with duty. It was the other kind. The kind two people can sit inside without either of them drowning.

Juniper lay back against the cold stone and looked up at the slice of stars between the canyon walls, and for the first time since Coldwater Ford, the silence in their chest had company in it.

"We should sleep," they said. "Tomorrow we cut north along the dry wash. Two days' hard riding on foot, and then—"

They stopped.

Because the sound had come again, faint, from far down the canyon: hoofbeats. Many of them. Moving at a walk, deliberate, unhurried — the pace of men who weren't chasing anything because they already knew where their quarry would be.

Sable heard it too. She was on her feet despite the arm, hand on her knife, and when she glanced at Juniper her expression had gone sharp and bright as broken glass.

"Corvane?"

"Who else walks like he owns the ground?" Juniper killed the fire with a boot heel and pulled her down behind the rocks, heart hammering — but not, they noticed, with fear. With something colder and cleaner. "He's not hunting us. He's herding us. North is the old capital ruins, and he knows it, and he's leaving us exactly one road."

"Then he wants to talk."

"He wants to offer." Juniper watched the distant torchlight flicker along the canyon rim, and understood, with terrible clarity, that tomorrow they were going to be offered everything they'd ever lost — and that the offer would be the hardest knife anyone had ever put in their hands.
