## 19. Daylight

The dark stopped being dark all at once.

One moment Odile was crawling on her elbows through a passage so narrow the stone pressed against her spine like a hand, and the next the ceiling opened, and there was light — real light, honest light, the gray-gold of early morning pouring through a crack in the world. She lay in the dirt of the cave mouth and breathed until her lungs forgave her.

Three days. Three days underground with no sky, no stars, no way to know if the passage would end in a wall of stone or a drop into nothing. Three days of Sable's candle stubs burning down to brass, of Juniper counting their water in whispers, of Odile learning that she was the kind of person who could keep moving when moving was all that was left.

"Is it morning?" Sable's voice came from the black behind her, hoarse with dust.

"It's morning." Odile's own voice sounded strange to her — cracked, thin, but steady. "Or it's the end of the world. Hard to say which."

Juniper emerged last, hauling the chest on a rope sling across their back, and stood blinking in the light like something born out of the earth. Which, Odile supposed, was close to true. The ranger's face was gray with cave dust, their lips split, their eyes red-rimmed and half-blind. They looked terrible.

They looked, Odile thought, exactly like what they were: the only three people alive who had come through the Uncharted Passage.

"Where are we?" Sable asked, crawling out into the light and shading her eyes.

Juniper turned a slow circle, reading the land the way other people read letters. "East side of the Verge," they said at last. "That's the Marrow valley. And that—" They stopped.

Odile followed their gaze down the slope, and her heart, which had spent three days learning to be small and quiet, began to hammer.

Below them, strung along the river road like beads on a wire, moved a column of riders. Survey colors. Corvane's survey colors, the blue-and-silver pennants snapping in the morning wind, wagons and outriders and at least sixty armed men, all of them marching — there was no other word for it — marching toward Marrow's Landing.

Toward the conclave.

"They came around," Juniper said flatly. "Through the pass. While we were under the mountain."

"Can they see us?" Sable had already dropped flat, and Odile followed her down into the scrub, her knees barking against stone.

"No." Juniper crouched, motionless. "We're behind them. Above them. They're looking ahead, not back. Nobody comes out of the Verge from this side. That's the whole point of the Verge."

"Then we have something they don't," Odile said.

Juniper looked at her.

"We have the passage," she said. "We have the chest. And we have the one thing Corvane can't buy, can't forge, and can't kill his way past." She heard her own voice harden, and recognized it — her father's voice, her grandmother's voice, the voice of every Vessane who had ever stood in a room full of people who wanted something from them. "We have daylight, and they think they've already won."

* * *

They came down off the slope through the rocks, wide around the column's flank, and Odile's legs shook the whole way — three days of crawling will do that, will hollow you out and leave you running on will alone — but she did not slow down, and neither did the others. Sable moved like water finding the low places. Juniper moved like a man walking to his own trial. Odile moved like a woman with a name she hadn't used in forty years, and the strangeness of that carried her.

Marrow's Landing came up out of the river haze by midmorning: the ferry docks, the low warehouses, the long hall of the old customs house where the delegates had been gathering for two days. Odile had been to the town a hundred times as a girl, before the name Vessane became something people said quietly and then stopped saying at all. It looked smaller now. It looked, she thought, like every place she had ever tried to hide.

The column would reach the town within the hour. Corvane would ride in with his survey colors and his official seals and his soft, reasonable voice, and the delegates — nine of them, from nine border towns and settlements, men and women who had spent their whole lives in the gray country between nations — would listen to him. Why wouldn't they? He was the Director of the Border Survey. He had papers. He had soldiers. He had the weight of a government that had spent forty years pretending Vessia had never existed.

And what did she have? A burned ferry. A false name. Two outlaws at her back and a chest full of paper that would either save the borderlands or start a war over them.

"Odile." Juniper had stopped at the edge of the last stand of cottonwoods above the customs house. "There's a back way. Through the ferry yard. Corvane will post men at the front doors the moment he arrives — he'll want the delegates contained before he makes his case. But he won't think about the river side. Nobody ever thinks about the river side."

"Because the river side belongs to me," Odile said. "It always has."

Sable grinned — a thin, exhausted, feral grin, dust-caked and beautiful. "Then let's go home through the front door nobody's watching."

* * *

The ferry yard smelled of pitch and old wood and the river, and Odile's chest tightened when she saw the blackened pilings where her father's boat had burned. She did not let herself look long. Grief was a luxury with a column of soldiers twenty minutes out.

The river door of the customs house was locked, but locks had never been a problem for Sable, and in less than a minute they were inside, in the dim corridor that smelled of lamp oil and old paper, hearing — for the first time — the sound of the conclave.

Voices. Many voices, echoing off the high ceiling of the great hall. The delegates had gathered early, and they were arguing. Odile caught fragments through the door: *survey rights, water claims, the old toll roads, who speaks for the border* — the same arguments her father had died inside of, dressed in new words.

"They'll seat Corvane first," Juniper murmured. "He'll have credentials. He'll have a formal introduction from the survey office. Protocol will eat us alive if we walk in cold."

"Then we don't walk in cold." Odile straightened. Her back ached. Her hands were still gray with cave dust. She had not slept in — she honestly could not remember. None of that mattered. What mattered was the door, and what she was about to do behind it. "I'm Odile Vessane," she said, and the name felt like taking off a coat she'd worn so long she'd forgotten her own shape underneath. "This is my river, my town's hall, and my family's claim those delegates are arguing about without me. I'm not walking in cold. I'm walking in *last*, which is where the person with the strongest hand always sits."

Sable looked at Juniper. Juniper looked at Sable. Something passed between them — Odile saw it, that silent arithmetic of two people who had learned each other's minds in the dark — and then Juniper nodded once.

"Corvane will have guards," they said. "When we go in, I stand between him and the chest. Sable, you watch the doors. Odile—" A pause. "When you speak, don't argue with him. Don't defend. Read. Let the paper do the killing."

"And if it doesn't?"

Juniper's jaw tightened. "Then it's a fight, and we lose it, and at least we lose it in front of witnesses. Corvane's whole method is doing terrible things where no one can see. Dragging it into daylight is the only weapon we have that he can't match."

Through the wall, a bell rang. A voice called the conclave to order. And beneath it, faint but growing, the drum of hooves on the river road.

Corvane had arrived.

* * *

They waited in the corridor while the Director of the Border Survey made his entrance, and Odile listened to it happen — the doors, the boots, the murmur of the delegates rising, the smooth warm baritone she knew too well saying, *Distinguished delegates, friends of the border, thank you for receiving me.* He was good. He had always been good. He had signed her father's trade papers once, years ago, with this same voice, and her father had said, *There's a man who understands that borders are just promises written down.* Her father had been wrong about that, and wrong about so much, and the wrongness had killed him.

Sable's hand found Odile's shoulder. A squeeze. *Now or never.*

Odile pushed open the door.

The great hall of the customs house had been built for the river trade in Vessia's last decade, and it still bore the old marks: the carved lintel, the faded crest over the speaker's chair, the long tables where nine delegates sat behind nine name-plates. Corvane stood at the center of the floor in his survey blues, silver at his collar, hands folded, the picture of patient authority. He was mid-sentence. He saw her, and Odile watched — with a satisfaction she would carry to her grave — the sentence die in his mouth.

For one heartbeat, the hall was silent.

Then a woman at the far table — Delegate Marsh, gray-haired, sharp-eyed, the closest thing the border had to a judge — said, "Name yourself."

"Odile Vessane." Her voice carried better than she expected. Maybe the hall helped. Maybe the name did. She saw delegates sit up straighter, saw an old man's mouth form the word *Vessane* like a prayer or a curse. "Daughter of Aldric Vessane. Last heir of the nation you are all, whether you admit it or not, still arguing about."

"Madam Director," Corvane said smoothly, recovering, his voice oiled and regretful, "this woman is a harbor thief operating under a false name, in the company of a deserter and a confessed felon. I must ask the delegates to—"

"Ask them nothing." Odile walked forward, and Sable and Juniper flanked her, and between Juniper's hands they set the bronze-bound chest down on the floor of the hall with a sound like a struck bell. Every head turned toward it. "You want to talk about false names, Director Corvane? Let's talk about names. Let's talk about the name Vessia, and who destroyed it, and why, and what your family got out of it. I have it all here. In writing. In your own ancestors' hands."

Corvane had gone very still. It was the stillness of a man watching a fire reach the dry field between himself and everything he owned. "Those documents are stolen government property—"

"They were never government property." Odile turned to the delegates, and she made herself look at each of them, one by one, the way her father had taught her: *a room is won one face at a time.* "They were Vessia's. They are mine. And before this conclave votes on any claim — his, mine, anyone's — you will hear every word inside this chest. All of it. Even the parts that shame the dead. Even the parts that will make some of you wish you'd never asked."

Delegate Marsh leaned forward. "And if we refuse to hear it?"

"Then you'll vote on Corvane's claim with your eyes shut," Odile said, "and you'll deserve what you get."

Behind her, at the doors, she heard the first of Corvane's guards arrive — boots, the jingle of gear, a hand testing the latch. Juniper moved without seeming to move, and stood in front of the doors with their back to them, arms crossed, ranger-still, and the guards stopped.

Corvane smiled. It was his generous smile, his reasonable smile, the one that had bought him forty years of obedience. "Delegates," he said, "I understand the theater. I do. It's compelling. But consider what's being asked of you: to reopen a dead nation's claims on the strength of documents of unknown provenance, produced by a fugitive, at the urging of a woman who burned a ferry two days ago rather than submit to a lawful survey. Is that the border you want? One ruled by whoever shouts loudest and burns fastest?"

"It's the border you're afraid of," Odile said. "Which tells me it's the border we should want."

Laughter — small, startled, quickly suppressed, but real. Odile felt it land, felt the room tilt a degree in her direction, and knew it was not enough. Not nearly enough. Corvane had sixty soldiers outside and forty years of credibility inside. She had a chest no one had opened and a name half the room thought was extinct.

Delegate Marsh raised her hand. "The conclave will hear the documents," she said. "All of them. In full. This afternoon." She looked at Odile, and her eyes were not kind; they were appraising, weighing, a border woman's eyes. "And I warn you, Vessane. If what's in that chest names you a claimant to a throne, we will treat you as a claimant. With everything that means. There is no walking away after this. Not for you. Not for anyone in this hall."

Odile looked down at the chest. Three days under the mountain. A burned boat. A lifetime under a false name. Sable's hand brushed hers, brief and steady. Juniper had not moved from the doors.

"I know," Odile said.

And then, through the high windows of the customs house, she saw them — the first of Corvane's outriders dismounting in the square, and behind them, more riders, more than she'd counted on the slope, fanning out through the town with the unhurried confidence of men securing ground they'd already been told was theirs. Corvane hadn't come to argue. He'd come to occupy. The conclave was, for him, a formality to be survived; the soldiers were the real argument, and they were already in position.

He had lost the room's first hour and he did not care, because he had never intended to leave the outcome to the room at all.

Odile caught Juniper's eye across the hall. The ranger had seen it too. Their jaw set, and their hand drifted — casually, terribly — toward the knife at their belt, and Odile understood that the daylight they'd fought three days underground to reach was about to become the most dangerous place any of them had ever stood.

The afternoon session would begin in four hours.

She intended to be alive to speak at it.
