## 1. The Debt in the Dirt

The dead don't mind what name you give them. That's the first thing I learned about salvage work, and the last comfort it ever offered me.

I'd been "Mara Voss" for eleven months at the Kessel site, long enough that the foreman had stopped squinting at my paperwork and started squinting at my trowel instead, which was the better trade. Mara Voss had no father. Mara Voss had a laminated certification from a correspondence course in Rotterdam that cost forty euros to fabricate and had gotten me onto four crews before anyone thought to check. Mara Voss was quiet, competent, and—this was the important part—forgettable. I had built her the way you build a flood wall: carefully, with an eye toward where the water always comes.

The water came on a Tuesday, in the form of a man in linen too clean for a dig site.

I noticed him from the bottom of Trench Four, where I was prying a bronze fitting out of river clay that had held it for two centuries. The Kessel site was a drowned trading post, half a village swallowed when the river changed its mind sometime in the 1700s, and we were pulling up its bones for a developer who wanted to put a marina over its corpse. Not glamorous archaeology. Not *archaeology* at all, if you asked anyone with tenure. But it paid, and it paid under a name that didn't weigh anything, and I had learned to love work that nobody would ever write about.

The man in linen stood at the trench edge with his hands clasped behind his back like he was inspecting troops. He wasn't wearing boots. Nobody comes to a working salvage dig without boots unless they came to look at something other than mud.

"Voss," the foreman called down, and there was a strange note in his voice, apologetic or afraid. "Company visitor. Says he wants a word."

I kept my eyes on the bronze fitting. My heart did something stupid and arrhythmic, the way it does when your body knows a thing before your mind consents to know it. I wiped my hands on my thighs, which only spread the clay around, and climbed the ladder rung by rung, giving myself twelve rungs' worth of time to decide how this would go.

He was younger than I expected. Collector's agents in my imagination were all gray suits and gray faces, men who smelled of filing cabinets. This one had a sunburned nose and a friendly crease between his eyebrows, like a camp counselor. He held a photograph in one hand, face down against his thigh.

"Mara Voss," he said warmly.

"That's me."

"Ivo Zhou," he said, just as warmly.

The trench might as well have opened under me. Twelve rungs of climbing and I was still falling.

"You've got the wrong person," I said. My voice came out level. Eleven months of practice will do that; you get very good at saying things that aren't true in a voice that sounds like it has never told a lie in its life.

"Probably," he agreed. He turned the photograph over.

It was my father. Of course it was my father. It's always my father. He stood on the deck of a research vessel with his arm flung out toward some coastline, mid-lecture even in a photograph, mouth open on some word I couldn't hear but could have recited anyway. *Context is everything. A thing torn from its context is just loot.* He'd said that to me when I was nine, standing in front of a museum case, and then he'd spent thirty years selling exactly that kind of loot to men who paid him enough not to care about the difference.

"The hairline is different," the agent said, tapping the photo with one manicured finger. "And she's got her mother's jaw, we think—the mother's harder to trace. But the eyes." He looked up at me, and his friendliness didn't waver, which was somehow worse than menace. Menace you can fight. Friendliness means they already think they've won. "The eyes are his."

"My eyes are mine," I said.

"They're on loan," he said, "against a considerable balance."

Around us the dig went on. The foreman had developed a sudden fascination with the sieving station. Two crew members were very interested in a bucket. The river moved past us, brown and patient, carrying whatever it carried.

"How much?" I asked. Because that's the question, isn't it? You learn to ask it fast so you can start calculating whether you can pay it, or outrun it, or whether the number is big enough that running is the only honest option left.

"It's not really a number situation anymore, Ms. Zhou." He said the name gently, like setting down something fragile. "It's been eleven years. Interest compounds. Assets have been... liquidated by the primary debtor without remittance. The family of Dr. Edmund Zhou is currently valued as collateral rather than as clients, if you follow me."

"I'm not his family."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You're Mara Voss, of Rotterdam, who has worked four contract digs under three slightly different surnames—we noticed the middle initial changes—and who sends money every month to a debt resolution office in a port city six hundred kilometers downstream. Cash. No return address." He smiled. "We didn't find you through the names. We found you through the payments. People who want to be invisible should stop being dutiful."

Eleven years. Eleven years of payments into an account I never explained to anyone, not to landlords, not to lovers, not to the one woman in Lisbon who'd almost gotten close enough to ask. Eleven years of paying down a debt I hadn't signed for, because when I was nineteen my father had used my name as guarantor on a line of credit against artifacts he'd already secretly sold, and by the time I found out, the collectors had my signature and I had nothing but the signature's consequences.

"He's alive, then," I said. "You'd know. If he were dead, you'd have written off the balance."

Something flickered across the agent's face—not quite a tell, but the shadow of one. "Dr. Zhou's status is currently classified as *irregular*."

Irregular. I almost laughed. That was my father all over: even his disappearance would be filed under a bureaucratic euphemism. Irregular. Like a heartbeat. Like a shipment seized at customs.

"Here's what happens now," the agent said. He tucked the photograph away inside his linen jacket, unhurried, a man with nowhere to be because everything was already coming to him. "Nothing today. I'm not authorized to detain anyone; I'm an appraiser, not a bailiff. But your employment here concludes at the end of the week—the company received a call this morning regarding irregularities in Ms. Voss's credentials. Rotterdam is looking into them as we speak. And after that—" he shrugged, spreading his hands to indicate the whole wide world and its lack of places left to hide, "—the debt follows bloodlines, Ms. Zhou. It's very old-fashioned that way. It doesn't care what you call yourself. It cares whose face you wear."

"And if I pay?"

"Then you're a client again, and clients are protected." He said it kindly. That was the cruelest part—he genuinely sounded kind, like a doctor delivering a diagnosis he'd delivered a thousand times. "But the balance is well beyond what a salvage diver earns. Unless, of course, you know where certain assets went. Certain assets that left an expedition upriver eight months ago and haven't been inventoried since."

The river made a sound. Or maybe I did. Upriver. Eight months. Three missed radio check-ins—I wouldn't learn that until later, until the university's forwarded message found me like a letter bomb—but standing in the clay at the edge of Trench Four, I felt the shape of it coming, the way animals feel weather.

"I don't know anything about my father's business," I said.

"No," he said. "But you know his habits. And habits lead somewhere." He stepped back from the trench edge, brushed a fleck of clay from his sleeve, and gave me a small bow, courteous as a knife going back in its drawer. "End of the week, Ms. Voss. Do think about which name you'd like to owe money under. It makes our records so much tidier."

He walked off across the site, stepping carefully between the spoil heaps, and I watched him go and understood that I had maybe ninety-six hours of Mara Voss left, and that Mara Voss had never really been a wall, just a door he'd been willing to wait outside.

* * *

That night I sat in my rented room above the chandlery with everything I owned laid out on the bed, which took less space than you'd think. One duffel bag's worth of a life. Two shirts that qualified as professional, four that didn't. A waterproof case of tools. A fake certificate I now considered a small piece of found art. And the compass.

My father gave it to me when I was ten. Brass, heavy for its size, the needle housing fogged with a crack across the glass. He'd set it in my palm on the deck of a boat much like any other boat and said, *A compass doesn't tell you where to go. It tells you where you are. Everything else is navigation.* Even at ten I'd understood this was less a gift than a philosophy, and philosophies from that man always came with invoices attached.

I should have thrown it in the river years ago. Instead I'd carried it through eleven years and four names, and I sat there turning it over in my hands while the chandlery below closed up and the harbor lights came on, and I tried to feel the thing I was supposed to feel, which was hatred, clean and simple, the kind you can build a life on.

What I felt instead was tired. Bone-tired, in a way sleep had never once touched. Eleven years of being a rumor of a person. Eleven years of flinching at men in clean linen. Eleven years of sending cash into an account for a debt that grew faster than I could dig, a hole I was bailing with a cup while the collectors owned the ocean. And underneath the tiredness, worse than the tiredness, a small traitorous voice asking the question I'd sworn never to ask:

*Is he all right?*

Because that was the trap of bloodlines, wasn't it. The agent was right—it follows bloodlines. But so does the worrying. So does the ten-year-old girl holding a compass on a boat deck, waiting for her father to notice her instead of the coastline.

I put the compass in my jacket pocket, where it sat against my ribs like a second heartbeat, and I started packing properly.

The end-of-the-week deadline was a courtesy. Agents like that one travel with associates, and associates don't do courtesies. By morning there would be someone watching the chandlery stairs. By tomorrow night, someone checking the bus depot and the ferry office. They'd found me through my diligence; they'd lose me through my speed or not at all.

There was one boat leaving before dawn that wasn't a ferry, wasn't scheduled, and wasn't picky about passengers. I knew because the chandlery owner mentioned it while handing me my change that evening—a pilot named Reyes, barred from legitimate runs, taking cargo upriver into weather no sane operator would sail into. The owner said it like gossip. I heard it like a door.

Upriver. Where the agent's eyes had flickered. Where my father's last known coordinates pointed, though I wouldn't confirm that until later, sitting in a leaking wheelhouse with a stranger's charts. Every sensible instinct I owned told me to run downstream, run anywhere, change my name again, become somebody new and flat and safe.

But the compass in my pocket swung its needle north-northwest, toward the interior, toward the silence, and for the first time in eleven years I stopped fighting it.

Not because I forgave him. God, no. Not because I owed him—what I owed was the collectors, and I intended to keep owing them nothing forever.

I went upriver because the debt followed bloodlines, and I was done letting it chase me. From now on, I'd be the one doing the following.

I left the rent under the door with a note signed *Mara Voss*, walked down the chandlery stairs past the window where a man in a gray jacket was pretending to read a fishing magazine, and went to find a pilot named Reyes and a boat that leaked.

The monsoon was due in three weeks. I had a compass, a duffel bag, and a face I couldn't change.

It would have to be enough.
