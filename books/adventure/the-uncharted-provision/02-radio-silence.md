## 2. Radio Silence

The message found me in a laundromat in Puerto Viejo, three days after I ran from the dig at Cañada Seca, and it arrived the way all bad news about my father arrives: secondhand, delayed, and addressed to someone I was pretending to be.

I'd been watching my socks spin. There's a particular meditative quality to a laundromat in a river town during the wet season—everything smells of mildew and diesel, the ceiling fan turns with the enthusiasm of a government employee, and the man behind the counter watches you like he's already decided what you're going to steal. I was Dr. Elena Marsh that week, consulting geomorphologist, unremarkable, invisible. I had gotten very good at being nobody. It's a skill, like whittling. You start with too much of yourself and carve away until what's left fits through doorways without catching.

The counter man held out an envelope. "For you," he said, which was strange, because nobody knew Elena Marsh existed, let alone where she did her laundry.

Inside was a single sheet, printed off a university server, forwarded twice, each forwarding adding a layer of institutional panic like sediment. The subject line read: **URGENT — FAMILY CONTACT REQUEST — ZHOU EXPEDITION**.

I stood there with wet hands and read it four times.

*Dr. Marcus Zhou's upriver survey has missed three scheduled check-ins. The last transmission received was on the 14th, position approximate 40 km above the Río Blanco confluence. University administration requests immediate assistance from next of kin in coordinating search efforts. Please respond.*

Three missed check-ins. My father had run field programs for thirty years, and in all that time he had missed exactly one scheduled check-in, and that was because a caiman ate the radio battery cable—his story, told forty times, improved with each telling until the caiman was the size of a canoe. Three was not an anomaly. Three was a silence with weight to it.

I should have felt fear. What I felt first, if I'm honest, was irritation. That's the thing about resentment: it gets there before grief does, every time, like a scout reporting back that the territory is hostile and best avoided. He had spent my childhood teaching me to read stratigraphy before I could read novels, naming rivers for me instead of friends, and then he'd spent my adulthood not answering letters—not the ones where I asked for money, and especially not the ones where I didn't. And now the university wanted *family*, wanted blood, wanted me to come running because the man who taught me that everything worth loving would eventually be excavated and catalogued and carried off had finally gone quiet himself.

I put the letter in my pocket and went back to watching my socks.

That was the plan: nothing. Let the university hire some other family member. Let him be found by professionals, or not found, or found in the way rivers find people, which is to say eventually and without comment. I owed him nothing. I owed a great deal of money to other people entirely, which was the whole architecture of my life at that point—a debt I never explained, not to landlords, not to employers, not to the men who occasionally appeared at the edges of dig sites with clipboards and patient smiles.

* *

They came for me on the fourth day, which told me the letter hadn't come from the university alone.

I was eating fried plantains at a stall near the docks when I saw him across the market: a tall man in a linen shirt too clean for the weather, standing under the awning of a chandlery, not buying anything. I knew the type. I'd been recognized once before, two years ago at a salvage site outside Barranca, by an agent named Ostrer who'd looked at my face the way you look at a photograph coming into focus and said, softly, almost kindly: *"You have your father's jaw."* That recognition had cost me a job, a name, and six months of moving every time a door opened behind me.

This one wasn't Ostrer. But he had the same stillness, the same way of standing in a crowd like a nail sticking up out of a floorboard. And when our eyes met across the fish stalls and the hanging strings of dried peppers, he didn't smile. He just touched his hat brim, a small courteous gesture, and turned away—which was worse than approaching. That was a message too: *we see you; we're in no hurry; the river is long.*

I paid for plantains I no longer wanted and walked, casually, the way you walk when your pulse is doing arithmetic, toward the water.

Here is what I knew, assembled from fragments over years, none of which I ever said aloud: my father owed them. Not the university—the *other* them, the collectors, the quiet men with clean shirts who bought artifacts out the backs of legitimate expeditions and financed legitimate archaeologists into holes they couldn't climb out of. I had built my entire adult life on the assumption that if I stayed small enough, far enough, borrowed-named enough, their ledger would never find my line in it. *The debt follows bloodlines.* Ostrer had said that too, at Barranca, holding my forged credentials up to the light like a specimen.

And now my father had gone silent 40 kilometers above the Río Blanco confluence, owing money to men who were already in the town I was hiding in.

You don't have to be a geomorphologist to read that deposit. You just have to be willing to look at it.

* *

The harbor office had one working radio and a woman named Consuelo who ran it with the weary authority of someone who had heard every lie a river town can produce. I told her I was Elena Marsh, affiliated researcher, inquiring after the Zhou expedition on behalf of the university. She looked at me for a long moment.

"Zhou," she said. "Big name. Big boat went up three weeks ago. Hired half the pilots on this stretch." She tapped ash off a cigarette the size of my finger. "Nobody's come back down."

"That's why I'm asking."

"Mm." She studied me the way the counter man had studied his customers, but with better instruments. "You've got his chin, you know."

My hand went to my face before I could stop it. A lifetime of aliases, and the one thing I'd never been able to forge was bone.

"I'm not—" I started.

"Everybody upriver is somebody's daughter," Consuelo said, and turned back to her radio. "There's one boat left taking cargo north. The *Salacia*. Pilot's name is Reyes. He'll take you as far as the confluence, maybe. He won't ask questions, which you'll appreciate, and you won't like his answers, which you also will." She slid a chit across the desk. "Be at slip nine by dawn. And, doctor?" She exhaled smoke through her nose. "Whatever you're running from—it swims faster than you do."

* *

I spent that night in a room above the chandlery—the same chandlery, yes, I'm aware how that sounds—with the shutters open and the letter open and my father's compass open on the table between them, three documents in negotiation.

The compass was the only thing I'd taken from his house when I left at nineteen. Brass, dented, the needle lazy in damp weather. He'd given it to me when I was eight, on a dig, kneeling in a trench, saying: *north doesn't care who your father is.* At the time I'd thought it was a gift. Later I understood it was a philosophy, and later still, a reproach. I had spent fifteen years proving north didn't care who my father was, and I had done it by making sure nobody knew who my father was, including, some days, me.

The letter sat there being reasonable. *Requests assistance from next of kin.* As if kinship were a service I could render. As if the man had ever once, in my memory, asked me for anything except silence about the second set of crates that traveled downstream from his camps, the ones that never appeared in any manifest.

Because here is the truth I kept below the waterline, deeper than the debt, deeper than the false names: I knew what he was. I'd known since I was sixteen, since the night I helped load a crate marked "ceramic samples" onto a barge flying no flag, and watched my father pay the barge's captain from a roll of bills that had no business existing on a university stipend. I never said anything. Saying something would have meant deciding what we were, and I wasn't ready to lose even the broken version of him.

So no, I didn't owe him a rescue. What I owed him was nothing, cleanly and completely, and I had earned that nothing through years of careful invisibility.

But the man in the linen shirt had touched his hat brim. And the debt followed bloodlines. And if I stayed in Puerto Viejo, they wouldn't need my father found—they'd simply wait for me to surface, and use me the way you use anyone with a face that matches a photograph: as leverage, as bait, as a signature on documents I didn't want to sign.

The river, Consuelo had said. The only way out was also the way toward him. That's the kind of joke geography likes to make.

I thought about staying anyway. I want that recorded. I lay on the chandlery cot until two in the morning constructing the case for doing nothing, and it was a strong case, well-argued, fully referenced. And then I got up, and took the compass, and burned the alias. Not literally—I folded Elena Marsh's forged credentials into the stove, because a name is a small thing to burn and I was out of practice burning big things—and I sat in the dark with my real name sitting in my mouth like a stone I'd been carrying so long I'd forgotten its weight.

Ivo Zhou. Daughter of Marcus Zhou. Next of kin.

If they wanted family, they could have family. Family, it turned out, was the one weapon I had that the collectors didn't: access. Access to his coordinates, his habits, his old campsites, the places he'd go when a dig went wrong. If he was alive upriver, I might reach him before they did. If he was dead, then the only inheritance on offer was whatever he'd left behind, and I intended to arrive before the men in clean shirts could claim it in his name—or mine.

Resentment, I've since learned, makes excellent fuel. It burns hot and dirty and it gets you upriver. You just have to be careful it doesn't get you killed arriving.

Dawn came gray and raining, the way dawn comes in the wet season, without conviction. I walked down to slip nine with a pack, a compass, and a letter I'd reread so many times the folds were going soft. The *Salacia* sat low in the brown water like something that had already given up once and was considering doing it again. Paint peeling. Hull patched in places with canvas that had clearly been patched before. A man was coiling rope on deck with the economy of someone who hated wasted motion—dark hair going gray at the temples, forearms like dock pilings, a face that had stopped expecting good news some years ago and made its peace.

"You the passenger?" he said, without looking up.

"Elena Marsh," I said, out of pure reflex, and then, annoyed at myself, at him, at the whole rigged machinery of my life: "No. Ivo Zhou. Marcus Zhou's daughter. Take me upriver."

That got his eyes. They were the color of the water, more or less, and they went over my face slowly, doing the same thing everyone did now, finding the father in it. I braced for the question—*the* question, the famous-name question, the jaw question.

He didn't ask it. He jerked his chin at the boat.

"Fare's twenty a day, plus fuel. I don't carry passengers past the confluence. I don't ask why you're going." He tossed the rope coil down and finally, fully, looked at me. "And I don't take anyone upriver in monsoon season who isn't desperate. So. Are you desperate?"

I looked at the leaking wreck that was apparently my only exit and my only direction, at the rain starting to thicken over the water, at the market behind me where somewhere a clean shirt was waiting for a nail to stick up out of the floorboard.

"Yes," I said.

"Then welcome aboard the *Salacia*," Tamás Reyes said, and spat over the side, into the river that was already, patiently, beginning to swallow everything I'd built my life out of.
