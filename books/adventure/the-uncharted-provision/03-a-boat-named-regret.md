## 3. A Boat Named Regret

I found the boat by following the smell of diesel and despair down the wrong end of the harbor, past the fishing fleet with their fresh paint and honest papers, to a hulk listing at its moorings like a drunk leaning on a lamppost.

The name painted on the bow had been sanded off and repainted so many times the wood beneath was thinner than the planks around it. The current name, stenciled in flaking white, read *Regret*. I took this as either honesty or a warning, and I'd been raised by an archaeologist, so I knew how to read warnings: they were usually just invitations wearing a hard hat.

The man aboard was patching the hull with a piece of canvas and what appeared to be roofing tar. He didn't look up when my shadow fell across him.

"Charter's full," he said.

"I haven't asked yet."

"You've got the look." He pressed the canvas flat with his palm, smearing tar up his wrist. "People who ask me for charters have the look. Like they're being followed by something with teeth."

"And you don't?"

He looked up then. He had a face that had been handsome once and had decided, somewhere along the way, that it couldn't be bothered anymore—stubble going gray at the jaw, eyes the color of river water after a storm, which is to say brown carrying a lot of suspended sediment. A scar ran through one eyebrow, old and clean.

"I'm not running from anything," he said. "I'm just sitting still where things can find me."

"That's not the flex you think it is."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, haunting the premises.

"Tamás Reyes," he said, standing, wiping his hand on trousers that had given up hope years ago.

"Ivo—" I stopped. The borrowed name sat on my tongue like a stone. *Mara Voss*, the name on my dig permits, the name the collector's agent had seen straight through two days ago while telling me the debt follows bloodlines. "Voss," I finished, and hated how it sounded, secondhand and slightly too small.

He didn't blink. Maybe he hadn't noticed. Maybe he noticed everything and filed it under *none of my business*, which turned out to be his native language.

"Where to?" he asked.

"Upstream. Past the confluence at Kilometre Four Hundred."

The ghost of the smile died quietly. "Nobody goes past the confluence this time of year."

"Somebody has to."

He studied me for a long moment, and I let him, because I understood the transaction we were both performing: he was looking for the shape of my damage, and I was looking for the shape of his, and we were each deciding whether ours were compatible. Mine fit in a coat pocket—a brass compass, my father's, heavy as a confession. His, whatever it was, seemed bigger. You could tell by the way the other pilots' boats gave his mooring a wide berth, the way nobody at the fuel dock met his eye.

"Three hundred," he said finally. "Half now. And if anyone asks, you're a geologist doing soil samples, because if the harbormaster sees me taking passengers past the confluence, he'll impound the boat out of pure spite."

"You're barred," I said. Not a question. The whole harbor had told me already, in shrugs and silences, before I ever walked down the pier.

"I'm *selective*," he said. "There's a difference. It's mostly a difference in paperwork."

I put the money in his hand. It was more than half; he noticed, and didn't say anything about that either, which is how I knew we'd get along.

* * *

From Tamás Reyes's logbook, later that night, in handwriting that slanted downhill like everything else in his life:

Passenger: Voss, M. Geologist, allegedly. Paid double without blinking, which means she's rich or desperate or both, and the ones who are both are the ones who end up in my section of the river.

Took her aboard at dusk. She watched the harbor lights go out behind us and did not look sad about it. That's rarer than you'd think. Most people carry their regrets like luggage—they keep checking on it. She carries hers like ballast. Doesn't touch it. Just knows it's there, keeping her upright.

Engine's running rough on the port cylinder again. Patched hull holding. Patched everything holding. That's all any of us are, really—patches over patches, holding.

* * *

The rain started an hour after dark, the soft preliminary kind, monsoon clearing its throat. I sat in the stern under a tarp rigged between the rail and the wheelhouse roof, watching the banks slide past—black shapes of trees, the occasional lit window of a house that would be underwater in three weeks and whose owners knew it, the way farmers know weather in their knees.

Tamás steered from inside the wheelhouse, door open, one arm hanging out like the boat was an extension of him. Which, I suppose, it was. You could see it in how he handled her—not the careful handling of a man protecting an investment, but the absent handling of a man touching a scar. He didn't pilot the *Regret*. He absolved himself onto her, hour after hour.

Around midnight he killed the engine and let us drift in a wide eddy below a drowned temple—the real kind, pre-colonial, its upper terraces poking out of the floodplain like knuckles through skin. My father had written a paper about it twenty years ago. I'd grown up on slides of its carvings projected on a bedsheet in our kitchen, back when I still thought his work was a religion worth converting to.

"Dinner?" Tamás called.

"What is it?"

"Rice and something that used to be fish."

"That's not a selling point."

"It wasn't meant to be. It's a warning label."

I ate in the stern. He ate in the wheelhouse doorway, close enough to share the silence, far enough to keep his own counsel. The rain thickened. Somewhere upstream, thunder rolled along the valley floor like a barrel being moved by someone in no hurry.

"Your compass," he said, eventually. He nodded at my jacket pocket, where the brass casing made a lump like a third rib. "You keep checking it. Every time the engine changes pitch, your hand goes there. You think I haven't noticed."

"You said you don't ask questions."

"I said I wouldn't ask about the ban. Nobody asks me about the ban. That's the deal I offer." He scraped the last of the rice from the tin plate. "The compass isn't the ban. The compass is yours."

I looked at him. Rain dripped off the tarp edge between us, a thin silver curtain.

"My father's," I said. "It's the only thing of his I kept."

"He's dead?"

"No." I heard how the word came out of me, and winced. "That's the problem."

Tamás nodded slowly, the way men nod when a thing confirms what they already suspected about the world—that fathers were trouble, that rivers were honest by comparison. He didn't ask what the problem was. He just got up, refilled my tin cup with coffee that tasted like tar and regret—appropriate, given the vessel—and went back to the wheel.

That was the moment I decided to trust him, which tells you something damning about the state of my judgment: I trusted him *because* he didn't ask. Because asking is what people do when they intend to use the answer.

* * *

From Tamás Reyes's logbook:

She slept sitting up against the transom, one hand in her pocket on that compass like it might bolt. Around two in the morning she talked in her sleep. Not words—numbers. Coordinates, maybe. Reciting them over and over, getting angrier each time, like the numbers owed her something.

I know that anger. I recited my own numbers for two years after the *Alba* went down. Eleven souls. I can still give you the draft, the load, the water level, the minute. The river keeps its books better than any bank, and it never forgives a balance.

People think a disgraced pilot drinks because of guilt. Wrong. Guilt you can carry. What broke me was simpler: I did everything right, and it happened anyway. There's no lesson in that. No redemption arc. Just weather, and weight limits, and eleven families who won't take my calls.

This woman—Voss, allegedly—has the same look. Not guilty. *Outvoted*. The universe held a meeting about her life and didn't invite her.

We'll make the confluence by tomorrow night if the engine holds. If it doesn't hold, well. I patched her once with prayer and canvas, and the prayer was the stronger material.

One more thing. When I woke her at dawn, she came up fast—too fast, hand already inside the jacket before her eyes opened. Whatever she's running from has taught her to wake up armed. I recognized the reflex. I have it myself, except mine points backward, at a night four years ago, and it always will.

Two damaged people on a leaking boat, heading into the monsoon toward a place nobody goes. If I believed in omens I'd have stayed at the dock. But I stopped believing in omens the same night I stopped believing in fair odds, and honestly, the boat's named what it's named. At least we're honest with each other about cargo.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and bruised, and the river had changed overnight. It ran higher, faster, carrying whole trees past us like matchsticks, and the water had gone the color of strong tea.

I stood at the rail while Tamás coaxed the engine awake, and I saw it before he did—or rather, I saw what it meant, because he saw the thing itself first: a wake on the far bank, a kilometer back, holding pace with us. Too steady for driftwood. Too deliberate for debris.

"Company," Tamás said quietly. He didn't turn around. He adjusted nothing—no throttle, no heading—but I watched his shoulders change, settle lower, the way a man's do when he picks up a weight he'd been hoping to set down.

"Fishermen?" I said, knowing the answer.

"At this speed? In this water?" He spat over the rail. "Fishermen don't run twin engines."

The wake held its line behind us, patient as a debt.

"How long since anyone's been past the confluence?" I asked.

"Legally? Two seasons." He fed the engine more fuel, and the *Regret* leaned into the current like a tired animal deciding, once more, to try. "Illegally? Somebody's about to break that streak. Us, or them."

Ahead, the valley narrowed, and the rain began in earnest, and somewhere above the cloud line the monsoon was stacking itself like a fist deciding whether to close.
