## 4. What the River Keeps

The river keeps everything. That's the first thing they don't tell you when you take up piloting—that you're not navigating water so much as negotiating with a hoarder. It swallows villages and spits out their roofs years later, mid-channel, waiting to gut your hull like a fish. It takes dredges, temples, livestock, the occasional optimistic missionary, and it files them all away in the same brown archive, unindexed, uncatalogued, available for retrieval at any moment of its choosing.

By the third day I was beginning to suspect it had also filed away my career, my reputation, and roughly forty percent of my self-respect, because the *Regret* was taking on water again.

"Port side," Ivo said. She didn't look up from her father's coordinates. "Same seam."

"I know it's the same seam. It's been the same seam since before you were born, probably."

"You're older than this boat?"

"I'm older than most things that leak." I wedged the bilge pump handle into its socket and started working it, one-two, one-two, the rhythm I could do in my sleep, had done in my sleep once, though that was a story with a locked door on it. Water gurgled up through the boards and I sent it back where it came from. An honest job, pumping. You can see what you've accomplished. The river just laughs and sends more.

We'd fallen into something like a routine, which is a dangerous thing to fall into on a boat named *Regret*. She took the morning watch; I took the night. She ate whatever I cooked without complaint, which either meant she had no palate or no interest in giving me ammunition. Neither of us asked questions. This was the contract, unwritten but ironclad: she didn't ask about the ban, and I didn't ask why a woman who moved through the world like a rumor—borrowed names, cash payments, eyes always doing that slow sweep of every dock before she stepped onto it—was carrying a brass compass she touched like a rosary.

I kept my end of it. Mostly. The compass sat on the chart table between us now, needle swinging lazy circles, and I found my eyes going to it the way you worry a loose tooth.

"That's his?" I asked, and immediately regretted it, because it was a question.

Ivo's pencil stopped moving. For a second I thought she'd enforce the treaty, sink me with a look, resume the silence. Instead she turned the compass so the light caught the engraving on the case. Initials worn nearly smooth. *E.Z.*

"He gave it to me when I was nine," she said. "Told me a person who knows true north can't be lied to about direction." A short laugh with nothing in it. "He lied to me about everything else."

She went back to the coordinates. I went back to pumping. Somewhere upstream, thunder cleared its throat.

* * *

The drowned village appeared around noon, or rather, its rooftops did—a congregation of terracotta crowns standing in two meters of brown water, tilted at angles that suggested the river had grown bored of them and knocked them over. We threaded between them at dead slow. A church bell tower rose off our port bow, windows dark, and hanging from the belfry rail, absurdly, a fishing net, still strung, still taut, as if the whole town had simply stepped out for lunch eleven years ago and left the nets set.

"People live here?" Ivo asked.

"Not anymore." I eased the throttle forward. "River took the low ground in '09. Government promised relocation funds. You know how those promises travel."

"And the dredge?"

I followed her chin. Half a kilometer on, listing against a sandbar like a drunk leaning on a lamppost, lay an abandoned bucket dredge, its gantry arm folded down into the water, rust bleeding orange streaks into the current. Beyond it, another. And past that, the skeletal frame of a third, stripped of anything portable.

"Somebody was pulling gold out of this stretch," Ivo said.

"Somebody was *trying*. Then somebody else decided the gold belonged to them, and the somebodies after that decided it wasn't worth dying over, and now it's just scrap with opinions about hull integrity." I swung wide of the nearest wreck. "Keep your hands inside the ride."

But she'd gone still, chart pencil hovering. "Tamás. Look at the mud."

I looked. On the dredge's flank, above the current waterline, someone had painted a mark in white that hadn't weathered yet—crisp edges, fresh enough to sting. A stylized hand closed around a coin. I knew the mark. Every pilot on this river knew the mark, the way sailors know the plague flag.

"The Meridian Collectors' Guild," Ivo said quietly. "That's their sigil."

"It is."

"They're not a salvage concern. They're not even really collectors. They're—" She stopped, jaw tightening, and I watched her decide how much of herself to spend on the sentence. "They buy things people can't afford to keep. Debts. Leverage. Silence."

"Mm." I killed the engine and let us drift, listening. Water on wood. Wind in the bell tower, making the old bronze mutter. Nothing else. "And you know this how?"

The look she gave me said the treaty was holding, barely, on both sides. But she answered anyway, which told me more than the answer did. "Because my father owes them. And because if they're marking wrecks along this channel, they're not salvaging." She tapped the fresh paint. "They're leaving a trail. For boats coming behind them. Or ahead."

Ahead, then. Someone had come upriver recently, flying the guild's colors, marking their passage like a dog with territory. My neck did the thing necks do when the arithmetic finishes itself.

"How fast does your boat go?" Ivo asked.

"On a good day, with a following wind and forgiveness from the mechanical gods? Six knots."

"And theirs?"

"Twelve, if it's the boat I think it is. Twin diesels, shallow draft, room below for cargo that doesn't like sunlight." I restarted the engine, gentler than the engine deserved. "Which means if they're a day ahead of us, we're not catching them. And if they're half a day behind us—"

"We're the ones being caught."

"There it is. You'd have made a fine pilot."

"I'd have made a fine fugitive. Same skill set."

* * *

We made camp that night on a temple rise—the river's other habit, leaving the high holy places stranded like islands while it rearranged everything mortal around them. The *Regret* sat in the shallows, tied to a strangler fig with more grip than my mooring lines deserved, and I spent the last hour of light with a roll of canvas, a pot of pitch, and the port-side seam that had been betraying me since the monsoon before last.

Patching a hull is prayer with tools. You press the canvas to the wood and you say, hold, please, hold, and the wood says nothing, because wood has heard it all before, and you slather the pitch on anyway and hope the Almighty grades on effort. I worked by feel mostly, fingers reading the grain the way Ivo read her father's coordinates, and somewhere in there I realized she'd climbed down to sit on the gunwale above me, knees drawn up, chart paper spread across her thighs.

"Your lamp's attracting every moth in the province," I said.

"Then they're the only company I've got." She held the paper closer to the flame. "These are garbage. He encoded the check-in grid references—he always did, in case the radio was monitored—and half the cipher key is smudged. I've got three possible positions for the camp and they're thirty kilometers apart."

"Thirty kilometers of flooded ruins."

"Flooded ruins with a storm behind us and a gunboat somewhere on this river." She chewed the pencil. In the lamplight, for a moment, she looked young—not the flinty contract diver with three aliases and a debt, but somebody's daughter, squinting at a parent's handwriting, trying to find the man inside the mess of it. I recognized the expression. I'd worn it myself, once, reading a transcript of an inquiry hearing.

"Second position," I said. "The one near the confluence."

Her head came up. "Why?"

"Because your father was an archaeologist, not an idiot. If he needed a defensible camp with high ground and fresh water, he'd put it where the rivers meet. Everyone puts camps at confluences. Confluence is Latin for 'the mosquitoes are terrible but the sightlines are good.'" I pressed the last of the pitch into the seam and sat back on my heels. "Also, the guild marks we saw today were trending toward the fork. Whoever painted them was headed where you're headed."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Down in the shallows, the *Regret* knocked gently against the fig root, and I chose to believe it was encouragement.

"You patched the same seam twice today," Ivo said finally.

"I'm aware."

"It'll hold?"

"It'll hold long enough to matter. Whether that's a blessing or an insult depends on what's waiting at the confluence." I stood, knees announcing their grievances, and looked north, where the sky had gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom. The storm had been stacking all evening—cloud on cloud on cloud, a wall of it, lit from inside by heat lightning like something thinking hard behind a curtain. "That's early," I said. "Monsoon's not due for another week. That system's running ahead of schedule, and everything else on this river seems to be too."

Ivo folded the chart, tucked it into her jacket, next to where the compass rode. "Then we leave at first light."

"We leave at first light, and if the guild boat is between us and the camp—"

"Then we'll know whose side the river is on."

I almost laughed. Almost. Because I'd spent six years learning exactly whose side the river was on—it was the river's side, always, and everyone else was cargo—and here was this woman talking about it like it might take a personal interest in her family drama.

But that night, lying in the stern under a tarp while the first fat drops began ticking against the canvas, I heard her up on watch, murmuring to the compass. Not words I could make out. Just a low voice, steady, the way you talk to something you're afraid of losing.

And beneath the rain, far downstream—or maybe upstream, maybe everywhere, sound lies on water—I thought I heard an engine. Two diesels, running easy, unhurried.

Like a boat that knew exactly where it was going, and exactly what would be there when it arrived.

I didn't wake her. There was nothing to wake her for that sleep wouldn't make worse. I just lay there counting drips through the new patch—one-two, one-two, the rhythm of the bilge pump, the rhythm of prayer—and waited for first light to tell me whether I'd imagined it.

The patch held. The engine noise, I feared, had not been imagination.
