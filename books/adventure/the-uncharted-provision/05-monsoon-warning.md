## 5. Monsoon Warning

The clouds came in like a debt collector—patient at first, then all at once.

I'd been watching them stack on the horizon for two hours, gray towers building themselves out of nothing, and I'd said nothing, because saying it out loud would make it weather instead of scenery. Tamás saw me looking and followed my gaze with the particular calm of a man who has already decided how he'll die and finds the current candidate acceptable.

"Monsoon's early," he said.

"You sound pleased."

"I'm never pleased." He spat over the rail, a ritual I'd come to think of as punctuation. "Early just means honest. Late ones are the liars—they let you believe you have time."

We had been on the river four days. The *Regret*—his name for her, not mine, though I'd stopped arguing—had developed three new leaks since the drowned villages, each one announced by a soft gurgle beneath the floorboards like the boat clearing her throat before saying something unpleasant. Tamás patched them with canvas and what I suspected was prayer, and I had stopped asking which of the two was structural.

The engine chose that moment to die.

Not sputter. Not hesitate. Die—the way a man dies mid-sentence, one hand still raised to make his point. The propeller shaft shuddered once through the hull, a feeling I felt in my teeth more than my ears, and then the river took us sideways and the silence that followed was so complete I could hear the rain still forty minutes away, gathering its argument.

"Fouled," Tamás said, already kneeling at the housing, ear pressed to the deck like a doctor listening for a heartbeat he already knew was bad. "Something's wrapped the screw. Vine, net, cable—river keeps everything you throw in it, and eventually it gives it back."

"Can you clear it from above?"

"With the propeller off? Yes. With the propeller on, and no winch, and this boat?" He looked up at me, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before—not fear exactly, but an accounting. A man adding numbers he didn't like. "It has to be done from below."

I looked at the water. It was the color of strong tea left too long, opaque all the way down, carrying branches and silt and whatever else the river had decided to keep. The shallows here were maybe eight feet—I could see the sandbar's pale shoulder breaking downstream—but opaque is opaque, and eight feet of brown water is its own kind of night.

"I'll go," I said.

Tamás didn't argue, which I appreciated, and didn't offer to go himself, which I appreciated less until I remembered he'd told me, on the second night, that he couldn't swim. He'd said it the way other men confess gambling debts or illegitimate children: flatly, daring me to make it mean something. I hadn't. Some inheritances you don't ask about.

I stripped down to my shirt, tied the dive knife to my wrist with a length of cord, and knelt at the transom while he held the boat steady against the current with one oar and both arms.

"There's a trick to it," he said. "When you find whatever it is, don't pull against it. Cut toward yourself. The river will help you if you stop fighting it."

"That sounds like advice about more than propellers."

"Everything I know is about more than what I say it's about." He almost smiled. It was the closest thing to warmth the man had produced in four days, and I found myself absurdly proud of it, the way you're proud of a stray cat letting you touch it. "Go. Before the sky finishes deciding."

I went.

* * *

Underwater, the world became a rumor of itself.

Brown light, thick as broth. My hands were the only real things—pale shapes swimming ahead of me, leading me down past my own arms, down to where the propeller should have been. I found the shaft by feel, then the blades, and then the thing itself: a fishing net, old and rotten-strong, wound around the screw in a dozen turns, trailing off into the dark like something reaching back upstream. There was a body in it, or part of one—a catfish, long dead, bloated and blind, tangled in the mesh as if the river had dressed it for burial.

I got the knife out. I cut toward myself, the way he'd said, and the first three turns came free easily, the rotten twine parting like old promises. The fourth was newer. Nylon, maybe, or good hemp, wound tight and holding, and when I pulled against it—stupid, instinctive—it held me back.

That was the first mistake. The river only needed me to make one.

The current took my hair first. Then my shirt, pressing it flat against my back like a hand between my shoulder blades, pushing me down and forward into the mud. My feet left the bottom. The cord on my knife went taut and I felt it bite my wrist, and then the knife was gone—torn loose or dropped, I couldn't tell you, because by then I couldn't tell you anything except that up was a guess and my lungs had started making their demands.

I've been in tight spots before. Collapsed trench walls, a tunnel in Laos that breathed when I did, a collector's agent standing too close in a market in Vientiane. In every one of them, some cold clerk in the back of my skull had kept taking notes: *options, exits, odds.* That clerk was gone now. What replaced him was simpler and older and much less useful: a white animal panic, hands clawing at water, at net, at my own hair, burning through air I didn't have in flailing quantities.

The last coherent thought I had was absurdly specific. I thought: *the compass is in my jacket on the deck, and nobody who knows what it means will ever find it,* and I was furious about that, furious in a way that had nothing to do with drowning, and then the fury ran out of air too, and the brown closed over everything like a ledger being shut.

* * *

Hands. Under my arms, hauling, and then air—air like a slap, like a betrayal, like the best thing I had ever tasted—and I was coughing river water onto the deck of the *Regret* while Tamás knelt over me with an oar laid across his knees and an expression I'd never seen on him: terror, worn openly, like a shirt inside out.

"Breathe," he said. "Just do the one thing. Breathe."

I did the one thing. It took a while.

The rain arrived while I was still coughing—first fat, scattered drops, then the whole sky letting go at once, drumming the deck, erasing the horizon, turning the world into the inside of a drum. Tamás dragged me under the awning, wrapped me in a blanket that smelled of diesel and mildew, and sat across from me, dripping, watching me with those accounting eyes again.

"The net?" he asked finally.

"Gone. Mostly. Enough cleared." My voice came out scraped, unrecognizable, a stranger's voice. "There was a fish in it. Dead."

"River keeps everything." He said it gently this time, without the spit, without the punctuation. "Including, nearly, you."

I laughed. It hurt, and came out wrong, half cough, but I laughed, because the alternative was something I wasn't ready to do in front of another person.

He didn't laugh with me. He just waited, the way he waited out engine trouble and weather and my silences, with a patience that wasn't passive at all but a kind of labor, and after a while I understood that the waiting was the offer. He was holding the door open. Whether I walked through it was mine.

"My father taught me to swim," I said.

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere below us, the bilge pump Tamás had rigged from parts of three different engines chugged away at its losing battle.

"He was—" I stopped. Started again. This was the problem with telling the truth; it had edges, and you cut yourself deciding where to begin. "Everyone thinks they know him. The great Zhou. Books, documentaries, a lecture hall named after him at a university I'm not allowed to set foot in. And I spent my whole childhood being introduced as *his daughter*, like a footnote. Like provenance. You know what provenance is, on the artifact market? It's the story that makes a thing worth money. That's what I was. The line in the catalog that justified the price."

Tamás said nothing. The rain said plenty.

"So when I dig now, I dig under false names. Chen. Marlowe. Once, memorably, a woman named Okafor whose paperwork I still feel guilty about. Because if I use my own name, I'm not a field tech with steady hands, I'm *Zhou's daughter chasing his shadow*, and every site director starts managing me instead of employing me, and every collector's agent starts doing arithmetic with my face." I pulled the blanket tighter. My hands wouldn't quite stop shaking, and I let him see it, which was its own strange relief. "And the debt. You've been polite enough not to ask, so I'll ruin your record. The debt isn't mine. It's his. He borrowed against everything—against the house, against his name, against me, probably, if anyone had been fool enough to take me as collateral—and then he went upriver and stopped answering his radio. The collectors can't collect from a ghost, so they collect from bloodlines. That's their policy. I heard it said to my face like a warranty term."

"Why tell me this?"

"Because I died a little down there." I nodded at the brown torrent sliding past the hull, indifferent, enormous. "And the clerk in my head—the one who takes notes, who keeps the options list—he didn't come back up with me. Or he did, and he's quit. Either way, I'm sitting here with no notes left, and you're the nearest person, and it turns out that's what people do. They tell the nearest person."

He absorbed that. Outside, lightning stitched the clouds together somewhere over the far bank, and for half a second the whole river stood out silver, every wave visible at once, and then it was gone.

"My disaster," he said quietly. "The one with the ban. Eleven people. A ferry run I took in weather I'd been warned off twice, because the pay was triple and my pride was cheap. The boat broke up on the shoals below Kengtung. I lived because I was in the wheelhouse and the wheelhouse floated. They put me ashore and took my license and I let them, because a punishment was easier than a reason." He looked at his hands—big, scarred, useless in the water, useless in every way that mattered that night eleven years ago. "So. Now we're even. You drown for your father's debts; I float for mine. Between us we'd make one competent person."

"Two halves," I said. "Both of them sunk."

"And yet." He gestured at the engine housing, at the rain, at the whole ruined enterprise of us. "The screw's clear. The boat floats. The storm's early, which means it's honest, which means it'll pass by morning if we live through tonight, and I've lived through worse nights with worse company and better boats."

I wanted to argue with him on principle, but the truth was the truth: something had come loose in me down there, and what rushed into the space wasn't grief, exactly, or relief. It was lighter than either. It was the specific weightlessness of having said a true thing aloud and finding the world still standing.

Then Tamás went still.

Not relaxed-still. The other kind—the kind that precedes motion. His eyes had gone past my shoulder, downstream, to where the rain thinned for a moment in a ragged seam of gray light.

A boat. Far off, running without lights, low in the water, moving fast against the current the way only a big engine can. And on its bow, briefly lit by its own running lamp before someone killed it, a shape I recognized from a market in Vientiane and a warning delivered in a whisper:

The collector's flag.

"They're ahead of schedule too," Tamás said. He was already reaching for the starter cord. "Hold on to something. We're going to need the engine, and I'm going to need you to pray to whatever's left down there that it starts."
