## 6. The Camp That Stopped Answering

The coordinates put us in a bend of the river that didn't exist on any map Tamás owned, which he took as a personal insult. "I've run this water eleven years," he said, throttling down as the channel narrowed into a corridor of drowned bamboo. "Eleven years, and the river grows rooms I've never seen."

"Rivers do that after rain."

"Rivers do that when they're hiding something," he said, and neither of us laughed, because we were both thinking it.

We smelled the camp before we saw it—woodsmoke gone sour, wet canvas, and under all of it the sweet-rot smell of a place where food had been left to die. Then the trees opened and there it was, strung along a shelf of ground maybe two meters above the current: six tents in a ragged line, half of them flattened, the others shredded so thoroughly they looked less like shelter than like something had eaten its way out of them.

Tamás nosed the boat against a fallen kapok trunk and killed the engine. The silence afterward was enormous. Water dripped off the canopy in slow percussion. Somewhere upstream, a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling and then thought better of it.

"No smoke," I said.

"No people," he said.

"Those aren't the same thing."

"No," he agreed. "They're not." He handed me the machete without being asked, which I was learning was his version of a speech about trust.

I went up the bank first. Habit. When you grow up on digs you learn the grammar of an abandoned site before you can read it consciously—the way a collapsed trench wall says *water*, the way scattered tools say *panic*, the way neatly stacked equipment says *they meant to come back*. This camp spoke all three languages at once, fluently, and every sentence ended badly.

The main tent had been the radio tent; you could tell by the aluminum case bolted to a folding table, except the case wasn't bolted anymore and neither was the table. Someone had taken a rock—or a boot heel, or pure fury—to the radio itself. The housing was caved in, the dials punched through, the antenna snapped in three places and arranged, deliberately, I thought, in a shape like a broken arrow.

"They smashed it from inside," Tamás said behind me. He'd followed me up without my noticing, which should have worried me more than it did. "See? Glass on this side only. They weren't defending. They were cutting themselves off."

Or someone was cutting them off. I crouched and picked a splinter of Bakelite out of the mud, turned it over. My father had taught me radios before he'd taught me to swim. *A voice is a fragile thing, Ivo. It needs a wire or a wave to travel on, and both can be cut.* He'd said it like a lesson in engineering. It had always sounded more like a confession.

* * *

The ledger was in the survey tent, in a waterproof pouch that told me someone had expected it to matter.

I found it wedged under a crate of theodolite parts, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with surveyor's twine in the double knot my father used for everything—sample bags, Christmas presents, other people's lives. My hands knew how to untie it before my brain decided whether it wanted to. That's inheritance for you. It lives in your fingers.

It was a field ledger, the kind every dig keeps: acquisitions, disbursements, chain of custody. The early pages were ordinary. Basalt grinding stones. A bronze mirror, catalogued, photographed, crated. Pottery lots numbered in his cramped left-slanting hand, the hand I'd spent twenty years trying not to recognize in my own notebooks.

Then, four days before the last scheduled check-in, the handwriting changed. Looser. Faster. The entries stopped being artifacts and started being prices.

*Lot 14 (mirror) — traded — K.'s people — against passage.*

*Lot 22–27 (ceramics) — traded — against balance owed.*

*Lot 31 (gold, funerary) — held back — leverage.*

*K.* I read the name four times before it resolved into anything, because some part of me had been hoping it would resolve into anyone else. But I'd seen that initial before, stamped in gold foil on the calling card of a man who had once stood in my rented room in another province and said, with terrible gentleness, *the debt follows bloodlines, Miss Zhou. Your father taught us that.*

My father hadn't been robbed. He hadn't been coerced, exactly, either—or no more than any drowning man is coerced by water. He had sold the dig. Piece by piece, lot by lot, he had fed the best of a Bronze Age river civilization to the collectors to buy himself out from under creditors whose names I knew because they were the same names that had been eating my life for six years. We had been running from the same wolves. He had simply run upriver, and I had run sideways, and the wolves, patient animals, had followed us both.

He'd even written it down. That was the part that made my teeth ache. Not hidden, not coded—recorded, in a ledger, in his own hand, with the meticulousness of a man who believed documentation was a form of virtue. As if writing *traded* instead of *stolen* made it honest. As if the ledger were a letter to whoever found it, saying: *you see? I kept accounts. I was never careless. I was never a thief.*

You were never careful, either, I thought. You were never here. Those are different failures, and you died—I don't know if you're dead, God help me, I don't know—but whatever happened to you, you managed to fail at being my father from four hundred kilometers away, in advance, in writing.

"Ivo." Tamás's voice came from outside, carefully level. "You should see the boats."

There had been three boats at the camp, moored to stakes that now stood in open water. Two were still there, swamped, sitting on the bottom in a meter of brown flood. The third stake was empty, its rope cut clean—not frayed, cut—and trailing in the current like something that had given up.

"He bought passage," I said. I heard my own voice and didn't recognize it; it had gone flat and administrative, my mother's voice, the one she'd used for reading bills aloud. "One boat left. Downriver. He took it."

"You don't know that."

"I have his handwriting, Tamás. I have *lot numbers*." I heard the ledger pouch crackle in my fist and realized I'd been crushing it. "He sold everything that mattered and left his crew the tents."

Tamás didn't say anything to that. He just looked at the empty mooring stake, then at the flooded treeline, doing pilot arithmetic—current, draft, time. I watched him decide not to offer comfort, and I could have kissed him for it. Comfort right then would have been a kind of insult, like flowers at a fire.

* * *

We found Bea because Tamás heard her breathing.

That sounds like a lie until you've spent three days on a boat with him. The man listened to rivers the way priests listen to congregations—for the thing underneath the thing. We were checking the cisterns behind the mess tent, stone-lined pits built by whoever had lived here a thousand years before anyone filed a permit, and Tamás stopped, head cocked, and said, "There," the way other people say *look out*.

The third cistern wasn't echoing right.

I got the rotted cover off and looked down into the dark, and the dark looked back and said, in a voice scraped down to gravel, "If you're K.'s men, I'm dead anyway, so you may as well know I'm out of ammunition."

"We're not K.'s men," I said.

A long pause. Water lapped against stone. "Prove it."

"With what? I have a compass and a grudge." I fished in my pocket and held up the ledger pouch. "And I have his handwriting. Lot fourteen. The mirror. Traded against passage."

Another silence, longer. Then, faintly, disbelievingly, almost laughing: "You're the daughter."

"I'm the daughter."

"The one who wouldn't come."

"The one who wouldn't come," I agreed, and something in my chest turned over and lay still. So he'd talked about me. To his crew, on a dying expedition, while selling their work out from under them—the daughter who wouldn't come. I wondered what else he'd said. I wondered if it had been kind. I found, examining myself honestly, that I didn't care, and that not caring felt less like freedom than like frostbite.

She came up the iron rungs slowly, one-handed. She couldn't have been more than twenty-four, hollow-cheeked, feverish, her left arm bound in a sling made from a shirt that had once been white. She stood at the rim of the cistern swaying like a plant in current, and she looked at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the look of someone deciding how much truth a stranger can be trusted with.

"Bea Okonkwo," she said. "Surveyor. Second season." Then her eyes went to the sling, to the way she favored her ribs, and she said, flatly, pre-emptively, "The arm's from the flood surge. Not from him. Before you ask."

"Him?"

"Your father." She let Tamás help her over the rim, and her legs nearly went, and she locked them and stayed upright out of what looked like pure spite toward gravity. "Dr. Zhou didn't hurt anyone. I want that understood. Whatever else—he carried Márquez for two kilometers when the water came. He put people in the boats. He gave away his own berth." Her voice cracked and she hated it, I could see her hating it. "Then the collectors' launch came at dawn and he went aboard and that was the last anyone saw. Three days ago."

"And the rest of the crew?"

"Six went downstream in the second boat, day before the storm. Trying to raise the university." She looked at the shredded tents, the broken radio, the whole ruined grammar of the camp. "The storm came first."

Tamás said quietly, "No boat survives that water in that weather."

"Yes," Bea said. "We've established that."

I stood there holding a ledger full of prices and did the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do since the treeline: one father, gone aboard the collectors' boat, owing them more than a dig could cover. One manifest, listing every artifact, every debt, every name—including, somewhere in the disbursement columns, I was suddenly certain, mine. *Against obligations of the daughter. Held.* Because of course. Of course he'd have signed for me too. The debt follows bloodlines. He'd known that longer than I had. Maybe he'd written it.

Grief is supposed to feel like grief. Nobody warns you it can curdle, that it can sit in you overnight and turn to something with teeth. I stood in the ruins of my father's last camp, in the rain starting again, holding the proof that he had never been the man I'd needed him to be—that he had been, instead, precisely the man I'd spent six years proving I wasn't—and what I felt was not sorrow.

It was fury. Clean, clarifying, directional fury. For the first time in years I knew exactly whom I was angry at, and it was wonderfully, terribly not myself.

"Bea," I said. "The vault. The pieces he held back. Do you know where?"

She blinked rain out of her eyes. "Why?"

"Because K.'s men are two days behind us, and when they arrive, they're going to want it. And I intend," I said, and heard my father's left-slanting certainty in my own voice and let it stand, "to be the one who decides what happens next."

Behind her, Tamás looked at the rising river, then at me, and said nothing at all—which, from him, was very loud indeed.
