## 7. Buyers and Liars

I heard the other boat before I saw it—because of course I did. A river pilot learns the language of engines the way a mother learns her children's footsteps in the dark, and this one was wrong in every particular: too heavy for its hull, running too fast for the channel, and coming upriver against the current like it had money to burn on fuel and a grudge to spend it on.

"Company," I said.

Ivo looked up from her father's coordinates, which she'd been staring at for an hour with the expression of a woman reading a letter from a dead man who refused to be dead. "How many?"

"One boat. Maybe eight men." I cut the engine and let us drift. The *Regret* settled into the current with a sigh, as if she too preferred not to be noticed. "Could be fishermen."

"Fishermen don't fly flags," Bea said from under the tarp where we'd stowed her since the camp. She'd been down in that cistern long enough to develop opinions about everything, and she deployed them now with the flat certainty of someone who had watched armed men murder her colleagues' radios and probably worse. "They flew the collector's flag at the camp. Green and gold."

Green and gold. The colors of men who buy things that were never for sale.

Ivo stood, and I watched her do the thing she does—the thing I'd been watching her do for two hundred kilometers without naming it. She went still. Not calm; stillness isn't calm. Stillness is what you get when you compress all the motion inside yourself into something small enough to hide. Her father's daughter, right down to the bones.

"How much ammunition do you have?" she asked me.

"Enough to make them polite. Not enough to make them leave."

"Then we're talking to them."

"We could run," I offered, though I already knew the answer. The channel ahead narrowed past the drowned temple, and my engine had a cough it hadn't had yesterday, and besides—Ivo Zhou did not run from things anymore. She'd stopped somewhere around the moment she surfaced from the silt with her secret in her mouth and handed it to me like a stone she no longer wanted to carry.

"They have the manifest," she said. "Or part of it. And they'll have questions about the vault." She paused, and something moved behind her eyes, quick as a fish under ice. "And I know where the vault is."

That landed between us and sat there.

"You've known this whole time?"

"I suspected. Now I know." She touched the compass at her throat—a habit, not a check; the needle hadn't pointed anywhere useful in days. "My father used to tell me stories when I was small. Before he got famous, before the digs got big. He told me about a temple that drank the river. About a door below the waterline that only opened in drought years. He called it his inheritance from a man who never gave him anything else." Her voice didn't crack. It just went very level, which was worse. "He buried his best pieces there. Under the flood line. Where nobody would dig."

"And now the river's rising."

"Now the river's rising."

* * *

The collector's boat came around the bend of the drowned temple like a debt arriving early. It was a converted patrol craft, military gray gone soft with civilian money, twin mounts on the bow that had been polished rather than removed. The flag snapped green and gold above the wheelhouse. Eight men visible, maybe more below decks, and standing at the rail with the unhurried posture of a landlord inspecting property—

I knew him.

Not by name. By type. That was the trouble with the river trade: you met everyone eventually, usually at your lowest point, and they remembered you even when you'd paid good money to forget yourself. This one had bought salvage rights off a bankrupt dredging company six years ago, back before my ban, back when I still took contracts. He'd shaken my hand. He'd told me I had honest eyes for a pilot working dishonest water.

His name was Captain Ferro, and he had honest eyes himself, which was the most dishonest thing about him.

He came alongside with a fender's width of courtesy and killed his engines, and in the sudden quiet I could hear the monsoon breathing behind us—a low grumble out of the southwest, thunder clearing its throat over hills we couldn't see yet.

"Reyes," Ferro said, warm as bathwater. "Tamás Reyes. I thought that name was retired."

"It keeps coming back. Like a bad tide."

"You're piloting for—" He turned to Ivo, and I watched him do the arithmetic. It took him three seconds. I'd seen it take other people longer; she'd spent years sanding her father off her face, but grief sands it right back on. "Ah," he said softly. "Zhou. You have his jaw."

"She has her own face," I said.

Ferro smiled at me like I was a dog barking at furniture. "Miss Zhou. Dr. Zhou. What do I call you?"

"Nothing. You can start with nothing."

"Fair." He spread his hands. Behind him, his men lounged with the studied looseness of professionals pretending not to watch us, though every one of them had marked the tarp where Bea hid, the fuel drums, the patched hull. They'd assessed us the way you assess a house before deciding whether to burn it or buy it. "Then let me be direct, because the weather won't wait and neither will I. Your father left debts. Significant ones. My employers financed his last three seasons against the promise of returns. The returns are sitting in a vault beneath our feet—or beneath the flood line, shortly—and the university, the insurers, and several gentlemen with less patient dispositions all want to know where."

"Not my problem."

"Except it is." He produced a folded packet from inside his jacket, oilcloth-wrapped, and held it up between two fingers. Even at ten meters I knew what it was. Paper has a way of announcing itself. "Pages from his expedition ledger. Financial records. And this—" he tapped the top sheet, "—is a signed statement of account. Every creditor listed. Every sum owed. Including the debts incurred in your name, Miss Zhou. Did you know he borrowed against you? Against your future earnings? He used your name as collateral four years ago. That's why the collectors found you in the dirt at Vellacorta. That's why they'll keep finding you, wherever you bury yourself. This document ends it. One signature from the creditors' syndicate, and you're free. No more borrowed names. No more looking over your shoulder in the dig camps."

I looked at Ivo.

I want to be honest here, because honesty is the only currency I have left and I've spent most of it. I expected to see her waver. Any human being would waver. Four years of invisibility, of digging under dead women's names, of flinching from her own reflection because it wore her father's jaw—and here was a man holding the receipt for all of it, offering to tear it up.

But she didn't waver. She went still again, that compressed stillness, and I realized I was watching something I didn't have a word for yet.

"That's mine," she said quietly. "He stole even that. Even my freedom—he sold it before I knew I had it."

"The world is full of fathers like that," Ferro said, with the gentleness of true understanding, and that was the worst part. He wasn't performing. Somewhere under the green-and-gold flag there was a man who genuinely believed he was offering mercy. Temptation always wears a familiar face; that's how you know it's temptation and not opportunity. Opportunity looks strange. It makes you work. Temptation fits like an old coat.

"What do you want?" Ivo asked.

"The vault. You take us to it. We open it together, we catalog the pieces, my employers honor the settlement, and you walk away clean. Everyone profits. Well—" he glanced at the darkening sky, at the water already climbing the drowned temple's steps, lapping at carvings that had waited centuries for exactly this weather, "—everyone profits quickly."

"And if I say no?"

Ferro sighed, and the sigh was real too, and somehow that made it more frightening than any threat. "Then the river takes the vault tonight or tomorrow, and my employers recover their losses another way. Debts don't drown, Miss Zhou. They transfer. Bloodlines, remember?" He said it kindly. Someone had told her that once, in chapter one of her life, and I saw it land in her like a hook set deep.

The thunder rolled closer. The first fat drops hit the deck, scattered like thrown gravel.

* * *

Afterward, while Ferro's boat fell back to a respectful distance—following us, herding us, patient as interest accruing—Ivo and I stood at the wheel while the rain came down in earnest and Bea emerged from under the tarp looking like a cat that had correctly predicted the bath.

"He's telling the truth," Bea said. "About the document. I saw the ledger at camp. Your father kept two sets of books. One for the university, one for the creditors. In the second one, there's a page with your name on it. I remember because I thought—it seemed so cruel, writing a person down like a line item."

"Thank you, Bea," Ivo said, "for that precise and devastating contribution."

"I'm a surveyor. Precision is the job."

I laughed despite everything, and Ivo almost did, and then the almost collapsed into something tired and true.

"So," I said. "Do we run the rapids?"

"There are no rapids yet. There will be, after the storm crest comes through, tomorrow night maybe. Today the channel's just fast." She stared south, where the sky had gone the color of a bruise going bad. "If I take him to the vault, I'm free. Actually free. I could sign my own name on a contract. I could dig in daylight."

"And?"

"And it's a lie." She said it simply, the way you'd report the weather. "It's not freedom, it's a ransom note with better handwriting. And those pieces—" her hand closed around the compass, knuckles white, "—those pieces are the only honest thing he ever did. He hid them so they wouldn't be sold. If I open that door for Ferro, I finish what the creditors started. I become the last line item in his ledger."

The rain thickened. Ahead, the drowned temple slid past on our port side, its crown just breaking the surface now, and beneath it—beneath twenty feet of brown, rising water—sat a door that only opened in drought years, holding her father's one act of grace.

"Besides," Ivo added, and here her voice changed, went dry as old rope, "he said the vault opens in drought years. It's monsoon season, Reyes. Ferro's buying a door that's about to be twenty feet underwater anyway. Let him spend his money on it."

I grinned at her, and she grinned back, and for a moment we were two criminals planning a fraud against a fraudster, and it felt—for the first time in longer than I could measure—like the correct shape for my life.

"Where are we actually going?" Bea asked.

Ivo unfolded her father's coordinates one last time, rain spotting the paper, and read them aloud. Then she stopped. Read them again.

"Reyes," she said slowly, "these aren't vault coordinates. These are evacuation coordinates. High ground. A temple rise, north fork, above any flood line." She looked up, and the rain ran off her chin, and her eyes were bright and furious and alive. "He wasn't hiding the treasure from the creditors. He was hiding *people*. Bea—you weren't the only survivor. There's a signal fire protocol. He marked it for anyone from the crew who got out."

Behind us, Ferro's engines turned over, following.

Ahead, the north fork split away through country no sane pilot would run in rising water—which meant, naturally, that I started turning the wheel.
