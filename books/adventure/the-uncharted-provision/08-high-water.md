## 8. High Water

The monsoon arrived the way my father used to arrive at dinner: late in every promise he'd made about it, and louder than anyone was prepared for.

We heard it before we saw it—a sound like a freight train rolling along the river's spine, except there were no trains out here, nothing but water and sky conspiring. The clouds that had been stacking on the horizon all morning went from gray to a bruised, personal sort of black, and the air pressure dropped so fast my ears popped twice.

"Early," Bea said, standing on the temple rise with her arms wrapped around herself. She hadn't stopped shivering since we pulled her out of the cistern, though it wasn't cold. "The forecasts said three days."

"The forecasts," Tamás said, "are written by people who don't have to be on this river." He was already moving toward the *Regret*, checking lines with hands that looked calm and weren't. I'd learned to read him by then—the stillness of his shoulders when he was afraid, the way his jaw worked sideways like he was chewing on words he refused to say.

Below us, the captain's men were still in the lower camp, six of them hauling crates toward their boat with the brisk efficiency of people who believed they had time. Captain Serrano himself stood at the waterline, watching the sky with an expression I was beginning to understand. Not greed anymore. Arithmetic. He was calculating how much river he could outrun.

He thought he had me figured out. A daughter who'd trade anything for a clean name. And the worst part—the part that made my skin crawl—was that he wasn't entirely wrong. I'd spent seven years wanting exactly what he was offering: the manifest pages that proved the debt collectors' claims against me were built on forged signatures, my father's forgeries, sold to men who'd decided the daughter should inherit the bill. Burn those pages or hand them to the right lawyer, and Ivo Zhou could exist again under her own name instead of a rotation of borrowed ones.

I just hadn't counted on the river having opinions.

* * *

Serrano came up the temple steps two at a time, rain starting to spit, and held out the manifest folder like a man offering a dog a treat. "Vault first," he said. "Then you get your copy, signed, witnessed, whatever makes you happy. Where is it?"

I looked at the folder. I'd been looking at it for hours, since the deal was struck, letting myself want it the way you let your tongue find a broken tooth.

"You remember," I said. "You said my father talked about it."

"He talked in his sleep, near the end. Delirious. But names stick." Serrano smiled with all the warmth of a customs stamp. "A chapel. A flood line. Something about his little girl and a story he told her."

And there it was. The knife of it. Because I did remember—not from delirium but from when I was nine, sitting on his knee in a dig tent while he drew maps in the dirt. *Every good site needs a place the water can't reach, Ivo. That's how you know it matters.* He'd told me about a shrine-chapel cut into the bluff above the old ferry crossing, pre-colonial, its floor higher than any flood the valley had ever recorded. He'd told me because I was his daughter and he loved me, presumably, in whatever way a man loves a thing he plans to use later.

"It's upstream," I said. "Half a kilometer past the camp. The bluff on the east bank."

"And the entrance?"

"A stair behind the fig trees. You'll want ropes." I paused, and gave him the truth like a gift, because he'd never believe it wrapped any other way. "Captain, that water is going to come up faster than you think. Whatever's in there, it isn't worth dying for."

"Nothing is worth dying for," Serrano agreed pleasantly. "That's why I send other people."

* * *

We went down to the vault in the first real rain—me leading, four of Serrano's men behind me with rifles slung but hands free for climbing, Serrano bringing up the rear. Tamás stayed with the boat and Bea, which was the arrangement, though the look he gave me as I left said he considered arrangements to be things that happened to other people.

The stair was where I remembered it would be, behind fig roots thick as ship rigging, cut into the rock by hands two thousand years dead. Forty-one steps. I counted them as a child counts, because counting was the only prayer I had left. Behind me the men breathed hard and said nothing, and above us the rain quit being weather and became a fact of geology.

The chapel door was stone, and it took three of them and a pry bar to shift it. Inside: a chamber no bigger than a kitchen, dry as a held breath after all these centuries, and stacked against the far wall, wrapped in oilcloth and wax-sealed, the crates.

My father's best pieces. The ones he hadn't sold. Bronze mirrors green with age. Jade that caught even that drowned light and turned it warm. A lacquered box I recognized from photographs on the mantel of a house I'd stopped visiting—photographs where I stood beside it, small and squinting, while he explained to some donor what it meant.

I stood in the doorway and felt absolutely nothing, and then I felt the nothing crack, and what came through it wasn't grief. It was arithmetic of my own. The water was coming. These men were between me and the exit. And every second we spent arguing over crates was a second the river spent closing its hand.

"Get them moving," Serrano said behind me. "Two trips. We'll make two trips."

"We won't make one," I said.

The first crate came up the stair and met the water coming down.

I heard it before I understood it—that deep, throat-clearing roar from the direction of the bend—and then the flood found the stairwell. Not gradually. The river doesn't do gradual. It came down those forty-one steps like it owned them, which, historically speaking, it did, and the man carrying the crate went off his feet with a shout that got swallowed halfway through.

What happened next happened in pieces, the way disasters do.

A man grabbing for the wall. Another one dropping his rifle into brown water that was already thigh-deep and rising. Serrano screaming orders that the river answered with more water. The chamber flooding from below too—I heard it, a gurgle underfoot, water finding the cracks in the floor that two millennia of drought had never tested—and realizing, with a clarity I'll carry longer than any artifact, that the vault's whole genius was also its trap. High above the old floods. Nothing in the old records about the new ones.

"Drop it!" I was shouting, at everyone, at the world. "Drop the crates and climb!"

They didn't. Of course they didn't. One of them got a second crate to the top of the stair and the current peeled it out of his arms and took it spinning into the white, jade and bronze and ten thousand dollars of provenance gone downstream to whoever the river sells to. The man himself went after it, lunging, and I caught his collar and hauled him back with a strength I didn't know I had, and we both fell against the fig roots and clung.

The water at the base of the stair was chest-deep now and moving like a train. Two of Serrano's men were on the high ground with us, coughing. One was not accounted for. Serrano stood hip-deep at the chamber mouth, staring at his drowning fortune, and I watched something go out of his face—not decency, nothing so dramatic, just the arithmetic finally finishing itself.

"Go," I told him. "Upstream bank. There's a ridge above the ferry crossing. Go now and live to steal something else."

For one strange moment he looked at me like he was going to say something human. Then the river shoved him, hard, and he chose living, and scrambled.

I climbed.

* * *

Tamás had the *Regret* moving before I reached her, which shouldn't have surprised me and didn't. He'd watched the flood come around the bend the way pilots watch everything—with the particular calm of a man who has already survived his worst day and files all subsequent days under miscellaneous.

Bea hauled me over the gunwale. My hands were shaking so badly I couldn't manage the rail.

"The others?" Tamás asked, meaning the armed men, meaning: are they our problem now.

"Alive, mostly. Scattered on the high bank." I got my breath back in pieces. "They're done. Serrano's done."

"He doesn't strike me as done."

"He's standing in a flooded vault holding a wet manifest, Tamás. There's done, and there's done."

But even as I said it I knew the truth, which was that a man like Serrano didn't drown his assets without learning something, and what he'd learned was that I'd led him true and the river had cheated us both. That was a debt, in his ledger. Everything was a debt, everywhere, always. It was the one lesson my father had actually taught me.

Downstream, the rapids waited.

I need to explain about the rapids, because everything afterward depends on them. Below the old ferry crossing the valley pinches shut, and the whole river funnels through a gap maybe forty meters wide, over a shelf of rock the dredging companies had mapped and then written off as unnavigable. Every pilot I'd ever heard of went around. There was no around. There was portage, twelve kilometers of it, through jungle that was currently being unmade by a monsoon.

"There's no around," I said to Tamás, anyway, because someone had to say it.

"No," he agreed. He was looking at the water, reading it the way I read strata. "There's through."

"Through kills people."

"Portage kills people slower." He glanced at me, and there it was—the thing he never talked about, surfacing in his eyes like a body in floodwater. Whatever disaster had ended his legitimate career, it had happened in water like this. Maybe because of water like this. "Ivo. The boat can run it if I keep her bow on and the engine honest. It's not the rapids that decide. It's whether I flinch."

"And if you flinch?"

"Then we find out what Bea and you weigh."

Bea, wedged in the stern with her arms around a bail bucket, said, "I'm very buoyant," in a voice that shook, and I loved her for it, and I was terrified, and both of those things were true at once, which is, I've decided, what being alive mostly consists of.

"Run it," I said.

* * *

I won't describe the rapids properly, because language failed me at the time and has never quite recovered. What I remember: the noise, total, like the world ending specifically for us. Water over the bow, over everything, the world reduced to brown violence and Tamás's forearms and his voice—low, constant, talking to the boat, to the river, to God, impossible to say. A rock the size of a house appearing where no rock should be and Tamás leaning the *Regret* past it by a margin I refuse to estimate. Bea screaming what might have been a prayer or a list of grievances. Me bailing with the bucket, uselessly, faithfully, the way you do.

At the worst of it—there was a worst of it, a hole in the river like a dropped floor—we hit something glancing, and the world spun, and for one full second we were airborne in the way that dreams lie about, and I thought, with absurd clarity: *this is what it costs, this is the price of choosing, pay it.*

Then we were through. Through, and out onto the broad brown flood-plain below, the rain gone suddenly gentle, the banks sliding past green and indifferent, and the three of us sitting in eight inches of water in the bottom of a boat named Regret, laughing. Or I was laughing. It sounded like crying. It may have been both.

Behind us, the valley we'd escaped filled with the monsoon, swallowing the camp, the vault, the manifest, the mantel-box, the whole architecture of my father's legacy and my father's debts. Somewhere in that brown water was the folder that could have cleared my name. Serrano had it or the river did; either way, it was gone from me, and I found—to my genuine astonishment—that I could breathe.

"Your father's life's work," Bea said quietly. She was wringing out her shirt, not looking at me, giving me privacy the way survivors learn to.

"My father's life work," I said, "is three people in a boat."

Tamás didn't say anything. He just put his hand on the tiller and steered us downstream, and after a while his hand found mine on the thwart, and stayed.

It was only later, anchored in the lee of a drowned church tower with the rain finally thinning, that Bea went pale and said, very carefully, "Ivo. When the vault flooded. Before you came up the stairs. Someone was shouting your name. Not the captain's voice."

I turned. Downriver, far off, a single light moved along the high bank—patient, unhurried, following.

Someone knew I was here. Someone had always known.

The compass in my pocket felt suddenly very heavy, and very much like a message.
