## 9. What Burns

The temple rise was the only dry ground for a mile in any direction, which made it either a sanctuary or an altar, and I've never trusted altars.

We came up its flooded stairway in the last grey light, Bea shivering between us with her arms locked around a surveyor's transit case she'd refused to abandon even while drowning was a live possibility. The monsoon had arrived like a creditor—early, loud, and demanding everything. Below us the river had swallowed the lower camp whole. I could still see the tip of the radio mast standing out of the brown water like a grave marker, and I tried not to think about what else was down there wearing the expedition's name.

Ivo built a fire from broken lintel wood and the last of our dry rations' packing paper. She built it the way she did everything: efficiently, without ceremony, as if fire were just another tool and not the small miracle it plainly was on a night like this. Then she sat down across from me, pulled the oilskin packet from inside her jacket, and set it on the stone between us.

The manifest pages. Water-stained at the corners, but legible. Every name, every debt, every transaction her father had made with the collectors—including the entries that would have cleared her. Proof, in her father's cramped handwriting, that Ivo Zhou owed nothing to anyone. That the debt following her bloodline had been his all along, laundered through her name because he'd needed a ledger entry he could disown.

She looked at it for a long time. I looked at her looking at it.

"You could be free," Bea said quietly, from her nest against the transit case. "That's what it does, right? You said."

"It's what it does," Ivo agreed.

"So burn it," I said, before I could stop myself.

Her head came up. In the firelight her face did something complicated—surprise, then suspicion, then something softer that she filed away fast, the way she filed everything.

"You want me to burn my own exoneration?"

"I want you to stop carrying it." I fed a stick to the fire. "There's a difference. Paper doesn't free anybody. I've watched men trade their whole lives for documents that were supposed to make them innocent again. It never works. The document becomes the new debt."

"That's very poetic for a man who patches his hull with canvas and prayer."

"Prayer is underrated. So is canvas."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then she looked back down at the pages, and I saw her thumb move over her father's handwriting the way you'd touch a scar to check if it still hurt.

* * *

Here's the thing about disasters: everyone assumes the worst part is the moment itself. The grounding. The capsizing. The water coming in where water has no business being. It isn't. The worst part is every hour afterward, when you replay it, editing your own decisions, casting yourself in the version of events where you were smart enough or slow enough or brave enough to save them. You become the curator of your own guilt. You keep it dusted. You show it to no one, and it grows in the dark until it's bigger than the disaster ever was.

Nine people. That's the number I don't say. Nine people on the *Mariana V*, the ferry run I'd piloted for eleven years without so much as a scraped fender, the run they gave me because I was careful, because I was the pilot who counted passengers twice and checked the weather three times. And on the night of the flood surge—the one the bureau's forecast missed by six hours, the one every other captain tied up for—I looked at the sky, looked at my schedule, and decided that eleven years of perfect runs meant something. That caution was a habit I'd earned the right to break, just once.

Eight of them are under the river now. The ninth was a boy who grabbed a floating crate and let the current take him somewhere I couldn't follow. They found him two days later, twelve kilometers downstream, alive, sunburned, furious at me for losing his mother.

They took my license. I would have taken more, if they'd offered. What nobody tells you about disgrace is that it's a relief, at first. Someone else finally agrees with the voice in your head. Someone official confirms that yes, you are exactly as bad as you suspected. There's a horrible comfort in it. A verdict means the trial is over.

What brought me back to the river wasn't courage. It was the discovery that I couldn't live away from it—that the sound of moving water through a window was worse than the sound of it under a hull. So I bought the wreck you've been riding in, patched her with canvas and prayer, and started running cargo nobody legitimate would carry, for people who didn't ask questions, because questions were the one cargo I couldn't afford.

That's what I should tell her. That's what I'm going to tell her, I realized, watching her hold those pages like they might hold her back.

Because here's the other thing about disasters: sometimes you meet someone else's, and you recognize it. Not the details—the details are always different—but the shape. The weight distribution of a guilt carried too long. I'd seen it in Ivo from the first night, the way you see a boat riding low in the water. Something heavy below decks. Nobody loads a hull like that by accident.

* * *

"I knew a man once," I said, "who ran a ferry. Eleven years, no incidents. Then one night he decided his luck was a skill instead of a gift, and eight people died for the arithmetic."

Ivo's eyes came up slowly. Bea had gone very still.

"The bureau grounded him," I went on. "Rightly. And he spent years telling himself the forecast was wrong, the river was wrong, the world was wrong—anything except that he was wrong. Because if he was wrong, then those eight people died for nothing, and if they died for nothing—" I shrugged. "You can't put that down. It doesn't fit in any pocket."

"What happened to him?" she asked. Her voice was careful. She already knew. She'd known since the first night, probably, the way I'd known about her compass and her false names and the way she flinched whenever anyone said the word *Zhou* out loud.

"He's sitting on a temple talking to you," I said. "Still wrong. Still carrying it. But lighter than he was, lately, and I've been trying to work out why."

"Why?" Bea whispered.

"Because somebody else on this boat is carrying something heavier," I said, "and watching her carry it makes mine look survivable."

Ivo laughed—a short, cracked sound, nothing like humor. "That's a terrible reason to feel better."

"It's the only honest one I've got."

She looked at the fire. Then at the packet. Then at me, and whatever she found in my face must have been enough, because she exhaled, long and slow, like a diver surfacing.

"My father borrowed money," she said. "Years ago. Before I was born, maybe—he was never clear, and I think clarity would have required admitting things he couldn't admit. He borrowed from collectors. The kind who don't file paperwork with anyone's bureau. And when the interest grew faster than his salary, he started using my name. My signature. I was nineteen. He told me it was temporary. He told me the dig would pay everything back." Her jaw tightened. "He told me a lot of things. I spent seven years digging under false names to service a debt that was never mine, because if I used my real name, they found me. If they found me, they took the wages. And if I stopped paying, they'd have gone after—" She stopped. Started again. "It didn't matter who. Anyone with my blood. That was the terms. The debt follows bloodlines."

"And the manifest proves it was his," I said.

"The manifest proves everything was his. Every artifact sale. Every payment. He wasn't funding an expedition upriver—he was buying himself an exit. The collectors weren't chasing *me*. They were chasing *him*, and using me as collateral because I was the only asset he couldn't sell." She picked up the top page. Her hands were steady. Her voice wasn't, quite. "This clears my name. This ends it. One match, and I lose it forever. One buyer, and I walk away rich and clean and exactly as owned as I've always been."

"How do you figure owned?"

"Because I'd still owe him," she said simply. "Every day I kept those pages safe, I'd be keeping *his* story intact. His debts, his crimes, his cleverness—all of it preserved, archived, honored. As long as this paper exists, I'm his daughter the way a vault is a vault. Sealed. Guarded. Full of his valuables." She turned the page over. "I don't want to be a vault anymore."

* * *

Bea was asleep by then, curled against the transit case with her cheek on her folded jacket, and the rain had settled into the steady, patient drumming it would keep all night. Ivo stood, walked to the edge of the rise where the floodwater lapped the lowest step, and stood there holding the packet over the black water.

I didn't go to her. Some doors you have to open yourself, and some fires you have to light yourself, and standing behind someone breathing on their neck helps neither.

But I heard her talk. Low, half to the river, half to herself—or to him, wherever he was, if he was anywhere, which the river keeps to itself along with everything else.

"You could have told me," she said. "One letter. One honest sentence. *Ivo, I am in trouble, Ivo, I lied.* Seven years. Seven years I thought I was the coward, hiding under other names, and the whole time the man whose shadow I was hiding from was the one who pushed me into it." A breath. "I used to think if I dug deep enough I'd find something true underneath. Some artifact of you that was actually you. And there isn't one. There's just this. Paper. Transactions. You were a ledger all the way down."

She struck the match. I watched the flame climb her fingers' worth of air, and I watched her hesitate—one heartbeat, two—and I understood the hesitation completely. It wasn't temptation. It was grief. Burning a thing is easy; burning the last physical proof that someone you loved existed at all, that's different. That's a second funeral, and this one you attend alone.

Then she let it go.

The pages caught on the wind off the water, flared orange, and rose—burning birds, a dozen of them, climbing into the rain. Most drowned in the river. A few made it higher before the rain killed them. Ivo watched every one go down.

When she turned around, her face was wet, and not entirely from the weather, and she didn't try to hide it, which from Ivo Zhou was its own kind of confession.

"Well," she said, wiping her nose with the back of her wrist. "Now I'm nobody's daughter, nobody's debtor, and nobody's vault."

"Now you're whoever you decide to be tomorrow," I said. "That's the whole trick. Nobody tells you. There's no ceremony. You just wake up and choose."

"That's terrifying."

"Immensely. I've been terrified for nine years. It beats being guilty."

She came back to the fire and sat down beside me—not across, beside, close enough that her shoulder touched mine and stayed there, which I decided not to remark upon, because some cargoes you acknowledge by carrying them quietly.

"Tell me about the ninth one," she said, after a while. "The boy on the crate."

I told her. All of it—the surge, the decision, the water, the twelve kilometers I searched until my hands were shredded, the fury on that boy's sunburned face when they pulled him off the rescue launch and he looked at me like I'd personally sunk the world. And how I'd tracked him down a year later, found him working a dock in the delta, and apologized, and how he'd said the strangest thing: *You're the pilot. You're supposed to drown with us. That's the job.* And how I'd gone home and thought about it for a month before I understood he was right, and that understanding it was the beginning of being able to come back to the water at all.

When I finished, she was quiet for a long moment.

"Thank you," she said.

"For what? It's not a pleasant story."

"For trusting me with it." She looked into the fire. "Nobody's trusted me with anything except debt in seven years."

Above us the rain worked the old temple stones, patient as erosion. Below us the river held everything it had taken—the camp, the artifacts, the manifest's drowned ashes, and a man named Zhou whose fate it intended to keep. Somewhere downstream, the collectors were regrouping, and Bea's family was waiting, and a season of runs was waiting after that, and none of it was solved. But the fire was warm, and the woman beside me was lighter by one funeral, and for the first time in nine years, so was I.

Ivo leaned her head against my shoulder. "Tomorrow," she said, "we get Bea home."

"Tomorrow," I agreed, "we go downstream."

And far below us, faint through the rain and the dark, the river answered—moving, always moving, carrying what it carries, letting go of what it won't.
