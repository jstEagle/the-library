## 10. Downstream

The river gives you back changed, or it doesn't give you back at all. That's what my father used to say, standing on the banks of a dozen different waterways with his hat pushed back and that look on his face like he was reading a letter addressed to someone else. I spent thirty years assuming he meant it as a metaphor. Turns out he meant it literally. The river took him and gave me back instead—thinner, salted with silt, carrying nothing but a compass and a woman named Bea who slept against the gunwale like the boat was the first safe place she'd found in months.

We came down out of the high water slow. Tamás wouldn't push the engine after what we'd asked of it in the rapids, so we drifted through the drowned country at the speed of forgiveness, past villages where people stood on their roofs and waved us off because they'd already been rescued twice and didn't trust a third boat. Bea woke when the current gentled, sat up with her hair full of burrs, and looked at me like she was checking whether I was still real.

"You're going to have to tell them your name eventually," she said.

"I know."

"The real one."

"I said I know." I was coiling rope that didn't need coiling. It's amazing how much work your hands can invent when your mouth is being asked to do something difficult.

Tamás said nothing from the wheel, which was his way of saying everything. He had a talent for silence the way other men had a talent for music. He let it sit there between us, patient as ballast, until I finally put the rope down and admitted what we both already knew: that "Ivo Zhou" was a name I hadn't answered to in six years, that I'd buried it under four others like bodies in shallow graves, and that digging it up was going to hurt more than anything the river had done to us.

"Good," Tamás said.

"That's not the word most people would use."

"Most people haven't watched you burn a manifest to keep a promise you never made." He adjusted the throttle a quarter inch, eyes on the channel ahead. "Names are just hulls, Ivo. What matters is whether they float."

* * *

Bea's family lived up a tributary in a town called Sengkar, which means, according to Bea, either "place of the white heron" or "place where the ferryman overcharges you," depending on which grandmother you asked. We arrived on a morning so bright it felt like an apology, and her mother came down to the dock before we'd even tied off, running the last fifty meters with her skirt hiked in her fists, and I learned something about grief that day: it doesn't end when the person comes home. It ends when you can finally stop holding the shape of their absence.

Bea's mother held her daughter and then, over her shoulder, held me too, which I did not expect and did not handle gracefully. Then she held Tamás, who accepted it the way a dock accepts a mooring line—without resistance, without comment—and then she stood back and looked at the three of us like we were cargo she intended to inspect.

"You're the archaeologist's daughter," she said.

Here it was, then. Sooner than I'd planned. Word travels faster than boats in this country; the collectors' men must have talked, or the survivors of the camp had scattered stories like seed grain, and somewhere between the flood line and this dock my name had sprouted.

"Ivo Zhou," I said.

It came out steady. That surprised me more than anything else that season. Six years of aliases—Mara Voss, Elena Reyes, K. Tran, a half-dozen others scribbled into site ledgers by supervisors who never looked twice—and here was my own name, spoken aloud on a public dock in daylight, and the sky didn't fall. The collectors didn't materialize. My father didn't rise dripping from the water to claim me.

Just a woman nodding, and a girl grinning, and Tamás beside me not smiling but doing something better, which was looking at me like a man watching a hull take its first load and hold.

Inside, over rice and river fish and a soup Bea's grandmother made that could strip paint or heal wounds depending on dosage, we told the story. Not all of it. Some of it belongs to the river now, and some of it belongs only to the three of us—the cistern, the vault flooding, the pages going into the fire on the temple rise while Tamás finally told me about the ferry disaster that broke him, eleven souls, a captain's order he obeyed, and I told him about the debt, the real debt, the one I'd run from: money my father borrowed in my name when I was nineteen, because his credit was gone and mine was clean, and how the collectors had followed the bloodline ever since, patient as erosion.

The claim died with the manifest. Everyone at that table understood it. Without those pages proving the loan's terms, the collectors' leverage dissolved into legal fog—they couldn't collect on a debt whose documentation rested at the bottom of a flooded valley. Bea's uncle, who apparently handled such matters for half the province, confirmed it over tea with the grim satisfaction of a man who enjoyed telling creditors bad news.

"They'll come sniffing," he warned. "Men like that don't accept drowning as an answer."

"Let them sniff," Tamás said. "They'll find a burned ledger and a pilot with nothing left to lose."

"And an archaeologist," Bea added, "who is apparently very good at making things disappear."

I raised my teacup to that. It was, I decided, the first toast of my actual life.

* * *

My father's fate stayed where the river wanted it. The university sent two inquiries, then a representative, then a form letter expressing condolences and requesting any personal effects for archival purposes. I sent them the compass.

No—I'm lying. I thought about sending them the compass. I sat with the box and the twine for a whole evening in Sengkar, turning it over, imagining the curator's hands on the last thing my father ever touched. And then I imagined some graduate student cataloguing it—*brass field compass, property of Dr. Elias Zhou, recovered from downstream, provenance uncertain*—and something in my chest closed like a fist.

He'd sold the artifacts. He'd forged my signature on loan papers. He'd abandoned his students to buy himself passage out of his own catastrophe, and whatever end met him upriver—a flood, a deal gone wrong, a quiet walk into the current—he'd chosen it over coming home to face what he'd made. I owed the archive nothing. I owed his memory less. But the compass wasn't his memory. The compass was the one thing he'd given me that pointed true, and I'd earned it honestly, in the water, with my own hands.

So I kept it. First honest possession of my adult life. It rides in my breast pocket now, and sometimes, when the current gets tricky, I take it out and check north—not because I need to, but because it's good to hold a thing that has never lied to you.

* * *

The *Regret* needed three weeks of work. Tamás and I hauled her out at a yard upriver from Sengkar where the owner charged fair rates and asked no questions, possibly because Tamás had once pulled him out of a flood, possibly because asking questions of Tamás Reyes was widely understood to be a poor investment. We re-planked the port side, replaced the shaft seal, rebuilt the carburetor from parts Tamás produced from a crate labeled SPARES with the confidence of a man performing surgery with tools he'd hidden inside the patient.

I worked beside him every day. My hands remembered things I didn't know they knew—my father had kept me around engines as a child, one of his few reliable gifts—and by the second week I was doing the shaft alignment alone while he supervised from a lawn chair with a beer, offering commentary of the quality you'd expect from a man who communicated primarily in silence.

"You're enjoying this," I said, wiping grease on a rag that was mostly grease itself.

"No."

"You're smiling."

"That's sunstroke."

On the last day, with the *Regret* sitting in the slings ready to go back in the water, Tamás climbed down into the bilge one final time, checked something for a long while, and came up holding a small tin box I'd never seen. He opened it on the deck between us. Inside: papers. Registration, insurance, a bill of sale, all bearing the same name.

*Tamás Reyes & Partner.*

The partner line was blank.

"I can't sign that," I said. "There's no partner."

"There will be." He handed me the pen. "Next season starts in six weeks. High water runs are double pay and triple trouble, and I need someone who can read channels, fix engines, and lie beautifully to customs officers. You're overqualified."

"Tamás—"

"It's a business arrangement," he said, with the elaborate casualness of a man describing anything but. "You owe me nothing. That's rather the point. Everything you've done since the temple rise, you've done free and clear. This is just—" he gestured at the river, the yard, the light coming down gold through the work shed, "—the first thing anyone's ever offered you that you didn't have to run from."

I looked at the blank line for a long time. Longer than the pen deserved. Because signing it meant signing my real name next to his, in ink, on paper that would exist in offices and filing cabinets and government registries—paper that could be found, traced, read. A visible life. An accountable one.

Six years ago I would have thrown the pen in the river.

I signed.

*Ivo Zhou*, in handwriting I barely recognized as mine, above the printed words *Partner*. Tamás took the pen back, blotted it carefully, and put the papers away like they were worth more than everything the vault had drowned.

"For the record," I said, "if this partnership goes the way of your last crew, I'm renegotiating."

"My last crew quit because I'm impossible to work with."

"That tracks."

He almost smiled. On Tamás, an almost-smile was a parade.

* * *

We launched at dawn on the first of the month, Bea waving from the dock with her surveyor's kit already packed for a university job she'd sworn she'd take only if it involved dry land, her mother beside her holding a bag of food sufficient for a voyage to Antarctica. The *Regret* sat high and clean in the brown water, patched and repainted, REGRET still lettered on the stern because Tamás refused to change it—"a boat should be named for what it cost you," he said, "so you remember what you're carrying"—and I stood at the wheel while he cast off, and for the first time in my adult life, leaving a dock felt like arriving somewhere.

The channel ahead ran wide and slow through the flooded green country. Somewhere upriver, under all that water, my father's ruins were keeping their new secrets, and the collectors were discovering that some debts dissolve, and the river went on doing what rivers do, which is take everything and pretend it gave you something in exchange.

But it had. That was the joke of it. The river had taken my name, my inheritance, my father, and the last shreds of the life I'd built on hiding—and it had given back a compass, a partner, a girl alive because we came for her, and a boat named Regret that floated better than she had any right to.

Tamás came and stood beside me at the wheel, close enough that our shoulders touched, and said nothing, which from him was a speech.

"North by northeast?" I asked.

"North by northeast." He glanced down at my pocket, where the compass rode. "You'll navigate?"

"I'll navigate."

And we turned the bow toward the open water, and the *Regret* carried us out—two people with nothing left to hide from each other, one name between us on the registration, and the whole drowned, dangerous, generous river ahead.
