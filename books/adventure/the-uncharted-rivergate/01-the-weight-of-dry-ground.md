## 1. The Weight of Dry Ground

The delta lowlands woke slowly that morning, the way they always did—first a gray hush, then the herons lifting off the shallows like torn paper, then the smell of mud and diesel and drying fish that clung to everything, including Marguerite Winterbourne's hair, her clothes, her dreams. She had been awake since four, lying in her bunk aboard the *Corvina* with the ceiling fan turning uselessly overhead, listening to the river breathe against the hull. Ten years on the water had taught her the difference between the river's ordinary voices and its warnings. This morning it was only breathing. Ordinary. Safe.

She rose before the crew, as she always did, and stood at the rail with her coffee going cold in her hands, watching the mist peel off the water in long slow ribbons. The survey season was nearly over. Three months of soundings, sediment cores, channel maps for a dredging consortium that paid promptly and asked nothing. It was honest work, or close enough to it. It kept them fed, kept the boat patched, kept her name out of journals and off conference panels and away from the people who still, after all this time, wanted to ask her about her father.

That was the whole architecture of her life, if she was honest: a structure built around an absence. She had become very good at it.

Marguerite Winterbourne, thirty-four, ran a survey crew of five in waters most academics wouldn't sully their boots for. No institutional affiliation. No grant applications. No lectures. When colleagues from the old days wrote asking her to contribute to festschriftons and symposium volumes, she didn't answer. When students emailed asking about the Rivergate—the drowned temple complex upriver in the monsoon country, the site where Dr. Elias Winterbourne had vanished without remains, without wreckage, without so much as a final radio transmission—she deleted those too. There were perhaps six people alive who knew what she looked like, and she preferred it that way. Her father had spent his whole brilliant, ruinous life wanting to be seen. She had built hers on the opposite principle.

The crew called her Skipper, which suited her fine. A title with no history attached.

By six-thirty the deck was alive. Odile Fournier, her boatman—a broad-shouldered woman of fifty-odd with forearms like mooring cable and a face that suggested she'd been disappointed by every institution ever created—was already at the engine hatch, cursing in three languages at the fuel line. Sefu Okonkwo, twenty-three, the tech, sat cross-legged near the bow calibrating the sonar array, his laptop propped against a coil of rope, headphones leaking some noise she pretended not to hear. Priya Raghavan, the medic, emerged last from the cabin, hair unbound, carrying two mugs and handing one to Odile without being asked, because that was Priya's way: she noticed things nobody asked her to notice.

They were not friends, exactly. They were something more durable than friends—people who had chosen each other across the gap of ordinary life, the way survivors choose. Odile had lost a fishing license and then a marriage and then, in roughly that order, her faith in anyone holding a clipboard. Sefu had washed out of two university programs and carried his rejection letters laminated in his wallet like talismans, proof that he'd survived worse than monsoon season. Priya had left a hospital job after a night she never talked about, and now stitched up fishermen and treated fevers and said things like *the body keeps its own records* when she thought no one was listening.

And Marguerite—Marguerite kept them all at exactly the distance that let her sleep at night. Close enough to trust with her life. Far enough that none of them could reach the sealed room inside her where her father lived, still living, ten years gone and never once mentioned above the waterline of casual conversation.

Until this morning.

* * *

The mail came by launch, twice a week, from the river town of San Esteban—two hours downstream if the current was kind. The pilot, a laconic man named Bruno who had delivered their supplies for six years, tossed the canvas mailbag onto the deck with the same indifference he tossed everything, and Marguerite sorted through it standing up, as always: invoices, a parts catalog, a postcard from Priya's sister in Kochi, a government notice about revised channel markers that Sefu would need to update the charts.

At the bottom of the bag, wedged beneath a roll of tide tables, was an envelope that stopped her hands.

It was heavy stock, cream-colored, the kind of paper that cost more per sheet than their entire monthly diesel budget. It had been forwarded twice—two sets of stamps, two addresses crossed out in two different hands—and the final address, in careful block capitals, read: M. WINTERBOURNE, C/O SURVEY VESSEL CORVINA, SAN ESTEBAN POST, DELTA DISTRICT. Someone had taken trouble to find her. Someone who understood that a woman running a survey boat under a company registration rather than a university appointment would be hard to locate, and had located her anyway.

But none of that was what stopped her hands.

What stopped her hands was the handwriting on the front. The address block itself—not the forwarding labels, not the postal marks, but the name written in ink above the printed label—was in a hand she would have known blindfolded, underwater, at gunpoint, at the bottom of her own grave. A slanted, impatient script, letters leaning forward as if the writer were always in a hurry to get somewhere else. The capital W with its peculiar doubled stroke. The way the r's hooked like little anchors.

Her father's handwriting.

Elias Winterbourne had been dead—or disappeared, which in the delta amounted to the same thing with fewer documents—for ten years, four months, and eleven days. Marguerite knew the count the way some people know the number of days sober. She did not mark the anniversary. She simply knew it, the way you know a wound is there under weatherproof skin.

She stood on the deck holding the envelope for a long moment while the crew moved around her, oblivious, and the river went on doing what rivers do, which is everything, indifferently. Then she turned it over. The return address was a law office. Beneath it, in smaller print, in a different hand entirely—neat, cold, typewritten or printed with a fountain pen by someone who liked the look of control—was a single line:

*Forwarded at the request of E. Voss.*

Edmund Voss. She knew the name the way you know the name of a storm season. Her father's oldest rival, his sharpest critic, his—depending on which account you believed and which decade—closest friend, bitterest enemy, or both simultaneously, which was probably the truth. Voss had published the review that gutted her father's first book. Voss had also, according to her mother's few bitter recollections, been the one person who wrote to Elias every year, every December, whether Elias answered or not. Twenty years of letters into silence. Marguerite had never decided what that made him. She had made sure never to find out.

Now his name was on an envelope bearing her father's handwriting, addressed to her, and the past had found an address.

She took it below, alone, and shut the cabin door, which she never did in daylight unless something was wrong.

* * *

The letter inside was short. That was the first shock; she had braced herself for pages, for a testament, for the rambling unfinished magnum opus of a man who had died mid-sentence. Instead: one page, written—she judged by the ink and the pressure—in the last weeks, maybe the last days. Paper that had traveled through at least two postal systems and someone's archive and a lawyer's filing cabinet and arrived smelling faintly of camphor, as if it had spent years in a drawer with someone else's secrets.

The handwriting was looser than she remembered. Shaky in places. The letters of a man writing fast, or tired, or both.

*Margerite*—he had misspelled her name, dropped the u, the way he always did when rushing, and she felt something twist in her chest at the sight of it, because it meant he wasn't performing. He wasn't writing to posterity. He was writing to his daughter, hurriedly, badly, the way you write when you think you might not get another chance.

The text, transcribed here as she read it that morning, knees drawn up on the cabin bunk, coffee abandoned, door bolted against a crew that must not see her cry:

> My girl —
>
> If this reaches you, I am beyond retrieval, and you will be angry with me, and you will be right. I've left instructions with Voss, of all people, because he is the only one stubborn enough to carry them out and honest enough to hate me properly while doing it.
>
> Do not come looking. That is the whole of my request and I know you will ignore it, because you are mine. But understand what I understood too late: the Rivergate does not give back what it takes. Not men. Not answers. Not even grief. It keeps everything and returns nothing but water.
>
> I found the stair. I want you to know that, wherever I am. Forty years I looked for it and in the end it was exactly where the old flood-charts said it would be, which is either a joke the universe played on me or a lesson, and I no longer have time to work out which.
>
> Whatever Voss offers you — and he will offer, he cannot help himself — remember that he loved me. Remember also that loving me is what ruined half his life, and mine, and possibly yours. Love is not a reason. It has never once been a reason.
>
> Burn this if reading it hurts more than keeping it. I mean that literally. I would rather it be ash in your hands than a chain.
>
> — Your father, who was wrong about nearly everything except the water.

She read it three times. Then a fourth. Then she folded it along its original creases—which were soft, worn, as though the letter had been unfolded and refolded many times before it was ever posted, as though someone had read it repeatedly over ten years, and she found herself wondering, with a coldness that surprised her, whether that someone was Voss—and put it in the chart drawer next to the sextant, behind the spare batteries, in the place where she kept the three objects she could not explain to another living soul: her mother's ring, a child's drawing of a river monster she'd made at age seven, and now this.

Then she went back on deck and did not tell anyone, and made herself useful, and checked the fuel line Odile was fighting with, and laughed at Sefu's joke about the government's new channel markers, and let Priya refill her coffee without comment, and the day proceeded as days do, and the crew noticed nothing, because she was very, very good at this.

But at night, lying in her bunk with the fan turning and the letter burning a hole in the chart drawer three feet from her head, she lay awake and did the arithmetic she had sworn off a decade ago.

Voss had held this letter for ten years. Or had he? The forwarding stamps told a story: first mailed somewhere upcountry, returned, readdressed, forwarded again, sitting in offices, migrating. Maybe it had only just escaped. Maybe Voss had released it deliberately, now, for a reason. And underneath that question, darker, the one she could not stop circling: her father's letter said *I found the stair.* Which meant that in the final days of his life—if these were the final days—he had known where the entrance to the Rivergate was. Had stood on it. Had perhaps descended. Had vanished into it, or beside it, or beneath it, and left behind a letter asking his daughter not to follow.

Nobody in the history of the Winterbourne family had ever obeyed that kind of instruction. It was practically the family crest: a man drowning, waving away rescue.

She got up at two in the morning and stood at the rail in the dark, watching the black water slide past, and let herself say the thing aloud for the first time in ten years, quietly, to no one, to the river:

"You found him."

The river said nothing. Rivers never do. That was the thing about them her father had understood at the end, the thing she was only beginning to understand now: they keep everything, and they don't talk.

* * *

Three days later, the supply launch brought more mail, and with it a telegram—actual paper telegram, which in the current decade was either eccentric or deliberate, and with Voss she suspected deliberate—requesting, in formal language, a meeting at the Hotel Meridian in San Esteban, at her earliest convenience, regarding "a matter of mutual professional interest concerning the upper valley."

She read it on deck, in full view of the crew this time, because secrecy had a cost too and she was starting to feel the edges of it. Odile saw the name on the wire and her face did something complicated—Odile knew who Edmund Voss was; everyone in the delta trade knew; he owned interests in two shipping lines and a salvage outfit and had a reputation for paying well and owning what he paid for. Sefu whistled low. Priya said nothing, but watched Marguerite's hands, which had gone very still around the paper.

"Triple rates," Odile said flatly, reading the room and the telegram in one pass. "If it's Voss, it'll be money. Question is what he wants bought."

"I know," Marguerite said, and crumpled the wire in her fist, and threw it in the river, which was theatrical and stupid and exactly the kind of thing her father would have done, and she caught herself doing it and hated the recognition, the way you hate seeing your own face in a photograph you didn't pose for.

That evening she sat in the stern with the letter again, rereading the line about Voss—*whatever Voss offers you, and he will offer, he cannot help himself*—and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that her father had written those words knowing they would arrive late. Knowing they would arrive after the offer. He had timed nothing, because he couldn't—but the letter's delay was itself a message. By the time she read his warning, the trap he described would already be sprung, or about to be. He had known her well enough to know she'd read it anyway. He had known Voss well enough to know the man would wait her out.

Two rivals, dead and living, playing a game across ten years of water, and she was the board.

She slept badly. In the morning she told Odile to make ready for the run downriver to San Esteban—routine resupply, she said, which was true, and also a lie, and both of them knew it, and neither said so, because that was how Odile and Marguerite communicated: in the space between a statement and its meaning.

The *Corvina* cast off at dawn. The mist was thick, the current sluggish, the engine note steady for once. Sefu ran the depth sounder and called out numbers. Priya sat on the roof of the cabin reading a paperback with a cracked spine. Odile steered and smoked and occasionally muttered at floating debris.

And Marguerite stood at the bow with the river opening ahead of her—downriver, toward town, toward the hotel, toward Voss, toward whatever her father had set in motion a decade ago with a page of shaky ink and a misspelled name—watching the banks narrow and widen and narrow again, feeling the weight of dry ground under her feet for the first time in weeks, and knowing, with a certainty she could not yet justify to herself, that the dry ground was not going to hold.

The letter had found its address. Now the address would have to decide what it was worth.
