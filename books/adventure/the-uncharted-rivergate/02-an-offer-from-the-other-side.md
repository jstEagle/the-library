## 2. An Offer from the Other Side

He came upriver on a Thursday, in a boat too clean for the water.

Marguerite saw it first as a smudge against the brown current, a white hull with a canvas canopy and an outboard running quiet and expensive, and her stomach dropped before her mind caught up—because nothing that clean came this far into the delta without wanting something, and the only thing worth wanting out here was her.

"Company," Odile said from the engine well, not looking up from the carburetor she'd had apart since dawn. Her hands were black with grease and steady as bedrock. "Government?"

"Government boats fly flags." Marguerite shaded her eyes. "That's money."

Sefu emerged from the survey tent with the total station's tripod still over his shoulder like a rifle, twenty-two years old and allergic to stillness. "Is it the ministry? Because if it's the ministry about the boundary markers again, I told Priya I'd handle it—"

"It's not the ministry."

"How do you know?"

"Because I can smell the cologne from here."

The boat swung wide at their mooring, courteous, deliberate, and a man stepped onto the mud-slick landing stage as though the river had been built for his convenience. He was sixty or thereabouts, tall without stooping, silver at the temples in a way that looked maintained rather than earned. Linen shirt, sleeves rolled to the forearm with precision. A single waterproof case under one arm, hard-sided, the kind surveyors carried instruments in and men like him carried leverage.

Edmund Voss.

She had seen him last at a memorial service eleven years ago, standing at the back of a hall full of grieving academics, and even then he had not looked like a mourner. He had looked like a man taking inventory.

"Ms. Winterbourne." His voice carried across the water without effort. "Thank you for receiving me."

"I didn't receive you. You came anyway." She stayed on the bank, arms crossed, letting the mud be visible on her boots. "You could have written again."

"I did write. You burned my letter." He smiled faintly, as if the burning were a point in his favor. "I find fire is usually a yes that hasn't admitted itself yet."

Behind her, Sefu made a small sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Marguerite did not turn around.

"You have until your fuel runs low," she said. "Say what you came to say."

Voss glanced past her at the camp—the sagging mess tarp, the drying nets, Odile's boat riding low with survey gear, the little cluster of instruments that constituted everything Marguerite had built in ten years of staying deliberately, aggressively unremarkable. She watched him read it all the way he'd once read her father: quickly, completely, and without mercy.

"A working camp," he said. "Not a famous one. No university banner, no grant numbers stenciled on the crates. You've done an admirable job of disappearing, Ms. Winterbourne. Your father would be proud of the craft and heartbroken at the purpose."

"My father is dead."

"Your father is *missing*," Voss said gently, "which is a different country entirely, and I have been its ambassador longer than you have been its exile."

* * *

They sat under the mess tarp because she refused to invite him into the tent, and because Odile refused to leave, and because Priya appeared from the medical stores with her arms folded and the particular stillness of a woman triaging a situation. Sefu hovered at the edge of the tarp pretending to coil rope. Nobody coiled rope that badly by accident.

Voss drank the camp coffee without flinching, which Marguerite resented. It was terrible coffee. It should have been a weapon.

"The Kestrel Valley Dam," he began. "You've heard of it."

"Everyone's heard of it. The ministry announced it two years ago and has broken ground on none of it."

"They broke ground in March. Quietly. The funding shifted hands after the elections, and now it is real—concrete contracts, resettlement schedules, a completion date." He set down his cup. "In fourteen months, the coffer dams close. In eighteen, the valley begins to fill. Every village, every forest, every ruin between the confluence and the high gorge goes under ninety meters of water. Permanently."

"That's a tragedy for the villages," Marguerite said. "For ruins, it depends on the ruin."

"Don't do that." For the first time something moved behind his composure—not anger, but its exhausted cousin. "Don't perform indifference. I knew Elias Winterbourne for thirty years. I reviewed his first paper when you were learning long division. I know exactly what you are, because you are precisely what he was, wearing better camouflage."

The rain drummed on the tarp. Somewhere downriver a heron screamed.

"What I am," Marguerite said evenly, "is a survey crew that maps channels and boundaries for people who pay on time. That's all."

"Then this will be simple business." Voss unlatched the waterproof case. "The government, sensibly, wants a salvage record before the flooding. Nothing elaborate—an archaeological assessment, site documentation, a determination of whether anything in the valley warrants rescue excavation before the reservoir closes over it. They've contracted the preliminary phase to a private firm. Mine." He drew out a folder, oilcloth-wrapped, swollen slightly with old damp. "I need someone to lead the field team. Someone who knows the upper gorge, who can run a boat through the monsoon narrows, whose name carries weight with the local crews. Triple your best annual contract. Four months' work. And the satisfaction—" here his voice did something almost delicate, almost kind, which was worse than cruelty— "of finishing what your father started."

"I don't want to finish anything of my father's."

"No," Voss agreed. "You want to burn it. Which is why I'm offering you the match."

The tarp went very quiet. Even the rain seemed to lean in.

Marguerite kept her face still, but her pulse had gone loud in her ears. *How does he know that.* Eleven years of careful nothing—no publications, no conference appearances, no whispered ambitions—and this man sat drinking bad coffee in her camp and named her secret like a menu item.

"You're wondering how I know," Voss said, watching her. "Your father wrote to me, near the end. Did you know that? The last letter I ever received from Elias Winterbourne was four pages about hydrology and half a page about you. He said you'd inherited his stubbornness and none of his curiosity, and that someday you'd come upriver anyway, because the blood always comes upriver. He asked me to look after you if it happened." A pause. "I intend to honor that. In my own fashion."

"He vanished six weeks after that letter."

"Yes."

"And you never went looking."

"I went looking for three years," Voss said, and for a moment the polish thinned enough to show the scar tissue underneath. "I spent more money than you will earn in your life dragging gorges with divers and helicopters. I found his second-to-last camp. I found his spare spectacles. I found a boot. What I did not find was him, or the Rivergate, or proof that either existed outside his magnificent, maddening certainty." He straightened. "And then the government decided to drown the question forever, and I discovered I was not willing to let them."

"So you need a scapegoat with the right surname."

"I need the best field leader on this river," Voss said, "and I am prepared to pay for her, flatter her, or fight her, in whatever order she prefers. But before you answer—" he slid the oilcloth folder across the folding table, and his hand rested on it one heartbeat longer than necessary— "you should see what I'm actually offering. Not the money. This."

He pushed it toward her and withdrew his hand.

Marguerite looked at the folder and did not touch it.

She knew that handwriting. She had grown up beneath it—on chalkboards, on grocery lists, on the margins of her school essays where he'd corrected her grammar with more love than correction deserved. Ten years dead or missing, and Elias Winterbourne's handwriting could still reach across a table and take his daughter by the throat.

"That's his field book," she said. Her voice came out flat, which took work. "From the last season."

"The final one. Recovered from the waystation cache at Kiln Bend—I'll show you the recovery coordinates if you doubt me. His official disappearance date is eleven years ago this autumn. This log ends nine days *after* that date." Voss let that sit. "He was alive, Ms. Winterbourne. Alive, writing, working—nine days past the day the world decided he was gone. Whatever happened to him happened inside the Rivergate. And whatever he found in there is in these pages."

"Why show me at all?" she asked. "If you want me to lead your dig, why hand me the one thing guaranteed to make me refuse?"

"Because I'd rather have you refusing honestly than accepting blindly. And because—" he paused, and the pause cost him something; she watched it cost him— "because I promised him I'd let you decide. Not your grief. You."

Marguerite stood up.

"No," she said.

Voss blinked. For the first time since the boat had appeared, he looked genuinely off balance.

"Excuse me?"

"No. I don't lead salvage surveys for dam companies. I don't go upriver. I don't open that folder, and I don't finish, start, or burn anything of my father's, because my father chose a drowned temple over a daughter and I have chosen not to compete." She walked him toward the edge of the tarp, past Odile—who rose, unhurried, a woman ending a meeting with her body if not her words—and Priya, and Sefu, who had stopped pretending about the rope entirely. "Triple pay doesn't buy what you're asking. There isn't a number."

Voss stood in the rain-shadow at the tarp's edge and regarded her for a long moment. Water dripped off the canvas between them, patient as a metronome.

"Your father said you'd say exactly that," he remarked. "Word for word, nearly."

"Then he knew me better than you do."

"He knew you better than anyone alive," Voss said quietly. "Which is why he left instructions for the version of you that says no." He picked up his case but left the folder on the table, oilcloth darkening with damp, patient as everything else about him. "Keep it. Read it or burn it—that's yours to decide, and I meant what I said about the match. My offer stands for thirty days. After that, I hire someone lesser, the valley gets documented badly, and in eighteen months the water closes over the only answers either of us will ever get."

At the landing stage he paused, one foot on his clean white deck, and turned back.

"For what it's worth," he said, "he talked about you the way other men talk about God. With faith, and fear, and no expectation of being answered." Then he stepped aboard, and the outboard woke, and the white boat shrank upriver into the rain until the river closed behind it like a mouth.

* * *

She burned dinner. Not deliberately—she simply stood at the camp stove with rice going to carbon while the folder sat on the supply crate three meters away, and Odile finally took the pan out of her hands without comment and finished cooking herself, because Odile had been finishing things Marguerite abandoned since before Sefu was born.

Night came down on the delta the way it always did, fast and total, the frogs starting their electric chorus, the generator humming its one flat note. Priya retired to the medical tent. Sefu lay in his bunk reading a battered paperback by headlamp, and every ten minutes Marguerite could feel him not-asking her a question.

The folder sat where Voss had left it.

She made coffee at midnight—the good kind, hoarded, real—and carried the cup and the folder to the bow of the moored boat, because she could not do what she was about to do on dry ground. Dry ground had opinions. Dry ground had a decade of carefully constructed silence in it. The river, at least, only ran forward.

She unwrapped the oilcloth.

Inside: a standard surveyor's field book, green cover, spine cracked, pages furred with damp at the edges. The label in her father's hand—*RIVERGATE EXPEDITION, FINAL SEASON*—and beneath it, smaller, the thing that stopped her breath:

*For M., if she ever stops running.*

Her coffee went cold untouched.

She read.

The early entries were ordinary and therefore unbearable—depth soundings, tributary flow rates, complaints about the cook, a sketch of a kingfisher with a note in the margin (*M. would insist this is a belted, not a ringed. He is wrong, but I miss arguing with her about it.*). She read them with her jaw tight, hearing his voice so clearly it was obscene, eleven years of silence collapsing in a single evening like a bad coffer dam.

Then the entries changed.

By the third week of the final season the surveys gave way to speculation. He'd found cut stone in the gorge walls below the high-water line—dressed blocks, mortared, *impossible*, because no known culture in the region had built with mortar. He'd found a stair. He wrote about the stair the way mystics write about light. And then, in an entry dated nine days past his official death, the handwriting loosening with excitement or exhaustion or both:

*The tablets are real. Hundreds of them. A records-vault, deliberately flooded—deliberately, M., they opened the gates themselves rather than surrender the archive. A civilization that chose drowning over conquest. Who does that? Who looks at the water rising and decides the library matters more than the city?*

*I think I understand them. I think I have become them.*

*If you are reading this, then either Voss kept his promise or you disobeyed me, and knowing you it is the latter, and I find I am proud of that too.*

*But listen to me. Listen, because I may not get to say it twice. Some doors are load-bearing. Some inheritances are traps dressed as gifts. I have spent thirty years digging toward this place and I no longer know whether I am its discoverer or its next exhibit. If the water takes me, let the water keep me. Do not dig for my bones. Do not trade your life for my mistake.*

*Go home, little fish. The river is not a road. It is a mouth.*

Marguerite sat in the dark at the bow of her boat until the frogs went quiet and the sky went grey, and she read the field book twice more, cover to cover, and somewhere in the smallest hours she understood—with the terrible clarity that arrives only after midnight and only once—that Edmund Voss had played her perfectly.

Because she had meant to refuse. She had refused, loudly, with witnesses.

And now she knew what was in the valley. She knew her father had been alive nine days past the day the world buried him. She knew there were tablets down there—records, voices, a whole civilization's testimony sealed underwater like letters in a bottle—and she knew that in eighteen months a reservoir would close over them forever, and that Voss's crew would document them badly or blast them carelessly or sell the story to whoever paid, and that she would spend the rest of her life above ground knowing it, which was, she realized, exactly the life she'd been living already, just with the wound reopened.

He hadn't offered her triple pay. He'd offered her the one thing money couldn't buy and pride couldn't refuse: a reason.

Dawn came up the color of tin. Odile found her there, still in yesterday's clothes, the field book closed on her knees, and read the answer in her face before Marguerite said a word.

"Well?" the old woman asked.

Marguerite looked upriver, where the gorge waited with its drowned stair and its sealed archive and whatever was left of her father, and felt the dry ground tilt beneath her exactly as she'd known it would.

"Wake the others," she said. "We're going to need a bigger boat."
