## 3. Crew of Strays

The letter burned in my duffel bag for six days before I made the call.

Not burned—of course not burned. I'd read it forty times, held it to the light looking for watermarks, traced the pressure of the pen where my father had pressed too hard on the downstrokes, the way he always did when he was excited. *Marguerite. If you're reading this, then Voss found you first, and I'm sorry for that too.* Six days of carrying it around camp like a coal in my pocket, and I hadn't told a soul.

But I'd started packing.

I told myself it was practical. If I was going to refuse Voss properly—refuse him in a way that would stick, that would make him stop looking at me with my father's face ghosted over his ambition—I needed to know what I was refusing. I needed the crew assembled, the route priced out, the season's risks tallied in ink. Due diligence. That was all. Any surveyor worth her license would do the same.

The coffee pot hissed on the camp stove as dawn came gray over the delta, and I spread my father's field notes across the folding table for the first time, weighting the corners with mugs and a stone from the riverbank. The handwriting was his. The obsession was his. The route he'd sketched upriver—the narrows, the waystation at Kilometer Forty, the drowned stair he'd marked with a question mark and then, three pages later, underlined twice—was his.

And the season was wrong. Odile would tell me that first, and she'd be right, and I'd ask her anyway.

* * *

I found Odile where I always found her, elbow-deep in the engine of the *Cormorant*, swearing at it in three languages with the affection she never showed to people.

"Your impeller's shot," she said without looking up. "And before you ask, no, I'm not fixing it for free again. You run that girl hard, Marguerite, and she remembers."

"Then it's good I'm here to buy her a new one."

That got her head up. Odile Fontaine was fifty-three years old, built like a bollard, with forearms that had winched boats off sandbars on two continents and a face that suggested she'd personally resented every one of them. She'd run river freight out of Port Sable for twenty years, and she'd run my surveys for six, and in all that time I had never once seen her surprised. I saw something close to it now.

"Upriver," she said.

"Upriver."

"Monsoon country."

"Monsoon country."

She wiped her hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime in the previous decade and looked at me the way she looked at weather reports she didn't like. "The rains are early this year. Fishermen are saying the birds moved south two weeks ahead of schedule. You don't take a boat into the gorges in a wet year unless the boat is on fire and the fire is worse."

"I know."

"You know." She folded her arms. "So either someone's paying you enough to be stupid, or someone's got a reason stupid can't touch. And you don't have money, so."

I should have lied to her. I'd considered it on the walk over—telling her it was a routine contract survey, a client with deep pockets and a schedule problem. Odile didn't need my ghosts. But she'd known my father too, in the way everyone on the river had known him, the tall foreigner who paid in cash and asked questions about water levels nobody else cared about. She'd carried his gear once, the summer before he disappeared. She'd never said so. I only knew because I'd found his name in her old manifests, and when I'd asked, she'd said, *He tipped well and he listened. That's more than most.*

I put the field notes on the workbench between us.

She didn't reach for them right away. She looked at them for a long moment, and then she picked them up with a care that didn't match her hands, and she read. I watched her face do very little, because Odile's face did very little, but her thumb moved once across the margin where my father had drawn the river bending north, and I understood that something in her had already decided.

"Voss," she said finally.

"You know the name."

"Everyone on this river knows that name. He bought the dredging concessions in the dry years. Half the boatmen owe him." She set the notes down. "And the dam."

"You've heard."

"Everyone's heard. The government put out the survey notice in the spring. Whole valley goes under the reservoir inside two years." Her eyes came up to mine, flat and dark and unfooled. "You're not going up there to survey it for him."

"No."

"You're going to burn it."

I didn't answer, which was an answer, and Odile nodded slowly, the way she nodded at a river gauge that confirmed what she'd already read in the clouds.

"Triple rate," I said. "Plus a bonus if we come back with the boat."

"The boat comes back or I do, and not necessarily in that order." She picked up her rag, wound it around her fist, unwound it. "One condition. I pick the second boat. The *Cormorant* won't run the narrows loaded, and if we're doing this we do it with a backup hull and a second engine, because I am not dragging your corpse through rapids in a wet season. I've done it once." A pause, and something old moved behind her eyes. "Never again."

"Done."

"And Marguerite." She turned back to the engine, dismissing me, conversation over. "Your father's notes. Keep them dry. Paper's the first thing the river takes."

* * *

Sefu came to me, which was how it always went with Sefu—everything came to Sefu, usually at a run, usually slightly out of breath.

I'd posted the contract notice on the board at the Port Sable chandlery the way I always did: *Survey crew, upriver contract, six weeks, hazardous conditions, triple standard rate.* No details. Details attracted questions, and questions attracted the wrong kind of attention, and besides, the people I wanted didn't read the details. The people I wanted read the rate and the word *hazardous* and made their own calculations about what their lives were currently worth.

Sefu Okonkwo was twenty-four, a drone pilot and sonar tech who'd washed out of two university programs and one government hydrology office, and who could pull a bathymetric map out of muddy water the way other people pulled rabbits out of hats. He found me at the chandlery counter before I'd finished paying for rope.

"I can do it," he said. "Whatever it is. I can do it."

"You don't know what it is."

"I know it's upriver and it's triple rate and you're hiring in monsoon season, which means either it's illegal or it's desperate, and honestly I've done both." He grinned, and the grin was the problem, because it was the grin of someone who had never once been afraid of the correct things. "Also your dad's notes were in the manifest office last week. Somebody was copying pages. I saw the handwriting on the route sketch. That's the Rivergate bend, isn't it? Nobody maps that bend. Nobody's mapped that bend in ten years."

I set the rope down slowly. "You saw someone copying pages."

"Big guy. City shoes. Bad idea for the docks." Sefu shrugged. "I didn't say anything. Office guys copy stuff all the time. But if it's *that* bend—" He stopped himself, and for a second the grin dropped and I saw the thing underneath it, the thing that made him take hazardous contracts at triple rate. "My sister's in the valley. Kessuma, up past the narrows. When the reservoir fills, the relocation plan puts her village last. Nobody's surveyed the upper settlements because nobody cares. I've been trying to get a drone up there for a year to map the flood line myself, and I can't afford the batteries, let alone the boat."

So there it was. Not greed. Worse. The dangerous money was always worse when it was love-shaped.

"Six weeks," I said. "Maybe more if the water turns. You'd be running sonar, drone survey, and comms, and when it goes wrong—and it will go wrong—you carry packs and you keep your head down. I'm not hiring a hero."

"I'm not a hero. Heroes have plans." He stuck out his hand. "When do we leave?"

"Nine days. There's gear to buy and a second boat to find, and I want the drones weatherproofed before we're anywhere near rain."

"Nine days." He was already calculating, I could see it, batteries and memory cards and the flood line at his sister's village. "I'll need an advance."

"Half up front. Standard."

"Triple standard," he said, grinning again. "You posted it."

* * *

Priya took the longest, and not because she was hard to find.

Anand Priya—everyone called her Priya, including, I suspected, her own mother—had been the medic on my last three surveys, and the medic on four others before that for crews I wouldn't work with. She kept a clinic room above the chandlery that smelled of antiseptic and diesel, treated boatmen for free on Sundays, and charged everyone else exactly what she judged they could afford, which was an accounting system with no paperwork and no appeals.

I found her stitching a fisherman's forearm by lamplight, and I waited, and when the fisherman left with his arm wrapped and his pride intact, she looked at me over her glasses and said, "You've got the look."

"What look?"

"The look of someone about to ask me to do something I'll regret." She snapped off her gloves. "Sit down. You've been sleeping badly. Don't argue, I can see it from here."

I sat. I told her all of it—the letter, Voss, the dam, the notes, the plan that wasn't yet a plan but was hardening into one the way silt hardens into stone. I told her about the burn. She listened the way she did everything, completely and without interruption, and when I finished she was quiet for a while, cleaning instruments that were already clean.

"Your father," she said at last. "You never talk about him."

"There's not much to say. He chose the river over us."

"Mm." She set down the forceps. "And now you're choosing it over yourself. You see the resemblance, I hope."

"That's not—" I started, and stopped, because it was, and she knew it was, and lying to a medic about your own symptoms is a waste of both your time.

"Here's what I know about you, Marguerite Winterbourne." Priya pulled a chair around and sat across from me, knees nearly touching, the way she did when she was delivering a diagnosis. "You keep your crew small and your margins thin and your name off every publication list in the delta. You're the best surveyor on this river and nobody under forty has heard of you, because you've spent ten years making sure of it. That's not humility. That's a woman hiding in a cellar from a fire she thinks is still burning." She took off her glasses. "So either this trip is the fire, or it's you finally walking out of the cellar. I honestly can't tell which. But I'm coming either way, because you'll do something stupid with that arm you hurt last season if there's no one there to stop you, and because—" she paused, and for a moment she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour, "—because my registration lapsed in March and I haven't renewed it, and a woman who can't practice medicine might as well practice it somewhere the paperwork can't find her. Triple rate buys a lot of silence."

"Priya—"

"Don't. I'm not asking for absolution, I'm asking for a boat." She put her glasses back on, and the tiredness folded itself away, and she was my medic again, brisk and certain. "Medical kit for eight, wet-season additions, and I'm telling you now: whatever's up there, if someone's bleeding, I stop the bleeding first and ask whose side they're on second. That's the deal. That's always the deal."

"That's the deal," I agreed.

"And Marguerite." She caught my hand as I stood, turned it over, and pressed her thumb into the old tendon scar from a winch cable three years ago, checking, always checking. "Whatever your father left up that river—it isn't yours to burn until you've read all of it. Promise me that much. People who burn letters they haven't read spend the rest of their lives smelling smoke."

* * *

We met around the folding table in the chandlery's back room on the eighth night, the four of us, with bad coffee and my father's notes spread under the lamplight and the rain starting against the shutters—early, too early, the first taste of a monsoon that was coming up the valley to meet us.

Odile stood with her back to the wall, arms crossed, watching the notes the way she watched a horizon. Sefu sat forward with his elbows on the table, drone schematics already annotated in three colors of ink. Priya sat with her hands around her mug like it was the only warm thing in the room.

"Six weeks," I said. "Maybe eight if the water turns against us. We go up through the narrows to Kilometer Forty, then past it, into the valley the government's about to drown. We survey what we find, we document it, and we come back down before peak flood. That's what the contract says."

"And what it doesn't say?" Odile asked.

I looked at the three of them—my strays, my dangerous money, my reasons. The rain thickened against the shutters.

"What it doesn't say," I said, "is that when we get there, some of what we find is going in the fire."

Silence, except the rain. Sefu's pen stopped moving. Priya looked at me over her glasses and gave the smallest nod, the one she'd promised me at the stitching table. Odile uncrossed her arms, crossed them again.

"And Voss?" Sefu asked. "The man whose contract this technically is?"

"Voss thinks we're his survey crew. Let him think it as long as possible."

"You know he'll have someone watching," Odile said. "Men like Voss don't hand out triple rate and look away. He'll have eyes on this crew from the dock."

"Then we give the eyes nothing to see," I said. "We're a survey team. That's all anyone needs to know until the valley closes behind us."

Odile snorted, which from her was a speech about the optimism of the young. Priya refilled the coffee, which was terrible, which none of us mentioned, because terrible coffee shared at midnight over a map of a place that might kill you is its own kind of sacrament. Sefu slid his annotated schematics to the center of the table, and Odile leaned in despite herself, and for a moment—just a moment—the room held something that wasn't quite trust, because none of us trusted anything yet, but was the ground trust gets built on: four people with their reasons showing, agreeing to carry them up the same river.

We signed the articles at one in the morning. Real articles, old-fashioned, the kind river crews used to sign—names, shares, conditions, and a line at the bottom that Odile insisted on: *The boat comes back or we do.*

I was the last to sign. My father's route sketch lay open beside the page, the drowned stair underlined twice, the question mark he'd never answered. I signed beneath my crew's names, blew the ink dry, and told myself the tightness in my chest was just the coffee.

Outside, the rain kept falling. And when I stepped out into the chandlery yard to check the sky, I saw him—a stranger standing at the dock rail in the wet dark, city shoes, watching the *Cormorant* with the stillness of a man memorizing it. He turned, and even across the yard, even in the rain, I caught the shape of a smile that was already preparing its own introduction.

He raised a hand. Like he'd been waiting for me.

Like he knew exactly who I was.
