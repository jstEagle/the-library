## 4. The Stranger at the Dock

The rain caught him at the harbor mouth, which was the first thing I noticed about him. Everyone else in Puerto Viejo had done what sensible people do when the sky splits open—ducked under awnings, retreated into cantinas, waited out the downpour with rum and patience. This man walked through it as if weather were a rumor he'd chosen not to believe, a canvas duffel over one shoulder and a hard-shell case in his other hand, moving down the pier with the unhurried gait of someone who had already decided how his day would go.

I was watching him from the wheelhouse of the *Cormorant's Wake* before I knew I was watching him. That's a habit you develop in the survey trade, or maybe it's a habit you develop when your father disappears upriver and strangers start appearing in your life with documents in their hands. You watch. You catalog. You decide later whether the watching was paranoia or instinct.

He was maybe thirty-five, lean in the way of men who work outdoors but don't perform it—no sun-blasted bravado, no knife on the belt, none of the costume that hired muscle wears in river towns. His clothes were good but worn, the kind of gear bought once and maintained carefully: a field jacket with the cuffs frayed from map cases, boots resoled twice. His hair was dark and rain-flattened, and he carried himself with a particular kind of stillness that I'd learned to read in the delta. Stillness like that belongs to one of two kinds of men. The kind who are hiding, and the kind who are hunting. Sometimes both.

He stopped at the *Cormorant's Wake*, looked her over the way a man looks at a horse he's considering buying—hull, gunwale, the patched seam above the waterline where Odile's work was honest but visible—and then looked up at the wheelhouse window and found my eyes through the glass.

He didn't pretend he hadn't. That was the second thing I noticed. Most men would have looked away, made a show of studying the dock. He held the look for a three-count, gave me a nod that was almost respectful, and then climbed aboard without asking permission.

I came down to the deck with the rain drumming on the wheelhouse roof above us. Up close he was taller than he'd seemed from the window, and there was a faded scar through one eyebrow that hadn't made it into my catalog from a distance.

"Marguerite Winterbourne," he said. Not a question.

"You have the advantage."

"Callum Reyes." He shifted the hard case to offer a hand, and his grip was dry and firm, calloused across the palm in the pattern of a man who handled instruments more than rope. "Edmund Voss sent me."

The name landed the way Voss's name always landed now, three weeks after he'd stood in my camp with his polished shoes and his folder of government permits and his voice full of my father's name. I kept my face still. That's the other thing you learn.

"Did he."

"He said you'd take some convincing." Reyes set the hard case down on the deck plates with a care that told me what was inside before he opened it. "I'm a hydrologist. Current modeling, flow rates, flood-stage projection. He wants you to have the best data available before you run the gorge, and he wants me on your boat to make sure you have it."

"Voss wants a lot of things."

"He does." Reyes's mouth moved, not quite a smile. Something tired underneath it. "He's paying me to be on this boat, Ms. Winterbourne. He's not paying me to lie to you about it."

I looked at him for a long moment while the rain came down and the harbor water slapped against the hull in its patient, indifferent way. There was a version of this conversation where I told him to get off my deck. I'd rehearsed it, in a way—Voss had warned me in his last letter that he'd be sending support, and I'd spent a week composing refusals in my head, elegant ones, the kind you write when you want the refusing to be remembered.

Instead I said, "You're wet. Come under the wheelhouse before you rust."

* * *

The *Cormorant's Wake* was built to carry six and had spent most of her life carrying four, and she made her opinions known about crowding. Odile took one look at Reyes's duffel and one longer look at his face, and said nothing, which from Odile was a paragraph. Sefu was friendlier—Sefu was friendly to everyone, it was his whole strategy for staying alive in a trade full of hard men—and had Reyes's instruments unpacked and admired within the hour: a current profiler, a laser rangefinder, a set of bathymetric charts of the upper valley that were better than anything I owned.

Better than anything I owned, in fact, than anything my father had owned. I stood at the chart table while Reyes explained the flood projections, and I looked at contour lines my father had spent twenty years trying to map, drawn by a stranger's hand, and I felt something cold move through me that had nothing to do with the rain.

"Where did these come from?" I asked.

"Voss's archive. Some from government surveys." Reyes rolled the chart closed with practiced hands. "Some from me. I ran the valley six years ago. Dry season, low water. I got farther upriver than most."

"Farther than my father?"

He was careful with the pause. Not long enough to be evasive, not short enough to be casual. A pause with engineering in it.

"I never met your father," he said. "But I found one of his markers. Kilometer ninety-one, on the east bank. It was old then. Six years of weather on it."

My father's markers. Brass caps set in stone, numbered in his own hand, scattered up the valley like a trail of bread crumbs he'd been leaving for twenty years and never got to follow to the end. I had grown up learning to read them the way other children learned to read street signs.

"You're hired to do more than run currents," I said.

"I'm hired to run currents." He met my eyes, and there it was again, that stillness, that careful architecture of a face. "Everything else I do on this boat is my own business."

"Everything on this boat is my business."

"Then we understand each other." And he almost smiled—almost, the way a man almost opens a door and then decides to knock instead—and went back to his charts.

* * *

I want to say, in fairness to him, that he was good. That's what made it worse, in the end. He was very, very good.

He worked without performing work. He ran the profiler off the stern in the brown delta water and logged readings in a small notebook with a pencil, because pencils don't fail in humidity, and he never once explained his methods unless asked, and when Priya asked—Priya, who trusts no one's competence until she's tested it herself—he answered in the flat, precise language of a man who actually knew his trade. He knew the gorge. He knew the flood cycles. He knew things about the upper river that Odile confirmed with grunts that, from Odile, amounted to applause.

And he was pleasant. Quietly, undemandingly pleasant. He fixed the wheelhouse door hinge that Sefu had been meaning to fix for a month. He made coffee at dawn without being asked and left it for whoever woke next, and it was good coffee, which in the delta is worth more than most credentials. By the third day Priya had stopped watching him when he moved. By the fourth, Sefu was showing him photographs of his sister's graduation.

I watched him the whole time. That was my job now, apparently—captain, surveyor, and counterintelligence, all for one salary.

It was on the fifth night that I caught him.

We were moored at the last fuel depot before the gorge, a rusted pontoon operation run by a woman who charged double and watered the diesel, and I'd gone back aboard late for the chart case I'd forgotten. The wheelhouse was dark except for the chart light, and Reyes was at the table with my father's field notes spread open in front of him.

Not Voss's copy. Mine. The original, the one I'd kept locked in the chart case since Voss had shown me his facsimile—the one with the marginalia, the crossed-out soundings, the last page where my father's handwriting deteriorated from surveyor's precision into something ragged and hurried, like a man writing against a clock only he could hear.

Reyes had his phone above the page. The camera light was off, but I knew the angle, knew the stillness of his hands, knew exactly what he was doing, because I'd spent a decade learning what men do with other men's papers.

I didn't say anything. That surprised me, later—that my first instinct wasn't fury but observation. I stood in the shadow of the companionway and watched him photograph the last page, the ragged one, and then—this is the part I keep returning to, the part that wouldn't leave me alone—he lowered the phone and just looked at the page. For a long time. His lips moved slightly, reading, and his face did something complicated and quiet, and he reached out and touched the edge of the paper the way you'd touch something that might break.

Then he heard me. I don't know how—maybe the hull, maybe the rain stopping—but his head came up and he saw me in the companionway, and to his credit he didn't move, didn't hide the phone, didn't reach for the notes. He just set the phone down on the table, screen up, and looked at me.

"Well," he said.

"Well," I said.

I came into the wheelhouse and stood across the table from him, and the chart light made a small island of yellow between us, and my father's handwriting sat in the middle of it like a third person.

"Voss wants copies," I said.

"Voss wants a lot of things."

"That's the second time you've said that."

"Because it keeps being true." He turned the phone around on the table, deliberately, so the screen faced me. "I've sent him nothing. Check the outbox if you want. I'll leave the phone with you tonight if you want that too."

"Why photograph them at all?"

The question hung there. He looked down at the last page, at the ragged handwriting, and when he answered, his voice had changed—lower, stripped of the careful pleasantness he wore like a jacket.

"Because your father wrote something on this page that I've only ever seen in one other place," he said. "And I needed to know if it was the same."

"What thing?"

He was quiet for a moment. The harbor water knocked against the hull. Somewhere on the pontoon, the fuel woman's dog started up and quit again.

"He wrote, *the river keeps what was given to it.*" Reyes's eyes came up to mine. "Six years ago, in the gorge, I found that sentence carved into stone. Old stone. Underwater stone, at low water. And I have spent six years trying to decide whether your father saw the same stone, or whether he put the words there himself, and either answer is worse than the question."

I should have put him off the boat that night. I've replayed it a hundred times since, and every version of me that's smarter, harder, more careful—the version my father would have wanted me to be—puts him ashore at the fuel depot and never sees him again.

But here is what I knew at the time, standing in that yellow island of light: Voss had offered me triple pay and my father's ghosts, and I'd said no, and then I'd read the notes all night and signed on anyway, and every person on this boat had a reason to be here that wasn't the reason they'd given. Odile's silence was a paragraph. Sefu's friendliness was a strategy. Priya's contract had a clause she wouldn't discuss. I was running from a burning house myself, and I'd brought the fire with me.

And I needed hands. The gorge was coming, the monsoon was early, and a hydrologist who knew the upper river better than any living surveyor was not a man you put ashore because he'd touched your father's handwriting gently.

"Give me the phone," I said.

He handed it over without a flinch. I scrolled the camera roll. Thirty-one photographs, all of them of the notes, all of them taken tonight, none of them sent. I deleted them one by one while he watched, and he watched without protest, and when the last one was gone I set the phone on the table between us.

"The next thing you photograph on my boat," I said, "you photograph for me."

"Understood."

"And Reyes." He looked up. "If Voss has a man on this boat, I'll find out what he's carrying eventually. Everyone on this boat is carrying something. Just make sure what you're carrying is worth what it costs."

Something moved through his face then—there and gone, fast, like a fish under brown water. Something that looked almost like relief, which made no sense at all, and I didn't have time to make sense of it, because that was the moment the fuel woman's dog started up again, and this time it didn't quit.

Odile came up the dock at a fast walk, which Odile never did, and put her head into the wheelhouse, and the look on her face was one I'd seen once before, years ago, on the night the radio brought the first news that my father's boat had been found empty.

"Engine on the river," she said. "Two hundred meters down. Running dark. Been sitting there since before you came aboard."
