## 5. Upriver

**Marguerite**

The river narrowed on the second day, and by the third it had stopped pretending to be a river at all. It became a throat.

Marguerite stood at the bow of the *Perdiz* with one hand on the rail and watched the gorge walls close in around them, limestone the color of old bone, streaked black where a century of rain had bled down the stone. The water beneath them ran green and deep and fast, folding around boulders the size of houses, and the sound of it filled the gorge like breathing—slow, enormous, indifferent.

Behind her, Sefu was arguing with the depth sounder again.

"It keeps losing bottom," he called up. "Either the sensor's fouled or we're floating over an abyss."

"Both are possible," Marguerite said. "Welcome to the Rivergate country."

She felt Callum's eyes on her before she turned. He was at the stern with Priya, ostensibly helping her inventory the medical stores, but he'd been drifting toward wherever she stood for two days now, the way iron filings drift toward a magnet—or toward something they intend to steal. She hadn't confronted him about the photographs yet. Not because she'd forgotten. Because she wanted to see how long he thought he could keep the mask on, and what he did when it started to slip.

Odile came up from the engine well wiping her hands on a rag that had been gray since before Sefu was born. She squinted up at the sky, which was doing something Marguerite didn't like: piling clouds against the eastern ridgeline, tall and white-shouldered, building towers the way children build them—too fast, too high, with no regard for what happens when they fall.

"Look at that," Odile said.

"I see it."

"You don't. You're looking at clouds. I'm looking at weather." She spat over the rail, a boatman's habit she performed with the gravity of a sacrament. "Monsoon's early. I told you it would be. My mother told me, and her mother before her, and every one of them was right more often than your instruments."

"How early?"

"Days." Odile held up three fingers, then folded one down. "Maybe four. The birds went quiet yesterday evening. Birds go quiet when the pressure drops and they know to sit tight. Your father knew the signs too." A pause, deliberate as a knife laid on a table. "He ignored them once. Near here."

Marguerite kept her eyes on the water. "You knew him."

"Everyone who worked this river knew him. Knew *of* him." Odile dropped the rag into her apron pocket. "There's a difference, and you'll learn it before this trip is done."

She went back below, and Marguerite stood alone at the bow and let the gorge swallow them.

* * *

**Callum**

Callum had charted this stretch of river six years ago, from a helicopter, in dry season, with Voss's money paying for the fuel and Voss's patience wearing thin over the radio. He remembered thinking then that the gorge looked like a wound that hadn't decided whether to heal. From the water, at eye level, with the walls rising four hundred feet on either side and the light arriving in narrow gold blades that lasted minutes instead of hours—it didn't look like a wound anymore. It looked like a place that had already made its decision about intruders.

He was cataloguing that decision when Priya caught him watching Marguerite.

"You know she knows," Priya said quietly, not looking up from the ampoule tray she was securing with rubber bands. Her hands never stopped moving. They were very good hands. "About the photos."

"I assumed."

"And you're still going to pretend otherwise?"

"For now." He tested a strap on the supply crate. "Telling her what I actually am doesn't help anyone. Telling her what she thinks I am might help all of us."

Priya finally looked up. She had the kind of face that made lying feel like a physical risk. "You know what I did before this? Six years in border clinics. You learn to read people fast when the wrong read gets someone killed." She snapped the case shut. "I've read you, Reyes. Whatever debt you're carrying, it's got weight. People with light debts don't check the treeline every ninety seconds."

He said nothing, which was answer enough, and she let him keep it.

That night they moored in a side channel Odile trusted—a slot of black water behind a gravel bar, willows thick on the bank, the gorge wall at their backs. Odile set the anchor watch herself and refused help, which meant she sat alone in the bow with a rifle across her knees and her ears open to the dark.

Callum took the middle watch instead, and it was Callum, at half past two in the morning, who heard it.

An engine.

Not theirs—their engine ticked as it cooled, a small domestic sound, familiar as a heartbeat. This was different. Distant, upstream, muffled by the gorge walls into something almost shapeless. Almost. It rose, held, faded. Rose, held, faded. Someone running their motor at low power in the dark, moving slowly, the way you move when you're searching a riverbank by ear and don't want to be heard until you choose to be.

He lay still in his bunk and counted the cycles. Four. Then silence, total, the kind of silence that has weight.

In the morning he said nothing. Neither did Odile, though when he passed her at dawn he saw the rifle sitting closer to hand than it had been the night before, and saw that she had heard it too.

* * *

**Marguerite**

The fourth day brought the first rapids worth the name, and the first proof that Odile's weather sense was worth more than Sefu's entire instrument kit.

The gorge pinched to less than thirty meters where an ancient slide had dumped a fan of broken rock into the channel, and the river went from deep green to churning white in the space of a breath. Odile took them through standing at the wheel, jaw working, hands making small corrections that looked like nothing and were everything, while Marguerite worked the bow line and Sefu bailed and Callum—Callum read the water ahead of them and called the lines.

"Right channel, past the drowned tree—no, hard right, there's a strainer under the surface—" His voice was calm, precise, a surveyor's voice, and Marguerite hated how much she trusted it. "Now left! Left, left—"

They came through the tail waves with the hull singing and everyone laughing in the way people laugh after terror, too loud and too relieved, and Marguerite caught Callum grinning at her across the deck and hated that too, hated the warmth of it, because men who photographed dead men's field notes in secret didn't get to grin at her like they were crew.

But the rapids cost them. The *Perdiz* shipped water over the bow and soaked a third of their rice, and the afternoon sun that followed was hot enough to steam the gorge like a kettle, and by evening the clouds that had been building for two days had organized themselves into something with intent.

Odile put them ashore early, in a hollow above a shingle beach where an overhang of limestone gave them shelter without giving away their firelight. She built the fire small and screened, and while they ate she talked, which she almost never did.

"When I was a girl, my uncle ran cargo through this gorge. Dry season only, same as everyone." She turned her cup in her hands. "One year he pushed it. Late start, big contract, monsoon coming. Everyone told him. He went anyway." She drank. "They found the boat forty miles downstream. They found my uncle eleven years later, in a cave upriver, sitting down, facing the water. He'd walked out of the flood alive and then just—stopped. Sat down in the dark and waited." She looked up. "The river doesn't kill you, girl. It just removes everything else, and then asks if you're sure."

Nobody spoke after that. Sefu cleaned his glasses twice. Priya banked the fire. And Callum sat with his elbows on his knees, staring into the flames with an expression Marguerite couldn't file under any of her categories for him—not spy, not liar, not hired man. Something older and wearier than any of those.

It was Sefu, on last watch, who woke them.

* * *

**Callum**

"—engine," Sefu was saying, urgent, whispering it like a curse. "Same as before. Upstream. Coming down."

Callum was awake before the words finished, boots on, pistol in hand out of a reflex he wasn't proud of. He climbed to the lip of the hollow beside Sefu and looked upriver into the dark.

Nothing. Black water, blacker walls, stars in a thin slice overhead. But the sound was there, unmistakable now that he knew its shape: a motor at low throttle, riding the current down, pausing—pausing—moving on. Searching.

"They're running dark," he murmured. "No lights. Nobody runs the gorge at night without lights unless they know it already or don't care if they die."

"Do you know it?" Sefu whispered.

"From the air. In daylight. Which means whoever's down there either has a pilot's memory or a local guide." He watched the dark. "Or both."

Below them, Marguerite emerged from her tent without a lamp, moving by feel, and crouched at the edge of the overhang. In the starlight her face was all planes and shadow. "How long?"

"Ten minutes, maybe less," Callum said. "They'll pass us or stop. If they pass, we've lost nothing but sleep. If they stop—"

"If they stop, they've found our cold fire pit and our tracks to the water." She was already calculating; he could hear it, the same cold arithmetic he used himself. "Odile. Kill the fire completely. Sefu, everything valuable goes in the boat tonight. We may leave in a hurry."

For twenty minutes the whole camp held its breath. Callum knelt at the lip of the hollow with the pistol heavy in his hand and listened to the engine grow from a rumor into a fact, and beside him Marguerite breathed slow and even, and he understood suddenly that she had done this before, some other river, some other night, and that the calm was real but so was whatever had carved the channels for it.

The engine sound reached its closest point—close enough that he could pick out the individual sputter of a tired two-stroke—and then, horribly, it slowed.

And stopped.

Silence poured back into the gorge. Somewhere downstream, water dripped off stone with metronome patience. Callum counted seconds. Thirty. Sixty. Two hundred.

Then the engine restarted, farther away, and faded downriver into the dark, and no one in the hollow moved for a long time after it was gone.

"They passed," Sefu whispered.

"They stopped," Marguerite said quietly. "For two hundred seconds, they stopped. Right across from our beach." She turned her head, and in the faint light her eyes found Callum's and held them, and there was no accusation in them, which was worse than accusation. "Somebody on that boat knows exactly where we are. And somebody on this one knows why."

She stood and walked back to her tent, and Callum stayed at the lip of the hollow with the pistol in his lap, listening to the river breathe, knowing she was right, knowing which name she suspected, and unable—in the dark, in the gorge, with the monsoon four days out and closing—to make himself say it aloud.

Above the eastern ridge, the clouds had eaten the stars.
