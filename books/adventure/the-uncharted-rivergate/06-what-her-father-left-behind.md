## 6. What Her Father Left Behind

The waystation announced itself before they saw it—a stand of mango trees too orderly to be wild, their shade arranged around a clearing like guests around a table. Then the roofline, corrugated tin gone the color of old blood, sagging over a low stone building with a porch that had once been painted government green.

Odile cut the engine a hundred meters out and let the boat drift. "Nobody's been there in years," she said. "Look at the grass."

The grass had grown through the porch boards. A rusted sign leaned against the doorframe, its letters scoured to ghosts: *RIVERGATE SURVEY STATION 3 — DEPT. OF HYDROGRAPHIC WORKS*. Below it, someone had stenciled a smaller warning in three languages, all faded to the same gray whisper.

"Survey Station Three," Sefu said, reading from the chart on his knee. "It's on the old government maps. Decommissioned in—" he squinted "—huh. Decommissioned the same year your father disappeared."

Marguerite felt the sentence land somewhere beneath her ribs. "Convenient."

"Or not convenient at all," Priya said quietly. She was watching the treeline the way she watched a patient's face—reading what wasn't said. "If your father was using government infrastructure off the books, this is where he'd stage from. Dry storage, fresh water, a roof that mostly holds. It's exactly what I'd pick."

"It's exactly what *he* picked," Marguerite said, and heard how her voice had changed—flatter, more careful. She hadn't spoken about her father in the present tense in ten years, and here she was doing it in front of three near-strangers, as if the building itself had given him back to her.

They tied off in the shallows and waded the last stretch. The air under the mango trees was cooler, thick with the smell of wet stone and overripe fruit. Somewhere in the canopy a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling.

The door wasn't locked. It wasn't even latched. It swung inward on hinges that screamed, and the darkness inside breathed out at them—dust, mildew, kerosene gone to varnish, and underneath it all something older, mineral, like the inside of a cave.

Sefu got the lanterns lit. The main room was a single long chamber: a workbench along one wall buried under drifted sand, a camp cot with its canvas rotted to lace, a shelf of specimen jars whose contents had long since dissolved into amber shadow. A wall map, yellowed and cracked along its folds, showed the valley as it had been forty years ago—before the logging roads, before the first dam proposals, before everyone agreed this stretch of river belonged to nobody worth counting.

Marguerite stood in the doorway a long time before she went in.

She had been eleven the last time she'd seen her father's field camps, and her memory of them was all textures: the grit of pumice underfoot, the smell of his pipe baked into canvas, the particular way he pinned notes to walls so that a whole room became one continuous thought. She had spent ten years telling herself she remembered nothing useful. Standing in this room, she understood that she remembered everything, and had simply refused to know it.

"He lived here," she said. "Not camped. *Lived.*"

The evidence was in the details. A tin cup worn smooth on one side where a thumb had rested ten thousand times. A second cot, folded and never unfolded—someone had visited but not stayed. A row of books on the shelf, their spines broken in the way of books read many times: geology, hydrology, three volumes on pre-colonial river cultures, and a paperback novel with a lurid cover that made her laugh once, sharply, a sound that startled her more than the dark had.

"He hated that book," she said to no one. "He used to read it to my mother to make her furious."

* * *

It was Sefu who found the floor.

They had been working through the room in a grid, the way she trained every crew to work a site—slow, methodical, touching nothing before photographing it. Sefu was on his knees at the eastern wall, tapping flagstones with a knuckle, and Marguerite heard the change in the sound before she understood it: a dull knock becoming a hollow drum.

"Marguerite." He didn't look up. "There's a void under here."

The stone came up with Priya's pry bar and Odile's hands, grating on sand, and beneath it was a cavity perhaps half a meter deep, lined with sheet lead gone white with oxidation. In the cavity: a metal ammunition box, army surplus, its latch fused with corrosion. And on top of the box, weighted down with a river stone, a single envelope.

Marguerite knew the handwriting before she knew she was reading it. She would have known it upside down, underwater, burned halfway to ash. *M. Winterbourne, if she ever comes. If she doesn't, burn all of it.*

Her name. Her father's hand. Forty years in the ground, waiting.

Nobody spoke. Odile took Sefu's shoulder and steered him toward the door; Priya followed, and then it was just Marguerite and the box and the afternoon light going long and orange through the broken window.

She sat on the floor with her back against the workbench, the way she'd sat as a girl doing homework at his knee, and opened the envelope first because it was addressed to her and she was, God help her, still his daughter before she was anything else.

*Marguerite.*

*I don't know if you'll ever read this. I don't know if I want you to. If you're reading it, then I'm dead or worse, and you've come looking, which means you're either forgiving me or avenging yourself on me, and I've known you long enough to know you'll do both at once and pretend it's neither.*

*You'll want to know what I found. Everyone always wants to know what I found. Your mother used to say I loved the finding more than the found, and she was right, and it cost her, and I've never stopped paying that bill. So let me tell you what I found, and then let me tell you why I'm not coming home, and you can decide which of us the Rivergate ruined.*

She read it twice before she could keep going.

The letters beneath the first one were older—dated across three field seasons, written in the shorthand of a man thinking aloud to himself, some on survey paper, some on the backs of supply manifests, one on the flyleaf of the lurid paperback. She read them in order, by lantern light now, while outside the first fat drops of an evening shower ticked against the tin roof like something asking to be let in.

The story they told was not the story the universities had told.

The official record—what little there was—held that Elias Winterbourne had gone upriver in pursuit of a rumored temple complex, that the monsoon had caught him, that the river had taken him, and that his obsession in his final years had been the product of grief and altitude and too much time alone. A cautionary tale. Marguerite had built her entire careful, hidden, beneath-the-radar life on the foundation of that tale: *this is what the work does to you; this is what it did to him.*

The letters said otherwise.

*Third season, Station Three. The stair is real. I've seen it twice now, in the drawdown years, when the dry season bites deep enough. A stair of black stone going down into the river channel, cut so fine the water slides off it without turbulence—Callum Reyes would weep, if he ever saw it, the masonry is beyond anything in the valley's known cultures. It is not a temple that flooded. It was built to flood. The whole complex is designed to be drowned.*

She stopped. Read the sentence again. *Built to flood.*

*The tablets confirm it—or I believe they do; my reading is crude, pieced together from the trade glyphs we've catalogued downstream. Here is what I think happened, and I want it written down plainly, because if I'm wrong I'd rather be wrong on paper than wrong in silence: there was a city here. Not a temple, a city, a civilization with a written record, and when the conquerors came down from the highlands—and they came, the burn layers don't lie—the people did not fight and did not flee. They opened their own river. They let the water in on purpose. They drowned their libraries, their records, their names, everything that could be taken from them, and they went into the highlands as no one, leaving nothing behind but a river that has kept their secret ever since.*

*They chose the water over conquest. Do you understand what that means, Marguerite? It means the Rivergate isn't a ruin. It's a decision. It's the only intact record of a people who decided that their memory was safer drowned than captured, and the water has honored that decision for a thousand years, and I am the first man stupid enough to come and ask the river to break its word.*

The lantern hissed. Outside, the shower thickened into a real rain, drumming the roof, filling the silence the way water fills a hull.

Marguerite sat with the letters in her lap and felt awe move through her like a current—and then, beneath the awe, something darker and more familiar, the feeling she'd spent a decade outrunning. Because she understood. That was the terrible thing. She read her father's hand and there was no distance in it at all, no estrangement, no decade of silence between them. She read *the water has honored that decision* and felt her own pulse answer it, felt the same vertiginous pull that had eaten him alive, and she understood with total clarity how a man could walk into a river valley in pursuit of a drowned library and simply never prioritize coming back.

He hadn't been destroyed by obsession. He'd been *converted* by it. And the letters were trying to do the same to her.

*If you've come this far,* the last letter said, *then you've already felt it, and I'm sorry. I'm sorry the way my own father was sorry when he handed me his maps. It's an inheritance, Marguerite, and inheritances are the one debt you can't refuse—you can only decide what to do with it. I've decided. I'm going down the stair one more time, in the drawdown, and this time I'm going to the bottom of it, and whatever I find there, I'm going to leave the valley a letter of my own. If the record should stay drowned, I'll say so, and I'll mean it. If it should be raised, God forgive me, I'll say that too.*

*Either way, don't follow me. That's not a father's pride talking. It's the one piece of advice in all these pages that I'm certain of.*

*—E.W.*

She read the date on the last page. It was eleven days after his official disappearance. Eleven days after the world had already written him into the past tense, he had been sitting in this room, writing to her, planning his descent.

*He was here alive after they stopped looking.*

The thought arrived cold and complete, and with it came a second thought that made her go still: the rope. The fresh rope she'd found tied to his last survey marker, two days upriver, weathered by one season, not forty. Someone had been on that stair recently. Someone had read these letters—or knew what they contained—and had come looking for the same thing her father had died inside of.

She looked up. The rain had settled into a steady gray curtain. Through the doorway she could see her crew under the porch awning—Odile smoking with her collar up, Sefu hunched over his tablet, Priya repacking the med kit with the unnecessary thoroughness of someone giving the others privacy. Good people. People she'd chosen, mostly, and trusted, mostly.

And somewhere behind them, patient as the river itself, a second engine that never showed itself in daylight.

Marguerite folded the letters along their old creases, slid them inside her shirt against her skin, and put the ammunition box back in the ground with the stone on top—everything exactly as it had been, except the one thing that couldn't be unburied. Then she knelt there a moment longer in the dark, her palm flat on the cold lead lining, and said, quietly, in a voice she hadn't used in ten years:

"I'm sorry, Dad. I had to follow you. It's the one debt I couldn't refuse either."

When she stepped out onto the porch, the rain was coming down in sheets, and Odile was standing at the rail, staring downriver into the gray, her cigarette forgotten in her hand.

"Marguerite," she said, without turning. "You need to see this."

Downstream, where the channel bent toward the gorge, a light had appeared. Not the wandering beam of a fisherman—one steady white point, holding position against the current, getting slowly, deliberately, closer.
