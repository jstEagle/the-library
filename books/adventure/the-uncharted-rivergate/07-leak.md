## 7. Leak

The river told you things before it did them, if you knew how to listen. Callum had learned that years ago, in this same valley, with different people and a different conscience. The water ahead of them was speaking now—a low, guttural mutter under the engine's drone, the kind of sound that meant the channel was about to narrow and drop.

He stood at the bow with the depth pole, pretending to read the current, actually watching Marguerite at the wheel. She hadn't slept more than two hours since the waystation. He'd seen her sitting up past midnight with her father's letters, her face lit by the camp lantern like a woman reading by candlelight in a house where someone had died. There was a particular grief that made people reckless, and he recognized it because he'd worn it himself once, briefly, before it calcified into something more useful: guilt with a work ethic.

"Shoal line," he called back. "Starboard side's going to shallow out. Give me thirty meters of room."

Odile grunted from behind him, coiling rope with the economy of a woman who never wasted a motion on anything, including speech. "River drops fast after the bend," she said. "Last time I ran it, there were teeth."

"Teeth?"

"Rocks." A pause. "Big ones."

Sefu laughed from the mid-deck, where he'd been nursing the fuel gauge like a sick relative. Priya looked up from the supply crates she was re-securing—she'd been re-securing them all morning, which told Callum she was nervous, though she hid it better than most medics he'd known, who tended to hide nothing at all.

Marguerite brought the boat around the bend wide and slow, and the gorge closed in around them like a throat.

It was beautiful, in the way that places built to kill you are always beautiful. Walls of black granite streaked with ochre, hung with vines that poured down forty meters of stone like green water frozen mid-pour. The river ran fast and glassy here, deep enough to hide its intentions, and then the glass broke—white water ahead, a long churning staircase of it, maybe six hundred meters of rapids dropping through a slot no wider than the boat twice over.

"That's not a rapid," Sefu said. "That's a rumor of a waterfall."

"It's runnable," Odile said. "Left of center. Stay off the left wall—there's an eddy that'll eat you."

Callum crouched, gripping the bow rail. "I'll call the line."

Marguerite's eyes found his in the mirror she'd rigged above the wheel. For a moment neither of them said anything, and the whole history between them—the photographs, the notes, the careful politeness stretched over mutual suspicion like canvas over a frame—passed between them without either naming it.

"You've run whitewater?" she asked.

"I've mapped rivers." Which wasn't an answer, and they both knew it.

"Then map us through this one."

She gave it throttle and the bow came up, and the world became noise.

* * *

Later, Callum would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and find only fragments: Odile's voice cutting through the roar—*left, LEFT*—the granite wall sliding past close enough to touch, spray hitting his face like thrown gravel, the boat slewing sideways across a standing wave and Marguerite correcting with a touch so light it seemed like instinct rather than skill. He remembered thinking, absurdly, that she drove a boat the way she refused offers: precisely, and with total commitment once committed.

Then the sound changed.

Every riverman knew that sound—the hollow *thock* of hull meeting stone—and every riverman heard it in his spine before his ears. The impact came up through the deck plates and into Callum's knees, a shudder, a grind, and then a second hit, harder, and the boat swung broadside into the current.

"Hold!" Odile was shouting. "Hold the line!"

Water came over the gunwale in a cold sheet. Callum got both hands on the rail and saw, downstream, the channel splitting around a fang of rock the size of a bus, foam boiling off it in a permanent white plume. They were going to hit it. Everything in the geometry said they were going to hit it.

"No we're not," Marguerite said—to the boat, or the river, or herself—and threw the wheel hard over.

The engine screamed. The stern kicked. For one impossible moment the boat stood almost still in the middle of the chaos, pivoting, and then the current took the bow and swung them through the gap with meters to spare, close enough that Callum could have spat on the rock as they passed.

And then the river calmed, as rivers do, instantly and without apology, and the silence afterward was enormous.

"Everyone alive?" Marguerite called.

A chorus of ragged affirmations. Sefu was laughing, high and shaky. Priya had braced herself over the medical kit with both arms wrapped around it, protecting it like a child.

Odile was already moving aft, and Callum saw her stop, and saw her face change, and knew before she said it.

"We're taking water."

* * *

They beached her twenty minutes later on a gravel bar backed against the canyon wall—a shelf of grey stones and driftwood just high enough, just dry enough, barely. By then the bilge pump was running continuously and losing ground anyway, and when they hauled the bow up onto the shingle and pulled the inspection hatch, the truth was plain: a seam had opened along the port strake where the first rock strike had flexed the hull, hairline from outside but gaping from within, weeping river in a steady silver thread.

"Not a repair," Odile said flatly. "Not out here. We beach her, strip what we can carry, and walk the ridge until we find a village or a landing."

"How far?" Marguerite asked.

"To the next settlement? Two days, if the weather holds." Odile looked up at the sky as she said it, and everyone else looked too, and nobody liked what they saw: cloud building over the western rim of the gorge in stacked grey towers, the kind of clouds that didn't drift so much as arrive.

"And our supplies?" Priya asked.

That was the arithmetic nobody wanted to do aloud. The leak hadn't just holed the boat; it had soaked the aft storage bay where they'd stowed the bulk rations. Two sacks of rice gone to porridge. Half the canned goods rusted and swelling. And worse—the spare fuel cans, lashed to the stern rail for exactly this kind of emergency, had been swept clean off during the rapids. Callum had watched them go, three yellow drums tumbling away downriver like toys, and had not been able to do a single thing about it.

They spent the afternoon salvaging. Everything came off the boat and got sorted on tarps: what was dry, what was wet-but-salvageable, what was lost. It made for grim accounting. Sefu laid out the electronics under a rain fly, praying silently over the satellite phone, which had survived in its case and was the single most valuable object on the gravel bar. Priya inventoried the medical stores twice, lips moving. Marguerite worked without speaking, hauling crates, wringing out bedrolls, her jaw set in a line that Callum was beginning to recognize as the shape of decisions being made behind it.

By dusk they had a camp: two tarps strung between driftwood posts, a fire of driftwood and damp kindling that smoked more than it burned, and a boat sitting dead on the stones with the river running past it, indifferent, carrying their fuel somewhere toward the sea.

"Two days of food," Priya reported finally. "Three if we stretch it. After that, we're hunting and foraging, and I'd remind everyone that monsoon country is generous with plants and half of them will put you in my care."

"Then we move at first light," Marguerite said. "All of us, everything portable. Odile leads."

Nobody argued. That was the thing about disaster, Callum thought—it simplified command structures faster than any org chart ever could.

* * *

He took first watch because he volunteered before anyone could assign it, and because he wanted to be alone, and watch duty was the only socially acceptable form of alone left on a gravel bar with five strangers.

Except she wasn't a stranger anymore, quite. He heard her footsteps on the stones before he saw her, and then Marguerite settled onto the driftwood log beside him with two tin mugs, handing him one. The coffee was terrible—burnt, gritty, made with water that tasted faintly of iron—but heat was heat.

"You drove well today," he said.

"I nearly killed us today."

"There's a difference. Narrow one, but real."

She almost smiled. In the firelight her face went young for a second, and tired, and he felt the old dangerous pull—the one that made people tell each other true things at night beside fires, because the dark made honesty seem cheaper than it was.

"Ask me," she said suddenly.

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever you've been not asking since the dock. You photograph my father's notes and say nothing. You know this river—know it *well*, I think, better than you've admitted. You look at Voss's name on the charter papers like it's a debt collector. So ask."

The fire popped. Downstream, the river muttered over its rocks, patient as ever.

"All right," Callum said. "Fair trade, then. Because you deserve more than suspicion, and I'm tired of carrying it."

He turned the mug in his hands.

"Six years ago I surveyed this valley. Not this stretch—the whole watershed. Tributaries, gorges, floodplains, the lot. I was working for a consortium doing feasibility studies, except the consortium was a shell, and the money underneath it was Edmund Voss. He wanted the valley mapped to the meter. Every rapid, every shoal, every possible dam site and access route." He paused. "I was good. I was the best he'd hired, and I knew it, and I let him pay me to know things about this place that its own government didn't know."

Marguerite said nothing, which was worse than anything she could have said.

"The Rivergate itself—I never found it. Nobody had. But I mapped the hydrology around it. I identified the flood cycle, the seepage patterns, the water table. If anyone ever builds that dam—" he nodded upstream, toward the drowned temple none of them had seen yet, "—it will hold because of numbers I produced. And if anyone ever wants to dig it out safely, drain it, blast it—they'll use the same survey. Mine."

"Why?" she said quietly. "Why would you do that work and then come out here to help stop it?"

"Because of what he's holding over me." The words came out flatter than he intended, stripped of drama, which somehow made them heavier. "I can't tell you what. Not yet. Maybe not ever. It's not mine to tell—it belongs to someone I love, and Voss has it, and as long as he has it, saying no to him isn't free. Nothing is free with Edmund. He doesn't threaten people. He just presents invoices."

"So you're here under duress."

"I'm here because he asked, and because I owed him, and because I told myself I could thread the needle—do the job, keep him satisfied, and somehow keep the Rivergate buried anyway." He laughed, short and bitter. "I believed that for about four days. Then I met you, and I watched you burn your father's letters' worth of grief into pure stubbornness, and I realized I don't want to thread any needle. I want the site to stay drowned. I want Voss to lose. I just—" His voice caught on the last of it, and he hated that. "I just can't afford for him to know that yet."

The fire cracked. Somewhere up the canyon, thunder rolled, a long low sound like furniture being dragged across the sky.

Marguerite studied him for a long moment. Whatever she was looking for, he couldn't tell whether he'd provided it.

"My father trusted no one," she said at last. "His notes say so, in his own hand—he wrote it like a warning to whoever came after him. *Trust the river, trust the stone, trust no man with a contract.*" She looked into the flames. "And here I am, on a gravel bar, with a man who admits he built the blueprint for destroying everything he cared about."

"When you say it like that—"

"When I say it like that, it sounds insane that I believe you." She turned to face him fully. "But I do. That's the part that frightens me. People lie beautifully, Callum. You didn't. You lied badly for a week and then told the truth badly, and both times you were terrible at it, and somehow that's the most convincing thing about you."

Despite everything, he laughed. "I'll take it."

"Don't. I haven't finished." Her voice dropped. "Whatever Voss is holding over you—your sister, your brother, whoever it is—if it comes down to a choice between their safety and this site, I need to know now. Not on the day. Now."

The question sat between them like a third person at the fire.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "I'd like to tell you I'd choose the right thing. I haven't been tested. That's the truth, and it's the best I have."

She held his eyes a moment longer, then nodded slowly, and something in her shoulders eased—not forgiveness exactly, but the decision to keep walking beside him anyway.

Above the western rim of the gorge, the storm towers had finished building themselves and begun, quietly, to move east. The first fat drops hit the tarp overhead like fingers testing a drum.

"Rain early," Odile said from the darkness beyond the fire, where she'd apparently been listening to all of it. "Monsoon's coming days ahead of schedule. We should not be on foot in this gorge tomorrow."

"Then we move tonight," Marguerite said, already rising, and Callum saw it in her face—that recklessness again, grief converted directly into velocity.

And then Sefu's voice cut across the camp, sharp with alarm: "Marguerite! The sat-phone—there's a signal. Someone's pinging us. From upriver."
