## 8. The First Marker

The river narrowed all morning until it felt less like a waterway and more like a throat.

Cliffs rose on either side, green-black with moss, dripping even in the dry hours between rains. The current ran fast and brown, carrying whole trees past us like matchsticks, and Odile stood at the wheel with her feet planted wide, reading the water the way Priya read a pulse—constantly, silently, with small adjustments of the tiller that kept us alive without ever announcing it.

"Half a klick," she said at last, and cut the engine.

The silence that followed was enormous. Sefu killed the depth sounder's chirp out of instinct, as if we'd wandered into a church. Ahead, where the gorge bent eastward, a tributary slid in from the north through a curtain of hanging vines, and beyond it—half-hidden, half-swallowed—the rock face opened into a dark seam no wider than a doorway.

My father had drawn this exact bend in his notes. I'd stared at his ink sketch for three nights running: the tributary entering from the north, the cliff seam like a keyhole, and beside it, in his cramped hand, *M-7 — verify datum — rope rotted, replace.*

Marker seven. The last one in his logbook with coordinates attached.

"We anchor here," I said. "Walk the rest."

Odile looked at the sky, then at me, then back at the sky, performing her usual silent negotiation with weather gods who had never once answered her. "Two hours of light," she said finally. "After that you're walking back in the dark or not at all."

"Then let's not waste them."

We took the dinghy across to the northern bank, Sefu carrying the GPS unit wrapped in oilcloth like an infant, Callum hauling the pack with the climbing line. He'd been quiet since the rapids—quieter than his usual guarded quiet, which was already quiet enough to make conversation feel like defusing something. But when I glanced at him now, he was watching the cliff seam with an expression I hadn't seen on him before. Not suspicion. Recognition.

"You've been here," I said. It wasn't a question.

"I mapped this valley six years ago." He said it flatly, offering nothing around the edges. "Voss wanted contour data for the dam feasibility study. I flew the upper gorge twice in a wet season and once in a dry one." A pause. "I never landed here. There wasn't time, and there were men on the boat who asked questions I didn't want to answer."

"What kind of questions?"

"The kind Voss pays people to ask." He adjusted the pack strap on his shoulder. "He's wanted this site since before your father disappeared, Marguerite. Whatever you think you're burning down upriver, he's been building toward it longer than you've been avoiding it."

I didn't answer. There was nothing to say that wouldn't become an argument, and arguments cost light, and we had two hours of it.

The bank rose steeply from the waterline, terraced by centuries of flood deposits, and we climbed through ferns thick enough to hide a body. Sefu went first with the machete, then me, then Callum, with Priya bringing up the rear because she refused to be anywhere but last—"so I can see everyone bleeding," she'd explained once, deadpan, and none of us had laughed, because she hadn't been joking.

The GPS put us within thirty meters of my father's coordinates. After that, it was eyes.

Sefu found it.

"Here!" His voice cracked upward with excitement, and then, immediately, with something else. "Marguerite. Here. You need to see this."

I pushed through the fern screen and stopped so abruptly that Callum walked into my back.

The marker stood against the cliff base where the seam opened—a steel survey pin driven into living rock, capped with a brass plate gone green with age. My father's initials were stamped into it: **E.W.** Edmund Winterbourne had driven thousands of these pins across four continents; I'd grown up thinking every mountain came with one, like a flag on a finished map.

But it was the rope that stopped my breath.

A length of braided polypropylene, red-and-white, was tied off around the pin in a bowline—my father's knot, the one he'd taught me before I could ride a bicycle, the one he used for everything because he claimed a bowline never slips and neither should a man—and the rope was *bright*. Unfaded. The fibers still held their factory sheen. Rain had beaded on it and dried, leaving faint mineral tidemarks, but there was no mold, no sun-bleaching, none of the gray rot that would have claimed it after even one full monsoon cycle.

"This rope is new," Priya said quietly, beside me. She touched it with two fingers, professional, clinical. "Less than a year old. Possibly less than six months."

My father had disappeared eleven years ago.

The official record—coast guard filings, the expedition insurance claim, the memorial service I'd sat through at nineteen with my hands folded and my jaw locked—placed his last known position two hundred kilometers downstream, near the delta trading post where his boat had been found adrift and empty. Drowned, they said. Taken by the river, they said. I'd spent a decade letting them say it, because the alternative was admitting he'd chosen this place over coming home, and I hadn't decided yet which truth would hurt more.

But he had stood here. Tied this knot here. And not eleven years ago—recently. Recently enough that the rope hadn't lived through a single wet season.

"He faked it," Sefu breathed. "The disappearance. He came back."

"Or someone else tied it," Callum said.

I turned on him. "Who? Who else ties a bowline exactly like—"

"Someone who read his field manuals. Someone who studied him." Callum's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Marguerite, look at the ground. Look at the fern break. This path has been cut more than once, and recently. Those stems are regrowing over older cuts." He crouched, parting the vegetation at the marker's base. "There are boot prints under here. At least two sets. One set's yours, going by the size."

"And the other?"

He looked up at me, and for once there was no calculation in his face—only a bleak, plain warning. "Bigger. Heavier tread. Military surplus, if I had to guess." He stood slowly. "Your father may have come back. But he wasn't alone, and whoever was with him—or whoever came after him—has walked this exact path within the last year. Maybe within the last month."

The gorge suddenly felt very narrow. Very loud with dripping water.

Priya was scanning the cliff seam with her headlamp beam, though the daylight made it useless. "If he replaced the rope, he went in," she said. "You don't re-rig a descent line for sentiment."

"No," I said. My own voice sounded far away. "You don't."

*Verify datum,* his note had said. Not *explore.* Not *descend.* Verify. As if he'd expected to come back again. As if marker seven were a door he intended to walk through more than once.

I knelt and untied the bowline—not pulling it loose, but untying it properly, the way he'd taught me, preserving the rope's memory of the knot—and coiled it into my pack. If he'd touched this rope, I was keeping it. It was the closest thing to holding his hand that the world had left me.

"Two hours of light," Odile called up from below, her voice flattened by distance into something almost gentle. "Whatever you found, decide what to do with it fast."

What I'd found was proof that my father's death was a story someone had told me to keep me away from this river. What I'd found was boot prints that weren't his. What I'd found was a door, freshly oiled, that other people knew how to open.

"We go back," I said. "Tonight we cross-reference the logbook against the survey pins downstream. If he re-rigged seven, he'll have touched the others."

Callum said nothing, but I caught him looking north along the ridge line, upstream, toward the deep country where the Rivergate waited—and the look on his face was the look of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like the sum of.

*

We reached the boat as the light failed.

Odile had dinner going on the single burner—rice and tinned fish and the chili paste Sefu hoarded like currency—and the smell of it almost made the day feel ordinary. Almost. We ate in the wheelhouse with the rain starting again on the roof, a soft static hiss, and I spread my father's logbook on the chart table under the lamp while the others pretended not to watch me read.

The entries grew stranger as they approached the end. Coordinates repeated. Soundings recorded in a unit system he'd abandoned twenty years ago. And on the final page, in handwriting pressed hard enough to emboss the paper beneath:

*M-7 re-rigged. The stair shows at low water. I saw carvings. I am not ready to write down what they say.*

*I am not ready to write down what they say.*

Eleven years of anger sat in my chest like ballast, and beneath the anger, rising slowly through it, something worse: relief. He hadn't drowned. He hadn't abandoned us for a temple. He had simply run out of time to finish becoming the man who could explain himself—and the explanation was still down there, behind the seam, waiting at the bottom of a stair that only showed at low water.

"Marguerite." Odile's voice, low. "Kill the lamp."

I looked up. Her hand was on the wheelhouse door, her ear tilted toward the rain. "What—"

"Engine," she said. "Upstream. Idling. It's been idling for ten minutes."

The rain filled the silence, patient, indifferent. Then, beneath it: a sound like a slow heartbeat, muffled by water and stone. An engine holding position. Holding *still*. No boat holds still on this current unless it's anchored, and nobody anchors in a monsoon gorge at night unless they don't want to be seen moving.

"Kill everything," I whispered.

Sefu killed the burner. The cabin plunged into orange-dark, lit only by the dying coals' glow through the port. We froze—four people and their held breath—while the heartbeat sound persisted somewhere above the bend, close enough now that I could hear the tick of a cooling manifold between its pulses.

"They've been following us since the second night," Callum murmured. "This is the first time they've stopped."

"Why stop?" Priya whispered.

"Because they're done following," Odile said.

It happened fast after that.

A splash forward—port side, near the bow line—and then a shape over the gunwale, black on black, moving with the economy of someone who had done this before. I heard Priya's sharp intake of breath, heard Odile swear and lunge for the wheelhouse, and then the intruder was inside our circle of darkness, and there was a scuffle I couldn't parse by sound alone: a grunt, a body hitting deck plating, the crack of something breaking.

The lamp flared back on—Odile, slamming the switch, choosing sudden light over blindness—and in its glare I saw a figure in a hooded rain shell halfway over the stern rail, and in his fist, trailing its lanyard, the waterproof satchel with my father's field notes.

"No—" I was moving before I'd decided to, grabbing for the satchel strap as he swung over the rail. My fingers closed on canvas. For one suspended second we were both holding it, him dangling above the black water, the rain hammering down between us, and through the gap of his hood I saw a young face, calm, almost apologetic.

"Sorry," he said, in accented English, and meant it, which was the most frightening thing about it.

Then he let go of the rail and dropped, and the river took him the way the river takes everything—swiftly, silently, without argument. The satchel tore from my hands. I saw the white flash of it sinking, saw the wake of his swim stroke bending away toward the far bank, toward the anchored engine, toward whatever waited upstream in the dark.

"Boat!" Sefu shouted. "Get the boat—"

"No." Odile's hand closed on my shoulder, iron-hard. "Night water, monsoon current, no lights. You chase him, you drown, and he keeps the notes anyway."

She was right. I knew she was right. Knowing it changed nothing about the animal fury tearing through my chest, the need to *do* something, anything, besides stand on my own deck in the rain and watch my father's last words sink into the same river that had supposedly taken him.

Callum appeared at my elbow, breathing hard, a gash above his eyebrow welling blood. In the struggle he'd gotten between the intruder and the wheelhouse; the intruder had paid for the notes with a rifle butt across Callum's skull, judging by the shape of the wound.

"Are you—"

"I'm fine." He wiped the blood with the back of his wrist, staring at the empty water. "Marguerite. That man apologized. Before he took it. He *apologized*."

"So?"

"So hired guns don't apologize." His voice dropped. "That was a man following orders he hates, from someone who knows exactly what those notes contain. Someone who's known for a long time." He turned to look at me, rain streaming down his face, and the lamplight caught something raw in his expression that I couldn't name and didn't trust. "Voss didn't send us up this river to find the Rivergate first. He sent us to find it *for* him. We've been the advance party since the dock."

The engine upstream started—once, briefly, adjusting position—and then fell silent again, satisfied, retreating into the rain's white noise.

I stood at the stern rail until my hands stopped shaking, watching the black water where the satchel had gone down. Somewhere south of us, Voss was reading my father's handwriting by lamplight. Somewhere north of us, the stair showed only at low water, and the low water was coming.

And somewhere inside our own crew—because the intruder had known exactly which satchel, exactly where it hung, exactly when the lamp was out—I understood, with a cold settling certainty, that the thief had needed help.

I turned and looked at the three faces in the wheelhouse doorway. Odile, arms crossed. Priya, already reaching for the gash on Callum's brow. Sefu, hugging himself against the rain.

One of them had talked.

Or one of them was lying right now, standing in the warm lamplight, watching me watch them, waiting for me to guess wrong.
