## 9. Monsoon Makes Landfall

**MARGUERITE**

The first drop hit the canvas of her tent like a thrown stone.

She was awake before it finished landing—some animal part of her brain that had been counting the days since they left the delta lowlands, ticking off a calendar that said *not yet, not for another week at least*. She lay in the dark listening to the second drop, and the third, and then the sky simply opened, as if someone had cut the bottom out of the clouds with a knife.

By the time she got her boots on and pushed through the flap, Odile was already standing in the downpour beside the boat, one hand flat against the hull as though taking its pulse. Rain ran off her jaw in ropes. She didn't turn when Marguerite came up beside her.

"Early," Marguerite shouted over the roar.

"Four days." Odile's voice carried anyway; she had a boatman's habit of pitching her voice to cross water. "Maybe five. The river will come up fast now. Tonight, tomorrow. We're on a shelf of rock, so we won't drown—but we won't leave either."

"How long?"

Odile shrugged, a slow rolling motion like water finding its level. "Until it decides to stop. Could be two days. Could be ten."

Marguerite looked upriver, where the gorge walls vanished into gray curtains of rain. Somewhere up there was the drowned stair, the marker with her father's fresh rope, and whoever had crept into their camp three nights ago and taken his field notes out from under her own sleeping body. The rain would cover tracks. The rain would cover everything.

"Get everyone moving," she said. "Everything we own goes above the high-water line tonight. I don't care if we sleep standing up."

* * *

**CALLUM**

Callum spent the storm's first hour doing the thing he was actually good at: reading water.

He stood under the tarp they'd rigged between two trees and watched the river change its mind. It had been green all week, sluggish, almost polite. Now it was turning the color of milk chocolate, and it had begun to talk—a low continuous mutter under the rain-noise, the sound of a current picking up arguments with the rocks. He'd mapped this valley once, years ago, contour by contour, and he knew what that mutter meant. Somewhere upstream, whole hillsides were letting go.

Sefu crouched beside him under the tarp, hauling their electronics into dry bags with the quick, fussy precision he brought to everything he loved. "If this keeps up," the kid said, "the satellite window closes. No more fixes until it clears."

"Then we navigate the old way," Callum said. "Ask Odile. She has the whole river in her head."

"Odile has the whole river in her head," Sefu agreed, zipping a bag, "and maybe other people's property in her pack."

Callum went very still. "What did you just say?"

Sefu looked up, and there was no joke in his face at all. "I went back for the depth sounder last night. Her duffel was open. There's paper in it, folded small. I didn't look. But it smelled like your coffee, Callum—the notes always smell like coffee, Marguerite spills on everything she reads." He tied off the bag with a hard little jerk. "I'm not saying anything else. I'm saying you should know I know."

He picked up the dry bags and walked out into the rain toward the high camp, leaving Callum alone with the sound of the rising river and a cold spreading through him that had nothing to do with the weather.

Because here was the arithmetic he couldn't escape: the notes had been stolen three nights ago. If Odile had copied them before handing them over—or instead of handing them over—then the theft hadn't been an outside job at all. And if the torn pages he'd seen this morning meant what he thought they meant—

No. He made himself stop. Accusations were like survey lines: draw one wrong and every point downstream was wrong forever.

But he found himself watching Odile across the camp all the same, and hating himself for it.

* * *

**PRIYA**

Priya had treated gunshot wounds in border towns and cholera in camps where the dead outnumbered the living, and she had learned one law above all others: fear spreads faster than any pathogen, and it kills slower but more thoroughly.

She saw the first symptoms by lamplight that evening, in the cave they'd claimed above the flood line—a limestone overhang deep enough for six bedrolls and a cook fire, with the river roaring past thirty feet below like a freight train that never passed.

Nobody spoke much. That was symptom one.

Sefu sat closest to the entrance, which put him farthest from Odile. Symptom two.

And when Marguerite laid out the evening ration of rice, she gave Odile her portion last, with a pause before it—half a heartbeat, nothing, less than nothing—and Priya, who could read a pulse through a shirt sleeve, read it perfectly.

Symptom three.

"You should eat," Priya told Odile quietly, settling beside her at the mouth of the overhang. The old woman was watching the water. She'd been watching the water for hours.

"I'll eat."

"You've been saying that since noon."

Odile's laugh was a dry bark. "You doctors. You see too much and say too little." She took the bowl finally, held it in both hands without eating. "Thirty years I've run boats on this river. I have been robbed by soldiers, cheated by companies, shot at twice and stabbed once. You know what none of them ever managed?" She looked at Priya, and her eyes in the firelight were ancient and furious and hurt. "None of them ever made me a traitor. And now some piece of paper does it in one night, without me even touching it."

Priya kept her voice level. "Did you touch it?"

"That is not my answer to give tonight." Odile looked back at the river. "Ask again when the water drops. Maybe I'll be brave by then. Water rises faster than courage."

* * *

**MARGUERITE**

They found the rest of the notes at dawn.

Not all of them. Not even most of them. Sefu spotted the color first—a scrap of white caught in the roots of a fallen fig tree at the river's edge, forty meters below the cave, plastered flat against the wood by the rain. Then another, snagged on a reed clump. Then another, already dissolving, ink bleeding into gray ghosts of her father's handwriting.

Marguerite waded out along the bank with the water pulling at her knees, gathering what she could, and each piece told her something worse than the last.

These weren't random pages. They'd been torn deliberately—ripped into quarters, eighths, some pieces shredded past reading—and scattered along fifty meters of bank where anyone walking the shore would find them. This wasn't litter. This was a message written in the only language everyone in this valley could read: *your father's words are garbage, and one of you is the one who threw them away.*

She climbed back up to the overhang and laid the salvageable fragments on a flat stone by the fire. Eleven pieces. Fragments of tables of measurements. Half a sketch of the gorge profile. One corner bearing a single legible line in her father's tight, slanted hand: *—cannot be allowed to reach the tablets, whatever Voss promis—*

The silence around the fire had weight, like the water below.

"They were taken three nights ago," Marguerite said. Her voice sounded strange to her, flattened, as if the storm had pressed it thin. "Whoever took them kept them at least one day. Then tore them up and scattered them along the bank—along *our* side of the bank, where we'd walk. That means they came back. That means someone left this camp, or never left it, and wanted us to find these."

"Or the river took them from whoever stole them," Priya said carefully. "A bag lost in the rapids tears exactly like that."

"A bag lost in the rapids doesn't tear along fold lines," Callum said quietly. "Those are quarter-folds. Deliberate."

Every eye went where his words pointed, and then, as if pulled by gravity, slid sideways—to Odile.

The old woman sat with her arms crossed, looking into the fire, and she did not flinch and she did not explain herself. That was the worst part, Marguerite thought. Guilt would have been easier. Guilt would have given them something to hold.

"Say it," Odile said finally, to the flames. "Whatever you're all chewing on. Say it and let's be done."

"Your duffel was open last night," Sefu said. His voice cracked in the middle and he hated it, Marguerite could see him hating it. "There was folded paper in it."

"And you looked through my things like a thief, and found paper, and decided paper equals guilt." Odile turned her head at last and looked at each of them in turn, unhurried, terrible. "Yes. There is paper in my bag. There is paper in everyone's bag. Maps, receipts, letters I don't show strangers. I am sixty-one years old and I do not owe my laundry to children."

"Nobody's calling you a thief," Marguerite said.

"No," Odile said. "You're all standing around the fire deciding how to say it politely. That is worse." She rose, joints cracking, and gathered her bowl and her bedroll. "I'll take first watch. Alone. Apparently that's all I'm good for now."

She walked to the mouth of the overhang and stood with her back to them, a silhouette against the gray hammering rain, and nobody followed her, and everybody watched her, and the trust of four people who had signed on together in bad coffee light came apart as quietly as wet rope.

* * *

**CALLUM**

Callum couldn't sleep. He sat against the cave wall with his knees drawn up, running the survey of his own memory the way he'd run surveys of rivers—again and again, looking for the error bar, the place where his readings diverged from truth.

Three nights ago. The night the notes were stolen. What did he remember?

Rain starting near midnight—heavy, drumming on the tarps. Priya asleep mid-sentence. Sefu's radio playing low until Odile told him to kill it. Himself, awake later than he admitted to anyone, because he'd been sitting in the dark writing a letter he would never send to his sister, and he had heard—

What had he heard?

Footsteps. Soft ones, going down toward the boat, and he'd assumed—God help him, he'd assumed it was Odile checking the moorings, because that was what Odile did, that was the most natural thing in the world, and he'd rolled over and gone back to his letter.

But there had been two sets of footsteps. He was almost sure now. One going down, heavy, unhurried. And later—one coming back up, lighter, faster, stopping briefly near the tents before continuing past.

Stopping near the tents. Where the notes were.

He closed his eyes. Two sets of footsteps meant two possibilities, and one of them was that Odile had gone down to the boat to meet someone, and that someone had come up alone.

The other possibility he refused to write down even in his own head, because writing it down would make it a survey line, and every point downstream would be wrong forever.

Across the fire, Marguerite was awake too. He could tell by her breathing—not sleep-breathing, thinking-breathing. She caught him looking and held it, and in the ember-light her face was stripped down to essentials: grief, fury, and a question she hadn't asked anyone yet.

"The bank," she said softly, so as not to wake the others. "Where the pieces were scattered. I keep turning it over. It's our side, yes. But it's also—" she stopped, jaw working.

"It's also the side facing the water," Callum finished. "Visible from a boat."

"Yes." Her eyes were very bright and very cold. "Someone wanted us to find them. But someone else might have wanted us to *be found looking.*"

Down at the mouth of the overhang, Odile stood her watch, motionless as a post driven into the rock, while below her the river climbed and climbed, brown and roaring, carrying toward them everything the mountains were giving up—branches, silt, secrets—and somewhere inside that rising water, Marguerite thought, was the drowned stair, and her father's last marker, and the people who had followed them this far and were not done following.

The rain did not slacken. It did not even pretend to.

Toward morning, Sefu's radio crackled once through static—a fragment, half a word, a voice speaking English with an accent none of them owned—and then died, and no one slept after that at all.
