## 10. The Drowned Stair

**MARGUERITE**

The river rose a hand's width overnight.

Marguerite knew it the way she knew weather in her bones—by the smell of the water, by the way Odile stood at the bow at dawn and said nothing, which was worse than anything he could have said. The monsoon had come early, and it had come hungry. What had been a brown ribbon threading through the gorge three days ago was now a broad, muscular animal, shouldering against the banks, carrying whole trees past their camp like matchsticks.

"Another meter and we lose the lower shelf," Priya said, crouching beside the fire they'd built under an overhang of rock. She was rewrapping Sefu's bandages from the rapids—nothing serious, a gash along his forearm, but she treated it like a ritual, something to do with her hands while her eyes kept going to the water.

"Then we move everything up tonight," Marguerite said. "All of it. No arguments."

Sefu looked up from the satellite phone he'd been cradling like a dying bird. "Still no signal. The storm cell's sitting right on top of us."

"Then we're on our own until it moves." She said it lightly, and hated how easily the lie came now.

It was Callum who found the stair.

She'd sent him upriver along the eastern bank that morning—not because she trusted him, but because she needed to know whether she did, and there was no better test than sending a man alone into country where he could simply walk away. He came back at midday, soaked to the chest, breathless in a way that had nothing to do with the climb.

"You need to see this," was all he said.

* * *

**CALLUM**

He'd been following the seam in the rock for two hours before he understood what he was looking at.

Callum Reyes had mapped this valley once, years ago, from a helicopter and a hundred grainy aerial photographs, and he had marked the eastern cliff face as unremarkable limestone—karst, fractured, boring. He had been wrong. The rising water had changed the light. Where the flood backed into the side channels, it pooled against the cliff base and threw reflected sun upward onto stone that had never seen direct daylight, and in that slanted, trembling illumination, the cut lines appeared.

Not natural fractures. Chisel work. Straight as surveyor's string.

He'd stood there a long time, water climbing his thighs, feeling the particular vertigo of a man whose profession is being wrong in public. Then he'd waded closer, and found the top step.

It broke the surface like the back of some enormous sleeping animal: a single slab of black basalt, maybe four feet wide, its edge worn round by centuries of current, but its face carved—and here his breath actually caught—with a line of glyphs running lengthwise, each one filled with silt that made them stand out pale against the dark stone. A stairway. Descending into the cliff itself. Descending into the flood.

He counted eleven visible steps before the water swallowed the rest.

Now he led Marguerite back along the bank, and watched her face as the same seam of rock came into view, and thought: *this is the moment I've been paid to document, and I have never hated a job more.*

"There," he said, and pointed.

Marguerite stopped walking. For a moment she didn't move at all, and Callum saw something pass over her expression that he recognized from photographs of her father—the same stillness, the same hunger held on a very short leash.

"The entrance was never supposed to be at the temple platform," she said quietly. "Everyone assumed you approached from the ridge. From above." Her voice had gone strange, reverent and furious at once. "You approach from the water. You always approach from the water."

"The valley floods every year," Callum said. "It's been flooding for—what, four thousand years? The builders didn't fight the river. They built for it. This stair is only reachable when the water's high." He gestured at the swollen channel, the drowned steps below. "Your father wasn't looking in the wrong season. He was looking in the wrong decade. The river's been lower than it was for most of history. Climate cycles. Sediment loads. He couldn't have known."

"He knew." Marguerite was already pulling off her boots. "Look at his notes—the last entry, the one about 'the door that opens only to the patient.' He wasn't being poetic. He was being literal. He knew the water would rise again someday. He just—" She stopped. Swallowed. "He just ran out of patience himself."

"Marguerite." He said her name the way you'd say *careful* without saying it. "We don't know what's down there. We don't know how far it goes, or whether there's air on the other end, or whether the whole thing comes down the moment we put weight on stone that hasn't been load-bearing since the Bronze Age."

"I know."

"And Voss's people are somewhere behind us, and the water is still rising, and we have maybe—"

"I *know*, Callum." She looked at him then, and her eyes were bright and terrified and absolutely certain, and he understood in that moment that he was going to follow her down, whatever he said now, whatever he believed. That had already happened, somewhere between the leak in the rapids and the night he'd told her half the truth about Voss. His objections were ceremonial. "But my father died trying to find this door. And it's open. Right now. Today. And tomorrow the flood takes it back, and we may never see it again."

The water lapped at the eleventh step, patient as everything else in this valley.

"We go down together," Callum said finally. "Rope on both of us. Thirty minutes, no more. And if the water starts moving faster than a walk, we come straight back up. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

He went to get the rope.

* * *

**MARGUERITE**

The water was warmer than she expected, and murkier, and it took the first five submerged steps by feel—her boots sliding over silt-slicked stone, Callum's hand locked around her forearm, counting aloud so she could hear him through the roar of blood in her ears. At the eighth step her head went under entirely, and for three seconds the world was green-brown chaos and pressure, and then she surfaced inside.

Inside.

The sound hit her first—a vast, hollow resonance, the acoustic signature of a space too large for the darkness to suggest. Their headlamps swept across walls that drank the light and gave back almost nothing, and then, as her eyes adjusted, gave back everything: carved panels, floor to ceiling, receding into blackness on either side of a corridor wide enough for six abreast. Processional figures. Rows of them, hundreds, thousands, all facing the same direction—all facing *outward*, toward the stair, toward the river—as if the entire carved population of a dead civilization had turned to watch whoever came in from the water.

"Oh," Marguerite said. It was not a word so much as an exhalation that had given up trying to be language.

"Don't touch the walls," Callum said, but his voice had lost all its authority; it was the voice of a man talking to keep himself anchored. "Silt on everything. One brush and we lose detail that's survived four thousand years."

She wasn't listening. She was reading.

The glyphs here were larger than on the exterior step, cleaner, and her father's letters—those desperate, beautiful, maddening letters she'd read by lamplight in a waystation a lifetime ago—had included rubbings, fragments of transliteration, his own speculative glossary. Enough that the first panel resolved into sense the way a face resolves out of shadow:

*When the enemy came, we did not meet them with iron.*

*We met them with water.*

Her legs nearly folded. She put a hand out and caught herself on air, remembering the silt at the last instant, and stood shaking in the dark while four-thousand-year-old sentences rearranged everything she thought she knew about the woman who'd raised herself on her father's absence.

"They weren't conquered," she whispered. "They were never conquered. They *chose* this. They flooded their own city rather than surrender it, and then they sealed the records down here where only the river could reach them, and they—" She laughed, and it came out broken. "They left instructions. For whoever was patient enough. For whoever came back when the water rose."

Callum's lamp found her face. "Marguerite, your thirty minutes started six minutes ago."

"I know." She wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist, tasting river water and salt. "One more panel. Just one more."

* * *

**CALLUM**

He let her have the panel, because he was already photographing it, and because some traitorous part of him wanted to know what it said too.

The second panel showed the drowning itself—or the ceremony of it, the human figures rendered small and orderly against the monumental architecture, walking down stairs exactly like the one they'd climbed down, carrying what looked like tablets, bundles, children. Below them, rendered in careful registers, the water rising in measured bands. Not a catastrophe. An evacuation conducted with the calm of people who had rehearsed it, who had perhaps spent generations preparing for the day the enemy would come and the answer would be the river.

And at the bottom register, the last row of figures, the ones who had stayed longest: they were shown kneeling, hands flat on the stone, heads bowed. Waiting for the water. Welcoming it.

Callum had surveyed dams. He had drawn flood lines for men who moved villages the way you'd move furniture, and he had told himself the arithmetic was clean—that progress was a flood you planned for, and the people displaced were a cost entered in a column. Standing in this corridor, watching carved ancestors kneel willingly into the dark water, he felt the arithmetic crack down the middle.

Voss wanted to blast this place apart to salvage what could be sold before the dam reservoir covered it forever. Callum's map was the instrument that would make it efficient. Clean. Cost-effective.

His camera clicked. Clicked. Each frame a small betrayal he was committing in advance.

"Callum." Marguerite's voice, sharp, suddenly. "Listen."

He listened. Above the drip and settle of the flooded corridor, beneath the distant boom of the river working at the cliff face, there was a new sound: a low, grinding groan, deep in the structure, like a ship changing its mind about sinking.

"The water's pushing into chambers that haven't been pressurized in centuries," he said, and the professional calm in his own voice frightened him more than the sound did. "Something's giving way. Upstream of us, maybe. Maybe downstream. We're leaving. Now."

"But the corridor keeps going—it goes *down*, Callum, there are more panels, there could be the archive itself—"

"And it'll be there next year, and the year after, because unlike my father—" She stopped. Corrected herself with visible effort. "Unlike *me*. Unlike us. The river doesn't run out of patience."

She grabbed his sleeve. For one heartbeat neither of them moved, standing in the carved dark between the past and the flood, and then the groan came again, louder, and somewhere ahead of them in the blackness there was a sound like a giant clearing its throat, and the water around their knees began, gently, horribly, to *move*.

* * *

**MARGUERITE**

The current found them on the ninth step.

It came from deeper in the complex—a surge of displaced water funneling toward the entrance shaft, and suddenly the stairwell that had been a calm flooded throat was a drain, dragging at their legs, shoving them sideways against the carved walls. Marguerite got one arm through the safety rope and felt Callum slam into her back, both of them wedged against the stone while the brown water boiled past at thigh height, then waist height, then—

"Up!" Callum's mouth was at her ear, screaming over the roar. "*Up, up, UP!*"

They climbed. The submerged steps that had taken five careful minutes on the way down took ninety seconds of pure animal panic on the way up—heads down, lungs burning, the surface a shimmering rumor above them that seemed to retreat every time they reached for it. Marguerite's boot slipped on the silted twelfth step and she went under completely, and in the green chaos she felt Callum's fist close on her collar and haul, and then her head broke water and she was coughing, gasping, alive, hauling herself up the final exposed steps on hands and knees while the flood poured out of the doorway behind them like the building was exhaling.

They collapsed on the bank, twenty meters back from the cliff, and lay in the mud and the rain and breathed.

For a long time neither of them spoke. The river boomed against the entrance. Water still sheeted from the stairway mouth, but slower now, the surge spent, the structure settling back into its long patience.

"It's closing," Marguerite said at last. Her voice was wrecked. "Isn't it. By tomorrow the whole stair will be under. Maybe by tonight."

"Maybe." Callum rolled onto his back. Rain hit his face and he didn't blink it away. "The dam reservoir will put forty meters over it. Permanently. Whatever's down there—the rest of the panels, the archive, your father's desk, all of it—" He stopped.

"All of it stays drowned," she finished. "Unless someone gets to it first. With explosives. With a *map*."

She sat up. Rain streamed off her hair. Downriver, faint through the gray curtain of the storm, she heard it—an engine note, low and diesel-heavy, quite different from Odile's boat. Not approaching. Holding position. Waiting, the way Voss waited, the way the river waited.

Callum heard it too. She watched him hear it, watched something shut behind his eyes, and thought: *he knows exactly whose engine that is, and he knows exactly why it's here, and he has not yet told me the whole truth.*

Later. There would be time for that later, or there wouldn't, and either way the water was rising and the decision was making itself.

"Back to camp," she said, standing, offering him her hand. "We need to move everything to high ground tonight. And Callum—" She pulled him up, held his eyes. "Whatever you're not telling me about Edmund Voss, you have about twelve hours to tell me. Because after that, none of us will have the luxury of secrets."

The engine note throbbed somewhere in the rain, patient as stone.

They walked back into the storm.
