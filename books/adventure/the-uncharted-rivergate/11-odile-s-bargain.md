## 11. Odile's Bargain

The rain came in sideways off the river, and Odile Marchand stood in the lee of the overturned boat and watched Marguerite Winterbourne read the torn pages like a woman reading her own death warrant.

She had seen that face before. On her brother Étienne, the night the collectors came to Port-au-Prince with Voss's ledger and a photograph of their mother's house. That particular stillness. That particular way the jaw set, like a door being locked from the inside.

The notes had turned up that morning, wedged in the root tangle of a fallen fig tree forty meters downstream, wrapped in oilcloth that had not been wrapped by anyone in this crew. Torn along the spine. Pages missing. Sefu had carried them back to camp with the reverence of a man returning a body, and Priya had said nothing, and Callum had looked at the river instead of at anyone, and Marguerite had stood in the rain holding her father's handwriting in pieces and asked, very quietly, who.

Nobody answered. Nobody had to. There were four of them left on this ridge, and Odile had known since the third night upriver, since the moment she'd slid the notebook from the chart case while Marguerite slept and copied it by headlamp in the hold, her hands shaking so badly she'd smeared ink across her father's survey of the eastern stair.

She had told herself she wouldn't give it to them. That was the lie she'd built her bunk on, the one she'd slept on every night since. *I'll copy it and keep the copy. I'll never pass it along. Voss can have the words, not the meaning.* As if a map were a thing you could halve. As if a betrayal could be kept in a drawer and stay a betrayal only on paper.

The rain drummed the hull above her. Marguerite folded the torn notes with terrible care, the way you fold a flag, and turned.

"Odile."

Not a question. That was the worst of it. Not *did you*, not *why*—just her name, spoken the way you speak the name of a storm you've been tracking for days.

Odile came out from under the hull. Let the rain hit her, because she deserved the weight of it. She was sixty-one years old and she had run boats through three wars and she had never once been the one the others looked at when something went wrong, and now she stood in front of a woman half her age and understood that she had finally, at the end of everything, become the thing that goes wrong.

"You want to say it out loud," Odile said. "Or you want me to."

"I want to know why."

Simple. Merciless. Marguerite had a way of asking simple questions that landed like anchor stones.

Odile looked past her, at the camp—Priya's tarp shelter, the cold stove, Sefu sitting with his arms around his knees watching them with the wide eyes of a boy watching his family come apart. The river had come up two meters in the last day. It moved through the gorge below like something with shoulders.

"Étienne," Odile said. "My brother. He ran cargo out of Cap-Haïtien. Not the kind of cargo you put on manifests." She heard her own voice go flat, the voice she used for river reports and engine trouble. "He borrowed. From a man who borrowed from a man who borrowed from Voss. That's how it works with men like Voss. You never owe him. You owe the chain, and the chain owes him, and every link squeezes." She wiped rain from her face with the back of her wrist. "Three years ago the debt came to me. Forty thousand dollars, or the name of the man who could get a survey team into the valley without government permits. Voss had heard of me. He'd heard I worked the delta, and he'd heard whose boat ran the lowlands, and he'd heard—"

She stopped. The rain filled the silence.

"He'd heard I'd run a boat for Édouard Winterbourne. Twice. Before."

Marguerite flinched. It was small, a contraction around the eyes, but Odile saw it, and hated herself for seeing it.

"He didn't ask me to steal anything," Odile said. "Not at first. He asked me to take the job and report. What the crew was. What the plan was. And then you brought his notes aboard, and Voss's man on the satellite phone said, *describe the notes*, and I did, and then he said, *we need them*, and I said no." She laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry broken thing. "I said no. I want you to know that. I stood on this deck and told a man with forty thousand dollars of my brother's blood on his books *no*. And then I sat up all night thinking about Étienne's daughter, who is nine, and who lives in a house that men can find, because men like Voss always know where the houses are. And I got up, and I copied them."

"You copied them." Marguerite's voice was very level. "And then what. You were going to hand them over at the marker. Is that what the night engine was? A handoff?"

"No handoff ever happened." Odile held her ground. "I copied them and I kept them, and every day I told myself tomorrow, and every day I looked at you and couldn't. And then the leak, and the camp raid—I didn't do that. I swear on Étienne's grave I didn't. But when the notes went missing I knew who'd take the blame, and I knew it was me, and I knew I deserved it, and I still couldn't say it. Because saying it meant the copying was real. Saying it made me the woman who did it, instead of the woman who almost did."

"You are the woman who did it."

"Yes."

Marguerite turned away. Walked three steps to the edge of the shelter and stood looking at the brown water rising through the trees, and Odile let her look, because there was nothing else to give her.

When she turned back, her face had done something Odile hadn't expected. It hadn't hardened. It had *cleared*, like silt settling out of floodwater, and what was left underneath was tired and young and grieving.

"Where are your copies now?"

"Gone." Odile said it before the shame could stop her. "The night the camp was raided. Whoever hit us took the chart case and the hold. Everything I had was in the hold." She spread her hands. "I kept the originals safe and lost the copies. That's the whole of my treachery, Marguerite. A copy of a copy, and the river took even that."

"And Voss?"

"Still has Étienne. That doesn't wash off. I know that." Odile stepped closer, boots sucking in the mud. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to let me work. Give me the worst jobs between here and the temple. Let me earn my way back one rope at a time. Or put me ashore at the next rise and I'll walk out of this valley alone, and I'll deserve that too."

Behind them, Sefu had gotten up. Priya's hand on his shoulder kept him where he was, but his voice carried through the rain, young and cracking: "She fed us. She pulled the boat off the bar at the rapids with her own hands. You were all going to die on that bar and she didn't hesitate—"

"That's not the point, Sefu," Marguerite said, without turning.

"Maybe it's exactly the point."

"It's a point," Marguerite agreed. "It's just not the one that decides."

She looked at Odile for a long moment. Rain ran off her chin. Somewhere up the gorge, thunder rolled and kept rolling, longer than thunder should.

"My father trusted you," Marguerite said. "That's what I keep coming back to. Ten years I've been angry at him, and the one thing I know about him is that he didn't trust easily, and he trusted you with his boat and his life twice. So either he was a fool twice, or there's something in you worth the risk." She exhaled. "I don't know which. I don't think you know which."

"No," Odile said. "I don't."

"Then here's what I know." Marguerite's voice didn't rise at all, and somehow that was louder than shouting. "Voss took my father's notes out of my camp, through your hands or someone else's, and he's coming up this river with armed men and a map to my father's grave. Every hour we spend on this ridge, he gets closer. So I don't have time for exile. I don't have time for punishment. I have time for work." She held out her hand—not a handshake, Odile saw, but the flat open palm of someone offering a tool. "You want to earn your way back? Then from this minute, everything you know about Voss—his routes, his timetables, the name of the man on the satellite phone, every link in that chain—you give it to me. All of it. And when his boats come around the bend, you stand on this deck with us. Not because I forgive you. Because the water's rising and I need every hand I trust *enough*."

Odile took the hand. It was not forgiveness. It was something harder and more useful than forgiveness, and she gripped it with both of hers like a woman gripping a line in current.

"All of it," she said. "You'll have all of it."

"Then get the stove lit. We've got a long night of maps ahead of us."

Odile turned toward the shelter—and stopped.

Because downriver, around the black shoulder of the gorge, two lights had appeared where no lights should be. Running without running lights, low against the water, coming fast through flood season.

Coming straight for them.

* * *

They killed the fire and doused the lamp and stood in the dark listening to engines.

Odile knew engines the way priests knew scripture, and these were wrong. Two of them, outboard, running rich and rough—the sound of boats overloaded past their rating, pushed hard by a man who didn't care if the engines lived. Voss's boats. Coming up a gorge in flood water at night, which meant he wasn't being cautious anymore, which meant he believed he already knew where they were.

Which meant someone had told him.

"Odile." Marguerite's voice in the dark, barely breath. "The satellite phone. The man on the other end. Can you reach him?"

"I can reach his frequency." She was already moving, pulling the tarp off the equipment crate, hands steady now that there was work, because work was the only penance she'd ever been any good at. "Whether he'll talk is another question."

"Tell him the notes were destroyed in the raid. Tell him the copies are gone. Tell him whatever buys us until dawn."

"And if he doesn't believe me?"

Marguerite was a silhouette against the faint grey of the water, and when she spoke, Odile heard her father in her voice for the first time.

"Then we'll know by how fast the lights come."

Odile keyed the handset and spoke into the rain, lying smoothly in two languages about destroyed papers and drowned men, and the voice on the other end was quiet a long moment, and then said: *Tell Marchand the debt is due at the temple. Tell her to leave the door open.*

The transmission died. Odile lowered the handset with hands that had finally, fully stopped shaking, because they had nothing left to protect.

"He's not coming to take the notes," she said. "He's coming because there's nothing left to take. He's coming for the temple itself."

Marguerite turned toward the two lights sweeping the gorge wall, and Odile watched her do the arithmetic every boat captain dreads—water, distance, time—and arrive at the only answer there was.

"Then we'd better get there first."
