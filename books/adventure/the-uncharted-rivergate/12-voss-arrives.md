## 12. Voss Arrives

The rain had settled into a rhythm by the third day on the ridge—steady, gray, indifferent—so when Odile's voice cut across the camp, hoarse with something that wasn't quite alarm, Marguerite knew before she turned that the waiting was over.

"Boats," Odile said. "Two of them. Coming up the channel."

Marguerite climbed the slick granite shelf above their camp, Callum a half-step behind her, and looked down into the drowned valley that had swallowed her father's life. The river had swollen past every marker Odile had known in thirty years of running these waters. Whole trees rode the current like drowned men, and the channel that had been a brown ribbon two weeks ago was now a lake in slow motion, lapping at the base of the ridge.

The boats were beautiful. That was the first thing she noticed, and the thing that made her chest tighten. They were aluminum-hulled, twin-engined, with canvas canopies rigged against the rain and cargo strapped under waterproof tarps with a precision that made her own expedition look like what it was—a handful of strays in a leaking boat. They came up the flooded channel against the current with the unhurried confidence of machines that had never been asked to fail.

Behind them, faint through the rain, a third engine. The one that had haunted their nights since the gorges.

"They've been following us since the narrows," Callum said quietly. "Why wait until now?"

"Because now there's nowhere left to run." Marguerite watched the lead boat swing toward the only landing on the ridge—a shelf of flat rock below their camp, the same shelf her father must have used twenty years ago. "And because he wanted us to spend our supplies first. He wanted us hungry when he arrived. It's not a threat. It's a negotiation tactic."

"You've met him before."

"I met him once. I was nineteen." She remembered it with embarrassing clarity: a hotel bar in the provincial capital, her father's colleague from some earlier dig, a man with a quiet voice who had bought her dinner and asked her questions no adult had ever bothered to ask—what she thought the tablets said, whether she believed the flood story was memory or metaphor. She had thought he was kind. She had told him everything. Two months later, a paper appeared under Voss's name with her father's data in it and her own theories in the footnotes, uncredited. Her father had laughed it off. *That's how the game is played, little bird.* She had never played it again.

The boats grounded. Men stepped out—six, seven, eight of them, moving with the ease of people who had done this before, and Marguerite counted the shapes under the tarps they carried: not just crates. Long cases. The kind that held rifles or drilling equipment, and on a salvage survey there was no honest reason for either.

And then a figure in a gray rain jacket stepped onto the rock, steadied himself against a mooring line, and looked up the slope directly at her, as if he had known exactly where she'd be standing.

Edmund Voss was older than she remembered. Sixty, maybe more, with a white beard trimmed short and a face weathered into something like kindness—the kind of face that made strangers tell him their troubles. He stood at the base of the slope with his hands visible at his sides, an old man's courtesy, and called up through the rain in a voice that carried without strain:

"Marguerite Winterbourne. You have your father's habit of choosing the high ground."

She descended anyway. It was foolish and she knew it, but she would not conduct this conversation shouting across forty meters of rain, and she would not have him think she needed the advantage. Callum came with her, and behind her she heard Odile mutter something in river dialect that she was fairly sure was a curse on Voss's ancestors.

They met on the flat shelf, the water lapping six meters below, the rain coming down between them like a curtain neither of them acknowledged.

"You're early," Marguerite said. "The dam survey isn't due to report for another four months."

"The dam survey reported eight months ago." Voss smiled, and it was a warm smile, genuinely warm, which made it worse. "The government moved the timeline up. I didn't tell you the whole truth in my letter because you'd have refused, and I needed you to come. Forgive me. It's a habit I learned from the best." His eyes, pale and intelligent, went briefly up the slope toward the ridge. "I learned it from Elias."

"Don't say his name like you knew him."

"I knew him better than you did, I'm afraid. Toward the end." Voss took a step closer, unhurried, and one of his men shifted behind him—not threatening, just present, the way furniture is present in a room where something might be thrown. "You found the waystation cache. The letters. I know, because I've been looking for it for fifteen years, and if you found it in a week, it's because he wanted *you* to find it. He always did prefer you. Even when you stopped writing back."

Marguerite kept her face still. It cost her. "You've been reading my camp?"

"I've been listening to your engine at night. There's a difference." He gestured, mild, at the flooded valley. "Look at this, Marguerite. Really look. In five months this whole valley is a reservoir. Everything below the high-water line—every hall, every stair, every tablet your father died next to—will be under sixty meters of standing water and government concrete. The site won't be destroyed. It will be *erased*. No one will ever study it. No one will ever even argue about it. Is that what he would have wanted?"

"He wanted it left alone."

"Did he?" Voss's voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about him; she could feel the machinery under the gentleness, the way you can feel current under still water. "Because the man I knew spent thirty years and two marriages and his entire reputation trying to get back here. You read his letters. Tell me honestly—did they read like a man who wanted to be forgotten?"

*The Rivergate should stay drowned.* Her father's last entry, in her pocket, in oilcloth, against her skin. She said nothing, and Voss read the silence with the skill of a man who had spent a lifetime reading silences.

"He changed his mind at the end," she said. "People do that. It's allowed."

"Or he was alone, and sick, and drowning in a hole in the rock, and the last thing a dying man writes is not always the truest thing about him." Voss shook his head, and for one disorienting moment he looked genuinely grieved. "I loved him, Marguerite. I want you to understand that before anything else. Whatever you think of me—and your boatman's face suggests it's nothing flattering—I loved your father. I loved him the way you love a rival, which is the only honest way to love anyone in this profession. We spent forty years trying to beat each other to the same answers. When he disappeared, I spent my own money searching. Two years. Every channel, every village. Do you know what the academic community said? That I was mourning. That it was *sentimental*." He laughed, short and bitter. "They were right. It was sentimental. And then the dam was approved, and sentiment became a deadline, and deadlines, as you know, are the only gods this work answers to."

"So you hired guns and followed us upriver in the dark."

"I hired a hydrologist." His gaze moved to Callum, and something passed between the two men—brief, cold, a debt acknowledged without words. "And I sent a support team to keep watch on the only people foolish enough to run this river at the start of monsoon season. If I'd wanted your camp hit, Marguerite, it would have been hit. I don't burn maps. I *collect* them."

The stolen field notes. Her father's handwriting, in someone else's crate, probably wrapped in the same waterproof precision as everything else Voss owned. Marguerite felt the fury rise in her throat and made herself swallow it, because Voss was watching for exactly that.

"You have them," she said. "His notes."

"I have copies. The originals are in your possession, I believe—the letters from the waystation." He tilted his head. "I'd very much like to read them. I'd like it more than I'd like anything else in this valley, and I'm offering you a great deal of money for the privilege."

"I'm not selling my father's letters."

"You're misunderstanding the offer." Voss clasped his hands behind his back, and Marguerite recognized the posture—she had seen it in old photographs of him and her father standing on some dig site thirty years ago, two young men with the same ambition wearing different faces. "I'm not buying the letters. I'm offering you the site. Full partnership. Your name and mine on the salvage concession—your father's name too, if you want it, though I suspect you don't. My funding, my equipment, my permits. Your knowledge of his work, your crew, your—" he glanced at Callum again, and this time the glance lingered, "—cartographer. We have perhaps four months of dry-season work before the reservoir gates close. Together we could document the Rivergate properly. Every hall. Every tablet. A real excavation, with his daughter leading it. Think about what that means. Think about what it would have meant to *him*."

And the worst part—the part that made her sick with herself—was that for three full seconds, she thought about it. She felt the whole shape of it: the lights rigged in the drowned halls, her own hands on the tablets her father had touched last, his name spoken in lecture halls again, redeemed. It was a beautiful picture. It was built on the same foundation as every beautiful picture Voss had ever painted: other people's work, taken politely.

"You followed us for two weeks," she said. "You let us run the rapids at Kessou Bend with a leaking hull. My crew nearly died."

"My crew nearly died too, in '09, running that same bend with better boats." Voss's voice hardened by a single degree, and the men behind him went very still. "This river has killed better expeditions than both of ours, and it will kill more, and the only variable we control is each other. So here is my last offer, and I'll make it plainly, because I respected your father and I'm trying to respect you. Join me, and everyone on this ridge walks out of this valley rich and alive. Refuse me, and I proceed without you—with the notes, with the map I already hold, and with whatever cooperation the water leaves me." He spread his hands. "I don't want a war on this river. I've read enough about what happens to expeditions that turn on each other in monsoon country. But I will finish this. You should decide whether you'd rather finish it beside me."

Marguerite looked at him for a long moment. The rain ran off his gray hood and dripped from his beard, and he let her look, patient as stone, because he believed—she could see it in his eyes—he believed with his whole heart that he was the hero of this story. That he had loved her father, and love entitled him. That the site belonged to whoever was ruthless enough and rich enough to save it, and that saving it and taking it were the same word.

And she understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that she was looking at a mirror. Not of what she was. Of what she could become—what she might already be becoming, up on this ridge with her father's letters against her skin and her father's obsession growing in her like a second spine. Ten years of running from the Rivergate, and in three weeks she had descended a drowned stair and stood in the dark with her heart hammering *wonder, wonder, wonder*, and if Voss had never come, would she have walked away? Would she have burned the evidence? Or would she have stayed, and documented, and told herself it was for science, and been here when the water rose, unwilling to leave?

"Odile," she said, without turning around. "How long until the peak flood?"

The old woman's voice came flat from behind her. "Four days. Maybe five."

"Then you have four days," Marguerite told Voss. "Then this valley closes for the season, and everything below that line—" she pointed at a stain of old floodwater on the rock face, high above the current, "—is the river's. Not yours. Not the dam's. The river's."

Voss studied her, and slowly, he smiled again, and this smile was different—sharper, almost admiring, the smile of a man who has just learned something useful about an opponent.

"Four days," he agreed. "Your father used to bargain the same way. He'd give me a deadline that sounded like a concession and was actually a challenge." He turned back toward his boats, then paused. "Marguerite. One thing, as a courtesy. The man who took the notes from your camp—check your own crew before you check mine. I paid for copies. I never ordered a theft. Someone wanted me to have them badly enough to risk your trust to do it."

He walked down to the water. The engines woke, twin-throated, and the boats pulled away from the shelf into the rain, toward the far bank where his men were already rigging a camp with the crisp efficiency of people who expected to stay.

Marguerite stood on the rock a long time after the sound of the engines faded, the rain soaking through her jacket, Voss's words turning over in her mind like a stone in a current.

*Check your own crew.*

She thought of Odile's silences. Of the two days of supplies lost at the rapids, and Callum's confession that had explained too little. Of Sefu's long nights with the radio. Of Priya's careful, unasked questions.

Behind her, her crew was climbing down from the ridge to hear what had been decided. She watched them come, four people she had chosen and trusted, and for the first time since the dock, she looked at each of them and wondered which one of them had been Voss's all along.

And below, on the far bank, she saw the tarps coming off one of the long cases, and the rain was not heavy enough to hide what it held.
