## 13. High Water

The river spoke first.

I'd been awake since before dawn, listening to it change its mind. All night it had run with the low, steady voice of a river doing its work, and then somewhere in the black hours that voice had deepened, gained a rasp underneath, like a man muttering in his sleep who was beginning to shout. By the time the sky went grey I didn't need to look to know what had happened upcountry. Somewhere above us, in the drowned gorges where we'd spent two days hauling the boat over shallows that should have been deep, the monsoon had found a second wind.

I climbed out of my tent into a world the color of wet slate. The lower camp sat on a gravel shelf maybe four meters above the river's edge—a good site when Odile chose it, sheltered from the wind, close to the water we needed, defensible if Voss's men came at night again. It had seemed like wisdom. This morning it looked like a mistake drawn on a map by someone who had never met a flood.

Marguerite was already up, standing at the waterline with her arms crossed, watching the current carry whole trees past us. Not branches. Trees, root-balls first, spinning slowly like things drowning in their sleep. She didn't turn when I came up beside her.

"How far up is the gorge mouth?" she asked.

"Six hours' walk. Maybe five, in this." I nodded at the water. "If that's coming out of the narrows, it'll hit here inside an hour."

"An hour." She said it the way she said most things—flat, unhurried, as though the word were a specimen she was examining rather than a countdown. But I'd been watching her for weeks now, and I knew the tells. Her thumb moved against her forefinger, once. "Get everyone up. Everything that matters goes uphill. Now."

* * *

We worked without speaking much, because there wasn't time for speech. Sefu hauled the survey equipment while Priya broke down the medical station into three packs that could be grabbed blind. Odile stood at the boat and argued with it—actually argued, low and vicious in Portuguese, as though the *Almirante* could be shamed into holding together one more day. Marguerite moved between all of them like a shuttle, and I did the thing I was best at, which was reading ground: tracing the slope behind camp, finding the line where the gravel gave way to clay and the clay to rooted earth, marking with broken branches the path we'd take when the water told us to go.

The water told us at half past seven.

It didn't come like a wall—I'd seen flash floods do that once, in another valley, another life, and I still dreamed about the sound. This one came like a tide that had lost its patience. The river simply rose, fast and continuous, a brown muscle flexing along its whole length, and then the lower shelf was gone, and then the gravel bar below it, and then the water was climbing the bank toward us with the calm inevitability of something that had never once been refused.

"Go!" Marguerite's voice cracked across the camp like a whip. "Leave it—go!"

Sefu was last down the slope from the equipment cache, and he was carrying too much. I saw it happen before it happened: the box of fuel canisters he'd insisted on salvaging, strapped across his back, the weight of it turning his footing uncertain on the wet clay. He slipped at the steepest section and slid, caught himself on a root, and the box tore loose and went cartwheeling down toward the rising water.

He went after it.

"Sefu!" Priya's scream was the first fully human sound I'd heard all morning—everything else had been weather and urgency, but that was pure terror, and it cut through everything.

He reached the canister box at the water's edge just as the current took it. He got one hand on the strap. The river got the rest of him.

What happened next took perhaps ten seconds and lasted, I think, considerably longer for everyone who watched it. Priya went down the bank at a dead slide, no hesitation, no calculation—the kind of decision that isn't made so much as released. She hit the shallows, dove, and came up with a fist in Sefu's collar as the current tried to peel them both away. For one long moment they were a single dark shape being pulled downstream, and I was already running for the rope coil at the tree line when Odile's arm shot past me with a throw that would have shamed a harbor master. The line arced out, dropped across Priya's shoulder, and then it was six people on a rope against one river, and the river, for today, lost.

We dragged them onto the clay above the waterline, coughing and streaming. Sefu was white-faced and shaking, clutching nothing. The fuel was gone—all of it, every liter, swallowed in one brown gulp. Priya knelt beside him with her hand flat on his chest, feeling his heart hammer, and said, very quietly, "Never again. You hear me? Never again."

He nodded, which was all he could manage.

Above us, on the ridge, someone else had been watching. I turned and saw them: figures along the crest of the opposite spur, maybe four hundred meters east, where the terrain narrowed to a spine of high ground neither camp could leave. Voss's men. They'd been driven uphill by the same water, and now they stood in the rain watching us save each other's lives, and one of them raised a hand—not a wave, exactly. An acknowledgment. *We see you. We're stuck too.*

Marguerite looked at them for a long moment. Then she looked at our own ridge—narrow, clay-and-root, barely room for six tents—and at the water still climbing, patient as arithmetic.

"Well," she said. "It seems we're neighbors."

* * *

By noon the river had taken everything below the old camp line and showed no interest in stopping. Our ridge ran northeast about three hundred meters before it merged into higher forest; Voss's spur ran parallel, separated from ours by a flooded saddle that had been, two days ago, a shallow ravine with a stream in it. Now it was a channel thirty meters wide and running hard enough to kill anyone who tried it. Two islands of high ground, one strait between them, and a storm overhead that intended to keep falling until further notice.

Voss crossed at four o'clock.

I'll give him this: he did it properly. A line rigged across the channel, anchored on both sides, a pulley and harness—his men had come equipped for exactly this kind of country, because Edmund Voss did not enter wilderness without assuming it would try to kill him. He came across alone, hand over hand, a heavyset man in his sixties moving with the deliberate economy of someone who had done hard things for a long time, and he stepped onto our mud in dry boots and looked around at our soaked, exhausted camp with an expression I couldn't read.

"Ms. Winterbourne." He inclined his head. "Mr. Reyes." His eyes rested on me a beat longer than politeness required, and I felt the debt sit up inside me like a dog hearing its name. "I've come to propose a pooling of resources. Fuel, food, shelter. My camp has provisions for twenty. Yours has—" he glanced at the sodden wreckage of our stores, "—considerably less."

"We have what we need," Marguerite said.

"You have tea, some rice, and a medical kit that Ms. Okafor has already opened twice today. You have no fuel, which means no hot food, no dry clothes, and no ability to run your pump or your radio. And you have a boat that, if I'm not mistaken, is currently filling with rain faster than you can bail it." He spread his hands. "I'm not offering charity. I'm offering arithmetic. The water won't drop for days. We can be miserable separately or uncomfortable together."

"And your price?" Marguerite asked. There it was—she never let a gift sit unexamined. "You don't cross a flood channel to share soup, Mr. Voss. What do you want?"

"What I've always wanted." He said it simply, without performance, and that was somehow worse than any threat. "To talk. To understand why you're so determined to treat your father's life's work as something to be destroyed rather than finished. And to convince you—before the water drops and we go back to being enemies—that there's a better way." He paused. "Also, my cook makes an excellent dhal, and yours is currently a man who nearly drowned this morning."

Behind him, on the far spur, his men had set up tarps and a stove, and the smell of actual cooking was drifting across the channel like a briar made of smoke. Sefu, wrapped in a blanket and gray-faced, looked at it with naked longing. Priya put her hand on his shoulder, but she was looking at it too.

Marguerite was silent for a long moment. Rain drummed on the tarp. On the far ridge, Voss's men waited, patient, well-fed, armed, and I thought: *this is how he wins. Not with guns. With arithmetic.*

"One condition," Marguerite said finally. "Your men stay on your side. You eat with us, you talk with us—but nobody crosses that line except you. And Callum and I hold the rope anchor on our end. If anything crosses that channel that isn't food or conversation, we cut it."

"Agreed," Voss said, without hesitation, and offered his hand.

She didn't take it. "Then we have a truce," she said. "Not a handshake."

Something flickered across his face—amusement, maybe, or respect. "A truce," he agreed. "For as long as the water stays high."

* * *

Dinner was the strangest meal of my life, and I have eaten war rations in three countries and a wedding feast in a fourth. Voss's men ferried across sealed containers—dhal, rice, flatbread, strong sweet tea—and retreated to their side as agreed, and the six of us ate real food for the first time in a week while the man who had hunted us upriver sat on a crate among us and talked about the monsoon patterns of 1987 as though we were all colleagues waiting out a delay at a field station.

He was good at it. That was the thing I kept forgetting and having to relearn: Edmund Voss was genuinely likable. He asked Priya about her field hospital work in a way that showed he'd actually read her published paper on hydration protocols. He drew Sefu into a technical argument about sediment coring that left the kid simultaneously furious and delighted. He complimented Odile's boat handling with the specificity of a man who had watched it through binoculars and meant every word. And through all of it, his eyes kept returning to Marguerite, with a patience that had nothing to do with strategy and everything to do with grief.

"You knew her father," I said, when the others had drifted toward their tents and the rain had settled into its long nighttime rhythm.

"I loved him," Voss said, simply. "That's not a confession, Mr. Reyes. It's a complication. You love someone for forty years, you fight with them for thirty-five of those years, and then they vanish up a river chasing something you told them was a myth, and you spend the rest of your life wondering whether you could have stopped them." He turned his cup slowly. "I intend to finish his work. Not for the dam company. Not even for the money, though I won't pretend the money doesn't matter. Because Hartwell believed the Rivergate held the records of a civilization that chose the water over conquest, and if that's true, it belongs in a museum, in daylight, in human memory—not at the bottom of a reservoir built by men who will never know what they drowned."

"And if Marguerite burns it instead?"

"Then I'll have failed twice." He smiled, and it was the saddest smile I'd ever seen on a powerful man. "Once at the temple, and once at the only thing that ever mattered more."

Across the fire, Marguerite was pretending to sleep. I knew she wasn't, because her breathing was wrong, and because when Voss said *the only thing that ever mattered more*, her thumb moved against her forefinger, once.

* * *

I woke near midnight to voices.

Low ones, urgent, on the far spur. I lay still in my tent and listened, mapping the sound the way I mapped ground: two speakers, maybe three, positioned near the channel anchor. Then a third voice, sharper—Voss himself, cutting across the others with a single word I couldn't make out but whose tone needed no translation. *No.*

Silence. Then footsteps moving away.

I crawled out into the rain and found Marguerite already awake, sitting under the tarp with the rope anchor's carabiner in her lap, watching the far camp.

"You heard it too," she said.

"Some of it. Someone wanted to cross."

"Someone always wants to cross." She turned the carabiner over in her fingers. "The question is whether Voss can hold his own leash. Tonight he could. I'm not sure about tomorrow—or the night after. We're trapped on this ridge with a man who loves my father, men who follow orders, and a channel that gets narrower every hour the rain keeps falling." She looked up at me, and the firelight caught the exhaustion in her face and the iron underneath it. "When the water drops, Callum, everything starts again. Whatever happens between now and then—we use it. Every hour. Because the moment that channel is passable, we're not neighbors anymore."

Downstream, invisible in the dark, the river kept rising. And beneath us—beneath all of us, under however many meters of brown water—something older than either of our expeditions waited in the flooded dark, keeping its records, keeping its silence, keeping its own counsel about which of us would reach it first.

Somewhere out in the rain, on the far spur, a lantern moved. Then another. Voss's men were awake too, and nobody on either ridge was sleeping, and the water was still climbing.

Three days, I thought. Maybe four. Then the channel opens, and the truce dies with it.

I just hoped that when it did, we'd be ready—and that whatever Marguerite planned to use the hours for, she'd tell me before the water told her.
