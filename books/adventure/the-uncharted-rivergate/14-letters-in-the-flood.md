## 14. Letters in the Flood

The cave took them in like a mouth.

It opened on the eastern face of the ridge, a half-moon of black stone above the drowned terraces, and by the time Marguerite had counted everyone through its entrance—her four, Voss's nine, two men bleeding, one man limping—the rain outside had thickened into something that was no longer weather but a condition. A state of the world. The river below had swallowed the last of the lower camp an hour ago; she had watched the fuel drums go under, spinning slowly, like coins dropped into a well too deep to search.

Now there were thirteen people and one oil lamp in a chamber that smelled of wet limestone and old bat guano, and nobody was speaking.

Priya moved among Voss's wounded with a competence that made his men watch her the way hungry men watch bread. Sefu sat against the wall with his bandaged arm cradled in his lap, gray-faced but conscious, and Odile had planted herself between him and the nearest of Voss's crew with the fixed ferocity of a dog guarding a bone. Callum stood at the cave mouth, watching the water rise over the valley floor, and Marguerite could read the cartographer's arithmetic in his shoulders: how many meters to the ridge line, how many hours until the peak surge, how long before the map he'd drawn for Edmund Voss became a map of nothing at all.

Voss sat apart, near the lamp, drying his glasses on his shirttail with maddening patience. He looked older without the expedition's polish—rain had gotten into everything, into the seams of him—and yet there was something almost restful in his face, as if the flood had relieved him of some decision he'd been dreading.

Marguerite went to her pack.

She had carried the letters since the waystation, since the tin box wedged behind the rotted shelf where her father had hidden them thirty years ago and where they had waited for her like a debt. She had read them twice by headlamp in the privacy of her tent, once in fury and once in grief, and she had told no one what they said—not Callum, not Priya, not anyone. They were hers. The last private thing her father had ever given her.

But the cave was small, and the night would be long, and thirteen people trapped above a drowning valley needed something more than silence to hold them together. And there was a voice in her that had been getting louder since the waystation, a voice that sounded unsettlingly like her father's, saying: *You cannot carry this alone, and you were never meant to.*

She knelt and untied the oilcloth.

Callum saw it first. He crossed the cave without a word and crouched beside her, close enough that she could feel the rain still steaming off his jacket. "Marguerite," he said quietly. "You don't have to."

"I know." She worked the knot loose. "That's why I'm going to."

* * *

She stood where the lamplight reached, the letters in their bundle against her chest, and waited until the cave noticed her. It took longer than she expected. Rain drummed the stone at the entrance like fingers on a door, and the river spoke from below in a low continuous roar, and men who had been shooting at each other twelve hours ago did not look at one another easily.

"My father wrote these," she said. "Some of them before I was born. Some after. The last ones—" Her voice caught, and she hated it, and she pushed through anyway. "The last ones were written here. In this valley. After the date the university recorded his disappearance. He was alive down there for eleven days after the world decided he was dead."

Voss's hands went still on his knees.

"I've spent ten years being angry at him," Marguerite said. "For leaving. For choosing this place over us. For making me inherit a question instead of a father. I read these letters hoping to find proof I was right to be angry." She looked around the lamplight, at Odile's hard eyes, at Sefu's young face, at the strangers with guns who had followed her upriver for reasons she now understood better than they did. "I didn't find proof. I found him. So you're going to listen, because I can't hold all of this by myself anymore, and because every one of you came here chasing something he left behind, and you deserve to know what it actually was."

She unfolded the first letter. The paper had gone soft as cloth with damp and years.

* * *

*"March 3rd. M—*

*You'll be seven this month. Your mother writes that you've begun collecting stones, sorting them by color into matchboxes, and I laughed until I had to put the letter down, because I did exactly the same thing at your age and my own father despaired of me. Keep the stones, little bird. Most people spend their whole lives walking over the ground without once wondering what it's hiding. You already know to look down. That's half of everything I could ever teach you."*

Someone in Voss's crew shifted. Marguerite kept reading, and the letters did what letters do—they built a man out of ink, one stroke at a time, until he stood in the cave among them: a tall, restless, self-mocking man who loved stratigraphy and puns and his daughter with a love so large it embarrassed him on paper, a man who signed himself *your father, the famous absent one,* and drew a small bird beside his name in every letter, always the same bird, wings folded.

She read the early ones quickly—there were nineteen, and the night was not infinite—but she gave each its moment. The one from her twelfth birthday, apologizing for missing it, enclosing a pressed orchid from the gorge country. The one written the winter her mother died, which was barely a letter at all, just three lines that shook visibly in the handwriting: *I should be there. I am not there. I have no words that survive the distance. Forgive me or don't, but know that I am sorry in a language I haven't learned yet.* Priya wept openly at that one, and Marguerite found she could keep reading only by pretending the words belonged to someone else.

Then the letters changed. The dates clustered. The tone sharpened.

* * *

*"They voted today. The council of the institute—Voss among them, though he argued longest and hardest for me, I'll grant him that—to withdraw support from the Rivergate survey. Too dangerous in flood season, too speculative, too much money for a hypothesis. A hypothesis! As if the staircases visible at low water were a rumor. As if the civilization that built them left no other trace than rumor.*

*I understand them, is the terrible thing. I sat in that room and heard myself through their ears: a man past sixty chasing a drowned city because he cannot bear to stop. But understanding is not agreeing. There are records down there, M. Clay tablets, hundreds of them, sealed in chambers the water never reached. I've seen two through a crack in the rock, brought up on a line by a boy from the delta villages who dives like an otter. Writing, Marguerite. A library. The whole memory of a people, sitting in the dark, waiting."*

She glanced up. Voss was watching her with an expression she had never seen on him—unguarded, stripped of strategy, the look of a man hearing a voice he'd spent decades trying not to miss.

* * *

The last five letters were different. Shorter. The handwriting deteriorated, then steadied, as if he had made a decision about how he wanted to end.

*"Day 4 below. The entrance stair is real and it is beautiful beyond anything I have earned in this life. Nine flights, each carved with figures going down—men, women, children, oxen, all descending in procession, none looking back. We believe now we understand what happened here. This was not a city destroyed by conquest. It was a city that chose the water. When the empire came—and the tablets make no secret of what the empire did to cities that resisted—the people of the Rivergate opened their own sluices. They drowned their temples, their granaries, their records, everything, rather than let it be taken. They wrote it down first, deliberately, sealed the tablets in clay rooms, and walked into the flood in procession. Not defeat. Refusal. They decided what their memory would belong to, and it was not the enemy."*

The cave had gone absolutely quiet except for the rain.

*"I have been sitting in their reading room for two days. It is dry. It has been dry for three thousand years. I could take a tablet out—I have the means, the line, the strength. One tablet would buy the survey another decade. One tablet would make my reputation immortal and feed the institute for a generation.*

*I am not going to do it.*

*Here is what I finally understand, M, at the bottom of the world: those people's last act was authorship. They chose how their story ended. If I carry even one tablet up into the light, I become the empire. I become the thing they flooded themselves to refuse. Every hand that reaches into that dark room after mine takes something they died to keep, and calls it scholarship, and calls it rescue, and it is neither. It is theft with good lighting.*

*Let it rest. That is what the figures on the stair say, going down, not looking back. Let it rest. Whoever finds this—if anyone finds this, if my bones are ever found by someone with a shovel and a grant—let it rest. Flood it deeper. Seal it. Do not dig, do not raise, do not exhibit. Grief is not a resource. Memory is not cargo. Some things are finished, and finishing is holy."*

Marguerite stopped. The last page trembled in her fingers, and she let it.

"There's one more," she said. "It's addressed to me alone. I'm not reading that one aloud." She folded the bundle along its old creases, carefully, the way you handle a sleeping animal. "But I'll tell you what it says. He tells me he's sorry. He tells me he loved me more than the work, and that the work was never the point—the refusal was. And he asks me to do for him what the Rivergate did for itself. To choose how the story ends. To walk away without looking back."

She looked at Edmund Voss.

"You argued for him," she said. "In that room. He says so. He says you were the only one who understood, and that when the vote failed, you shook his hand and told him you'd fund it yourself someday." Her voice roughened. "So tell me, Edmund. Someday is here. The dam will bury this valley in six months either way. You have the maps, the explosives, the men. You can blast the entrance open and haul out whatever the water hasn't claimed, and your name goes in every journal on earth, and his discovery becomes your monument. Or—" she held up the letters, "—you can hear what he actually asked for. From his own hand. Read aloud in a cave above the place he died keeping his word."

The lamp hissed. Below them the river rose and rose.

Voss stood slowly. He crossed to her, and for one vertiginous moment Marguerite thought of her father's description of him—young Voss, arguing longest and hardest—and she braced for the strategist, the rival, the man with nine armed followers.

He took the final letter from her hand—the last one, the private one—and read it in silence while the whole cave watched. His lips moved slightly. When he finished, he held the page out at arm's length, the way a man holds something that has suddenly become heavier than paper, and his eyes were wet, and he made no attempt to hide it.

"He signs it with a bird," Voss said hoarsely. "He used to draw that bird on his field sketches. Thirty years. I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought if I got there first, I'd be finishing what he started. I told myself it was for him." He looked toward the cave mouth, toward the black roaring dark where the valley was vanishing inch by inch. "It was never for him. It was for me."

He handed the letter back. Then he turned to his men, and when he spoke, the command voice was gone; what remained was quieter and somehow harder to disobey.

"Pull the charges," he said. "All of them. Every stick comes off this mountain tonight."

One of his men—a scarred lieutenant named Brandt, who had led the raid on the marker camp—started forward. "Sir, the entrance—"

"Is staying drowned, Mr. Brandt." Voss's voice cracked across the cave like the river below. "That's an order. And the next man who touches those crates answers to me personally."

Brandt held his gaze a moment too long. Then he nodded, once, and went.

Marguerite exhaled a breath she felt she'd been holding for ten years. Callum's hand found hers in the dark beside the lamp, and neither of them pulled away.

But as Voss's men filed toward the entrance to begin hauling the explosives down through the rain, Marguerite caught Brandt at the cave mouth, pausing, looking back—not at Voss, not at her, but at the flood-swollen valley below, with an expression she recognized with a cold drop in her stomach.

It was the expression of a man doing arithmetic.

And the numbers, she realized, did not include Edmund Voss.
