## 15. The Map Callum Hid

The rain had found a rhythm by the third day on the ridge, and Callum had learned to hate it in a new way each hour. It came down in gray sheets that erased the far bank of the swollen river, drummed on the tarps until speech became something you leaned into, and turned the clay underfoot into a slick brown grease that took the strength out of every step. The two camps—his old crew and Marguerite's strays, Voss's hired guns and the people they'd been sent to follow—shared the cave mouth and the overhang like survivors of a shipwreck who hadn't yet decided whether to be grateful or afraid.

He was mending rope when he heard her voice cut through the rain.

"Callum."

Not *Callum, can you check the water gauge.* Not *Callum, Priya needs hands with Sefu's dressing.* Just his name, flat as a sounding line dropped into deep water. He looked up from the frayed hemp and saw her standing at the back of the cave, where the supplies were stacked under oilcloth, and she was holding his survey tube.

His stomach went cold in a way the monsoon never managed.

It was a battered aluminum case, dented at one end from a fall he'd taken on the escarpment three years ago, waterproofed with waxed canvas and bound with a leather strap gone soft with age. He'd kept it buried at the bottom of his kit since the day they'd left the delta, wrapped inside a spare shirt, weighted down beneath Odile's spare anchor line because no one ever volunteered to dig through boatman's gear. He'd told himself it was safe there. He'd told himself a lot of things.

"That's not mine," he said, and even as the lie left his mouth he knew how it sounded—how it had always sounded, every time he'd deflected, every time he'd given her a piece of truth small enough to hold without cutting himself.

Marguerite didn't answer. She set the tube on a flat stone between them, unclipped the strap, and drew out the roll of drafting film with the care of a woman handling something venomous. Even rolled, even edge-on, he could see the linework through the translucent mylar—the contour intervals drawn in his own hand, the elevation notations, the grid references in the tight, slanted script that had won him a fellowship before he'd lost everything else.

"You said your survey was destroyed," she said. "The night we met. You said Voss burned it."

"I said what I had to say to get on that boat."

"Yes." Her voice didn't rise. That was the worst of it. He'd braced for fury—for shouting, for the crack of her open hand, for anything he could fight against. Instead she stood very still in the cave's gray light, rain streaming off the overhang behind her, and looked at him the way she'd looked at her father's letters: reading something she'd already begun to suspect. "I've been telling myself for weeks that I believed you. Do you understand that? I *chose* to believe you, because the alternative meant putting you off this boat, and I—" She stopped. Breathed. Started again, quieter. "Where did you find it?"

"It was never lost."

* * *

He'd mapped the valley in the dry season four years ago, working alone on contract for a mining consortium that turned out to be a shell for Voss's interests. Three months of solo traverse work up gorges that didn't appear on any government sheet, shooting elevations off ridgelines that had never been named, fixing the position of the drowned temple complex to within a meter. He'd been good at it. He'd been the best hydrographic surveyor on the coast, and he'd taken the job because the money was enough to buy his sister Marisol out of the trouble she'd fallen into in Cádiz—a customs fraud charge, forged manifests, a name on paperwork that would have put her in prison for six years if someone hadn't quietly made the evidence disappear.

Someone had. And then someone had sent him the bill.

Voss hadn't threatened him, not exactly. That was the elegance of it. Voss had simply shown him the file—one afternoon in an office that smelled of cedar and old paper, sliding a folder across the desk with two fingers, letting him read the customs manifest with Marisol's signature on it, the shipping ledger, the sworn statement of the port officer who'd have testified—and then closed it again and said, *I don't collect debts, Mr. Reyes. I hold them. There's a difference. Held debts keep men honest.*

So when the consortium asked him to produce a final product—a precise structural map of the Rivergate's upper chambers, sufficient to calculate charge placement against load-bearing walls—he'd produced it. What else could he do? He'd drawn the map that would let them blast the temple safely, efficiently, with minimal collateral collapse, and he'd told himself the site was going to be flooded by a dam anyway, so what did it matter, so the drawings would sit in a drawer, so—

So he'd kept a copy. Of course he'd kept a copy. A surveyor keeps his work; it's the only immortality the trade offers. He'd told himself that too.

And then Jonah Winterbourne had vanished upriver, and the consortium's interest had curdled into something hungrier, and Callum had started to understand what his beautiful, precise lines were actually for.

* * *

"How long?" Marguerite asked.

The question was simple. The answer wasn't. He weighed it while the rain hammered down and somewhere outside one of Voss's men shouted about the fuel stores, and finally he gave her the only version of honesty he had left.

"The map? Four years. The copy in your hands—since before I met you. Since before Voss came to your camp." He watched her face and saw nothing move in it, which frightened him more than anger would have. "I didn't bring it to give to him. I need you to hear that. I brought it because I couldn't destroy it. Every time I tried, I—" His voice caught on something he hadn't planned to say aloud. "It's the best work I've ever done. It's the map of the place your father died looking for. Burning it felt like burning him."

"Don't," she said softly. "Don't put his name in your mouth right now. Don't make this about him."

"Then let me make it about what it is." He stepped closer, and she didn't step back, but her whole body went rigid, and he stopped. "Voss has a file, Marguerite. Customs fraud, forged manifests, a port officer ready to swear my sister signed her name to stolen cargo. Six years. She was twenty-three when he collected that paper. If I refuse him—if I burn that map, if I stand in front of his guns—Marisol goes to prison, and I will have handed her there myself."

"And if you give him the map?"

"Then the Rivergate comes down in controlled demolition instead of dying under the reservoir. Either way it's destroyed. But one way, my sister walks free, and the other way—" He spread his hands. Rain dripped from the overhang between them like a curtain neither of them could part. "That's the arithmetic. That's all it's ever been. I'm not a traitor. I'm a man doing sums."

"No." For the first time her voice cracked, and he flinched at the sound of it. "No, that's not the arithmetic. Here's the arithmetic. My father is dead on a desk of clay tablets in a hall you have *drawn*, Callum. You sat in some office with cedar walls and inked the chambers where he starved, and you knew—I watched your face when we found his marker, I watched your face at the waystation—you knew what those lines led to. Every day on this river, every leak, every night we heard that second engine, you knew exactly what Voss was following. And you let me sail us straight toward it because you couldn't burn a piece of plastic."

"I couldn't tell you."

"Why not?"

"Because you'd have put me off the boat!" It came out louder than he intended, and heads turned along the cave. He lowered his voice to something raw. "Because I needed to be here. Because someone on this crew had to know the valley well enough to get us out alive when the flood came, and that someone was me, and if you'd known what I was carrying you'd have chained me to the mast. I chose the mission over the confession. Every single day, I chose it. And I'd choose it again, because the alternative was watching you walk into this valley blind."

"So you protected me." Something terrible and gentle entered her voice. "The way Voss protects your sister. The way your debt protects you. Everyone in this story holding something over everyone else, and calling it love."

The words landed harder than any blow could have, because they were true, and he had no defense built for true things.

* * *

She picked up the map.

He watched her do it—watched her carry the roll to the cave mouth where the light was better, watched her unroll a meter of drafting film against the wet stone and pin it flat with two rocks, and he understood, with the sick clarity of a man watching his own x-ray being read aloud, that she was going to study it. That she couldn't not. It was her father's grave rendered in her lover's hand.

The others gathered without being asked. Odile first, arms crossed, jaw set—she wouldn't meet his eyes, and he remembered the notes she'd copied, the betrayal she'd confessed, and understood that she recognized the shape of this moment from the inside. Then Priya, still smelling of carbolic and river mud. Then Sefu, bandaged shoulder and all, propped against a crate with his young face carefully blank. Last came Voss's people, drifting over from their side of the cave with the unhurried confidence of men who owned the outcome, and among them Voss himself, gray at the temples, moving like the weather was a personal inconvenience.

"Well," Voss said mildly, looking at the map. "There it is."

Nobody spoke. The rain filled the silence.

Marguerite traced the linework with one finger—chamber by chamber, the upper galleries, the stair, the great hall where the tablets waited, all of it fixed and labeled in Callum's slanted hand. He watched her find the annotation he'd written four years ago in a moment of professional pride: *Structural integrity excellent. Charge points viable at marked nodes.* Eleven red circles. Eleven places to drill and blast.

"You drew this," she said, not looking at him.

"Yes."

"Before my father disappeared."

"A year before."

She was quiet for a long moment. When she looked up, her eyes were dry, and somehow that was worse than tears. "Then here's what happens now. This map goes into the fire tonight—not because you're forgiven, but because Voss doesn't leave this valley with it. And you—" her voice caught, steadied— "you can stay on this ridge with the men you've been working for, or you can help me finish what I came to do. Those are the only two doors, Callum. I don't have a third."

"There's always a third door," Voss said pleasantly. "There's the one where Mr. Reyes remembers his obligations, and the dam project proceeds on schedule, and his sister's file finds its way to a prosecutor in Cádiz by month's end. I'd hate for anyone to forget that door exists."

Every head turned to Callum. He felt the weight of the whole cave—the rain, the rifles, the map drying on the stone, Marguerite's face like carved wood—and underneath it all, faint and steady as a pulse, the memory of Marisol laughing on a dock in Cádiz, twenty years old and invincible, before any of it.

He reached down and took the map off the stones.

Marguerite's breath stopped. Voss smiled.

Then Callum held the drafting film over the cooking fire, and the heat came up through his fingers, and for one suspended second the linework glowed translucent against the flame—four years of the best work he would ever do, curling at the edges, his own handwriting blooming brown and then black—

And Voss's man moved. Fast, silent, a hand like a dock cleat closing on Callum's wrist, wrenching the map sideways out of the fire, half of it already ash, the lower chambers—the great hall, the stair, the eleven red circles—still intact, still legible, smoking gently in a stranger's grip.

"Forgive me," Voss said, and he sounded like he meant it, which was the cruelest thing yet. "But I've come too far and spent too much to watch sentiment burn the last bridge. Half a map is more than enough. It always has been."

Marguerite was already moving toward the cave mouth, toward the rain, toward the entrance below—and Callum, wrist throbbing, heart in ashes, understood that whatever happened next, he had done this. He had drawn the road to her father's grave, and now the road was open, and the water was rising, and there was no third door after all.
