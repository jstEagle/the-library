## 16. Two Fires

The rain had stopped pretending to be weather. It was now a condition of existence, a gray curtain that fell without pause over the ridge, and Marguerite had learned to measure time not in hours but in the small changes it carved: the waterline climbing the far bank by hand-widths, the river's voice deepening from its whisper to a roar, the mud between the tents going from firm to treacherous to something almost alive.

She stood at the mouth of the cave shelter they'd shared for two days with Voss's expedition—two camps on one ridge, separated by forty meters of drowned grass and a decade of history—and watched the enemy work.

Voss's men were at the drowned stair. She could see them through the rain-haze, six figures in yellow slickers moving with the unhurried competence of professionals, hauling crates down the slope toward the stone lip where the water had revealed her father's last discovery. The stair itself had gone under again two nights ago; the flood was rising faster than anyone had predicted. But the men worked anyway, rigging lines, anchoring a winch to a granite outcrop, preparing for the window when the water would drop—or when they would make it drop.

"They're bringing up the explosives," Sefu said beside her. He had binoculars pressed to his eyes, his young face tight beneath the hood of his poncho. "I count four cases. Maybe five. That's enough to take the whole entrance chamber down to rubble."

"Or enough to widen it," Marguerite said. "Blast a channel so their divers can get equipment in before the peak."

"Does it matter?"

It did, and they both knew it didn't. Either way, Voss intended to open the Rivergate. Either way, whatever slept below—the tablets, the records, the testimony of a people who had chosen drowning over chains—would be hauled into daylight, catalogued, claimed, and argued over in conference halls by people who had never stood in this rain.

"No," she said. "It doesn't matter."

* * *

Callum found Odile at the boats.

Their two vessels and Voss's three were moored together in the flooded stand of trees that had become an impromptu harbor, hulls knocking against each other in the swell like tired animals. Odile sat in the stern of theirs, mending a fuel line with the grim patience of a woman who had spent thirty years keeping things running past their designed death. Her hands moved automatically. Her eyes were somewhere else.

"You've been quiet," Callum said, crouching on the dock plank beside her.

"I'm always quiet."

"Quieter than usual. Since the notes turned up torn along the bank." He let it sit there. The accusation he wasn't making hung in the wet air between them.

Odile tied off a knot, tested it, and finally looked up. Rain ran off the brim of her hat and pooled in the creases of her face. "You want to know if I'm going to do it again," she said. "Sell us out. Copy another map, pass another message."

"The thought crossed my mind."

"It crosses mine too." She said it flatly, without apology. "Every morning I wake up and I think: today I could end this. One radio call to Voss's lead man—his name is Duret, by the way, the big one with the scar—and my brother walks free of that debt in Jakarta. One call." She flexed her fingers, watching them as if they belonged to someone else. "And every morning I don't make it. Do you know why?"

"Because Marguerite forgave you."

"Because Marguerite forgave me," Odile agreed, "and because I have discovered that being forgiven is heavier than being hated. Hated, you can live with yourself. Forgiven—" she shook her head. "Forgiven, you owe something back. And I am done owing Voss. Whatever comes next, I'm paying my debts in the other direction."

Callum studied her. He had spent years reading people professionally—reading coastlines, currents, the tells of rivers about to turn—and Odile read like bedrock. Not stable, exactly. Bedrock never is. But load-bearing.

"Duret," he said. "The explosives. Where does he keep them?"

Something flickered in her face—not quite a smile. "Aft hold of the middle boat. Under a tarp, under a false floor, because Duret is careful. But I helped them load it at the last supply stop, and I noticed something." She leaned closer, voice dropping beneath the drum of rain. "The detonators are packed separate. In a green case. Duret keeps it in the wheelhouse, next to the chart table, because he thinks proximity means control." Her eyes met Callum's. "He's wrong. Proximity just means I know exactly where it is."

* * *

Marguerite laid it out on the chart table that evening, in the cave, by lamplight, with the combined noise of the river and the rain pressing in from outside like a living thing.

Priya sat with her arms crossed. Sefu leaned over the map, still favoring the shoulder he'd wrenched in the flash flood. Callum stood at the edge of the lamplight, and Odile kept watch at the cave mouth, ostensibly scanning for movement from Voss's camp but really, Marguerite suspected, listening to every word.

"Two targets," Marguerite said. "The detonators first. Without them, the explosives are ballast. Then the charges themselves—we soak them, scatter them, sink them in the flood. Even if Voss has spares, he won't have time to fly more in before the valley closes. The dam engineers start controlled releases upstream in"—she checked her father's almanac, swollen with damp—"four days. After that, no boat can fight this current. It's now or never."

"And how," Priya said quietly, "do we propose to board three armed boats guarded by eight men, in the dark, in a flood?"

"We don't board them," Callum said. Everyone looked at him. He stepped into the light, and Marguerite saw the thing in his face she'd seen once before, at the leak, at the confession—the look of a man handing over the last piece of himself. "We burn them."

Silence, except the rain.

"Not the boats. The cache. We take the detonator case off the wheelhouse—that part has to be quiet, that part is Odile and me—but the charges themselves stay where they are. We set a fire above them. Fuel, canvas, anything that burns hot. Nitro-based compounds in a sustained fire—" he glanced at Sefu. "Tell them."

Sefu's eyes had gone wide. "They cook. They detonate. All of it, at once, right in the hold. Nobody goes near those boats for a week, and by then the charges are slag at the bottom of the valley."

"And Voss's men?" Priya asked. "Eight men sleeping twenty meters from a bomb?"

"That's why it happens at shift change," Callum said. "Four a.m. Their rotation puts everyone ashore except one sentry. Odile knows the schedule."

All eyes went to the cave mouth. Odile turned around slowly, and Marguerite braced herself for objection—for the woman who had already betrayed them once to find one more reason.

Instead Odile said, "Sentry smokes. Every ninety minutes, he steps behind the wheelhouse to light up out of the wind. Ninety seconds, maybe less. That's your window for the wheelhouse." A pause. "For what it's worth—I'd rather it were fire than guns. Fire gives everyone a chance to run."

"Guns give us a chance to keep what we take," Marguerite said. But even as she said it, she heard her father in her own voice, heard the obsession talking, and she looked down at the map and made herself say the rest. "No guns unless there's no choice. If we're shooting at each other on a flooded ridge, we all die, and the Rivergate wins either way. Fire. Quiet hands. And then we're done."

Priya exhaled. Sefu nodded slowly. Callum caught Marguerite's eye across the lamplight, and in it she read both the promise and the warning: *this works, or it ends all of us.*

"All right," she said. "Tomorrow night. Four a.m."

Outside, the river roared, and somewhere below the brown water, a stone stair waited, patient as it had been for three thousand years, for someone to decide whether it would ever see light again.

* * *

The night of the fourth day came down black and hammering.

They went in two pairs. Odile led Callum along the tree line toward the moorings, moving with a sureness that reminded Callum she had grown up on water like this, in places where a misstep meant the river took you and nobody asked questions. Behind them, further up the slope, Marguerite and Sefu carried the fire kit: jerrican of diesel siphoned from their own tank, rags, a storm lighter, and Priya's field kit in case fire became blood.

The sentry was where Odile said he'd be—a broad silhouette under the wheelhouse awning of the nearest boat, cigarette ember pulsing. They crouched in the flooded ferns and waited. The rain filled the world. Callum counted his own heartbeats and lost track somewhere past four hundred.

The ember moved. The man stepped aft, cupping his hands against the wind, and Odile was already moving—low, fast, silent across the gangplank, Callum a step behind her. The wheelhouse door was unlatched. Inside, the chart table sat under a swaying lamp, charts weighted with brass bars, and there, beside the radio, the green case.

Odile lifted it. Tested the weight. Nodded once.

That was when the sentry finished his cigarette early.

Callum heard the footsteps on the deck behind them and did the arithmetic in half a second: door, distance, the man's hands—one held a rifle loosely at parade rest, the other a lighter. Unready. Surprisable.

He stepped out of the wheelhouse and said, quietly, "Don't."

The sentry froze. Young, Callum saw—younger than Sefu, rain streaming off a jaw that hadn't decided yet whether to be afraid or angry. His eyes went from Callum to the green case in Odile's arms and understood everything at once.

"You're stealing from Mr. Voss," the boy said.

"We're taking back what he stole," Odile said.

The rifle came up an inch. Callum moved without deciding to—stepped inside the barrel's arc, palms open, close enough that the muzzle pointed at his chest instead of anywhere useful. "Listen to me. In about four minutes, that hold is going to be a fireball. You want to be standing on this deck? Go wake your friends. Go. Now."

For one long moment the boy's finger tightened on the guard, and Callum felt the future narrow to a single point of contact—then the boy broke. He dropped the rifle to sling, turned, and crashed away down the slope shouting for Duret, and the shout was the best possible outcome and the worst, because it meant seconds, not minutes.

"Fire," Callum said into the rain. "Now. Forget quiet."

* * *

Marguerite heard the shout and stopped pretending this was a theft.

"Sefu—the boats. Go, go." They ran the slope together, sliding in the mud, and she saw it all in snapshots through the rain: Callum emerging from the wheelhouse with the green case, Odile behind him, the sentry's torch beam swinging wild, and then lights blooming all across Voss's camp—men spilling from tents, Duret's voice cutting through the storm like a blade.

"Diesel!" she shouted, and Sefu swung the jerrican in a wide arc across the aft deck of the middle boat, over the tarp, over the false floor, the reek of it cutting through even the rain-washed air. She struck the lighter. Once—nothing. Twice—

The first shot cracked the night open.

It came high and wide, a warning, but warnings end, and the second shot didn't. Marguerite felt it pass close enough to part the rain, and then Sefu jerked beside her and went down onto the deck with a sound that was not a cry, just a short punched-out breath, and she knew—knew in her stomach before her mind caught up—that the bullet hadn't gone high at all.

"*Sefu.*"

"I'm—" He pressed a hand to his side, above the hip, and it came away black in the lamplight. "I'm okay. I think I'm—"

"Down. Stay down." She threw herself over him, struck the lighter a third time, and the rag caught.

She threw it into the diesel slick and the fire went up with a soft whump, orange light rolling across the floodwater, and everything after that happened fast and happened badly. Men shouting on the slope. Duret roaring orders. Return fire from somewhere—Callum, she realized dimly, firing not at men but at the torches, the lamps, the lights, buying darkness. A figure charged the burning boat's gangplank—Duret himself, trying to reach the charges—and Odile stepped out of the smoke and put the butt of a borrowed rifle across his temple, and Duret went down into the flooded grass and did not get up, and did not move again, and Marguerite would spend the rest of her life not knowing whether it was the blow or the water or the fall.

Then Voss's voice, amplified by nothing but fury, carrying across the ridge:

"Let it burn! Everybody off the boats—let it BURN!"

* * *

They got Sefu back to the cave with Priya's hands hard at work before they'd even cleared the slope. The bullet had gone through the outer muscle, low and clean—through-and-through, Priya said, twice, once to Sefu and once to herself, as if saying it made it true. She packed it, stitched it, dosed him against infection, and Sefu lay white-faced on a camp cot making jokes in a thin voice while the fire outside lit the rain orange through the cave mouth.

The middle boat burned to the waterline. The near boat caught from the radiant heat and burned too. By dawn, both were smoking hulks, and the third—Voss's command vessel, moored furthest out—had been cut loose and pushed into the current, crewed by grim-faced men who now had nowhere to sleep but the ridge.

One of Voss's men was dead. Duret, the big one with the scar, the man Odile had named in confidence and killed in the same week. They could see Voss's camp from the cave mouth: men standing in the rain around a body laid under a tarp, hats in hands, and Voss himself apart from all of them, looking not at the body but at the burning boats, and then—Marguerite saw it clearly, and wished she hadn't—at her cave.

Across forty meters of drowned grass and ten years of history, Edmund Voss looked at her, and she looked back, and neither of them moved.

"No clean hands," Priya said quietly, coming to stand beside her. She nodded toward their own shelter, where Sefu slept feverish and Odile sat alone at the cave mouth, staring at the tarp across the way with an expression Marguerite couldn't read and didn't try to. "Not on either side. You understand that now?"

"I understood it before," Marguerite said. "I just hoped I was wrong."

Behind them, deeper in the cave, Callum was packing. Rope, lamps, the last of the dry rations. When Marguerite turned, he met her eyes and said only: "The stair. While they're burying their dead and counting their losses. The water drops tonight—storm surge shifts at moonrise, Odile says. Four hours of lower river. It's the last window, Marguerite. After tomorrow, the valley closes forever."

She looked at the fire, at the body under the tarp, at the man she loved loading rope in the dark.

"Then we go down," she said.
