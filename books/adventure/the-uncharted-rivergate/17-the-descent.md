## 17. The Descent

The water told them everything now. It had become the valley's clock, its voice, its verdict—rising a hand's width every hour against the drowned stair, swallowing the lower terraces one by one with a patience that felt deliberate. Marguerite stood at the lip of the entrance shaft and watched the brown current curl over the third step down, and she understood that the river was not rising. It was arriving.

"Four hours," Callum said beside her. He had his watch out, though the sky was too low and gray to read anything from it. "Maybe five if the crest stalls at the gorge mouth. It won't."

"Then we do this in four."

"You're not doing this." He said it quietly, which was worse than shouting. Since the cave, since she'd found his map folded inside her father's letters like a knife slipped between pages of scripture, they had spoken to each other in this careful, flattened register—the voice people use walking on ice. "Marguerite. The stair is already under. You'd be diving into a chimney with no guarantee of an air pocket past the second landing. Even if you get down, even if you set the charges, the blast could collapse the shaft before you're out. There is no version of this where you surface on schedule."

"There's a version where I don't surface at all," she said. "I've made peace with the arithmetic."

"That's not peace. That's just grief wearing a life vest."

She almost laughed. Almost. Instead she crouched and began checking the charges laid out on the tarp: four blocks of Voss's own explosive, repurposed, wired with fuse cord Sefu had spliced twice over because their proper detonators were somewhere at the bottom of the flooded lower camp. Improvised. Ugly. Good enough.

Behind her, the ridge camp was a tableau of exhaustion. Priya was re-dressing Sefu's bandage by lamplight, her movements brisk because slowness would have meant tenderness and neither of them could afford tenderness right now. Odile stood apart, arms crossed, watching the waterline the way she'd watched it for three days—like a woman counting someone else's sins. Voss's men were farther along the ridge, huddled under tarps, unarmed since the firefight, waiting for an order their leader hadn't given.

Voss himself sat alone at the far end of camp, and when Marguerite glanced back at him, he was already looking at her. He didn't nod. He didn't look away either. Whatever he had decided in the dark beneath the temple, he was still deciding it, hour by hour, and his silence was the only reason she had these four hours at all.

"Callum." She stood, shouldering the pack of charges. "You mapped this valley. You know better than anyone what happens at peak flood. If the Rivergate survives this season intact—even half intact—Voss's backers will come back with a new survey, a new crew, better equipment. The dam gives them cover forever. A reservoir is the perfect grave robber. Nobody asks what's under a lake."

"I know what happens. I drew the map that makes it possible." His jaw worked. "That's why it should be me going down. My survey, my debt, my—"

"No." The word came out harder than she intended, and she saw him flinch, and she hated herself for the small satisfaction of it. She softened nothing else. "Your sister's file dies with Voss's goodwill or it doesn't—that's between you two, and I won't pretend to understand it. But my father is down there. Whatever's left of him. And whatever he found, whatever he finally understood in those last weeks alone in the dark—" Her voice caught. She let it. Some things you earned the right to carry badly. "This is mine to finish. Not yours. Not his memory's. Mine."

The rain came again, fine and needling, and the light went the color of old pewter.

"If you're not back in ninety minutes," Callum said finally, "I'm coming after you."

"You'll be coming after a corpse. Stay on the surface. Keep the boat ready at the shelf rock. That's an order, Reyes."

"I don't work for you."

"No," she agreed. "But you love me, and you're going to do what I ask anyway, because you know I'll know if you don't."

He looked at her for a long moment, and the rain ran down both their faces, and there was nothing left to negotiate with. He touched her cheek once—briefly, like sealing a letter—and stepped back.

"Ninety minutes," he said.

She went down into the water.

* * *

The first three steps were easy. The fourth was where the river lived.

Marguerite descended with the charge pack held high against her chest, feeling the cold close over her shoulders, then her chin, then her ears, until the world above was a trembling silver ceiling and the world below was green-black stone. She had rigged a spare regulator line from the dive tanks—Priya's idea, salvaged from the medical stores—and she breathed off it in short, disciplined pulls, counting her rhythm the way her father had taught her to count rope lengths when she was nine years old and afraid of deep water. *In for four. Out for six. The panic is a liar. Keep moving.*

The shaft was exactly as they'd seen it three days ago from the threshold: a staircase cut into living rock, descending beyond any lantern's reach, the walls carved with figures that the floodwater had spent centuries softening into suggestion. But the water changed everything. Where before there had been dry dark, now there was moving dark—a slow, muscular current pulling downward through the shaft, dragging at her legs, trying to fold her against the steps. She gripped the guide rope they'd left anchored at the top and paid it out hand over hand, and the stone figures slid past her face like a procession of the drowned: women with jars, men with nets, children holding hands in a frieze that spiraled down and down.

At the second landing, the air pocket her father's notes had promised was gone. Flooded through. She hung in the black water, breathing recycled air that tasted of rubber and fear, and felt the current strengthen below her like something inhaling.

*Turn back.* The thought arrived fully formed, reasonable, almost kind. *You've proven your point. Let the river have it.*

She thought about her father instead. About the skeleton at the clay desk she hadn't reached yet—she knew it was there; the letters had told her, in his careful postscript, *if you are reading this, I did not leave, and I am sorry, and I am also not sorry at all*. She thought about ten years of keeping her back to this valley, of taking survey jobs in flat country, of never once saying his name aloud except to correct other people's pronunciation of it.

She kept descending.

The third landing opened sideways—a corridor, horizontal, running back into the heart of the complex. Here the water shallowed to her waist, then her knees, and she waded with the pack held overhead, her boots finding a paved floor slick with silt. Her headlamp carved a cone out of the darkness and the darkness pressed back on it from all sides, patient as water.

The corridor walls were covered in tablets.

Not scattered fragments—*installed* tablets, set into niches in ordered rows, thousands of them, receding beyond her light like a library built by ants. Each niche bore a small carved lamp bracket above it, long empty. Each tablet was dense with the wedge-script her father had spent thirty years learning to read, and though she couldn't decipher a word of it, she understood the shape of the thing instantly, the way you understand a cathedral without knowing the liturgy.

A record. A whole civilization's record, deliberately housed below the waterline, behind a door designed to be found only by people willing to drown for it.

Her father's letters had said: *They were not destroyed. They chose. Every tablet down here says the same thing in different hands—we were offered conquest or the river, and we chose the river, and we wrote down why so that no one who came after could pretend we had been defeated.*

Marguerite stood in the flooded corridor with water at her thighs and her father's ghost at her shoulder, and she set the first charge.

* * *

Callum counted seconds the way other men prayed.

He stood at the shelf rock with the boat tied off behind him, watching the rope Marguerite had carried down disappear into the churning water at the stair's mouth. Ninety minutes. Fifty-one gone. The line lay slack across the wet stone, twitching occasionally with the current, and every twitch was a small electric shock up his arm.

Odile came and stood beside him without being asked. For a while neither spoke. The rain had thickened into a steady drumming roar, and downstream the gorge was beginning to make a sound he recognized from his survey years—the deep, subsonic groan of a valley filling faster than it could drain.

"She told you to stay," Odile said.

"She told me to stay."

"And yet here you stand, looking at that rope like a man pricing a coffin."

Callum didn't answer. He checked his watch. Fifty-eight minutes.

He had spent the last three days understanding precisely what kind of man he was, and the inventory was not flattering. A man who sold maps. A man whose love for his sister had been converted, by Voss's lawyers, into a leash. A man who had let Marguerite find the survey folded among her father's letters because some cowardly part of him had wanted her to find it—wanted the confession extracted from him rather than having to perform it. And now a man standing in the rain obeying an order given by a woman walking into a drowning temple, because obedience was easier than following her.

Except it wasn't. That was the thing. Obedience wasn't easier. It was just quieter, and quiet had stopped working for him somewhere around the moment she'd said *this is mine to finish* and he'd realized she was right, and that being right didn't make it survivable.

"Odile," he said. "If I go down that rope and don't come back, tell Priya the medical kit has a false bottom. There's money in it. Not much. Enough for Sefu's mother's roof."

Odile looked at him for a long moment. Then she spat into the water, a boatman's benediction, and said: "Ninety minutes was her number. Yours can be seventy-five. After that, even a fool knows the arithmetic."

Sixty-four minutes. He unclipped the spare tank from the boat, shrugged into the harness, and took the rope in both hands.

The water took him like a debt collector.

* * *

The second charge went in at the great hall's western colonnade, and Marguerite was running on something beyond adrenaline now—some cold, clear channel that her father must have found too, in his last months, the place where fear burns off and leaves only purpose. The halls were half-flooded, thigh-deep in places, chest-deep where the corridors dipped, and she moved through them with the charge pack growing lighter block by block, placing each one against load-bearing pillars marked on the rough plan she'd memorized from Callum's own survey. His map. She was using his map to erase what his map had been drawn to expose. She suspected he'd understand that. She suspected he'd already forgiven her for it, which was somehow harder.

In the central chamber she found the desk.

She had known it would be there. The letters had described it—*a table of fitted stone, facing the tablet wall, where I have arranged my translation of the final inscription*—but knowing and seeing were different countries. The clay tablets were still stacked on it in neat rows, forty or fifty of them, her father's field tags still tied to the corners with the same green twine he'd used on every dig of his career. His lantern sat beside them, corroded to a sculpture of itself. And against the wall behind the desk, settled gently by the water into a posture that might have been rest, were the bones.

Small bones. A man who had been smaller than his reputation. One arm extended toward the tablets, fingers resting near the top row, as if he had died mid-sentence—or mid-reading, which for him amounted to the same thing.

Marguerite stood in the flooded chamber with water at her waist and her headlamp shaking slightly, and she said, out loud, in a voice that broke on the second word: "Hi, Dad."

The water said nothing. The tablets said everything, in a script only he had ever bothered to learn.

"I brought the charges," she told him. "I'm going to bring the ceiling down and let the river finish it. Nobody's ever getting a grant proposal off this place. Nobody's ever going to argue about you in a journal. It's just going to be theirs—all of theirs—the way they wanted." She wiped her face with a wet hand, pointless, and laughed, or sobbed, or both. "You'd hate it. You'd also, if I'm honest, be the first person to say it's right. Both things. You were always both things."

She placed the third charge at the base of the central pillar, twenty feet from the desk, angled to drop the roof across the tablet wall. Then she knelt in the water beside the bones, and took the topmost tablet from the stack—one small record, one voice out of thousands, heavy as a paving stone—and tucked it inside her jacket against her skin.

*One voice gets out,* she thought. *One witness. That's not robbery. That's a funeral guest taking a flower.*

Then the current changed.

She felt it before she heard it—a sudden reversal in the water around her thighs, a sucking pull toward the corridor she'd come by, and beneath it, transmitted through the stone floor into her boots, a deep rhythmic concussion. Like footsteps. Like the mountain clearing its throat.

The peak surge. Early. Hours early.

The water in the chamber rose six inches in the time it took her to turn, and the corridor behind her was no longer a corridor. It was a throat, and it was swallowing.

* * *

Callum hit the second landing with his lungs full of fire and the rope burning through his gloves, and the current nearly took him there—slammed him against the carved wall hard enough to crack something in his shoulder, spun him, dragged him down two steps before he got his boots wedged under a figure's stone foot and hauled himself against the flow. The air pocket was gone. Of course it was gone. He breathed off the tank in panicked gulps, forced himself to slow, *in for four, out for six*, her father's trick, everyone in this story taught by the same dead man.

Below him, faintly, a light swung in the black.

"MARGUERITE!"

His voice went nowhere in the water. He dove.

The corridor was chest-deep now and rising as he waded it, the current shoving at his back like a crowd in a hurry, and he followed her headlamp's glow through tablet-lined halls that he had once drawn in ink for money and was now drowning in for free. He found her at the junction by the great hall, braced against the flow, hauling the last charge's fuse cord taut.

"You're supposed to be topside!" she shouted over the roar.

"So are you!" He reached her, grabbed her arm. "The surge is here—it's hours early—the shaft is a waterfall by now, we have to go *now*—"

"The last charge isn't set—"

"It doesn't matter! Three charges will drop half this wing, the river does the rest—"

"Three charges drops the *east* wing!" She was screaming now, and her face in the headlamp light was wild and streaming and more alive than he had ever seen it. "The tablet hall runs west! If the ceiling holds over the records, Voss comes back with divers and pumps and a hundred years of funding and they dig her out like a—"

"Then we set the fourth charge together and we run together and if we're too slow we die together, but we are not splitting up in a flooding building, Marguerite, I am not doing that—"

For one heartbeat she stared at him, and he watched the war happen behind her eyes—duty and love tearing the same muscle in opposite directions—and then the water shoved them both sideways and made the decision physical. She thrust the last charge into his hands.

"West colonnade, base of the third pillar, sixty seconds. Fuse cord's already cut. I'll meet you at the corridor mouth." She grabbed his collar and pulled his forehead against hers, one fierce press, salt water and rain water and river water indistinguishable. "Run, Callum."

He ran.

The west colonnade was neck-deep when he got there, the carved pillars rising out of the flood like the masts of a sunken fleet, and he jammed the charge against the third pillar's base with numb hands, thumbing the fuse clamp twice before it bit. Behind him, through the flooded archway, he saw her headlamp swinging toward him, fast, fast, the water at her shoulders now—

And then the lights went out.

Not hers. All of them. The whole valley's—because he surfaced into open air for one gasp and saw, through a collapsing archway, the ridge camp's lamps vanish, and heard, rolling down the gorge like the end of the world clearing its throat, the sound of the dam's diversion gates failing upstream.

The river stood up.

It came down the corridor behind Marguerite as a single black wall, and Callum, clinging to a pillar in the west colonnade as the flood lifted him off his feet, saw her headlamp tumble end over end in the churning water—saw it go under—saw it come back up, still lit, still moving, twenty feet away and receding—

"—CALLUM—"

The fourth charge's fuse hissed at his back, burning down through its last inches, and the Rivergate began, at last, to come down.
