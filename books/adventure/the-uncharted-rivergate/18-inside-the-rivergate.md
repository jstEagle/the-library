## 18. Inside the Rivergate

The water took her to the knees before the stair even ended.

Marguerite had imagined this descent a hundred times since she was a child—had drawn it in the margins of school notebooks, built it from river mud on the banks of the delta, always with herself as the one who went down. But none of those imaginings included the cold. The water in the stairwell was snowmelt-cold, fed by some deep vein of the mountain, and it climbed her body like a hand closing finger by finger. Knees. Thighs. The waistband of her trousers, where she'd tucked the charge timer against her spine.

Her headlamp carved a cone out of the black. Beyond it, nothing. Behind her, the sound of Callum's breathing, three steps up, where she'd ordered him to stay.

"You're past the point where I can argue with you," he'd said at the top, rain hammering the entrance behind them both.

"I know," she'd said. "That's why I'm going alone."

Now his voice came down the shaft, flattened by stone. "Marguerite. The water's rising faster than Odile said it would."

"Then I'll be quick."

She didn't tell him what they both knew: that quick might not be possible. That the halls below might go on for a kilometer, that her father had spent—what? Days? Weeks?—down here in the dark with his lamps and his notebooks, mapping a city no living person had seen. She didn't tell him because saying it aloud would make it true, and she needed, for a few minutes more, to believe she could walk in and walk out.

The stair turned. Her boot found the next step by faith rather than sight, and the water deepened to her chest, and then—mercifully—the ceiling rose. The stair opened into a gallery, and the gallery's vaulted roof stood a full arm's length above her head, and the water spread out flat and still as a lake floor.

She swam.

It wasn't far. Twenty strokes, maybe thirty, her lamp beam sliding across walls that made her stop mid-stroke and tread water just to look.

They were carved. Every surface. Not the crude geometric fretwork of the valley's known ruins—she'd grown up on photographs of those, had traced their patterns in her father's books—but figures. Processions of them, hundreds, thousands, rendered in low relief so fine that the floodwater seemed to make them move. People carrying jars. People carrying children. People carrying books—tablets, bundles of reed script, things held against their chests the way you hold the thing you cannot bear to drop.

And above them all, repeated along the entire gallery like a refrain, a single image: a figure standing waist-deep in water, arms open, face upturned.

*We chose this,* the walls said. *We walked into the water with our records in our arms.*

Marguerite's throat closed. She swam on.

* * *

The hall beyond the gallery was smaller, drier—or had been, once. Its floor sat higher, on a shelf of bedrock the flood hadn't fully claimed, and when her feet found bottom again she stood in ankle-deep water under a ceiling lost in darkness. Her lamp found furniture first.

A table. A camp stove, corroded green. A folding cot collapsed against the wall, its canvas rotted to lace. A crate of tinned food with labels she recognized from her childhood—the same brand her father had packed on every expedition, the same brand he'd stacked in the pantry of the house on the delta road while telling her stories about temples that were never on any map.

His camp. His final camp.

The realization arrived not as thought but as temperature—a heat behind her eyes, a pressure in her chest, her whole body suddenly too small for what it contained. Ten years of anger stood in that room with her. Ten years of *he chose it over us*, of the funeral with no body, of her mother's long gray silence, of every man at every university who said *brilliant but unstable, brilliant but obsessed*. All of it standing in ankle-deep water, looking at a rusted stove.

She made herself walk forward.

The desk was against the far wall—a slab of stone, levelled, and on it rows upon rows of clay tablets, each one wrapped in waxed cloth against the damp, each one numbered in her father's handwriting in grease pencil on the wrapping. He'd catalogued them. Of course he had. Even down here, even dying, Elias Winterbourne had kept his records straight.

And at the desk, in the chair—

Her lamp found him gently, the way you'd want to be found. He was seated, upright, leaned slightly forward over the tablets as if he'd paused mid-translation to think. The water had taken everything soft. What remained was clean and light as driftwood, and small—smaller than the man in her memory, though memory always makes fathers large. His hands rested on the desk on either side of a final tablet, one still unwrapped, its cuneiform facing the ceiling.

He hadn't been reaching for an exit. He hadn't been crawling toward the stair. He had died at his work, in the posture of a man listening.

"Dad." The word came out cracked, half-drowned. She said it again, louder, and the hall took it and gave it back to her from four directions, and for a moment she was nine years old calling up the stairs of the delta house, and he was answering, and the world was intact.

Then the moment passed, and she was forty-one years old in a drowning temple, and the only answer was water dripping somewhere in the dark, patient as a clock.

* * *

She didn't touch him. That mattered to her, though she couldn't have said why—some instinct about the dead, about the dignity of the posture he'd chosen. Instead she knelt in the shallows beside the desk and began, with hands that shook, to unwrap the tablets.

The waxed cloth peeled away stiff as bark. Beneath it the clay was dark, dense, fired hard—these had been baked, not sun-dried, someone had taken care—and the script covering it was unlike anything in her training. Older than the valley script. Denser. But interspersed through it, in columns down the left edge, ran translations in her father's hand, pencilled onto the clay itself in his small, precise capitals.

She read.

*The enemy came in the third year of the siege. They burned the orchards on the terraces. We could have fought. We had walls enough and grain enough. But we had seen what conquest does to a people—it does not kill them, it unmakes them, it takes their names and gives them new ones, it takes their gods and puts them in museums.*

*So the council voted. Not all of us. Some stayed to fight and are honored. The rest of us carried the library down the stair.*

*Everything we are is written here. If we must die, let us die complete. Let the water keep what the enemy cannot.*

Marguerite lowered the tablet to her lap and sat very still in the ankle-deep water, and something in her chest that had been clenched for ten years began, slowly, to unclench.

They hadn't been conquered. They hadn't been destroyed. They had looked at the choice every civilization faces—to survive compromised or end intact—and they had chosen the water, deliberately, together, with their records in their arms. And her father had found them. Had sat in this room month after month reading their last testament, translating their farewell, keeping them company at the end of their ten-thousand-year vigil.

*Let the water keep what the enemy cannot.*

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, Dad. You understood."

Because now she did too. It wasn't obsession. It had never been obsession—not the way Voss's men meant it, not the way the universities had smeared it. Her father had spent his life studying drowned cities because he was looking for the one people who had done it on purpose, who had left a message instead of just bones. And he'd found it, and the message was addressed to whoever came next, and it said: *some things are worth more than survival. Some knowledge is worth sealing away.*

He had died understanding them. And she had spent ten years hating him for it.

"I'm sorry," she whispered. The hall carried it away. "I'm sorry I stopped reading your letters. I'm sorry I let them tell me what you were."

She reached out—not to the remains, but to the desk's edge, laying her palm on the cold stone beside his hand. Close enough. As close as the dead allow.

And then she saw it, half-submerged at the base of the tablet stack: a oilskin packet, tied with cord, her name on it in grease pencil.

*MARGUERITE.*

* * *

Her fingers wouldn't work right for the first attempt. The second attempt worked. The cord slid free; the oilskin unfolded; inside were pages torn from his field notebook, water-stained at the edges but legible, dated across the final weeks of his life.

The early pages were survey notes, coordinates, depth soundings—the working mind, familiar as her own heartbeat. Then the entries shortened. Then they changed.

*Day 19. The air is bad in the lower galleries. I've moved my camp to the high hall. I am not afraid. I want that written plainly, wherever this ends up: I am not afraid, and I do not regret a single mile of the road that brought me here.*

*Day 23. I have read perhaps a tenth of the library. It would take ten lifetimes. I have one, and most of it is spent, and I find I don't mind. Better one person who understands than a hundred who would only take.*

*Day 26. If Marguerite ever reads this—I tried to come home. I did. I got as far as the waystation and I sat there two days with the boat loaded, and I thought about her face, and I turned around. I don't expect that to earn forgiveness. I only want her to know it wasn't the work that kept me. It was this message. Someone had to stay and finish listening. Someone had to be the witness they asked for. I was the only one who came.*

*I told Edmund once, years ago, over brandy: some sites should never be opened. He laughed and called me a romantic. He'll come here someday—he can't help himself, it's the engine that drives him, the same engine that drove me. If he comes, give him the tablets. He loved this place before I did, in his way. But Marguerite—if it's you, my darling, if it's you who finds this—*

*Let it rest. Let the water keep them. You'll understand, because you're mine, and you always understood more than you admitted.*

*Don't dig me up. Sit with me a while, and then go home, and live above ground. Live in the sun. That's all I ever wanted for you.*

*—E.W.*

The packet blurred. She let it.

She knelt in the flooding hall with her dead father's last words in her hands and cried the way she hadn't cried at the funeral, hadn't cried in ten years of careful, controlled grief—ugly and loud and kneeling, the sound bouncing off the carved walls, off the processions of people who had also said goodbye to everyone they loved and walked into the water anyway. The figures on the walls bore witness. That was what they were for, she realized. Not decoration. Witness. A civilization's last act had been to carve its own funeral procession into the stone so that someone, someday, would stand here and watch them go under.

She was watching. She was the witness now.

When it was done—when the crying burned itself out the way fires do, leaving her hollow and strangely clean—she wiped her face on her sleeve, folded the oilskin with the care it deserved, and sealed it inside her jacket, against her heart, above the charge timer.

Then she stood, and put her hand on the desk one more time.

"I understand," she said. "I'm going to finish it for you."

The charges in her pack weren't Voss's demolition charges, heavy and indiscriminate. Hers were shaped, placed with Sefu's coaching, aimed at the gallery supports—the pillars holding the upper vaults, the ones that, brought down, would collapse the entrance passage and the stair and bury the halls beneath fifty meters of shifting rock and permanent floodwater. Not destruction. Sealing. The way the ancients themselves had sealed their crypts. The water would keep them, and the stone would keep the water, and no dam project, no salvage crew, no dynamite line would ever reach them again.

She set the first charge at the gallery mouth, pressing the plastic explosive into a joint between blocks, seating the detonator, checking the wire. Her hands were steady now. Grief had burned off everything unsteady in her.

The second charge went at the base of the stair, halfway back toward the entrance. The third—

The third she never placed.

Because the water in the hall shifted. Not the slow patient rise she'd been tracking, not the drip-clock seep of the mountain vein—a displacement, sudden and directional, a swell rolling in from the direction of the stairwell like something large had entered the water upstream.

Her headlamp snapped toward the sound.

Far down the flooded gallery, at the ragged edge of her beam, a light answered hers. Moving closer. Steady. Unhurried.

And behind it, faint but unmistakable across the water, the scrape of a boat hull against stone, and a voice she knew—low, roughened by age and rain and something that might have been grief—calling her name.

Voss had followed her down.
