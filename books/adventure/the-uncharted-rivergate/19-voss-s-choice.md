## 19. Voss's Choice

Edmund Voss had spent forty years learning to read water, and the water in the Rivergate was telling him the truth: they had perhaps three hours before the valley closed like a fist.

He stood at the lip of the drowned stair with the rain hammering his shoulders and watched Marguerite Winterbourne's headlamp shrink into the dark below. She had not asked permission. She had not looked back. She had simply turned from the cave shelter where her father's letters still hung in the wet air like something flayed and open, and she had gone down into the temple with a satchel of charges on her hip.

"Sir." Brandt at his elbow, rain sheeting off the man's slicker. "Do we stop her?"

Voss did not answer immediately. Below him, the stair descended in flooded steps, each one catching the last grey light of the storm-day like teeth. Somewhere down there was the answer to thirty years of his life—the tablets, the records, the civilization that had chosen drowning over conquest. Somewhere down there was Hartwell's final camp. And somewhere down there, if the old man's letters were to be believed—and Voss believed them, God help him, he had always believed them—was a request that it all be left alone.

*Let it rest.*

"She's setting demolition charges," Brandt said again, louder, against the rain. "On a site worth more than everything either of us has earned in our lives."

"I know what she's doing, Brandt."

"Then—"

"You have my permission," Voss said quietly, "to shut up."

Brandt shut up. The rain filled the silence instead, drumming on stone, on canvas, on the black surface of the floodwater that had already climbed two steps since dawn. Voss looked at his own hands—sixty-one years old now, scarred at the knuckles, steady as they had ever been—and found that they were shaking anyway.

He thought about the gun in his belt. He had carried it upriver for weeks without once drawing it, and every man in both camps knew why it was there. Not for Sefu or Priya or Odile. Not even for Callum Reyes, whose treachery Voss had purchased years ago and whose betrayal he had seen coming like weather. The gun was for this moment. For the contingency no contract could cover: the possibility that Marguerite Winterbourne would try to destroy the Rivergate rather than let him have it.

The contingency had arrived. The gun sat against his hip like a question.

He went down the stairs after her.

* * *

The water was waist-deep by the fourth landing and rising fast—a cold, mineral-smelling push that tugged at his legs like hands. His lamp carved a cone out of the dark, and in that cone the temple revealed itself in fragments: bas-reliefs of processions, figures carrying jars, figures carrying children, all of them moving downward, always downward, toward the water that had eventually taken them. He had studied photographs of such work his whole career. Photographs were taxidermy. This was the animal itself, alive and breathing around him, and it was dying while he watched.

"Hartwell," he said aloud, to no one, because there was no one left to say it to but the dead man himself. "You absolute bastard. You found it first after all."

His voice came back off the stone, flattened by water.

He had been twenty-nine when he'd first heard Elias Winterbourne lecture on the monsoon country—lean, brilliant, insufferably certain, drawing river systems on a chalkboard as though he were conducting an orchestra. They had drunk together afterward until two in the morning, arguing about whether the drowned sites of the upper valley were flood casualties or something stranger. Elias had said something strange. *What if they didn't drown? What if they submerged themselves?* And Voss had laughed, and Elias had laughed, and neither of them had ever stopped thinking about it for the rest of their lives.

That was the thing no one understood—not Marguerite, not Reyes, not the men on his payroll who thought this was about salvage value and government contracts. It had never been about the money. It had been about being twenty-nine years old in a bar in Colombo with the only man who had ever asked the same impossible question he asked, and spending forty years trying to answer it before the other man did.

And then Elias had vanished upriver, and Voss had spent ten years hating him for winning.

The water reached his chest. He swam the next stretch, lamp held high, boots dragging, until his feet found stone again on a lower gallery where the ceiling rose and the current slackened. Ahead, faint through the flooded doorway, a light moved. Her light.

He followed it into the great hall.

* * *

He knew it was the great hall before his lamp finished its first sweep, because Elias had described it—in the notes Voss had carried for a decade, in the letters Marguerite had read aloud yesterday with her voice cracking only twice. Forty columns. A floor of fitted clay brick. And along the far wall, raised above the highest recorded flood line on shelves of the same fired clay, the archive.

It was underwater to the knee now. It would be gone within hours. But his lamp caught it—one long wall of tablets, hundreds of them, thousands maybe, stacked in their niches like books in a burning library, and beside them, at a desk built against the column base, the remains of a camp. A rotted tent. A rusted stove. A lantern. And a chair, and in the chair—

Voss stopped wading.

He didn't need the light to tell him what sat in the chair. The posture told him. Ten years of imagining had already drawn it: Elias Winterbourne, seated at his desk, facing his life's work, done. The skeleton was dressed in rotted canvas, one hand resting on the desk among the tablets, arranged—or fallen, it was impossible to know—as though mid-sentence.

Marguerite stood ten feet away, her lamp lowered, her hand pressed over her mouth. She had gotten here first. Of course she had. She always got everywhere first; it was the family disease.

"Don't," she said, when she saw his light. Her voice was raw. "Don't come any closer."

Voss kept coming. Slowly, through the waist-deep water, his free hand nowhere near the gun, though they both knew it was there.

"He waited for you," Marguerite said. "Look at where he's sitting. He faced the door. He faced the way he came in. He was waiting for someone to follow him down, and it took ten years, and it wasn't me, and it wasn't—" Her voice broke and reassembled itself. "It was you, Edmund. He knew it would be you."

"That's sentiment," Voss said. His voice came out wrong. Too thick. "He was facing his work. Men die facing their work."

"Yes," she said. "They do. And he wrote you a letter, didn't he. You never showed me that one."

He had not. There had been a letter—found among the effects forwarded from the last expedition's base camp, addressed to *E.V.* in that upright, impatient hand. Voss had read it four hundred times and never shown it to anyone, because it ended with the same words the others ended with, and he had spent ten years pretending those words were grief talking rather than judgment.

*If you're reading this, Edmund, then you've beaten me to it, and I don't mind half as much as I would have expected to. But hear an old rival out: some things are answers, and some things are questions, and the Rivergate is a question that took a whole civilization to ask. Don't you dare turn it into an answer. Let it rest.*

Water surged past his thighs. Somewhere above, distantly, the valley was making its decision.

Marguerite had opened her satchel. The charges sat in their cradles, dull yellow cylinders, obscene against the pale clay of the archive shelf. She worked quickly, competently—he recognized the rigging style; he'd taught a version of it to river surveyors in another lifetime—and she did not look at him while she worked, and he understood that she expected him to draw the gun at any moment, and that she had decided not to care.

"Marguerite."

"Don't." She pressed the first charge into a niche between tablets. "You followed me down to stop me. Stop me, then. Or stand there and watch. Those are your options. I'm not negotiating."

"The dam consortium will send divers," he said. "In five years, ten. Sonar mapping, saturation crews. If you bring this ceiling down they'll dig through the rubble anyway, slower, uglier, and they'll take every tablet that survives."

"If I don't bring it down, your map does it in six months with dynamite and a crane barge." She set the second charge. "At least mine is a burial. Yours is an autopsy."

"And which one would your father have chosen?"

She went still. Water moved around them both, patient, rising.

"He chose this," she said finally. "He sat down in that chair and he chose it. He found the whole answer, Edmund—the records, the names, the reason they went under the water with their eyes open—and he wrote it all down and then he asked us to leave it. That's not defeat. That's the hardest choice there is." She looked at him across the black water, and her lamp caught her face, and she looked so much like Elias that Voss's chest hurt. "You know it is. You've known for ten years. That letter you keep in the false bottom of your document case—you've read it enough times to know."

The rain was a distant roar now, filtered through forty meters of stone. The water climbed. And Voss stood in a drowning temple with a gun on his hip and forty years of ambition in his throat, and found that he could not make his hand move.

* * *

Here is what Edmund Voss had told himself on the journey upriver, and on every expedition before it, and in every boardroom where he had leveraged debts and bought maps and ruined reputations in service of this site: *I am doing this for him. Elias would want it found. Elias would want it saved from the dam, catalogued, preserved, understood. I am the executor of his life's work because no one else is capable.*

It had been a good story. He had told it so well for so long that he had almost believed it.

But standing in the great hall, watching Marguerite place charges among the tablets with her father's skeleton keeping watch behind her, the story collapsed all at once, the way bad masonry collapses—all at once and from the bottom. Because the truth was simpler and much worse. Elias had beaten him. Elias had found it, understood it, written it, and chosen to stay, and Voss had spent ten years and a great deal of other people's money trying to prove that the finding mattered more than the choosing. That possession was the point. That if he could just hold the answer in his hands, the question would stop hurting.

The water was at his ribs. The tablets gleamed in the lamplight, row upon row, the voices of people who had walked down these stairs on purpose, carrying their children, carrying their records, choosing the water over the sword. He had wanted to read them his whole life. He was reading them now, in the only way that mattered: they were drowning a second time, and they had asked to be allowed to.

"Marguerite." His voice barely carried. "How many charges?"

"Six. Enough to drop the east gallery onto the archive and crack the stair. Nothing gets salvaged clean after that. Not by divers, not by anyone." She paused, the last charge in her hands, and looked at him. "Last chance, Edmund. Say the word and I'll stop. We take one tablet each—we carry them out ourselves, tonight, and the rest goes under the reservoir, and you spend the rest of your life wondering. That's the deal you came up here ready to force. So force it."

He thought about the gun. He actually reached for it—his hand went to his belt, closed on the grip, and Marguerite watched him do it without flinching, standing in the floodwater with a demolition charge against her chest, and in her face there was no fear at all, only a terrible patience, the patience of someone who had already grieved him and was waiting for him to finish.

And Edmund Voss, who had ruined men for less than this, who had held a young cartographer's sister hostage with a file folder, who had bought and bullied and bargained his way upriver through the worst monsoon season in a decade—Edmund Voss began to cry.

It was not graceful. It was not the single dignified tear of a man in a novel. It was ugly, chest-deep sobbing, the kind he had not done since he was a boy, and it bent him double in the water, and the gun stayed in the holster because his hand would not coordinate grief and violence at the same time, and Marguerite Winterbourne stood in the flooding hall of the Rivergate and watched a ruthless old man come apart, and did not look away.

"She died in March," he said, when he could speak. "Your mother sent word. I didn't go to the funeral. I told myself it was because of the expedition funding cycle." He wiped his face with a wet hand, uselessly. "I loved him, Marguerite. In whatever broken way men like us are permitted to love anyone. And he chose the water over me too, in the end. He chose it over everyone. And I have spent ten years furious about that, and calling the fury ambition, because ambition has better manners."

"I know," she said quietly.

"The charges," he said. "Set them properly. All six. If we're burying it, we bury it past recovery—I won't have the consortium picking through the bones in five years because you economized on explosives."

Her breath caught. "Edmund—"

"I'm an old man standing in a flood," he said. "Don't make speeches at me. Set the charges."

* * *

She worked. He helped her—there was a bitter comedy in it, the two of them wading together through the rising water, passing detonator wire hand to hand beneath the gaze of a civilization that had made this exact choice three thousand years ago and recorded it in clay for anyone stubborn enough to follow them down. At the desk, Elias kept his counsel. Voss paused beside the chair on his way to the stair, water surging at his chest, and rested his hand for a moment on the desk's edge, next to the skeletal fingers.

"You win," he said. "You always bloody well won. I hope wherever you are, you're insufferable about it."

Then the water lifted the tent scraps from the floor, and the current strengthened, and there was no more time for anything.

At the foot of the drowned stair, Marguerite unspooled the firing wire toward the surface, and Voss put his shoulder under Callum Reyes's arm—the boy had come down after her, of course he had, dripping and desperate—and the three of them fought upward through the flood together, toward the thinning light, while below them the great hall filled and the archive went dark niche by niche, going under the way its makers had gone under: deliberately, completely, at peace.

Above, on the last dry landing, Marguerite crouched over the detonator with the wire in her hands, and looked at him one final time.

"Together?" she asked.

Voss realized she meant it literally—that she was offering him half the honor of it, or half the blame, and that after everything, she could not tell the difference, and neither could he.

"No," he said. "It should be family."

And Marguerite Winterbourne closed the circuit, and the Rivergate began to die for the second and final time, and the mountain came down.
