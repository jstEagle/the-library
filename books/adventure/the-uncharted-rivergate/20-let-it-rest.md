## 20. Let It Rest

The water was at my knees when I lit the first fuse.

I had imagined this moment a hundred ways on the climb down the drowned stair—solemn, terrible, holy—but I had never imagined it would feel so much like arithmetic. Two charges at the eastern colonnade where the ceiling was thinnest. One at the stairhead to seal our own way in behind us. The math of collapse: how much stone, how much water, how little time. My father had taught me surveying with numbers like these, chalked on the back of an old chart when I was nine years old. *The river always wins, Maggie. Your job is only to decide what it wins.*

I decided.

The fuse hissed, a small orange serpent crawling away from my hand into the dark, and I turned and waded for the stair as fast as the water allowed, which was not fast at all. The hall around me was a cathedral of black water and older blackness—the columns rising out of the flood like the legs of drowned giants, the clay tablets still ranked on their shelves where a civilization had filed its own last testament. Somewhere in that dark was his desk. His skeleton. His final camp, and the forgiveness I had already spoken aloud to no one but him.

I did not look back. Looking back is how people drown.

* * *

Callum caught me at the third landing.

He was supposed to be topside. He was supposed to be exactly where I'd ordered him to be, on the ridge with Priya and Sefu's bandaged shoulder and Odile's silence, counting seconds. Instead he came down through the spray with a rope in his teeth and fury in his eyes, and when he reached me he didn't shout—he grabbed my wrist and put his mouth against my ear because the river was already speaking over both of us.

"Forty seconds," he said. "Maybe less. The surge is coming early—I can hear it."

"You can't hear a flood before it—"

"Marguerite." Just my name, once, and everything else inside it. He had followed me against orders into a drowning temple, and there was no time to be angry about it, and I understood suddenly that I wasn't angry at all. That was the worst part. Some betrayals you forgive in advance because you know you'd have done the same.

We climbed. The water climbed faster.

It came up the stairwell like something alive—not rising but *arriving*, a brown wall of monsoon peak funneled through the gorge, carrying branches and silt and the whole furious weight of a sky that had been emptying itself for six days without pause. The first charge went off while we were still on the stairs. I felt it more than heard it, a deep bone-knock through the stone, through the water, through my own ribs, and then the dust came—a gray bloom rolling up the flooded corridor below us, swallowing the columns, the tablets, the desk, everything.

"Second one's short," Callum shouted. "Move!"

We moved. Above us the stairhead charge was timed off the same burning line, and I could hear it spitting somewhere overhead, and I made the mistake then—I looked back, just once, down into the gray roil where the Rivergate was beginning to come apart. A column went. I saw it go, a slow tilting of something that had stood three thousand years, and the ceiling above it folded like wet paper, and the sound was not a sound at all but a pressure, a god closing a book.

Then the surge hit the entrance behind us and the world became water.

* * *

Drowning is quiet, in the moment. Everyone imagines thrashing, screaming, but what I remember is the hush—the roar cut off all at once, replaced by a green-brown silence and the taste of the whole valley in my mouth. I was tumbling. Stone scraped my back. My lungs were a fist clenching closed. And through the murk I saw, absurdly, clearly, a single clay tablet turning end over end in the flood, its cuneiform rows flashing pale, like a page of a letter being read aloud by the river itself.

*A civilization that chose the water rather than be conquered.* My father had believed they'd left their records here deliberately, sealed them in the deep halls like a message in a bottle thrown not forward but downward. Let the conquerors take the fields and the herds and the names. The water keeps what matters.

Now the water kept them for good. I had seen to it.

A hand closed on my collar and hauled.

Callum pulled me up through the churn and we broke surface together in the entrance chamber, which was no longer a chamber but a throat of white water, and the ceiling was coming down in slabs I could hear cracking like ice on a spring lake. He got an arm across my chest and drove for the light—a gray smudge high on the right wall where the stairhead charge had blown outward instead of in, opening a gap onto the gorge side. Luck, or engineering, or my father's ghost leaning on the geometry. I didn't care which.

We swam for it. The current fought us the whole way, wrapped a fallen beam across my shin, tried to fold me under twice. Callum's grip never loosened. Once I felt him go slack for half a second—something striking him, a stone, a log—and I clawed at his shirt and screamed his name into the water and he came back, coughing, swearing, climbing.

And then we were through. Out. Spat onto the rocks below the ridge like the river spitting out something it had no use for.

I lay on my back on the wet granite and the rain hit my face and I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out of me in great ugly whoops, half sob, and Callum collapsed beside me doing the same, both of us flat on the stone like two things the flood had examined and found insufficiently interesting to keep.

Behind us, the Rivergate finished dying.

It took perhaps two minutes. We couldn't see it—we were below the bluff line, in the spray shadow—but we heard all of it: the long grinding surrender of the upper galleries, the boom of the stairwell sealing itself into rubble, and then, underneath everything, a vast exhalation as the mountain of water found the new channels we had given it and poured itself into the tomb. When it was done, the sound settled into ordinary river-noise, and that was somehow the most terrible part. It sounded like nothing had ever been there at all.

"It's done," I said. My voice was shredded. "It's done, it's done."

Callum rolled toward me. Rain ran off his jaw. There was blood in his hairline and he didn't seem to know it. "You're hurt," he said.

"So are you."

"I know." He touched my face, briefly, as if checking I was real. "I followed you anyway. I want you to know I'd do it again, and I'm sorry, and I'm not sorry."

"I know," I said. "Me too. All of it."

Above us, on the ridge, someone was shouting our names.

* * *

Priya reached us first, sliding down the scree with a med kit held over her head like an offering, Sefu limping behind her with his bandaged arm cradled and his face gray but upright. She went to work on us without a word—cutting Callum's shirt away, pressing on the gash at his temple, checking my pupils with a penlight against the rain. Her hands shook. I don't think she knew that either.

"The lower camp's gone," Sefu said. "Voss's boats too, most of them. Everything below the forty-meter line is underwater or moving. They've pulled up to the east shelf."

"How many?" Callum asked.

"All of them, far as we can tell. Nobody left behind. They're—" He hesitated, glancing at me. "They're not coming after you. I watched. Voss called them off the ridge twenty minutes ago. Before the charges even blew."

I sat up too fast and Priya pushed me back down with two fingers, gentle and absolute. "Twenty minutes ago," I repeated.

"Yeah. He just—stood at the edge looking down at the water. Then he told his men to break camp."

Odile appeared last, coming down the slope with the unhurried tread of a woman who had spent her life letting rivers make decisions for her. She looked at the two of us sprawled on the rock, at the wall of spray where a temple used to be, and she said nothing for a long moment. Then she crouched, took my hand, and pressed something into it: her old brass compass, worn smooth, the lid dented from a decade of boat decks.

"For the crossing home," she said. "You'll want to steer yourself for once."

"Odile—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked, just once, and she mastered it. "I copied the notes. I nearly killed us all for a debt that was never mine to pay. You let me stay aboard anyway. That's more mercy than I earned, and I'll carry it however you let me. But today—" she nodded at the flood, at the gray curtain where the Rivergate slept forever now, "—today you did the thing nobody else had the spine for. So take the compass, Marguerite Winterbourne, and let an old woman give you one true gift."

I closed my fingers around it. It was warm from her pocket. "Thank you," I said, and meant every buried thing beneath it.

She squeezed my hand once, hard, and stood, and became again the gruff boatman who complained about coffee and read water better than any living soul.

* * *

We saw Voss's expedition from the ridge at dusk.

They were breaking the east shelf camp in the rain, methodical, disciplined—crates lashed, tarps folded, the remaining boats loaded with the economy of men who had done this a hundred times in worse places. Through Priya's field glasses I picked out figures: the broad-shouldered man who'd taken a bullet near the explosives cache and lived; the young one who hadn't; a body wrapped in canvas, laid with a kind of rough honor in the bow of the second boat. No clean hands. None of ours either. Sefu slept drugged and breathing in the cave behind us, and I counted that as the day's only unambiguous victory.

Edmund Voss stood apart from all of it, at the lip of the shelf, facing the water.

Even at this distance I could read the set of his shoulders. He wasn't watching his men. He was watching the flood line creep up the bluff—the reservoir-to-be, the dam valley filling ahead of schedule, burying everything we had spent the season bleeding over. He stood there a long time. Then he raised one hand—not a wave, not quite a salute—and brought it slowly down, the way a man closes a ledger.

"He's not going to pursue," Callum said beside me. He'd borrowed the glasses. "Look at the boats. They're pointed downstream."

"He has nothing left to pursue us *for*." I said it and heard how hollow it sounded, because it wasn't true and we both knew it. Voss still held Callum's sister's file. Still held whatever leverage had bought him a cartographer's silence for seven years. A ruined temple didn't erase a debt; men like Edmund Voss collected on paper, not stone.

But the boats pointed downstream, and no order came, and when the lead vessel swung out into the current I saw Voss finally turn away from the water and step aboard without looking back at the ridge, at us, at any of it.

"They're leaving," Priya said softly. "Actually leaving."

"Let them," I said.

I thought of him in the temple—I'd learned enough from the aftermath, from the shape of the sabotage, from the way his men had hesitated—to know he had followed me down. Callum had told me on the ridge, in fragments: *he came down after you, Marguerite. He stood in front of those tablets with his gun drawn and he couldn't do it. I saw his face.* I hadn't asked what the face looked like. I hadn't needed to. I had stood in that same drowned hall and felt the same thing crack open in my chest, and if it could happen to me, his father's rival's daughter, then it had happened to Edmund Voss decades ago and he had simply spent thirty years refusing to say so.

Some debts die with the men who hold them. Maybe some die with the temples too.

The last boat vanished around the bend as full dark came down, and the rain eased for the first time in six days, and the river below us ran brown and swollen and utterly indifferent, carrying its secret deeper than any map would ever reach again.

* * *

That night we crowded into the cave shelter with a single burner flame and Sefu's fever breaking at last, and Priya doling out the last of the good tea like a woman administering sacrament. Odile sang—quietly, half under her breath, some delta work song about a woman who married the flood and lived to tell it—and Callum sat against the wall with his arm around me, his heartbeat slow and astonishing under my ear, alive, alive, alive.

"We should talk about downstream," he murmured eventually. "Supplies. What's left. Where we even go."

"Tomorrow," I said.

"There may not be much tomorrow gives us."

"Then it can wait until the day after." I tilted my head back to look at him. In the burner light his face was exhausted and bruised and more familiar to me than my own handwriting. "Callum. The temple's gone. Voss is gone. Whatever comes next, it comes to people who are still breathing, and I intend to spend one entire night being one of them before I plan anything ever again."

His mouth curved. "One night," he agreed. "Then logistics."

"Then logistics."

Outside, the rain had stopped altogether. I could hear the river working in the dark, patient and enormous, doing what rivers do—carrying away, covering over, keeping what matters. Somewhere beneath that brown water, past the collapsed stair and the sealed galleries, my father rested at his desk among the tablets of a people who had chosen the flood, and the flood had chosen them back, and I had finally, finally kept the promise neither of us had known he was asking for.

*Let it rest,* his last letter said. I had. God help me, I had.

I was almost asleep when Sefu stirred by the fire and said, in a voice still thick with fever, "There's a boat coming upriver."

I opened my eyes.

Far out on the black water, small and steady against the current, a single running light was climbing the gorge toward us—too small for Voss's expedition, too deliberate for driftwood luck, and moving, in this drowned and emptied valley, directly toward the only fire on the ridge.
