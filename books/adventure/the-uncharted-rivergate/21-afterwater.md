## 21. Afterwater

The river gave us back slowly, the way it took everything else.

Three days downstream of the ridge, where the valley opened its throat and let the flood spread thin across a hundred braided channels, we made camp on a sandbar that would not exist in another week. Odile called it a *banc de passage*—a crossing place, a thing the river built and unbuilt at will. It seemed fitting. Everything we had was temporary now: the shelter of tarps strung between poles, the fire pit lined with stones we'd carried from higher ground, the small inventory of what remained.

I kept the list in my head because writing it down felt too much like an epitaph. Two boats, one swamped and beyond repair. Half a drum of fuel, waterlogged but usable if Sefu could coax it dry. Priya's medical kit, diminished by everything she'd used on Sefu's shoulder and the cuts and the fever that followed. Fourteen tins of food, a case of condensed milk, Odile's coffee, which she guarded like scripture. And six people who had gone into the monsoon country as two expeditions and come out as something harder to name.

Marguerite slept most of the first day. I watched her breathe under a mosquito net and counted her breaths the way I'd once counted soundings on a chart—one after another after another, until the numbers themselves became a kind of prayer. She had come out of the Rivergate with river water in her lungs and clay dust in her hair, and for one terrible hour on the ridge I'd thought the mountain was going to keep her. Then Priya had worked, and Marguerite had coughed, and the world had resumed its imperfect turning.

Now she slept, and I sat at the edge of camp with my ruined survey case open on my knees, doing the quiet arithmetic of what I'd done.

The original map was gone. I'd fed it to the fire on the second night, page by page, while Voss stood watching with his hands clasped behind his back like a man at a graveside. He hadn't stopped me. He hadn't even spoken until the last sheet caught, and then he'd said only, "That was ten years of your life," and I'd said, "It was ten years of hers," and neither of us had meant the same woman, and both of us had been right.

* * *

Voss came to me on the third evening, at the hour when the light goes bronze and the insects begin their shift change. He moved through our camp the way he moved everywhere—deliberately, unhurried, a man who had learned that urgency was for people without resources. His expedition had lost a man in the firefight; they'd buried him that morning on the slope above the waterline, and the raw earth showed brown against the green like a wound. Voss had spoken the words himself. Whatever else he was, he did not leave his dead to strangers.

He lowered himself onto the log beside me without asking, which was its own kind of message. We watched the river run gold and black.

"Your sister," he said.

My whole body went still. Somewhere behind us, Sefu was arguing gently with Priya about changing his own dressing, and Odile was singing something low and tuneless over the fire, and none of it touched the cold space that had just opened in my chest.

"Lena," I said. Her name came out rough, unused aloud for years. In my head she was always nineteen, always waiting at a bus stop in a city I couldn't reach, always paying for a brother's ambition.

"I've carried her file for eleven years," Voss said. "Customs fraud, falsified hydrological certifications, a pattern of forged permits that would have put her in front of a tribunal if anyone had ever pulled the thread. She did it to fund your education. You knew that part."

"I knew that part."

"You didn't know how deep it went." He turned and looked at me, and his eyes were tired in a way I hadn't seen before—not the performance of weariness he wore like a coat, but the real thing underneath. "You didn't know because I made sure you never looked. That was the debt. Not money. Knowledge. I held the knowledge, and you mapped for me, and every year the map got better and the file stayed in my safe."

The river filled the silence. Downstream, somewhere out of sight, the dam crews would be running their surveys now, marking the basin boundaries, measuring the water that would bury everything we'd left behind. The thought should have hurt. Instead it felt like setting down weight.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked.

Voss reached into his jacket and produced a folder—thin, manila, soft-cornered from years of handling. He set it on my knee the way you'd set down something breakable.

"Because some debts die with the men who hold them," he said. "And I'm done holding men."

I stared at him. "You're not dying."

"No." He almost smiled. "But I've spent three weeks watching a woman drown her father's life's work to keep it safe, and I've stood in a temple full of five thousand years of human stubbornness and found I couldn't give the order to take it. Something in me has been reorganized, Reyes. I don't expect you to understand it. I barely do." He stood, brushed off his trousers, and paused. "The file is complete. There's nothing in it I've withheld. Burn it or use it—I don't care anymore. Lena Winterbourne is no longer collateral. She never should have been."

"She's not a Winterbourne," I said automatically, and then heard myself, and Voss laughed—a short, genuine bark of surprise.

"No," he agreed. "She isn't. God help her, she's a Reyes." He walked three steps toward his own camp, then stopped. "For what it's worth—and I know it isn't worth much coming from me—your map was the finest piece of work I ever commissioned. I'm sorry I made you pay for it twice."

Then he was gone, into the dusk and the smell of woodsmoke, and I sat holding my sister's freedom in a folder lighter than a Bible.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I read the file by lamplight instead, every page, every forged signature in Lena's careful hand, every date and amount and lie. It was worse than I'd imagined and smaller than I'd feared—the whole architecture of my servitude reduced to forty-one sheets of paper. Eleven years. Two continents. A decade of maps drawn by a man who told himself he had no choice, when all along the choice had been sitting in a safe in a house in the capital, waiting for someone brave enough to ask about it.

At dawn I burned it. All of it, page by page, feeding the fire while the camp woke around me. Marguerite came and stood beside me without speaking, her shoulder warm against mine, and when the last corner curled to ash she took my hand and held it, and neither of us needed to say what we were burning or why.

Odile found us there. She stood at the edge of the fire ring with her arms crossed, looking older than she had a month ago, the way stone looks older after rain.

"The boats are ready," she said. "Sefu coaxed the fuel dry. We can make the lower station in two days if the weather holds." She hesitated, and I saw her jaw work through whatever came next. "I'm not coming."

Marguerite turned. "Odile—"

"Don't." The boatman's voice cracked like a rope under strain. "Don't forgive me again. I copied the notes. I led them to the marker. Men died because of what I carried in my pocket, and you forgave me because you're better than the rest of us, and forgiveness is easy when you're not the one who has to live with it." She looked at the fire, at the ash of Lena's file still ghosting up in gray spirals. "Voss offered me work. Real work, honest terms, my brother's debts cleared in full—his actual debts, the money ones, not the kind you carry in a folder. I said yes."

"You don't owe him anything," I said.

"I owe everybody everything," Odile said simply. "That's the problem. And I can't sit in a boat with people whose trust I broke and call it a new start. I have to go where the breaking happened and fix it from there." She extended her hand to Marguerite, formal as a treaty. Marguerite took it, held it, and pulled her into an embrace that lasted long enough for Odile's eyes to shine and spill.

"You saved my life twice," Marguerite said into her shoulder. "That's also true. Both things are true."

"That's the worst part," Odile said. "Both things are always true."

She left at midmorning, poling Voss's lead boat upstream with two of his men, her back straight, her face forward. She did not look back, and I understood that not looking back was the hardest work she'd ever done.

* * *

We went downstream the next day, the three of us and what remained of our gear, through country already transforming. The tributaries ran high and brown, carrying the monsoon's first great delivery toward the lowlands. Birds we hadn't seen in weeks returned to the flooded margins. The world was closing the wound we'd traveled through, sealing it with water and green.

On the second afternoon we passed the mouth of the gorge where the Rivergate slept. The water there was deeper than any sounding I'd ever taken, flat and dark and utterly silent, and above it the cliffs rose sheer and dripping. There was nothing to see. That was the point. Somewhere beneath that patient surface lay stairways and halls and a desk of clay tablets, and a skeleton who had finally been forgiven, and all of it pressed under fifty fathoms of permanent dark.

Marguerite stood at the bow for a long time. When she turned back to me her eyes were clear.

"It's done," she said, and it wasn't grief in her voice, or triumph. It was something quieter than either. Rest, maybe. "Let's go home."

Home. The word sat strangely in my mouth when I tried it. I had a rented room in a port city, a sister I hadn't seen in eleven years, and a profession I'd practiced under duress. But then I looked at Marguerite folding charts in the stern—her charts now, drawn fresh, of rivers nobody had paid us to map—and I thought: home is not a place yet. Home is a heading.

That night, moored at the lower station with the lamps of the settlement glowing across the water, I dug out the last item in my case: a letter I'd written in the cave on the ridge, during the worst hours, when I'd believed we might not all come down alive. I had addressed it to Lena. I had never sent it, because I'd written it as a goodbye.

I read it once by lamplight, then turned it over and wrote on the back, in the clean sure lines of a man drawing a new chart:

*I'm coming north. Not to be forgiven—to start over. Bring the bus schedule; I'll bring the maps.*

In the morning there would be mail to send, wages to divide, a government office to visit about the dam records, and a long journey north to a woman who had waited eleven years for a brother to stop paying for his choices and start making them. But tonight the river ran quiet past the station, carrying the season's rain toward the sea, and for the first time in as long as I could remember, every debt I carried was one I'd chosen myself.

Marguerite appeared at the cabin door, hair damp from the evening wash, and held out a tin cup of Odile's coffee, brewed from the last of the hoard.

"To the uncharted," she said.

I clinked my cup against hers. "To what the river keeps."

We drank. Downstream, far away, the reservoir gates waited to close, and the maps would mark nothing where the temple slept—and that was exactly as it should be. Some things are not lost when the water takes them. Some things are only finally, mercifully, out of reach.
