## 1. The Weight of Fair Weather

The supercell had been building over the flats since noon, stacking itself up like a bad idea, and Zephyrine Iversen loved it with her whole entire heart.

"Come on, sweetheart," she murmured to it through the windshield, one hand on the wheel of the truck, the other holding a coffee that had gone cold two counties back. "Show me what you've got."

What it had was a wall cloud rotating with the lazy confidence of something that knew exactly how beautiful it was. The kind of rotation that made weather services nervous and made storm-chasers like Zephyrine—well, not rich, because nothing made Zephyrine rich anymore, but *employed*. Dr. Ansel Voss at Corvid State University paid four hundred dollars for verified mesocyclone data with photographic documentation, and four hundred dollars was four hundred dollars, which was to say it was roughly one-fortieth of what she owed the Optimists.

She pulled off onto the gravel shoulder where the flats went on forever in every direction, wheat stubble and fence lines and a sky doing something obscene above it all. Out came the camera, out came the anemometer, out came the little ritual she'd performed so many times it had worn grooves in her: tripod, level, shoot the base, log the wind, note the time.

"Rotation confirmed," she said into her recorder, because talking to herself was free and therapists were not. "Wall cloud tightening, inflow strong from the southeast, and I am going to say—yes, yes, there it is—" A finger of dust rose off the fields beneath the cloud's belly, curling, reaching, deciding whether to become something. "Possible funnel development. God, you're gorgeous. Absolutely no regard for my insurance premiums."

The funnel didn't drop. It thought about it, teased the horizon for twenty minutes, then sucked itself back up into the mother cloud like a cat declining a lap. Typical. But the data was good, the photos were better, and Voss would pay, so Zephyrine packed up her gear feeling the closest thing to victorious that her current life allowed.

Which lasted approximately until she got back to the truck and saw the man sitting in the passenger seat.

He'd gotten in somehow—the lock was supposed to be decent—and he sat there with his hands folded on his knee like a parishioner waiting for the sermon to start, wearing a pale yellow jacket that belonged at a garden party and absolutely did not belong in a storm-chaser's cab with its floor of tangled cables and its dashboard shrine of expired parking permits. He was smiling. That was the worst part. Nobody smiled like that unless they were being paid to.

Zephyrine stood outside her own door for a moment, weighing her options. She could walk away. She could walk away very fast, in fact, except the truck was her home, her office, and her only appreciable asset, and the man inside it was almost certainly aware of all three.

She opened the door. "You're sitting on my emergency chocolate."

"I don't believe I am," said the man pleasantly, lifting slightly so she could see the melted bar wedged under his thigh. "Ah. So I am. My apologies." He did not move off it. He extended a hand instead. "Merritt Fenn. Optimist Mutual Assurance. I'm your friendly neighborhood account representative."

"The Optimists don't have neighborhoods," Zephyrine said, climbing in and starting the engine just to have something to do with her hands. "They have territories."

"And you're in mine." He settled deeper into the seat as she pulled onto the road, radiating the contentment of a man whose expenses were someone else's problem. "I watched you chase that storm, by the way. From the ridge back there. Remarkable hobby. Most people run *from* dangerous weather. You drive toward it. There's a metaphor in there somewhere, but honestly, I'm more of a numbers man."

"What do you want, Fenn?"

"Straight to business! I respect that." He produced a folder from inside his jacket—pale yellow, naturally, everything about him was pale yellow, like a canary that had gone into finance—and laid it on her dashboard with two fingers, gently, the way you'd set down a sleeping infant or a live grenade. "Your balance, as of this morning. Would you like me to read it aloud? Some clients find it clarifying."

"No one finds it clarifying."

"Then let's skip to the good part." He tapped the folder. "Three weeks, Miss Iversen. In three weeks, your account enters what we call the Sunshine Phase, which is our term for when we stop being cheerful and start being thorough. At that point we collect collateral." His smile widened by a precise, professional millimeter. "And per your loan agreement—which you signed, initialing page nine specifically, though nobody ever reads page nine—the collateral is the vehicle you are currently driving."

Zephyrine kept her eyes on the road. The flats unspooled ahead, indifferent and golden, and the supercell was already shrinking behind them into something picturesque and harmless, the way disasters always looked afterward.

"You know what's funny," she said. "I chased a tornado today. Actual rotating column of destruction, half a mile wide, capable of flinging my truck into the next county. And it was less frightening than you, and I want you to know that's not a compliment to you. It's a compliment to the tornado."

"I'm going to take it as one anyway." Fenn finally shifted off the chocolate, which she noted he did not return to her, but tucked into his own jacket pocket. "Look. I'll be honest with you, because frankly, you seem like a woman who appreciates honesty even when it's ugly. You're not going to make this balance chasing storms. Four hundred dollars a job? I ran your numbers before I drove out here. Even in a record season—even if the sky throws hailstones the size of turkeys—you clear maybe six thousand by first snow. Your interest alone eats five of that." He shook his head with genuine, almost tender regret. "You're bailing a boat with a teaspoon, Miss Iversen. And the boat has opinions."

"Thank you for that vivid image."

"I'm here to help." He meant it, too—that was the horrible genius of the Optimists. They weren't thugs in dark cars. They were *cheerful*. They sent you birthday cards with your payment reminders. They called you by your first name. They genuinely, sincerely believed that debt was just another form of friendship, and friendship was just another form of leverage. "So here's what I can offer. Not today—I'm not authorized for offers, I'm authorized for *reminders*—but if you came into some money, some real money, in the next three weeks, I could make certain arrangements. Flexible terms. A fresh start." He turned in his seat to look at her fully, and for just a second the garden-party warmth flickered and she saw the machinery underneath, the cold little gears turning behind the smile. "People come into money in strange ways out here. Inheritances. Lucky strikes. Odd jobs for odd people. If that happened—if you found yourself suddenly liquid—I'd want to be the first call you make. Before you spend a dime of it anywhere else."

"That's a strangely specific thing to hope for."

"I'm a strangely specific man." He gathered his folder, patted his jacket where the chocolate now lived, and gestured grandly at a crossroads coming up. "You can let me out here. I've got a car. Well—technically it's the bank's car, but everything's the bank's, isn't it? That's rather the whole philosophy."

Zephyrine stopped at the crossroads. Fenn opened the door, then paused, one polished shoe on the gravel, and looked back at her with that unbearable benevolence.

"One last thing, free of charge. Professional observation." He nodded toward the western horizon, where the mountains had begun their evening performance, catching the light and hoarding it. "You know what I see when I look at those? Opportunity. All that country up there nobody's properly surveyed. All those old claims, old maps, old men who know where things are buried." His eyes came back to hers, mild as milk. "Desperate people head for the hills, Miss Iversen. It's practically a law of nature. And the Optimists—we never stand in the way of nature. We just like to know which trail you took."

"Goodbye, Fenn."

"Three weeks!" he called cheerfully, stepping down and closing the door with a soft, courteous click. "Do give my regards to the mountains!"

She watched him stroll to a pale yellow sedan parked discreetly behind the windbreak, get in, and drive off east without once looking back, because men like Fenn never needed to look back. They already knew where you were going. That was the product they sold: certainty, purchased with other people's desperation.

Zephyrine sat at the crossroads for a long moment, engine idling, hands at ten and two.

Then she opened the glove box, dug past the expired permits and the spare fuses and the photograph of her parents she kept face-down because looking at it too often wore it out, and pulled out the envelope where she kept her actual finances. She counted it twice, hoping arithmetic had changed while she wasn't watching.

It hadn't. Two hundred and eleven dollars, and a debt that would take eleven years of perfect weather to outrun.

"Three weeks," she told the windshield.

The mountains said nothing. Mountains were like that. They just stood there being enormous and full of secrets, letting the flatlands do all the talking, and Zephyrine had spent thirty-one years telling herself she was a flatlands person—a storm person, a horizon person—because the mountains had taken enough from her family already.

She put the truck in gear and drove toward the last gas station before the foothills, because it had cheap coffee, a bathroom, and the only phone signal for forty miles, and because a person who owed the Optimists eleven years of her life might as well start thinking about odd jobs for odd people.

The station's sign buzzed against the dusk: GAS — DIESEL — ICE — WE BUY SCRAP. Beneath it, propped against the ice machine like a man who'd been standing there long enough to become part of the inventory, was an old prospector in a coat older than the station, coughing like his lungs were trying to leave without him.

He looked up as she parked. Looked at the truck—mud-caked, antenna-bristling, dented with character. Looked at her. And then he smiled, and it was nothing like Fenn's smile; it was crooked and tired and real, and somehow that was far more alarming.

"You the one been chasin' that storm?" he called, voice like gravel sliding downhill.

"Depends who's asking."

"Name's Halvard." He pushed off the ice machine, swayed, caught himself, and grinned through the cough. "And I've got a proposition for you, miss. One that pays better than weather."

* * *

Inside, under the buzzing fluorescents, Zephyrine poured herself the station's terrible coffee and tried very hard not to look interested, because looking interested was how you got robbed.

Halvard sat across the little counter from her, hands wrapped around a cup he hadn't drunk from, and told her what he wanted in three sentences: "I need to go upriver. High country. Past the closed pass. Before first snow."

"That's not a destination," Zephyrine said. "That's a death wish with directions."

"It's both." He coughed, wet and deep, and when he surfaced his eyes were bright and feverish and utterly steady. "I'll pay five hundred. Cash. Now, for the drive."

"Five hundred won't get either of us past the first roadblock. That pass has been closed for thirty years. There's signage, there's sensors, there's probably bears with government clearance."

"Seven fifty."

"Halvard—"

"A thousand." He leaned forward, and up close she could see how bad he really was—the gray in his lips, the tremor in his hands, the way his breath rattled like a truck on a washboard road. Whatever was killing him was in a hurry. "A thousand dollars, cash, tonight. And I'll tell you the rest when we're on the road, because the rest is the part that makes it worth ten times that, and I'm too tired to argue with a stranger about it in a gas station."

Zephyrine looked at the coffee. She looked at the old man. She did the math she always did—the debt, the deadline, Fenn's cheerful gears grinding somewhere out east—and felt greed and instinct arm-wrestle somewhere behind her ribs, and hated that greed was winning on points.

"Everybody who wants to go up that river wants something," she said slowly. "Gold. Revenge. A grave. Which one are you?"

Halvard smiled again, that crooked, dying, honest smile, and said:

"All three, miss. But not in that order."
