## 2. A Stranger at the Last Gas Station

The last gas station before the high country was called the Thirsty Marmot, which was optimistic on two counts: the marmots up there were not thirsty so much as perpetually damp, and the station itself had not sold anything as lively as thirst in a decade. It sat at the elbow where the paved road gave up and became a rumor of gravel, its pumps sun-bleached to the color of old bone, its single window displaying a museum of expired lottery tickets and one rubber boot, size thirteen, that Zephyrine suspected had been there since before she was born.

She pulled in on fumes and stubbornness. The storm-chasing fee from the flats—three hundred dollars for sitting in a truck for eleven hours while a supercell decided whether or not to be interesting, and then deciding not to be—had gone straight into the fuel tank and a bag of jerky that cost more per ounce than silver. That was the arithmetic of her life lately. Everything earned went into the hole, and the hole was shaped exactly like the word *Optimists*.

The Optimists. Even now, thinking the name made her jaw tighten. They'd started as some kind of community lending circle, all sunshine-yellow logos and slogans about brighter tomorrows, and somewhere along the way "brighter tomorrow" had come to mean *we will find you*, and their collectors smiled the whole time they did it. You could owe a bank and hate it cleanly. Owing the Optimists was like owing a birthday party.

Zephyrine stepped out of the truck, stretched until something in her back went off like a distant firework, and went inside to pay for gas she couldn't afford with money she didn't have.

The man behind the counter—Dell, according to the name tag that had outlived three owners of the station—nodded at her without looking up from his crossword.

"Storm's done," he said.

"Storm's done."

"Any good?"

"It stood still over Miller's Flats for six hours and then apologized and left." She laid bills on the counter. "Forty on pump two."

"That the one with the lightning that hit the grain elevator?"

"That was Tuesday. Different storm. This one just sulked."

Dell grunted, which from Dell was a full conversation, and Zephyrine turned to go—and that was when she saw him.

He was sitting at the little formica table by the window that passed for the Thirsty Marmot's café, hunched over a cup of coffee like a man sheltering under it. Old—not old the way Dell was old, comfortably, but old the way weathered rope is old, strong-looking and one bad strain from finished. His coat was canvas gone soft as cloth from years of rain, patched at both elbows with what looked suspiciously like duct tape applied by someone who had run out of patience for sewing. A prospector's pack leaned against his chair leg, scuffed brass buckles, the kind of pack that had been carried so long it had learned the shape of a spine.

And he was coughing. Not politely. This was a cough with ambitions, a cough that intended to go somewhere, and when it finally released him he sat there breathing in small careful sips, the way you drink soup too hot.

Zephyrine would have walked past him. Honest to God, she would have. But he looked up as she passed, and his eyes caught her the way a hook catches a sleeve—she felt it snag and held, and cursed herself for holding.

"You're the storm woman," he said. His voice was gravel that had been rained on.

"I'm nobody's anything," she said. "I chase weather for money. It doesn't make me a woman of storms. It makes me broke."

"The white truck. Antenna on the roof like a porcupine fell in love with it." He almost smiled. Almost. The cough interrupted the attempt, and she found herself standing there like an idiot, waiting for him to finish surviving it before she left.

"You've been watching me?"

"I've been waiting," he said. "Watching was incidental. You were the only vehicle that came through going the right direction today."

"And what direction is that?"

"Up." He said it simply, the way other people said *home* or *sorry*. He nodded at the window, past the rubber boot, toward the mountains stacked against the sky like furniture under a sheet—blue-gray shapes getting less blue and more gray as the light died. "Into the high country. Before first snow."

Zephyrine laughed. It came out shorter than she intended. "Mister, do you know what season it is?"

"I know precisely what season it is. That's rather the point."

"It's thaw season turning to melt season turning to *maybe snow next week if the ridge gets moody*. The passes are soup. Half the goat tracks are underwater and the other half are vertical. There is no driving into the high country right now. There's drowning into the high country, slowly, in a truck."

"So you've done it before."

"I've done stupid things before, yes. I have a talent for it. It's why I'm broke."

"How broke?" he asked, and there was nothing cruel in it. Just direct. Prospectors, she thought, probably didn't have time for the polite fiction of not asking.

"Broke enough," she said, "that I chased a storm today for three hundred dollars, and I'd have done it for two-fifty, and we both know that's a terrible thing to admit out loud in public."

Something moved across his face—not pity. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man who had been broke in the same specific flavor and remembered the taste.

"My name is Halvard," he said.

"Congratulations."

"Zephyrine Iversen," he said, and she stopped moving entirely.

Because she hadn't told him that. She was sure—absolutely sure—she hadn't told him that. Her truck was unmarked. Dell knew her only as "the storm girl." Nobody in this county called her anything but Iversen or *hey, you*, and half of them got even that wrong.

"How—" she started.

"Sit down, Zephyrine Iversen," said Halvard, and patted the bench across from him with a hand that trembled slightly, though she got the feeling the trembling was exhaustion, not fear. "I'll buy you a coffee. It's terrible coffee. It's the worst coffee between here and the coast, possibly the worst coffee in the hemisphere, but it comes with information, and information is the only thing worse than this coffee that anyone's offering today."

Against every instinct she owned—which, granted, were mostly instincts for trouble wearing disguises—she sat down.

* * *

Dell brought two coffees without being asked, which meant either he'd been eavesdropping or the machine had simply given up on its own and this was a mercy killing. The coffee was the color of a flooded creek and tasted approximately like it too. Zephyrine drank it anyway, because free was free, and wrapped her hands around the cup for the warmth.

Halvard watched her over the rim of his own cup with those hook-and-sleeve eyes. Up close she could see the damage better—the gray in his lips, the way his breath whistled faintly at the top of each inhale, like wind through a cracked window. Whatever was wrong with him wasn't a cold. It was a tenant.

"You know my name," she said. "That's a debt. Start paying it."

"Fair terms." He set down the cup. "I knew your grandfather. Soren Iversen."

The name landed in her chest like a dropped tool. She kept her face still, mostly because she'd had practice—people mentioned her family sometimes, usually carefully, the way you mention a bridge that fell down. Surveyors. Both sides of the family, going back generations, people who drew lines on maps for a living and vanished doing it. Her parents' expedition had gone upriver when she was nine, and the official story had been so smooth and so empty that she'd stopped asking questions around age twelve, when she realized the questions themselves made officials look at you funny.

"Soren Iversen has been dead longer than I've been able to vote," she said.

"Yes. I was at his funeral. You were there too. Small. Furious. Bit a man who tried to pick you up."

"That tracks," she admitted. "I bit people well into my teens."

"He talked about you. After your parents—" Halvard paused, chose his footing on the word the way you choose footing on scree. "After the expedition. He talked about you the way men talk about lighthouses. Something steady to steer by, far away, that you'll never reach but keep looking for anyway."

Zephyrine stared into her coffee. The surface reflected the fluorescent light back at her in a way that made it look like it was glowing, which was the most flattering thing the coffee had done all day.

"That's very poetic for a man who looks like he eats rocks," she said. "What do you want, Halvard?"

"A ride."

"Where?"

"Up." He nodded at the mountains again. "To the source of the Sorrowline."

She laughed again, and this time it was real laughter, startled out of her. "Nobody goes to the source of the Sorrowline. The source of the Sorrowline isn't *on maps*. I've checked. I check everything—it's a compulsion. Every survey, every atlas, every government chart, the river just... starts. Mid-valley. Like it crawled out from under a rock and decided to be a river. No headwaters marked. No tributaries above the flood line. Nothing."

"That's because the surveys were corrected," Halvard said.

"Corrected."

"Erased. Pick your word. The government picked theirs; they used a nice neutral term in an internal memo I once read, something about 'cartographic simplification.' Which is a lovely phrase for burning a valley off the face of every map in the country." He said it flatly, factually, the way you'd report the price of feed, and somehow that made it worse.

Zephyrine set down her cup. "You're either crazy or you're telling me something I've spent twenty years suspecting, and I genuinely don't know which option I like less."

"Both can be true. Usually are." He leaned forward, and the chair creaked under him like a ship. "I need to get to the headwaters before first snow. I don't have a vehicle. I don't have much time—no, don't give me the look, I mean it medically, my doctor gave me a number and the number is smaller than the distance to where I'm going, so I'm spending what's left on a driver with a good truck and a bad sense of self-preservation. You came up in my research as both."

"Your research."

"I've been planning this for two years. You, specifically. The truck, the routes you run, the fact that you take jobs nobody else will take because you need the money and you tell yourself you don't care where they lead." He said it without apology, and she hated how accurately he'd read her, and hated more that he'd clearly read her from a distance, patiently, the way you watch a river to learn where it floods.

"What's the job?" she asked. "Exactly. What am I driving you into?"

"I can't say."

"You can't say."

"Not yet. Not first. Ask me anything else."

"Anything else," she repeated. "Fine. What's it pay?"

Halvard reached into his coat—slowly, telegraphing it, which she appreciated; she'd already catalogued three exits from the room—and set something on the table between them.

It was a roll of bills, folded flat, held with a rubber band. Old bills. She didn't touch it, but her eyes did the math automatically, the way they always did, the curse of being broke: five hundreds, maybe eight, creased soft as cloth.

"Five thousand," Halvard said. "Half now, half at the summit. Drive me to the source of the Sorrowline before first snow, wait while I do what I have to do there—no more than a day, maybe two—and drive me back down alive, or at least deliverable. Five thousand dollars."

Five thousand dollars. The number went through her like current through wet rope. Five thousand dollars was the Optimists paid off with change left over. Five thousand dollars was the truck's transmission fixed, the rent caught up, the particular low-grade nausea of debt replaced by the unfamiliar sensation of *enough*. Five thousand dollars was—

Was a stranger who knew her name and her grandfather and wouldn't say what he wanted, driving her into erased country, in melt season, with a body that sounded like it was filing for bankruptcy from the inside.

"No," Zephyrine said.

Halvard blinked. For the first time, the composure slipped, just a hairline crack, and she saw underneath it: not anger. Fear. Real fear, the kind that had been living in him so long it had gotten comfortable.

"No?"

"No. I don't drive blind men into closed valleys for cash, even excellent cash. Especially excellent cash—that's how people end up in ditches. And I especially don't do it for someone who knows things about my family and won't say how." She stood up. "Find another truck."

"There is no other truck," Halvard said quietly. "There's you, and there's the weather, and the weather is winning."

"Then lose gracefully."

She got four steps toward the door. Four steps, boots on linoleum, the bell over the door within reach. Then the cough took him—worse than before, a long tearing fit that bent him double over the table, and Dell actually came around the counter with a glass of water, and Zephyrine stood with her hand on the door and her back to all of it and discovered that her legs had opinions of their own.

When the fit passed, Halvard was gray as ash and gripping the table edge, and he looked up at her with eyes that were wet and furious and, worst of all, amused.

"Four steps," he rasped. "That's further than most people get."

"Don't flatter yourself. I'm leaving because it's sensible."

"You're leaving because it's sensible," he agreed, "and you've been standing at that door for thirty seconds deciding whether sensible is worth it." He fumbled at his coat pocket again, and this time what came out was not money. It was a photograph, bent soft at the corners, and he laid it on the formica between the two cups of terrible coffee.

Zephyrine told herself not to look. She looked.

Two figures on a ridge, decades ago by the film stock, arms slung over each other's shoulders, squinting into sun. A young man with her grandfather's exact stubborn jaw. Beside him, a woman laughing, dark-haired, with a surveyor's transit case at her feet—and Zephyrine knew that laugh, knew it from a handful of photographs in a shoebox she still kept at the bottom of a drawer, knew it from the mirror some mornings when she wasn't careful.

Her mother. Her mother, young and laughing, standing next to a young Halvard on a ridge above a valley that was green in the photograph and drowned on every modern map.

"First snow is coming early this year," Halvard said softly. "I can feel it in my hands. I've got maybe ten days, twelve if the ridge is kind. Ten days, Zephyrine Iversen, and one truck in the world that might make it, and one driver." He pushed the roll of bills across the table after the photograph. "Ten thousand. Half now, half at the summit. And I swear to you on your grandfather's grave—on the actual dirt of it, I've been there—that when we reach the source, you'll understand everything. Why the maps lie. What your parents found. What I did, and what I failed to do, and why I have to put it back before I stop."

The bell over the door jingled in a draft. Outside, the light was failing, and the mountains had gone the color of iron, and somewhere up in all that gray the first snow was already deciding.

Ten thousand dollars. Her whole body leaned toward it like a plant toward a window. And every instinct she owned—the ones for trouble, the ones for survival, the ones she'd never managed to tell apart—stood up inside her and started arguing loudly.

Greed said *take it*. Instinct said *run*. And underneath both, quiet as groundwater, something older than either of them said: *you were always going up that river. You just didn't know what the fare would be.*

Zephyrine looked at the photograph a long moment. Then she sat back down, picked up the worst coffee in the hemisphere, and drank it like a woman signing something.

"Terms," she said. "Over breakfast. And if you're lying to me about any of it, Halvard, I will leave you at the first crossroads, and I won't feel bad, and I'll keep the five thousand as a hazard fee."

For the first time, Halvard smiled all the way—a crooked, exhausted, enormously relieved smile, the smile of a man who had just set down something very heavy and was not yet sure the ground would hold it.

"Fair terms," he said. "One condition of mine. We leave at first light."

"Why first light?"

"Because," Halvard said, glancing out the darkening window at the road behind them, at the long empty miles of flats where the storm had sulked and gone, "because I think we're being followed, and I'd rather be uphill when they arrive."
