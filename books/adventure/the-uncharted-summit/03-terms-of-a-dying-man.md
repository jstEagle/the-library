## 3. Terms of a Dying Man

The coffee at the Last Gas Station was the color of regret and tasted roughly the same. Zephyrine had been drinking it for eleven years, ever since Doreen's husband passed and she stopped caring about anything except the crossword and the lottery numbers taped to the register. It was the kind of coffee that didn't wake you up so much as remind you that you were already awake, and that this was your life now.

Zephyrine sat across from Halvard in the corner booth, watching him wrap both hands around his cup like it was something precious. He hadn't touched it. Smart man.

"You're not going to drink that," she said.

"I'm dying, not suicidal."

Fair enough. She took a sip of her own out of spite, mostly to prove she could survive things he couldn't. The coffee scalded her tongue and she kept a straight face through sheer professional pride.

Halvard had insisted on the booth. He'd walked in from the parking lot like a man crossing a frozen river—slow, deliberate, testing every step—and now he sat with his back to the wall where he could see the door, which told her more about his past than any conversation would have. Old habits. The kind you don't pick up unless someone once came looking for you.

"So," she said. "You were going to tell me why a man who can't climb a flight of stairs wants to be driven into mountains that eat staircases for breakfast."

He was quiet for a moment. Outside, the wind pushed a tumbleweed of road grit across the lot, and somewhere in the back a freezer compressor kicked on with a groan. Doreen glanced over from behind the counter, decided they weren't worth eavesdropping on yet, and went back to her crossword.

"Before I show you anything," Halvard said, "I need to know what you know about the Sorrowline River."

"It's a river."

His eyebrows rose a fraction. "That's it?"

"It starts up in the Calloway Range, runs south past Ferrow Bend, floods every spring because nobody upstream will build proper levees, and it's brown, which I've always found insulting. Rivers in postcards are blue. Ours looks like it's apologizing for existing." She shrugged. "It's also the reason my truck has rust holes you could post letters through."

"That's what most people know." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer, a quarter turn at a time, like he was winding something. "Now let me tell you what the surveys say. Go on. Ask me how many official government surveys mark the headwaters of the Sorrowline."

"How many?"

"None."

She waited for the punchline. It didn't come.

"The river just... begins?" she said. "Rivers do that. They begin. That's generally how rivers work."

"They begin *somewhere*, Ms. Iversen. And every survey of the upper Sorrowline, going back forty years, ends at the same coordinates with the same notation." He pulled a folded piece of paper from inside his coat, opened it flat on the table between them, and smoothed it with one cracked thumb. "See for yourself."

It was a photocopy of an old survey sheet, the kind with the fine gray hatching that meant *unsurveyed territory* or *restricted* or whatever euphemism the cartographers had settled on that decade. The hatching covered the entire upper valley like a scar. But someone had drawn over it—in ink, in a careful, patient hand—a line. A river line, thin and sure, running up into the hatched country and terminating at a small circle. Around the circle, in the same ink, someone had written a single word:

*SOURCE.*

"You drew this?" she asked.

"Forty years ago." His voice was steady but his hands weren't quite. "I was there when the hatching went down. I watched them erase a valley off the face of the maps, Ms. Iversen. Not lose it. Erase it. There's a difference, and the difference is intent."

Zephyrine looked at the little circle for longer than she meant to. Something about it itched at her—not recognition exactly, more like the feeling of a word on the tip of your tongue. She'd grown up two hundred miles downstream of that hatching. Her parents had been surveyors themselves, before the expedition, before the year everything went quiet. Before she'd learned to stop asking questions about it, because the adults in her life had developed a remarkable talent for changing the subject whenever the upper Sorrowline came up.

She pushed the thought down. It wasn't relevant. Nothing from that part of her life was relevant; that was the whole point of having buried it.

"This is a nice ghost story," she said. "But I chase storms for money and drive people around for slightly less money. I'm not a treasure hunter. I'm not even a very good taxi."

"I'm not offering you treasure."

"You said payment enough to erase my debt. That's either treasure or a bank robbery, and you don't look like you could rob a piggy bank."

Something moved across his face—amusement, maybe, or grief wearing amusement's coat. "What I'm offering is a job. Drive me to the source of the Sorrowline before first snow. That's all. One old man, one truck, one road that gets worse the higher you go. When we come back down—" he named a figure.

Zephyrine's coffee nearly came out of her nose.

She made herself set the cup down slowly, because she'd learned long ago that the fastest way to lose a negotiation was to look like you needed it. But her heart was doing arithmetic without her permission. That number was the debt. All of it. The Optimists' cheerful little ledger, closed. No collectors smiling in her passenger seat. No three-week deadline hanging over her like weather. She could sell the truck if she wanted. She could buy a *different* truck. She could—

Stop, she told herself. This is how they get you. Hope is just interest compounding.

"Where's a dying prospector getting that kind of money?"

"Savings. Forty years of savings, and no heirs, and nothing left I want to spend it on except this." He tapped the map fragment once, lightly, as though the paper might bruise. "I've been liquidating. Slowly. Carefully. If you're wondering whether I can actually pay, ask Doreen—I wired a deposit to her till this morning, enough to cover your fuel and then some. Check with your bank tonight. The rest is in an account you can verify before we leave."

She did wonder, briefly, if it was stolen. Then she looked at him—the coat mended at both elbows, the boots resoled past the point of dignity, the way he'd ordered the cheapest thing on the menu and then only pretended to eat it—and thought: *no thief dresses this poor.* Thieves dressed well. That was practically the definition.

"Why me?" she asked instead. "There are other drivers. Better ones. Guys with winches and satellite phones and actual insurance."

"There are," he agreed. "And I talked to two of them yesterday. One wanted half up front and the other wouldn't go past Ferrow Bend no matter what I offered. You'll notice neither of them is sitting here."

"That doesn't answer the question."

"No," he said. "It doesn't." And he looked at her then, straight on, with eyes the pale washed-out blue of high-altitude sky, and for a second she felt like a specimen under glass—like he knew something about her that she hadn't gotten around to learning yet. "Let's say I liked your driving. Yesterday, on the flats, ahead of that supercell. I watched you read that storm for six hours. You never once drove into the part of it that kills people. Do you know how rare that is? Everyone else out there is either terrified or in love. You were *working*. You treated that storm like a colleague you didn't particularly like."

"She owed me money too," Zephyrine said dryly.

But underneath the joke, unease was setting in, cold and slow, like wading into the Sorrowline itself. Because he'd been watching her. For hours. A dying man with a fortune in savings and a map the government had spent forty years burying, and he'd spent yesterday watching *her*.

"What happens at the source?" she asked. "And don't say 'nothing,' because nobody pays that number for nothing."

Halvard was quiet for a long time. The freezer compressor cycled off. Doreen filled the silence by loudly circling something in her crossword, which was her way of announcing she was listening now.

"I need to return something," he said finally. "That's all I'll tell you tonight. Something that belongs to the mountain, and to the people who used to live there. I've carried it a long time, and I'm out of time to carry it farther." His hand went briefly to his chest—an unconscious gesture, she noticed, the kind of touch you give a wound or a keepsake. "You don't need to believe me. You just need to drive."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll find another driver, and probably die in a ditch, and you'll still owe the Optimists until they take the truck, and after that you'll owe them in ways I genuinely hope you never find out about." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "I did my research, Ms. Iversen. I know who holds your paper. I know what their 'collateral' clause covers. I'm not threatening you. I'm just the first person in months to offer you a way out that doesn't involve losing everything."

The trap of it was beautiful, really. She almost admired it. Every instinct she had—the ones that had kept her alive in hail corridors and flash-flood washes—was standing at attention, hackles up, whispering *too good, too strange, too timed.* And every one of those instincts was being shouted down by a number. By the image of the collector's smile, wiped clean off his face. By the simple, animal longing to be *out*, to owe nothing to anyone, to own her own name again.

Hope, she thought, is just interest compounding. But God, the rate was tempting.

"One condition," she said.

Halvard raised his eyebrows.

"We do this my way. My route, my timeline, my calls. If I say we turn around, we turn around—if you argue, I will absolutely leave you at a trailhead with your memories and your map. And if anyone asks, this is a scenic tour for my elderly uncle. Nobody drives toward restricted hatching on purpose. Not if they want to keep their permits."

"Agreed."

"And you drink the coffee next time. Doreen takes it personally."

A dry sound escaped him that might once have been a laugh. "Doreen takes it personally anyway. I saw her watching us."

"She watches everyone. It's the entertainment out here."

He extended his hand across the table. His grip was surprisingly strong for a man held together by cough drops and stubbornness, and his palm was cool and dry as old paper.

They signed on the back of a receipt, because neither of them had anything fancier and because, Zephyrine reflected, all the important contracts of her life had been written on garbage. Terms of employment: one driver, one passenger, destination undisclosed, payment verifiable upon return. Halvard printed his name in careful block letters—HALVARD EIDE—and beneath it, after a pause, added a small, precise signature that looked like it belonged on engineering documents, not gas station receipts.

She stared at it a beat too long.

*Eide.* Why did that snag? Somewhere, in some drawer of her childhood, in some file among her parents' papers that she'd never had the heart to throw out and never the stomach to open—

No. Stop. Coincidence. The world was full of Eides. Half the river towns were.

"You alright?" Halvard asked.

"Peachy." She folded the receipt into her jacket pocket, where it sat against her ribs like a coal. "We leave day after tomorrow. I need to service the truck, and you need to see a doctor, and I need to pretend for forty-eight hours that I haven't just agreed to drive a dying man into a place that isn't on any maps."

"Two days," he agreed. He gathered his coat, slow as syrup, and stood. At the edge of the booth he paused, one hand on the table, and looked down at her with that pale sky gaze again.

"For what it's worth," he said, "you're right to be suspicious. I'd think less of you if you weren't. But I promise you this much, and I've never broken it in sixty years: when this trip is done, you'll understand why it had to be you. Not today. Maybe not halfway up. But before the end."

"That's exactly what a con man would say."

"Yes," he said pleasantly. "It is."

And then he shuffled out into the wind, coat flapping, small and bent against the big gray evening, leaving Zephyrine alone with two cups of terrible coffee and the growing, sinking, exhilarating certainty that she had just made either the best decision of her life or the last one.

She fished out her phone and checked her balance. The deposit was there. Real money, real and immediate, sitting in her account like bait on a hook.

Behind the counter, Doreen looked up from her crossword. "Well?" she said. "You taking the job or not?"

"Yeah," Zephyrine said, draining the dregs of her coffee and immediately regretting it. "Yeah, I'm taking the job."

"Huh." Doreen chewed her pencil, studying her. "Funny thing. That old man asked me this morning what your family name was. Before he ever asked if you'd drive him."

The freezer compressor kicked back on. Outside, the first stars were coming out over the flats, hard and cold, and somewhere far to the north, under forty years of official hatching, a river was running down out of a valley that officially did not exist—toward her.

Zephyrine told herself it was just a job.

She was already lying to herself, and the trip hadn't even started.
