## 4. The River That Runs Backward

The Sorrowline ran backward.

That was Halvard's phrase, delivered somewhere around the second hour of driving, in the tone of a man pointing out a flaw in the universe that everyone else had agreed to ignore. Most rivers, he said, were born clean and died dirty. This one was the opposite. It came down off the high country carrying nothing but cold and clarity, and by the time it reached the flats it was thick with silt, runoff, tailings, and — he paused here for effect, which I appreciated, because a man his age who could still manage dramatic timing deserved encouragement — *things better left unnamed.*

"You're enjoying this," I said.

"I'm dying," he said pleasantly. "I'm allowed to enjoy things."

We'd been on the road since before dawn, the truck loaded with enough fuel cans, dried food, and rope to make us look like what we were pretending to be: a survey crew doing routine spring gauging. The Optimists' collectors would be looking for a prospector and a storm chaser, not two bureaucrats with clipboards. I'd even bought clipboards. Two of them. They sat on the dashboard like props in a play nobody had rehearsed, and Halvard kept picking mine up and making little ticking noises with his tongue as though he were actually measuring something, which I'll admit made me laugh the first four times and only slightly less the fifth.

The river itself was beautiful in the way that guilty things are beautiful. Wide and gray-green, sliding alongside the highway through country that got greener and steeper by the mile. Willows crowded its banks. Birds I couldn't name worked the shallows. And every so often we'd pass a bridge, or a culvert, or a stretch of gravel bar where the water spread out all silver and patient, and I'd feel something in my chest I didn't have a name for either. Recognition, maybe. Or the memory of recognition. Like hearing a song from another room.

"Thirsty?" Halvard asked, holding out a canteen.

"I'm fine."

"You keep looking at the water."

"I'm looking at the road."

"The road is boring. The water is interesting." He settled back into his seat with the satisfaction of a man who had won an argument without having one. "My mother used to say the Sorrowline remembers everybody who ever drank from it. She said that's why they named it what they named it. Not because it's sad. Because it *keeps.*"

"That's not how rivers get named."

"How do rivers get named, then?"

"Somebody important gets there first and names it after his feelings."

Halvard laughed, then coughed, then laughed again through the cough, which was worse to listen to than either alone. "Somebody important," he repeated, wiping his mouth with the back of his hand. "Yes. That sounds about right for this valley."

* * *

The first town was called Merrow Bend, according to the sign, and the sign was the last thing in it that acknowledged the river at all.

I noticed it gradually, the way you notice a room has gone quiet. Gas station, feed store, a diner with a hand-painted OPEN sign and a porch full of chairs arranged to face away from the water. No marina. No fishing shop. No faded mural of trout leaping, which in my experience is practically mandatory for any town within sight of a decent river. And on the water tower, where towns usually paint their little civic boasts — HOME OF THE CHAMPION SOMETHING-OR-OTHER — there was just the town name and a coat of paint so fresh it looked embarrassed.

Inside the diner, I ordered coffee and pie and asked the waitress, a woman of about sixty with forearms like dock rope, whether the fishing upstream was any good.

She stopped wiping the counter.

It wasn't dramatic. She didn't gasp or drop anything. She just stopped, the way a clock stops, and looked at me with an expression I can only describe as *careful*, and said, "We don't really go in for that around here."

"For fishing?"

"For asking."

Then she refilled my coffee without being asked, which I understood to mean she wasn't hostile, just practiced, and went back to her other customers, and I stood there holding my cup feeling like I'd asked her to describe a funeral.

Halvard was waiting in the truck when I came out. He'd gotten us fuel and a local paper, and he held up the paper as evidence. "Look at this."

It was the Merrow Bend weekly. Front page: a school fundraiser, a harvest festival announcement, a letter to the editor about fence lines. Nothing about the river. In a river town. I flipped through all eight pages. There was a classified ad for a canoe — *never used* — and that was the closest the paper came to acknowledging that water existed.

"Forty years," Halvard said. "That's how long it takes to teach a whole valley not to look out its own front windows."

"Or the paper's just boring."

"Sure." He folded it with great care. "And I'm just an old man going for a drive."

* * *

The second town didn't have a name on the sign at all. Just a population figure — 312 — and below it, in smaller letters, ELEV. 4,100 FT, as if elevation were identity enough. The people there were friendlier and somehow more unsettling for it. A man at the general store chatted with me about tire pressure for ten minutes and steered the conversation away from the river three separate times with the smoothness of long habit. When Halvard, from the doorway, said loudly, "Fine stretch of water you've got here," the man smiled at him with his whole face and said, "Is there? Hadn't noticed," and rang up our jerky and our batteries and wished us safe travels, and I swear to you his eyes never once flicked toward the window, through which the river was visible, enormous, forty yards away.

In the truck, I said, "They're scared."

"They're polite," Halvard said. "Scared and polite look the same after enough years. Like rust and paint."

"And which are we looking at?"

"Both, probably. Fear with a fresh coat." He looked out at the water sliding past, green and cold and keeping whatever it keeps. "You want to know the strange part, Zephyrine? Nobody told them to stop talking about it. There's no rule. You could check. There's no ordinance, no posted notice, no fine. Somebody decided, a long time ago, that the cheapest way to bury a thing is to make everyone believe they buried it themselves. Voluntary forgetting. Very efficient. No enforcement costs whatsoever."

"Until now."

"Now there's us," he agreed cheerfully, "two strangers driving upstream asking questions, and I promise you that by tonight, somebody three towns back has already made a phone call. Not to the police. To somebody else."

"Who?"

He smiled at the windshield. "You'll know them by how cheerful they sound."

I thought of the collector in my truck seat, of the smile that had too many teeth in it, of the deadline sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone. Three weeks. I had three weeks, and I'd spent one of them already buying clipboards.

"Tell me about the map again," I said. "The part you're not showing me."

"Ah." He closed his eyes, settling in like a cat. "No."

* * *

The roadblock appeared an hour later, and I want to be honest about my first reaction, which was relief. Because a roadblock is at least an *answer*. After two towns of smiling silence and a newspaper with no river in it, a man in a reflective vest standing in front of a sawhorse felt almost comforting. Here, finally, was the enforcement Halvard kept promising. Something I could argue with.

There were two vehicles — white, unmarked, government-plain — and three men, and only one of them wore anything official-looking. That one had a clipboard of his own, a real one, laminated, and he came to my window with the unhurried gait of someone who has turned back a hundred cars and will turn back a thousand more.

"Road's closed," he said. "Seasonal hazard. Melt damage up ahead."

"Melt damage," I repeated. "In this section?"

"Whole upper valley. Bridge out past Kettle Flats." He said it smoothly, but I'd spent years reading skies, and skies and bureaucrats lie the same way: too evenly. "You'll need to turn back."

"We're survey," I said, tapping the clipboard on my dash. "Gauging permit. We're expected above Kettle Flats."

He leaned in and looked at the permit — the real one, the legitimate one I'd pulled under my own name because I do occasionally do honest work — and something crossed his face. Not suspicion exactly. More like a man checking a lock and finding it already unlocked, which is somehow worse.

"Gauging," he said. "On the Sorrowline?"

And there it was. The name. Out loud, in the open air, from a government man whose job presumably involved the river daily. He said it the way you'd say the name of a dead relative at a funeral you weren't sure was really over — correctly, quietly, and while watching to see who flinched.

"Hazard assessment," I said. "Standard practice."

"Nothing standard practices up there anymore." He straightened up. "Turn back at the next junction. Take the low road east. You'll find the detour well marked."

We found no detour well marked. We found a junction, and a low road east, and we took it for six miles until Halvard said, "Left here, past the dead elm," and we left the marked world entirely.

* * *

The goat track — he insisted on calling it a goat track, though goats would have filed a complaint — climbed away from the river through scrub pine and scree, switchbacking up a spine of rock that some ancient flood had left standing like a wall between the valley and everything east of it. The truck hated it. The truck expressed its opinions in the language of grinding differentials and one alarming metallic shriek that Halvard chose to interpret as enthusiasm.

"She's a good rig," he said.

"She's a two-wheel-drive confession," I said. "She's telling on us."

But she held, and by late afternoon we'd gained enough height that the valley opened out below us, and I pulled over onto a shelf of stone just wide enough, because some views you don't drive past.

The Sorrowline from up there was a single bright thread stitched through dark country, running from the hazy south all the way north into mountains that still wore their winter like a debt unpaid. And I could see, now, what the maps in Halvard's jacket hinted at and the roadblock confirmed: the upper valley simply *stopped*. Not faded, not thinned — stopped. Roads ended mid-arrow. Towns appeared and then didn't. There was a stretch of country, maybe sixty miles of it, where the thread of the river ran through blankness, and the blankness had edges too clean to be natural.

"Nobody lives there," Halvard said, following my gaze. "Officially."

"And unofficially?"

"Unofficially," he said, "the river has people. It always did. They just got subtracted." He tapped the windshield, right where the blank country lay. "Subtraction, Zephyrine. That's the whole trick. You don't burn a valley down — burning makes smoke, and smoke makes questions. You subtract it. Move the people, flood the low ground, close the passes, redraw the maps, and wait. Give it thirty years and even the people you moved will defend the blank space, because the blank space is all they've got left of home, and defending it is the only way they have of saying they remember."

His voice had gone strange — not feverish, exactly, but lit from underneath, the way a man gets when he's talking about the inside of his own chest.

"You've seen it happen," I said.

"I've done paperwork for it," he said, and the light went out of him as fast as it had come. He coughed, long and wet, and waved off my hand. "Drive. We're losing the light, and there's a place I know above the flood line where we can sleep without dreaming about clipboards."

* * *

We camped that night in a fold of hills where the track dipped behind a ridge, and I built a small fire because Halvard's hands had started shaking in a way that wasn't entirely the cold, and hot food fixes some fraction of most problems.

He ate slowly. Then he hummed.

It was nothing, at first — just a tuneless little run of notes while he looked into the fire, the kind of thing old men do without knowing. But it had a shape. It rose twice and fell once, rose twice and fell once, and there was a turn in it near the end, a small lift like a step up onto a porch, and I knew that lift. I knew it the way you know a staircase in the dark, by the body and not the mind.

My mother used to sing it. Badly, and always while working — kneading bread, mending nets, packing expedition crates — and I used to think it wasn't a song at all, just the sound she made when her hands were busy. I hadn't thought of it in twenty years, and here it was, rising out of a dying stranger beside a fire in stolen country, note for note, including the porch-step lift at the end that no one else in the world had ever gotten right.

The fire popped. The river murmured somewhere below us in the dark, keeping whatever it keeps.

"Halvard," I said carefully. "Where did you learn that song?"

He didn't answer right away. He fed a stick to the flames and watched it catch, and when he finally spoke his voice had gone quiet and old in a new way.

"From a woman who sang it badly," he said, "while she packed crates for a trip upriver she never came back from."

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere far off, an animal called out across the blank country, and got no answer.

"Sleep," Halvard said gently, before I could ask the question forming in my throat. "Tomorrow we climb. And tomorrow night, if the weather holds, you'll meet people who can tell you the rest — people the maps forgot, who never forgot a single thing."

I lay back against the truck's tire and stared up at more stars than the flatlands ever allowed, and I did not sleep, not really. I lay there turning over a lullaby with a name I didn't know yet, and above the tree line the blank country waited, patient as water, keeping everything.
