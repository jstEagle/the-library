## 5. What the Collectors Drive

The trouble with auditing permits, Wren Calloway had learned over eleven years of doing it, was that everyone lied to her eventually. The farmers lied about their water rights. The loggers lied about their cut lines. The tourists lied about their fishing licenses and then, when caught, lied about why they'd lied. Even the mountains lied—she'd once spent three days verifying a boundary marker that turned out to have been moved by a landslide in 1974, which meant the mountain had been lying longer than she'd been alive.

So Wren had developed a professional relationship with dishonesty. She didn't hate it. She understood it. Lies were just people trying to keep something small and precious out of the reach of paperwork.

Which was why it bothered her so much, sitting in the survey office at Meridian Falls with a lukewarm cup of vending machine coffee, that she could no longer remember what her own small precious thing had been.

"Permit audit," said the memo on her desk, stamped with the cheerful sunburst logo of the Optimist Lending & Development Cooperative. *A brighter tomorrow, financed today.* The Optimists had been sponsoring government surveys for six years now, ever since the provincial budget went soft and someone in the capital discovered that a lending syndicate would happily pay for road assessments if the roads led somewhere they wanted to foreclose on. Wren's audits now came pre-annotated in optimistic yellow highlighter: parcels of interest, holdings of concern, families whose land might be worth repossessing if their luck ran true to form.

She highlighted nothing this morning. She drank her bad coffee and looked out the window at the Sorrowline, running gray and patient through the town below, and she thought about the rumor that had arrived three days ago in an envelope with no return address.

*Old man Halvard is going upriver,* the note said, in handwriting she almost recognized. *He has a map. You know whose map.*

She did know. That was the problem. She knew exactly whose map, because she'd spent half her life pretending she didn't.

* * *

At ten o'clock, a black sedan pulled into the lot outside the survey office, and Wren felt the familiar drop in her stomach, the one she'd learned to disguise as indigestion.

Everyone in the valley knew what the collectors drove. Not because it was secret—nothing the Optimists did was secret; secrecy was for people with something to hide, and the Optimists genuinely believed they had nothing to hide, which was somehow worse—but because their cars were so aggressively pleasant. Black paint polished to a mirror shine. Little sunburst decals on the doors. License plate frames that read HAVE A BRIGHT DAY. It was like being repossessed by a children's television host.

The man who unfolded himself from the sedan was named Mr. Feliz, which Wren suspected was not his real name but which he wore the way he wore everything: like a gift he was delighted to give you. He was broad and pink-faced, with the build of a man who lifted weights recreationally and smiled professionally, and he crossed the office floor with his hand already extended.

"Officer Calloway!" he boomed, in a voice calibrated to be friendly at volume. "You look wonderful. Mountain air agrees with you."

"It's the altitude," Wren said, shaking his hand. "It makes people look slightly alarmed."

"That's just oxygen enthusiasm." He helped himself to the chair across from her desk without asking, the way he always did, settling into it like a man visiting his own summer home. On the desk between them he placed a single sheet of paper, face down, and left his hand flat on top of it. "I'll be quick. I know you're busy."

"I'm auditing permits," Wren said. "Busy is a strong word."

"And yet vital work." Mr. Feliz beamed at her. He had very white teeth. Wren had once spent an entire meeting trying to determine whether they were real and had concluded that no one's teeth were that uniform without divine intervention or a payment plan. "Now. We understand there's a gentleman passing through your jurisdiction. Older fellow. Prospector type. Coughs like a bellows. Traveling with—" he consulted a card he did not need to consult, "—a storm chaser. Zephyrine Iversen. Drives a green Ford with a hail-dented roof and a bumper sticker that says honk if you love barometric pressure."

Wren kept her face still. It was a skill she'd practiced for years, mostly across desks from men like this one. "That's a very specific truck."

"We're thorough people." He turned the paper over. It was a photograph, grainy, taken from above—a gas station forecourt, a green truck, two figures beside it blurred almost past recognition. Almost. "We're also generous people, Officer Calloway. You've been very helpful to us over the years, and we don't forget our friends."

*Helpful.* There was the word, doing its quiet work. Six years ago, Wren had made a mistake. Not a large one, as mistakes went—she'd filed a report on an expedition permit, an expedition upriver, a failed one, and in the report she had mentioned, carelessly, that certain survey records from twenty years prior appeared to have been altered. Redacted. Erased. She'd noted it as an anomaly. A clerical curiosity.

Two weeks later, Mr. Feliz—not this Mr. Feliz; the previous one, who had since been promoted somewhere with better weather—had come to her office with a folder containing her father's name, her father's debts, and a settlement offer that would make both disappear. Her father had gone upriver once too, years before, chasing the same blank space on the map, and had come back owing money to cheerful men, and had died still owing it, and the debt had passed to her like a family heirloom nobody wanted.

The Optimists had bought her silence the way they bought everything: politely, permanently, and at a discount.

"You want me to find them," Wren said.

"We want you to *do what you already do*," Mr. Feliz corrected gently, as though she were a student who had nearly gotten the right answer. "Audit. Verify. Follow the permits. If your professional duties happen to bring you into contact with the Iversen vehicle—if you happen to observe its position, its heading, its condition—well. That's just good record-keeping." He slid a second card across the desk. It had a phone number and, beneath it, a sunburst. "And if the old man's map should surface in any official capacity—seized, impounded, surrendered—you'd be doing us all a service by making sure it reached the right hands first. There's a bonus structure. Very fair. Very bright."

"What's on the map?" Wren asked. She asked every time, in some variation, and every time she received the same answer, and every time the answer told her more than the speaker intended.

Mr. Feliz stood, buttoning his jacket, smiling his payment-plan smile. "Nothing you need to worry your head about. Old prospectors draw maps to old holes in the ground. Sentimental nonsense." He tapped the desk twice, a little farewell rhythm. "But sentiment can be valuable, Officer Calloway. To the right buyer. Have a bright day."

He let himself out. Through the window Wren watched the black sedan pull away, sunbursts gleaming, and she sat for a long moment with the photograph in front of her.

In the corner of the image, half-cropped, was the gas station's air pump, and beside the air pump, leaning against the wall where anyone could see it, a bicycle. Red frame. Basket on the front. It belonged to Tamsin Oduya, who ran the station, who was Wren's only friend within forty miles, and who had sold Wren coffee every morning for four years while carefully never asking why a survey officer sometimes needed to know things that weren't in any survey.

Tamsin had seen them. Tamsin had let them go. And Tamsin had, presumably, mentioned it to no one—which meant the photograph hadn't come from Tamsin.

It had come from the road camera on the north highway. Which meant the Optimists had cameras on the north highway. Which meant—

Wren put the photograph down and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

Six years ago she had told herself it was temporary. One report buried, one debt cleared, and then she'd be free. But the Optimists didn't sell freedom; they leased it, month to month, and the rent was always one more small thing. A position here. A delay there. A name passed along, a door left unlocked, a blind eye turned until it forgot how to do anything else. She had become a woman composed entirely of small compromises, stacked so neatly and for so long that from the outside she probably looked like integrity.

From the inside, she looked like a woman holding a photograph of a dying man and a stranger's truck, deciding how much they were worth.

* * *

She drove north that afternoon, because that was what the audit said to do, and because she wanted to see the truck with her own eyes before she decided anything.

The Sorrowline ran alongside the road the whole way, going the wrong direction—or rather, she was driving the direction rivers weren't supposed to matter, upstream, toward the high country, toward the pass that had been closed since before she was born. The signs started around kilometer sixty. OFFICIAL SURVEY IN PROGRESS. ROAD CLOSED FOR REMEDIATION. And then, the older ones, rusted down to orange lace: RESTRICTED WATERSHED. NO PUBLIC ACCESS. AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY, the authorization expired decades ago, the fence behind the sign folded open like a book nobody had read since.

Her father had driven this road once. She'd found the fuel receipts after he died, tucked in a tin with his other papers, dated the spring before the Incident—the word everyone used, the word that meant *we agreed not to say*. He'd gone upriver looking for whatever the government had erased, and he'd come back with nothing but debt and a story he told only when drunk, about lights on the mountain and a river that tasted of metal for a week.

The Optimists had bought his silence too. She understood that now. They'd bought everyone's silence, one cheerful envelope at a time, until the whole valley was quiet as a held breath.

The truck was easy to find. Storm chasers, in Wren's experience, parked the way they lived: boldly, badly, and wherever the weather seemed most interesting. The green Ford sat in a pull-off above the drowned flats, hail dents catching the afternoon light, bumper sticker facing the road like a dare. Nobody was in it. Down the slope, near the water's edge, two figures moved slowly—one upright and steady, one bent and halting, an arm steadied through the other's elbow.

Wren stopped her own vehicle fifty meters back and watched through the windshield.

The old man—Halvard, it had to be—stood at the edge of the water with his hat off. He wasn't looking at the view. He was looking at the river itself, the way you'd look at a face you'd loved and wronged. His mouth was moving. From this distance she couldn't hear whether it was prayer or apology, and she suspected, watching the set of his shoulders, that there wasn't much difference.

Beside him, the storm chaser waited. Patiently, Wren noticed. Without checking her watch or her phone. Zephyrine Iversen, whom the debt records described as a liability and the gas station cameras described as a blur, stood in the wind with her hands in her pockets and gave a dying man all the time he needed, and something about the sight of it landed in Wren's chest like a stone dropped down a well.

She knew that kind of patience. She'd grown up around it. Her mother had it, before. People who'd lost someone to the mountain carried it—it was the patience of the not-allowed-to-mourn, the terrible courtesy of grief with nowhere to go.

*Oh,* Wren thought. *Oh, you don't even know, do you.*

She got out of her car.

It wasn't the plan. The plan—the Optimists' plan, the plan she'd been leasing for six years—was observation, notation, a phone call with coordinates and a bright-day sign-off. Instead she walked down the slope with her survey clipboard held like a shield, boots crunching on frost-heaved gravel, and by the time the storm chaser turned to look at her Wren had assembled a face she hoped read as bureaucratic boredom.

"Afternoon," she called. "Survey officer. Routine permit check."

Zephyrine Iversen looked at her the way people look at weather they've decided not to fear. Up close she was younger than Wren expected and more tired, with wind-chapped cheeks and eyes that did rapid arithmetic—clipboard, badge, distance to the truck, weight of the man on her arm—and arrived, visibly, at *not worth running from*.

"Of course it is," Zephyrine said. "Everything's routine out here. That's what makes it sinister."

"The permit for this vehicle is a recreational survey pass," Wren said, reading from the clipboard she wasn't reading. "It doesn't cover transport of—"

"Hitchhikers?"

"Livestock, hazardous materials, or persons engaged in resource extraction." She glanced at Halvard. Up close he was worse than the rumors: gray-lipped, breath whistling, a man built out of stubbornness and spare parts. He watched her with pale, level eyes that made her feel like the one being audited. "Sir, are you engaged in resource extraction?"

"I'm engaged in dying," Halvard said, voice like gravel in a bucket. "Slowly. It's not lucrative."

"Then you're fine." Wren clicked her pen. Clicked it again. Somewhere behind her ribs, six years of small compromises shifted uneasily, like furniture in a house that knows it's being measured for sale. "I'll need to note your route. For the record. Where are you headed?"

"Upstream," said Zephyrine.

"That's not a route."

"It's a direction."

"Directions get logged." Wren kept her voice flat, official, and prayed to whatever governed closed passes and buried stations that neither of them could hear the part of her screaming underneath. Because here was the thing she had realized somewhere around kilometer sixty, watching the rusted signs, and was realizing again now, harder, watching this woman stand between a bureaucrat and a dying man: the Optimists didn't want the map for leverage. Leverage was the polite fiction. They wanted it because of what it proved, and what it proved was the thing her father had died owing money for trying to find, and if it went into a black sedan with a sunburst on the door it would go into the ground again, deeper this time, and the valley would hold its breath for another thirty years.

She couldn't let that happen. She also couldn't, quite yet, imagine stopping it. So she did the only thing available to a captive with a clipboard.

She stalled.

"There's a bridge closure at Miller's Crossing," she said. "Flood damage. You'll be rerouted to the north track."

"There's nothing on the north track but goat paths," Zephyrine said.

"Then I'd take the goat paths." Wren wrote something on the clipboard—an actual something, coordinates, the pull-off, the time, honest data, the first honest data she'd logged in months—and tore off the carbon copy and handed it over. "Copy of your inspection. Keep it. If any other officers stop you, show them this. It says you've been checked."

Zephyrine took it, frowning at the slip of paper as though it might be rigged. "Why are you being helpful? You people usually aren't."

*You people.* Wren almost laughed. Yes. Us people. The ones who drive black cars and smile in photographs and file reports that erase valleys. "Departmental policy," she said. "We're very bright."

Halvard made a sound that might have been a laugh or a cough; with him it was hard to say. He was still looking at her, those pale eyes narrowed, and then he said, quietly, in a voice pitched for her alone: "Calloway. Any relation to Tomas Calloway?"

The wind moved the river. Somewhere upslope, a truck door ticked in the cold.

"He was my father," Wren heard herself say.

Halvard nodded slowly, as if confirming a measurement he'd long suspected. "Good man," he said. "Bad maps. He trusted the printed ones." He settled his hat back on. "Tell him, next time you see him, that some of us kept the handwritten ones."

And Wren stood there on the frozen bank with her clipboard and her six clean years of complicity, watching an old man turn back toward the water, and understood suddenly and completely that she had been sent to hunt a ghost and had instead been recognized by one.

She walked back up the slope. She got in her car. She sat with her hands on the wheel for a full minute, engine idling, the river running gray and patient beside her, going where it had always gone regardless of who was allowed to follow it.

Then she picked up her radio, keyed the channel that connected her—officially—to the survey office, and reported her position: north patrol, no contacts, all permits in order.

Then she picked up her phone, keyed the number on the card with the sunburst, and prepared to lie to the cheeriest man she knew.

"Officer Calloway!" Mr. Feliz answered, bright as noon. "What have you got for me?"

"Truck's heading south," Wren said, watching the green Ford pull onto the north track and vanish into the trees. "Back toward Meridian Falls. Whatever they're doing, it isn't the pass."

A pause, brief as a blink. "South," Mr. Feliz repeated pleasantly. "Are you certain?"

"Certain enough to log it," Wren said, and smiled at the windshield, and hated herself with a brightness that surprised her. "Have a bright day."

She hung up. North, the dust of the green truck was already settling. In two days, maybe three, the lie would unravel—collectors were patient, but they weren't blind—and when it did, Mr. Feliz would drive up from the city with his payment-plan teeth, and there would be a conversation, and Wren Calloway's eleven careful years would come due all at once.

She put the car in gear and followed the river anyway.

Behind her, far downstream, the first clouds of the season were stacking up over the flats, tall and blue-black and organized, the kind of sky Zephyrine Iversen probably loved. Wren watched them grow in the mirror and thought, with something that was not quite hope but had its shape: *good. Let it snow early. Let it snow everywhere.*

Let the whole valley go white, and every road with it, and every camera, and every cheerful black car—let the mountain hide them the way it had hidden everything else, until somebody finally dug the truth out and brought it home.
