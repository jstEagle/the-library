## 6. Thaw Line

The goat track, in fairness, had been Halvard's idea, and he had at least had the decency to look sheepish about it afterward.

"It was a road when I last came through," he said, from the passenger seat, as Zephyrine's truck tilted at an angle that suggested it was considering a career change into aviation.

"When was that?"

"Nineteen sixty-one."

"Ah," said Zephyrine. "So within living memory. Of a goat."

The track clung to the flank of the valley like a scar, a shelf of gravel and old slate cut into the mountainside above the Sorrowline, which ran silver and swollen far below. The roadblock had turned them back two days ago—a cheerful young man in a survey uniform who had checked their permits twice, smiled three times, and mentioned the word *restricted* four times in a way that made Zephyrine's skin crawl in a way the word itself never had. So Halvard had unfolded his memory of the goat track, and Zephyrine, who should have known better, had driven onto it, because the alternative was turning around, and turning around had a smell to it she refused to breathe.

The morning had been warm. That was the whole problem, though she wouldn't understand the arithmetic of it until later. Three days of sun on the high snowfields, and all that water had to go somewhere, and the somewhere was through the old cut of the track, which had been quietly rotting for sixty years while everyone pretended the mountain was finished with it.

She heard it before she saw it—a sound like a giant clearing its throat underwater.

"Halvard."

"I hear it."

The shelf of road ahead of them simply *left*. One moment it was there, gravel and slate, holding up the sky's opinion of itself; the next, a slab of it the size of a barn slid out from under the world and went down into the valley with a sound like the end of a long argument. The truck's front wheels were still on solid ground when the crack ran underneath them.

Zephyrine did not remember deciding. Her body decided for her—brakes, door, gravel biting her palms, and then she was standing ten feet back, watching her truck, her truck, the last thing she owned that the Optimists hadn't put a number on, slide with terrible dignity off the shelf and into the air. It seemed to hang there. It seemed, for one absurd moment, to be considering its options.

Then it went down, end over end, twice, and hit the scree below with a boom that came back off the far wall of the valley a full second later, like the mountain laughing at a joke it had been saving.

Zephyrine stood very still.

"Well," said Halvard, arriving beside her at a pace that suggested his lungs had filed a formal complaint. "I've had worse luck."

"Have you."

"I once lost a mule, all my gear, and a tooth in the same afternoon." He patted his coat pocket, checking for the map fragment the way other men checked for a pulse. "The tooth was my own."

Zephyrine looked at the scree field where her truck was now a sculpture titled *Debt, Remaining*. Her sleeping bag was in it. Her stove was in it. Her mother's tin of survey pins was in it, though she didn't tell Halvard that, because some losses you keep in a drawer inside yourself and don't display.

"Half my gear," she said, "and a tooth."

"Your tooth's fine."

"It's *fine*," she agreed, and spat to check, and it was, and somehow that made it worse.

* * *

They salvaged what they could, which was an education in what mattered. Zephyrine climbed down the scree with her heart in her throat and came back up with a rope, a tarp, one sleeping bag, a dented pot, a bag of oats, a brick of tea, a first aid kit, a hatchet, and—after twenty minutes of grim excavation—the truck's toolbox, because a person who owns one wrench in the mountains is a person with a future, and a person who owns none is a person with a story.

Halvard carried the tea and the map and nothing else, though he tried twice to take the toolbox and she let him try, because there are kindnesses that consist entirely of letting a proud man fail slowly.

They made camp that evening in a hollow above the river, out of the wind, under a lean-to of tarp and slate that Zephyrine built with the particular bad temper of a person who has just lost a truck and gained a perspective. Halvard got a fire going with a competence that surprised her—dry spruce, small sticks first, the patience of someone who had made ten thousand fires and intended to make a few more if the mountain would permit it.

The altitude was already working on him. She'd noticed it on the track that morning—the way he breathed between sentences, the way his color had gone from weathered to wax. Now, sitting by the fire, he looked like a man being slowly erased, an old photograph left out in the sun.

"You should eat," she said, and put oats in the pot, and he ate, and then he didn't eat, and she pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of work.

"Tell me about the debt," he said, instead of anything about himself. He'd developed a talent for that—redirecting. She'd caught him doing it with the roadblock guard, with the gas station woman, with her. A dying man hoarding his remaining breaths like a miser.

"Tell me about yours first."

"Mine's not money."

"Then we're not trading." She stirred the pot. "Fine. The Optimists. I chased weather for them—storm data, hail surveys, the kind of fee that's technically a fee and spiritually a rounding error. Then a season of bad hail hit the orchards I'd insured my truck through them for, and the fine print turned out to be written by someone with a very cheerful soul and a very dark heart. Now I owe them an amount I could say out loud only if we were better friends."

"How much better?"

"Dead better."

Halvard laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing went on long enough that Zephyrine put down the spoon and watched him with her stomach tightening. When it passed he waved her off with two fingers, an old man's gesture, *I'm fine, I've done this before, don't make it a thing.*

"They smile a lot," he said, when he could. "The Optimists."

"They smile a lot."

"My mother used to say a smile is just a door you haven't opened yet. Could be anything behind it."

"Your mother sounds like a woman who'd have done well in lending."

"She'd have done *terribly* in lending. She'd have given the money away." He looked into the fire. "She was a singer. Not professionally. Just—everything was a song. Waking up, a song. Boiling water, a song."

Zephyrine found herself smiling despite the truck-shaped hole in her life. "What happened to her?"

"She's still singing, I expect. Somewhere I'm not." He said it lightly, and it wasn't light at all, and they let it lie there between them like a stone neither wanted to step on.

* * *

The cold came down with the dark, the way it does up high—no negotiation, just an abrupt change of management. Zephyrine got Halvard into the sleeping bag and wrapped him in the tarp over it, and sat up feeding the fire, because he'd stopped shivering and that frightened her more than shivering would have. Shivering meant the body was still fighting.

"Zephyrine," he said, after a long time. He said her name carefully, all four syllables, like a man handling something breakable. "Your mother's name was Maren."

The fire popped. Somewhere down the valley the Sorrowline kept up its constant cold commentary.

"Yes," Zephyrine said.

"And your father—Tobias. Tobias Iversen." He was looking at the fire, not at her. "He surveyed the upper valley. Before."

Zephyrine's hands went still on the hatchet she was sharpening, though she made herself keep the rhythm, stroke, stroke, because stopping would have been an admission and she wasn't ready to admit anything. "Lots of people survey," she said. "Lots of people have parents. You're feverish, Halvard."

"I'm not feverish about this." He turned his head then, and his eyes in the firelight were clear in a way they hadn't been in days, clear the way water is clear right before it freezes. "I knew them. I knew them both."

Stroke, stroke. The hatchet's edge caught the firelight.

"They came to the station," he said. "The summer of the Incident. Your mother had a voice like—" he stopped, and his throat worked. "She used to sing to the instruments. Said the seismographs liked it. Said the needles settled."

The lullaby arrived in Zephyrine's memory before she gave it permission to. Not whole—she'd never had it whole. A fragment, four or five notes, the kind of thing you carry from before memory properly starts, a sound from a kitchen or a truck cab, a woman's voice, warm smoke, a feeling like being held. She had assumed for thirty years it was nothing. A radio song. A dream. Everyone had a sound like that, a little fossil of being small.

Halvard hummed it.

Four notes. Five. His voice was a ruin, thin and cracked at altitude, and it was the most beautiful thing she had heard in years, because it was *hers*, it was the fossil, it was the exact shape of the hole in her that she'd stopped noticing the way you stop noticing a limp.

The hatchet stopped.

"That's—" Her voice came out wrong. She started again. "Where did you get that?"

"From your mother." He said it gently, the way you'd tell someone a fire was out, or a debt forgiven. "She made it up. For you. She told me—she used to sing it in the lab, when she was missing you. She said it was the only thing she'd ever made that was better than a map."

The fire crackled. The river ran. Zephyrine sat very still and felt thirty years of careful, competent, self-managed solitude develop a crack straight down the middle.

She had told herself this was a job. She had told herself it so many times the words had worn smooth, a river stone of a sentence, and now she picked it up and it crumbled in her hand like the road had. He hadn't hired the nearest driver with a truck and a death wish. He had found *her*. Out of all the broke storm chasers in the lowlands, he had walked into her gas station, coughing, with a fortune he may or may not have had, and he had known exactly what he was doing.

"Why?" she asked. It came out smaller than she meant it to. "Why me? Why not just—go yourself, tell someone, put it in a newspaper—"

"Because the newspaper is the government's, and the government buried it, and I'm the last one alive who saw what's in the log." His breath caught, steadied. "And because I promised her. Before the valley flooded, I promised your mother that if anything happened to them—if the truth stayed down—someday I'd carry it back up. It took me fifty years to find a way. It's taking me everything I have left to carry it." He looked at her, and there was no fever in his face at all now, only an old, worn tenderness. "You have her chin, you know. And her terrible habit of asking questions she already knows the answers to."

Zephyrine laughed, or made a sound adjacent to laughing, and wiped her face with the back of her hand, and was grateful the fire was between them and the dark was on her side.

"Sing the rest," she said.

"I don't know the rest. She only ever sang me the part she'd written so far. She said she'd finish it when she got home." His eyes went to the dark above the valley, upstream, toward the closed pass and the buried station and the spring that fed the Sorrowline. "She never got home. So the song's been waiting fifty years for its last line. And I mean to hear it sung, Zephyrine, before I go. Up there. At the source. Where she left it."

The fire burned down. Zephyrine fed it another stick and watched the sparks go up, and thought about her truck at the bottom of the scree, and her debt with its cheerful face, and the four notes of a song that had been living in her all along like a seed in frozen ground.

She didn't sleep much. Toward the coldest hour, Halvard's breathing went shallow and quick, and she sat up all the way and kept the fire high and told herself it was the altitude, it was the cold, it was nothing a warm morning wouldn't fix.

But when the light came, gray and thin over the eastern ridge, Halvard couldn't stand without her shoulder under his arm, and the valley ahead of them rose toward a notch in the mountains she did not remember from any map, and far below, on the river trail they'd abandoned, she caught a flash of movement—figures, small with distance, moving upstream.

Moving the way collectors move. Cheerfully. In no hurry at all.

"Halvard," she said quietly. "How much farther to these river-people you mentioned?"

He followed her glance down the trail, and something in his face closed like a door. "A day," he said. "If the river's kind."

"And if it isn't?"

He smiled the Optimists' smile, upside down. "Then we'd best hope they remember me."
