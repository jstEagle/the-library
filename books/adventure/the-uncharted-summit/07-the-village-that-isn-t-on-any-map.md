## 7. The Village That Isn't on Any Map

The goat track gave out an hour after noon, which Halvard insisted was a kindness.

"Could've gone at dawn," he said from the passenger seat, his voice worn down to gravel and patience. "Could've gone in the dark. Noon's generous, for a road."

"It's not a road," I said. "It's a rumor with potholes."

We'd been climbing along the Sorrowline since morning, the river running beside us the wrong way in my head—downhill, obviously, rivers always ran downhill, but everything about this valley felt reversed. The towns thinned out. The signs got older. Somewhere behind us was a roadblock with a bored officer and a clipboard, and somewhere ahead of us was nothing official at all, which by now I understood to be the point.

The track ended where the hillside had shrugged one shoulder and let a slab of thaw-soaked earth slide into the trees. Not impassable on foot. Definitely impassable for a truck that owed me money.

I killed the engine and sat with my hands on the wheel for a moment, doing the arithmetic I'd been avoiding all day. Fuel, food, two people, altitude, winter coming down the mountains like a landlord.

"Well," I said. "I've walked away from worse vehicles."

"You cried over that sedan in Vellmark," Halvard said.

"I *mourned* that sedan. There's a difference. Grief is dignified." I grabbed my pack and stepped out into air so clean it felt like an accusation.

He climbed down slower than he let me see him planning to. Two days of altitude had taken a visible toll—the cough had deepened into something structural, and his color had gone from weathered to wintry. He waved off my hand, then accepted it anyway about four steps later, which told me more than any confession had.

"We're close," he said. "There are people above the flood line. Old families. They'll take us in if I do the talking."

"People." I looked up the valley. The Sorrowline narrowed ahead into a gorge stitched with pines, and high on the northern slope, half-hidden by the angle of the light, there were roofs. Three of them. Maybe five. Cedar shingles gone silver with age, chimneys breathing thin gray threads into the blue.

"They're not on any map," Halvard said.

"Neither am I, most months."

"That's why you'll get along."

* * *

The path up to them was a goat's idea of a switchback, and the goats would have complained. Halvard stopped twice to breathe, and the second time I pretended to be fascinated by a lichen so he wouldn't have to be embarrassed. By the time we crested the last rise, the sun had swung low and gold across the water below, and the village—if three houses, a smokehouse, a long low barn, and a garden terraced into the hillside counted as a village, and I could already tell these people would insist it did—sat above us like something set down carefully by hands that knew exactly what they were doing.

A dog found us first. Big, gray-muzzled, built like a footstool with opinions. It announced us at length and without any real conviction, tail going the whole time, and then a woman came out of the nearest house drying her hands on her apron and took stock of us the way you'd check a fence line: quickly, thoroughly, without alarm.

She was maybe sixty, broad through the shoulders, with a face that had decided decades ago what it thought of weather and wasn't going to reconsider. Her eyes went to Halvard and stopped. Something moved through her expression—not recognition exactly. More like the feeling of hearing your own name said in a stranger's voice.

"Halvard," she said. "They said you were dead."

"Reports vary," Halvard rasped.

"They say that about a lot of things here." She looked at me. "Who's this?"

"Zephyrine Iversen. I'm driving. Well. Was driving. The truck has retired to the riverbank."

"She's the one," Halvard said quietly.

Something passed between them then, some current I didn't have the map for, and the woman's jaw tightened in a way that might have been anger or grief or both sharing a coat.

"Maren Ostvik," she said to me, and stuck out her hand. Her grip was honest. "You'll be hungry. Everyone who arrives on that path is hungry; it's how the path apologizes."

* * *

The house smelled of woodsmoke, dried fish, rosemary, and bread, which is a combination I recommend to anyone who wants to make a stranger lower their guard without lifting a finger. Maren fed us soup thick enough to stand a spoon in, dark bread, butter with actual character, and tea brewed from something piney that she called "whatever was closest." Halvard ate like a man remembering how, between coughs he tried to hide and failed to.

Around the table gathered the rest of them, drawn in by the gravity of news from outside. Maren's brother Anders, who fished the high lakes and spoke mostly in shrugs. A young couple, Petra and Tomas, who kept bees and kept arguing about bees. And at the far end, oldest of all, a man introduced simply as Uncle Eirik, though I gathered no one was related to him and everyone was.

Eirik was the one who stared.

It started when Halvard pulled off his cap to eat. Eirik went still, spoon halfway to his mouth, and just—looked. Not rude, exactly. The way you look at a door you thought was painted shut and find it open. He was ninety if he was a day, with a face like a riverbed, white hair cropped short in a style I'd only ever seen in old photographs, and eyes the pale gray of ice under strain.

"You worked the station," Eirik said finally. Not a question.

Halvard set down his spoon. "Long time ago."

"I carried mail up that road before they closed it. I knew every face that came down off that mountain." His voice was flat and careful, like a man walking on a frozen lake he didn't trust. "Every face. And I stood at the memorial ground when they read the names, and yours wasn't among them, and I remember thinking: there. That's the lie. That's where the whole thing cracked."

"Eirik," Maren said gently.

"No. Let him sit there." But there was no cruelty in it. Eirik leaned back, and his gaze shifted to me, and softened into something almost puzzled. "And you. You have the look."

"What look?" I asked, around a mouthful of bread, because grace under pressure has never been my strong suit.

"The Iversen look. The surveyors' family. Your mother had that same way of sitting—like she was ready to get up and measure something." He said it plainly, the way you'd tell someone it looked like rain.

The bread turned to clay in my mouth.

"My parents were surveyors," I managed. "They died on an expedition. Upriver. When I was small."

"Yes," Eirik said. "We know."

* * *

Later, while Halvard slept by the stove under a quilt older than the republic, Maren and I washed dishes in a kitchen that hadn't changed its mind about anything since before I was born. Outside, the light went the color of cider, and the river murmured its one long sentence far below.

"You can ask," Maren said, handing me a towel. "Everyone in this house is waiting for you to ask. Anders has rehearsed answers. Tomas has prepared a speech. I'd get it over with before bedtime."

"Why does everyone here know my family name when I barely know it myself?"

Maren dried her hands slowly. "Because after the Incident—and yes, we call it that, because that's what the radio called it, once, before the radio stopped calling it anything—because after, the government moved people out of the valley. Bought them cheap, paid them fast, told them the water was unsafe. Some took the money. Some—" she nodded toward the window, toward the other rooftops catching the last light, "—some took the hillside instead. Moved up above the flood line and stayed. Off every grid, off every list. My grandmother walked her cows uphill in the rain and never went back down."

"And my parents?"

"Your parents were the ones who tried to warn us." She said it simply, but her hands had stopped moving. "Before the flooding. They came down the valley with documents, maps, measurements—telling everyone the water was coming and the reason for it wasn't natural. Most people thought they were mad. Then the water came, and they weren't mad anymore, and they weren't anywhere anymore either. Lost up near the source. Officially: an accident. Unofficially:" She spread her hands. "Unofficially, we don't say. We just don't forget."

The towel in my hands had become a lifeline and I gripped it accordingly. "Halvard knew them."

Maren looked at me for a long moment, and whatever she saw in my face made her decide something. "Child, that man has been carrying this valley on his back for forty years. Whatever he's brought you up here for—it isn't treasure, and it isn't trouble. Or rather," she allowed, with the ghost of a smile, "it's both, but the good kind. If there is such a thing."

"There isn't," I said. "In my experience."

"Then you'll feel right at home."

* * *

I stepped outside after supper to give the stove room and my thoughts more of it. The cold came up clean off the snowfields, and the sky had gone ferocious with stars—no town glow for a hundred miles to dilute them. Below, the Sorrowline caught starlight and carried it away toward all the towns that wouldn't speak its name.

Tomas found me at the fence, ostensibly checking on his bees' winter wraps, actually escaping an argument he'd been losing about hive insulation.

"First time up here?" he asked.

"That obvious?"

"You keep looking at the valley like it owes you an apology." He leaned on the fence beside me. "It does. Everybody down there knows it too, they just signed papers saying otherwise. You get used to being a rumor. It's not so bad. No taxes."

I laughed despite myself—a real one, surprising in my chest like a bird flushed from cover. "Is that the pitch? Off-grid living: no taxes, occasional government erasure?"

"And honey," Tomas said solemnly. "Don't undersell the honey."

From inside the house, muffled by log walls, I heard Halvard coughing, and then—fainter, threaded through the coughing—the lullaby again. That tune. Half-remembered, half-haunting, the one he'd hummed at the fire two nights back, the one that had been itching at the back of my skull like a word on the tip of the tongue ever since.

Eirik was sitting on the bench by the door in the dark, wrapped in a blanket, listening too. He didn't turn when I came to stand beside him.

"He used to sing that to the station children," the old man said. "When the mail run brought me up, sometimes I'd hear it through the walls. Funny thing to carry forty years, a song."

"It's not funny," I said quietly. "I know that song. I just don't know from where."

Eirik turned then, and in the starlight his pale eyes held something I couldn't name—pity, maybe, or the particular tenderness of a man about to hand someone a weight he'd been keeping safe.

"You will," he said. "Ask him tomorrow. Ask him whose song it is. And then, Miss Iversen, you'll want to sit down first."

Inside, the lullaby wound down into silence, and the mountain wind picked up where it left off, and somewhere far downstream, I had the distinct feeling, the way you feel weather coming in an old scar, that our quiet was borrowed and the lenders were already on the road.
