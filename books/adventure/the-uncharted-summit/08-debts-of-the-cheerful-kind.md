## 8. Debts of the Cheerful Kind

The radio crackled at dawn, and Wren Calloway lied to it before she'd even finished her tea.

"Survey vehicle four-one-one, position report." The voice belonged to Denny Okafor, the Optimists' regional coordinator, a man whose cheerfulness had the specific texture of laminated wood. "Confirming grid reference for the Sorrowline corridor audit."

Wren looked out at the actual view: gray river, gray fog, and, two switchbacks below on the old haul road, a green pickup truck with a cracked windshield crawling north like an insect that knew it was being watched.

She consulted her map with theatrical care, though Denny couldn't see her. That was the thing about radio. It was all theater.

"Grid seven-two-niner, mark one-four," she said, reading coordinates from a spot forty kilometers east of where the truck actually was. "Terrain's slow going. I'll need two days to work the eastern tributaries."

A pause. She could hear Denny smiling. He was very good at it; you could hear the teeth.

"Two days! Wonderful. Take your time, Calloway. Thoroughness is what we pay for." A beat. "Speaking of which — any sign of our other traveler? Big fellow, older, coughs like a bellows? Travels with a woman who owes us rather a lot of money?"

"Not so far."

"Mm. Well. If you do see them, remember: observation only. We wouldn't want you getting your hands dirty." Another smile, audible, terrible. "That's what Mr. Voss's people are for."

"Understood," said Wren, who understood perfectly well, and who had already spent last night deciding exactly how badly she intended to misunderstand.

She signed off, set down the radio, and drank her tea while it went cold, which felt appropriate. Below her, the truck disappeared around the bend toward the drowned valley, carrying a dying prospector, a debtor, and — if Halvard still had what Wren believed he had — the only unredacted log of what had happened upriver thirty years ago.

Her father had died looking for that log. Or looking for something in the same country, anyway. With her father it was hard to separate the looking from the dying; they'd been the same activity by the end.

"Two days," she told the fog. "I bought you two days. Try not to die in them."

* * *

The village contact was named Sefa, and she ran a drying shed full of fish that smelled like money would smell, Wren thought, if money were honest about itself.

Officially, Wren was there to audit an unregistered structure. Unofficially — and everything about her life now lived in the gap between those two words — she was there because Sefa was the best-informed person on the river, and because Wren needed to know whether the truck had stopped here, and whether a certain old man had been seen alive.

Sefa met her at the shed door with the expression of a woman who had been audited many times and had never once been impressed by it.

"Survey officer," Sefa said. "Come in, come in. Mind the racks. Mind the cat. The cat minds nothing, but I say it for form."

The cat in question was an enormous orange tom sprawled across the central walkway like a fallen emperor. Wren stepped over him. He opened one eye, assessed her, and closed it again, having concluded she was not worth the effort. She found this deeply relatable.

"I have to ask some questions," Wren said. "You know how it is."

"I know how it is," Sefa agreed. "Ask them slowly. The fish aren't going anywhere, and neither are we. Nobody comes upriver unless they're lost or lying, and you don't look lost."

It was, Wren reflected, a fair hit. She took out her notebook, opened it to a page of official-looking blanks, and then — because she was tired, because the fog made everything feel unofficial, because Sefa was watching her with the patience of someone who gutted fish for a living and could wait out anything — she did something she'd never done in six years of audits.

She lowered her voice.

"Sefa. Off the record. Did an old man come through? Gray beard, sick, traveling with a woman driving a green truck?"

Something moved behind Sefa's eyes — not surprise exactly, more like a door being tested. "An old man," she repeated. "Lots of old men on this river."

"This one used to work at the station. At the source."

The cat stood up, stretched, and left the room, as if even he understood that the conversation had just changed categories.

Sefa picked up a fish, considered it, set it down. "Why does a survey officer want to know that?"

"Because if certain people find him first, he dies before he gets where he's going. And because—" Wren stopped. She hadn't planned to say the next part. It came out anyway, quiet, into the fish-smelling air. "Because my father went up that river in 'ninety-one and didn't come back, and I've spent half my life being paid not to wonder why. And I'm tired of it."

The shed was silent except for the river, which was always saying something, though nobody had ever successfully transcribed it.

Sefa looked at her for a long moment. Then she reached under the worktable and produced a tin mug and a bottle of something amber and unlabeled, and poured two fingers into each.

"You're either the worst liar they ever sent," Sefa said, handing her a mug, "or the first honest one. Either way, drink. And then I'll tell you what I saw, which was a truck, a coughing man, and a young woman who looked like she was being dragged uphill by hope, which is the heaviest thing there is to carry."

"They went upstream? Toward the pass?"

"Toward the pass." Sefa raised her mug slightly. "And officer — if you're planning to follow them, follow slowly. Some things catch up too fast and ruin everything."

Wren drank. It tasted like pine tar and forgiveness. "I'm planning," she said, "to be extremely slow."

* * *

That night, camped above the flood line with the radio off — officially, a dead battery; actually, a decision — Wren sat by her small fire and let herself remember the day the Optimists found her.

She'd been twenty-three. Her father had been dead eleven months, officially lost in a surveying accident, though the official story had holes in it you could drive a convoy through, and Wren had spent those eleven months driving convoys through them. She'd written letters. Filed requests. Stood in government offices with her mother's good coat buttoned to the throat, asking to see expedition records from the upper Sorrowline, and had been told, with increasing firmness, that no such records existed, that the region was closed, that perhaps she should consider grief counseling, which was available through the provincial office.

Then a man in a cheerful yellow jacket had sat down next to her on a bench outside the records office. He'd offered her coffee. His name had been Voss, though he'd introduced himself as "a friend of people who appreciate thoroughness."

"You've filed eleven requests," he'd said, sipping his coffee. "We counted. Eleven. Do you know what happens to people who file twelve?"

"No," Wren had said.

"Neither do I," Voss had said pleasantly. "That's the remarkable part. Nobody ever has. But here's the thing, Miss Calloway — your father also asked questions. And look how that turned out. Whereas you — you're employable. You're smart, you're licensed, you know that river country better than anyone in the service under forty. And we are an organization that believes in opportunity."

"What kind of opportunity?"

"The auditing kind. Good salary. Flexible ethics." He'd smiled, and his smile had been genuinely warm, which was the horrifying part. "We're called the Optimists, Miss Calloway. We lend people what they need, and we collect what we're owed, and everyone smiles. It's a beautiful system. You'll like it. Everyone likes it right up until they understand it, and some people never understand it at all, and those are the happiest ones."

"And if I refuse?"

Voss had looked at her with real sympathy, which was worse than the smile. "Then you keep filing letters to offices that burn them, and in a year or two you'll do something reckless, like go upriver yourself, and then we'll have this conversation again, except I'll be less friendly and you'll be less free. This way, you get paid. Your mother keeps the house. And you get to be near the river." He'd spread his hands, generous, reasonable. "Everyone wins. That's the whole philosophy. That's why we're cheerful."

She'd taken the job. She told herself it was to stay close to the truth, to work from inside, to find out what happened to her father someday when the moment came. Six years later, she could admit the machinery worked differently: every year inside made the outside harder to see, every favor purchased a little more silence, until the question she'd started with — *what happened up there?* — had become a thing she kept in a drawer instead of a thing she carried.

Compromise, she'd learned, doesn't arrive like a storm. It arrives like weather. Gentle. Daily. Until one morning you can't remember what the sky looked like before.

But now the sky was changing. An old man was walking toward a sealed pass with a stolen history in his pocket, and a woman in debt was driving him there, and Wren had just reported their position forty kilometers wrong and drunk contraband liquor with a fish-dryer who knew too much.

The fire popped. Somewhere downvalley, a truck engine that wasn't hers ground through a low gear — Voss's collectors, moving fast, following the real road she'd steered them off of, or trying to.

Wren pulled out her notebook, tore out the page of official-looking blanks, and fed it to the fire. Then she drew a fresh map by firelight: the goat track, the crossing, the cairn at the mouth of the closed pass. She thought about leaving a note in that cairn. She thought about what a note would cost her.

She thought about her father, who had also once believed he could serve the truth from inside an institution built to bury it.

"Two days," she murmured to the fire, to the river, to whoever was listening. "I'm coming up slow. Don't waste them."

She banked the coals, rolled into her bedroll, and slept the sleep of a woman who had finally, after six years, done one small unforgivable thing — and found, to her enormous surprise, that it fit like her mother's good coat: snug, warm, and buttoned to the throat.

Above her, unseen through the fog, the pass waited. And somewhere on its far side, an old man was running out of time faster than she could run out of lies.
