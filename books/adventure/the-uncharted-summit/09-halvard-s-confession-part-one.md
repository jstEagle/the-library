## 9. Halvard's Confession, Part One

The fever came on the third night above the flood line, and it came the way weather does in the high country—all at once, and with an opinion.

One moment Halvard was sitting up by the fire complaining about my coffee the way he always did, which was to drink three cups of it and then describe each one as a personal insult. The next moment he was listing sideways like a fence post in a gale, and when I caught his shoulder his skin was hot enough to fry an egg on.

"Halvard. Hey. Look at me."

He looked at me. That was the trouble. He looked at me the way you look at a window when what you're really seeing is the view behind it.

"Beautiful evening," he said. "The ice is singing."

It was not a beautiful evening. It was drizzling sideways, and the ice was doing nothing but sit there being ice. But I'd grown up around men who talked to storms, so I knew the tone. This wasn't poetry. This was a man reporting conditions from somewhere else entirely.

I got him into the tent, got the wet layers off him, got the sleeping bag zipped to his chin. He weighed less than he should have. Somewhere between the goat track and here, the old prospector had started coming apart at the seams, and now the seams were going all at once.

"Medicine," I said, digging through the kit. "I've got aspirin and I've got optimism, and frankly neither one is rated for this altitude."

"Save your optimism," he murmured. "You'll need it for the paperwork."

"What paperwork?"

But his eyes had gone somewhere else again, tracking something across the ceiling of the tent that wasn't there, and I sat back on my heels and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the thaw line took my truck: two days' walk to the village below us, four days down to anywhere with a doctor, and a patient who was burning through himself like a signal flare.

I built the fire up. I fed him water in small sips. I wrapped his hands in mine because they were shaking and because, if I'm honest, mine were too.

Around midnight, he started talking.

* * *

Not to me, exactly. Feverish men talk to whoever is nearest, and I had the good fortune—or the misfortune—of being nearest. At first it was fragments, station chatter, the kind of thing you'd expect from an old technician: valve pressures, sample rotations, a woman named Ostergaard who was apparently late returning from the lower array. He apologized to her twice. Then he apologized to someone else, someone whose name he said softly, carefully, the way you set down something breakable.

"Iversen," he said. "Iversen, I told them. I told them the readings were wrong."

The fire popped. My own name sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

"Who are you telling?" I asked, keeping my voice level, the way you do around spooked animals and feverish old men. "Halvard. Who's Iversen?"

His eyes found me then, and for a moment—just a moment—the fever cleared out of them, and what was left underneath was lucid and ancient and terribly tired.

"You know," he said. "Don't you."

"I don't know anything," I said. "That's been my professional condition for years."

He laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and I held the canteen to his lips until the fit passed. When he could speak again, he didn't go back to drifting. He stayed. Whatever wall he'd been holding up between himself and the truth had come down with the fever, and now there was just the truth, sitting in the tent with us like a third traveler.

"My name isn't Halvard," he said.

"Wonderful," I said. "Neither is mine, some days."

"It was. It is. But before that—" He drew a breath that seemed to cost him. "Before that I was Technician Second Class Halvard Voss. Station Nine. Sorrowline headwaters." He watched my face as he said it, watching for the flinch, and when it came he nodded slowly, like a man confirming a diagnosis. "So you've heard of Station Nine."

"Nobody's heard of Station Nine," I said. "That's the point of Station Nine. It's not on any survey. The government maps show a glacier and a warning about unstable terrain, and every kid in the valley towns grows up knowing not to ask why."

"And yet you're driving an old fool up toward it."

"You're paying me to."

"I'm paying you," he said quietly, "to carry something heavier than me."

* * *

I should have pressed him then. A smarter woman would have. Instead I stoked the fire and made him eat half a ration bar, because the fever had him by the throat and I wanted him strong enough to finish whatever he was about to say. There's a particular kind of cowardice in caretaking—you get to feel useful while you delay the thing you're afraid of.

He talked in pieces, the way old men do, circling back, filling gaps, sometimes losing the thread and finding it again somewhere unexpected. I'll give it to you as straight as I can, though even now I'm not sure I have all of it.

Station Nine had been a research station, forty years ago. Officially: glaciology, hydrology, atmospheric monitoring. Unofficially—and here Halvard's voice dropped, though we were alone on a mountainside with nothing to hear us but rain—it was a containment watch.

"There's an aquifer under the headwaters," he said. "Deep one. Old. The surveys found it during the first expedition, and the surveys found something else too—mineralization, chemical signatures, things in the water that shouldn't have been in the water. The government didn't want it studied. They wanted it *managed*. Quietly. So they built the station over it and called it weather research, and for eleven years a few dozen of us lived up there measuring the lid on something we weren't allowed to name."

"Managed how?"

"Pumps. Filters. Monitoring wells. And a schedule." His jaw worked. "There was always a schedule. Drawdowns, pressure releases. You learn not to ask what you're releasing, or where. You learn to read the numbers and not the implications. I was twenty-six years old and I was very good at not asking questions. They gave me a commendation for it."

"And then?"

He was quiet long enough that I thought he'd drifted off. Then: "And then the readings went wrong, and I asked one question too many."

* * *

Here is what he told me, in the small hours, while the fire burned down and the rain kept its rhythm on the canvas:

In the spring of the last year, the monitoring wells showed a shift. Pressure building where it shouldn't build. Contaminant levels climbing in the deep samples—not gradually, the way the manuals promised, but fast, like something had moved. Something had cracked, or shifted, or woken up. Halvard flagged it. He flagged it through channels, and when the channels returned silence, he flagged it louder. And there were others—a hydrologist named Ostergaard, and a field surveyor team working the upper valley, husband and wife, who'd been taking independent readings along the river and had reached the same conclusion from the other direction.

"The water was going to fail," Halvard said. "The containment, the whole arrangement. Downstream towns, the villages on the flood line—everyone was drawing from the Sorrowline. We had weeks, maybe. Iversen—" his voice caught on the name, steadied, went on— "the surveyors compiled everything. Their own data, ours, the lot of it. A full report. Evidence. They were going to take it downriver themselves if the station wouldn't transmit it."

"What happened?"

"The station transmitted something else." He stared into the fire. "A different report. One that said the anomaly was stable, the readings were instrument error, and that the surveyor team had been compromised by—what was the phrase—" his mouth twisted around it— "*environmental psychosis.* Altitude sickness of the mind. Very tidy. Very official. Two weeks later the aquifer let go anyway, earlier than anyone predicted, and the government had a choice: evacuate and admit everything, or seal the records, blame the dead, and drown the valley on purpose."

"They flooded it."

"Controlled release. They called it emergency management. The villages downstream got a day's warning and a lifetime of silence. The station staff were rotated out, debriefed, scattered, and made to understand—with great warmth and many smiles—that our pensions and our families and our continued health depended on our discretion." He said the word *warmth* the way you'd spit out a bad seed. "The surveyors never came down. Officially they were lost to the flood they themselves had caused. Psychotic, negligent, dead by their own hand or their own arrogance. The report blamed them, Zephyrine. The report blamed them for the disaster they died trying to prevent."

The fire had burned to coals. I fed it another stick without looking at my hands, because my hands were not steady.

"Halvard," I said. "These surveyors. The Iversens."

"Yes."

"What were their names?"

He turned his head and looked at me, and I will spend the rest of my life remembering how gently he did it, this dying man delivering a blow he'd apparently been carrying up a mountain for forty years.

"Maren and Aldous Iversen," he said. "And their daughter was three years old the spring they went up the river and didn't come back."

* * *

I don't remember standing up. I remember being outside, in the rain, with no coat, staring at the black shape of the mountain where the pass was, breathing like I'd run there. I remember thinking, absurdly, about the lullaby—the one he hummed at the fire, the one I'd spent two weeks telling myself was a coincidence, some common tune every valley mother sang. I remembered my father's voice singing it off-key. I remembered my mother correcting his key.

When I came back inside, Halvard hadn't moved. He watched me wring out my hair with eyes that were clear now, fully clear, the fever having done its work and burned away whatever was left to hide behind.

"You chose me," I said. "At the gas station. Out of every truck in the valley."

"I chose the only Iversen left," he said. "Yes."

"Why? Why not just give me the map and be done with it? Why drag me up here to hear all this?"

"Because the map isn't the treasure, girl." He fumbled beneath his shirt, at the cord around his neck, and pulled free a small oilskin packet, worn soft as cloth from decades of carrying. He held it against his chest with both hands, like something alive. "Everyone thinks there's gold up there. Let them. Gold keeps people climbing. But this—" he tapped the packet— "this is the original log. Station Nine, unredacted. Every reading, every flag I raised, every order that came down, every transmission they altered. Ostergaard smuggled it out the day before the end and gave it to me because I was the youngest and the fastest and she figured I might live long enough to matter. Forty years I've carried it. Forty years I've been a prospector, a drifter, a cheerful old fool nobody looks at twice. Because the moment anyone looks at what I actually am, I'm a loose end."

"So burn it," I heard myself say. "Bury it. Live."

"Live?" He smiled, and it was the saddest thing I'd seen since the thaw line ate my truck. "Child, I'm dying whether I climb this mountain or not. The doctors gave me a season. What I have left isn't life, it's *errands*. And this is the last one." He pressed the packet flat against his sternum. "Your parents wrote the truth down and died for it. The least I can do is walk it home. The log goes back to the source. To the station. Where it belongs, where the record lives, where someday—when the seals rot and the smiles wear thin—someone will find it and know that Maren and Aldous Iversen were right, and brave, and murdered by paperwork."

"That's it? That's the fortune you promised me? A dead man's diary?"

"That's the only thing worth more than gold," he said. "Testimony. Everything buried was documented, Zephyrine. Everything. They were thorough, but they weren't perfect, and they never counted on a frightened twenty-six-year-old with a good memory and a guilty conscience." His breath rattled. "You want payment? Fine. Here's my last offer, and it's better than money: help me finish the errand, and you'll finally know who you are. Not a debtor. Not a storm chaser running from collectors. An Iversen. Daughter of the two people who tried to save this whole valley while the rest of us kept our heads down and our pensions safe."

I sat down heavily on my bedroll. Outside, the rain was easing, and somewhere above us the glacier creaked like an old house settling.

"The lullaby," I said. "Sing it. All of it. Not humming. Words."

And he did—his voice cracked and feverish and quiet—in a language I hadn't heard since I was three years old, and every word of it landed in me like a stone dropped down a well, falling and falling toward a splash I couldn't hear yet.

When he finished, he was asleep, truly asleep, the fever still riding him but the packet still clutched to his chest. I sat by the fire until dawn, turning the oilskin's shape over in my mind, thinking about debt and currency and what a person is actually worth.

And that was when I saw it—downslope, far off across the dark valley, a light moving where no road ran. Steady. Deliberate. Sweeping side to side, the way you'd search for something.

Someone was coming upstream after us.
