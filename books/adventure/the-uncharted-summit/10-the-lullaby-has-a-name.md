## 10. The Lullaby Has a Name

Morning came to the village the way it came to no place Zephyrine had ever lived: slowly, and without apology.

In the flats, dawn was a transaction—light arriving on schedule to start the machinery of the day. Here, above the flood line of a river that officially did not exist, the sun simply took its time, sliding down the far ridge in long amber bars while mist lifted off the water in ropes. Somewhere below her, someone was already laughing. A dog was arguing with a goose about territory. The whole settlement smelled of woodsmoke and river mud and something baking, which Zephyrine found frankly offensive, because she hadn't been invited.

She sat on the porch of the house they'd been given—the *loaned*, she corrected herself; nobody here seemed to understand lending as anything but an act of grace—and watched Halvard sleep through the window. He'd slept most of yesterday too, fevered, his breathing doing that wet whistle thing she'd stopped pretending not to hear. The elder's granddaughter, a girl of maybe fourteen named Sif with the forearms of someone who hauled nets for a living, had changed his poultices without being asked and refused payment twice, which by Zephyrine's accounting made Sif either a saint or a terrible businesswoman.

The lullaby was still stuck in her head.

That was the trouble. It had been stuck there since the fire two nights ago, when Halvard, half-delirious, had hummed those six descending notes and Zephyrine's spine had gone cold in a way no altitude could explain. She knew the tune. She didn't know from where. It lived somewhere below memory, in the cellar of her, down where the boxes were packed and labeled by other people.

"You didn't sleep," said a voice behind her, in accented but excellent Common.

Maren Voss—the elder, seventy if she was a day, built like a dock piling—came around the corner carrying two cups of something steaming. She handed one over without ceremony. Zephyrine took it. It was tea, or tea's rugged frontier cousin, and it tasted like pine needles had opinions.

"Thank you," Zephyrine said. "And I did sleep. Four hours. That's practically hibernating."

"Mm." Maren settled onto the bench beside her with the groan of a woman who had earned every joint complaint individually. They watched the mist together for a while.

"You hummed it too," Zephyrine said finally. "Last night. When you were checking Halvard's bandages. You thought I was asleep."

"I did not think you were asleep. I thought you were pretending." Maren sipped her tea. "You pretend very loudly."

Zephyrine laughed despite herself—a short, surprised bark—and then the laugh died in her chest, because the lullaby rose up again behind it, six notes, patient as water.

"Where is it from?" she asked. "The song. Halvard won't say. He just smiles at me like I've told him a joke he hasn't gotten to the punchline of yet, and honestly it's starting to feel like a hostage situation."

Maren was quiet for a long moment. Down by the water, the dog appeared to be losing the argument with the goose.

"That song has a name," the old woman said. "Most songs do, even the ones people are forbidden to sing. This one we called *Vuggesang for de Små Kartleggerne*—the Lullaby for the Little Surveyors. The Iversen family wrote it. Or the mother did, anyway. Astrid Iversen. She used to sing it to her daughter on survey trips, back before—" She stopped. Her hands tightened around the cup.

"Before what?" Zephyrine asked, though some cold, clever part of her brain had already begun assembling the answer like a storm front assembling itself over flat country: pressure dropping, light going strange, everything ordinary about to become otherwise.

"Before the Incident," Maren said. "Before they drowned our valley and erased us from the surveys and told the world the Sorrowline began at a spring that has never once been its beginning." She turned and looked at Zephyrine then, fully, and her eyes were pale gray and terribly kind. "You have her face, you know. Astrid's. Around the eyes. I kept thinking all night, where have I seen those eyes, and then this morning I remembered I saw them thirty years ago on a woman who argued with me about fish prices and sang to her little girl while she worked."

The cup was very hot in Zephyrine's hands. She noticed this distantly, the way you notice weather happening to somebody else.

"My parents' names were Astrid and Torvald Iversen," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded fine. Steady, even. Which was suspicious, because nothing inside her was steady; something enormous had shifted underground and the surface just hadn't caught up yet. "They died when I was seven. An expedition. Up a river. Nobody ever told me which river. There was a settlement payout and a lawyer who talked a lot and a file that was sealed, and my aunt raised me and never spoke their names, and I grew up thinking that was grief. Just—grief. The regular kind."

"It isn't the regular kind," Maren said gently. "It's the confiscated kind."

Zephyrine set the cup down before she dropped it. Her hands had started doing something independent of her intentions.

"Halvard," she said. "Halvard knew them. He knew them, and he found me, and he hired me, and he never—he let me think it was about money. He let me think *I* thought it was about money." She stood up, sat down, stood up again. "I've spent three weeks telling myself I took this job out of greed. Clean, simple greed. Greed I can live with. And the whole time—"

"The whole time, a dying man was trying to bring a lost child home," Maren finished. "Yes."

"I'm going to kill him," Zephyrine announced, with feeling. "I'm going to walk in there and strangle a man with pneumonia. It'll be a mercy killing but also a murder. Both charges will apply."

"He would probably find that funny," Maren said. "He always did."

Zephyrine stopped mid-stride toward the door. Turned. "*Always?*"

Maren's mouth did something complicated—grief and amusement sharing a small space, like two people in a coat. "Halvard Brenn came through this valley every summer for eleven years. Station technician, upstream at the source. He'd come down on rest rotation and drink terrible coffee with us and complain about the government's taste in equipment. He carried our mail when the official couriers 'lost' it, which was always. He taught three generations of children to cheat at cards." She paused. "He was there the night the sirens went off. He was one of the ones who tried to stop it."

"*Tried to stop it?*" Zephyrine's voice cracked upward. "Tried to stop *what*? Everyone talks about the Incident like it was weather. Like a rockslide. What actually happened up there?"

Maren looked at her for a long time, and Zephyrine watched an entire decision happen behind the old woman's eyes—the same decision, she suspected, that Halvard had been making and unmaking across a hundred miles of bad road.

"Not from me," Maren said at last. "Not secondhand, and not half. You've waited thirty years without knowing it. You can wait one more conversation." She rose, joints protesting, and nodded toward Halvard's window. "But go wake him now. Because I've watched dying men, girl, and his mornings are getting shorter than his nights, and there are things he's been carrying since before you could read. Let him put them down while he still can."

* * *

Halvard was awake when she came in, propped against pillows Sif had sourced from apparently every household in the village, looking like a map of himself drawn by someone in a hurry—lines everywhere, color wrong, the original contours barely suggested beneath.

"There she is," he rasped, and smiled, and it was the smile she'd been cataloging for weeks as *smug old man* and was only now, with the ground gone soft under her, rereading entirely. "You've been talking to Maren."

"You knew my mother." She kept her voice level. It cost her. "Astrid Iversen. You knew her, and you knew me—or knew of me—and you drove a hard bargain with a woman who didn't know she was driving home to her own name. Say it isn't true. Lie to me well enough and I'll take it."

Halvard closed his eyes. When he opened them they were wet and clear at the same time, which should not be possible and yet.

"I couldn't lie to Astrid either," he said. "Believe me, I tried. She caught me skimming station coffee rations my first month and made me inventory the entire supply shed as penance. Took me four days. She alphabetized it *afterward*, out of pity." His breath whistled. "You sit like her. When you're angry, you sit forward, like you're about to argue with the world and expect it to take notes."

Zephyrine sat down on the edge of the bed because her legs had filed a formal complaint. "Why me?"

"Because you were the only one left." Simple as that, as if it explained everything, which after a moment of horror she realized it did. "Your parents' files were sealed. Their colleagues scattered or silenced. The valley was emptied. But a daughter survives, doesn't she? A daughter grows up chasing storms across the flats with her father's compass habits and her mother's stubbornness, and every few years I'd hear about her—storm chaser, they said, good in bad country, pays her debts." His mouth twisted. "I'm sorry about that last part. I needed someone the Optimists had a hook in. Someone hungry enough to say yes and honest enough to hate herself for it. It was a terrible way to choose a person. It was the only way I had."

"You could have just *told* me."

"Told you what?" He coughed, deep and long, and she hated how long it lasted. "That your parents died in a hole the government buried, on a river that doesn't exist, protecting people who were forbidden to mourn them? With what proof? A story from a dying man is worth less than nothing—it's worth *suspicion*. No. You needed to see the erasure yourself. The roadblocks. The towns that won't say the name. You needed to stand in a village full of living witnesses and feel the shape of the lie." He managed the smile again, weaker. "Besides. Your mother had a rule about treasure maps. Never show the whole route to someone who hasn't walked the first mile."

"She sounds insufferable," Zephyrine said, and her voice broke clean in half on the word, and then she was crying—not prettily, not quietly, but the way storms break over the flats, all at once and sideways, thirty years of confiscated grief arriving with interest. Halvard didn't tell her it was all right. He just reached out one papery hand and rested it on her wrist, and hummed the six notes, and this time she hummed them back, and they fit together like a door fitting its frame.

It took a while. Neither of them counted.

When she could speak again, she wiped her face on her sleeve and said, "The lullaby. Maren said your mother—" she stopped, corrected, "said *my grandmother*—" she stopped again, laughing wetly at herself, "apparently I need a genealogy chart—said it was written for surveyors' children. For me. How do you know the end of it? My version stops at six notes. That's all I ever had."

Something moved across Halvard's face—guilt, tenderness, purpose, all arriving at once like collectors at a door.

"Astrid wrote more verses than anyone ever heard," he said. "She'd add one every season, about whatever the valley had done that year—the flood year, the wolf winter, the year the aurora came south. She said a lullaby should grow with the child." His grip on her wrist firmed. "There's a last verse, Zephie. She wrote it the final summer, and she made me promise—if anything happened, if the truth ever got the chance to come home—I'd teach it to her daughter. On the mountain. At the source. She said—" his voice frayed, held, "she said the song shouldn't end until the valley does mourning properly. Until the names come back."

Zephyrine stared at him. Outside, the mist was burning off the river, and the dog appeared to have negotiated a truce with the goose, and the whole impossible village went about its morning as if the world hadn't just tilted.

"You're waiting until we get there," she said. "To teach it to me."

"I'm a dying man with one verse left," Halvard said. "I'm allowed exactly one piece of leverage, and I intend to use it shamelessly. Now help me up. Maren's making breakfast, and if you don't eat, she'll take it personally, and that woman has held grudges across decades—I've seen it done."

Zephyrine helped him sit upright, careful of the poultices, careful of everything. And as she steadied him, she felt it settle into her chest—not peace, not yet, but direction, the way a river finds its line downhill. Whatever was buried at the source of the Sorrowline, she wasn't walking toward treasure anymore.

She was walking home to collect a debt owed to her family for thirty years.

She was almost to the door when Sif burst in, breathless, cheeks red from running.

"There's a stranger on the far trail," the girl said. "A woman, alone, with survey gear. She's asking after a truck and two travelers." Sif swallowed. "She says her name is Calloway."
