## 11. Collectors at the Crossing

### Wren

The bridge was older than Wren's career, which was saying something, because her career had been built on paperwork so old it crumbled when you sneezed near it.

It hung across the Sorrowline where the gorge narrowed to a throat—two cables gone brown with rust, a deck of slats that had been replaced, apparently, whenever someone had a spare plank and a free afternoon. The far side climbed into pine shadow. The near side was where she stood, holding a signal flare in one hand like a cigarette she'd decided against smoking.

The Optimists' instructions had been simple, the way everything about the Optimists was simple: cheerful voices, simple terms, ruinous arithmetic. *When you have visual confirmation of the Iversen woman and the old man, light the red flare. Our people are forty minutes behind you.*

Forty minutes. She'd checked her watch four times in the last ten.

Below her, the river ran green-white over stones, loud enough that she could pretend she couldn't hear her own thoughts. That was the trick of rivers, she'd learned as a girl. They gave you something to blame the noise on.

She thought about her father's boots, sitting by the door for eleven years after the expedition upriver failed to come back, until her mother finally gave them away and cried for a week and never said why. She thought about the letter the Optimists' man had slid across a diner table seven years ago—you look like a girl who understands debt—and the number he'd named, and how she'd signed because the alternative was watching them take the house, and how every year since, the work had gotten a little less defensible and a little more familiar, the way rot does.

And she thought about Zephyrine Iversen's face two nights ago at the fire, when Halvard hummed that lullaby and Zephyrine went still as a held breath.

*You know it,* Wren had almost said. *I know it too. My father used to sing it wrong on purpose to make my mother laugh.*

She hadn't said it. Some things you keep in your mouth like a hot stone, testing whether you can speak around them.

The wind came up the gorge, smelling of snowmelt and pine sap and, faintly, of rust. Somewhere upstream—twenty minutes back, maybe twenty-five—the truck tracks ended at a collapsed cutbank, and from there it was footprints, and footprints didn't lie about direction. Two sets. One dragging.

One dragging. Halvard was failing. Anyone could see it. Wren had seen it from fifty yards through binoculars and felt something in her chest she refused to name, because naming it would make it actionable, and actionable things got people arrested.

"Come on," she murmured to the bridge, to the river, to nobody. "Be slow. Be slow."

Because here was the thing she'd been circling for three days like a moth around a bulb: if she lit the flare, they would come. Four men in cheerful jackets with cheerful voices and a cheerful little contract clause about *asset recovery by any reasonable means*, and what counted as reasonable had a way of expanding when the asset was worth millions. If she lit the flare, Halvard—a dying man carrying the only unredacted record of the thing that had eaten her father's expedition—would be taken apart politely, in an organized fashion, with receipts.

And if she didn't light it—

If she didn't light it, there was no going back to being the kind of person who lit flares.

A stone clacked somewhere up the trail. Wren's whole body went to attention before her brain caught up, and then she saw them: figures at the tree line. Not the collectors. Not yet.

Zephyrine first, moving with that long flat stride of someone who'd spent years walking away from things that wanted money from her. Then Halvard, leaning on a staff cut from green alder, each step placed like a man crossing ice. And behind them both, half-hidden in the pines, a third figure—an old woman with river-people braids, who stopped at the edge of the trees and did not come out onto the open ground.

They hadn't seen her yet. The wind was in their faces; the river ate her footsteps.

Wren looked down at the flare.

*Red means come,* the Optimist man had said, tapping the tube against his knuckles, smiling like the sun coming up over a foreclosure. *Red is the friendliest color there is.*

She turned it over in her hand. The striker cap sat under her thumb.

Somewhere behind her, forty minutes out and closing, were men who trusted her. That was the worst part. They trusted her completely, because she had been reliable for seven years, and reliability is just betrayal with good attendance.

Zephyrine reached the head of the bridge and stopped, scanning the far bank, and for one absurd moment Wren had the wild impulse to wave.

Then Zephyrine saw her.

Wren watched it happen—the recognition, the calculation, the hand drifting toward the knife at her belt—and raised both palms, empty, the flare held up between two fingers like a fish she was releasing.

"Don't," she called over the water. "Please. Just—don't."

* * *

### Zephyrine

Of all the faces Zephyrine had expected on the far side of a rope bridge in the middle of nowhere—collectors, rangers, God, possibly a bear wearing a ranger's hat—she had not bet on Wren Calloway standing alone with her hands up and a signal flare pinched between her fingers like something dead she'd found on the beach.

"Halvard," Zephyrine said quietly, without turning her head. "Behind me. Slow."

"I'm always slow now," Halvard said, wheezing. "It's my one consistent virtue."

The old woman from the village—Maren, she was called, though she'd told them names meant little above the flood line—melted back into the trees. Smart. One of them being visible was a tragedy waiting; two was a massacre.

Zephyrine stepped onto the first plank. It bowed under her boot with a sound like an old man clearing his throat.

"That bridge has killed better people than either of us," Calloway called across the gap. "The middle three slats are rotten. Step wide, left side."

"You're giving me bridge safety tips."

"I'm giving you *left side* tips. There's a difference and it matters enormously in about nine seconds."

Zephyrine hated that she almost smiled. She kept her eyes on the officer instead—on the flare, mostly, because a flare in a hand was a decision not yet made, and Zephyrine had built a life on reading decisions not yet made. Collectors taught you that. Debt taught you that. You learned to watch the hand, not the smile.

"Why aren't they with you?" she called. "Your cheerful friends. The ones who drive like the invoice depends on it."

"They're forty minutes back." Calloway's voice didn't waver, but her thumb moved on the flare cap, a small restless circle. "There was a washout on the logging spur. They had to double back."

"Lucky washout."

"There's no such thing," Calloway said. "I reported it."

The words dropped into the roar of the river and Zephyrine stood very still on the swaying planks, one hand on the cold cable, and let that sentence rearrange the furniture in her head.

"You reported a washout that didn't exist."

"I reported a washout that *hasn't happened yet*," Calloway corrected, with the pedantic precision of a career bureaucrat committing her first crime. "There's a difference and—" she stopped herself, huffed something that might have been a laugh aimed at herself, and shook her head. "God. I've been doing this too long. Everything sounds like a permit violation."

Halvard came up beside Zephyrine on the bank, breathing in the shallow careful way that had gotten worse every day since the pass. He looked across the gorge at Wren Calloway for a long moment.

"Calloway," he rasped. "Joss Calloway's girl."

Zephyrine felt the name land in her own chest like a dropped tool. *Calloway.* A name off the lost expedition roster, a name from the memorial-that-wasn't, a name her mother had once said softly over dishes the way you say the names of the drowned.

Wren's jaw tightened. "You knew my father."

"I knew everyone on that mountain," Halvard said. "That's rather the problem."

For a moment nobody spoke except the river, which had opinions about everything and shared them constantly.

"The flare," Zephyrine said. "What's it for?"

"It's for signaling my employers to your exact position so they can recover the asset." Calloway said it flatly, like reading a citation. "That's the job. That's been the job for seven years, and I've done it well, and I hate myself in a way I've gotten very efficient at managing."

"And now?"

Calloway looked down at the flare. Then past Zephyrine, at Halvard—at whatever she saw in his face, which Zephyrine suspected was the same thing the elder Maren had seen: a ghost, a witness, a man carrying paper that weighed more than he did.

"Now," Calloway said, "I'm going to do something stupid."

### Wren

Here was the thing about betrayals: nobody warned you how quiet they were.

Wren had imagined this moment, in the bad hours, in the truck cab with the heater ticking—invariably she imagined shouting, guns, some cinematic rupture. Instead she stood alone at a rope bridge with a river roaring below her, unscrewed the cap off a signal flare, and discovered that the entire moral crisis of her adult life fit in a cardboard tube six inches long.

Zephyrine was halfway across the bridge now, moving left-side-wide exactly as instructed, because even enemies took your safety advice when you delivered it correctly, and there was something in that which made Wren want to laugh and also sit down in the gravel and cry.

Halvard stood at the near bank, leaning on his alder staff, watching her with eyes gone pale and patient as winter light.

"Your father sang that lullaby wrong on purpose," Halvard called across the gap, in the tone of a man making small talk about weather. "Drove your mother mad. Said the real words were too sad for a kitchen."

Wren's breath left her all at once.

"He told you that?"

"He told everyone that. Joss Calloway never met a sad thing he didn't try to fix with a joke." Halvard coughed, deep and wet, and steadied himself. "He'd have liked what you're about to do. He'd have joked about it first. But he'd have liked it."

The cable hummed under Zephyrine's weight. Three slats from the far side. Two.

Wren struck the flare.

It hissed alive in her fist, red and furious, spitting sparks—and she turned, wound up like a girl throwing a fastball at the county fair, and hurled it out over the gorge, high and arcing, a comet in reverse.

It hit the Sorrowline with a hiss like the world's largest kettle being taken off the world's largest stove, and sank, still burning, a red heart beating down through the green water until the current folded over it and put it out.

Silence, except the river.

"Well," Zephyrine said from the last plank. "That's one way to file a report."

"Position confirmed," Wren said. Her voice came out strange and light, unburdened and terrified in equal measure. "Signal deployed. Asset location: the river. Let them dig it out of the hydrology records."

Zephyrine stepped off the bridge onto solid ground, close enough now that Wren could see the exhaustion carved under her eyes, the split knuckles, the way she stood slightly angled to keep Halvard behind her shoulder. Old habit. Bodyguard geometry from a woman who claimed she was only in it for the money.

"You understand what you just did," Zephyrine said. It wasn't a question. It was a warning, or maybe an offering.

"I falsified a government report, impersonated a survey audit, and destroyed Optimist property valued at approximately eleven dollars." Wren ticked them off on her fingers. "The flare was departmental issue. I'll be invoiced."

"Glad you're keeping perspective."

"One of us has to. You've been perspective-free since the gas station."

Halvard came across the bridge slowly, plank by plank, refusing Zephyrine's offered arm with the gentle stubbornness of a man who intended to walk into his grave upright. When he reached the far bank he stopped in front of Wren, and up close she could see how thin he'd become, how the mountain was already halfway through taking him back.

"Joss's girl," he said again, softer. "I'm sorry I waited so long to come home. All of us were sorry. We just weren't brave enough to say it while it could still help."

Wren found she couldn't answer. She nodded instead, once, the way you nod at a grave.

Then, because the silence was becoming a place she couldn't afford to live in: "They'll figure it out within the hour. The washout report won't hold once they reach the spur and find it passable. And when it doesn't hold, they'll stop trusting my coordinates, and then they'll stop being polite." She pulled off her cap, dragged a hand through her hair, put the cap back on crooked. "There's a route up the east bank. Goat track, then scree, then the closed pass. It avoids the sensor line if you cross below the second cairn—there's a gap in the tripwire grid, maintenance access, it's not on the public schematics but it's on mine."

"You're coming with us," Zephyrine said.

It wasn't a question either, and Wren opened her mouth to produce some bureaucratic objection—unauthorized escort, liability, form 12-B—and discovered she didn't have one. Seven years of forms, and not a single one covered this.

"I'm coming with you," she agreed.

"Good." Zephyrine shouldered her pack and glanced back at the bridge, at the far shore, at the direction from which four cheerful men in cheerful jackets were even now discovering that the river does not accept deliveries. "Because I know that look you get when you're doing math in your head, and I want you doing it where I can see you."

"I don't have a look."

"You absolutely have a look. It's the look of a woman about to commit her second crime of the day."

Wren considered denying it. Then, downstream, faint through the trees, came the sound she'd been dreading: an engine, gunning hard, taking the spur road at a speed no permit had ever authorized.

The math, as it turned out, was already done.

"East bank," Wren said, and pointed uphill. "Run."

* * *

They ran.

Behind them the engine noise swelled and broke around the bend of the gorge, and a voice—bright, breezy, horribly pleasant—carried across the water:

*"Ma'am! Officer Calloway! Lovely weather for a hike—we just have a few signatures for you!"*

Ahead of them the goat track switchbacked up into the pines, toward the scree, toward the closed pass, toward the source of the Sorrowline and everything buried there.

Halvard laughed once, breathless and delighted, like a boy at a fair.

"They always did sound happy," he said. "Right up until the end."
