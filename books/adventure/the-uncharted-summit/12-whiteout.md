## 12. Whiteout

The storm came down on us like a debt collector—no warning, no negotiation, just sudden and total.

One hour we were picking our way along a shelf of scree above the frozen river, the sky a hard clean blue that Halvard kept squinting at with suspicion. The next, the blue curdled white, the wind arrived from every direction at once, and the world shrank to a circle of flailing arms-length visibility with me in the middle of it, shouting Halvard's name into a wall of snow.

"Here," his voice said, from somewhere left of nowhere.

I found him by grabbing wool. He was bent double against the wind, coughing in that deep wet way I'd stopped commenting on because commenting didn't help either of us.

"Cave!" I shouted. "There has to be a cave!"

"There's always a cave!" he shouted back, which struck me as the kind of optimism that gets people killed, except that ten minutes later, half-blind and swearing, I fell through a gap between two boulders into exactly that—a hollow under an overhang of granite, floored with old drift, big enough for two people who had recently stopped being picky about personal space.

I got him inside, unpacked the stove, and lit it with hands that wouldn't stop shaking until the flame caught. Outside, the storm thickened from weather into a fact of geology. It sounded like the mountain itself had started talking, low and continuous, saying something enormous that I couldn't quite make out.

"Well," Halvard said, settling back against the rock with the sigh of a man checking into a hotel he'd been dreading. "That's us pinned."

"For how long?"

He tilted his head, listening to the wind the way other people read newspapers. "Two days. Maybe three, if she's feeling dramatic."

"She? The storm has a gender now?"

"All storms do. This one's a woman scorned." He pulled his blanket around his shoulders. "Best thing we can do is stay out of her way and let her finish shouting."

I checked our supplies, mostly so my hands would have something to do while my stomach did arithmetic. Two days of fuel. Food for four days if we were careful, three if we were honest. Water we could have by the bucketful, if we were willing to melt snow, which we were, because the alternative was dying.

"We're fine," I told him.

"You're lying beautifully," he said. "Your mother lied the same way. Terrible poker face, magnificent delivery."

I looked up. He was watching me with those faded eyes that saw too much, and there it was again—that flicker of familiarity, that sense that he knew things about my family that I hadn't given him and couldn't account for any other way.

"Eat your soup," I said.

* * *

The first night was the worst, or I thought it was, until the second night outdid it.

Cold does something strange to time. It doesn't pass; it pools. We took turns sleeping, though sleep in a snow cave is less rest than rehearsal for death, and I spent most of my watch listening to Halvard breathe and cataloguing the sounds he made—the rattle low in his chest, the pauses that lasted a beat too long.

Around what I estimated was midnight, real time having abandoned us entirely, he started talking. Not to me, exactly. To the fire, maybe. To the dark.

"The station had a hum," he said. "You'd forget about it, living there. Then you'd go outside for a shift and the silence would hit you like a wall, and you'd realize you'd been living inside a machine for months. A friendly machine. But a machine."

I fed the stove another tablet and said nothing, because I'd learned by then that Halvard talked when you let him, and stopped when you asked.

"Everyone hummed back at it, eventually. Little tuneless thing. You'd catch yourself doing it in the shower." He smiled at the flames. "Marta used to say the station was teaching us its own lullaby."

My chest went tight. "Marta."

"Marta Iversen." He said it easily, plainly, the way you say the name of someone you've carried for thirty years. "Lead hydrologist. Sharpest mind on the mountain and the only person who could make Dr. Osei laugh before eight in the morning." He glanced at me. "She had a daughter. Four years old, down in the valley with the grandmother. Marta used to sing to her over the radio line on Sundays. Bad reception. She'd sing anyway."

The wind screamed past the mouth of the cave. I put another fuel tablet in the stove, although it didn't need one.

"What happened to the daughter?" I asked, in a voice I barely recognized as mine.

He was quiet long enough that I thought he'd fallen asleep sitting up. Then: "She grew up. Chases storms, I hear. Runs up debts. Picks up dying old men off gas station forecourts because some part of her never learned to leave people behind in bad weather." His eyes found mine across the small orange light. "Hello, Zephyrine."

* * *

I didn't sleep after that. I sat with my back against the rock and made myself breathe slowly while the storm shouted its enormous unfinished sentence outside, and somewhere around the coldest hour I realized I wasn't angry. That surprised me. I kept waiting for the anger, the way you wait for a sneeze. It didn't come. What came instead was a strange, cold clarity, like the moment on a summit when the cloud drops away and you see how far you've already climbed without noticing.

"You chose me," I said finally. "At the gas station. It wasn't luck. You were looking for me."

"I was looking for anyone with a truck and no sense," Halvard said. "You were simply the best qualified."

"Halvard."

"Yes, all right." He shifted, wincing, and I saw how much the simple act of moving cost him now, how much thinner he'd gone in six days on the mountain. "Yes. I chose you. Your mother saved my life twice—once in a flood, once from my own stupidity, which was the harder rescue. When the letters came back undeliverable, year after year, when they told us the families had been relocated and records were sealed—" He stopped. Coughed. Started again, quieter. "I promised her I'd see the truth surfaced someday. And when my doctor gave me a number instead of a plan, I thought: the truth needs legs. Young ones. Stubborn ones. Preferably ones that owe money to terrible people, because such a person cannot afford to quit."

"That's a horrible reason to trust someone."

"It's the only reliable one. Desperation makes people honest." He smiled. "Besides, you sang the lullaby back to me at the fire, that first night. Half of it, wrong notes and all. Nobody sings that song anymore. Nobody alive knows it." He closed his eyes. "Except us."

I turned toward the cave wall so he wouldn't see my face do whatever it was doing. The stone was freezing and I pressed my forehead against it anyway, letting the cold hold me together.

"Tell me about the station," I said. "Tell me all of it. We've got two days and nothing else to spend them on."

* * *

So he told me. Not all at once—that was the thing about Halvard's story, it came in pieces, like ice breaking off a glacier, and you had to float alongside and gather what drifted near.

The station, he said, had been called Meridian Deep Ice Core Facility, which everyone shortened to Meridian because government names are designed to be forgotten. Forty-one staff, winter and summer rotations, drilling cores out of the summit glacier to read the climate record going back nine thousand years. "Boring, holy work," he said. "We were reading the mountain's diary. You don't expect the diary to bite you."

The first fragment: a core, deep down, that came up wrong. Not wrong scientifically—wrong chemically. Something locked in the ancient ice at depth, something that reacted when it met modern air.

"The lab techs started calling it the Sorrow," Halvard said. "Because of what it did to the instruments. Made them read grief, Marta said. Made every gauge on the bench droop like a funeral." He laughed, a small dry sound with no humor in it. "Jokes are how you survive a research posting. Nobody jokes about a problem unless it's already too big to solve."

Second fragment: the order from below. Not from the science directorate—from security. Seal the core. Stop publication. Continue drilling.

"Why would security care about ice?" I asked.

"Because somebody above us had known for years," Halvard said. "Somebody had drilled here before us, quietly, and buried what they found, and our little academic expedition had gone and stuck a straw directly into it." His voice had gone flat, recitation-flat, the voice of a man who has rehearsed this testimony alone for decades. "They didn't want data. They wanted containment. And containment, when it failed—and it failed, Zeph, on March ninth, at 3:40 in the morning—it failed in a way that could not be allowed to have witnesses downstream."

Third fragment, and this one he almost didn't give me. I got it at dawn on the second day, when the storm paused for breath and the light through the cave mouth went pearl-gray, and Halvard lay so still I checked twice to make sure he was breathing.

"The flood wasn't natural," he said. "The river was redirected. Deliberately. Upstream channels blown, the valley drowned, six villages gone under water in a single afternoon. The official report called it a glacial outburst. An act of God." He stared at the ceiling of the cave. "It was an act of paperwork. There was a signature. I've seen it. I copied the log before they sealed the vault—all forty pages of unredacted entries—and I have spent thirty-one years keeping it dry and keeping my mouth shut, because the men who signed it were not men you survived disagreeing with."

"And my parents?" My voice cracked on it. "The official word was that their survey team got caught in the outburst. That's what my grandmother was told. That's what I grew up with—a weather accident."

Halvard turned his head and looked at me for a long moment, and in the gray light his face was all angles and shadow, older than old.

"They weren't caught in anything," he said. "Your father got the evacuation warning out to three of the six villages. Three. Hundreds of people walked uphill ahead of the water because Torben Iversen broke into the comms room and disobeyed a direct order. Marta stayed on the radio with him until the line went down." He swallowed. "The villages that heard the warning are the villages whose descendants are camped above the flood line right now, waiting for someone to bring the proof home. Your parents didn't die in the disaster, Zeph. They died *opposing* it. And then their names were erased along with everything else."

The stove hissed. The wind resumed its sentence. I sat very still and felt something in my chest break open—not painfully, exactly. More like a window finally opening in a house shut up for thirty years, letting air into rooms that had been waiting.

"They were heroes," I said stupidly.

"They were inconvenient," Halvard corrected gently. "Which history keeps mistaking for the same thing, until somebody brings the receipts."

* * *

We passed the second day the way prisoners pass sentences: in small ceremonies. I melted snow and made tea too weak to insult. I inventoried the food again and got the same answer. I wrapped Halvard's feet in the spare blanket and massaged warmth back into his fingers, which frightened me more than I let show—they were cold in a way that didn't come back properly anymore.

"Tell me something good," he said, when the silence got heavy. "Not the story. Something from your life. I've been carrying mine for thirty years; it's your turn to carry the conversation."

So I told him about my first supercell, age nineteen, chasing it across the flats in a borrowed car with a boy named Denny who cried the entire time and later became an insurance adjustor, which Halvard found so funny he had to stop and cough. I told him about the year I slept in my truck outside a weather service office to be first in line for a contract. I told him about the Optimists, and he listened without flinching, even at the parts where I'd done things I wasn't proud of to keep the collectors smiling.

"You know what I hear?" he said, when I ran out. "I hear a woman who's been paying rent on someone else's idea of her worth. Debt's just a story with numbers in it, Zeph. Some stories you pay off. Some stories you burn."

"Easy for a man with nothing left to lose."

"Oh, I've plenty to lose." He patted his breast pocket, where the folded log rode against his heart like a second heartbeat. "Thirty-one years of losing would've been for nothing. That's a fortune, girl. Bigger than anything the cheerful men could forgive."

Outside, the storm began, very gradually, to lose its temper. By late afternoon the shriek dropped to a mutter, and by dusk I could see the shape of the wind instead of just hearing it. I crawled to the cave mouth and looked out at a world remade—drifts sculpted into impossible arches, the stars coming out hard and close overhead, the whole mountain holding its breath under fresh white silence.

Behind me, Halvard was humming. The lullaby. All of it now, beginning to end, and I realized with a jolt that I'd never actually heard the ending before—that the version my grandmother sang had trailed off unfinished, and I'd assumed that was the song.

"Halvard," I said. "How does it end?"

He smiled and patted the snow beside him, and I came and sat, and he taught me the last verse in a whisper, note by note, like handing over something fragile. It was a name. The last line of the lullaby was a name, sung soft, the way you'd sing a child home.

When I had it right, he nodded once, satisfied, and closed his eyes.

"Rest," he said. "Tomorrow night we cross the closed pass. And Zeph—" He opened his eyes again, and there was something in them I hadn't seen before. Not fear. Readiness. "If you find a cairn at the treeline with a red ribbon on it, look inside. Someone left us a gift."

"A gift from who?"

But he was already asleep, breathing shallow and steady, and outside the stars wheeled slowly over the pass we'd have to cross in the dark, past the rusted signs and the sensors, into the last stretch of country between us and the source of the Sorrowline.
