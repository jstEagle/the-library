## 14. The Pass the Government Closed

The elder's name was Maren, and she fed us like we were going to war instead of over a mountain.

"Rice," she said, pressing a third helping into Halvard's bowl before he'd finished the second. "Fat with butter. You'll thank me at four thousand meters when your legs are lying to you."

"I'm already thanking you," Halvard said, and coughed, and lied about it being nothing, which by now was practically a prayer between us.

Maren's house sat above the flood line on stilts older than either of us, its floorboards worn smooth by three generations of feet that official maps insisted had never existed. Outside, the Sorrowline muttered through the dark like a man rehearsing an argument he'd already lost. Somewhere downriver—two days, maybe three—the collectors were regrouping around whatever Wren Calloway had or hadn't told them.

I kept thinking about Wren. I kept trying not to.

"Here." Maren set a bundle on the table: dried fish wrapped in oilcloth, a tin of matches, and a strip of jerky so dense it could have been used as ballast. "And this." A folded paper, soft at the creases from handling.

I opened it. Handwriting, small and slanted leftward, the letters of someone who'd learned to write in moving vehicles:

*The pass sensors run a sweep every ninety seconds along the marked line only. Stay west of the third cairn until the switchback, then cross fast during the gap after the horn — they test the horn at midnight and 4 a.m., and the sweep resets on the horn. Rust-red sign at the top is live wire. It is not decorative. Do not touch it even to steady yourself. — W.C.*

I read it twice. "She left this *when?*"

"Three days ago," Maren said. "Came up the river alone, wearing that survey coat like it was armor. Asked more questions than she answered. Left that in the cairn by the footbridge." She paused, and her eyes did something complicated. "She also asked about your parents. The Iversens."

The fire popped. Halvard stopped chewing.

"What did you tell her?"

"The truth," Maren said simply. "That they tried to warn us. That some of us listened too late." She took the paper back, folded it along its worn lines, and pressed it into my hand. "A woman who walks two days upstream to leave instructions for strangers she's supposed to be hunting—that woman is not your enemy. Or she is, but she hates it, which is nearly the same thing."

"She works for the Optimists," I said. "Or she did. Or she does and wishes she didn't. I genuinely don't know anymore."

"Nobody knows what anybody is," Maren said, packing the rice. "That's why we watch their hands instead of their words. Her hands wrote you a map through the tripwires." She shrugged. "Judge the handwriting."

* * *

We left at dusk, because Wren's note said the sweeps were thinnest in the dark and because Halvard could no longer walk in daylight heat without his lungs filing formal complaints.

He was worse. I'd been cataloguing it for days the way you catalogue a leaking roof—small entries, each one deniable, the total unbearable. The cough had gone wet. His color had gone the gray of old snow. He walked with a pole Maren's grandson had cut for him and he made jokes about it—"lighter than my first wife"—and I laughed every time, because what else do you do.

"You could still turn back," I said, for maybe the hundredth time, as the village lights shrank behind us.

"And miss the view?" He gestured ahead, where the pass cut through the range like a wound that had never quite healed shut. Even in the last light I could see the signage: rusted posts leaning at angles of surrender, and one large plate, red once, now the color of dried blood, bearing letters the weather had eaten down to suggestions: *CL—D BY OR—R OF TH— D—PARTM—NT.*

"They didn't even bother with a full sentence," I said.

"Closure notices are expensive," Halvard wheezed. "Consonants especially."

We climbed. The trail was goat logic made stone—switchbacks stacked on switchbacks, each turn promising a summit and delivering another turn. Above us the mountains held the last light like a secret. Below, the valley filled with blue shadow, and somewhere in that shadow the river ran backward toward everything we'd left.

At the third cairn I checked Wren's note against the terrain. West of it, she'd written. The marked line—the government's line, the swept line—ran east along a graded shelf where the ground had been leveled decades ago by machines nobody admitted to sending. Even in the dark you could feel the difference underfoot: the official path was smooth as a scar; ours was honest and terrible.

"Horn at midnight," I murmured. "Sweep resets on the horn. We cross during the gap."

"You've memorized it," Halvard said.

"Three times."

"That's twice more than you ever memorized anything for school."

"School never tried to kill me if I got the timing wrong."

"Ah," said Halvard. "Motivation."

* * *

Midnight came up the valley as a sound first—a single flat horn note from somewhere below the pass, mechanical and lonely, like the mountain clearing its throat before reciting something it hated. Then silence. Then, faintly, a hum I felt in my back teeth rather than heard: the sweep, running its patient electronic fingers along the official line.

"Now," I whispered, and we crossed.

Fast, Wren had written, and I finally understood why. The open stretch between the western scree and the eastern wall was maybe eighty meters of ground that had been graded flat and then abandoned to the weather, and crossing it felt like walking across a stage mid-performance with the whole auditorium dark. Every step I expected light. Every step, nothing. Just the hum behind us, the crunch of frost under our boots, and Halvard's breath sawing beside me like a man politely refusing to admit he was drowning.

Halfway across, he stumbled.

I caught his arm. He weighed almost nothing. That was the part that frightened me—not the stumble, but how little there was of him left to catch.

"Keep going," he said. "Don't you dare make it a moment."

"It's not a moment."

"You're making the face."

"What face?"

"The face your mother made," he said, steadying himself on my shoulder, "every time I told her the equipment readings were fine and she knew I was rounding down."

My heart did something unprofessional. "You knew her readings?"

"I knew all her readings." He pushed off my shoulder and walked on. "Later. When there's a roof and a fire and I'm not busy humiliating myself on government gravel."

We reached the eastern wall. Behind us the hum continued its rounds, oblivious, having counted nothing. Ahead, the trail bent upward into black rock, and the rusted signage began again in earnest—posts every fifty meters, and wires strung between them, glinting faintly where the starlight caught them.

Wren's voice in my head: *the rust-red sign at the top is live wire. It is not decorative.*

"Touch nothing that looks official," I breathed.

"In this country," Halvard said, "that's most things."

* * *

We climbed through the small hours. The cold came down off the icefields in layers, each one more sincere than the last. By two in the morning my fingers had filed for independence and Halvard had stopped joking, which scared me more than any sensor.

We stopped in the lee of a boulder the size of a chapel, and I built the smallest legal fire—Maren's matches, a fistful of scrub pine—and we thawed in shifts, sharing the jerky and passing the silence back and forth like a flask.

"Tell me about her readings," I said eventually.

Halvard looked into the fire for a long time. Above us the pass walls rose sheer, and the stars came down to meet them, and for a moment the whole world seemed to be holding its breath along with him.

"Your mother ran the hydrology suite at the station," he said. "Elin Iversen. Sharpest instrument up there, and I include the seismographs. She noticed things. That was her whole gift—noticing things and then being unable to stop talking about them, which is a gift and a curse in roughly equal measure, as I believe you may have inherited."

I let that land. "What did she notice?"

"Pressure anomalies in the containment aquifer. Numbers that only made sense if the mountain was doing something the reports said it couldn't do." He turned his hands over the fire. "She brought them to the section chief. The section chief brought them to the ministry. And the ministry sent back a directive that can be summarized, in the spirit of the signage, as: *CL—SE UP.*"

"Elin didn't close up."

"Elin," Halvard said, with a tenderness that undid me slightly, "started writing her own report. Your father started carrying it to people downstream. And I started making copies of everything, because I was the technician and technicians are invisible, which is the only superpower the lowborn ever get." He coughed, wet and long, and waved off my hand. "They went down the valley to warn the villages themselves, the week before. Officially they were on leave. Unofficially they were committing treason with a clipboard."

"And then the Incident."

"And then the Incident." He stared at the fire. "Which was not an accident, Zephyrine. Which was a failure they knew was coming and chose to manage with water instead of warnings. The valley flooded. The records sealed. The dead assigned convenient causes." His jaw worked. "I stayed at the station. I was told to stay. I have spent forty years knowing exactly what staying cost."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above us, wind moved through the pass with a sound like distant applause—for what, I couldn't say.

"Why me?" I asked quietly. "Out of every truck at every gas station. Why mine?"

Halvard smiled, and in the firelight he looked briefly forty years younger and infinitely tired. "Because you hummed," he said. "Back at the station, when you thought I wasn't listening. You hummed a lullaby you claimed not to know the name of." He looked at me. "Elin used to sing it to the river gauges when they misbehaved. Said everything responds better to a song than a calibration. I'd know that tune anywhere, child. I've been looking for someone who carried it for a very long time."

I didn't say anything. There wasn't anything in me that could come out as words. So I put another branch on the fire, and Halvard pretended not to notice that my eyes had gone unreliable, and the mountain stood around us keeping its old secrets, minus one.

* * *

An hour before dawn, we found the second cairn.

It sat just off the trail at the head of the final switchback, and someone had built it recently—I could tell by the fresh mortar of snow packed between stones that hadn't weathered yet. My stomach dropped before my brain caught up.

"Wren," Halvard said.

I dismantled the top three stones with numb fingers. Inside, wrapped in waxed paper against the damp, a second note. Same slanted handwriting, hurried now, the letters leaning like runners coming into a finish:

*Sweep schedule changed as of yesterday — new rotation, 60 seconds, no horn. Trust the shadows, not the timings. The red sign at the summit is dead now; they cut power Tuesday. But the cabin below the ridge is manned. One man, half asleep, radio check at 5:15. Go wide right, through the rocks, and you'll be behind him. I will be at the source before you. Don't ask how. Burn this. — W.C.*

I read it three times. Halvard read it once, slowly, lips moving.

"She's ahead of us," he said.

"She's *ahead of us.*" I held the paper over the fire and watched it curl. "How is she ahead of us? She was downriver. Two days ago she was downriver being interrogated by men with cheerful ties."

"Maybe she wasn't," Halvard said.

"Maybe she—" I stopped. Reran the last week. The roadblock that had waved us through with suspicious boredom. The collectors who always seemed to arrive an hour late and a lie short. The flare, according to the river-people, that had burned out over the water instead of up the trail. "She's been running interference the whole way. Since the bridge. Maybe since before the bridge."

"Since the gas station," Halvard said mildly. "You really think a survey officer audits permits in a place with no permits?"

I sat with that. The fire ate the last corner of the note, and Wren Calloway's handwriting went up in a breath of orange, and I realized that some part of me had already decided to trust her and had simply been waiting for the evidence to catch up.

"One man in a cabin," I said. "Radio check at 5:15."

"Mm."

"We go wide right, through the rocks, behind him."

"Mm."

"Halvard. If this is a trap—"

"Then it's the most elaborate trap ever set," he said, levering himself upright with the grandson's pole, "for a dying man and a broke storm chaser with nothing left to steal but opinions." He offered me his free hand. "No, girl. That woman is spending herself like a match, burning from both ends to keep the light ahead of us. The least we can do is walk fast enough to justify it."

I took his hand. It was cold, and light, and certain.

Above us the pass waited—the closed pass, the erased pass, the pass the government had signed and wired and swept and renamed, and which had nonetheless, tonight, been crossed by a prospector's memory and a traitor's handwriting and a debt-ridden daughter humming a song she hadn't known she knew.

We went wide right, through the rocks.

Behind us, at 5:15 exactly, a radio crackled its check into the dark, and a bored voice answered, and neither of them ever knew how close we'd passed.

Ahead, past the sleeping cabin, past the dead red sign, the ridge dropped away—and beyond it, white under the first gray hint of morning, stood the summit.

And standing on the ridge line, small against the brightening sky, coat whipping in the wind, waiting for us like she'd promised—

Wren Calloway raised one hand.
