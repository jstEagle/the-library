## 15. Wren Comes Upstream

The order came in at 6:14 in the morning, which told Wren everything she needed to know about how badly the Optimists wanted this handled before anyone official was awake enough to ask questions.

*CALLAWAY, W. — FIELD DIRECTIVE 44-C: Intercept and detain subjects IVERSEN, Z., and HALVARD, H. (unregistered travel, closed-pass violation). Deliver to Optimist liaison at Sorrowline Crossing by 1800 hours. This directive supersedes all prior survey authority.*

She read it three times on the cracked screen of her field tablet while the kettle screamed itself hoarse on the camp stove and she did absolutely nothing about it.

"Supersedes all prior survey authority," she said aloud, to the tent, to the frost furring the canvas, to nobody. "How lovely for you."

Here was the thing about Field Directive 44-C: it hadn't come through the Survey's channels. It had come through a relay that Wren knew, because she had helped install it four years ago, routed directly from an Optimist regional office in a city whose name she'd promised never to say out loud. The Survey letterhead at the top was real paper over rot. She'd been signing things like this for years — little favors, small redirections, a permit here, a blind eye there — each one reasonable, each one deniable, each one a single grain of sand added to a scale until one morning you looked down and found yourself standing on the wrong side of it entirely.

Her father would have noticed immediately. Her father had noticed *everything*, which was, in the end, what had killed him upriver.

Wren poured the coffee, burned her tongue on it out of habit, and sat with the tablet glowing in her lap like a small accusing sun.

They were two days ahead of her. Maybe less. Halvard was dying — she'd known that the moment she'd seen him cough into his sleeve at the gas station and pretend he hadn't, the way old mountain men pretended everything, including that they weren't climbing into a stranger's truck to go home to die. And Zephyrine Iversen drove like a woman being chased by her own biography, which, as far as Wren could tell, she was.

All Wren had to do was follow the river upstream, catch them at the Crossing, and hand them over.

All she had to do.

She finished the coffee. She packed the tent. She said, to the mountains, "I'm going to need a better plan than 'all I have to do,'" and the mountains, being mountains, declined to help.

* * *

The collectors caught up with *her* first, which was either incompetence or strategy, and with the Optimists it was always both.

There were three of them, waiting at the trailhead above the drowned valley where the old road gave out: two men and a woman arranged around a parked utility vehicle with the cheerful yellow-and-white livery of a company that sponsored children's river festivals. The woman leaned against the fender eating an apple. The taller man wore a fleece vest with the Optimists' logo — a smiling sun rising over a stack of coins — embroidered over the heart, which Wren had always felt was the most honest piece of branding in the entire economy.

"Officer Calloway!" the apple woman called, waving with the fruit. "There she is. Right on schedule."

"I'm always on schedule," Wren said. "It's my most expensive quality."

"You have no idea," said the tall one, whose name was Merrick and who had shaken her hand once, four years ago, in a room that smelled of lemon polish and fear. He was still smiling. He had the kind of smile that had been maintained so long it had gone structural, load-bearing, something the rest of the face hung off of. "Directive 44-C. You got it?"

"I got it."

"And?"

"And I'm standing here," Wren said, "being intercepted."

Merrick laughed like she'd said something charming. The third collector, a young man with the build of someone who lifted weights specifically so he wouldn't have to think, watched her hands. That one worried her more than Merrick did. Smiles could be managed. Watchfulness couldn't.

"They're ahead of us," Merrick said. "Two days, maybe. Your survey gear reads their track better than ours does. So here's the arrangement — same as ever. You lead. We follow. At the Crossing, you make the arrest, we make the delivery, everybody goes home happy." He spread his arms, presenting the valley, the river, the whole bright cold morning. "Everybody goes home *lighter*, if you take my meaning."

"I always take your meaning," Wren said. "It's my second most expensive quality."

The apple woman snorted. Merrick beamed.

And Wren stood there in the wreckage of her own arithmetic, doing the sums she'd been avoiding since the directive arrived.

Because here was what she knew that they didn't, or rather here was what she knew that they knew but had never bothered to understand: there were no copies of the log. There was one log, in one dying man's coat, going up one river, toward one grave. If the Optimists took it, it wouldn't surface. It would sit in a vault in a building with a fountain in the lobby, and it would become leverage — quiet, patient, compounding leverage, the kind the Optimists were made of. Twenty years of sealed records, twenty years of flooded families who'd been told the water was an act of God, all of it convertible to debt. That was the whole business model. They didn't collect money. They collected *owing*.

And if Wren delivered it to them, she would go home lighter too. Lighter by exactly the weight of everything she had left.

"So," Merrick said. "Crossing. Sixteen hundred hours tomorrow. Yes?"

The river ran past them, brown and muscular with snowmelt, heading down toward villages full of people who had never once been permitted to say its name in public. Somewhere up that river, an old man was humming a lullaby to a woman who was only now learning whose lullaby it was. Somewhere up that river, Wren's father had died trying to warn those same people, and the report of his death had been filed under *equipment loss*.

"Yes," Wren heard herself say. "Sixteen hundred."

She smiled back at Merrick, and her smile, unlike his, still had feeling in it, which meant she had to work harder, and she did.

* * *

That night she sat by her stove and wrote the coordinates.

This was the part she'd been turning over since morning: not whether, but *how*. Because a lie that gets discovered is worse than useless — it burns the liar and accelerates the hunt. A lie has to be built like a bridge, out of true materials, in a place where the truth would plausibly stand.

So she started with truth. The truck tracks ended at the collapsed goat track below Thaw Line; that was real, she'd read it herself off the satellite pass. From there, on foot, with a dying man? Truth said: slow. Very slow. Slower than two days.

Truth also said: the high route. Any experienced surveyor looking at that terrain would conclude that Iversen would cut east toward the old shepherd stairs rather than push the river gorge. Wren knew — because she'd left a note in a cairn at the Crossing telling them exactly that, in pencil, unsigned, in the shorthand her father had taught her — that they were actually going straight up the gorge, the way no sane person would.

Which meant every conclusion the collectors drew from her data would be wrong in the most confident possible way.

She wrote the numbers carefully. Easting, northing, elevation, timestamp. A party moving at survival pace, trending northeast, last fixed position near the stairs. Then she aged it honestly — backdated the fix, degraded the confidence intervals, added the little asterisks of doubt that made a fabricated field report look like a tired officer's honest work. She even misspelled *northing* the way she genuinely misspelled it when her hands were cold, which they had been, which was the sort of detail you couldn't fake unless you'd spent ten years freezing.

"There," she said quietly. "A lie with excellent references."

Then she sat with it a while longer, because there was one more thing, and it was the thing that mattered.

If she sent them east and the collectors found nothing — and they would find nothing, eventually, after a day or two of crashing through scree — they would come back angry, and they would come back knowing. Unless, by then, there was nothing left to find. Unless the log was already out of the game entirely: copied, distributed, released, *home*. Wren didn't know how Zephyrine meant to play the endgame. But she knew the shape of the choice the woman faced, because Wren had stared at the same choice for four years from the comfortable side of it, and she knew that once you saw the vault clearly, you stopped being able to sell to it.

So the coordinates weren't just a stall. They were a gift of time. Two days, maybe three. Enough for a dying man to reach a spring. Enough for a drowning woman to decide what she was.

Wren sealed the report, addressed it to Merrick's relay, and held her thumb over the send key for a long moment, watching the fire.

Her father used to say that duty wasn't a wall you defended, it was a river you waded — you kept your feet, you kept your direction, and you accepted that some days the current would carry silt. She'd thought of herself as wading for years. Standing here now, thumb hovering, she understood she had long since stopped wading and started floating, face-up, agreeable, going wherever the current was paid to go.

Duty inverted, then. Fine. Rivers could turn. Hers was turning now.

She pressed send.

The tablet chirped, satisfied. Somewhere downstream, in a building with a fountain, a machine filed her betrayal under *routine field coordination*, and Wren Calloway, twelve-year veteran of the Survey, laughed until she had to put her head between her knees — not because it was funny, though it was a little funny, but because it was the first free breath she'd taken in four years and it hurt going in, like cold water, like altitude, like all the best air does.

* * *

She went upstream alone the next morning, and she told herself it was reconnaissance.

It was not reconnaissance. Reconnaissance doesn't require you to pack the good coffee, the one you'd been saving for a promotion that was never coming. Reconnaissance doesn't require you to leave a note on your own desk — *Gone to verify upper gorge access, back Thursday, W.C.* — written in handwriting steady enough that whoever eventually went through her effects would know she hadn't run. Reconnaissance does not, generally speaking, involve a person walking up a closed pass with a flare gun in her pocket and a speech rehearsed under her breath like a schoolgirl's recitation.

But she caught them at the rope bridge anyway, late the following afternoon, and none of that mattered anymore, because plans are just the story you tell yourself until the moment arrives and tells you its own.

They were crossing when she reached the far bank's overlook — Iversen first, quick and sure-footed as a rumor, then Halvard coming across the swaying ropes one agonizing plank at a time, pausing halfway to cough into the river wind, and Wren crouched behind a boulder with her heart going like a trapped bird and her hand on the flare gun and absolutely no idea which way she was going to use it.

Below, faint but unmistakable, came the sound she'd been dreading since dawn: engines. The collectors, following the gorge road, an hour behind her at most. Merrick's voice would be in one of them, smiling structurally.

*Iversen,* Wren thought. *Halvard. Listen to me. You cannot be here when they arrive.*

She stepped out onto the open rock, raised both arms, and shouted across the water:

"Off the bridge! Now! Both of you — move!"

Zephyrine spun, hand going to her hip, and for one suspended second the two women looked at each other across forty meters of roaring river — the hunter and the hunted, the officer and the fugitive, the woman with the badge-shaped hole where her conscience used to be and the woman who had never had a badge at all — and Wren saw understanding arrive in Zephyrine's face like weather.

Then the engines rounded the bend below, and Wren turned her back on the bridge, on the fugitives, on the whole upstream truth of her life, and walked down toward the road to meet the collectors alone.

The flare gun sat warm in her pocket.

She had one signal cartridge left, red, standard issue, visible for miles. Every protocol she'd ever sworn to said: fire it now. Mark the position. Bring the law down on the bridge like a hammer.

She had rehearsed a different ending on the walk up, and she stuck to the rehearsal. When Merrick climbed out of the lead vehicle, smiling his load-bearing smile, she met him with a report already open on her tablet and doubt already arranged on her face.

"Bad news," she said. "They're not on the river."

Merrick's smile held. "Your coordinates said—"

"My coordinates said where they *were* yesterday," Wren said. "I've spent today finding out where they aren't. And I've got a fix from the seismic station that says they doubled back in the night. Downstream. Toward the reservoir road." She let a beat pass, let him watch her check the tablet again, a tired officer re-verifying bad data. "We can still cut them off at the ford. If we leave now."

The young watchful one frowned. Merrick didn't. Merrick looked at the river, at the light failing gold along the ridgeline, at Wren's honest, exhausted, meticulously constructed face — and Wren understood, with a calm that surprised her, that he might believe her and might not, and that it no longer much mattered which, because she had already done the only thing that counted. Behind her, upstream, the bridge swayed empty over the water. Whatever happened next would happen above the flood line, beyond her reach, in the country where people went to give things back.

"Well?" Merrick said.

"Well," Wren said, and smiled her own smile — the one with feeling in it, the one she'd stop needing soon — "we'd better hurry."

They hurried. She rode in the back seat with the watchful young man's eyes on her the whole way, and the flare gun rode in her pocket like a small red heartbeat, and somewhere above them, in the last of the light, two figures climbed toward a summit that appeared on no map, carrying a truth home through the gathering dark.

Wren watched the ridgeline until the road bent and took it from her.

Then she took out the flare cartridge, turned it over once in her fingers — red, standard issue, worth nothing now but the choosing of it — and slipped it into the river through the gap in the door, where the current took it the way the current takes everything, downstream, toward the sea, toward the places where drowned things are finally allowed to rest.
