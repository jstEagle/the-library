## 16. The Source of the Sorrowline

The river stopped pretending to be a river about an hour before dawn.

Zephyrine noticed it first as a sound—or rather, the absence of one. For eleven days the Sorrowline had been their metronome, its water talking constantly under everything else: over Halvard's coughing, over her own muttered curses at scree and switchback, over the wind that never quite stopped in these valleys. Now, climbing out of a stand of frost-bent pines onto a shelf of bare granite, she realized she couldn't hear it anymore.

"Halvard." She turned to help him up the last of the slope. "The river's gone quiet."

He leaned on her arm, breathing in short, careful sips of air, the way he'd learned to somewhere in the last two days. His face was gray as old candle wax, but his eyes—those were still working fine. They went past her, up the shelf, and something moved through them that she could only describe as homecoming.

"It hasn't gone anywhere," he said. "It's just been born."

She looked where he was looking, and forgot how to breathe.

The headwaters spread out below them like something a cartographer would be fired for inventing. A vast bowl of stone, ringed by peaks still wearing their winter white even in late summer, and at its center—a spring. Not a modest trickle, not a polite mountain seep, but an upwelling the size of a village square, water rising out of the earth with such force it seemed less like a source than a wound. It pooled impossibly blue-green, then spilled over a lip of rock in a hundred small silver threads that braided themselves together downstream into the river they'd followed all this way.

And rising out of the spring's far edge, half-swallowed by a glacier tongue that had spent decades eating toward it, stood the station.

Even ruined, even buried to its second floor in ice, it was unmistakably human. Concrete and steel, a tower with its top sheared off, windows like dark eye sockets. A great dish antenna drooped from the roof at a drunken angle, furred with frost. Somewhere on its flank, mostly hidden under the glacier's shoulder, a painted marking caught the early light—the ghost of an official seal, sandblasted or scraped, but not quite thoroughly enough.

"That's it," Zephyrine said quietly. "That's what they erased."

"That's what they *tried* to erase," Halvard corrected. "Governments are very good at burning paper, girl. Very bad at erasing mountains."

She wanted to laugh. She wanted to sit down and cry. She did neither, because Halvard took three more steps, swayed like a sapling in wind, and folded.

* * *

She caught him before his head found the granite, which was the only piece of good luck the morning intended to give her. She got him propped against his pack, got the stove going, got broth into him in increments small enough that he wouldn't waste them. He drank obediently, like a man following instructions from long practice, and watched the station the whole time with an expression she couldn't read.

"You knew we wouldn't both make it," she said. It wasn't an accusation. She'd known it too, for days now, in the way you know weather is coming without checking the sky.

"I knew *I* wouldn't," he said. "I've been carrying my exit paperwork for a while now." He patted his chest, where beneath layers of wool sat the oilcloth-wrapped logbook that had started this whole impossible journey. "You, I wasn't so sure about. You're stubborn in a way that worries me. Stubborn people survive things they shouldn't."

"Flattery will get you nowhere."

"Good. Nowhere is exactly where I need you to go." He nodded at the station. "Across the spring. Into the vault. And then back down the mountain alive, which is the part everyone always skips in the planning."

Zephyrine followed his gaze. The spring wasn't wide—but it was deep, and glacial melt ran cold enough to stop a heart in minutes, and the only crossing she could see was a line of stones laid across the outflow, some of them tipped, some of them missing entirely, all of them wearing a skin of black ice.

"How long since anyone crossed that?" she asked.

"Forty years, give or take." Halvard coughed, a wet, rattling sound that made her flinch. "I laid those stones myself. I was twenty-six and thought I was immortal." He smiled, and there was something underneath the smile that frightened her—not fear exactly, but the particular tenderness of a man looking at the last thing he'll ever see clearly. "They'll hold. They held for me when I came down. But your feet will be numb before you're halfway, so don't stop to admire the view."

"The view," Zephyrine said, "is trying to kill me."

"The view has been trying to kill me for forty years," Halvard said placidly. "We've made our peace."

* * *

They waited for full sun, because Halvard insisted the ice on the stepping stones would soften enough to grip by midmorning, and because neither of them said aloud that waiting also meant resting, and rest was the only medicine left in the kit. Zephyrine melted snow, checked the rope, repacked the bag she'd carry across: the logbook, the key, food, the flare Wren had pressed into her hands back at the bridge with a look that had said more than any of her words ever managed.

Halvard dozed against the rock. Watching him, Zephyrine did the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do. Two days ago he'd walked four hours and needed six to recover. Yesterday, two hours and a night. Today he'd made it up the shelf on her arm and his legs had simply announced they were finished taking suggestions.

"Halvard," she said, when his eyes opened again. "Tell me about the last verse."

His gaze sharpened. Of everything she might have asked him—the disaster, the logbook, the sealed records, the parents whose names he'd carried like stones in his pockets for forty years—it was the lullaby she chose, and she saw him understand why. Some doors you can only open once. You save them for last.

"You've had most of it wrong," he said. "The way children do. You keep the melody and lose the meaning." He settled himself, and his voice, thin as it was, found the tune easily—forty years of carrying it will do that. 

"*Sleep, the mountain watches over you,*" he sang, barely above a whisper. "*Sleep, the river knows your name.*"

Zephyrine's throat closed. Her mother's voice lived inside that melody; she hadn't let herself think it directly in years, but there it was, note for note, coming out of a dying stranger's mouth.

"And the end goes—" He paused. Gathered himself. "The end goes:

"*When the water runs clear and the names come home,
the summit will sing you to sleep.
No chart can hold it, no hand can keep
what the mountain gives back to the deep.*"

Silence. The wind moved through the pass above them. Somewhere on the glacier, ice ticked and settled, the mountain shifting in its sleep.

"'When the water runs clear,'" Zephyrine repeated. "And the names come home."

"That's the whole of it," Halvard said. "Your mother wrote that verse. Did you know that? Not inherited it—wrote it. She used to sing it to the other technicians' kids at the station, back before anyone understood what was building under us. She said a song should tell you the truth gently, so you can carry it until you're strong enough to hear it loud." He looked at Zephyrine, and his eyes were bright and terrible. "She sang it to you. I know she did. I heard her through the comms room door more than once, when you were small and she was home between survey seasons."

"She never told me she wrote it."

"She never told anyone anything, that woman. Your father did the talking. She did the *knowing*." He coughed again, longer this time, and Zephyrine held the cup to his lips and hated how little it helped. When he could speak, he said: "The names come home, Zephyrine. That's the job. Not gold, not leverage, not whatever price the Optimists would pay for that book. Names. Hers. His. The ninety-one others. Read out loud, in daylight, where the river can hear them." He pressed the oilcloth bundle into her hands. It was lighter than it had any right to be. "Everything else is weather."

* * *

The sun crested the eastern ridge at midmorning and turned the whole basin to brass. Zephyrine stood at the edge of the outflow with the rope coiled over her shoulder and looked at the stepping stones, and made herself count them twice, and memorized which ones leaned.

Halvard insisted on standing to watch her cross. She argued. He won, the way dying men win arguments—by making the cost of continuing the argument higher than the cost of surrender. So she fixed the rope to his harness, anchored it to a boulder behind him, and paid it out as she went, and if he fell while she was mid-crossing she'd have to choose between finishing and turning back, and they both knew it, and neither of them said it.

The first three stones were easy. The fourth was tipped and wore a rind of ice that her boot edge skated off before biting. The water around her ankles was not cold so much as *absolute*—a temperature that didn't register as sensation, just as fact, the way a wall registers as fact. By the sixth stone her feet had filed a formal complaint and resigned. By the eighth, halfway, she stopped feeling her legs from the knee down and understood Halvard's warning completely.

She did not stop to admire the view. But the view admired her anyway: the spring roaring up out of the earth ten feet to her left, so blue it looked lit from below, so forceful she could feel its breath on her face. This was the heart of the Sorrowline. This was the place every map had a blank space and every town went silent. Forty years of official nothing, and here it all was, running clear as glass.

*Running clear,* she thought, and her chest hurt.

The ninth stone was missing. There was a gap of eighteen inches of open current, and beyond it the tenth stone sat at the base of the station's concrete apron, right where the glacier's toe met the old foundation. She stood on the eighth stone with dead legs and a live heartbeat and thought about the phrase *point of no return*, which she'd always considered dramatic nonsense. Mountains were honest. You could always turn around.

Except you couldn't, actually. Not anymore. Not with ninety-two names in her pocket and a man on the shore singing her mother's song.

She jumped.

Her lead foot hit the tenth stone square and true; her trailing foot hit water and slid, and for one long, screaming second the river had her hip-deep and pulling—and then the rope snapped taut behind her, Halvard's arms doing what his legs no longer could, and she hauled herself out onto the concrete apron gasping like a landed fish, soaked to the ribs, laughing in the high unhinged way of people who have recently almost died.

"STONES HELD!" she shouted across the water.

"I TOLD YOU THEY WOULD!" Halvard shouted back, and then sat down heavily on the rock, because shouting costs what shouting costs.

* * *

Up close, the station was worse and better than it had looked from the ridge. Worse, because the decay was intimate—rusted rebar reaching out of cracked concrete like exposed bone, a door hanging by one hinge, forty years of frost-heave tilting everything a few degrees off true. Better, because it was unmistakably, stubbornly *built*. Humans had come this far up, this far in, believing they belonged, and the mountain had disagreed, and the argument was still visible everywhere you looked.

Zephyrine wrung out what she could, changed her socks with hands that shook until the shivering warmed into ordinary tremble, and explored the apron while her blood remembered how to work. The main entrance was choked with ice. But on the station's landward side, half-hidden behind a drift, she found what Halvard had described on the third night of their journey, in one of his rare lucid stretches: a secondary access, a maintenance stair cut down into the foundation itself, leading to the lower levels. The vault levels.

A steel door, frosted shut. A lock, ancient but honest-looking. A keyhole.

She climbed back up to the shelf where Halvard sat propped against his boulder, facing the station like a man facing a stage. She knelt in front of him. Neither of them spoke for a moment. Below them the spring thundered up out of the earth, patient as geology.

"Key," she said.

He reached inside his coat and brought out a small iron key on a length of cord, worn smooth as a river stone. He'd carried it for forty years. It probably weighed less than the coins in her truck's ashtray.

"Second stair, landward side," he said. "Down two flights. The vault door is green—was green; it'll be rust and green now. The key turns left. Everyone tries right first. Everyone." He put the key in her palm and closed her fingers over it with both of his hands, and his grip, for all his weakness, was still a grip. "Zephyrine. Listen to me. Once you're down there, you'll want to read everything. Every page. Every marginal note. You'll want to stand in that room until you understand all of it." His voice frayed. "Don't. Take the log. Take the photographs if they're there—they'll be in the flat cabinet, third drawer, wrapped in wax paper. Then come back up. Grief keeps. Truth keeps. But daylight doesn't, and I'm nearly out of mine, and there's a thing I need to say to you while the sun's still on the water."

She swallowed hard. "Then say it now, and I'll go quick."

"No." He almost smiled. "You'll go quick because you're you. Say it after. Go."

She went.

* * *

The stair was dry, which surprised her, and dark, which didn't. Her headlamp carved a tunnel out of the blackness, two flights of concrete steps descending into the station's bones. The air smelled of cold stone and old machine oil and, faintly, improbably, of paper—the way libraries smell, the way archives smell, the way the past smells when someone has taken the trouble to preserve it.

At the bottom: a corridor, and at the corridor's end, a door. Green once. Rust now, bleeding down from the hinges like old wounds. Stenciled beside it, faded but legible, official lettering that had survived every attempt to erase it:

**HYDROLOGICAL RESEARCH — LEVEL 2 — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY**

Below that, smaller, in someone's handwriting rather than the government's, in grease pencil that had outlasted paint:

*We were here. We tried to tell you.*

Zephyrine stood in front of the door with the key in her fist and her heart going like a trapped bird, and she thought about her mother singing a lullaby through a comms room door, and her father doing the talking, and ninety-two names, and a cheerful syndicate that would pay millions to keep this door shut forever, and a village downstream that had never once been allowed to mourn.

She put the key in the lock.

It turned left.

Behind her, faint but clear, drifting down the concrete stairwell from the bright morning above, she heard Halvard's voice—one thread of the lullaby, the last verse, sung out over the water for the mountain to hear:

*"When the water runs clear and the names come home..."*

The vault door swung inward, exhaling forty years of held breath into her face, and somewhere far below, deeper than the vault, deeper than the foundations, the mountain made a sound she felt in her teeth before she heard it with her ears.

Low. Long. Like something enormous, turning over in its sleep.

Zephyrine clicked on her lamp, stepped through the door, and pulled it shut behind her.
