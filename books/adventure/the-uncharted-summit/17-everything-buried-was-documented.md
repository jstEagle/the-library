## 17. Everything Buried Was Documented

The key was small and brass and warm from Halvard's pocket, which felt wrong, somehow, like holding a bird that should have been cold. I stood at the vault door with it in my fist for a long moment, listening to the station breathe around me.

That's what it did, breathe. The old research station had been half-swallowed by the glacier the way a cat swallows a moth—slowly, absently, keeping it alive in there somewhere. Wind moved through broken vents and made a low whistle that rose and fell. Ice had claimed the lower corridors entirely; you could see their blue shapes through gaps in the floor grating, like rooms drowned in glass. But the upper level still held. Somebody had built this place to last, back when lasting mattered to somebody.

Behind me, Halvard lay wrapped in every blanket we owned, propped against his pack in the entry hall where the last of the daylight came through the shattered roof panels. He'd walked as far as he could walk, which was forty meters past where any reasonable person would have quit, and then he'd sat down on a crate marked PROPERTY OF THE DIRECTORATE — SURVEY DIVISION 9 and said, "Well. Home again," in a voice so mild you'd have thought he was commenting on the weather.

"Go on," he said now, when I didn't move. "It won't bite. It's paper. Paper never bites. People do, but paper just waits."

"You're sure it's—"

"Zephyrine." He said my name the way you set down something heavy. "I've been carrying this door key for thirty-one years. The least you can do is let me watch you open it."

So I opened it.

The lock turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and the door swung inward on hinges that had been oiled within living memory—which stopped me for a second, because nothing else in this place had been touched in decades. Then I saw why. A camp stove sat in the corner of the vault room, cold but clean. A tin of matches, half full. A bedroll rolled tight against the wall. And stacked beside them, neat as library books, were the boxes.

Somebody had been coming here. Recently enough to leave matches.

I filed that away in the part of my mind where I kept things I couldn't afford to think about yet—the same shelf where I kept the memory of Wren Calloway burning that flare into the river instead of signaling the collectors, and the note we'd found in the cairn at the pass, written in her precise surveyor's hand: *Sensors at 40-meter intervals. Cross below the third cairn, single file, after dark.* An enemy who taught you how to evade her own people was either a traitor or a friend, and I genuinely could not tell which frightened me more.

But that was later. That was all later. Right then there was only the light of my headlamp moving across the boxes, and the stenciled labels, and the dust lying over everything except the top box, which was dust-free, which meant someone had opened it, read it, put it back.

*CONTAINMENT LOG — UNREDACTED. DO NOT DUPLICATE. DO NOT CIRCULATE.*

Halvard had told me, back at the gas station, a hundred years ago: "They'll have redacted copies in every archive in the country. Black bars everywhere. What I've got is what the machine actually wrote down before anyone got to it with a pen."

He hadn't exaggerated. The log was a continuous strip printout, the old kind, on paper gone amber at the edges, and the handwriting in the margins was my mother's.

I knew it before I consciously knew it. I knew it the way you know a lullaby—by shape, by rhythm, before meaning. The tight slanted letters. The way she crossed her sevens. I used to practice forging her signature as a child, for signing my own disastrous report cards, and I had never gotten the crossbar right.

She had been here. In this room. With her hands on these pages.

I sat down on the floor because standing had stopped being an option, and I started at the beginning.

The official story—I'd grown up on its edges, heard it in fragments from foster-house televisions and one bitter uncle—was that a research team upriver had caused an "unspecified industrial accident," that the valley settlements had been evacuated "as a precaution," and that the Sorrowline had been rerouted and its headwaters closed pending environmental remediation. Nobody ever said remediated from what. Nobody ever said why the evacuation maps showed villages being emptied three days *before* the accident date.

The log said it plainly, in the flat voice of instruments:

*Day 214. Containment integrity at 61% and falling. Recommend immediate shutdown of deep core operations.*

*Day 217. Integrity 44%. Secondary seals showing stress fractures. Request authorization to evacuate support staff.*

*Day 219. AUTHORIZATION DENIED. Director's office: shutdown would render nine years of investment unrecoverable. Continue monitoring.*

And then, in my mother's hand, pressed hard enough to dent three pages beneath:

*They are going to keep the core running until it fails. I have asked what happens to the water table if it fails. I have been told that question is above my clearance. The water table feeds the Sorrowline. The Sorrowline feeds eleven towns. I am a hydrologist, not a prophet, but I can count.*

My mother could not count high enough, it turned out. Or she counted exactly right, and the men with the authority simply declined to hear the total.

*Day 223. Integrity 19%. Evacuation of downstream settlements proposed by Survey Division 9. Proposal rejected. Rationale on file: risk of public alarm exceeds projected risk of event.*

*Risk of public alarm,* I read, and read it again, and heard my own voice say it out loud in the empty vault, ugly and disbelieving. Eleven towns. Thousands of people. And the projection wasn't about whether they'd die—it was about whether they'd be *scared*. You could fix scared afterward. Scared didn't show up in ledgers.

*Day 225. Event occurred at 0347 hours.*

The instrument record after that was chaos—pressure spikes, seal failures listed in a cascade, numbers climbing past the margins. Someone had drawn a line down the page and written across it: *CORE BREACH. THIS IS NOT CONTAINED. REPEAT: NOT CONTAINED.*

Then the human record took over, and this was the part I will carry until I die.

My parents hadn't run. The log shows their signatures on dispatch after dispatch through the night of Day 225 and into Day 226—messages sent downstream to every settlement on the Sorrowline, warnings in plain language, sent on every channel, some of them flagged *SENT — NO ACKNOWLEDGMENT*, some flagged *TRANSMITTED — CHANNEL SEVERED*, and the last few, in my father's blocky engineer's capitals: *DO NOT WAIT FOR OFFICIAL INSTRUCTION. THE WATER IS COMING. LEAVE NOW. WE WILL HOLD THE STATION AND KEEP SENDING.*

They held the station and kept sending. That was the whole of their heroism, and it was enormous. They stayed at their posts in a failing facility to shout into severed channels because it was the only thing left they could do, and the log preserves every shout.

And then, two days later, the order came down—not from the mountain, not from the disaster, but from a directorate office with letterhead:

*Directive 9-C. Headwaters zone to be flooded under emergency protocol. All records pertaining to Days 200–226 to be sealed. Personnel losses to be recorded as 'expedition mishap.' Surviving staff to sign separation agreements. This directive supersedes all prior communication.*

*Personnel losses to be recorded as 'expedition mishap.'*

My parents died holding a microphone, and the government wrote *mishap* next to their names, the way you'd write it next to a man struck by lightning while fishing. And the flooding—the flooding that drowned the valley, that scattered the river-people off land their families had worked for generations, that erased eleven towns' worth of history—that hadn't been the disaster at all. The disaster was the breach. The flooding was the *cleanup*. Ordered, deliberate, signed. They drowned the evidence and called the water remediation.

I don't remember deciding to be angry. I remember looking down and finding my hands shaking, and the log cradled in them like something rescued from a fire, and understanding that the shaking had been building since Day 214, since before I was born, since a woman with my chin and my terrible signature-crossing habit had written *I can count* and been told to stop counting.

* * *

"Zephyrine." Halvard's voice, from the doorway, hoarse and careful. "You've been quiet a long time."

"They knew." My voice came out scraped. "Halvard, they knew for *eleven days*. They had the readings. She begged them—there are requests in here, formal requests, she filed them properly because that's who she was—and they denied every one because shutting down would cost money."

"I know."

"And then they flooded it. On purpose. After. To hide it. The villages—" My throat closed. "The river-people. The elder's grandmother. Everyone upstream of the flood line. They weren't casualties of an accident. They were casualties of a *memo*."

"Yes." He lowered himself onto the crate by the door, moving like a man made of wire and patience. "Now you understand what I've been carrying."

"Why?" It burst out of me. "Why seal it? Why erase the whole valley? Who benefits from—"

"Follow the money, girl. Same place it always goes." He nodded at the log. "The core project had backers. Investors. Men whose names are in the funding annexes, some of whom went on to very distinguished careers. One of them founded a certain lending syndicate that's been very cheerful about helping folks in debt ever since." His smile was thin as frost. "Where do you think the Optimists' first capital came from? Containment doesn't fail for free. Somebody profited from those nine years of investment right up until Day 225, and then somebody needed the whole valley forgotten before the investors' names surfaced in an inquiry."

The Optimists. My debt. The cheerful voices on the phone, the collector smiling in my truck, the three-week deadline. It had always been the same river, running through everything, and I'd been swimming in it my whole life without once asking where the source was.

Except now I was literally standing at the source.

"The log proves the order to flood came from the directorate," Halvard said. "It proves the warnings were sent and ignored. It proves your parents—" He stopped. Started again, gentler. "It proves they were heroes, Zephyrine. Documented heroes. Not mishaps."

I looked down at my mother's marginalia. At the bottom of the final page, dated Day 226, hours before Directive 9-C, she had written one last line—not to the directorate, not to any colleague. Just to whoever might someday read this:

*If anyone finds this: we tried. Tell the valley we tried.*

"Tell the valley we tried," I repeated aloud, and something in my chest cracked along a seam I hadn't known was load-bearing.

Halvard watched me with eyes gone bright and wet. "There's more," he said quietly. "Last pages. Annexes. Names of every technician, every surveyor, every village liaison who signed the warnings. Your father drew the evacuation routes himself—they're reproduced in there, the actual routes, the ones the flood never gave people time to use." He paused. "And there's a grave, Zephyrine. Behind the station, under the cairn with the iron rod. He asked to stay with the station. We buried him facing downstream, toward the towns he was trying to save."

"We?"

"The surviving staff. Before they made us sign the separations and scattered us." His breath caught, wheezed, steadied. "I signed mine. That's my shame, and I've carried it up every mountain since. But I kept a copy of the log. The only unredacted one. I told myself someday. Someday is a word young people think is generous and old people know is a thief."

Outside, the wind changed pitch, dropping from its whistle to something lower, longer—a sound like the mountain clearing its throat. I thought of the clean camp stove, the dustless box. Somebody else had been here. Somebody who might come back.

And I thought of the collectors, two days behind us at most even with Wren's stall, and of the offer I already knew was coming, because men like the Optimists' lead collector never climb a forbidden pass without a price list in their pocket. This log was worth millions in leverage—proof that could blackmail careers, topple the syndicate's founders' heirs, ransom the directorate itself. Every instinct I had honed in twenty-nine years of drowning was already doing the arithmetic: *hand it over, walk away free, debt erased, truck replaced, life restarted.*

Every instinct except one. The one that had learned a lullaby around a fire and discovered it had a name, and the name was mine.

"Halvard," I said. "When they come—and they're coming—what do I do?"

He smiled, and for a moment the fever left his face and I saw the young technician he'd been, the one who'd promised my mother something at a door much like this one.

"You already know," he said. "You Iversens always count. Now finish the sum."

I looked down at the log—at my mother's last line, at my father's blocky capitals shouting *LEAVE NOW* into dead channels—and I picked up the pages, all of them, and held them against my chest the way you hold a child, or a coal you're carrying through wind.

Down the valley, faint but unmistakable, engines. More than one. Coming upstream.

They'd found the pass.
