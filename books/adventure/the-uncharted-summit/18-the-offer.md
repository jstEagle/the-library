## 18. The Offer

The helicopter came up the valley like a wasp looking for a window to break.

I heard it before I saw it—a thin, insistent buzzing that didn't belong in a place where the loudest thing had been, until that morning, the creak of the glacier and Halvard's breathing. I stepped out of the station's shadow into the flat white light of the headwaters and watched it rise over the eastern ridge, small as a beetle at first, then bigger, then too big to pretend about.

"Wren," I said, though she wasn't there to hear it. She'd gone downriver two days ago with a promise to stall them, and I had let myself believe her, because hope is cheaper than oxygen up here and just as necessary.

Halvard lay inside on a cot we'd dragged from the old barracks, propped against a rolled sleeping pad, his map spread across his knees like a blanket he refused to be tucked under. He'd insisted on being carried to the threshold of the vault yesterday—had touched the door with both hands, the way you'd touch a coffin or a cradle—and since then he hadn't had the strength for much besides watching me work and offering commentary.

"That'll be them," he said when I came back in. His voice was a dry leaf now. "You can tell by the sound. Optimists fly cheerful."

"They fly expensive," I said. "Cheerful's just what they charge extra for."

He laughed, which turned into coughing, which turned into that long rattling silence afterward that I was learning not to panic over, because panicking used up both our rations of calm. I knelt and put my hand flat on his chest until the rattling eased.

"Listen," he said, gripping my wrist with fingers that had once been strong enough to haul drill casing through permafrost and were now barely strong enough to hold a grudge. "When they offer—and they will offer, Zephyrine, they always offer—"

"I know what they'll offer."

"You don't." His eyes were fever-bright and absolutely clear at the same time, which is a trick only the dying manage. "They won't threaten first. That's the trap everyone falls into. They lead with kindness. They've practiced kindness the way other people practice marksmanship. So when the man with the smile tells you your debt is forgiven, you remember: nothing is forgiven. It's *purchased*. And everything they purchase, they own."

The helicopter settled on the ice shelf below the station with the delicacy of something that had never once had to land anywhere it wasn't welcome. Three figures climbed out. Two of them wore the pale gray suits that the Optimists' collectors favored—cheerful gray, they called it, in the brochures, though I'd never met anyone who felt cheerful near it. The third figure walked between them with her hands zip-tied in front of her and her chin up like she was leading the parade instead of being its prisoner.

Wren Calloway looked, if anything, annoyed. At the collectors. At the altitude. Possibly at the universe in general.

"Oh no," Halvard said softly. "Oh, that poor woman."

"I'm going out there."

"You're going to do something stupid."

"I'm going to negotiate," I said, "which is the same thing but with paperwork."

* * *

The ice shelf crunched under my boots like packing foam. I stopped ten paces from them, close enough to talk, far enough that the tall collector would have to take an actual step if he wanted to hurt me, and men like him never liked taking actual steps. It ruins the tailoring.

The lead collector was a broad, pink-faced man with silver hair combed into a part so precise it could have been surveyed. He wore an Optimist lapel pin shaped like a rising sun, and when he smiled—which he did immediately, warmly, the way a furnace smiles at a matchstick—I recognized him from the collection notices. Mr. Aldous Vane. My account manager. He'd sent me birthday cards. Actual cards, with handwritten notes: *Another year brighter! Your friends at the Optimists.*

"Miss Iversen," he said, spreading his hands. "You have no idea how pleased I am to see you alive. Truly. When the truck went off the books at Thaw Line, I took it personally. I said to my team, that girl has weather sense, that girl doesn't die in ditches."

"She nearly did, though."

"And yet!" He gestured at the station behind me, the glacier, the whole glittering forbidden amphitheater of the source, as if presenting it. "And here you are, higher than any survey permit allows, standing on ground that officially does not exist, in the company of a man who officially retired forty years ago. You understand my dilemma. I'm contractually obligated to be impressed."

Behind him, Wren cleared her throat. "Vane. The zip ties are cutting off my circulation."

"We'll get you new ones," Vane said pleasantly, without turning around. Then, to me: "Where is he? Mr. Halvard. Our records indicate he boarded your vehicle at the Last Gas. Our records also indicate he is worth more dead than this entire valley, but we'd prefer him alive. Sentiment, you understand. We're very sentimental."

"He's resting."

"May I?"

"No."

Vane sighed, and for one unguarded moment the smile dropped and I saw the machinery underneath—not cruelty exactly, but arithmetic. A man doing sums with people. Then the smile came back up like a shop shutter.

"Let me save us all the frostbite," he said. "You found the vault. Don't insult us both by denying it; the seismic survey flagged the door opening three days ago. Inside that vault is a logbook—the field log of Station Sorrowline, unredacted, the only copy in existence. Do you know what that document is worth, Miss Iversen?"

"A history lesson?"

"It's worth *leverage*." He said the word the way other men said *darling*. "There are eleven families downstream who were paid settlements to stay quiet about the flooding. There are four officials whose signatures authorized it. There are two corporations still operating whose insurance depends on the official record staying official. That logbook, in the right hands, is worth millions. In the wrong hands—" he shrugged, expansive, "—it's worth millions to different people. Either way, Miss Iversen, everybody gets paid. That's the Optimist philosophy. We find a way to say yes."

"And the yes you're offering me is?"

Vane clasped his hands behind his back and rocked slightly on his heels, a man about to enjoy himself.

"Full forgiveness. Every cent. The principal, the interest, the late fees, the—frankly creative—restructuring fees. Gone. Zeroed. And a line of credit besides, because a woman of your talents shouldn't have to chase storms for gas money. All of it, for the logbook and the map, delivered today, with your signature on a transfer of custody. You walk away free. Free, Miss Iversen. Do you know how rarely we say that word?"

I did, actually. I'd spent six years trying to buy it back one storm at a time, and every payment had somehow made the number bigger, the way feeding a fire makes it hungrier. Free. The word landed in my chest and sat there glowing like a coal.

That's the thing nobody tells you about debt: it isn't the money. Money is abstract. What debt actually takes from you is the future—you spend years of your life in advance, mortgaged, and after long enough you stop believing the years belong to you at all. Vane wasn't offering me cash. He was offering me myself back.

And Halvard's voice in my ear: *nothing is forgiven. It's purchased.*

"What happens to the logbook after I sign?" I asked.

Vane's smile widened a millimeter. "It goes where leverage goes."

"Which is nowhere. Leverage isn't meant to be used. It's meant to be held. You'd put it in a drawer, and the drawer stays shut, and the eleven families go on being paid to be quiet, and the names on the memorial wall—" I stopped, because my voice had done something embarrassing. I tried again. "The names stay erased."

"The names," Vane said gently, "were never on the wall to begin with. That's rather the point."

Behind him, Wren made a small sound. Not a word. Just a sound, like a hinge deciding whether to give.

* * *

That was when the station door banged open.

Not dramatically—Halvard didn't have drama left in him—but with the slow, deliberate scrape of a man determined to walk across a room on his own terms even if it took him a quarter hour. He came out into the light blinking, wrapped in every blanket we owned, moving like a ship in a heavy sea, and Vane's collectors both took a half-step forward and then stopped, because whatever their briefing had said about Halvard, it clearly hadn't prepared them for what forty years and a dying lung had made of him.

"There he is," Vane said warmly. "Mr. Halvard. Do you know, I have your employment file on my desk. Station technician, second class. Commendation for valve maintenance. It's a good file. It ends abruptly."

"Everything ends abruptly," Halvard said. "That's what an ending is." He shuffled forward until he stood beside me, and I got my shoulder under his arm without either of us making a production of it. He weighed almost nothing. He weighed everything. "You're Aldous Vane. You have your father's chin. He collected from my brother in '61. Smiled the whole time. Told him it was a fresh start."

"We prefer to think of ourselves as facilitators of fresh starts, yes."

"You're standing," Vane's taller colleague said, and there was genuine unease in it, "on restricted ground. Both of you. This is a federal closure—"

"So arrest us," Halvard said. "Go on. Radio it in. Say the words: *apprehended two civilians at the source of the Sorrowline.* Say it slowly and listen to yourself. Because the moment those words exist, this place exists, and the moment this place exists, someone downstream asks why it was closed, and then someone else finds the settlement payments, and then—" he smiled, and it was the first time I'd seen him smile like a young man, "—then your tidy little drawer gets opened whether you like it or not. You haven't come here to arrest anyone, son. You've come here hoping nobody ever has to say where you were."

The tall collector looked at Vane. Vane's expression hadn't moved, but something behind it had recalculated.

"Well," Vane said. "This is why we pay for the helicopter."

* * *

Here is what happened next, and I want to set it down precisely, because later—when the elders tell it, when it gets written into the valley's own record—it will probably grow teeth and wings, and I'd like one version of it to stay human-sized.

We negotiated on the ice shelf for the better part of an hour. Vane offered. I countered. He offered again, sweeter. Halvard sat down on a supply crate partway through, because standing had become a negotiation of its own, and he participated from the crate with the serene ruthlessness of a man who had already seen the last page of his own book and was simply enjoying watching everyone else turn theirs.

Vane went to nine hundred thousand in forgiveness plus a stipend. He offered Wren's release as a sweetener, which told me exactly how much he valued her, which is to say as a rounding error. He offered to make the charges for the closed pass evaporate. He offered, at the end, a thing I hadn't expected: he offered to leave the logbook *with* us—copies, he said, copies for everyone, the Optimists were generous—and I understood then that he thought I was stupid, and that he'd been thinking I was stupid since the day he approved my first loan, and that this was, in its way, the most honest moment of the entire morning.

And through all of it, Halvard grew quieter.

I noticed it the way you notice a clock stopping—not at once, but as an absence you can't name. His answers got shorter. His hand on my shoulder got lighter. Between one counteroffer and the next, he leaned into me and his weight changed, and I knew, the way you know a storm has turned, without instruments, without wanting to.

"Zephyrine," he said. Just my name. Then, when I bent down: "Don't sign. Don't even for the copies. Some things only work if they're given."

"Halvard—"

"Listen. Listen, girl, I haven't got the breath for repeating." His eyes found mine and held on with the last of his grip strength, which turned out to be considerable. "Your mother sang that lullaby to me once. Did you know that? Third shift, station year, I'd cut my hand open on a coupling and she sat with me and sang until I stopped shaking. She said, *if it ever matters, if it ever truly matters, you bring this home.* I thought she meant the log. She didn't. She meant the song. She meant—" his breath caught, held, released, "—she meant the people. Truth isn't leverage. Truth is a lullaby. You sing it *to* somebody."

"Mr. Halvard," Vane said, and for the first time all day there was something real in his voice—not warmth, but urgency, the arithmetic suddenly unstable. "If we could conclude—"

"One moment," Halvard said, without looking at him. To me, quietly: "Tell them. The elders. Tell them I'm sorry it took forty years. Tell them the valve failed on a Tuesday and I was on shift and I will answer for that wherever answering happens. And tell them—" the breath again, thinner, "—tell them the river remembers. That's the last verse. Nobody sings it anymore. *The river remembers.*"

His hand slid from my shoulder.

I want to be able to say I caught him. I didn't, quite. I lowered him, which is different, and gentler, and maybe that's the version I needed: that I didn't grab at him, I set him down on the ice of the valley he'd carried home to, and I stayed kneeling there while somewhere above us Vane was saying something about concluding arrangements, and the words fell around me like weather on a window.

Then I stood up.

* * *

"Miss Iversen," Vane said. "I sympathize, genuinely. Grief is costly. Which is why my offer stands, improved by circumstances: the logbook, the map, signed transfer, and you walk free this afternoon. Think of it as his last gift to you."

I looked at him for a long moment. Behind him, Wren had gone very still, her tied hands resting on her knees, watching me with an expression I couldn't read—or could, and didn't dare.

"Here's my counteroffer," I said.

Vane brightened. Collectors love a counteroffer; it means the prey is still playing.

"You keep the debt," I said. "Every cent. Compound it. Add the helicopter fuel. Bill me for the emotional labor. And in exchange, you take Halvard home—to the village, to the elders, with honors—and you forget you ever saw this valley, and the logbook goes to the people it belongs to, tonight, free of charge, from me, with my compliments."

The silence that followed had texture. The tall collector shifted. Somewhere on the glacier, a serac cracked and boomed like distant artillery, as if the mountain itself were clearing its throat.

"Miss Iversen," Vane said at last, and the smile was gone now, fully gone, and what remained was worse and more honest: the accountant. "Do you understand what you're refusing? You're refusing *yourself*. Six years of your life. Your truck. Your name on every blacklist in three provinces. You'll owe us until you're sixty."

"I know."

"You'll be running from collectors for the rest of your—"

"No," I said, and heard my own voice come out steady, which surprised me, because inside I felt like the thaw line itself, everything shifting and cracking and going soft all at once. "No, I don't think I will. Because you can't collect from someone who's already given everything away, Mr. Vane. I've got nothing left to levy. No collateral. No credit. No leverage. I'm the first person you've ever met who's genuinely worthless to you." I smiled at him, and it felt wonderful, and terrible, like the lullaby's last verse. "Congratulations. You finally found a customer you can't say yes to."

Vane stared at me. And I watched something extraordinary happen behind his eyes: not understanding—he may never understand—but doubt. The first hairline crack in the arithmetic. Because he had built his whole cheerful empire on the certainty that everyone had a price, and here stood a woman on forbidden ice, freshly orphaned twice over, holding a fortune in her pocket, asking to be ruined.

"You're making a mistake," he said quietly.

"Probably," I agreed. "But it's mine. That's the difference between us. Yours were all bought for you."

He turned to his collectors. "Take the logbook. Take the map. Take them both into custody—"

"*No.*"

Wren's voice. She was on her feet, zip-tied hands raised, and her face had gone the color of the glacier and twice as hard. "Vane. Touch that logbook and I testify. Everything. The false permits, the bought audits, the settlement ledger—I kept copies, you knew I kept copies, it's why you never let me near accounting. You burn this valley's truth to protect your leverage, and I will spend the rest of my life making sure yours costs more than hers ever did."

Vane looked at her. Really looked, for the first time. "Calloway. You'd torch your career over a stranger's sentiment?"

"It's not sentiment," Wren said, and her voice cracked clean down the middle, and she let it. "My father died on that river. I've been working for the people who drowned him for eleven years. This isn't betrayal, Vane. It's the first honest day I've had since."

The tall collector's hand drifted toward his coat. Vane raised two fingers—an order, or maybe just a pause—and the whole ice shelf balanced on it, the four of us and the body of an old man and a logbook full of Tuesdays, while the helicopter idled and the glacier groaned and the Sorrowline began, somewhere beneath our feet, its long cold run toward the sea.

Vane exhaled through his nose.

"Get in the aircraft," he said. "All of you. We're going to discuss this somewhere warmer."

And I put my hand in my pocket, closed it around the vault key, and started walking toward the station—because whatever happened next, the truth was going home first.
