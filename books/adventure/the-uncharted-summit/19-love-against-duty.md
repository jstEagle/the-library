## 19. Love Against Duty

**Wren**

The trouble with working for cheerful people, Wren had learned over nine years, was that they never stopped smiling at the exact moments when smiling should have been impossible.

The lead collector—his name was Merrick Stave, and he smiled the way frost formed on glass, slowly and from every edge at once—stood in the snow outside the research station with his hands folded behind his back like a man admiring a garden. Behind him, four of his people held rifles with the loose, comfortable grip of men who had never once needed to worry about paperwork.

"Miss Calloway," Stave said warmly. "You've been a tremendous help. Truly. The Optimists value loyalty above all things."

"I know," Wren said. "It's in the brochure."

He laughed. His men didn't. Somewhere behind her, at the mouth of the vault corridor, Zephyrine Iversen stood with a satchel full of the only unredacted record of the Sorrowline disaster pressed against her chest like a shield, and Halvard lay dying on a stretcher of pack frames that two of Stave's men had carried up the pass with surprising gentleness, because even the Optimists were tender with things that were about to become assets.

Wren's hands were cuffed in front of her. They'd done it almost apologetically. That was the worst part—the courtesy. Nine years ago they had bought her silence with the same courtesy: a warm office, a warm contract, a warm explanation of how her father's failed expedition would be remembered as incompetence unless she cooperated, in which case it would be remembered as nothing at all, which was kinder.

She had told herself for nine years that she'd made a reasonable choice under unreasonable conditions. Standing here now, watching Zephyrine's jaw set in that particular stubborn way she'd come to know across a rope bridge and a snowstorm and a note left in a cairn, Wren understood she had simply been a coward with excellent filing skills.

"Here's how this goes," Stave said, turning to Zephyrine with his garden-admiring smile. "You hand over the log. Every debt you carry—every debt *anyone* in your little caravan carries—is dissolved. You walk out of these mountains rich, free, and unbothered. The Optimists are not monsters, Miss Iversen. We're *bankers*. There's a difference, though I'll grant it's subtle."

"And the villages downstream?" Zephyrine said. "The ones that were flooded? The families that were never allowed to mourn?"

"The Optimists," Stave said, "are not historians either."

Behind him, one of his men shifted his weight, and Wren saw it—the small tell, the half-second glance toward the corridor where Halvard's breathing had gone thin and rattling. She saw something else too. Her cuffs were standard Optimist issue, double-lock, and she had spent three years of her life auditing their equipment manifests. She knew exactly which batch had the faulty ratchet pins. Batch 44-C. Recalled twice. Never actually recalled, because recalls cost money and the Optimists preferred apologies.

She had filed that report herself. It had been marked *resolved* without ever being resolved. Everything in her life, Wren thought, came back to paperwork that lied.

Zephyrine looked at her then. Just a look—half a second, snow-light and tired—but it contained an entire conversation. *Are you with them or aren't you.*

Wren had been asking herself the same question since the rope bridge, when she'd burned the signal flare into the river instead of into the sky. Since the fabricated coordinates. Since the cairn. Each betrayal of her employers had felt, at the time, like a small leak in a dam. Now she understood the dam was already gone and had been for years, and all that remained was to stop pretending she was holding anything back.

"Stave," she said pleasantly. "Before we conclude negotiations, there's a compliance matter."

"There is no—" He turned, still smiling, still patient, the smile of a man who had never been interrupted successfully in his life.

Wren brought her cuffed hands up between her own wrists, twisted them against each other the way the equipment manual—her equipment manual—said the faulty 44-C pin allowed, and felt the lock give with a click so quiet the snow barely noticed it.

Then she stepped forward, took the nearest rifle out of the hands of the most surprised man in the Sorrowline watershed, and swept his legs out from under him with the calm efficiency of someone completing a routine audit.

"Compliance matter," she repeated.

* * *

**Zephyrine**

Later—and Zephyrine would think about this later, in the truck, in the cell, in the years after—she would try to explain to herself why she hadn't run.

There had been a moment. A real one. Stave's offer hung in the frozen air like a coin mid-flip: the log for her freedom, her debts dissolved, her whole crooked ledger wiped clean. She had stood in the mouth of the vault with her parents' names fresh in her ears—*Edda Iversen. Tomas Iversen. Surveyors. Warners. Dead for telling the truth*—and she had done the arithmetic she always did, the desperate quick math of a woman who had been counting money she didn't have since she was nineteen years old.

The log was worth millions. Her debt was worth thousands. Even the margin was a fortune.

And then she looked down at Halvard.

He was lying on the pack-frame stretcher with his eyes closed, but he wasn't asleep. Dying people aren't asleep; they're listening. His breath came in long, careful pulls, like a man carrying water across ice. And when Zephyrine knelt beside him, his eyes opened—not cloudy anymore, the fever broken by the cold or by purpose, she couldn't say—and he looked at her with an expression she'd only seen once before, on the face of the village elder, when she'd hummed six notes of a lullaby in a kitchen that smelled of woodsmoke.

"Whatever they're offering," Halvard rasped, "it isn't enough. Not because the number's wrong. Because the thing isn't theirs to buy."

"I know," Zephyrine said.

"Do you?" He almost smiled. "Because I've watched you do sums your whole life, Zephyrine Iversen. Your mother did them too. Always calculating the angle of escape." His hand found hers, papery and freezing. "Your father never calculated anything. He just turned toward whatever needed doing. You've got both of them in you. This is the day you pick which one drives."

Up the slope, Wren disarmed the second man with what could only be described as bureaucratic violence—precise, unhurried, thoroughly documented in advance. Two of Stave's collectors raised their rifles, hesitated, and lowered them again, because it is very difficult to shoot a woman who used to sign your expense reports and looks genuinely disappointed in you.

"Stave," Wren called out, rifle leveled, cuffs dangling from one wrist like a bracelet she'd decided against. "I'm going to save us both some correspondence. The log has already been copied. Three copies. One went downstream with a river-runner yesterday morning. One is in a cairn at the pass—I'll give you the coordinates if you'd like to dig through forty feet of snow before spring. And one—" she nodded at Zephyrine— "is wherever she says it is, which is nowhere you'll find it, because she's better at hiding things than your entire records division, and I would know, because I *am* your entire records division."

That wasn't entirely true. Zephyrine had given one copy to the elders' runner, yes. But the cairn copy was a bluff, and the third copy was the satchel currently pressed against her ribs, and Wren knew it, and Zephyrine knew she knew it, and Stave did not know any of it, which was the whole architecture of the lie: a building made entirely of trust between two women who had spent most of their acquaintance pointing problems at each other.

Stave's smile finally flickered. It was, Zephyrine thought, like watching a glacier calve—slow, structural, inevitable.

"You'd throw away your career," Stave said to Wren. "Your pension. Your *protection*. Do you understand what happens to people who cross the Optimists?"

"I've read the file," Wren said. "I wrote most of it."

And then Zephyrine picked which parent drove.

She stood up, walked past the rifles—past Stave's widening eyes, past Halvard's soft exhale that might have been a laugh—until she reached the two remaining collectors who were holding the stretcher's handles, and she held out the satchel.

"Take him home," she said.

Stave blinked. "What?"

"Halvard. His body. Take it down the river to the village above the flood line. There's an elder there named Signe who will know what to do with him. You'll do it gently, and you'll do it today, and if a single button is missing from his coat when he arrives, I will spend the rest of my life making the Optimists' name mean something ugly in every valley from here to the sea. I have the log. I have copies. I have nothing left to lose, which makes me, and I want you to appreciate the irony, the single best-armed person on this mountain."

"That's—" Stave started.

"Insane?" Zephyrine offered. "Yes. My mother warned me about that too."

She set the satchel in the collector's arms. Inside it: the unredacted station log, wrapped in oilcloth, along with a note she'd written in the vault by lantern light, addressed to Signe, to the elders, to whoever kept the names of the drowned. Her leverage. Her freedom. Her parents' testimony, going home the long way, in the arms of the men who had chased it up a mountain.

Her hands were empty. She had expected the emptiness to feel like falling.

It felt like setting down something heavy she'd forgotten she was carrying.

* * *

**Wren**

Wren had arrested people before. It was, technically, her job—or had been, twenty minutes ago, in the previous life. She knew the choreography: the reading of charges, the walking of the perp, the careful maintenance of professional distance.

So it was strange, cuffing Zephyrine Iversen's wrists behind her back on the ice below the source of the Sorrowline, to find that her own hands were shaking worse than the prisoner's.

"Comfortable?" Wren asked.

"Cozy," Zephyrine said. "You missed a spot."

"I did not miss a spot. I'm an excellent cuff-er. I have certificates."

"You have certificates for putting handcuffs on people?"

"I have certificates for *everything*. It's the tragedy of my life." Wren tightened the last strap, gentler than protocol required, and lowered her voice. "For what it's worth—you could have traded it. I watched you decide not to. I don't think anyone's ever shown me that in person. I've only read about it in reports I falsified."

Zephyrine glanced back at her, snow in her lashes, and grinned—the same grin she'd worn at the gas station, at the roadblock, on the collapsing goat track, the grin of a woman whose plans had just caught fire and who found the warmth agreeable. "You're going to be insufferable about this, aren't you. 'I arrested the woman who toppled the Optimists.'"

"'Assisted,'" Wren corrected. "'Arrested' implies I was on their side at the end, and I have a reputation to—"

She stopped. Down the slope, Stave's men were lifting Halvard's stretcher with exaggerated care, and Halvard's eyes were open, finding Zephyrine across the snow.

He couldn't speak anymore. Everyone could see it. But his hand rose off the stretcher frame—two fingers, lifted, the old surveyor's signal, the one that meant *line confirmed, position true*—and he held it there until the bend of the trail took him, and then it was just the five collectors and a dead man's body going down the mountain carrying the truth like pallbearers, which, Wren reflected, was probably the first honest work the Optimists had done in decades.

Zephyrine watched until the trail was empty. Then her knees buckled, just slightly, and Wren caught her elbow, and neither of them said anything about it, because some things are better handled by paperwork.

"So," Zephyrine said finally, voice rough. "They're taking me in. What happens to me?"

Wren looked down the valley—toward the pass, the drowned towns, the village where an elder named Signe was waiting for news, toward a river that ran backward through every map she'd ever trusted.

"Honestly?" she said. "I have no idea. By tomorrow morning, that log will be in every kitchen in the watershed. By next week, in every paper. Arresting you will be politically impossible by Thursday." She paused. "Also I no longer work for anyone, my truck is at the bottom of a melt channel, and I've committed approximately eleven kinds of treason. So the two of us are going to ride down out of these mountains together, you in cuffs for appearances, and we're going to have a very long conversation about what I'm supposed to do with the rest of my life."

"Could always go into storm chasing," Zephyrine said. "Terrible pay. Excellent company."

"Is that a job offer?"

"It's a job *offer-shaped object*. Read the fine print."

Wren clicked her tongue, shook her head, and began walking her prisoner down toward the light.

Behind them, the spring at the summit kept feeding the river, unredacted, running clear.
