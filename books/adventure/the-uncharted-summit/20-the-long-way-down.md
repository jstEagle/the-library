## 20. The Long Way Down

They arrested me on a Tuesday, which felt rude. Arrests, I'd always assumed, happened on Fridays—end of the week, end of the rope, everyone eager to get home. But the two officers who came up the valley trail with their radios crackling and their forms in triplicate did it on a Tuesday, in the gray hour after dawn, while the river-people stood along the bank and watched without saying a word.

"Zephyrine Iversen?" the taller one asked, though my hands were already behind my back and the cuffs were already on, so the question seemed mostly ceremonial.

"That depends on who's asking," I said. "If it's the Optimists, I'm a ghost. If it's the government, I'm a hero-in-waiting. If it's my bank, I'm definitely not here."

The shorter officer made a noise that might have been a laugh, strangled at birth by professionalism. "You're being detained pending investigation into trespass on closed federal land, removal of restricted documents, and—" he squinted at his form, "—'unauthorized dissemination of sealed records.' That last one's a mouthful."

"Tell me about it. I watched a man die to earn it."

That killed the mood nicely. The taller officer—Officer Brannic, according to the tag on his chest, though the way he carried himself suggested he'd have preferred to be anonymous—steered me toward the bank where a government skiff idled against the stones. Downriver, the Sorrowline ran broad and brown with snowmelt, the same river that had carried me up this valley in pieces: first in a truck, then on foot, then on hope, which floats worse than anything.

The elder, Maren, came down to the water's edge. She was small and weathered as driftwood, and she took my cuffed hands in hers like she was checking a knot.

"They're taking you down the river," she said.

"That's the general direction of jails, I'm told."

"The log goes with us." Her eyes were dry and steady. "By tonight, every settlement on this water will have read it. By tomorrow, the towns. By the day after—" she smiled, and it was not a comforting smile, at least not if you were an Optimist, "—everyone."

"Good," I said. And then, because my mouth has never met a feeling it didn't immediately try to turn into a joke: "I'd have preferred a parade, but a paper trail will do."

Maren squeezed my hands once, hard, and let go. "Your mother had that same mouth."

I didn't get to answer, because Officer Brannic said "ma'am" in the tone of a man who wanted very much to be anywhere else, and the skiff pushed off, and the village that wasn't on any map slid away behind us into the morning mist.

* * *

The descent of the Sorrowline takes three days by water. I know this because I made the trip twice—once up, in a truck with a dying man who hummed a lullaby he had no right to know, and once down, in custody, with two officers who couldn't decide whether I was a criminal or a story.

They settled on both, eventually. Officers Brannic and Oduya. Brannic was tall, taciturn, and secretly sentimental—I caught him reading the photostats of the log over my shoulder on the first night and pretending he wasn't. Oduya was short, talkative, and had opinions about everything, including my hair, my driving record (which she'd apparently pulled), and the quality of government rations.

"You're the storm chaser," she said, somewhere past the drowned valley on the second morning, handing me coffee I was permitted to hold but not, apparently, to enjoy with both hands free. "The one who chases supercells for the insurance consortiums."

"Chased. Past tense. The Optimists have a lien on my truck, my gear, and—until recently—my future."

"And now?"

"Now I'm a cautionary tale with a body count." I said it lightly. It didn't land lightly. Oduya looked at me with something like pity, and I looked at the river, and we let it go.

The towns came and went. The first one wouldn't speak the river's name—hadn't for thirty years, since the flooding, since the Incident that officially never happened. I watched from the skiff as Maren's messengers arrived there, on foot and by raft, carrying copies of the log wrapped in oilcloth. I watched a woman in a shop doorway take one, read the first page, and sit down slowly on her own step like her legs had been unplugged.

By the second town, they weren't just reading it. They were copying it. By hand, by press, by whatever means a river town keeps alive. A schoolteacher had typed the whole thing overnight on a manual machine and was distributing pages to anyone with a pocket. A ferryman read passages aloud to his passengers like scripture. Somebody had already set the casualty list—the real one, the unredacted one, the one with my parents' expedition on it—to a broadside ballad, which was premature and sentimental and made me cry into my coffee where Oduya could pretend not to see.

"You knew," I said to her, on the second night, camped on a gravel bar under a sky full of indifferent stars. "You two knew what was in that log before you ever saw me. That's why the arrest warrant came so fast. And that's why you're taking the slow route downriver."

Brannic looked at the fire. Oduya looked at me.

"The warrant says expedite," she admitted. "We're averaging four knots."

"The river averages four knots."

"Then we're right on schedule." She poked the fire. "Look. I've been on this water eleven years. My father fished it before the closure. I've read the official report—the one that says a natural flood, an unforeseeable event, a tragedy of geology. I read that report at the academy and I thought, huh, funny how the geology only flooded the villages that were asking questions." She shrugged. "I'm not a hero. I'm following the river at river speed. If the river happens to be slow this week, that's hydrology."

"Hydrology," I repeated.

"Hydrology," Brannic said gravely, and for the first time I saw the corner of his mouth move.

* * *

The Optimists, meanwhile, were having a very bad week, and I confess I enjoyed it more than I probably should have.

You have to understand the Optimists to appreciate what happened. They were cheerful the way a landslide is cheerful—lots of movement, lots of noise, everything downhill and on schedule. Their collectors smiled. Their contracts smiled. Their interest rates smiled so hard they had teeth. For thirty years they had run the river towns like a gentle machine, and the machine's fuel was debt, and the machine's product was silence. Everyone owed them something, and everyone knew that the price of falling behind was collateral, and everyone knew the collateral was never the thing you signed for. It was the thing you couldn't afford to lose. Your boat. Your shop. Your name.

And the log—the unredacted log, Halvard's log, the one I'd carried out of a frozen vault with my own frostbitten hands—was worth millions to them. Not for the paper. For the leverage. A document proving the government drowned a valley on purpose, and that the Optimists had spent thirty years buying up the grief of the survivors at ten cents on the dollar? That was a crowbar that could open anything. Ministries. Contracts. whole towns. They hadn't sent collectors after me for trespassing. They'd sent them because I was carrying a vault key the size of a nation's conscience.

Which is why what happened next was so beautiful, and so simple, and so exactly the kind of thing Halvard would have grinned at from whatever cloud or cairn he'd settled on.

They couldn't buy it anymore.

Maren's people didn't sell the log. They *published* it. Every page, every redaction line struck through and the truth printed underneath, every name restored—including, on the third page of the casualty annex, in a clerk's careful hand, *E. Iversen, surveyor, and R. Iversen, cartographer, lost attempting to warn downstream settlements, 14th of the Thaw Month.* My parents. In print. On every ferry landing from the headwaters to the sea.

You cannot leverage a secret that everyone has read. You cannot hold debt over a town that has stopped being ashamed. The Optimists' whole architecture was silence, and the log was daylight, and daylight, as it turns out, is the one solvent that eats cheerful.

I heard about it in fragments, because news travels upriver slower than water travels down. Oduya had a radio she wasn't strictly supposed to use, and she used it liberally, and she reported to me each morning like a herald with a hangover.

"Day one: the Optimists' regional office in Corvel issued a statement calling the log 'an unverified fabrication.' Day one, later: three of their own former collectors confirmed it's real. One of them cried on the radio. It was very moving."

"Day two?"

"Day two: the ministry announced an 'independent inquiry,' which is what governments say when they've checked which way the crowd is running and decided to jog out in front of it. Also, the Optimists' chief counsel resigned. Also, two of their biggest creditors called in their paper. Turns out nobody wants to be owed money by a drowning man."

"Day three?"

Oduya grinned. "Day three, they withdrew the lien on a 1974 storm-chasing truck, tan, one owner, extensive hail damage."

I put my coffee down very carefully so as not to spill it on my own disbelief. "They what?"

"Withdrew. The. Lien. Apparently when your leverage evaporates overnight, so does your enthusiasm for repossessing the vehicle of the woman at the center of the biggest scandal in a generation. Bad optics. Their words. I've been reading their internal memos, which are now also public, because day three was a *very* good day."

I sat with that. My truck. Half a lifetime of payments, of fees chased across the flats, of collectors smiling in my passenger seat with deadlines in their teeth—dissolved, not because I'd paid, but because the thing they'd been holding over me had turned to smoke in their hands. Halvard had promised me payment enough to erase the debt. He'd just neglected to mention the payment was the truth, and the interest was everything.

"Your charges," Brannic said that evening, in his way of dropping a stone into a conversation and watching the ripples, "are becoming politically impossible."

"Is that a legal term?"

"It's a real term. It means the ministry can't prosecute you without prosecuting itself. The trespass warrant came from an office that's now under inquiry. The sealing order came from a director who's now resigning. Every signature on your file is a signature on theirs." He paused. "It doesn't mean you're free. It means they're waiting to see which way the inquiry breaks before they decide what you are."

"And until then?"

"Until then," Oduya said, "you're our prisoner, and we are taking you downriver at four knots, and the river is very calm this time of year."

* * *

We passed the roadblock on the third day—the one that had turned us back on the way up, back when the river's name was contraband and Halvard had led me up a goat track instead. The barriers were still there, the rusted signage still standing: CLOSED BY ORDER OF, and then a department name that had since stopped returning calls. But the checkpoint hut was empty. The officers had better things to do, or worse ones, or they'd simply walked down to the landing to hear the ferryman read.

A woman at the crossing—old, in a river-people's coat, one of Maren's kin—flagged our skiff to the bank. Brannic tensed, then didn't, because she was carrying a pot of soup and the expression of a woman distributing soup to soldiers of whichever army happened to be hungry.

"You're the one they arrested," she said to me. Not a question. News on this river travels at four knots and arrives anyway.

"Allegedly arrested. We're still negotiating the verb."

She ladled soup into a tin cup and put it in my cuffed hands, which required some engineering and no small amount of dignity on both our parts. "My sister drowned in the flooding," she said. "Thirty-one years. There was no stone. There was no name. They told us the river took her, as if the river had a mind, as if the river hadn't been *sent*." She looked at the water, brown and patient and full of snowmelt. "Now there's a name. Now there's a stone being cut in every town from here to the delta. My sister's name is on three of them already."

"I'm glad," I said, and found I meant it in my bones, in the place where the lullaby lived.

"Your parents' names are on all of them." She said it simply, the way you'd report the weather. "The surveyors who tried to warn us. The whole valley knows. The whole *river* knows." She patted my shoulder once, hard, the way Maren had, as if it were a regional greeting. "Eat your soup."

I ate my soup. It was too hot and too salty and I would remember it for the rest of my life.

* * *

On the fourth morning, the skiff rounded the last bend above the delta towns, and the world had changed color.

Not literally—the Sorrowline was the same brown, the sky the same pale iron. But the landings were papered. Broadsheets everywhere, nailed to posts, pasted to shuttered Optimist kiosks, the casualty list and the containment report and the flooding order, all of it, in ink that ran a little in the river damp and didn't care. Children were selling copies. An old man had chalked the first line of the lullaby on the customs house wall, phonetically, the way you write a song you've only ever heard.

And on the dock where we tied up, waiting, with her hands in her coat pockets and an expression I'd last seen on a woman burning a signal flare into the river—stood Wren Calloway.

She looked terrible. She looked like a woman who had spent a week fabricating coordinates, disarming her own employer's men, and filing a resignation letter she'd written on the back of a survey form. She looked, in other words, fantastic.

"Officer Calloway," Brannic said carefully. "You're supposed to be in custody too, as I understand it."

"I surrendered myself this morning," Wren said. "They haven't decided what to do with me either. There's a lot of deciding not happening in the ministry this week. It's very restful." She looked at me, and her voice did something it almost never did, which was soften. "The elders released everything at dawn. Every settlement, every town, all at once, so nobody could stop it. Maren coordinated it like a military operation. It was beautiful. There was a man from the Optimists at the Corvel office trying to buy the printing plates and the printer's grandmother threatened him with a bread paddle."

"Good grandmother," I said.

"The best." Wren glanced at the officers, then back at me. "There's news. Two pieces. One: the inquiry opened formal hearings this morning, and the first witness called was a river elder. Not you. Not me. Maren. They put the *victim* on the stand first, which has never once happened in the history of this valley, and the room stood up when she walked in."

"And two?"

Wren's mouth twitched. "Two: your truck's at the Corvel impound. Released this morning. There's a parking ticket on the windshield from three weeks ago that they also, apparently, decided was politically impossible."

I laughed. It came out of me sideways and surprised, the first real laugh since the snow cave, and it turned into something wetter halfway through, and Wren had the grace to look at the river while I got myself back together.

"So," I said finally, wiping my face with the back of my cuffed hand, which is harder than it sounds. "What now? They walk us to a cell and wait for the inquiry to decide we're martyrs or felons?"

"Actually," Wren said, and here her voice did the other thing, the thing it did when she was about to break a rule and had already finished feeling guilty about it, "the hearing's adjourned for the Thaw Month observances. Nobody can be sentenced during the mourning days—it's an old river law, one of the few they kept. Which means you, Officer Brannic, Officer Oduya, and I are all technically free persons of the valley for exactly one week."

Brannic cleared his throat. "That is a technically accurate reading of the statute."

"Brannic," Oduya said, delighted, "you beautiful bureaucratic criminal."

"And there's a memorial being held at the source," Wren went on, watching me. "At the spring. The elders are cutting the stone themselves. Your parents' names, Halvard's name, all of them, read aloud where they were silenced. Maren sent word downriver this morning." She paused. "She asked for you. By name. She said—" Wren's voice caught, just barely, and she covered it with dryness, "—she said the valley doesn't return its dead, but it does return its daughters."

The river ran on behind us, brown and broad and finally, after thirty-one years, allowed to be called what it was. Somewhere upstream, under ice that was letting go, a research station sat open to the light for the first time in a generation. Somewhere upstream, a cairn waited for a compass.

"Well," I said to the officers, to Wren, to the whole grinning, grieving, wide-awake valley. "Anyone got a truck?"
