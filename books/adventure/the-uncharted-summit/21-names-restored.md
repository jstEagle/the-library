## 21. Names Restored

Spring came up the Sorrowline the way forgiveness does—slowly, and from the bottom.

Zephyrine watched it happen from the porch of Maren Ostergaard's house, which was not really a house so much as a stubborn argument with gravity, built on stilts above the flood line with a view of a valley that had spent forty years pretending to be empty. The ice on the river went gray, then rotten, then went all at once in a single warm night, and the whole village stood out on the banks to listen to it crack and groan like an old man getting out of a chair. Nobody cheered. They just listened, the way you'd listen to someone finally telling the truth after decades of lying about it.

"First thaw I can remember that doesn't feel like holding your breath," Maren said, handing her a mug of something that claimed to be coffee and was mostly ambition.

Below them, children were doing the dangerous thing they always did—testing the new water with sticks, daring each other closer to the edge. One of them, a girl of maybe eight named Tove who had appointed herself Zephyrine's shadow since the arrest, looked up and waved with both arms, nearly losing her stick in the process.

"She's decided you live here now," Maren said.

"I'm under investigation."

"You're under investigation *here*, though. That's different from being investigated somewhere else." Maren shrugged, as if this settled the matter of residency entirely, and in the valley's accounting, perhaps it did.

The charges hadn't stuck. That was the thing nobody quite knew how to talk about yet—the way the whole apparatus had simply... stalled. Wren's fabricated coordinates had bought three days, and in those three days the elders had done what the elders did: they'd read. Every household got a copy of Halvard's log, printed on the village's ancient duplicator until the ink ran thin and they started copying pages by hand, schoolchildren tracing the redacted lines and filling them back in with pencil, letter by letter, like a congregation restoring a burned scripture. By the time the collectors reached the low country with their prisoner and their seized documents, the documents were already everywhere. Leverage, it turned out, evaporates fast in daylight. The Optimists' cheerful men had arrived at the district courthouse smiling and left smiling too—it was policy—but the smiles had gone thin as river ice in April, and the debt ledger with Zephyrine's name on it had been quietly sold to a holding company that was itself quietly dissolved, and somewhere in that shuffle her number simply stopped existing.

She didn't trust it. She kept waiting for the collector in the good coat to appear in her passenger seat again with his terrible patient smile. But spring kept coming instead, day after day, and no one came, and slowly she began to entertain the radical possibility that she was, for the first time in her adult life, unowned.

Not free, exactly. Free was a word for people with nothing to answer for. But unowned. It would do for now.

* * *

They built the memorial where the station used to be, or rather where its bones still jutted from the ice like the ribcage of something enormous that had lain down to die and been politely covered by winter ever since. The reopening crews had come through in March—actual government surveyors, nervous ones, mapping a valley that official maps had refused to acknowledge for two generations—and they'd cordoned off the vault with yellow tape that everyone ignored within a week, because you cannot tape over a grave and expect people to act like it's a hazard instead of a door.

Halvard's body had gone into the ground up there in the fall, before the deep cold, in a spot he'd marked on his own map with a small inked X that Zephyrine hadn't understood until she'd stood on it. The map, she realized late, had never been about treasure at all. It had been a set of directions home, drawn by a dying man for whoever was stubborn enough to drive him.

Now it was May, and the snow had pulled back far enough up the pass, and the elders declared that the names would be read at the source, where the water came out of the mountain clean and cold and indifferent, the way truth is when you finally get your hands on it.

The whole valley walked up. That was the part that undid her—not the ceremony, not the words, but the sheer arithmetic of it. Two hundred and some people climbing the old goat track in their Sunday clothes and their work boots, carrying children on their shoulders and thermoses of coffee, because for forty years there had been nowhere to carry grief *to*. Grief needs an address. The government had deleted the address. Now here they all were, walking uphill through melting drifts to deliver four decades of mail.

Maren's brother Soren had built a cairn from stones hauled up in sledges, and against it leaned a slab of slate on which the names had been chiseled—every name from the log, the technicians and the surveyors and the downstream families whose villages had drowned on a schedule drawn up in an office, all of it listed in the same hand, same size letters, because Soren had insisted. "No hierarchy in a graveyard," he'd said, spitting on his palms. "That's how they got away with it. They ranked the dead."

The elder who had once stared at Halvard like a ghost—her name was Ingrid Solheim, and she was small and straight-backed and had hands like tree roots—stood before the slate with a paper that trembled only slightly in the wind.

"We read these names," she said, "because reading is a kind of digging. For forty years we were told the river's source was nothing. A blank space. We were told our grandmothers' stories were exaggerations. We were told to be grateful for the resettlement." She paused, and the wind moved through the crowd like a held breath. "We were lied to cheerfully. And we believed it, some of us, because the lie came with a payment plan."

A ripple of laughter, dark and healing, went through the crowd.

"So today we read the names. Not to accuse. Accusation is for courts, and courts are coming, let them do their work. We read the names because a person who is named can be mourned, and a person who is mourned can rest, and a valley full of resting dead is a valley you can live in again."

Then she began, and the names came one by one, and each one was taken up by someone in the crowd—a descendant, a neighbor, someone who repeated it aloud so it landed twice, once on the air and once in the stone.

Zephyrine stood at the edge of it with her hands in her pockets, telling herself she was fine, right up until the moment Ingrid reached the survey team.

"Iversen, Astrid. Surveyor. Iversen, Tomas. Surveyor."

Her mother's name. Her father's name. Spoken aloud in daylight, in the open, in a voice that wasn't hers, by people who had waited their whole lives to say them.

Someone near her took up the names—a broad-shouldered woman Zephyrine didn't know, repeating them clear and strong—and Zephyrine found that her legs had opinions of their own and had sat her down on a rock without consulting her. She stared at the spring, at the water rising out of the mountain, and thought: *they died trying to warn these people.* The log had said it plainly, in her father's handwriting, in the margins: *downstream villages must be evacuated, we have maybe six days, why is no one answering the radio.* Six days. Her parents had spent their last week shouting into a silence that had been manufactured on purpose, and then the flood had come on schedule, and the silence had been filed as an accident.

And now a woman with root-hands was saying their names into that same silence, and the silence was breaking apart like the river ice, and Zephyrine sat on her rock and cried the way she imagined you cry when a debt is forgiven—not with relief exactly, but with the strange vertigo of realizing how long you'd been braced.

Tove appeared beside her, solemn as a magistrate, and put a stick in her hand. For poking the spring, apparently. Or for leaning on. Zephyrine chose leaning on, and the girl nodded approval, and they stayed like that until the last name was read and the crowd began, gently, to become a picnic.

* * *

Wren arrived at the memorial late, on foot, dusty, and trying very hard to look like an officer of the survey corps and not like a woman who had torched her own career in a rope bridge incident and a series of increasingly creative falsifications.

The uniform was new. That was the first thing Zephyrine noticed. Same cut, but the insignia was different—no more audit division, no more permit enforcement. Just the plain badge of the field service, the lowest rung, the one they give you when they don't trust you with anything worth stealing.

"They demoted me," Wren said, dropping onto the rock beside her with a groan. "Officially. Unofficially, they gave me the reopened valley file and told me to map it, which is either a punishment or the best assignment in the department, depending on whether you ask my supervisor or me."

"What happened to the collectors?"

"The Optimists?" Wren snorted. "Under investigation themselves, now. Turns out when you spend forty years buying survey officers and drowning valleys, somebody eventually reads your paperwork. There's a hearing. I'm expected to testify." She pulled a face. "I've never testified before. I've only *not* noticed things professionally. It turns out noticing things out loud is terrifying."

"You'll be good at it. You're a terrible liar. It's why I trusted you."

"That's the nicest thing anyone's said to me since the bridge." Wren dug in her pack and produced a flask, two tin cups, and a look of ceremonial importance. "Also, I have news. Two pieces. One of them involves your truck."

Zephyrine went very still. "You're joking."

"Recovered in the spring melt below the collapsed track. Mostly intact. The meltwater took the passenger door and everything in the cab, but—" Wren handed her a key ring, worn brass, unmistakably hers. "It's in the impound yard at the district depot. I signed it out. Well. I signed it out *aggressively*."

"My truck," Zephyrine said, turning the keys over. The brass was cold and familiar as an old scar. "I assumed it was at the bottom of a ravine financing somebody else's retirement."

"It nearly financed mine. I considered it. Briefly." Wren poured two cups from the flask and handed one over. "Which brings me to piece two. The valley file needs a surveyor. Someone who knows the high country, someone the river-people will actually talk to, someone with—" she gestured vaguely at all of Zephyrine, "—a documented history of ignoring roadblocks. The pay is modest. The hours are criminal. The scenery is—" she nodded at the spring, the cairn, the slate with its hundred-odd names catching the light, "—well. You've seen the scenery."

Zephyrine looked at the cup in her hand, then at the water coming out of the mountain, and felt something in her chest do a thing she refused to name in front of Wren Calloway.

"You're offering me a job mapping the place I just got arrested in."

"I'm offering you a job mapping the place you just got *un*-arrested in. There's a difference. Legally, I mean. Probably. My understanding of the law has gotten refreshingly flexible lately." Wren raised her cup. "Also the truck comes with the position. Department vehicle. Sort of. It's complicated. There's paperwork."

"There's always paperwork."

"There's always paperwork," Wren agreed solemnly, "and then there's the Sorrowline, which has never once appeared on any of it."

Down at the spring, the children had given up on ceremony entirely and were racing sticks down the outflow channel, and Tove was winning, and Ingrid Solheim had begun telling a story to a circle of young people with her hands moving like birds, and the wind off the ice carried the smell of stone and running water and, faintly, coffee from someone's thermos.

Zephyrine drank. The flask's contents were terrible—Wren's coffee had the same relationship to actual coffee that the old maps had to the actual valley—and she said so, and Wren laughed, and somewhere above them a patch of cornice let go with a soft boom and slid harmlessly into the sun.

"So," Wren said. "Do we have a deal, or do I have to double the offer? Because I've heard that works on you."

Zephyrine looked at her for a long moment. Then at the keys in her palm. Then at the spring, running clean and unhidden for the first time in forty years, going wherever water goes when it finally stops being a secret.

"Double it," she said, "and I'll want hazard pay for your coffee."

They shook on it, and the mountain, which had heard every promise ever made in its presence and believed almost none of them, seemed—for once—to believe this one.
