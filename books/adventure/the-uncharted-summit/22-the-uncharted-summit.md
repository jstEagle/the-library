## 22. The Uncharted Summit

The spring came late to the high country that year, the way old men come home from long trips—slowly, and all at once.

Zephyrine felt it first in her boots. All winter the ground had been a rumor of stone under snow, and then one morning in April she stepped out of the elder's guest hut and the earth gave back a soft answer instead of a hard one. Meltwater ticked in the eaves. Somewhere down-valley, a river that had spent four months holding its breath began to exhale.

She'd stayed through the cold months without quite deciding to. That was the thing about belonging, she was learning: it didn't announce itself with paperwork or signatures. It just kept feeding you breakfast until you noticed you lived somewhere.

"You're up early," Wren said, appearing at her elbow with two tin mugs and the particular smugness of a person who has been awake for an hour already. Wren had arrived in February with the truck—Zephyrine's truck, resurrected from the melt-scour below Thaw Line by means Wren refused to explain beyond "a winch, three favors, and a very patient mule"—and had never quite left either. She claimed it was for the survey work. The valley was being remapped now, officially, its name restored to the documents like a bone set straight after decades of healing crooked. There was plenty of survey work. There would be for years.

Zephyrine took the mug. It was tea, mostly, with ambitions toward coffee. "I couldn't sleep."

"Because of tomorrow?"

"Because of everything." She blew on the tea. "But yes. Tomorrow."

They stood together at the edge of the village terrace and watched the light come down the far wall of the valley, gilding the terraces one by one, the way it had every morning since the world began and only recently started to feel like something anyone was allowed to watch.

Tomorrow they would climb to the source.

* * *

The village had voted on it in March, which had surprised Zephyrine—she'd assumed the memorial was simply going to happen, the way funerals happen—but Elder Sefa had insisted. "Nothing about this place happens anymore without everyone saying so," she'd said, rapping her stick on the floor for emphasis. "For forty years things happened *to* us. We are done being terrain." The vote had been unanimous except for one abstention from a man who explained, at length, that he did not object to the memorial but objected to speeches, and could the speeches please be under ten minutes each. He was appointed to the speech-timing committee as a joke and took the responsibility with terrifying seriousness.

The log had gone public in January. Zephyrine still remembered the night: the elders reading Halvard's unredacted pages aloud by lamplight while a runner carried copies downriver through the dark, station to station, town to town, faster than any official denial could travel. By morning the Sorrowline's true history was everywhere—the containment failure, the sealed records, the ordered flooding, the survey team that had died trying to warn the villages downstream. Her parents' names were in the third paragraph of every account. Not as casualties of an accident. As people who had been right, and been silenced for it.

The Optimists had folded within the week. Leverage, it turned out, was a currency that hyperinflated overnight when someone printed the truth. Their cheerful collectors had become suddenly less cheerful, then suddenly scarce. Zephyrine's debt hadn't been forgiven—it had simply stopped existing, the way a shadow stops existing when you finally get the lamp lit. She'd expected to feel triumphant. Mostly she'd felt tired, and then hungry, and then, sitting in Sefa's kitchen eating bread while the radio read out names, she'd felt something she didn't have a word for yet. She thought maybe the word was *home*, but she wasn't ready to say it out loud. Words like that needed to be earned slowly, like trust, like altitude.

* * *

They left at dawn, the two of them plus a small procession that thinned as the trail steepened. Sefa came as far as the tree line, walking with her stick and refusing all offers of an arm. "I'm coming next year," she said, breathing hard but not stopping. "Next year there'll be a proper path. This year I'll settle for seeing you off properly." She pressed something into Zephyrine's hand—a twist of cloth around bread and dried berries. "For the summit. You don't arrive anywhere empty-handed. That's not how mountains work."

Above the tree line the world opened into the wide pale country of rock and lingering snow that Zephyrine had once thought of as empty. She knew better now. Every slope here had a name in the river-people's tongue; every boulder field had a story. Wren had spent the winter learning them, notebook full of phonetic spellings that made Sefa laugh, and she narrated as they climbed—"That's the Sleeping Shepherd, and no, don't ask why he's sleeping, it's a sad story and we haven't got the wind for it"—until Zephyrine told her she sounded like a tour guide, and Wren said, "I am one, technically. First licensed guide this valley's had in fifty years. They gave me a badge." She showed the badge. It was a wooden button with a sheep carved on it. She wore it like a medal.

They reached the cairn by mid-morning.

It had grown over the winter. What had been a modest pile of stones when Zephyrine last stood here—a marker Halvard's shaking hands had helped build, back when building it was the only funeral anyone could give him—was now a proper cairn, waist-high, ringed with offerings the villagers had carried up through the spring thaw: carved birds, a child's mitten, a rusted lantern polished bright, strips of cloth in colors that meant mourning in some families and celebration in others and, apparently, both at once in this one. Someone had planted a rowan sapling in the lee of the largest stone. It looked improbable and stubborn and exactly right.

Halvard's name was cut into the flat capstone, along with eleven others. The twelve who had died at the station, or because of what the station buried. And beneath them, added more recently in Sefa's careful hand, two more names:

**MAREN IVERSEN. TOBIAS IVERSEN.**

Zephyrine knelt and touched the letters. The stone was cold and honest under her fingers. She had read her parents' names a hundred times since January—in printouts, in newsprint, in the vault's own ledgers—and it had never stopped feeling like reading about strangers. But carved here, in this valley, in the stone of the place they had died trying to protect, the names finally fit their owners. She could hear her mother's laugh in the chisel marks. She could almost smell her father's pipe smoke on the wind off the icefield.

"Take your time," Wren said quietly, and went to stand lookout at the ridge, which was unnecessary, since there was nothing up here to look out for except weather and weather was currently behaving beautifully, but Zephyrine understood it was the polite fiction of privacy, and she was grateful for it.

She talked to the stones for a while. Told them about the winter. About the memorial vote and the speech-timing committee. About how the schoolhouse had reopened with nine children in it, and how one of them, a ferocious girl named Petra, had announced she intended to be a storm chaser when she grew up, and how Zephyrine had given her a lecture about safety that lasted twenty minutes and convinced no one, least of all herself. About the dreams she still had sometimes—not nightmares anymore, just dreams, her parents' expedition moving through these same passes in better weather, waving.

Then she took out the compass.

It had ridden in her breast pocket all winter, through the arrest and the descent and the hearings and the strange anticlimactic spring. The brass was worn smooth where Halvard's thumb had worried it for sixty years. The needle swung lazily, found north, and settled, pointing not at the summit above them but at the spring below—the headwater, the source, the place where the Sorrowline began and where, in the end, Halvard had asked to stop walking.

"Here you go, old man," she said, and set the compass on the capstone, needle aligned with the cairn's long axis, pointing home. "You made it. Took the scenic route, but you made it."

The wind moved. The needle trembled and held.

* * *

Wren came back down the ridge when Zephyrine waved, and they ate Sefa's bread sitting against the warm south face of the cairn, passing the twist of cloth back and forth like a couple of kids splitting contraband.

"So," Wren said, brushing crumbs off her survey jacket. "Speeches. Are we doing speeches? Because if we're doing speeches, I should warn you I prepared one, and it has visual aids."

"You did not prepare visual aids."

"I prepared a chart." Wren produced a folded paper from inside her jacket with the flourish of a magician. She unfolded it. It was a hand-drawn graph titled, in careful block letters, **THINGS THAT TURNED OUT TO MATTER MORE THAN MONEY**, with a single bar on it, labeled *everything*, stretching off the edge of the page.

Zephyrine laughed until her ribs hurt, the kind of laughter that starts as a response and becomes a release, and Wren grinned and folded the chart away, satisfied.

"I keep thinking," Zephyrine said, when she'd caught her breath, "about what I'd have said, a year ago, if someone told me where I'd be standing today. I'd have laughed at them. I was so sure the whole point was to get out. Get clear, get paid, get free. I thought freedom was a number in a ledger that finally hit zero." She turned the empty cloth over in her hands. "Turns out I was measuring the wrong thing."

"That's the trick of it," Wren said. "Took me thirty years and one burned flare to figure out my ledger was wrong too." She looked out over the valley, at the river threading silver through the greening floor, at the rooftops of the village that wasn't on any map and was now, thanks to her own revised surveys, on several. "You know what my favorite part of the new map is? The margin note. Official cartography, government printing, and there in the corner it says: *This valley was always here.*"

"They let you put that?"

"I may have snuck it in before the final proofing." Wren's smile was entirely unrepentant. "Nobody reads margins. That's where the true stuff lives."

Below them, the spring emerged from beneath the ice lip in a pulse of impossible blue, gathering itself, becoming the river. Zephyrine watched it the way you watch a sleeping face.

"Halvard said something, near the end," she said. "In the snow cave. I've been turning it over all winter. He said, 'Some summits aren't climbed, Zeph. Some are returned to.' I thought it was the fever talking. But he knew. He knew exactly what he was asking me to do—not to conquer anything, not to plant a flag. To carry something back up the mountain and set it down where it belonged." She shook her head. "A year ago I'd have called that a waste of a perfectly good adventure."

"And now?"

"Now I think it might be the only kind worth having."

* * *

The lullaby had come back to her in pieces, all winter, the way water comes back to a dry streambed—one thread finding another thread until suddenly there was a current. Sefa had taught her the verses she'd forgotten, the ones her parents must have sung and never gotten the chance to finish teaching. The elder had wept the first time Zephyrine sang it through complete, and then scolded her for making her weep, and then demanded she sing it again.

Now, standing at the cairn with the wind at her back and the whole valley listening below, Zephyrine sang it all.

Her voice cracked on the second verse and held on the fourth. The melody was simple, older than the disaster, older than the maps—it was a rope song, Sefa had explained, a working song, sung by families hauling loads up the switchbacks generations ago, each verse a stretch of the climb, until the last verse, which wasn't about climbing at all. The last verse was about arriving. About setting your burden down. About how the mountain doesn't take anything from you that you don't also give it, and how what you give it, it keeps safe.

*Sleep, the river knows your name,*
*it carried it up before you came.*
*Sleep, the stone remembers too—*
*what was hidden will shine through.*
*Rest now, rest, the climb is done,*
*the summit was the way back home.*

When she finished, neither of them spoke for a long time. The wind moved through the rowan sapling. Far below, tiny as toys, figures were coming up the lower trail—Sefa, having ignored her own promise to wait until next year, leading a line of villagers carrying flowers and a thermos of something hot, because of course they were, because you do not arrive anywhere empty-handed and you do not let other people arrive alone either.

Wren put her hand on Zephyrine's shoulder. "Your parents would be proud of you," she said. "And Halvard would make some terrible joke about the acoustics up here."

"He really would." Zephyrine wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand and laughed. "He'd say the reverb was wasted on amateurs."

They stood together at the top of the world's most misunderstood mountain, two women who had each spent years running—from debt, from duty, from grief—and who had finally, by the roundabout route, by the goat track, by the long way down and the longer way back up, arrived.

Not conquered. Returned to.

Down in the valley, the Sorrowline ran bright and unhidden toward the sea, carrying its names home.
