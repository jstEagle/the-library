## 1. Miles, Not Years

The odometer on Silas Vasquez's truck read 214,882 miles, and he trusted it more than he trusted most people.

He'd learned the number by heart the way other men learned their children's birthdays. Every morning he wrote it in the logbook with a pencil stub worn down to a nub, and every evening he wrote it again, and the difference between the two numbers was the day. Some days were forty miles. Some were a hundred and ten. Once — the day of the lightning fire two summers back — it had been two hundred and six, and his hands had still been shaking when he logged them at midnight.

Miles didn't lie. Miles didn't leave. You could count on a mile to be exactly what it was, start to finish, no matter how many times you drove it.

He'd been counting them for twelve years.

The high desert opened out ahead of him in the gray-blue light before sunrise, all of it his jurisdiction and none of it his: sixty thousand acres of sage flat and rimrock, cut through the middle by the river that gave the valley its name and three towns their reason for existing. Corlissville downstream. Redrock Landing at the ferry crossing. Little Hatcher up where the canyon walls pinched together and the water ran fast and cold even in August. He patrolled them all in a rotation so fixed he could tell what day of the week it was by which fence line he was mending.

Tuesday meant the north loop. North loop meant the old Hollis grazing allotment, the one nobody grazed anymore, and the long climb up Coyote Rim where the road turned to two ruts and then to a suggestion and then to nothing at all.

Silas drove it with the windows down. The air came in cold and smelled like sagebrush and dust and the faint mineral tang of snowmelt still working its way down from the high country, though it was only April and the melt had weeks left in it. A jackrabbit broke from the shoulder and loped alongside for fifty yards, ears laid back, as if escorting him, then thought better of it and angled off into the brush.

"Morning," Silas said to it anyway.

That was the thing about talking to yourself out here. Nobody heard you do it, which meant nobody could hold it against you. He'd gotten so used to the sound of his own voice that it had stopped sounding like anything — just another instrument reading, another entry in the log. *Wind northwest, ten miles an hour. Road passable to the second cattle guard. No traffic.*

No traffic. There was never any traffic. In twelve years he had logged exactly four vehicles on the north loop, and two of those had been lost hunters who were grateful to see him and one had been a geologist who wasn't, and the fourth had been a black sedan with government plates that sat parked at the rim overlook for forty minutes before driving away without anyone getting out. He'd written that one down too. *Sedan, dark, plates obscured by dust. Occupant did not exit vehicle.* Dispatch had laughed at him over the radio. *You writing poetry now, Vasquez?*

He hadn't minded. Being laughed at was a kind of company.

The radio crackled once as he topped Coyote Rim — a burst of static, the carrier wave from the repeater station on Mount Aldous, thirty miles south and invisible behind the ridgeline. Then silence again. He checked the set without breaking stride, thumbed the volume up a notch out of habit, and let it go. Silence was the desert's native language. Everything else was translation.

From the rim he could see the whole northern half of his district spread out below him like something spilled: the sage flats going silver where the frost hadn't burned off yet, the dark stitch of the river willows, the blue wall of the far mesa. Somewhere down there, a coyote was finishing its night shift, singing the last verse of its song to no audience at all. Silas listened until it quit.

*Beautiful country,* people always told him, when they bothered to tell him anything. *Must be nice, having all that to yourself.*

And it was. That was the honest answer, the answer he'd worn smooth as river stone over twelve years of giving it. It was nice having all that to himself. Loneliness, done long enough, stopped being loneliness and became something else — a kind of weather you lived inside, a climate. You dressed for it. You stopped noticing it. You built your whole life around the assumption of it the way a cactus builds around the assumption of drought, and then one day you realized the cactus wasn't suffering. The cactus had simply made peace with the terms.

Silas Vasquez had made peace with the terms.

He ate breakfast on the tailgate — cold beans out of the can, coffee from the dented percolator he ran off the truck's battery, two tortillas gone stiff at the edges but fine once they hit the cast iron. He logged the mileage. 214,917. Thirty-five miles since dawn. He wrote the date, the wind, the temperature, the condition of the road, and under *observations* he wrote, after some consideration:

*Coyote active near rim. Jackrabbit sign heavy. No traffic.*

Then he closed the book, and that was Tuesday's first entry complete, and there was nothing left in the world that required anything of him except the next thirty miles.

He was forty minutes down the far side of the rim, following the faint double-track toward the river bottom, when he saw the tracks.

At first he took them for deer. That was the automatic read — anything moving fast across open ground at this hour was deer or antelope, and antelope didn't come down into the breaks this time of year. But the track pattern was wrong for deer, wrong in a way his eye caught before his brain did, the way a musician hears a sour note before identifying it. Too regular. Too deliberate. A rhythm instead of a scatter.

He stopped the truck and got out and crouched over the prints in the alkali flat where the ground held an impression like wet clay holds a thumbprint.

Boot prints. Human. Size — he measured with his hand, span of fingers — smallish. Narrow heel. And here was the detail that put a cold finger on the back of his neck: the tread was a Vibram lug sole, the kind you bought at an outfitter in the city, not the kind you bought at the feed store in Corlissville. Nobody within eighty miles wore boots like that except maybe the geologist, and the geologist had gone home to Tucson in October and Silas knew because he'd logged the departure.

The gait told the rest of the story, and the story was strange. Long strides. Consistent. A pace that said *hurrying* without saying *panicked* — someone moving fast on purpose, someone who knew where she was going. And the track line itself ran dead straight across the flat, ignoring the two-track entirely, ignoring the path of least resistance, aimed like a compass needle at the green line of the river willows two miles east.

A woman, probably. On foot. Alone. Moving fast toward the river at five-forty in the morning in country where the nearest house was eleven miles away and the nearest help was him.

Silas stood up slowly and turned a full circle, scanning. The flat was empty in every direction, silver and still, the frost burning off now in low wisps where the sun found it. Nothing moved but the light.

He looked back down at the prints. Fresh — the crisp edges hadn't crumbled yet, and a thread of shadow still lay in each depression, which meant they couldn't be more than a couple hours old. Made in the dark, then. Or right at first light. Someone had walked through here while he was eating beans on a tailgate thirty miles away, and the ground had kept her secret for exactly as long as it took him to arrive.

*No traffic,* he'd written. He almost smiled. The desert had just corrected his paperwork.

He got back in the truck and didn't drive on. He sat with his hands on the wheel and felt the strangest sensation, one he had to take a moment to name because it had been so long since he'd felt it: curiosity. Not the idle kind, not the *wonder-what-the-weather'll-do* kind he rationed out daily like everything else. This was the sharp kind, the hooked kind, the kind that caught in you like a barbed point and pulled.

Twelve years of miles, and the miles had never once surprised him. That was the arrangement. That was the whole quiet contract he'd signed with the world: I will cross you, and you will not ask anything of me. The desert had honored its side faithfully. Every track he'd ever found out here had resolved into something explainable — cattle, coyotes, a rancher's stray horse, the geologist's sampling routes, once a set of tire tracks that turned out to be his own from three days earlier when he'd gotten turned around in a dust storm and been too proud to admit it even to his logbook.

But these tracks didn't resolve. These tracks pointed at the river and asked a question.

*Who walks alone toward the river at first light?*

Ranchers rode. Hunters drove. Poachers came at dusk and left before dawn, and they came in trucks on the roads, because nobody poached on foot in April when the fish weren't running and the season was closed anyway. Pilgrims didn't exist out here. Neither did wanderers — not really, not in the breaks, not in the cold. The high desert forgave a lot of mistakes but it collected on all of them, and everyone who lived within a hundred miles knew the arithmetic. Water was eleven miles behind her and the river was two ahead, and she'd chosen the river.

Running to something, or running from something. Those were the only two reasons anybody crossed open ground alone at that hour, and Silas had spent enough years reading sign to know that the tracks themselves usually told you which. Fear scattered a gait. Purpose lined it up.

Hers was lined up like fence wire.

He got the binoculars off the seat and glassed the willow line, sweeping slow, top to bottom. Green blur, gray blur, the river showing through in cold slivers. A heron lifted off somewhere and flapped away heavy and prehistoric. No movement that resolved into a person. She was already in the trees, or already at the water, or already gone to ground somewhere he couldn't see.

Silas lowered the binoculars and looked at the tracks again, and something in his chest did a thing he didn't have vocabulary for — a shifting, like ice going out on the river in March, the whole frozen surface groaning and giving way all at once underneath you.

Because here was the truth he'd have logged if logs took that kind of entry: he didn't want to drive past. Twelve years of driving past everything, cataloguing and continuing, and for the first time he could remember, the job and the man wanted the same thing. The job said investigate. The man — the man who talked to jackrabbits and ate breakfast alone on a tailgate and called that peace — the man wanted to follow those boot prints straight into the willows and find out who in God's name was walking through his empty country like she owned the urgency in it.

He got back in the truck and eased forward, following the track line at walking speed, matching her route mile for imagined mile. The prints stayed true. Across the alkali flat, through a stretch of greasewood where she'd picked her way without breaking stride, along the base of a low bluff where the ground firmed up and the impressions sharpened — and there, at the edge of the willows, they stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. One last print deeper than the others, toes dug in, and beside it the clear round mark of a knee. Someone had knelt here. Looked back, maybe. Listened. Chosen her moment.

Then the willows swallowed the story.

Silas stood at the tree line with his hat in his hand, which was a habit from funerals and church and nothing else, and listened the way the desert had taught him: not with his ears but with the whole surface of himself. Wind in the willows. Water over gravel, steady as breathing. A dove saying its one word over and over somewhere upstream.

And underneath it — faint, so faint he might have invented it — the knock of wood on wood. Twice. A pause. Twice again.

Somebody signaling. Or somebody building something. Or somebody warning somebody else that a stranger was standing at the edge of the trees with his hat in his hand.

Silas put the hat back on. He thought about the radio, about calling it in, about the comfortable bureaucratic machinery of *found tracks, investigating, will report.* He thought about how dispatch would say *probably a hiker, Vasquez, don't shoot anybody,* and how the words would close the question like a lid.

He didn't key the mic.

Instead he stepped off the alkali and into the willows, onto ground no one had logged in twelve years, following a stranger's tracks toward the river — and for the first time since he'd taken this job, Silas Vasquez was not measuring the distance.

He was closing it.
