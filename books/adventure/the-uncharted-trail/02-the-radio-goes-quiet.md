## 2. The Radio Goes Quiet

The radio woke him the way it always did — a crackle first, like something small dying in the wires, then Dispatch's voice arriving flat and tinny from a hundred miles of copper and canyon.

"Unit Seven, Unit Seven, come back."

Silas Vasquez lay still on his cot and let the sound hang in the dark of the station house. Outside, dawn was doing its slow gray work on the eastern rimrock, turning the black edge of the mesa into something almost blue. He knew the time without looking at his watch. Five-forty-one. The radio always came at five-forty-one, because Dispatch believed rangers should be awake before the country was, and because in eleven years Silas had never once given them a reason to believe otherwise.

He swung his feet to the cold plank floor and crossed to the set on its scarred wooden table. The coffee pot from last night sat where he'd left it, half an inch of bitter dregs gone thick as tar. He drank it anyway, standing, while he keyed the mic.

"Unit Seven. Go ahead, Dispatch."

"Morning, Vasquez." A pause, papers shuffling somewhere in a warm office in Corlissville. "You copy any traffic yesterday?"

"No."

"That's the problem." Another shuffle. "We've got a scientific expedition upriver — Hollis party, out of the university. Eleven souls total. They're supposed to check in every seventy-two hours on the shortwave relay at Mile Forty. Last contact was sixteen days ago."

Silas set down the cup. Sixteen days. The number landed wrong, the way a stair that isn't where your foot expects it lands wrong. He'd been running this stretch of river country for eleven years, and expeditions missed check-ins all the time — radios drowned, batteries died, somebody decided the science was more interesting than the schedule. But sixteen days meant four missed windows, and four missed windows meant Dispatch had been sitting on it, or arguing about it, for most of two weeks before anyone picked up a phone.

"Why am I hearing about it now?"

Another pause. Longer. He could picture the dispatcher — Reyes? No, not Reyes, that was the name of some guide he'd met once; the dispatcher was a heavyset man named Culver who chewed toothpicks and spoke like a man reading a grocery list — hunched over his desk with the morning's stack of memos, deciding how much to tell a ranger whose job description ended at trail maintenance and lost hunters.

"There's been," Culver said, "some discussion at the administrative level."

"About whether to look for them."

"About how to look for them. There are considerations."

Silas looked out the window. The light had crept down off the rimrock now and was working across the flats below the station, picking out the sage and the greasewood, the dry wash that ran toward the river a mile east. A jackrabbit moved through it, unhurried, certain of nothing but its own legs. Eleven years he'd watched this same square mile of morning, and it had never once been the same twice.

"What kind of considerations?" he asked.

Culver's voice went carefully neutral, which was how Silas knew the answer mattered. "The expedition was privately funded. Benefactor's underwriting the search too, so there's no budget question. She just wants it handled properly. Discreetly."

"She."

A toothpick clicked against teeth, audible even over the static. "Ada Corliss."

The name sat in the station house like a third person. Silas didn't answer right away, and Culver, misreading the silence as a bad connection, repeated: "Corliss. You copy?"

"I copy." He keyed off, then keyed back on. "Eleven people, Culver. Two weeks. That's not discreet territory. That's coroner territory."

"The benefactor's position is that they've likely relocated camp without notifying the relay. River parties do it."

"River parties don't skip four check-ins."

"Then you'll have a pleasant trip and a story for the lodge." The toothpick again. "You're the closest unit. Pack for ten days. Take the truck to the end of the Corliss grade, horses from there. Report by radio at each evening window if you can raise the relay."

"Copy," Silas said, because there was nothing else to say, and because saying it was easier than asking the question actually sitting in his chest, which was why Ada Corliss cared enough to pay for a search and not enough to want it loud.

He signed off. The static died. The station house settled back into its own silence, the tick of the cooling stove, the wind worrying the loose flashing on the roof — a repair he'd been meaning to make since March.

* * *

He packed the way he did everything, by habit, and the habit was the point. Bedroll, tarp, ten days of food weighed out to the ounce. Water bags filled and slung. Rifle cleaned though it didn't need cleaning, because cleaning it gave his hands something true to do while his head worked the problem. Ammunition. The medical kit Dispatch issued and nobody expected him to use. Rope. The long knife his father had carried, which he told himself he kept for practical reasons.

By seven the sun was fully up and the day had committed itself to heat, and Silas loaded the truck at the station's lean-to with the economy of a man who'd done it so many times his body no longer consulted his mind. That was the arrangement he'd made with his life, eleven years ago, standing over a fresh-dug grave with a bottle in one hand and a ranger application in the other: keep moving, keep counting, keep the days measured out in miles and maintenance logs so there was no room left over for anything else. It had worked. It had worked so well that some mornings he mistook it for peace.

He drove north along the river road with the windows down, dust boiling behind him, the water showing silver through the cottonwoods whenever the road bent close. The country was doing its late-summer pretending — everything gold and dry and reasonable-looking, hiding the fact that every living thing out here was one bad month from ruin. Cattle on the open range, thin as fence posts. A hawk riding the thermal over the burned slope from the fire two years back. He logged what he saw without thinking about logging it: mile forty-two, range cattle, twelve head, strung along the fenceline; mile fifty-one, culvert washed at the low crossing, passable high-side; mile sixty, the ruins of the old Vasquez place coming up on the left like they always did, roofless now, the adobe melting back into the earth it came from, the windmill tower leaning at the angle of a man listening hard for something that stopped answering years ago.

He didn't look at it. He looked at it every time.

His father had farmed that quarter-section for thirty years on water that had belonged to the family since before the territory had a name, until the year the ditch associations got reorganized and the paper trail bent south toward the Corliss holdings and the headgate upstream closed to three-quarters flow. There were men who fought those things with lawyers. His father wasn't a lawyer kind of man. He fought it the way the land fights drought — slowly, invisibly, and losing — and when the bank took the place he fought it with whiskey instead, and the river collected him the following spring, face-down in the shallows below the diversion dam, and the coroner wrote *accidental drowning* in a hand so neat it looked printed.

Silas had been nineteen. He'd stood at the funeral between relatives he no longer spoke to and listened to a Corliss company man — sent, God help him, to pay respects, meaning to be seen paying respects — say what a fine operation old Tomás Vasquez had run, and how the valley would miss men of his generation, and Silas had understood even then that the sentence was a receipt. Paid in full. Account closed.

He took the ranger job eight months later because it was the only work that kept him in the country he loved and kept him moving through it fast enough that love couldn't catch up with him. Eleven years of mileage logs. Eleven years of radio silence broken only by Dispatch's grocery-list voice. It had worked.

So why, he asked himself, easing the truck around the washout at the old ferry landing, did the word *Corliss* sit in his chest this morning like a stone swallowed wrong?

Because of the phrasing. That was it. *Handled properly. Discreetly.* He'd heard those words before, in other mouths, about other things — about the water reorganization, about the scrip system at the company store, about a strike at the mill that ended with two organizers leaving town overnight and never writing back. The words always sounded reasonable. They always meant the same thing: *quietly, and in our favor.*

Eleven scientists and guides. Somebody's daughter or son in every one of them. And the richest woman in the territory paying to have them found before anybody made noise about their being lost.

He parked at the end of the Corliss grade — the road itself named for the family, graded and graveled with company money, better than anything the county maintained — and began transferring load to the horses. The bay mare, Juniper, stood for the packsaddle with her usual martyred patience. The mule, whom Silas had never named because naming a mule only encouraged it, watched him with the flat contempt mules reserve for all human endeavor.

"You and me both," Silas told it.

* * *

He made fourteen miles the first day, up the west bench above the river, and camped in the lee of a rock formation the old maps called the Priest but everybody else called the Monk, a hoodoo of red sandstone with a tilted crown that did look faintly clerical in evening light. He raised the relay at the evening window — three tries, nothing but carrier wave and the ghost-hiss of dead air — and logged it, and ate beans, and sat afterward with his back against the Monk's base watching the stars come out over the rimrock in their indifferent billions.

Two weeks. Eleven people. He turned the arithmetic over the way he turned over tracks in soft ground, looking for the story underneath.

If they'd relocated camp, someone would have left word at the relay — a note wired ahead, a marker cairn, anything. If the radio failed, they'd have sent a boatman downriver to the landing at Mile Forty; the river was their road, it ran right past the relay station, and any fool could float to it faster than hike away from it. If they'd had an accident — a flash flood, a collapse, a wrecked supply raft — there'd be debris on the banks, and the river would have carried some of it downstream where trappers and fishermen would have found it and talked about it, and Dispatch would have heard the talk, because Dispatch heard everything eventually.

None of that had happened. Which left the possibilities that didn't get said aloud over government radio: sickness, violence, or intent. Eleven people going silent together, on purpose, meant they were hiding from something. Eleven people going silent together by force meant someone had made them.

And either way, Ada Corliss wanted the finding done quietly.

He banked the fire early and lay in his bedroll listening to the river talking to itself a quarter mile below, that endless low conversation water has with stone. Sleep came the way it always came for him — fast, total, dreamless, a merciful blackout he'd learned to fall into like a man stepping off a ledge on purpose.

* * *

The second day he cut sign.

It happened at Mile Sixty-Eight, where the bench narrowed between the river cliffs and a talus slope, and the trail — such as it was, a stock route worn into the slickrock by a century of hooves — crossed a patch of ground where the sand held a print the way paper holds ink. He'd have ridden past it. Any rider would. But eleven years of measuring the country in miles had taught his eye to flag what didn't belong, and something about the pattern of disturbance at the edge of the trail snagged him the way a loose thread snags a sleeve.

He reined in. Dismounted. Stood a moment letting the horses settle, letting his own attention settle, the way you have to before reading ground, because a hurried tracker reads what he hopes to find.

Boot prints. One set, recent — the edges still crisp, the sand not yet slumped back into the hollows, which put it inside a day, maybe less in this dry air. A woman's boot by the size, narrow, medium heel, the tread worn smooth at the ball. Walking fast — stride length told him that, and the toe drag on the push-off, and the way the prints avoided the loose scree at the slope's base with a deliberateness that said *someone who knows the country doesn't forgive careless feet.*

One set. Alone. Moving upstream, toward the reach where the Hollis party had last reported from.

Silas crouched over the prints for a long time. A woman alone on foot, this far upriver, moving with purpose and speed, in country where the nearest settlement was two days' ride south and the second-nearest was a company town that existed to process ore and process workers. She wasn't lost. Lost people wander, double back, drift toward water in lazy arcs. This track ran straight as a survey line.

He looked upstream, where the canyon bent and the river disappeared around a shoulder of black rock. Somewhere up there: eleven missing people, a benefactor's money, a dispatcher's careful vocabulary. And now a woman walking alone toward all of it like she had an appointment.

Duty said report it. He keyed the radio at the next rise, tried the relay, got the same dead-air hiss, and realized with a small cold clarity that he couldn't report it even if the set worked — because reporting it meant telling Dispatch, and telling Dispatch meant telling whoever at the administrative level had decided this search should be *discreet*, and something in Silas Vasquez that had slept for eleven years under the mileage logs rolled over and opened one eye.

He didn't have a word for what he felt standing there over that woman's boot print. It wasn't the loneliness he'd worn so long it fit like an old coat. It wasn't quite unease, though unease was in it. It was closer to the feeling of a door he'd nailed shut years ago rattling on its hinges — curiosity, maybe, or the memory of being a young man who believed the country owed everyone a fair accounting, before he'd learned how rarely the ledger balanced.

Whatever it was, it pointed upstream.

He swung into the saddle, touched Juniper with his heels, and followed the tracks north, the mule grumbling behind, the river sliding dark and patient beside them toward whatever it was keeping.
