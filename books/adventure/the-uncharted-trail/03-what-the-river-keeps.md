## 3. What the River Keeps

The river ran the color of old pewter in the early light, and Silas Vasquez rode beside it the way a man walks beside an argument he's already lost.

He'd been on the water trail since before dawn, having broken camp in the dark with the economy of long practice — coffee gone cold in the canteen because hot coffee meant a fire and a fire meant smoke and smoke meant somebody upriver knowing exactly where he was. Not that anybody was. That was the whole point of this stretch of country. The San Rafael country above the last diversion dam didn't have people. It had geology, and it had weather, and it had him, twice a season, logging mileage and noting beaver sign for a district office that filed his reports without reading them.

Twelve years of that. He knew the arithmetic of it the way he knew the arithmetic of everything: 240 miles of patrol loop, eleven days out, four gallons of water on the packhorse, one man. Divide your life by those numbers and you got something that looked like peace from a distance.

Up close it looked like whatever it actually was, which he preferred not to examine.

* * *

The river narrowed through the first sandstone narrows by midmorning, walls rising sheer and red on either side, the water quickening over slickrock shelves. He let the horse pick its own line along the bench above the current. In the narrows you didn't fight the terrain; you negotiated with it. That was true of most things, if you thought about it long enough, which was why he tried not to think about things.

He was thinking about them anyway.

Dispatch's voice from yesterday sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. *Hollis expedition, missed check-ins, two weeks now.* Eleven souls, scientists and guides, funded — and here his supervisor had done the thing supervisors do, the too-casual drop, the name tossed off like it cost nothing — *by Ada Corliss, as it happens.*

Ada Corliss. The name did what it always did. It reached back down twelve years and thirty miles and put its hand on the back of Silas's neck. He'd been nineteen when the Corliss Water & Power attorneys came to the valley ranches with their transfer papers and their explanations, and his father had signed what he was told to sign or watched his ditches go dry, and either way the ranch died, and three years after that so did his father, face-down in the very river they'd all been robbed of, drunk enough to mistake the current for a bed.

Silas had taken the ranger job the following spring. People assumed it was about the land. It wasn't. It was about the moving. A man who kept measuring miles never had to measure anything else.

So no, he hadn't liked hearing Ada Corliss's name attached to eleven missing people. But liking and disliking were luxuries, same as hot coffee. There was a job. Upstream. He rode toward it.

* * *

He found the cache at a little past noon, tucked in a side canyon where the river bent north and a dry wash cut east into the mesa. He knew the spot. Every ranger who worked this district knew the good cache sites — high ground above flood line, shaded half the day, close to water but not so close the damp got into the stores. Somebody with expedition experience had picked this one, and picked it well. Hollis himself, maybe. The elder Hollis had run surveys in this country twenty years back, before the money and the university appointments. He'd have known where a cache wanted to be.

The problem was what the cache looked like now.

Silas reined in fifty yards out and sat still, doing what he always did first, which was nothing. He let the horse stand. He let the canyon talk to him. Wind in the cottonwoods along the wash, the river's low constant argument with its own bed, a raven somewhere up on the rim saying the one word ravens know. No human sound. No smell of woodsmoke, no sourness of unwashed men, nothing.

He rode in slow, circling wide, reading the ground the way other men read newspapers.

The cache had been a canvas fly stretched between two junipers over stacked crates and duffels, and it had come apart not all at once but in stages — he could see that from the way the guy-lines were cut rather than untied, one of them still knotted around the trunk where someone had simply abandoned it mid-job. Crates stood open. Not scattered — open, lids pried or lifted and set aside, contents gone through. A crate of canned goods sat with six cans removed and the rest squared neatly back inside. A duffel lay unzipped, clothing folded back over itself by hands in a hurry but not panicked hands. Hurried, yes. Panicked, no.

That detail snagged in him like a barb.

Because here was the thing he understood about ransacking, having seen it done to cabins and line camps and once to his own saddlebags by a thief he'd tracked forty miles into the La Sals: people breaking *in* made a certain kind of mess. They broke locks. They tore at things from outside. They took the valuable and left the rest strewn, because they were leaving anyway and mess cost them nothing.

This was different. This was a camp going through its own stores. This was people deciding, fast, what they could carry and what they couldn't, and closing what they left behind out of some habit of order that even fear hadn't burned out of them. You only closed a crate lid behind you if you expected — or hoped — to come back. Or if you were raised by somebody who'd have whipped you for leaving things open.

Someone had left here deliberately. And in a hurry. And those two facts together made a third fact that Silas didn't like at all: deliberate plus hurried equals *fleeing*, and fleeing means there is something to flee.

He dismounted and went through the cache the way he went through everything, slowly, touching little.

* * *

The inventory told its story in negatives.

Rope: gone, all of it, every coil, including the odd short lengths you'd leave behind unless you needed every foot you owned. That meant climbing or hauling or tying people together on bad ground. Food: mostly gone, the heavy staples — flour, beans, rice — left, which told him they weren't planning a long stay anywhere with room to cook. They'd taken what a man could eat walking. Medical kit: gone entire, canvas case and all. He found the impression of it in the dust under where it had sat, rectangular and clean, like a body's outline drawn at a crime scene.

Ammunition: this was the one that stopped him. Two partial boxes of .30-06 remained, and a full box of .22, and nobody fleeing through open country leaves ammunition behind unless their arms are full of more important things — or unless they're carrying a different caliber entirely and these boxes belong to someone who isn't coming back for them.

He stood holding the .30-06 box a long moment, thumb moving over the cardboard flap, and let the question form itself fully, because he'd learned that questions you refuse to form don't go away, they just stop announcing themselves.

Eleven people had walked out of this camp. Or fewer than eleven, and the ones remaining had been carried. Either way, they'd left in a rush with rope and medicine and not much else, and they'd left food behind, which meant they'd left in a rush *recently* — flour doesn't keep its condition long in canyon country, and what remained in the torn sack was still clean, weevils not yet arrived.

Days. Not weeks. Whatever had happened to the Hollis expedition, it had happened recently enough that the trail might still be warm.

He was crouched over the flour sack when he saw the boot prints.

* * *

There weren't many. That was the first thing. The ground inside the cache area had been churned to a general trampled confusion by however many feet had passed through it, and beyond the canvas fly the wash sand held wind-blur and the ghosting of old tracks gone soft-edged. But along the eastern margin, where the shade of the juniper kept the sand damp and firm, three prints survived intact, and they did not belong to any expedition member.

Silas knew this the way he knew his own heartbeat, because he'd spent the morning reading the expedition's tracks backward from the cache, and a man's boots introduce themselves eventually. The expedition party wore a democracy of footwear: lace-up hikers worn eccentric, two pairs of smooth-soled field shoes, a pair of packer's boots with a long iron heel calk on the left, a woman's small print with a cracked heel edge. He'd catalogued them without deciding to, the way he catalogued everything, the habit running beneath thought like groundwater.

These three prints matched none of them.

They were military. That was the second thing. Deep, even tread, machine-stitched welt, a heel pattern he recognized because he'd seen it on men who'd come through the district claiming to be prospectors and whose gear said otherwise. Size eleven, maybe a hair over. Weight well forward — a man walking soft, deliberately, the way you walk when you're approaching something you intend to surprise.

And they were fresh. The edges still held their crumb. The sand in the tread impressions hadn't slumped. One day old. Maybe less.

Silas rose from his crouch very slowly and turned a full circle, scanning the rims.

The canyon walls stood empty against the blue. The raven had gone quiet. Nothing moved anywhere in all that red rock and silver water except the light itself, sliding westward along the stone.

But the feeling arrived then, ahead of any understanding, the way weather arrives ahead of the storm — a pressure behind the sternum, a prickle at the hairline, the animal brain doing its ancient arithmetic faster than the reasoning one. *You are being read while you read.* He couldn't have said how he knew. He just knew, the way he knew rain coming by the smell of the dust.

He finished his circle casually, unhurried, a man stretching his back. Rim east: nothing. Rim west: nothing. The wash mouth: nothing.

Whoever had made those prints had come to this cache after the expedition fled it. Had gone through it — he crossed now to the far side and confirmed it, finding the same deep-tread print pressed into the spill of sand where someone had knelt to search the duffel — had searched it thoroughly and methodically, and had then walked away north along the wash, following the same direction the survivors' tracks had taken.

Following them.

Silas squatted again and studied the sequence of prints until the story assembled itself in order, frame by frame like film through a projector. The expedition flees south-to-north in disorder, many feet, burdened, one set dragging — he found the drag mark, a shallow furrow beside a right footprint, repeating every stride, someone injured or exhausted being helped along. Hours later, perhaps a day, the single set of military-tread prints arrives from the south, moves through the cache with purpose, and departs north on the survivors' line, unhurried now, the weight settled back on the heels. A tracker's walk. A patient walk.

One man. Following eleven.

Or — and here the dread, which had arrived ahead of understanding, finally caught up to it and showed him what it had been trying to show him all along — one man scouting ahead of others. Because a man like this, a man with government-surplus boots and a tracker's patience, was rarely alone. Men like this were the point of a spear. Somewhere south of here, behind him, between Silas and the district radio, was the rest of the spear.

He thought about dispatch. Thought about the two-week silence, and the supervisor's voice doing its casual trick with Ada Corliss's name, and thought about how a search-and-rescue assignment gets issued to one man on routine patrol instead of a mounted party with a wire team — because officially, on paper, in the language the Corliss interests used in this territory, nothing was wrong yet. Nothing was ever wrong yet. That was how the Corliss machine had eaten his father's valley: nothing wrong, just paperwork, just a transfer, just a dry ditch, just a man face-down in the water with a bottle for a pillow and a coroner's verdict of misadventure.

Nothing wrong yet.

Silas packed the .30-06 box into his saddlebag — evidence, and besides, ammunition was ammunition — and tightened the latigo, and stood a moment with his hand on the cantle, looking north up the wash toward the high mesas where the tracks went.

The sensible move was to go back. Radio the district, report findings, request support. That was procedure. Procedure was a fine thing and had never once saved anybody in this country, because procedure moved at the speed of paperwork and the man in the government boots moved at the speed of his own two feet, and by the time a relief party saddled up, whatever was going to happen to eleven frightened people in the high country would already have happened.

His father had waited for help that came in the form of lawyers. Silas had learned the lesson at the funeral.

He swung up into the saddle.

The tracks led north up the wash, and he followed them, and the dread rode along behind him without needing a horse, keeping pace easily, patient as the man whose boots he was following — patient as whatever waited at the end of this trail, which Silas was beginning to understand was not a rescue at all.

Two miles up the wash, where the sand gave way to slickrock and the tracks nearly vanished, he found the thing that took the last of his doubt and buried it.

A boot print — the same deep military tread — pressed into a patch of windblown silt beside a boulder. And beside it, smaller, lighter, half-erased by wind already: a woman's print, cracked heel edge, toe dug deep.

Running.

She'd doubled back. She'd seen him coming — or seen something — and she'd run, and the man in the government boots had been between her and the cache, cutting her line, herding her east toward the rim country where the canyons got deep and the getting-out got hard.

Silas looked at the two sets of prints for a long time. Then he turned the horse off the survivors' trail, off the easy route, and pointed him east after the woman, because a tracker knows that when the quarry runs, you don't follow the hunter.

You follow whoever's still alive.

The sun leaned west. Somewhere ahead of him, in the maze of red rock, a woman was running out of country, and Silas Vasquez gave the horse his head and went to find her before the man in the government boots did.
