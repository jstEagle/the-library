## 4. The Woman on the Trail

*Mara*

The morning they told her it was an accident, Mara Hollis learned that grief has a smell.

It was coffee and river mud and the chemical sweetness of the medic's antiseptic, all of it hanging over the camp like weather. Ruth Ann had brewed a pot because that was what you did when someone died — you made coffee, you kept your hands moving, you gave the living something to hold. Mara held the tin cup and didn't drink it and watched her father's boots sitting empty outside his tent, laces still tied, waiting for feet that would never come back to them.

Accident, they said. He'd gone down to the water at night — he did that, everyone knew he did that, he liked to sit on the rocks and look at the current and think about whatever it was scientists thought about — and the bank had given way. The river took him. The river takes people; that's its whole job description. Tomás said it gently, his big hand on her shoulder, his voice rough as rope. *The river took him, niña. There was nothing anyone could do.*

But there were things no one could explain, and Mara had spent twenty-four years being the kind of person who noticed things no one could explain. Her father's headlamp, found three days later wedged under a root forty yards downstream — dry inside, battery still good. If it had been underwater with him, it would have flooded. Her father's field notebook, which he'd carried everywhere for thirty years, missing. And the bank itself, when she finally made herself walk down there: not crumbled. Cut. A clean-edged slide where the earth had been levered away, deliberate as a shovel stroke, and below it a scuff pattern in the mud that looked less like a fall and more like a struggle.

She'd stood over that mud with her arms wrapped around herself and felt something in her chest turn from grief into a blade. Grief was heavy. It dragged you down. But a blade you could carry. A blade pointed somewhere.

*He found something in the cave.* That was the last thing her father had said to her, four days before the river. He'd come out of the box canyon at dusk with dust on his knees and light in his eyes like a man who'd struck gold, and he'd gripped her by both shoulders and laughed — actually laughed, a sound she hadn't heard from him since her mother died. *Mara, I found something in the cave. Something old. Something that's going to change everything about this valley.* Then he'd kissed her forehead and gone to his tent to work, and in the morning he'd been secretive and distracted, and two nights after that he was dead in the shallows with his notebook gone and his lamp dry.

Nobody else wanted to see it. She understood why. Seeing it meant believing that one of them might have done it, or that someone outside the camp had come in over the rocks in the night, and either answer was worse than the comfortable lie of the river. So the camp chose the lie. They packed their gear and mourned and started talking about going home, and Mara sat apart with her blade and sharpened it.

Then the men came.

They came upriver in a jet boat she'd never seen before, four of them, wearing clean canvas jackets and carrying rifles that were too nice for fishermen. Their leader was a wide man with a face like a closed door, and he introduced himself as Mr. Sallow, safety officer for the Corliss Land and Water Company, and he said the company was concerned about the expedition's welfare so far from help. He asked questions. Who found the body. Where the personal effects were. Had Dr. Hollis kept any records, any papers, anything written down. He smiled while he asked, but his eyes went around the camp like a man counting inventory.

That night Mara couldn't sleep. She lay in her tent listening to the river and thinking about the word *records*, and near midnight she heard low voices by the supply cache — Sallow's voice, and another voice answering, and she could not make out the words but she could make out the shape of them, the shape of men dividing something up.

By dawn she was gone.

She'd taken almost nothing. Her pack, her bow — she'd brought the bow because her father had taught her to shoot before he'd taught her to swim, and some lessons go deeper than others — a water skin, dried meat, the survey maps she'd drawn herself of every mile of canyon they'd run. She left before first light, moving north along the rimrock where the ground was hard and left no sign, and she didn't tell anyone, because telling anyone meant trusting someone, and trust was how her father had ended up dead in the shallows with his lamp dry.

She heard them find her empty tent an hour later. Heard Sallow's voice crack across the camp like a whip, heard boots scattering, heard Tomás shout her name into the canyon walls until the echo gave out. She lay flat behind a juniper with her heart slamming and her hand on her bow and thought: *good*. Let them chase me. Let them spend their energy on me instead of wondering what I know.

Because here was the thing she knew, the thing she'd been turning over for three days like a stone in her palm: if her father had been murdered for what he found in the cave, then the thing in the cave was still in the cave. Papers don't drown. And whoever killed him hadn't found it yet — or they'd have stopped asking about records.

So she went back for it. Alone. Armed. Awake on three hours' sleep and running on something hotter than sleep, something that tasted like copper and purpose.

Grief, weaponized.

Her father would have hated it. He'd have taken her face in his hands and said *Mara, mija, vengeance is a debt you pay forever* — but her father wasn't here, and the man who'd replaced him wore a canvas jacket and counted camps like inventory, and Mara Hollis had decided that the only person left to trust was the version of herself that noticed things.

She doubled back along the river at midmorning, cutting down through a side drainage where the willows grew thick enough to swallow a woman whole. The plan was simple: reach the cave ahead of anyone, take what was there, and disappear into country she'd mapped with her own two hands until she could decide who — if anyone — deserved to be trusted with the truth.

Simple plans, her father used to say, are just complicated plans that haven't met the terrain yet.

The terrain, in this case, was a ranger.

* * *

*Silas*

Silas read tracks the way other men read letters — fluently, without effort, the meaning arriving whole.

The woman's prints came out of the willow drainage at a jog, turned south along the riverbank, then stopped. He crouched over the place where they stopped and let the story assemble itself. She'd stood here maybe a minute, weight rocking heel to toe — the prints deepened at the ball of the foot, doubled over. Looking back the way she'd come. Listening.

Then she'd moved again, faster, and cut upslope through the bunchgrass toward the rimrock, and this was where it got interesting, because the grass told him two things. First: she knew how to walk soft. Most people broke grass at the stem; she broke it at the base, laid it flat, stepped on stone wherever stone offered itself. Second: she'd made one mistake anyway, forty yards up, where a flat sandstone shelf crossed the drainage — a single print in the fine dust against the rock's edge, small, narrow-heeled, the toe dug in like she'd pushed off hard.

Running now. Scared, or in a hurry, or both.

Silas straightened and worked his jaw, tasting the morning. Two days ago this woman had been a mystery walking north. Now she was a mystery walking south, back toward the burned camp and the shallow grave and whatever waited upstream, and mysteries that reversed direction were usually mysteries running toward something rather than away from it.

He checked the sun. Midmorning, plenty of light left. He marked her line of travel, noted the wind — steady out of the southwest, which meant she couldn't smell him and he couldn't hear her over the river unless he closed distance — and started up the slope at an angle that would intercept her trail half a mile ahead without ever crossing it.

His father had taught him that trick too, though his father had learned it chasing cattle, not people. *Don't follow the animal, mijo. Follow where the animal is going.* His father had known a lot of things about country and nothing at all about staying alive in it, and Silas filed the thought away in the drawer where he kept those thoughts, the one that stuck sometimes and had to be forced shut.

Half a mile on, he picked up her trail again at the top of the bench and stopped cold.

She'd changed her pattern. Instead of following the path of least resistance along the rim, she'd dropped into a rocky wash, walked it on the stones, climbed out the far side through a stand of prickly pear, and then — this was the part that made the hair on his neck lift — circled back on her own track for fifty yards before breaking away fresh.

Doubling back. Laying a false trail. Checking whether anyone followed.

That wasn't panic. Panic ran straight. This was craft. Somebody had trained this woman, or grief had, and Silas was beginning to suspect the two teachers used similar methods.

He gave her credit for it, adjusted his angle, and kept coming.

* * *

*Mara*

She felt him before she saw him, the way you feel weather change — not a sound exactly, more an absence of sound, a hole in the noise of the morning where noise should have been. Birds. There had been birds working the brush behind her, quail muttering their idiot two-note song, and now there weren't.

Mara went still behind a boulder the size of a truck, arrow nocked, breath thinned down to almost nothing. Her father's voice in her memory: *Stillness is a skill, mija. Everything alive can move. Almost nothing can be perfectly still.*

Thirty seconds. A minute. The river talked to itself below. Heat shimmered off the sandstone.

Then she saw him, and her blood went from ice to fire in the space of a heartbeat.

A man on horseback, maybe eighty yards out, riding the contour of the slope with the loose, patient seat of someone in no hurry at all. Dust-colored hat. Dust-colored shirt. A rifle in a scabbard and a pair of field glasses coming up toward his face —

Toward her.

He'd found her false trail already. Or he'd never been fooled by it. Either way he was reading the slope like a page, and she realized with a cold drop in her stomach that he wasn't searching for her at all. He was *predicting* her. He was riding to where she would be, not where she'd been, and no one did that unless they were very good, and no one was very good at finding her unless they'd been sent.

Sallow's men. It had to be. The company knew she'd seen them at the cache in the dark, knew she'd run, knew she was the loose thread that could unravel whatever they'd done to her father — and they'd sent a tracker to pull it.

The field glasses swept past her boulder. Paused. Came back.

Mara didn't remember deciding. Her body decided, the way it had decided things since the river — fast, cold, final. She rose out of cover, drew, anchored, and loosed in one motion, and the arrow hissed across eighty yards of open air and buried itself in the dirt six inches from the horse's front hoof. The horse shied sideways. The man reined it around, calm as Sunday, and looked directly at her, and did not reach for his rifle.

"Ma'am," he called, voice carrying flat and clear across the slope. "I'm not with whoever you're running from."

"That's exactly what they'd send you to say." Her second arrow was already nocked. Her hands were steadier than they had any right to be. "Throw the field glasses down. Take your boots off."

"My boots."

"You can't track me barefoot." The logic had seemed airtight in her head. Out loud, in the open air, it sounded slightly insane, and the man's expression suggested he agreed, and she hated him a little for that. "Do it."

He looked at her for a long moment. She watched him take inventory the way she'd watched Sallow do it — her bow, her stance, the angle of her draw — except where Sallow's gaze had been greedy, this man's was something else. Careful. Almost respectful. Like he was reading a difficult page and wanted to get it right.

"I'm Silas Vasquez," he said. "District ranger. Dispatch sent me looking for the Hollis expedition. Eleven people, two weeks of radio silence." He paused. "You're Mara Hollis. Your father's name is on the permit."

The name hit her like a hand across the face. She'd been nobody's daughter for five days — just a runner, a ghost, a woman with a blade where her heart used to be — and hearing it spoken aloud by a stranger in a dusty hat nearly buckled her.

"He's dead," she said, and her voice cracked on the word, and she despised herself for it. "They say the river took him. The river didn't take him."

"No," the ranger said quietly. "I saw the bank."

Something in her chest lurched. She kept the arrow up. "Then you know it was cut. You know somebody put him in that water."

"I know somebody ransacked your supply cache from the inside," he said. "And I know there's a man named Duke Sallow asking questions in towns a hundred miles from here, wearing a Corliss jacket, and that your expedition's benefactor signs his paychecks." He eased his horse a half step forward, hands visible, movements slow as syrup. "So before you put that next arrow through my chest, ask yourself one question, Miss Hollis."

"What question?"

"Why would Corliss money send a ranger upriver to find eleven missing people — and then send its own guns to make sure nobody finds them?" He tipped his chin at her bow, unhurried, unafraid. "I've been tracking you since dawn. If I wanted you dead, you'd never have heard my horse. If I wanted to rob you, I'd have waited till you slept. All I want is the truth about what happened to your father, and I'm starting to think you're the only other person in this canyon who wants it too."

The wind moved the brush. Somewhere downslope, a raven made its dry wooden call, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary, indifferent to the fact that Mara Hollis's entire future was balanced on the head of an arrow.

She should shoot him. Every instinct honed over five days of running said *shoot him, run, trust no one, trust no one, trust no one.* Her father was dead because someone he'd broken bread with for twenty years had smiled at a funeral and lied about a riverbank. Men were liars. Men with calm voices and honest eyes were the worst liars of all.

But.

But he'd said *I saw the bank,* and his voice had done something no voice had done since the funeral — it had believed her. Without being convinced, without argument, it had simply believed her, the way you believe rain is wet, and Mara hadn't known how starved she was for that until it arrived.

And beneath the belief, practical as a canteen: he knew the country. He'd tracked her across ground she'd thought erased her. If she was going back to the cave — and she was, alone if necessary, armed if possible — then the difference between doing it blind and doing it with a man who read this canyon like scripture might be the difference between dying and not.

"Lower your rifle sling," she called. "Unbuckle it. Let it hang."

He did, showing his palms.

"And answer me one thing first, Ranger Vasquez." Her draw arm ached. She held it anyway. "My father found something in a cave up this river. Four days later he was dead. If I tell you that — if I point you at that cave — what do you do with it? Do you file a report? Write it down? Tell your dispatch?" Her voice dropped, and the blade in her chest showed its edge. "Because paper gets read by the wrong people. Paper got my father killed."

For the first time, the calm in the man's face shifted. Not doubt — recognition. The look of someone hearing his own private arithmetic spoken aloud by a stranger.

"No reports," he said. "No paper. Not yet." He swung down out of the saddle, slow, keeping his hands loose and empty, and stood in the dirt between her arrow and the sky. "Not until I know who's worth writing to."

Mara held the draw three seconds longer. Five. Long enough to watch him not flinch, not bargain, not perform — just stand there in the heat with the patience of a man who'd been alone long enough that death was a familiar acquaintance.

Then she lowered the bow, and the morning exhaled.

"Pick up my arrow," she said. "You're going to need to learn to use one. Because we're going back to that cave, Ranger, and if half of what I think is true—" she slung the bow across her back and started downslope toward him, grief and purpose fused into a single stride— "then by the time we're done, every Corliss in this territory is going to wish the river had taken us both."

Behind them, far downstream, faint as a rumor, an engine coughed to life.
