## 5. Terms at Gunpoint

### Silas

He knew she was behind him four seconds before the arrow did.

Not because he heard her — though he heard her, a boot sole shifting weight off a slickrock lip, the smallest confession of a body that had been running on fear for days — but because the birds went quiet. All of them at once. That was how the desert told you something was wrong. It didn't shout. It stopped talking.

Silas kept walking. Ten more yards. Fifteen. He let his hand drift down to his side, not to the pistol but to the canteen strap, casual as a man adjusting his gear, and he said, to the juniper and the red sand and the girl behind him:

"That draw you're standing above floods in a hard rain. You'd want to be on the east lip, not the west. West side's undercut."

The silence stretched. Then a voice, low and cracked at the edges, like rope fraying under load:

"Turn around. Slow."

He turned. She was twenty feet off, half in the shade of a juniper, and she was the woman from the tracks — the long stride, the worn heel, the pace of somebody who'd stopped caring what the country cost her. Late twenties maybe, sunburned past caring, dark hair tied back with what looked like a strip of torn shirt. The bow was drawn to her ear. The arrowhead — a broadhead, filed by hand from a horseshoe rasp, which was either desperate or brilliant — held steady on the center of his chest.

Her hands were shaking. Her eyes weren't. That combination told him more than any wanted poster could have. Shaking hands meant she hadn't eaten properly in days, hadn't slept, had burned through every reserve she had. Steady eyes meant she'd decided something, and decided it hard, and whatever she'd decided, it wasn't about him specifically. It was about surviving. He was just the thing standing between her and the next mile.

"Ranger," she said. Not a question. She'd seen the badge, then, or the hat, or the way he stood — the way a man stands when he's spent twelve years learning that the country is always bigger than him.

"Silas Vasquez. Territorial ranger, out of the Redrock station." He kept his hands wide of his body, palms half turned, the old trick — look unarmed without being unarmed. "You're a long way from anywhere, ma'am."

"Don't call me ma'am." The arrow didn't waver. "Don't come closer. Don't reach for that gun, because I'm faster than I look and I've got nothing left to lose, which makes me the most dangerous thing you'll meet all week."

"That's usually true," Silas said. "Of most people out here."

"I mean it."

"I know you mean it. That's why I'm standing still." He let a breath out slow, and made himself look at her — not at the arrow, at her. Her lips were split. There was a scrape along her jaw crusted with red dust. Her boots were expedition-grade but worn through at the ball of the foot, the kind of wear you got from hard miles on hard rock, and the laces had been replaced with baling twine, which meant she'd scavenged, which meant she'd been out here longer than a woman on a day hike. "You've been following the river north. You crossed at the shale bar two days ago because the water was low and you were afraid of the narrows. You slept cold last night — you didn't risk a fire. And you've been eating whatever you could pull out of the ground, because there's a stain on your shirt cuff from prickly pear juice and your hands have thorn marks on them."

The arrow dipped. Just a fraction. Just enough.

"How do you—" She caught herself. Her jaw set. "You've been tracking me."

"Two days," Silas said. "You walk like you're being chased. You're not. Not by anything with legs."

"You don't know that."

"I know this country," he said. "I know it the way you know the inside of your own house. There's nothing behind you but coyote sign and my own boot prints. What's ahead of you, though—" he nodded north, toward the river, toward the gray-green line of cottonwoods that meant water and meant, three days upstream, the abandoned Hollis camp, "—that's worse. The river bends into box country up there. Sheer walls, no banks, no way out if the water rises. And the water's going to rise. Storm system's been building over the mesa for two days. You'd be walking yourself into a stone pipe with a flood coming down it."

The bowstring creaked. Her fingers were white on it.

"Why should I believe you?"

"Because I'm the only person you've met in a week who isn't trying to catch you," Silas said. "And because if I wanted to hurt you, I'd have done it while you were sleeping off that cold night two canyons back. You were out hard for four hours. I stood over you for a minute and a half deciding, and then I walked on. You never knew I was there."

Something moved behind her eyes — not softening, not yet, but the recalculation of a smart woman doing arithmetic fast. He watched her weigh it: the story checked out against her own missing time, or it was a lie designed to check out, and either way she couldn't verify it, and either way her legs were going to give out in another mile and the storm was coming and the box canyon was real because she'd have seen it herself if she'd walked another half day.

"Who's chasing me, then," she said. "If not you."

"That's what I came to find out." He let the truth stand there in the open between them, plain as a laid-out hand of cards. "Eleven people went up this river two weeks ago. The Hollis expedition. They stopped checking in. Dispatch sent me. I found their cache yesterday — cleaned out from the inside, in a hurry, by people who meant to leave and meant not to be found. And I found boot prints that don't belong to any scientist. Heavy men, iron-shaled heels, moving in pairs, covering ground like they were paid by the mile." He paused. "You're Mara Hollis. The leader's daughter. You were on that expedition."

The arrow came back up. Faster than he'd have guessed her arms could manage.

"Say that name again and I'll put this through your throat."

"Then we'll both stand here in the sun and bleed, and the storm will come, and neither of us will know anything," Silas said, and his voice didn't rise, didn't sharpen, just stayed level as a spirit gauge. "I didn't come up this river to arrest you, Miss Hollis. I came to find out why eleven people vanished. And it's starting to look like the answer is that somebody wanted them to."

The wind moved through the juniper. Somewhere downslope, a raven said one flat syllable and quit.

Her arms were trembling now in earnest. He could see the moment the shaking stopped being something she could override — the bowstring sagging an inch, two, the broadhead drifting off the line of his heart. She was going to collapse standing up. He'd seen it in mule deer run to death and in men lost in the snow: the body making its argument over the head's objections.

"You look like you haven't eaten in two days," he said. "I've got jerky, hardtack, and coffee in my pack, and a stove I can light under overhang where the smoke won't show. And I've got a map in my head of every overhang, seep, and side canyon between here and the mouth of the river. You need that map. You need it worse than you need that arrow in me."

"Why would you give it to me?"

"Because my job is to bring people home alive," Silas said. "All of them. Even the ones who put an arrow an inch from my heart."

A long moment. Then Mara Hollis lowered the bow — not surrender, he noted, just economics; she kept the broadhead nocked and the bow in her left hand, ready to raise — and her knees buckled, and Silas crossed the twenty feet in six strides and caught her before the sand did.

### Mara

She'd killed a man once.

Not with the arrow — she'd never put an arrow in anything but a target and, once, a rattlesnake that had gotten into the food stores. But she'd killed him the way you kill a man with a signature, three days ago, when she'd signed the incident report her father's own guide put in front of her. *Accidental fall. Slippery rock. No signs of foul play.* Tomás Reyes had written it. She had copied it. Her hand had shaken then too, and she had told herself the shaking was grief.

It hadn't been grief. It had been the body knowing what the mind wouldn't say: that her father, who had climbed rock since before she was born, who had taught her to read a route the way other fathers taught daughters to read a road, who was careful to the point of superstition — that man had not slipped.

She'd known it the way you know weather. And she'd run.

Now she sat under a sandstone overhang with her boots off and her feet wrapped in a ranger's spare socks, eating jerky that tasted like salt and salvation, watching a man she'd had at arrow-point build a fire the size of a cupped hand and put coffee on it, and she could not decide if she was the biggest fool in the territory or the luckiest.

The coffee smell alone was making her eyes sting. She blamed the smoke.

"You don't have to tell me anything," the ranger said. He didn't look at her when he said it. He looked at the fire, the way a man does when he understands that being watched is a pressure and pressure makes people shut. "But I'll tell you what I know, and you can correct me where I'm wrong."

"Why would I correct you?"

"Because people can't stand to hear their own story told wrong," he said. "Even when they're trying to keep the story secret. It's human. I've used it on lost hunters for years."

Despite everything — despite the last three weeks, despite the grave she'd helped dig with her own hands, despite the arrow she'd aimed at this man's heart an hour ago — Mara almost laughed. It came out as a bark, ugly and surprised, and she pressed her wrist to her mouth to keep the rest of it in.

"Go on, then," she said. "Tell me my own story wrong."

He poured coffee into a tin cup and handed it to her first, before his own. She noticed that. She noticed everything now; grief had made her a surveillance instrument. He drank his from the pot, which she also noticed, and which she decided — after a beat, watching his throat work, watching nothing bad happen — meant nothing, or meant he was honest, and she was too tired to tell the difference.

"Eleven people came up the river on a scientific expedition," Silas said. "Funded by Ada Corliss, which is a fact I didn't like when I heard it and like less every hour. They were surveying geology, botany, water flows. Your father led it. Somewhere upriver, he found something. I don't know what."

Mara said nothing. She drank her coffee.

"Three days later, he was dead. Ruled an accident. The camp broke apart — not scattered, *broken*, like a dropped plate. Records burned. A grave dug in riverbank ground, which is backbreaking work, done by somebody strong and in a hurry. Six people went north. The rest —" he glanced at her, quick and unreadable, "— I don't know. And men with iron heels came up the river behind all of it, moving like they were looking for something, or someone."

The fire ticked. Mara turned the cup in her hands.

"You got most of it right," she said. Her voice came out lower than she meant. "Which means you're either very good or you already knew."

"I'm very good," Silas said. "And I knew some. The rest was shape. I had the shape of it. You're giving it weight."

She looked at him then, really looked. He was maybe forty, lean in the way of men whose bodies had been pared down to function, with a face like the country he worked — weathered, unglamorous, hard to read until you learned the language. There was no pity in his face. That was the thing that undid her a little. Everyone since her father died had looked at her with pity, wet and useless as spring snow. This man looked at her the way he'd probably look at a storm front: something to be understood, worked with, survived.

"My father found a cave," she said. The words came out of her like blood from a reopened cut — she hadn't decided to say them, they'd just started. "Up past the narrows, in a side canyon. He wouldn't say what was in it. He got strange. Happy-strange, then quiet-strange. He told Tomás — Tomás Reyes, his oldest friend, been guiding with him twenty years — he told Tomás they needed to go back to town. All of us. That it was over, that he'd found what he was looking for, that some people were going to be very unhappy."

She stopped. The coffee had gone cold in her hands. She drank it anyway.

"Three days later he was at the bottom of a cliff below camp. Broken neck. And Tomás was the one who found him, and Tomás was the one who wrote *accident* on the report, and Tomás was the one who said we had to leave, now, tonight, split up, burn the papers." Her jaw ached; she realized she was clenching it. "Burn the papers, Silas. Scientists don't burn their own data. My father would have shot anyone who touched his field notes. And Tomás burned them and dug a grave and put six of us on foot north and told us if anyone asked, we were never a team at all."

"You think Tomás did it."

"I think Tomás *knows* who did," Mara said. "And I think he's protecting that person, or protecting himself, or protecting — I don't know. Something. And I think the men with iron heels are looking for whatever my father found in that cave, and I think they'll dig up that grave if they have to, and I think—" her voice cracked and she hated it and pushed through, "—and I think whoever killed my father is still out there, and I'm the only one asking the question, and I'm so tired I aimed an arrow at the first man who was kind to me in a week."

The silence after that was long and full of river sound, far off, and the small tick of the fire.

Silas set down the coffee pot. When he spoke, his voice was quieter than the fire.

"My father lost his ranch to the Corliss Water and Cattle Company when I was fourteen," he said. "They bought the judge first, then the water, then the land, in that order. He spent the rest of his life walking to the bottom of a bottle. I took this job because it keeps me moving, and I've kept moving for twelve years because stopping means thinking." He looked at her, and there was something in his face now that hadn't been there before — not warmth exactly, but recognition, the look of a man finding his own handwriting in someone else's letter. "I'm not telling you that to trade sob stories. I'm telling you because when you said *some people were going to be very unhappy*, I already knew the name of the people. And I think you know it too, and you haven't said it out loud because saying it makes it real."

Mara's throat worked. The name sat behind her teeth like a stone.

"Ada Corliss funded the expedition," she said finally. "Nobody funds a survey of water flows out of the goodness of their heart. My father thought she wanted the science. He was flattered. He was — God help him, he was *grateful* to her." She stared into the fire. "Whatever was in that cave, it's about water. It has to be. And whatever it's about water, it's about the Corlisses."

Silas nodded slowly, and she watched him file it away behind those unreadable eyes, and she realized she'd just crossed a line she couldn't uncross. She'd said it out loud. She'd made it real. And she'd made this stranger a witness.

"So here's where we are," he said. He crouched by the fire, forearms on knees, and looked at her straight. "Six of your people went north. The iron-heeled men are between them and anywhere safe. The storm's coming, and the narrows will flood, and I'm one man with a rifle and a badge that the wrong people have already bought and paid for. You're one woman with a bow and a grudge." He turned the tin cup once in his fingers. "Neither of us gets what we want alone. I can't find six people in this country without someone who knows their faces and their habits. You can't get justice — or your father's name back — without someone who knows how the law works and how it fails."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll point you to a route south that avoids the narrows and the flood, give you three days of food, and let you go," Silas said. "And I'll spend the next week finding your people without you, and probably fail, and probably get some of them killed. That's the honest arithmetic. I won't dress it up."

Mara looked at him for a long time. The firelight moved on his face. He didn't fill the silence, didn't press, didn't do any of the things men did when they wanted something from a woman — he just waited, patient as stone, as if the answer would be true whenever it came.

Her father had trusted people too easily. She had learned, in three weeks of running, to trust no one. Somewhere between those two failures there had to be a workable distance.

"Tomás Reyes is one of the six," she said. "If he's north with the others, that's where we're going. And if I find out you're one of Corliss's, Silas Vasquez, I'll put that broadhead somewhere it'll take a long time to matter."

"Fair terms," he said. And then, standing, shouldering his pack, he nodded north toward the darkening line of the canyon country, where the first far-off thunder had just rolled down off the mesa like something heavy being dragged across the sky.

"Then we'd better move," Silas said. "Because those iron-heeled men aren't the only ones who know the storm is coming — and I'm starting to think whatever your father found in that cave is worth killing a flood's worth of people to get back."
