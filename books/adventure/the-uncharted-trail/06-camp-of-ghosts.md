## 6. Camp of Ghosts

The river bent north an hour past noon, and the camp was where Mara said it would be — tucked into a cottonwood stand on the inside of the bend, where the current ran slow and the ground stayed soft enough to drive tent stakes.

Silas stopped at the edge of the trees and let the place come to him the way he let all country come to him: in layers. First the shape of it, the geometry of shade and water and open ground. Then the small wrongnesses. The way the grass lay flat in a lane leading down to the bank. The absence of birdsong where there should have been doves working the cottonwood seeds. A camp that had been lived in hard and left hard always kept a silence around it afterward, like a room after a fight.

"You feel it too," Mara said beside him. Not a question. She'd been reading his face for two days now, learning his tells the way he'd learned hers.

"Feel what?"

"That it's watching us."

He didn't answer that. He swung down from the horse and handed her the reins, which she took without being told, which told him something about how far they'd already come from the woman who'd put an arrowhead an inch from his windpipe on the trail. "Stay behind me until I've walked it."

"It's my father's camp."

"Which is why you stay behind me." He said it flat, no apology in it, and after a long moment she nodded once and did as he asked. That was the thing about grief, he'd learned watching men lose ranches and wives and whole ways of life: it made people either reckless or precise, and the precise ones were more dangerous and more worth protecting, both at once.

He walked the perimeter first. Old habit — you read a camp from the outside in, same as you read a wound from the skin toward the bone. The tent lines were still staked, canvas collapsed and sun-bleached, six weeks of weather written across them in mildew and bird droppings. A fire ring held ash gone gray and fused. A rope line where they'd kept the pack stock had been cut, not untied — cut low and quick, someone in a hurry or someone angry.

Then he came to the records.

They'd been burned in a shallow pit behind the main tent, and not casually. Someone had built a proper fire over them, fed it until the paper was ash and the leather covers were char, then raked the coals apart and scattered them so nothing could be reconstructed. Silas crouched over the pit and turned a fragment with a stick. A corner of a page survived, half a printed letterhead, the letters *HOLLIS EXPEDITION* in a serif typeface, and below it, in ink that had run but not vanished, a column of figures.

"They burned their own paperwork," Mara said from behind him. Her voice had gone careful, the way voices go when they're carrying something fragile across a long distance.

"Someone did." He stood. "Question is whether it was your father's order or somebody else's decision."

"Dad kept everything. Drove Mom crazy with it. Field notes, receipts, permits — he filed like an accountant." She was staring at the pit. "He wouldn't burn this."

No, Silas thought. And whoever did hadn't done it for tidiness. You raked coals apart like this when you wanted the evidence gone past recovery, and you only wanted that when the evidence pointed somewhere you couldn't afford to be pointed at.

He moved inward. The main mess area next — a folding table on its side, enamel plates scattered, one of them cracked clean through. A lantern hung from a cottonwood limb, glass sooted black on one side. He opened it. The wick had been pinched out, not burned down. Somebody left in the dark, or somebody wanted it to look that way.

And then the smell reached him.

Faint, old, rain-washed a dozen times over, but underneath the green rot of the cottonwoods it was still there, the sweet-heavy smell that every man who'd worked law of any kind in this territory knew and never got used to. He'd smelled it once before, at twelve years old, standing at the edge of a wash while men pulled his father's body out of the shallows. His stomach remembered before his head did.

"Mara." His voice came out lower than he intended. "Don't come over here."

She came anyway. Of course she did.

* * *

The grave was forty yards from camp, upstream, in a stand of willow where the ground went soft near the seep. Whoever dug it had chosen the spot with some care — shade, nearness to water, a view of the river. Or maybe those weren't choices at all. Maybe soft ground was just faster digging when your hands were shaking.

It was shallow. Six weeks of rain and scavengers had done their work; the depression had settled into a long low swell maybe two feet deep at the crown, and the marker stones had been arranged along it in a neat row, each one river-smoothed, each one placed by hand. Eleven stones. No — he counted again. Twelve. One at the head, larger than the rest, flat-topped, like a pillow for a sleeping man.

Mara stood at the edge of the willows and didn't move. Silas watched her take it in — the swell of earth, the stones, the twelve of them — and watched her face do something complicated and terrible, grief and arithmetic happening in the same expression at the same time.

"That's him," she said. It wasn't a question either. Everything she said now was her refusing to ask questions, because questions could be answered wrong.

"Likely."

"He's buried alone." Her voice cracked on *alone*, and she hated it, he could see her hating it. "Eleven people in that expedition and he's the only one in the ground. Where's everybody else? Why is he the one who—"

"Because he's the one who died," Silas said quietly. "And because whoever put him here wanted him to have dignity. Look at the stones. That's not a rushed job. That's a man taking time he probably didn't have, in a situation where every minute mattered, laying out twelve rocks because twelve was right."

"Twelve." She frowned. "There were eleven of us."

"Twelve stones."

She counted. He watched her count, watched her lips move, watched her arrive at twelve and stand there holding the number like something hot. "Tomás," she said finally. "Dad counted Tomás. He always said we were eleven plus one, eleven scientists and Tomás, because Tomás wasn't staff, Tomás was family. Dad would've—" Her voice failed and restarted. "Dad would have insisted the count include him even if Tomás didn't dig the grave. Even if Tomás was a hundred miles away."

"But he wasn't," Silas said. "Was he."

She looked at him.

"Come here," he said. "Look at the ground by the head stone. Careful where you step."

She picked her way along the grave's edge, and he pointed without touching — you didn't touch evidence you might need later, and besides, the less his hands were on this the cleaner the story would be when it needed telling. The ground at the head of the grave, where the digger would have knelt to set the last stone, held the ghost of a print. Rain had softened it, sun had baked it, but the outline survived: a boot heel, broad, with a distinctive wear pattern, the outer edge worn round and smooth.

"Big man," Silas said. "Carries his weight on the outside of his feet. Years of walking river rock does that. See how the heel's driven deeper than the toe? He knelt here a long time. Rocking back on his heels, not forward onto his knees. That's an older man's habit — knees don't forgive kneeling anymore."

Mara had gone very still. "Tomás Reyes is fifty-eight years old. He's six foot two. He's walked rivers since he was nine years old, guiding for my grandfather before my dad ever started the expeditions." Her hand rose halfway toward the head stone and stopped in the air, trembling, not quite touching. "That's his boot. I'd know it anywhere. He resoled them himself every spring, always the same way, always that rounded outer edge, he says it's how you keep your footing on wet stone—"

Her voice broke off. When it came back it had changed, gone flatter and harder, a blade laid flat on a table.

"Why would Tomás bury my father alone? Why would Tomás be the one here, doing this, if the rest of them walked away together?" She turned to face Silas, and her eyes were dry now, which was worse than tears. "You said the cache was ransacked from inside. You said somebody left in a hurry. Now you're telling me the oldest friend my family has knelt at my father's grave and then—" She stopped. Started again. "What happened here, Ranger? What am I looking at?"

"I don't know yet," Silas said honestly. "But I know what it isn't. It isn't a man sneaking back to hide a body. Men who hide bodies don't build stone markers. They don't choose shade near water. They don't kneel till their boot prints sink that deep." He looked down the length of the grave, at the twelve stones, at the big flat one at the head. "This is a burial. Done right, done slow, done by somebody who loved him. Which means whatever drove the rest of them north, Tomás Reyes stayed long enough to put your father in the ground with honor — and then he ran too."

"Ran." She tasted the word. "From what?"

"That's the question." He straightened up and scanned the willows, the river, the treeline going gold with late afternoon. "And there's another one. Because see this—" he crouched again, pointing at a second set of prints, fainter, overlapping the first, smaller boots with a square heel, "—somebody else stood here after. Stood right where you're standing. Watched the grave a long while — weight shifted back and forth, see, heel to heel. Then walked away north."

"North," Mara repeated. "Same direction the others went?"

"Same direction everything goes when it leaves this camp." He rose and brushed the dirt from his knees. "Your six survivors went north. This watcher went north. And two days ago, a woman on foot went north along this same river, moving fast, doubling back on her own trail twice because she thought she was being followed." He looked at her. "You want to tell me what you were running from when I found you? Because I'm starting to think it's the same thing that emptied this camp."

For a moment he thought she wouldn't answer. The light was failing, the cottonwoods throwing long shadows across the grave, and she stood with her arms wrapped around herself like a woman holding a door shut against wind.

"My father found something," she said at last. "In a cave, upriver. He radioed the supply boat about it — I heard him, I was on deck. He was excited. He said he'd found papers, old ones, and that they changed things, and that he needed to get them somewhere safe before anyone knew he had them." Her jaw tightened. "Three days later he was dead. Fell crossing the scree above the narrows, they said. Slipped. Bad luck." She spat the word *luck* like a seed hull. "I've crossed that scree twenty times in my life. My father taught me to cross it. He didn't slip."

"And the others believed the story?"

"The others wanted to believe it. There's a difference." She looked down at the grave. "Tomás didn't believe it. I saw his face at the funeral-in-camp, the little service Ruth Ann led. He stood there with his hat over his heart and his face was like carved wood, and when I caught his eye he looked away. Tomás Reyes has never once looked away from me in my whole life. Until that day."

Silas said nothing. The river talked to itself around the bend, patient, keeping its own counsel, the way it had kept counsel for ten thousand years and would for ten thousand more.

"So I watched him," Mara went on. "Two nights later I saw him leave his tent after midnight with a shovel over his shoulder, heading upstream. I followed. I lost him in the dark. And by morning the camp was awake and shouting, because Dad was missing, and by noon Ruth Ann found—" her breath hitched, steadied, "—found him. Here. Already buried. And Tomás was standing there saying he'd found the body himself at dawn, that he'd buried it because the heat, because the animals, because there wasn't time to wait for everyone to decide—"

"But you didn't believe him."

"I believed he buried him." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "I believe that part with my whole heart — look at these stones, look at the care in them, that's Tomás, that's exactly Tomás. What I don't believe—" and here her composure finally cracked, one seam, quickly mended, "—what I don't believe is that my father was dead when Tomás picked up that shovel. I think Tomás knows how he died. I think Tomás has known since that night. And I think that's why he ran, and why the others ran, and why nobody has answered a single radio call in two weeks."

Silas stood in the failing light and let the pieces lie where they lay. A dead expedition leader. A burned archive. A grave dug by a grieving friend who then fled. Six survivors gone silent as stones. A benefactor whose name his supervisor had mentioned too casually, the way you mention a storm front on the horizon. And hired boots — he thought of the prints he'd cut three days ago, the ones that matched no expedition member, heavy-soled, government-surplus, moving with purpose — already on this trail ahead of him.

Dread had arrived days ago. Understanding was still walking up the road.

"We're losing the light," he said. "We'll make cold camp downstream, out of sight of this place. At first light we follow the northbound trail."

"To the survivors."

"If they want to be found." He met her eyes. "Mara — understand what you're asking. If Tomás Reyes is hiding something, finding him means forcing it into the open. Whatever it is, however bad, however much it costs. Once I start down this trail I don't stop halfway. That's the job."

She looked at the grave a long moment. The last light caught the twelve stones, turning them the color of old bone.

"My father spent his life mapping trails nobody had walked," she said. "He used to say the country doesn't give up its secrets to people in a hurry." She turned back toward the horses, and her voice was steady now, steady the way ice is steady. "I'm not in a hurry, Ranger. Take me north."

* * *

They made camp two bends downriver, in a hollow behind a shelf of sandstone where the firelight wouldn't show. Silas picketed the horses, boiled coffee over a whisper of flame, and took first watch while Mara rolled into her bedroll without argument — she was learning to sleep when sleep was offered, another lesson of the trail absorbed fast.

He sat with his back to the warm stone and the rifle across his knees and let the night inventory itself. Coyotes working the far bank. An owl asking its one question over and over. The river, always the river.

His mind kept returning to the boot print at the head of the grave. The depth of it. The patience in it. A man kneeling a long time over the man he'd buried, rocking on his heels, saying whatever old friends say to the newly dead when nobody else can hear.

And beneath that thought, quieter and colder, the other one. The one he hadn't said aloud, not even to Mara, not even to himself in full sentences:

If Tomás Reyes had killed Hollis, he'd have buried him fast and shallow and been gone. But if Tomás Reyes *hadn't* killed him — if he'd buried a murdered friend and then run from whoever murdered him — then the thing Hollis had found in that cave was worth killing for. And whoever wanted it buried had sent men up this river with heavy boots and government rifles, and those men were between Silas and the survivors right now, tonight, walking the same northbound trail he meant to ride at dawn.

The owl asked its question again. Downstream, faint on the wind, so faint he might have imagined it, Silas heard something that was not an animal sound at all.

A shortwave transmission, clipped and coded, carrying on the night air from somewhere upriver.

Somebody was talking about them.
