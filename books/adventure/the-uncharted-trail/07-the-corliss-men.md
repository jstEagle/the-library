## 7. The Corliss Men

Duke Sallow had killed his first man at nineteen and his last one three weeks ago, and between those two facts lay twenty-two years of work that he had never once, in all that time, thought of as evil. He thought of it as work. It came with a wage, a bunkhouse, a saddle that fit his backside like it had been carved for it, and a boss who paid on time and asked nothing of him that he hadn't already decided he was willing to do. In the territory, that was more religion than most men ever got.

He sat in the foreman's shack at the Corliss river camp with the shortwave set humming in front of him and a cup of coffee gone cold at his elbow, waiting on the evening schedule. Outside, the light was going amber and long across the water, and the cottonwoods along the bank were throwing shadows that looked, if you let your eyes go soft, like a line of men walking out of the hills. Sallow didn't let his eyes go soft. He'd learned early that the desert rewarded attention the way a card sharp rewarded a drunk — quietly, and all at once.

The radio crackled. "Camp Six, this is Corliss main. Come in, Duke."

He keyed the handset. "Main, this is Six. Reading you."

"Stand by for Mrs. Corliss."

He waited. He was good at waiting. It was, he sometimes thought, the only skill he'd ever been born with that money couldn't buy and nobody could take.

The channel shifted, and when the voice came through it was dry and small and precise, like paper turning in a quiet room.

"Duke."

"Ma'am."

"Tell me about the ranger."

Sallow turned his cup a quarter-turn on the desk, a habit he didn't know he had. "Vasquez. Silas Vasquez. Signed out of the district station two days ago, headed upriver on the Hollis check-in. He's moving fast for a man on horseback. Made the burned camp by noon yesterday. I had Ruiz and Petty trail him at distance. This morning he picked up a woman's track about eight miles below the cache and followed it north."

Silence on the channel. Sallow had learned to read Ada Corliss's silences the way other men read weather. This one had weight in it.

"A woman," she said at last.

"Track's small, light boot, long stride. Moving like she had somewhere to be and didn't want company."

"Eleven went up that river, Duke. Eleven came off the manifest. If there's a twelfth person walking around out there, I want to know who she is before the ranger does."

"Ruiz is still on her. He'll have a name by tomorrow."

"See that he does." A pause, and then her voice came back a degree lower, the way it did when she was about to say the thing she'd actually called to say. "Duke. The ranger found the camp."

It wasn't a question, but he answered it anyway. "He found it."

"And the grave."

"He found the grave."

Another silence. Outside, a heron lifted off the shallows with a sound like a sail filling, and Sallow watched it go with the handset pressed to his ear and the coffee going colder.

"Mrs. Corliss," he said, because somebody had to say it, "you want to tell me what this is about? Eleven scientists go up a river and stop calling in, and suddenly I've got men riding night shifts and a pay envelope thick enough to choke a horse. I don't ask questions. That's the arrangement. But a man works better when he knows how deep the water is."

The voice that came back was not angry. He had heard her angry once, six years ago, over a fence line and a dead heifer, and it had been like watching a woman open a drawer full of knives one at a time. This was not that. This was worse. This was patient.

"The water is as deep as it needs to be, Duke, and no deeper. Here is what concerns you. A man named Hollis took eleven people up that river with my money, and something up there has gone wrong, and now a ranger is riding into the middle of it with a gun and a badge and the territorial government standing behind him. I have spent fifty years making sure that nothing in this valley goes wrong in a way that anyone can see. That is the whole of my business and the whole of yours. Handle it."

"Handle it how, ma'am? That's a lawman. You put a bullet in a ranger, that's not a problem anymore. That's a war."

"Did I say put a bullet in him?"

"No, ma'am."

"Then don't." The paper-turning voice again, flat and dry. "I said handle it. Rangers are like horses, Duke. They go where they're pointed. Point him. There's a stretch of bad country east of the narrows — dead ground, no water, no reason for a man to be in it. If his trail happened to bend that way, he'd spend a week finding nothing, and by the time he came out, whatever he was looking for would have had time to be somewhere else. Do you understand me?"

"I understand you want him lost, not dead."

"I want him *unproductive*," Ada Corliss said, and for the first time something moved under the dryness — not warmth, nothing like warmth, but a kind of tired satisfaction, the sound of a woman checking a column of figures and finding it balanced. "Dead men get investigated. Lost men get forgotten. I have buried this valley in forgotten things for fifty years, Duke. One more won't strain the ground."

She signed off, and Sallow sat in the shack with the radio hissing and the cold coffee and the heronless evening, and he thought: *fifty years of forgotten things.* He turned the cup another quarter-turn and decided not to think about it further. That was the arrangement.

* * *

Ada Corliss did not sleep well anymore, which was inconvenient, because she had less of it left than any year of her life before.

Dr. Hargrove had told her in March, in the careful voice doctors use when they are really reading you a bill. Her body, he said, had begun keeping its own ledger, and the entries were all in red. She had thanked him, paid him, and walked out into the Corlissville street past the company store and the company church and the row of company houses with their company curtains, and she had felt — this was the strange part, the part she never told anyone — *relief.* Not at the dying. At the arithmetic. Because a deadline, she had found, clarified everything. It told you what mattered. And what mattered, it turned out, had a name, and the name was Daniel, and Daniel was nineteen years old and had his grandfather's jaw and none of his grandfather's stomach for the work, and Ada Corliss had exactly one thing left to do before the ledger closed.

She sat in the upstairs study of the big house with the lamp turned low and the valley spread out below her windows — her valley, bought and paid for, every acre of it, and every drop of water in it. The river came down out of the mountains in the dark like a long gray animal, and it ran through her land and her town and her mills, and it had run that way for fifty years because in 1894 her father had ridden into three ranching valleys with a lawyer and a sheriff and a stack of papers, and the papers had said the water was his, and the men who'd worked that water for a generation had signed or starved. Most signed. A few starved. One — she remembered him still, a quiet man named Vasquez, with a boy who never once cried at the auction — had done neither, and had simply gone, and that had been the only signature her father ever failed to collect.

She opened the drawer of her desk and took out the letter she had been writing for three weeks and had not finished. It was addressed to Daniel. It was, she supposed, a confession, though she would never let the word come near it while she lived. It was an accounting. *Here is what was done. Here is why. Here is what it bought. Here is what it will cost you if you let it go.* She had gotten as far as the second page and stopped, because the second page was where the arithmetic stopped balancing, and Ada Corliss could not abide an unbalanced column, even one written in her own hand.

The knock came soft. The nurse — she'd stopped learning their names, there'd been too many — put her head in.

"Mrs. Corliss. It's past eleven."

"I heard the clock strike it, Marta, I'm old, not deaf."

"You're supposed to be resting."

"I'm supposed to be dead, and I've disappointed everyone on that score too, so we'll both have to keep waiting." She closed the drawer. "Is Daniel home?"

"He came in an hour ago. He's in the stables. He's been out there most evenings."

Ada looked at the window, at the black bulk of the stable roof against the stars. Most evenings. She knew what he did out there. Ruiz's boy had told her, the way boys tell things without knowing they're telling them: Daniel had been riding down to the tenancy rows, sitting with the hands, drinking coffee out of tin cups, asking questions. *What was it like before the scrip? Did your grandfather really lose his place on the river?* Questions with soft edges that were, all the same, questions.

"He'll catch cold," she said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Send him up when he comes in. No — don't." She waved it away. "Let him sit. A boy should sit with horses while he still thinks horses are the point."

The nurse withdrew. Ada sat alone with the lamp and the drawer and the unfinished letter, and she did the thing she did every night now, which was to hold the future up to the light and turn it like a stone.

Daniel would inherit the water. That was the whole design, the only design, the thing every hard decision of the past fifty years had been load-bearing for. Her father had been a thief, yes — she had never flinched from the word in the privacy of her own skull, and flinching was for people with time to waste — but he had been a thief who built. Three towns stood where three starving ranches had been. The mills turned. The valley ate. Whatever the ledger said about how it started, it could not say what it had become, and what it had become was *hers*, and after her it would be *his*, and a thing that belonged to Daniel Corliss could not, by definition, be stolen, because Daniel was not a thief. He was a boy who sat with horses. She had seen to that. She had spent his whole childhood seeing to that.

And now a scientist had gone up her river with her money and come back down out of the world, and a ranger was riding into the hills after him, and somewhere in the dark between the two there might be a piece of paper — there was always a piece of paper; her father had taught her that the only thing more dangerous than a gun was a signature — and if that paper existed and reached the wrong desk, the whole column would come down. Not on her. She was a closed account. On him. On a nineteen-year-old boy with his grandfather's jaw, who would spend his life in courtrooms paying for a crime committed in 1894 by a man he'd never met.

That was the arithmetic. She had run it a thousand times and it always came out the same. Whatever it cost to bury this — men, money, whatever the dark hills asked — it was cheaper than the alternative, and anyone who thought otherwise had never loved anything they couldn't afford to lose.

*Everything I do,* she thought, looking at the drawer, *I do so that he never has to know the price of his own name.*

It was the nearest thing to a prayer she had.

* * *

Sallow rode out at first light with Ruiz and Petty and a fourth man named Grieves who'd come down from the northern outfits and hadn't yet learned to keep his mouth shut.

They cut the ranger's trail where it left the burned camp, and it ran clean north along the river, and Sallow sat his horse at the edge of it for a long time with his hat brim shading his eyes, doing the thing Ada Corliss paid him for, which was thinking like the other man.

"She's heading for high country," Ruiz said. "The woman. Water, game, sight lines. If the survivors went anywhere, they went up."

"Then the ranger's trail bends east," Sallow said.

Ruiz looked at him. "East is dead ground."

"East is dead ground," Sallow agreed.

Grieves spat. "So we're running a lawman in circles for a woman who's scared of her own shadow. What'd the old lady promise these scientists anyway, a grant and a pat on the head?" He grinned. "Whole thing smells like a rich woman's hobby gone sideways."

Sallow didn't answer. He was watching the trail — the small light boot prints laid over the ranger's, the two lines of story written side by side in the sand, a man following a woman following whatever it was that had scared eleven people off a river. And under his professional calm, in the place where he kept the things he didn't think about, something shifted its weight.

Fifty years of forgotten things, she'd said. One more won't strain the ground.

He turned his horse east, and his men followed, and behind them the river ran on north through the hills, carrying its long gray secret down through the valley, past the company store and the company church, past the big house on its hill where an old woman sat with an unfinished letter, past all of it, all the way down to the towns that drank it without ever once asking where it had come from.

By noon the ranger's trail bent east, exactly as instructed, into the dead ground.

By noon the woman's trail had vanished north.

And Sallow, riding at the head of his men into a country with no water in it, found himself doing something he hadn't done in twenty-two years of work.

He found himself wondering what, exactly, was worth forgetting this badly.
