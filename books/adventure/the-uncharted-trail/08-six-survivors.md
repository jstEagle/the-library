## 8. Six Survivors

The box canyon announced itself the way a trap announces itself — too quiet, too convenient, walls of red sandstone rising on three sides like cupped hands. Silas stopped Mara with a raised fist fifty yards from the mouth and studied it the way he'd study any water hole in dry country: not for what was there, but for what wasn't.

No smoke. No sound of tools or voices. But the sand at the canyon mouth carried wheel ruts, weeks old, half-filled by wind, and above them on the north wall a glint that might have been glass catching sun or might have been nothing at all.

"You're sure this is where they'd go," he said.

Mara crouched beside him, her bow strung across her back, her eyes red-rimmed from two days of walking and grief she refused to set down. "Tomás Reyes taught me to read country when I was nine. If you want to hide six people and one wounded man, you want water you don't have to haul, high ground behind you, one way in." She nodded at the canyon. "He'd have found this place before my father ever picked the campsite downstream."

"One way in cuts both ways."

"I know." She said it flat, without drama, and something in her voice told him she'd already done the arithmetic: if Tomás had decided to bury secrets along with her father, this canyon was where he'd do the burying.

Silas checked his rifle out of habit more than need, then didn't sling it. "Stay behind me until they know your face."

"They know my face."

"People change fast when they're scared," he said. "Scared people shoot first at the face they least expect."

They went in along the east wall, single file, boots silent on packed sand. The canyon narrowed, turned, widened again into a bowl maybe two hundred yards across, and there Silas saw them all at once — because they were already looking at him.

Six figures spread across the rocks like a defensive diagram drawn by someone who understood defense. A big man with gray in his beard stood front and center with a lever-action leveled at Silas's chest, steady as a fence post. Behind him, a woman with close-cropped hair held a pistol low but ready; a younger man with wire-rimmed glasses clutched a rifle like it might bite him; a lean wrangler type sat a horse near the rear with a shotgun across his thighs; a small, sharp-eyed woman stood slightly apart with her hands visible and empty, which somehow made Silas more nervous than the guns; and on a bedroll in the shade of an overhang lay a heavyset man whose bandaged middle was stained brown in a way Silas had seen enough times to read at a distance.

"Ranger," the big man said. His voice was gravel over deep water. "That's far enough."

"Tomás Reyes," Mara said, stepping out from behind Silas before he could stop her.

The rifle didn't waver, but something moved behind the man's eyes — grief, or guilt, or both wearing each other's clothes. "Mara." He said her name carefully, the way a man handles a live shell. "You shouldn't be here."

"My father is dead, Tomás. I'm exactly where I should be."

"We buried him."

"You buried *something*," Mara said. "I dug down to the shoulder before I couldn't anymore. The boots were wrong. My father wore size elevens. Those stones were covering a pair of tens."

The silence that followed had weight to it, physical weight, like the air before a storm breaks. The woman with the empty hands said quietly, "Tomás," — just the name, just a warning — and the wrangler on the horse shifted his weight and said nothing at all.

Silas let it sit. He'd learned long ago that in a standoff, whoever spoke first gave away a piece of ground. Instead he looked around the bowl slowly, cataloguing: the tarp shelter lashed against the overhang, the smokeless fire pit ringed with stones and screened by brush, the cache of gear stacked under canvas, the water seep trickling from a crack in the south wall into a tin basin. Six people, eleven days missing, and a camp run tight enough to make a ranger's professional pride itch even while every other instinct screamed.

"You've got a wounded man," he said finally. "Fever?"

"Started yesterday," said the small woman with the empty hands. Her accent was coastal, clipped, educated. "The wound's clean. It's blood loss and exposure doing the work now. He needs a hospital, and we can't get him to one, because every road out of this valley has men on it who shoot first and ask questions never." She tilted her chin at Silas. "You'd be Vasquez. Dispatch has been trying to raise you for two days."

"And nobody's answered," Silas said. "Which tells me somebody upriver doesn't want dispatch knowing where I am or what I've found. Which tells me you smashed your own radio."

The young man with the glasses flinched. That was answer enough.

"Peter," the coastal woman said, mild as milk, and Peter Liu shut his mouth so hard his teeth clicked.

"I'm Ines Okafor," she went on, turning back to Silas with the composure of a woman giving a lecture. "Botanist. This is Ruth Ann Calloway, our medic — Ferro's hers, on the bedroll. Cass Duval, wrangler. Peter Liu, graduate student. And you've met Tomás." Her eyes flicked to Mara, softened a degree, came back hard. "Now you know our names. You want to tell us why a district ranger spent three days tracking one frightened girl through riverbreak country instead of reporting what he found at our camp?"

"Because what he found at your camp was a burned records chest, a grave with the wrong boots under it, and boot prints heading north that belonged to none of you," Silas said. "Because eleven people checked out of Hollis Station two weeks ago and six are standing here, which means five aren't, and nobody's said their names yet. And because when I asked dispatch who funded the expedition, the answer I got was Ada Corliss, delivered like a man spitting on a grave." He let that land. "So before anybody hands anybody anything, I'd like to know what happened to the other five."

Nobody spoke. The wind moved through the brush screening the fire pit, and somewhere above them a raven said one flat syllable and quit.

"Guthrie and Marsh took the supply raft downstream on the fourth night," Tomás said at last, his rifle lowering an inch, then another. "They were going to float to Corlissville and wire for help. We never heard from them again." He swallowed. "Delgado stayed behind at the main camp to watch the trail. When we sent Cass back for him on the sixth day, the camp was burned and Delgado was gone. No body. Just his hat."

"And the eleventh?" Silas asked, though he already knew, and hated himself a little for making a grieving daughter hear it in list form.

"The eleventh," Tomás said, "was Edmund Hollis. And Edmund Hollis is the man under those river stones, no matter whose boots are on him."

* * *

They built the parley around the fire pit because Ruth Ann insisted Ferro needed warmth more than the conversation needed privacy, and because, as she put it while boiling water over a flame so small it barely qualified as fire, "secrecy is a luxury the fevered can't afford."

Ferro was worse than he'd looked at distance. Big man gone hollow, skin gray as ash, the bandage across his gut spotted and re-spotted. Ruth Ann changed the dressing with hands that never hurried and never shook, talking to him the whole time in a low stream of reassurance that was clearly as much for herself as for him. Silas watched her work and revised his estimate of the group upward: whatever else was rotten in this story, the medic was real.

"He took a bullet the night we ran," Tomás said, crouching across the fire from Silas, Mara beside him. The others had drifted into a loose perimeter — not leaving, Silas noticed, just repositioning where they could see every face at once. Siege habits. They'd been living inside this circle for eleven days and it had gotten into their bones. "Boatman on the Hollis runs for nine years. Took it high on the hip going through the willows. Missed everything that matters, Ruth Ann says, but he bled near half of himself out before we got him here."

"Who shot him?"

Tomás looked into the fire for a long moment. When he looked up, his eyes were wet and furious and old.

"That's the question, isn't it, Ranger. That's the whole damn question." He wiped his face with a palm rough as tree bark. "You want to know about the body. Ask me straight."

"Did you kill Edmund Hollis?"

"No." No hesitation, no flinch. Whatever else was true, that word came up from bedrock.

"But you moved him."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because the man who killed him wanted us to believe it was an accident," Tomás said, "and I wanted there to be a grave worth finding when the truth came out. Because I've known Edmund since before Mara was born, and I was not going to leave him lying in the dirt where dogs and coyotes—" His voice cracked, and he mastered it the way an old horse masters a bad leg, by refusing to favor it. "And because by morning, everyone left alive at that camp was a suspect. Me most of all. I figured a man who moves a body looks guilty. A man who leaves one where the killer put him looks innocent. I chose guilty on purpose, Ms. Hollis, because guilty men get hunted, and hunted men get *found*, and I needed somebody to find us before Corliss did."

Mara stared at him. "You used yourself as bait."

"I used the truth as bait," Tomás said. "It's the only kind that brings the right animal."

Silas turned his hat in his hands. Around the fire, the others had gone still in the way of people listening through walls. "Tell me about the third night," he said. "Start wherever you want. Start with the cave."

* * *

The story came out in pieces, passed hand to hand like work gloves, each survivor holding it a moment and handing it on.

Peter Liu talked first, because Ines touched his elbow and he was young enough that a touch still moved him. He'd been cataloguing specimens in the side canyon on the third afternoon when he heard voices — Hollis and a stranger, arguing low and hard near the cave mouth. He'd crept close enough to catch fragments. *Water rights.* *1890s.* *"It belongs in a courthouse, not a vault."* Then footsteps, and he'd flattened himself behind a juniper and watched a lean man in a town suit walk away downstream toward Corlissville, walking fast, walking like a man with news to sell.

"A suit," Silas said. "Not range clothes."

"Town boots, town coat," Peter said. "I remember thinking he dressed like a funeral director."

Ines took it up next, her voice level and precise, a scientist's voice laying out observations. That evening Hollis had called the whole camp together. He'd been transformed, she said — a careful, methodical man suddenly lit through like a lamp. He told them he'd found papers in the cave. Old papers. A ledger, leather-bound, kept by a land office clerk in the 1890s, recording every water rights transfer on the upper river for thirty years — and recording, alongside each transfer, the *method*. Signatures witnessed under threat. Ranchers' marks entered against their will while armed men stood in their doorways. Page after page of names, and beside the names, in the clerk's neat hand, the same beneficiary appearing over and over, generation after generation, like a watermark: **Corliss**.

"Three towns drink from this river," Ines said. "Corlissville, Redrock, Millbrook. Every school, every church, every store in all three was built on paper signed in fear. Edmund stood in front of us with that book in his hands and said, 'This goes to the territorial capital Monday. Every courthouse, every newspaper. Let them choke on it.'" Her composure slipped, just once, just a hairline fracture. "He was so happy. That's the thing I can't get past. He was the happiest I ever saw him."

Cass Duval spoke from the saddle without dismounting, as if getting off the horse would count as surrendering ground. "Radio was smashed before sunup. Not broken — *smashed*, hammer-work, every tube. And Edmund was down by the shallows where he liked to wash. Fell, hit his head on the rocks, coroner'll say if a coroner ever says anything." Her jaw worked. "Except Edmund swam that stretch twice a day for two weeks and knew every stone under that water better than his own teeth. And except somebody dragged him ten feet further downstream than he needed to be, into the willow shadow, where nobody washing at dawn would see him."

"Who found him?" Silas asked.

"Ruth Ann," Cass said, and the medic's hands went still over Ferro's bandage.

"I found him," Ruth Ann said quietly. She tied off the dressing, covered Ferro to the chin, and only then looked up. "At first light. I knelt down and I checked, because that's what I do, and he was gone, and while I was kneeling there I heard a horse moving off through the willows. One horse. Walking away slow, the way you ride when you don't want to leave sign but you're not scared of being seen." She looked at Tomás. "I told Tomás first. I should've told everybody. That's on me. By the time we woke the camp, Tomás had already made his decision about the body, and Guthrie was already saying we should scatter, and Ferro was bleeding, and everything happened fast after that."

"It happened fast," Tomás agreed, "because that's how it was meant to happen. You burn the radio, you kill the leader, you scatter the witnesses, and every witness wakes up suspecting the witness next to him. Eleven days later we're sitting in a hole in the ground rationing trust like it's water." He shook his head slowly. "Whoever planned it knew camps. Knew people."

Silas felt the shape of it settle over him, cold and complete. Not a crime of passion. An operation. Radio, ledger, leader, witnesses — four targets, four nights, one direction of retreat herding six survivors deeper into country where a handful of hired guns could pen them like stock. And a man in a town suit carrying word to Corlissville before the killing was even done.

"There's one thing I don't understand," Mara said. Her voice was thin but steady. "If Corliss wanted the ledger, why burn the records chest? Why not just take it?"

"Because the chest didn't hold the ledger," Tomás said. "Edmund split it. Half the pages went in the chest with his field notes. The other half—" He reached inside his coat, slow, telegraphing every inch of the movement so that six guns wouldn't decide he was reaching for something else, and drew out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, bound with cord, thick as a Bible.

He laid it on the flat rock between them and untied the cord with fingers that trembled only at the end.

The cover was scarred leather, water-stained, stamped with a county seal worn nearly smooth. Inside, in faded iron-gall ink, columns of names marched down ruled lines, and beside the names, in a clerk's meticulous script, the words *coerced*, *witnessed under duress*, *signed at gunpoint*, repeated like a litany, like a prayer said backward.

Silas looked down at his father's world. Because it was there — he knew it would be, had known since Ines said the word *ledger* — and there it was, halfway down the second page, in a hand that could barely write: **Vasquez, R. — 40 acres, section 14 — mark witnessed, armed men present — transfer to Corliss estate, 1903.**

His father had been nine years old when a man with a gun stood in his father's doorway and watched him make his X.

Silas closed the ledger with great care, the way you close a coffin, and found Mara watching him with an expression he couldn't afford to examine right now.

"Well," he said, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "Now I know why nobody wanted dispatch hearing from me."

* * *

They argued about him for an hour after that, and made no particular effort to keep him from hearing it.

Cass wanted him gone before dark — "a ranger's a badge, and a badge draws lead." Peter wanted him to stay and was too scared to say it louder than a mutter. Ines wanted to know, precisely, what legal authority he could exercise this far upriver and whether it would survive contact with Ada Corliss's lawyers, and when he told her honestly that he didn't know, she nodded as though honesty were the first useful data anyone had given her in two weeks. Ruth Ann didn't argue at all; she just looked at Ferro's fever-bright eyes and then at Silas, once, and that look weighed more than all the arguments combined.

Tomás walked him to the canyon mouth as the light went long and copper.

"You understand I can't order these people anywhere," Silas said. "I can't even order myself anywhere, yet. There's a dead gunman's paybook in my saddlebag with Corliss cash in it and no name attached, and there are fresh boot prints on your backtrail that belong to men who move like professionals. Whatever we decide, we decide tonight."

Tomás leaned on the rock and looked out at the darkening riverbreaks. "Eleven days ago I moved a body to buy time," he said. "I've been spending it ever since, and the account's nearly empty." He turned, and in the last light his face was all planes and shadows and exhaustion. "You tracked a frightened girl three days through bad country instead of riding back to report. That tells me what kind of law you are, Ranger. So I'll ask you plain, and I'll ask you once."

Behind them, the camp had gone quiet. Even the wind seemed to be waiting.

"What do we do tomorrow?" Tomás said. "All of us. The ones still breathing. What do we do?"

Silas looked back at the fire glow on the canyon walls, at six people who had been running so long they'd forgotten there was any other direction, and heard himself say:

"First, we survive the night. Because those boot prints on your backtrail are less than a day old — and by morning, gentlemen, we are going to have company."
