## 9. The Ledger

Tomás Reyes had carried a lot of things in his sixty-one years — pack frames heavier than children, men heavier than pack frames, and once, for three days through the Chiricahua breaklands, a dying horse's saddle because he couldn't stand to leave it for the coyotes. But he had never carried anything that weighed what the leather book in the oilcloth weighed now, sitting on a flat stone between him and the ranger named Vasquez while six people held their breath around a box-canyon fire.

"You want to see it," Tomás said. It wasn't a question. The ranger had come upriver with a rifle he hadn't raised and questions he hadn't stopped asking, and Tomás had spent twenty years reading men the way he read water — by what moved underneath.

"I want to know why there are armed men hunting eleven scientists," Silas said. "I want to know why your camp burned from the inside. I want to know who's in that grave back at the river bend with river stones stacked over him." His voice was level, but Tomás heard the current under it. "And I want to know why a woman I tracked forty miles wouldn't let me get within thirty feet of her until she'd looked at my hands."

Mara Hollis sat across the fire with her bow across her knees, unstrung but close, and she didn't look away when the ranger glanced at her.

"My hands," Silas said again, and turned them palms-up in the firelight. Callused, scarred along the left thumb where a snare wire had bitten him years ago. "You were checking whether I'd ever worked for Corliss."

"Rope burns look different from rein burns," Mara said. "Rein burns look different from trigger burns."

Tomás almost smiled. Elena Brandt would have liked this girl. He reached for the oilcloth bundle instead, and his hands — steady enough on rope, steady enough on oars, steady enough the day they'd lowered Nathan Hollis into the ground — shook now.

* * *

Three weeks earlier, the cave had smelled of cold stone and bat guano and, faintly, of the past — that dry mineral smell old places keep like breath held a hundred years. Tomás had gone in first, because Nathan Hollis always went second, because Nathan Hollis believed a leader's job was to watch his people's backs, not their faces.

The cave was a seam in the redrock above the third bend, half-hidden behind a curtain of desert willow, and they'd only found it because Ines Okafor had stopped to photograph a fern growing out of a crack where no fern had any business growing. Botanists found things. That was the whole joke of it later — an empire built on rivers, undone by a woman chasing a fern.

"Tomás," Nathan had called from inside, and his voice had a sound Tomás had heard maybe four times in thirty years of friendship: the sound of a man standing in front of something bigger than his vocabulary.

Inside, past the first chamber where the light died, Nathan had his headlamp on a ledge of stone, and on the ledge was a tin document box, rusted at the corners, and inside the box — wrapped in waxed paper, wrapped in oilcloth, wrapped again in a wool shirt gone stiff as bark — was the ledger.

It was a rancher's ledger, the kind sold out of any dry-goods store from here to El Paso. Leather covers, cracked and dark as old blood. The spine read *CORLISS* in faded gilt, because even the books had to wear the name.

"Ninety-one," Nathan said. His fingers hovered over the cover without touching it, the way you hover over a snake. "The first entry is dated March 1891."

They took it outside because the light was better and because neither man wanted to be underground with it. They sat on the sandstone apron with the river talking below them, and Nathan put on his reading glasses, which he hated being seen in, and opened the book.

Tomás had kept cattle books himself, back when he'd still had cattle. He knew the format before he understood it: date, transaction, parties, amount. What he didn't expect was the other hand — a second script, cramped and furious, filling the margins in pencil, in Spanish, in English, sometimes both in the same sentence.

*March 14, 1891.* A transfer of riparian rights, two thousand acre-feet, from the Vargas family to E. Corliss. Consideration listed: *forty dollars and forgiveness of debt.*

In the margin, in the furious hand: *There was no debt. Papá signed because they said the well would go dry if he did not. It went dry anyway.*

*June 2, 1891.* The Arriola grant, eight hundred acres of bottomland. Consideration: *one wagon, one mule, passage to Tucson.* And beneath, in a different pencil, added later: *The mule was returned. The passage was not honored. We walked.*

Page after page after page. Tomás lost count of the names because the names kept doing something to his chest — pressing, like a hand laid flat on a wound. Vargas. Arriola. Ochoa. Delgado. Brandt. Reyes.

He stopped at Reyes.

His own name. His grandfather's mark — not a signature; the man couldn't write — an X witnessed by a county clerk whose own signature trembled across the page like a man signing at gunpoint, which, per the margin note, he may have been. *Consideration: retention of homestead cabin and spring access, in perpetuity.* And below, in the furious margin hand: *Perpetuity lasted nine years. The spring was fenced in 1900. My father rode to the courthouse and was told the paper in this book says otherwise. He came home and never spoke of it again and neither did anyone.*

"Tomás." Nathan's voice, quiet. "Breathe, old friend."

"I'm breathing."

"You're not."

He wasn't. Because here was the thing Tomás had grown up knowing without knowing he knew it, the way you know the shape of a scar: that the Reyes family had once had land. That somewhere back in the murk of the last century it had gone away, and the story the valley told was *hard times, drought, bad luck, the way of things*, and everyone's grandmother had a version and none of the versions matched except in one detail — that the Corliss name was always somehow on the winning side of every hard time, every drought, every piece of luck.

And here it all was. Not rumor. Ink. Dated, witnessed, filed, and annotated by some long-dead soul — a wife, a daughter, someone with a pencil and a memory and no power at all — who had written down the truth in the margins of the lie because writing it down was the only thing left to do.

"They didn't lose the water," Tomás said. His voice came out strange, flattened, like sound over a dead radio channel. "Nathan. They *sold* it. At gunpoint or near enough. Forty dollars. A mule they took back. This is a robbery ledger. He kept receipts for a robbery."

Nathan took off his glasses and cleaned them on his shirttail, which he did when he was buying time to think. "Elias Corliss," he said slowly, "built the diversion dam in '94. Everyone knows that. First water works in the territory. Built the towns, brought the railroad, endowed the church—" He stopped. Looked at the page. "Endowed the church with money from these transfers."

"The whole fortune," Tomás said. "Every brick in Corlissville. Every acre anybody's been renting from them for seventy years. It started here, in this book, with an X from a man who couldn't read what he was marking." He touched his grandfather's X with one finger, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you weren't sure wouldn't bite. "He was my grandfather, Nathan. This is my name in here."

Nathan was silent a long moment. Below them the river ran, indifferent, carrying snowmelt down toward the dams and ditches and scrip-paid company towns that this book had given birth to.

"There'll be more caves," Nathan said finally. "More ledgers. Elias Corliss was systematic — men like that file everything. If this one survived, others did." And then, and Tomás would remember the exact tone of it for the rest of his life, the tone of a good man making the worst decision of his life with the best intentions available: "This goes public, Tomás. Properly. Courts, newspapers, historians — everything documented, everything verified. Those families' descendants deserve restitution, real restitution, not a campfire story."

"That's not going to be simple."

"No," Nathan agreed. "It's going to be a war. But it's a war somebody should have fought in 1891, and nobody did, so it falls to us." He smiled then, that crooked Hollis smile, and wrapped the ledger back in its shirts and papers with the care of a man dressing a newborn. "Besides. What's Corliss going to do? It's just an old book."

* * *

That was the sentence Tomás came back to, night after night, sitting in a box canyon with six frightened people and a fire fed small so it wouldn't show. *What's Corliss going to do? It's just an old book.*

Nathan Hollis had mapped glaciers. He had forded rivers that didn't have names yet. He had once talked his way past a cartel checkpoint with nothing but a jar of honey and genuine charm. He was not a fool. He was a good man, and good men make a particular kind of mistake: they assume the world runs on the same rules they run on.

Three days after the cave, Nathan was dead at the bottom of a riverbank, and the camp radio was smashed, and the ledger was hidden in Tomás's bedroll, and Tomás was digging a grave by lantern light with his hands shaking so badly he'd had to sit down twice.

Now he unwrapped it again, here, in front of the ranger.

* * *

The firelight caught the gilt lettering — CORLISS — and Silas Vasquez went very still.

Tomás watched it happen. Watched the ranger's face do what faces did when history stopped being a story and became a weight: nothing dramatic, just a settling, like a house shifting on its foundation. Silas set down his tin cup with exaggerated care, the way men handle objects when their hands need something lawful to do.

"Corliss," he said.

"Elias Corliss's own ranch ledger," Tomás said. "Or half of one. The transaction pages. The rest burned with the camp — I made sure of that." He opened the cover. "Read the first entry. Out loud, if you can stomach it."

Silas read. "*March 14, 1891. Transfer of water rights, two thousand acre-feet, Vargas to E. Corliss. Consideration: forty dollars and forgiveness of debt.*" He stopped. Read the margin silently, and Tomás saw his jaw change.

"Keep going," Tomás said.

Silas turned pages. The fire popped. Somewhere up the canyon wall, a night bird called once and thought better of it. Ruth Ann had come to sit near Ferro, who slept propped against a pack frame with his bandaged leg stretched out, and Peter Liu had his knees pulled up to his chest, watching the ranger read the way a man watches weather turn.

"Forty dollars," Silas said at last. He said it the way you'd say a diagnosis. "Two thousand acre-feet of perpetual water rights for forty dollars." He looked up. "My father's place was on the west bank. Four hundred acres. We had a spring." His voice stayed flat, but Tomás heard the flatness for what it was — a roof holding. "Spring got fenced off in '61. Paperwork said the Corliss claim predated ours. My father spent four years and every dollar we had trying to fight it in the county courts." A pause. "The judge was Amos Corliss's brother-in-law. Nobody told us that until later. Nobody had to."

"Your father was Vasquez," Tomás said quietly. "I knew the name. West-bank outfit, bay horses, a windmill you could see from the ferry crossing." He'd known more than the name. He'd drunk beer with the man at the Brandt store twice a summer for years, a quiet vaquero with a boy who never talked and always watched. "I'm sorry about your father."

"Yeah." Silas turned another page. His finger stopped on the Reyes entry, on the X, and rested there. "This clerk. Ramírez. His signature shakes on every page after June."

"He was scared," Tomás said. "Or he was paid, or both. There's a second book somewhere — the consideration side, what Elias actually paid out and to whom. Nathan wanted to find it. Wanted the whole record." Tomás closed his eyes briefly. "Nathan wanted a lot of things done properly."

"And then he was dead."

"And then he was dead."

Silas looked at the ledger the way Tomás had seen him look at the country from high ground — measuring, weighing, calculating what a thing could do and what it could cost. "Do you understand what this is? Legally?"

"It's proof."

"It's more than proof. Riparian rights transferred under duress are voidable — have been since the territorial code of 1889, section fourteen. If these transfers were coerced, every downstream claim that chains back through them is built on sand. The Corliss canal system, the town charters, the scrip economy — all of it sits on top of these signatures." He tapped the page. "This isn't a scandal, Tomás. This is a title search. This is the whole empire's deed, and the deed is rotten at the root."

"That's what Nathan said." Tomás's throat tightened. "Word for word, nearly. He said it like it was good news."

"Wasn't it?"

"It was a death sentence wearing a suit," Tomás said, harsher than he meant to. Around the fire, the survivors had gone still. Cass Duval paused mid-motion with a log in her hands. Ines looked up from the notebook she was always writing in. "You want to know what happened, ranger? Fine. Here's what happened. Nathan finds this book, and for three days he walks around camp lit up like a lamp. He tells us we're going to change history. He writes letters — drafts of letters, to newspapers, to the university, to the governor. He makes copies of pages. And he talks. He talks on the radio — our satellite phone, scheduled check-ins, and I told him, I *told* him, don't say what it is, say you found artifacts, say geological survey, say anything — and Nathan Hollis looked at me like I'd spat in church and said, 'Tomás, the truth doesn't need disguising. The truth needs witnesses.'"

Tomás spread his hands. "So he said it. Over an open channel. To a dispatch office, to whoever else was listening, that he had found documentary evidence that the Corliss water rights were fraudulently obtained, and that he intended to take it to federal authorities. Three days later he was dead, the radio was smashed with a rock, and the camp burned."

The silence afterward had texture. Ferro stirred in his sleep and muttered something in boatman's Portuguese. The fire settled.

"You think Corliss killed him," Silas said.

"I think Nathan Hollis could swim the Colorado in April. I think he knew that riverbank better than his own kitchen. I think he fell exactly three days after announcing he was going to destroy a hundred-year-old criminal empire, and I think the same week, men started walking our perimeter at night — I saw their sign, boot heels, military spacing, and none of my people walk like that." Tomás leaned forward. "I've been guiding expeditions since before you were born, ranger. I know what hunting looks like. Somebody was hunting us, and then our leader was dead, and I did the arithmetic."

"So you hid the body."

The words landed like a slap and Tomás took them, because he'd earned them. "I moved him," he said. "Upstream, to the bend, and I buried him deep with river stones because—" His voice caught, and he hated it, sixty-one years old and his voice catching like a boy's. "Because the coroner in this county is elected on Corliss money. Because I've watched this valley bury the truth under nicer stones than that for a hundred years. Because if I brought Nathan Hollis's body downriver with a broken neck and a book full of stolen-water deeds in my pack, there would be an accident report and a quick funeral and a line in the paper about a tragic misstep, and that book would end up in a Corliss safe by Friday." He met the ranger's eyes. "I dug that grave to buy time. Not to hide a murder. To hide the evidence *from* a murder."

"Whose murder?" Silas asked softly. "Who killed him, Tomás?"

And there it was — the question Tomás had been circling for three weeks like a bird over a carcass, the question he had not answered even for himself, because answering it required saying the name, and saying the name made it true, and as long as it stayed unsaid there was still a version of the world where Nathan Hollis had simply slipped, simply fallen, simply died the way the paper said.

But the ranger had come forty miles up a dead river on a rumor. The ranger's own father's name was probably in this book too — Tomás hadn't checked, hadn't dared, but the west-bank outfits filled half the entries. And Mara sat across the fire with her father's bow strung now, watching Tomás with eyes that had stopped crying a long time ago and started aiming instead.

Tomás picked up the ledger. Held it a moment, feeling its weight, the whole drowned century of it.

"Ward Pellis," he said.

Silas frowned. "The expedition's sponsor liaison? The man who arranged Ada Corliss's funding?"

"The same man who drove out to camp twice while we were on the river. The same man whose truck I saw parked on the rim road the morning Nathan died, pointed down at the water, engine ticking, nobody in it." Tomás's hands had gone steady now, finally, the way they did when there was work to be done. "And here's the part I've only said out loud once, to the dark, where it couldn't grow: Ward Pellis has been eating dinner at the Hollis table for fifteen years. He's married to Nathan's sister."

Across the fire, Mara Hollis stood up so fast her bedroll slid into the ashes, and the sound of her voice when it came was the driest sound Tomás had ever heard a human being make.

"Say that again," she said.
