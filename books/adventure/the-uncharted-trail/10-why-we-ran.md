## 10. Why We Ran

Ines Okafor had catalogued four hundred and twelve plant specimens in her career, and she could tell you the Latin name of every one of them without opening a notebook. She could not tell you, with the same precision, the exact moment the Hollis expedition stopped being an expedition and became something else — something that ran, and hid, and buried its leader under river stones.

She suspected it was the night of the ledger.

Now she sat on a flat rock at the mouth of the box canyon, knees drawn up, watching the last light fail against the western rim while six people who barely trusted each other boiled water for dinner behind her. The canyon smelled of sage and cold stone and, faintly, of the antiseptic Ruth Ann kept using on Ferro's shoulder. Somewhere upriver, men with Corliss money in their pockets were almost certainly looking for them. And here she was, a botanist who had never fired a gun in her life, trying to find the words to explain why she had run.

Because Silas Vasquez had asked. That was the thing about him. He didn't demand; he asked, quietly, the way you'd ask permission to enter someone's house, and somehow that made it harder to refuse than any order would have been.

Mara came and sat beside her without speaking. The girl's grief had hardened into something Ines recognized from her fieldwork in drought country — that crust the earth forms over wet ground, which looks like strength until you step on it wrong.

"You don't have to," Mara said.

"Yes, I do." Ines pulled her jacket tighter. "You've all told your pieces. Tomás told his. Peter told his. Everyone keeps looking at me like I'm holding the last page of something." She exhaled. "Maybe I am."

* * *

The night it happened, they were camped on the gravel bar below the third bend, eleven people and two boats and a radio mast lashed to a cottonwood. It had been a good day, one of those days field scientists remember for years afterward — Mara's father wading the shallows with his trousers rolled to the knee, laughing at something Cass said, Peter photographing lichen like it was going out of fashion. Ines remembered thinking: *this is what the grant proposal promised us and never delivered.* A river unrecorded. A canyon no survey had touched since before the war.

Hollis found the cave that afternoon.

That was how he told it later, around the fire — *found*, as if the cave had been waiting for him, patient as a seed bank. He'd gone up a side drainage after a flash of white he thought might be gypsum, and instead found a seam in the rock, and inside the seam, wrapped in oilcloth inside a tin box inside a hollowed niche, a leather ledger with a brass clasp gone green with age.

He read it by headlamp, sitting cross-legged on the gravel, and Ines watched his face change. She'd known Edmund Hollis for nine years. She'd seen him lose funding, lose his marriage, lose a finger to frostbite on the Tanana. His face did many things. What it did that night was something she had never seen: it went old. Not tired. Old. Like a man reading his own obituary and finding it accurate.

"Ada Corliss funded this expedition," he said, when he finally looked up. He said it plainly, the way he announced data. "Did anyone else know that?"

Silence around the fire. Tomás shifted. "Her foundation letterhead was on the correspondence, Edmund. You knew."

"I knew her name was on it. I didn't know what the name meant." He set the ledger on his knee. "I'm going to read this to you. All of you. And then I want you to understand that once you've heard it, you can't unhear it, and I won't think less of anyone who wants off this river tomorrow morning."

Nobody wanted off the river. That was the first mistake, Ines thought now, all these weeks later. Curiosity is a beautiful trait in a scientist and a fatal one everywhere else.

* * *

Hollis read by firelight, turning pages with hands that weren't quite steady.

It was a water ledger. 1891 through 1899. Entries in three different hands, the earliest ones cramped and careful, the later ones looser, more confident — the handwriting of a man getting comfortable with what he was doing. Elias Corliss. Ada's father.

The entries recorded transfers of water rights. Ditch shares, spring claims, riparian parcels along forty miles of the river — the same river they were camped on. And next to nearly every transfer, in the margin, in that increasingly confident hand, small annotations. Ines had spent her life reading field notes; she knew annotation when she saw it.

*"J. Brandt — signed after well poisoned. Recovered in 6 days."*

*"R. Vasquez — refused twice. Cattle found dead at dry tank, third refusal unnecessary."*

*"P. Duval — burned out. Bought land at half assessment. Wife's family gone east."*

She heard Cass Duval stop breathing across the fire. She heard Tomás Reyes say, very softly, a word in Spanish that wasn't a prayer but sounded like one.

"The signatures are coerced or forged," Hollis said. "Mostly coerced. There are affidavits bound into the back — sworn statements from 1893, witnesses, a county clerk who tried to file honest records and got driven out of office. This book is the founding document of the Corliss fortune. Every acre, every ditch, every company store scrip dollar — it starts here, with a man poisoning wells to make ranchers sign away their water." He closed the ledger. The brass clasp clicked. "And the woman paying our salaries is his daughter, and she has spent sixty years making sure nobody reads this."

Peter asked the question Ines was too slow to ask: "Does *she* know it still exists?"

"She paid for an expedition to survey this exact stretch of river," Hollis said. "With instructions to log every side drainage above the third bend. What do you think?"

The fire cracked. Downstream, the river made its low continuous sound, indifferent as arithmetic.

"So we take it public," Cass said. "Newspapers. Territorial office. Hell, mail it to Washington."

"That's exactly what we do," Hollis said. "But not from out here, where a radio call and two weeks makes us all missing persons. We finish the survey — two more days, we're done anyway — then we run downriver to Corlissville, and I walk into the newspaper office with this under my arm and Elena Brandt's name in my mouth if I can get it." He looked around the circle. "I need everyone to understand the risk. Ada Corliss is dying — Tomás heard it from the freight pilots. Dying women with empires protect their legacies the way lionesses protect cubs. If she learns what we have before we're somewhere safe—"

"She'll already know," Tomás said quietly. "Or she'll know soon. The radio traffic on this river isn't private, Edmund. Her foreman talks to her every evening at seven. I've heard the schedule."

"Then we move fast." Hollis smiled, and for a moment he looked like himself again, the man who'd talked Ines into this trip over bad coffee two years ago. "Two days. Then the truth goes to work."

* * *

They never got the two days.

Ines woke sometime after midnight because the generator coughed — their little camp generator, which powered the radio and nothing else, and which only coughed when someone kicked it. She lay in her tent listening. Footsteps, quick, crossing the gravel toward the radio mast. Voices, low. Hollis's voice, sharp with alarm: *"Who gave you the frequency?"*

Then a sound she had never heard outside of films and newsreels and had always privately believed she'd misjudge the volume of. She did not misjudge it. It was enormous. It was the loudest thing in the world.

One shot.

She was out of her tent with her boots unlaced, and the camp was chaos — Cass shouting, Ruth Ann kneeling, Tomás running up from the boats with a lantern, and Edmund Hollis on his back on the gravel with his headlamp still on, the beam pointing straight up into the cottonwood branches like he was taking one last light reading of the canopy.

The radio mast lay cut through. Someone had taken an axe — not a knife, an axe — to the base of it, and the radio itself was smashed, its vacuum tubes scattered across the gravel like pale grapes.

And standing at the edge of the firelight, half-turned away, was a man Ines knew. Had known. Ward Pellis — the expedition's sponsor liaison, the smooth-talking man from the Corliss Foundation who'd joined them for the first week and then "gone back ahead to arrange resupply." Except he hadn't gone anywhere. He stood there with a pistol smoking in his hand and Hollis's brother-in-law's face, and when the lantern light reached him he didn't even run. He looked at all of them, one by one, the way a man counts stock.

"Nobody leaves the canyon tonight," Pellis said. "By morning this will be handled. Anyone who interferes will be handled with it." And he walked upstream into the dark like a man going to check a fence line.

Ines did not remember deciding to run. She remembered the gravel hurting her unlaced boots, remember Ruth Ann's hand closing on her arm, remember Tomás's voice — *the boats, the boats* — and then the black water and the cold shock of it and the far shore and the rocks, climbing, climbing, six of them by some count she never verified, breath tearing, until dawn found them in a fold of the hills with no boats, no supplies, no leader, and no idea whether the shot they'd all heard had been meant for one man or eventually for all of them.

* * *

"And that's why we scattered," Ines said now, to Mara and Silas and the fire. Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "Not because we're cowards. Because we each did the math."

She held up fingers.

"Cass — the ledger named her grandfather. P. Duval, burned out. If word gets back to Corlissville that Cass Duval was on this river when a Corliss agent killed a man, what happens? They don't just hunt her. They rewrite it so the Duvals were always trouble. Her whole family history becomes evidence."

Cass, across the fire, said nothing, but she met Ines's eyes, and that was its own confirmation.

"Tomás moved the body — he's told you why, to hide it from whoever came back for it, and God forgive us all for letting him carry that alone. But Tomás has a daughter in Corlissville. Sixteen years old. Works at the company store. One phone call puts her in a room with Duke Sallow, and Tomás knows exactly what Sallow is."

"Peter—" she began.

"Photographed everything," Peter said from the shadows, voice thin. "Yeah. We've covered mine."

"Ines Okafor." She said her own name like a specimen label. "Foreign-born, no family in the territory, employed on Corliss grant money. If I go to the authorities, I'm a stranger accusing the most powerful family in three counties of murder. If I stay silent, I'm complicit. Either way I'm finished as a scientist, because either way the story stops being about the water and starts being about me." She laughed, short and bitter. "I used to think guilt was something you felt. Out here I learned it's something you inherit. Edmund died carrying it, and then it divided itself among the rest of us like an estate."

Mara was staring into the fire. When she spoke, her voice was flat in the way Ines had learned meant the opposite of calm. "You all heard him say he'd take it public. You all heard Pellis threaten you. And you ran in six directions instead of staying together."

"We ran because together we were a witness list," Ines said. "Six names on a page. Apart, we were harder to count." She paused. "It didn't work. But it was the best math we had at three in the morning with our leader dead on the gravel."

Silas had been quiet the whole time, sitting slightly apart with his hat brim low, and when he finally spoke it was in that unhurried ranger's cadence that made even hard statements sound like weather reports.

"Pellis said 'by morning this will be handled.' Meaning the body, the scene, the story. Which means somebody came back for it." He turned his head, and the firelight caught the line of his jaw. "Tomás moved Hollis before they came. So when they arrived, the grave was empty, the camp was torn up, and six sets of tracks led north." He was quiet a moment. "They didn't find a murder scene. They found a mystery. Mysteries take longer to bury than bodies. That's the only reason any of you are alive."

The words settled over the fire like ash.

Ines looked at each of them — Cass sharpening a knife that didn't need it, Ruth Ann checking Ferro's bandage for the fourth time that hour, Peter hunched over nothing, Tomás standing watch at the canyon mouth with his back very straight, and Mara with her father's absence written across her shoulders like a brand.

Guilt as shared inheritance. She'd said it aloud, and hearing it, she understood something else: inheritance could be refused. Estates could be contested. That was, in fact, what the law was for — real law, honest law, a judge two hundred miles east who didn't drink Corliss water.

"Then let's contest it," she said.

Every face turned.

"The ledger, the photographs, Elena Brandt if we can reach her, Tomás's testimony, mine — all of it. Not sold, not bargained, not traded for safe passage. Filed. Sworn. On the record, in front of a federal judge, where Ada Corliss's money can't reach." Ines stood, because her legs wanted to stand. "I've been afraid since the night Edmund died. I'm going to be afraid either way. I'd rather be afraid *loudly*."

For a long moment nobody moved. Then Tomás, at the canyon mouth, turned around slowly, and there was something in his old face that hadn't been there since the gravel bar — not hope exactly, but the ground hope grows in.

Before anyone could answer him, Cass's hand came up, palm out, toward the dark mouth of the canyon.

"Quiet," she breathed. "Listen."

Downriver, faint but unmistakable, carried on the night air the way sound carries over water: engines. More than one. Coming up the river road.

Silas was on his feet before the echo faded, and the look on his face told Ines that the math had just changed again.
