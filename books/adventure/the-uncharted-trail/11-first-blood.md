## 11. First Blood

The light went amber, then copper, then the particular bruised red that meant the sun had found the notch in the western rim and was bleeding out through it. Silas stood at the mouth of the box canyon with his field glasses up, watching the shadow line crawl down the far wall, and he did not like what the shadows were doing.

They were moving wrong.

Shadow doesn't climb. Shadow doesn't detach itself from a boulder and walk three steps and crouch again. He'd been watching that wall for ten minutes, telling himself it was heat shimmer off the sandstone, telling himself a man who'd spent twelve years alone in the high desert ought to know the difference between shimmer and intent — and the shape moved again, deliberate, patient, and the telling-himself was over.

He lowered the glasses and turned back toward the camp without hurrying. Hurrying carried. Hurrying was a sound that traveled two hundred yards off bare rock and told every man on that rim exactly where the ranger was and exactly what he knew.

Inside the canyon, the survivors had made a life out of almost nothing. A cook fire burned low in a pit they'd lined with stone to kill the smoke — Tomás's work, the work of a man who'd guided hunters through country where hunters were hunted. Cass Duval had strung the tarps into lean-tos against the south wall. Ines Okafor was cataloging pressed plants by lantern light, her lips moving over Latin names like a rosary. Peter Liu sat hunched over his notebook, writing something he kept covering with his forearm when anyone passed. Ruth Ann knelt beside Ferro, who lay propped against a pack roll with his bandaged thigh stretched out before him, gray-faced but alive, complaining about the beans the way a man complains when complaining is the only proof he's still in the fight.

Mara stood apart from all of them, as she always did, at the canyon's inner wall with her bow strung, watching the dark come down. She heard Silas's boots before she saw him — he made sure of it — and turned.

"You've got that face," she said quietly.

"What face?"

"The one my father wore when the river gauge read wrong."

Silas glanced at the rim. The copper light was dying. In twenty minutes the canyon would be a well of blue-black dark, and the men on the rim would own it, because they'd have been waiting in it since noon and the people below would be stumbling around a fire they couldn't put out in time.

"Get everyone up," he said. "No lights, no voices above a whisper. Ferro needs to be able to move or we need to be ready to carry him."

Mara didn't ask why. That was the thing about her he was still learning — grief had burned every casual question out of her. She just turned and moved through the camp like a low wind, touching shoulders, and within two minutes the lantern was out and the camp had gone quiet as a grave, and Silas stood in the middle of it feeling the weight of six strangers' lives settle onto his back like a pack frame that had been measured for someone bigger.

* * *

He'd told them the truth that morning, or most of it: that the boot prints he'd found two days back didn't belong to any of them, that the prints had come up the river trail and circled the canyon rim and gone out again, unhurried, the prints of a man doing a job rather than a man doing a crime. He'd told them he thought it was Corliss men, and he'd watched that land on each of them differently — Tomás going still and old, Ines pressing her lips white, Cass spitting into the dust and saying he'd figured as much since the first night.

But he hadn't told them what he'd really believed, because he hadn't known it himself. He'd believed it was still a chase. Men hunting people to scare them, to take a ledger off them, maybe to put a scare into them hard enough that they'd hand it over and run home. Ugly, but a shape he understood. A shape with edges. A shape where everybody walked away.

The shape had changed at sundown.

Because the men on the rim hadn't come down at dusk. Any hired gun who wanted a ledger and a frightened girl would come at dusk, when the camp's eyes were tired and the fire made everyone below blind. Coming at full dark, after a day of patient positioning — that wasn't a robbery. That was an extermination. You came at full dark when you didn't want to look at what you were doing, and you positioned all day when you wanted no one getting out to talk about it afterward.

Silas crouched by the dead cook fire and did the arithmetic he hated. Seven of them, counting himself. One rifle — his — plus Cass's scattergun and Mara's bow and whatever knives the rest carried. Ammunition: two boxes for the rifle, half a box of shells. Against however many Sallow had brought up the river. Six at least, from the prints. Maybe more. And the canyon had one way in and out, which was why Tomás had chosen it, and which was now the exact thing that made it a trap with the door already watched.

"Ranger." Tomás Reyes materialized out of the dark at his elbow, moving silent as a man half his age. The old guide's face was a gray smudge above his collar. "You've seen them."

"Rim of the north wall. Three that I've counted, maybe a fourth behind the hoodoo formation. There'll be more at the canyon mouth."

"Then we go out the mouth."

"The mouth is where they want us. Open ground, moonrise in an hour, and they'll have the low rocks." Silas rubbed his jaw. "They've been up there since before noon, Tomás. They're not here to talk."

Tomás was quiet a moment. When he spoke, his voice had dropped into something Silas hadn't heard from him yet — not the guarded old guide, not the man rationing trust like water, but something older and wearier underneath. "I guided men through the Sierra Madre in the revolution years. I know what it looks like when they stop wanting prisoners." He paused. "Does the girl know?"

"Not yet."

"She'll want to fight."

"She'll fight better if she knows what she's fighting for. So will you. So will I." Silas stood. "Nobody panics in the dark. That's the whole battle plan. Nobody panics."

* * *

The first shot came forty minutes after full dark, and it did not come from the rim.

It came from the canyon mouth — a rifle cracking flat and hard off the stone, and the bullet went through the tarp lean-to where Ferro had been lying an hour ago and buried itself in the canyon wall with a sound like a knuckle cracking. Silas had moved Ferro at the last light, over the boatman's loud objections, back into a crease in the rock thirty feet deep in the wall, a slot barely wide enough for a man. The objections had saved his life. Silas noted that, filed it, and was already moving, because the shot at the mouth was a question — *where are you, where are you* — and the answer you gave it decided whether you lived through the next ten minutes.

He gave it no answer. He lay in the shadow of a fallen slab with his rifle against his cheek and his breath shallow and watched the canyon mouth, where a shape detached itself from a boulder and came on in a low crouch, and behind it another shape, and the moon was not up yet but the stars were enough for a man who'd spent twelve years learning to read starlight the way other men read print.

Two at the mouth, pushing in. Which meant the rim was the anvil and the mouth was the hammer, and they meant to drive everyone below into the open center of the canyon and finish it under the moon.

"Ranger Vasquez." The voice rolled down the canyon walls from the rim, big and unhurried and almost friendly, a voice built for auction blocks and hiring halls. Duke Sallow. Silas had never met him, but he knew the voice the way you know a brand — you'd seen it on everything you ever lost. "You're a lawman. This don't concern you. Hand over the ledger and the Hollis girl and walk out, and nobody here needs to write your name in anybody's book."

Silas said nothing. Saying nothing was a weapon. Silence made a man on a rim imagine ten riflemen below instead of one, imagine ambushes and crossfires and all the dark arithmetic of fear.

"I know you're there, ranger. Radio said you went upriver two days back." A pause, and Silas could hear the smile in it. "Corliss pays your wages same as the territory pays them. Same money. Think about whose is bigger."

That was the moment it stopped being a chase for Silas too — not when the first bullet came, but when Sallow said *the radio*. Because dispatch hadn't logged his position in two days; he'd been off-net in the canyon country, and everyone knew it. Which meant Sallow hadn't heard it from dispatch. Which meant someone inside the Corliss operation had a line into the ranger service, or someone in the service had a line out. Either way, the ground under his badge was thinner than he'd thought, and he was standing on it alone.

The second shot came from the rim, high and wide, a ranging shot, and then the mouth came alive.

* * *

It went the way firefights go, which is nothing like the way they're told.

Cass Duval fired the scattergun once, into the dark at the mouth, a great rolling boom that filled the canyon like the sky falling, and whether he hit anything or not it did the job: the two shapes at the mouth stopped, went to ground, and started shooting at the place the boom had come from, which was not the place Cass had already rolled away from. Silas put two rounds at the muzzle flashes, not to kill but to fix them, to make them keep their heads down, and the ricochets screamed off the canyon walls in a sound he would hear for the rest of his life.

Mara was on the rim side, and she was better than he'd guessed. She'd climbed into the rocks above the camp in the last light with her bow, and when a gunman on the north rim shifted position to get an angle on the camp, she put an arrow into the rock a hand's width from his head — not to hit him, Silas understood later, but because an arrow makes no muzzle flash, and a man who takes an arrow out of the dark beside his ear starts believing the dark is full of archers. The gunman broke and ran along the rim, and his running boots told every man on that wall that the people below were not sheep.

Then the rim shooters found the range.

A burst of rifle fire walked down the canyon wall and through the camp, and Silas heard the tarp tear, heard Ines scream a warning, and then heard the sound he'd been dreading since sundown — a cry from the crease in the rock, Ferro's voice, high and cracked with pain.

"They're in the slot," Ruth Ann shouted, and her voice had gone flat and clinical, the voice of a woman who had decided to be calm because everyone else's calm was going to be manufactured from hers. "They're shooting into the slot — I can't reach him —"

Silas moved.

Later he could not have told you whether it was courage or stupidity or just the protector's arithmetic running itself without him — seven lives, one of them bleeding out thirty feet away, and the only man with a rifle and the training to use it was lying in the dark doing nothing. He ran the thirty feet in a low crouch with bullets cracking off the stone around him, one of them close enough that he felt the wind of it kiss his ear, and he slid into the mouth of the crease with his back against the rock and his heart going like a stamp mill.

Ferro lay twisted in the slot, one hand pressed to his thigh, and even in the starlight Silas could see the bandage had gone black. The bullet hadn't hit him — Silas saw the fresh gouge in the rock above — but the old wound had torn, the boatman's own scrambling in the dark doing what the gunmen's lead hadn't. He was bleeding the way a man bleeds when the bleeding doesn't intend to stop on its own.

"Ruth Ann," Silas called back down the crease, low. "Come on. They can't see in here. Move."

She came, bent double, her medical bag clutched to her chest like a child, and knelt in the dark and got her hands on Ferro and started working by touch. Silas turned back to the crease mouth, put his rifle through it, and did the only thing left: he shot at the rim flashes, steady, deliberate, making himself a target on purpose, because every round they sent at him was a round not sent at Ruth Ann's back.

He was reloading when he heard it — a grunt from the canyon mouth, a body's weight hitting rock, and then Cass's voice, ragged and triumphant: "Got one. Ranger! Mouth of the canyon, I got one down."

The shooting faltered. The rim went quiet, then the mouth went quiet, and the silence that followed had a different texture than the silence before — the silence of men reassessing. Silas knelt in the crease mouth with his rifle hot and his ears ringing and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, exactly what had changed tonight.

Sallow hadn't pressed. He'd had them — seven people, one rifleman, one way out — and when the fight turned expensive he'd pulled his men back like a businessman closing a position. You don't do that for a ledger. A ledger is worth one more push, one more volley, one more dead hired gun. You pull back like that when the job isn't a robbery anymore. You pull back like that when the job is erasure and the schedule matters more than the prize — when what matters isn't getting the ledger but making sure that no one who has seen it is alive in a month, alive in a year, alive to testify in some courtroom or write some letter or tell some grandchild.

They weren't chasing witnesses. They were deleting them.

And he was the only thing standing between the deletion and seven people who trusted him, six of whom had never fired a shot in anger and one of whom was bleeding out on the stones while a medic worked on him by feel.

The burden came down on him then, all at once, the full weight of it, and it was heavier than he'd imagined and shaped differently — not like a pack frame but like a yoke, like something you lean into rather than carry, something that only works if you keep moving forward. He thought of his father for one unbidden second, the old man's face the last time Silas had seen it, and he put the thought away where he kept it, in the deep dark place, and he stood up.

* * *

The gunman at the canyon mouth was dead.

Cass had put a load of buckshot into him at twenty feet, and there was nothing Ruth Ann could have done and nothing anyone would have tried. He lay on his back with his hat a yard away and the stars coming out above him, a young man, younger than Silas, with a weak chin and a rifle he'd never got to aim. Tomás crouched over the body and went through the pockets with the efficiency of a man who'd done it in worse places, and came up with a paybook — a small, grease-stained ledger of payments received, and that was a bitter joke none of them made — and a tin of matches and a photograph of a woman in front of a board house, creased soft from handling.

Silas stood over the dead man for a long moment.

He had shot at men before. He had never stood over one. The difference was the whole distance between the high desert and everywhere else, and he felt it in his knees.

"He's nobody," Cass said behind him, not cruelly, just factually, the way you'd appraise a horse. "Some Corliss-town boy. Probably told his ma he was going to work the company store."

"He's somebody's somebody," Silas said. "Same as all of us."

He took the paybook. The entries were in different hands — cash payments, no names, no employer's name, just dates and amounts and a single initial stamped where a signature should be: a C in a circle, neat as a cattle brand. Deniable money. Money designed so that a dead man's pockets told nothing and a living man's testimony proved nothing. He'd seen scrip and company chits his whole life; he'd never seen money built like a weapon before.

Behind him, Ruth Ann's voice came out of the dark, tight with effort. "I need light in here. Real light. He's torn open and I can't — I need light, and I need this man to stop bleeding, and I need both of those things now."

Silas turned. In the starlight he could see the survivors gathered at the crease — Ferro gray and shivering, Ruth Ann's hands black to the wrist, Mara holding a lantern she hadn't lit, looking at him, waiting, the way they were all looking at him, all of them, even Tomás, even Cass, as if the light and the answer and the next hour were all going to come out of him.

He realized his hands were shaking. He closed them into fists until they stopped.

"Light the lantern," he said. "Low, behind the rock. They're gone for tonight, but they'll be back, and next time they won't knock." He looked at the paybook in his hand, at the neat C in its circle, and heard his own voice come out flat and sure, a voice he didn't quite recognize. "Ada Corliss didn't send men to take a book off you. She sent men to leave no one to read it. So we're done running scared and we're done splitting the difference. Tomorrow we decide how we fight — all of us, together."

Mara struck the match. The lantern bloomed, small and gold in the vast dark of the canyon, and Silas stood in its edge thinking of one more thing, the thing Sallow had said, the thing he hadn't told them yet:

*The radio said you went upriver.*

Somebody had been talking. And until he knew who, there wasn't a canyon in the territory deep enough to hide in.
