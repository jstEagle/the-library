## 12. Twelve Years of Miles

The fire burned low and blue at its heart, the way fires did in river cottonwood when the wood was half-green and the night was cold enough to want it anyway. Silas fed it a branch and watched the bark curl. Above the canyon rim the stars were out in their full desert numbers — more stars than sky, it seemed some nights — and the survivors had gone quiet in that particular way people went quiet when they were listening to a silence instead of talking through it.

Mara sat across from him with her knees drawn up and her father's compass open in her palm. She'd been turning it over for an hour. Not reading it. Just holding it, the way you'd hold a hand.

"You count everything," she said finally.

He looked up.

"Today. On the trail. You counted the switchbacks coming down off the mesa. You counted the water bottles twice. This morning you counted us." She nodded at the six shapes arranged around the fire — Tomás feeding the flames, Ines writing by lantern light, Peter asleep sitting up against a rock, Cass on watch with her rifle across her knees, Ruth Ann's hands still moving through the motions of checking Ferro's bandage even though he was sleeping. "You count everything like it might come up short."

Silas turned his hands toward the fire. They were scarred across the knuckles, rope-burned, sun-cracked. A working man's hands, a moving man's hands.

"Everything comes up short," he said. "If you're not counting."

"That's not living. That's inventory."

He almost smiled. She had her father's bluntness, then. He'd only met Nathaniel Hollis once, years back, at a ranger station potluck nobody important attended — a tall man who asked real questions about aquifer levels and actually waited for the answers. He remembered thinking: here's a man who counts things too, but he counts them because he loves them.

Maybe that was the difference. Maybe that was all the difference there ever was.

"My father kept a ledger," Silas said.

The word landed in the fire circle and Tomás's head came up from the flames. Ines's pen stopped. Silas felt the whole camp tilt toward him, subtle as water finding grade, and understood he'd said something larger than he'd meant to. But Mara was looking at him with those gray eyes, waiting, and twelve years of silence suddenly weighed more than the saying of it would.

"He kept accounts of everything we owned," Silas went on. "Head of cattle. Bales of hay. Rain, even. My mother thought it was a sickness. He said a man who doesn't know what he has doesn't know what he's losing."

* * *

The Vasquez ranch had been forty acres on the west bank of the Río Sombra, upstream of Corlissville, and it had been in the family since before the family had a name written down anywhere official. Silas's grandfather had filed on it with a mule and a divining rod. The well ran sweet. The river ran generous. For three generations the ranch had been poor the way most ranches were poor — thin years and fat years, boots resoled until the soles gave out — but never desperate. Never owned by anybody but themselves.

Then Ada Corliss's father died, and Ada took the company, and Ada looked upstream.

"It wasn't a theft the way you'd tell it in a story," Silas said. "Nobody rode in with guns. It was worse than that. It was paper." He watched Mara's face in the firelight. "They'd bought the irrigation district. Quietly, over years, little parcels of stock nobody thought mattered. Then one spring they announced new schedules. Upstream users got allocations. Downstream got what was left, which was arithmetic dressed up as law. And the district could call in liens — old ones, some of them forged, my father swore later, though proving forgery costs money and money was the one thing we didn't have."

"How much did they take?" Ines asked quietly. Her pen was down now. She wasn't writing anymore.

"The allocation cut us to a tenth of our historic flow. A tenth." He laughed, and it came out wrong, dry as the creek bed he was describing. "You can't run forty acres of alfalfa on a tenth. You can't run anything. The wells along that stretch started brackish when the river dropped — the water table followed the surface down like a dog following its master. By August our sweet well tasted of salt and sulfur. By September the cattle were drinking mud."

His father had fought it the way men like him fought things — with letters, with lawyers they couldn't afford, with trips to the county seat where the courthouse steps seemed to rise a stair higher every year. He'd spent the winter savings on a hearing. He'd lost the hearing. He'd spent the spring savings on an appeal. There was no appeal; the judge had been elected on Corliss scrip like everyone else in three counties.

"And then?" Mara asked. Though she must have known. Everyone in that country knew how those stories ended. That was the thing about the Corliss story — it wasn't one story, it was a hundred stories with the same ending, told in different kitchens, and nobody had ever stitched them together into a single tale anyone had to answer for.

"Then he started drinking," Silas said. "Not the way ranchers drink. Ranchers drink to stop. He drank to go somewhere else. Somewhere the ledger balanced."

* * *

The winter after the water died, Silas was sixteen. He remembered it in fragments the way you remember any bad season — not whole scenes but details, the way grief keeps only the edges of things. His mother's hands cracking from lye. The smell of the last hay, sold early and cheap to a Corliss feedlot at a price set by the buyer because there was no second buyer within a hundred miles. His father sitting at the kitchen table at two in the morning with the ledger open, running the same column of figures again and again, as if the numbers might get tired of being true.

"He'd stopped writing entries by then," Silas said. "That's how I knew it was bad. A man like my father stops keeping books when he can't stand to see the page."

In March, the bank called the note. Not because the Vasquez ranch had missed a payment — it hadn't; his mother had been selling her own jewelry to keep current — but because the bank had been bought too, or leaned on, or simply understood which side of the river its bread was buttered on. The notice used the phrase *material change in water security*. Four words. Forty acres, three generations, a grandfather's mule and divining rod: four words.

"They gave us ninety days," Silas said. "We were out in thirty. Couldn't stand to watch the place die slowly. My father signed the deed with a pen the banker handed him, and I swear on everything holy that man smiled. Not a mean smile. A relieved one. Like he'd been dreading having to be cruel and had gotten lucky."

Tomás spoke for the first time, his voice low and roughened by the fire. "Where'd they go?"

"Town. What there was of town. My mother cleaned houses for the families that had water. My father—" Silas stopped. Fed the fire instead. The green branch hissed and spat.

"My father walked the levee road every day," he tried again. "Out past the corrals they'd torn down, along the channel where our water used to come in. Every day. Rain, wind, didn't matter. People said he was crazy. He wasn't crazy. He was auditing. He'd stand at the headgate they'd locked and look down the empty ditch like the numbers might change if he checked often enough."

The summer Silas turned seventeen, in June, the river came up with the snowmelt the way it always did, high and brown and loud, and his father walked into it at the place where the old ditch met the channel, wearing his good coat, with the ledger buttoned inside it.

"They found the coat two miles down," Silas said. "Never found him. Never found the book. The river keeps what it takes."

Nobody moved. The fire cracked. Somewhere up the canyon a coyote said one short sentence to the dark and got no answer.

"I used to think the river drowned him," Silas said. "Now I think it just finished the job. The Corliss company killed him slow for three years and the river was kind enough to make it quick. Same water either way. That's the joke of it. The water that built their empire is the water that took him. It's all one river. It doesn't know whose it is. Only we do."

* * *

Mara's voice, when it came, was careful. "Is that why you became a ranger?"

He considered lying. He was good at lying in the small ways — the shrug, the deflection, the joke that closed a door. But she had held an arrow on him four days ago and hadn't flinched, and she'd buried a father under river stones, and she deserved the same honesty he'd have wanted at that graveside.

"I became a ranger because rangers move," he said. "That's the whole truth of it, ugly as it is. I told myself it was service. Protecting the country, helping folks, all the things the recruiting poster says. And some of that's real. I've pulled people out of flash floods. I've carried water to stranded prospectors. I believe in the work." He turned his hands again, palms up, as if showing the fire they were empty. "But underneath it, underneath all of it, I picked a job where every morning has mileage in it. Where there's always another ridge, another drainage, another stretch of trail that needs walking. Because a man who's moving doesn't have to arrive anywhere. And arriving means staying, and staying means a ledger, and a ledger means knowing exactly what you have."

"Which means knowing exactly what you've lost," Ines said softly.

"Yeah." He looked at her. "You understand that."

"I'm from a country that changed its name twice before I was ten," she said. "I count everything too, Ranger. I just write mine down."

Something passed around the fire then that Silas didn't have a clean name for. Not pity — he'd have gotten up and walked into the dark rather than sit in pity. Recognition, maybe. Six people who had each lost something to forces bigger than themselves, discovering they'd all been keeping private ledgers of it. Cass shifted on her watch stool. Even Peter, half-asleep, opened his eyes.

"So you never stayed anywhere," Mara said. "Twelve years."

"Longest I've held a station is this one. Three years. And that's because the district is big enough that I log two hundred miles a week." He smiled without much humor. "My supervisor thinks I'm dedicated. I let him think it."

"What happens when the miles run out? When there's no more country left?"

"There's always more country."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he admitted. "It isn't."

She uncurled, crossed the fire circle, and sat down beside him close enough that he could smell woodsmoke in her hair. She held out the compass — her father's, brass-cased, the glass scratched, the needle trembling toward north the way needles do when they're honest.

"He measured everything too," she said. "Every survey, every elevation, every mile of that expedition route. I used to tease him about it. Now I think—" Her voice caught, and she steadied it the way she'd been steadying it for days, by force. "Now I think measuring is how some of us love things. We pay attention because attention is all we've got to give."

"Your father loved this country," Silas said. "You could see it in how he asked questions. Most people ask questions to hear themselves talk. He asked them to listen."

"You knew him?"

"Once. Potluck at the Redrock station, maybe eight years ago. He wanted to know about the seasonal springs up the east fork. Nobody had mapped them properly." He paused. "I drew him a map. On a napkin. He kept it. Told me he'd check every one of those springs himself someday."

Mara made a sound that was almost a laugh and almost nothing else. "He probably did. He probably logged them all."

"He probably did."

* * *

The fire settled. Tomás rose, stretched his bad back with a wince, and added the last of the green wood, and the smoke rose straight in the windless dark, a gray thread stitching the canyon to the stars. Silas found himself doing what he always did — counting. Seven heads around the fire. Nine, counting himself and Ferro. Eleven originally. Two dead, one of them murdered, and the number of enemies unknown but certainly greater than zero. Water: eleven gallons in the good containers plus what the seep would give by morning. Miles to the narrows: fourteen. Miles to anywhere safe: more than he liked to say aloud.

"You're doing it right now," Mara said.

"Old habit."

"Counting us."

"Somebody should."

"Why you?"

Because someone has to, he almost said. Because if I don't count you, I can't lose you properly, and if I can't lose you properly I won't have to feel it. Because my father taught me that a man who knows what he has knows what he's losing, and I learned the lesson too well, and I've been losing things at a comfortable distance ever since.

Instead he said: "Because I'm the one with the map."

She studied him in the firelight, and he had the uncomfortable sensation of being read — tracked, the way he tracked game, her attention moving over him and finding the prints he left whether he meant to or not.

"Tomorrow," she said, "when we move out — count me first."

"That's not how it works. I count the water and the ammunition and the—"

"Count me first." No anger in it. Just that steady gray certainty, the same look she'd had at arrow-draw. "Because I've watched you for four days, Silas Vasquez, and you count the supplies like they matter and the people like they're cargo. I'm asking you to stop. Or at least to start with the people. Start with me. If we're going to get out of this, you'll need to care about the cargo less and the crew more."

The words went into him deeper than an arrow would have. He thought of his father at the headgate, auditing an empty ditch. He thought of the napkin map, and a man who'd checked every spring because the springs mattered. He thought of twelve years of mileage logs in neat columns, and not one entry in any of them that said: *today I stayed.*

"All right," he said. "You're first."

She nodded once, satisfied, and went back to her side of the fire, and the conversation closed like water over a stone.

But Silas sat awake long after the others slept, watching the coals breathe, and the past lay beside him like a drowned thing risen at last to the surface — his father's good coat, the locked headgate, the ledger going down into the brown water with all its careful columns of everything they'd had. All these years he'd thought the way to honor that was to keep moving, to keep the numbers, to never own anything the river could take.

Tomorrow there'd be fourteen miles to the narrows, and men behind them with rifles, and a dead man's ledger hidden in a saddlebag that could burn down an empire or get them all killed, quite possibly both.

And tonight, for the first time in twelve years, Silas Vasquez wrote a different kind of entry in the ledger of himself. Not miles. Names. Seven names around a fire, and his own name at the bottom of the list, where it had always belonged.

He was still awake when Cass's whistle sounded, soft and sharp, from the north rim.

"Rider," she breathed. "One rider. Coming down the draw. Carrying a white rag on a stick."
