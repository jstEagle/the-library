## 13. The Split

The fire was small — smaller than it needed to be, small enough that a man standing on the canyon rim a half-mile off would see nothing but darkness where a fire should be. Silas had built it that way on purpose, banking the coals low, and now the six of them and Mara crouched around it like conspirators around a dying thing, faces lit from below, shadows thrown huge against the canyon wall.

Ferro lay propped against a pack near the edge of the light, his bandaged leg stretched out, his breathing shallow but steady. Ruth Ann sat close enough to him to touch without reaching. Peter had his notebook open on his knees but hadn't written anything in it for an hour. Ines sat with her arms wrapped around her shins, and Tomás stood apart, the way he'd stood apart since they'd come down off the rim two days ago, a man keeping one shoulder between himself and the group.

Silas let the silence go on long enough that it stopped being accidental and started being a choice.

"All right," he said. "We've got Corliss men on the rim, maybe eight of them, maybe more by morning. We've got one rifle with eleven rounds, a bow with nine arrows, and a boatman who can't walk. We've got a ledger that proves the Corliss family stole the water out from under half the territory, and we've got every reason to believe they'll kill every one of us to keep it from being read. So before somebody makes a decision for the rest of us, we decide. Tonight. All of us."

"Decide what, exactly?" Cass Duval said. She was turned sideways to the fire, arms crossed, her hat brim low, and she spoke the way she did everything — flat, unhurried, like the words had to cross a long distance to get out. "That's the question, ranger. Decide what?"

"What to do with the ledger."

Tomás moved. It was the first time he'd come toward the fire all night, and when he stepped into the light his face was carved up with shadow and ember-glow, sixty years of desert written in the lines of it. He crouched, slow on his knees, and laid one hand flat on the ground between them as if the earth itself were part of the argument.

"I'll tell you what we do," he said. "We sell it."

Nobody spoke. The river muttered somewhere below the canyon's mouth.

"You all want to hear it, so listen." Tomás's voice was low and even, the voice of a man who had guided people through country that killed the unprepared for thirty years and had learned to make every word carry. "The Corlisses didn't build what they built alone. There are three other families in this territory older than dirt and hungrier than wolves — the Marchetti interests out of the south valley, the Ashcomb brothers, the Hollenbeck trust. Any one of them would pay more than we could count for a document that breaks the Corliss water claims in court. Protection. Lawyers. Armed men of our own. Passage out of this country alive." He looked around the fire, and his eyes stopped on Mara last and stayed there. "Your father found this thing. He'd want it used. Used to keep his daughter breathing."

"My father," Mara said quietly, "would want it read."

Tomás didn't flinch. "I buried him, girl. Don't tell me what he'd want."

The words landed like a slap and Mara took them like one, chin up, eyes wet and furious, and Silas watched her decide — visibly, the muscles of her jaw working — not to answer. It was the hardest kind of discipline, and he'd seen it in soldiers and he'd seen it in animals cornered, and he respected it.

"Newspapers," Ines said.

She said it simply, the way she said everything, a woman who had spent her career stating facts to people who didn't want them. She unwound her arms from her shins and leaned forward, and the firelight caught the gray at her temples.

"Tomás, I understand the arithmetic. I do. Sell it to the Marchettis and we trade one set of masters for another. That's not justice, that's a change in ownership. The ledger is evidence. Evidence belongs in the public record — every page, every signature, every coerced X-mark reproduced in every paper from here to the capital. You can't bribe ten thousand readers. You can't bury a story that's already in a thousand hands." She looked at Silas. "The ranger's territory has a territorial paper. The *Courier*. It's not much, but it's a start, and once it's printed it's printed. Corliss can kill us, but she can't kill the story."

"She can kill us before the ink dries," Cass said.

"Then we should have thought of that before we found the ledger," Ines said, and there was no anger in it, just the flat exhaustion of a woman stating the weather.

"It's not a question of thinking," Cass said. She took her hat off — Silas had never seen her do that, and something in the gesture made the whole fire go still — and turned it in her hands. "It's a question of who pays. You're talking about going public. Fine. Walk me through the morning after. Corliss owns the sheriff in three counties. She owns the judge in two more. She owns the paper in Corlissville, and the man who owns the telegraph line to the territorial capital. We send that ledger east and by the time it's read, every one of us is a name on a headstone and the story is a tragedy about a scientific expedition that came to a bad end in the desert. That's how these things go. I've watched it go that way my whole life." She put the hat back on. "I'm a wrangler. I know horse-trading. Tomás is right. You sell to the strongest buyer and you live."

"And what does living cost?" Ruth Ann asked. She hadn't spoken all night. She was a small woman, precise, with a medic's habit of looking at people the way she looked at wounds — assessing, deciding what needed cutting. "Because I've been counting, and I think it costs everything we came out here to be."

"The dead don't get to be anything," Cass said.

"No," Ruth Ann agreed. "But I didn't spend three years patching up ranch hands who got their water turned off for asking questions, just to hand the ledger to another family that'll do the same thing slower." She looked at Ferro, who had been silent so long Silas had almost counted him asleep. "Ferro. You've got the most to lose. You can't run. Say your piece."

Ferro opened his eyes. The fever had burned him down to wire and sinew, but his voice, when it came, was steady as a fence post.

"I don't care what you do with it," he said. "I care that we stop deciding like we're going to be alive either way. We're not. Not all of us. Somebody at this fire is going to die getting that paper out of this canyon, and the rest of us are going to carry them the rest of our lives. So whatever we choose, we choose knowing that." He closed his eyes again. "That's all I've got."

The silence that followed was the heavy kind, the kind that has weight and temperature, and Silas let it sit because he'd learned in twelve years of riding alone that silence was where people told the truth. He looked around the fire. Tomás with his hand still flat on the earth, selling survival. Ines with her spine straight, selling the record. Cass with her hat brim down, selling surrender to the strongest power available. Ruth Ann watching all of them like a pulse she was counting. Mara with her father's name caught in her throat like a fishhook.

And Peter, who hadn't said a word, staring into the coals with the look of a man carrying something in his chest too big for it.

"Your father's name," Silas said to Mara. "You haven't said it yet. Say it."

Mara looked up. For a moment he thought she wouldn't.

"They killed him," she said. "That's first. Before the newspapers, before the sale, before any of it — my father was murdered for finding that ledger, and the story they'll tell is that he died of exposure or a bad heart or his own foolishness, and I will not — I will *not* — let that be the last word on him. He was not a fool. He was not careless. He was a good man who found a true thing, and they put him in the ground for it, and if the ledger goes to the Marchettis or the *Courier* or anywhere else, his name goes with it, cleared, first, or I burn the thing myself before I let them have it." Her voice cracked on the last word and she didn't apologize for it. "That's my piece."

"Nobody's clearing anything if we're dead," Tomás said, but it was gentler now, and Silas heard the thing underneath it — the old guide's guilt, the man who'd moved the body and dug the grave and carried the secret, arguing for survival because he couldn't argue for forgiveness.

"Then we're dead anyway," Silas said.

Every head turned.

He'd been quiet through the whole argument, doing what he did — listening, mapping, letting the country and the people tell him their shape. Now he leaned forward and put a stick into the coals and watched it catch, because it was easier to talk to fire than to faces.

"Here's what I know," he said. "I know Sallow's men found this canyon once. They'll find it again. I know Corliss doesn't negotiate — she erases. The dead gunman's paybook had cash and no names, and cash and no names means we were never meant to be witnesses, we were meant to be a rumor. I know selling to the Marchettis buys us maybe a year before somebody buys *them*, and I know a newspaper story without a court behind it is a story Corliss sues into silence inside a month." He turned the stick. "So all three plans are right, and all three plans are half a plan. Tomás is right that we need protection. Ines is right that it has to be public. Mara's right that it starts with a dead man's name. And Cass is right that the way I'm about to propose is the hard way."

He looked up.

"We do all of it. At the same time."

Cass made a sound that was almost a laugh. "Ranger."

"We split the ledger — no. We *copy* the ledger. Peter, you photographed pages. Can you photograph the rest?"

Peter blinked, coming up out of whatever well he'd been down in. "I — the camera survived the narrows. The film's low. Maybe fifteen, twenty exposures."

"Then we make twenty copies and we separate them. One set goes public — newspapers, telegraph, every courthouse mailbox in the territory, mailed from three different towns so Corliss can't stop them all. One set goes to a buyer — not to sell, Tomás, but to *show*. The Marchettis learn that the Corliss water claims are built on fraud, and suddenly they've got a reason to keep Corliss's lawyers busy for a year while we move. And one set goes to whoever we can find in this territory who isn't bought — a judge, a federal man, somebody — with the affidavits and the testimony to make it stick." He looked around the fire. "And we do it while every one of us is still breathing, because a dead witness is worth nothing and a live one is worth everything. That's the plan. Everybody out alive, and the whole rotten thing in the sunlight. Both. At once."

The fire popped. Down the canyon, the river kept up its low, indifferent argument with the stones.

"That's not a plan," Cass said at last. "That's a fantasy. You're asking seven people with one rifle and a sick man to outrun the richest family in the territory, beat them to a courthouse, outmaneuver them in the press, *and* play poker with the Marchettis. I've seen men try to thread a needle in a windstorm with better odds than that." She shook her head slowly. "You want to know what I think, ranger? I think you've been out here alone so long you've forgotten what odds are."

"Maybe," Silas said. "Or maybe I've been out here alone long enough to know that the odds don't change whether you run or stand. Corliss's men are on the rim right now. Come morning they come down. There's no version of this where we sit in this canyon and live. The only question left is what we're living *for* when we move." He looked at her, and held it. "You want safe, Cass. There isn't any. There's only the difference between dying for nothing and dying for a thing that outlives you. I know which one I'm picking."

"Easy to say with a badge."

"It wasn't a badge that got my father killed."

The words were out before he meant them, and the fire went very quiet. Cass looked at him a long moment, and whatever she saw in his face made her take her hat off again and put it on her knee, and that, Silas understood, was as close as Cass Duval came to an apology.

Tomás stood, joints cracking, and looked down into the fire for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had changed — lower, wearier, the voice from the grave site.

"You sound like Hollis," he said. "Third night before he died, he stood where you're standing and said nearly the same words. *All of it, at once, Tomás. The whole rotten thing.*" He rubbed his hand across his mouth. "I told him to sell it. I told him to be careful. Three days later I was digging his grave with a river stone in my fist and hating myself for being right." His eyes came up and found Silas, and there was something in them that hadn't been there before — not trust, not yet, but the beginning of the ground trust gets built on. "If we do it your way, ranger, we do it my way on the river. I know this water better than any man alive. You get us to the current, I get us through it."

"Then it's settled," Ines said.

"It's not settled," Cass said. "It's *proposed*."

"It's settled enough," Ruth Ann said, "that we should sleep in shifts, because if we're doing the impossible, I'd like to be awake for it."

A breath of something moved around the fire — not quite laughter, but the shape of it, the first one in days, and Silas felt the strange mechanics of it working on the group: argument as mortar, disagreement as the thing that binds people tighter than agreement ever could. An hour ago they'd been seven strangers sharing a fire. Now they were seven people who'd told each other the truth and were still sitting in the same circle, and that was a different animal entirely. He'd spent twelve years measuring his life in miles because miles asked nothing of him. This circle asked everything. It terrified him more than Sallow's guns, and he found he didn't want it to stop.

Mara moved around the fire and sat down next to him, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, and didn't say anything, and didn't need to. Tomás was already talking river with Cass, low and fast, depths and channels and the narrows at night. Ines was making a list in the margin of her field book — towns, courthouses, papers, in order of distance and honesty. Peter had his camera out, checking the film count by feel in the dark.

It was Ruth Ann who ended it. She'd been at the canyon mouth, checking on the dark, and she came back fast, boots quiet, and crouched by Silas with her voice dropped to almost nothing.

"Fire's got a visitor," she said. "Downstream side. One man, walking slow, no light. He's been there ten minutes, just standing, looking at us."

Silas was on his feet before she finished, rifle in hand, and the circle of argument dissolved into the circle of readiness around him, seven people moving as one without anyone saying so.

The man in the dark struck a match.

The flame lit the bottom half of a face Silas knew — the easy grin gone, replaced by something older and more tired, and in his raised hand, instead of a gun, a white rag, hanging limp in the windless dark.

Duke Sallow stepped into the firelight with his empty hands held wide and said, "Evening, ranger. We need to talk business."
