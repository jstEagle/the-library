## 14. Sallow's Offer

The white rag came up the canyon floor at midmorning, tied to a rifle barrel like a flag of surrender that nobody believed.

Silas saw him first from the rim rocks — a single rider moving slow and deliberate, hands visible, the cloth snapping in the dry wind. Behind the rider, at the canyon mouth, maybe eight men sat their horses in a loose line, close enough to be seen, far enough to claim they hadn't been part of whatever happened next.

"He's coming alone," Cass said, glassing the mouth of the canyon. "That's either honest or it's theater."

"It's theater," Silas said. "Question is which act."

He climbed down from the rocks and walked out to meet the rider in the open, alone, his rifle slung muzzle-down across his back where everyone could see it wasn't in his hands. Mara watched from behind a boulder with an arrow nocked, because she'd refused to do otherwise and Silas had decided that arguing cost more than it bought. Tomás stood at the canyon wall with a pistol he wasn't supposed to have, and Ruth Ann kept Ferro flat in the shade of the overhang with a hand on the boatman's shoulder, holding him down by gravity and will.

Duke Sallow reined up ten yards out. He was a big man gone soft in the middle, with a face like a shovel — broad, blunt, built for moving dirt — and eyes that did none of the digging. He wore a Corliss brand on his saddle skirt and a gunfighter's calm that Silas recognized as purchased, not earned. Men who'd earned calm didn't wear it like a coat.

"Ranger Vasquez." Sallow touched his hat brim. "I looked you up before I rode out. Twelve years of service. No disciplinary actions. Commendation for the Harlow fire." He smiled, and it was almost a good smile. "You're a hard man to buy and an easy man to respect."

"That's the first flattering thing anyone's said to me since a horse kicked me in 1911."

Sallow laughed — a real laugh, brief and genuine, and that was the dangerous part. The man was good. "I'll come to it, then. Mrs. Corliss sent me to make an offer, and I'd take it as a personal favor if you'd hear the whole thing before you tell me to ride back up my own tail."

"I'm listening."

"First: nobody here has to die. That's not a threat dressed up nice, that's arithmetic. You've got six civilians, one wounded man, one woman who shoots a bow better than most men shoot rifles, and yourself. I've got eleven guns now, twelve by tomorrow, and Mrs. Corliss writes checks the way other people breathe. The math doesn't care how good you are in the rocks."

"Math rarely does."

"So here's what makes the math friendly." Sallow reached slowly into his coat — Silas's hand drifted toward his hip and Sallow froze the motion, showing his palm, patient as a schoolteacher — and drew out a folded paper, holding it up between two fingers. "The ledger. That's all she wants. The old book. Hand it over, walk your people out the south fork tomorrow morning, and every soul in this canyon goes home free. No follow. No questions. She gives her word."

"And the girl?"

Something flickered behind the shovel face. Not quite guilt. More like a man checking his footing on ice.

"The girl comes with me," Sallow said. "She's a Corliss ward now. Her father owed the family money — expedition costs, outfitting, the boat. It's all written down somewhere, legal as Sunday. Mrs. Corliss will raise her proper. Schooling. Manners. A future." He spread his hands. "Hell, ranger, half the orphan girls in this territory would trade places with her."

"She's not an orphan."

"Not yet," Sallow said, and then seemed to hear himself, and his jaw worked once. "Her father's dead is what I mean. Missing, presumed. The point is—"

"The point is you just told me you'd take a living woman off to be raised by the woman who likely had her father killed, and you called it a future." Silas let the silence sit. Down the canyon, wind moved through the junipers with a sound like low talking. "What's the money?"

Sallow blinked. "Sir?"

"You heard me. What's the number? Because a man doesn't ride into a box canyon under a white rag for wages. Wages keep a man fed. This is something else. So name it."

For a long moment Silas thought Sallow would ride off. Then the foreman huffed, almost sheepish, and named it. "Five thousand. For you. Cash. Plus a quarter section of river land, deeded, yours outright — water rights included, which in this country means more than the land." He held up the paper again. "That's not Corliss scrip. That's a bank draft out of Flagstaff, drawn on a house that isn't hers. She wanted it deniable even from herself."

Five thousand dollars. Silas had made forty-one dollars a month for twelve years. Five thousand was a ranch. Five thousand was his father's ranch back, with the fence fixed and the well deepened and twenty years of grief paid off in paper.

He understood, suddenly and completely, why men took this offer. That was the obscenity of it — not the threat, but the kindness of the number. Ada Corliss hadn't picked a figure to insult him. She'd picked a figure to *save* him, or enough of him that the saving felt like his own idea.

"That's a lot of river," Silas said quietly.

"It's a life, is what it is." Sallow leaned forward on his horn, voice dropping, and here at last was something unscripted, something the matriarch hadn't put in his mouth. "Vasquez, I've done this work twenty years. I've buried friends and I've buried strangers and I've noticed the ground doesn't argue about which is which. You strike me as a man who's been walking away from something for a long time. Here's a chance to walk toward something instead. Land. Water. Your own name on a deed instead of a payroll." He shrugged, big shoulders rolling. "Men don't get offered that twice."

* * *

Silas didn't answer right away. He walked a slow half-circle around Sallow's horse, looking at the canyon walls, the heat shimmer, the line of riders at the mouth sitting statue-still in the glare. Letting the offer fill the space the way smoke fills a room — everywhere, inescapable, choking.

Then he said, "Take off your glove."

"What?"

"Left glove. Take it off and hand it down."

Sallow's eyes narrowed, but curiosity is a leash on even careful men. He stripped the glove and passed it over, then watched, puzzled, as Silas dug into his own coat and brought out a small grimy book — a paybook, canvas-covered, army surplus, the kind issued to men who were never meant to be soldiers but needed to look organized.

"This came off a man named Whittaker," Silas said. "Came off him two nights ago, along with three bullets' worth of argument. One of yours?"

Sallow's face went carefully blank. "We lost contact with a couple of the new hires."

"New hires." Silas opened the book. "No name on the cover. No name inside. Look." He turned pages toward the foreman, holding them up one at a time. "Dates. Amounts. Initials only — and the initials change. Same hand writing them, different letters every month. 'J.W.' in March. 'T.M.' in April. 'R.C.' in May. Like a man signing for different versions of himself." He closed the book halfway. "Cash payments. No bank. No receipts. No employer listed anywhere in the book, because there is no employer, legally speaking. There's just money appearing in a dead man's pocket, and a widow in Prescott who'll never collect a pension because officially, her husband never worked for anybody."

The wind gusted. Somewhere above, a raven said one flat word and flapped off.

"You know what that book is, Sallow?" Silas held it out, and after a moment Sallow took it, heavy-handed, like it might go off. "It's the same ledger. Different century. Your boss's grandfather wrote numbers in a leather book and ruined families with them, and now she writes numbers in a canvas book and ruins families with those. Only difference is the paper. And here's the part I want you to carry back up the hill and turn over tonight when you can't sleep." He tapped the cover in Sallow's fist. "That book proves she pays cash so there's no trail. Which means when this goes wrong — and it's already going wrong, you've got one man dead in a draw and a wounded one bleeding in my camp — there's no trail protecting *you* either. You're not her employee. Employees have names. You're an expense."

Sallow looked at the paybook for a long time. His thumb moved across the initials without his seeming to know it was doing it.

"Whittaker had a kid," he said finally. It came out rough. "Girl. He showed everybody pictures at the staging camp. Wouldn't shut up about her." He looked up, and the purchased calm had cracked clean through, and underneath it Silas saw something older and tireder. "You saying she won't pay out to his family?"

"I'm saying there's no *her* to pay out. There's no contract, no company, no name on anything. If you bring this book to a lawyer, whose signature do you sue?" Silas shook his head. "You've been riding for a ghost with a checkbook, Duke. Ghosts don't honor drafts."

Sallow handed the paybook back. Slowly. Like returning something borrowed from a grave.

"And if I ride back and tell her no?"

"Then you'd better ride further than her property line," Silas said. "But that's not what I think you'll do."

"No?"

"No. I think you'll ride back and say the ranger wouldn't deal. And then you'll sit with your coffee tonight and read those initials again, and you'll start wondering which set belongs to you." Silas stepped back, giving him the road. "And somewhere in there, you're going to ask yourself a question I can't answer for you: when this is finished — however it finishes — does Ada Corliss need a man who's seen her ledgers, or does she just need the ledger gone?"

Sallow sat very still. At the canyon mouth, the line of horses shifted, restless, their riders small and dark against the glare.

"You talk like a man who's already decided how this ends," Sallow said.

"I talk like a man who reads tracks. Yours split three days ago, Duke. One set going where she points. One set going where you're looking." Silas touched his hat brim, mirroring the courtesy. "Tell her the girl stays. Tell her the ledger's not for sale. Tell her whatever keeps you useful another week — because the day you stop being useful is the day that book gets a final entry, and it'll be your initials, spelled wrong on purpose."

Sallow gathered his reins. For a moment he just looked at Silas, and the look had less hate in it than Silas expected — more like a man studying a map of a country he'd been told didn't exist.

"That land offer stands till sundown tomorrow," he said.

"I know. That's what makes it poison. Good bait always looks fresh."

Sallow almost smiled at that. Then he wheeled the horse and rode back down the canyon floor, the white rag still tied to his barrel, snapping behind him like something trying to get loose.

* * *

They watched him from the rim until he reached his men, until the whole line wheeled and moved out southward, dust hanging in the still air long after the riders were gone.

Mara came and stood beside Silas at the edge of the rocks. Below, Ferro's fever had broken in the night; Ruth Ann reported it with the guarded optimism of a woman who'd been fooled by fevers before. Tomás was cleaning the pistol he wasn't supposed to have, and Peter Liu was trying to write in a journal with hands that wouldn't steady, and Ines was cataloguing plants that grew in the canyon's permanent shadow, because cataloguing was how Ines prayed.

"You could have taken it," Mara said. "The land. Five thousand dollars." She wasn't accusing. She was testing the weight of it, the way you test ice. "Nobody would have known what it cost."

"Everybody would have known. That's the design." Silas squatted, picked up a chip of red rock, turned it in his fingers. "She doesn't buy silence with threats. Threats make martyrs. She buys it with ranches. Makes the traitor comfortable, so the story dies warm in a nice bed instead of screaming in a street." He tossed the rock away. "My father took a smaller version of that offer once. Signed a piece of paper, kept a piece of land, spent the rest of his life not looking at the river."

Mara was quiet a while. "And you? Did you want it? Even for a second?"

Silas thought about lying and found he didn't have the energy, or maybe the desire.

"There was a second," he said. "About four seconds long. A quarter section of running water, and my name on it, and no more miles." He stood, brushing grit from his knees. "Then I remembered that everything she offers is made out of the same material as everything she takes. Paper. And paper burns."

Down in camp, Tomás looked up from his pistol and called something to Ruth Ann, and she laughed — short, surprised, real — and the sound carried up the rocks thin and bright, six people making a life in a place designed to end them.

"They'll come back stronger," Mara said. "Not tomorrow. But soon. With more men."

"Yes."

"Then we can't stay here."

"No," Silas agreed. His eyes went north, past the canyon rim, to the blue haze of the high country and beyond it the river's narrows, where the water ran fast and loud and forgiving of nothing. "We can't stay anywhere. Which means it's time to stop being chased and start being moving."

He turned back toward camp, and behind him the dust of Sallow's departure was still settling over the canyon mouth like a question nobody had answered yet.
