## 15. The Narrows

The river told Silas what it intended before it ever showed him. He'd learned that as a boy, standing hip-deep in the shallows below his father's failing ranch, reading the water the way other boys read comic books. A river that ran quiet was lying to you. A river that talked all night — popping gravel, sucking at cutbanks, muttering over its rocks like an old man arguing with himself — that was a river being honest. And honest rivers were dangerous.

Tonight the river was talking plenty.

He crouched at the lip of the box canyon's downstream mouth and listened to it come up out of the dark: a low continuous roar from the narrows two miles below, where the whole watercourse of the valley pinched itself between walls of red sandstone no wider than a wagon road. In daylight it would be whitewater, churning and loud. At night it would be black thunder, blind and indifferent, which was exactly what he wanted. Nobody could track them through that noise. Nobody could hear a raft coming or going. The narrows didn't care who you were running from, only whether your nerve held longer than the current's patience.

"You're sure about this." Cass Duval stood beside him, arms crossed against the cold coming off the water. She'd been a wrangler most of her life and she read country like he did, but she read it with suspicion, like a horse that had been sold too many times.

"No," Silas said. "But I'm sure about the alternative."

Behind them, up the canyon, Sallow's men had found their sign by mid-afternoon — he'd watched through his field glasses as three riders worked the rimrock, quartering the ground the way good trackers do, patient as buzzards. They hadn't pressed the attack since First Blood. That was what worried him. Sallow wasn't a man who spent a day doing nothing. He was herding. Pushing them toward ground of his choosing, and every hour they stayed in this canyon the ground got more his.

"So we go through the throat of the river in the dark," Cass said flatly.

"We go through the throat of the river in the dark."

"And if we wrap a boat on rock?"

"Then we swim, and we lose everything except each other." He looked at her. "Which is more than Sallow means to leave us anyway."

Cass chewed on that for a while. Down the slope, the others were breaking camp by feel and memory, no fires, no lanterns, packing in the dark because light was a thing that could be seen for miles across open water. Ruth Ann moved among them checking straps and buckles, murmuring her medic's inventory under her breath. Tomás Reyes sat at the edge of the fire ring — cold now, stones still holding a ghost of warmth — turning something small over in his hands. The ledger. Even in the dark Silas could see the shape of it against his palms, held the way a man holds a sleeping child he's afraid will wake crying.

Mara came down last, moving quiet as spilled flour. Two weeks ago she'd been a scientist's daughter who'd never poled anything rougher than a lake canoe. Now she carried a pack frame cinched down tight and a bow slung across her back, and she scanned the canyon rim out of habit before she let herself look at Silas.

"They're on the rim," she said. "I heard a horse."

"I know."

"That's why tonight."

"That's why tonight."

She nodded once and went to help Ferro.

Ferro was the problem, and everyone knew it without saying it. The boatman had taken a bullet through the meat of his shoulder in the first firefight, and Ruth Ann had dug it clean and sewn it shut, but a wound like that wanted stillness and warmth and time, and none of those things were on offer. He'd gone gray around the mouth through the afternoon and kept insisting he could row, and everyone kept agreeing with him in the tone people use when they've already decided otherwise.

Silas gathered them at the water's edge where three rafts waited — the expedition's surviving boats, patched and re-rigged, cargo lashed down under tarps. He kept his voice low and even, the voice he used for spooked stock.

"One run, one chance, so listen close. We stay bunched until the last bend above the drop-in. After that the current takes over and there's no talking, no lights, nothing but hands and oars. Cass has the sweep oar on my boat. Mara, you're with me. Tomás takes the second boat with Peter and Ines. Ruth Ann rides with Ferro in the gear boat — you keep him flat and keep pressure on that shoulder if it opens. It'll be cold enough to slow bleeding some. That's not comfort, that's just physics."

"What about the supplies?" Peter asked. His voice cracked on the word *supplies*, and Silas understood why. Everything they had left in the world was lashed to those rafts: food, the medical kit, the spare ammunition, the ledger's oilskin wrapping, the photographs Peter had confessed to hiding in his shirt pocket like contraband scripture.

"The supplies are the price of admission," Silas said. "If the river wants a toll, we pay it. Boats can be replaced. People can't."

Nobody argued. That was the thing he was starting to notice about this group, the thing that both moved him and frightened him a little: they'd stopped arguing with him. Somewhere between the ambush and the fever hours and the long hungry days since, they'd begun to move like a crew instead of a collection of survivors, and crews trust their pilot past the point of sense.

They pushed off into the current.

* * *

The first mile was almost gentle, and that made it worse.

The river carried them along at walking speed, black water sliding under black sky, the canyon walls rising on either side as faint darker shapes against the stars. Silas knelt at the bow of the lead raft with a paddle, reading the water by sound — the pitch of the current changing around submerged rock, the hollow note of an eddy behind a boulder, the sudden hush where the channel deepened. Behind him Cass sat at the sweep oar, big hands wrapped in the leather, making the small constant corrections that kept them true. She steered like she'd broken horses: firm, unhurried, forgiving the river its moods without ever surrendering to them.

Mara knelt amidships, facing backward, watching the second and third boats hold formation behind them. Every few minutes she'd raise one hand — *all well* — and someone in the dark behind would answer with a knuckle rap on wood.

Then the walls began to close.

It came gradual, the way a fist closes. The star-field overhead narrowed from a road to a ribbon to a thread. The roar ahead swelled from mutter to argument to shout. The current picked up its pace beneath them, and Silas felt the rafts start to move like leaves instead of boats, spinning slightly, needing correction every few seconds instead of every few minutes.

"Cass," he said quietly. "Feel that?"

"Feel it." Her voice was calm, but he heard her shift her grip. "Water's pulling left-center. There's a tongue going through — hear it? Smooth part, middle of the noise."

He listened. Under the chaos of the roar there was indeed a smoother note, a sustained hiss where the main channel threaded the rocks. "Take us to it."

Cass leaned on the sweep and the raft swung obediently, and then the night simply ended.

There was no gradual entry into the narrows. The river swallowed them. Walls rose sheer on either side, close enough that Silas could have touched both with his paddle, and the water went from fast to furious in the space of ten heartbeats. Spray came off the waves in cold sheets. The roar filled the world so completely that thought became impossible; there was nothing left in Silas's head but the next rock, the next wave, the next command shouted not in words but in paddle strokes.

A hole opened dead ahead — a breaking wave standing in place over a submerged ledge, folding back on itself like a door opening and closing. "Right of the hole!" he bellowed, knowing the word was lost before it left his mouth, and Cass was already leaning, already swinging the bow left of where he'd pointed because she'd seen it too, because she'd been reading the same water he had, and they went through the gap side by side without a single word passing between them.

That was the moment, though he wouldn't understand it until later. Not the running of the rapid itself, but the silence inside it — the way six strangers' worth of fear had compressed itself into something else. Trust, maybe. Or just the animal competence of people who had decided, together, not to die tonight.

Behind them, a cry went up from the third boat.

* * *

Ruth Ann heard the sound before she understood it — a wet tearing, felt rather than heard, transmitted through the hull of the gear boat as Ferro's body jerked against the lashings holding him semi-upright in the stern.

"Ferro!"

"My shoulder—" His voice came out strangled. "It's — something gave—"

She got her headlamp on, cupped her hand around the beam to kill the spill of light, and looked. The bandage was soaked through, black in the red glow, and fresh blood welled between her fingers when she pressed. The jolt of the first big wave had torn two of her stitches loose, and the wound had opened like a seam.

Pressure. Keep him flat. Keep pressure. Her own instructions, spoken hours ago in daylight, came back to her now in the dark with the river trying to shake the boat apart beneath her. She wedged her knee against his chest, clamped her palm hard over the wound, and screamed at the night: "TOMÁS! BOAT BEHIND! FERRO'S OPEN!"

Whether anyone heard her she never knew. The river ate her voice whole.

What happened next happened without discussion, without plan, without any single person deciding it. Tomás, in the second boat, saw the headlamp's cupped glow stuttering wild in the stern of the gear boat and understood. He roared something to Peter and Ines — commands lost in the roar — and the second raft swung hard across the current toward the third, the two boats colliding gently, lines thrown and caught, the gear boat lashed alongside so that Tomás could reach across and add his hands to Ruth Ann's, holding Ferro flat while the boats rode the rapids tied together like a wounded animal and its keeper.

And Cass — Cass, who couldn't see any of it, who had only the sound of collision and shouting behind her — held the lead raft steady in the tongue of the current, slowing them by dragging the sweep oar, sacrificing speed to keep the convoy together, because a crew doesn't leave its wounded to the river.

Silas turned once, saw the two boats locked together riding a standing wave sideways, and felt his heart stop in his chest. Then the wave passed under them, the lashings held, and the little flotilla righted itself and plunged on into the dark.

* * *

They came out of the narrows twenty minutes after they'd entered, though it felt like a season.

The walls fell away. The roar diminished to its ordinary midnight voice. Stars returned overhead, wide and indifferent, and the current gentled until the rafts were merely floating again, drifting down a broad moonless stretch of water with the wreckage of their nerves scattered across every deck.

Silas counted heads twice by touch and voice. All present. Then he put across to the gear boat, and Ruth Ann lifted her headlamp to show him what she had.

Ferro lay flat, half-conscious, white as the foam on the rocks. But the bleeding had stopped. Ruth Ann's hands were black to the wrist, and she was tying off the last of a fresh bandage with her teeth and one hand, and when she looked up at Silas her face was streaked with spray and blood and something that might have been laughter.

"Held him together," she said. "Barely. He needs a real bed and a real doctor inside of a week, ranger, or I'm out of promises."

"He'll have them."

"Don't make vows on a river."

"I don't make vows anywhere else."

Tomás sat back on his heels, flexing fingers stiff from gripping. He looked at Silas across Ferro's body, and in the dim red glow of the cupped lamp something passed between them — not forgiveness yet, not quite, but the recognition of men who had held the same rope in the same storm.

"Lost the supply raft," Cass reported, poling over from the lead boat. She nodded downstream to where, snagged against a mid-channel boulder, hung the tattered remains of the second cargo boat, wrapped and folded around the rock like wet paper. Half their food, the spare ammunition, the tent canvas — all of it pinned in the teeth of the narrows, unrecoverable in the dark, probably unrecoverable at all.

Silas looked at it for a long moment. Then he looked at his people — soaked, shaking, bloodied, alive — and found, to his own surprise, that he was smiling.

"Sallow's trackers will find that wreck tomorrow," he said. "They'll spend half a day picking through it, certain we lost our nerve and our goods and maybe our lives. They'll report a disaster." He tapped the side of the raft beneath him. "Meanwhile we're a day ahead, running light, and nobody back there knows it. The river took a toll. It also bought us a day."

"A day," Mara said softly, beside him. She was looking at him with an expression he couldn't name in the dark — something between exhaustion and awe. "You planned for us to lose it."

"I planned for us to survive losing it. There's a difference."

"There is," she said. "My father used to say something like that. About fieldwork." Her voice caught on the last word, and she looked away downriver, and Silas let her look, because grief needed somewhere to go and the dark was as good a place as any.

They drifted on. Cass took the sweep again. Ruth Ann curled herself around Ferro like a wall against the cold. Tomás and Peter and Ines shipped their oars and let the current do the work, and somewhere in the second mile of quiet water, without anyone deciding it, the boats fell into a rhythm — corrections passed hand to hand, eddies taken in turn, the little fleet moving as one body down the throat of the night.

Silas knelt at the bow and read the water, and for the first time in twelve years of miles, he was not alone in the boat.

* * *

Dawn found them eight miles downriver, holed up in a willow thicket on the outside of a long bend, boats pulled out and camouflaged under cut brush. Ferro slept — actually slept, color creeping back into his face. Ruth Ann dozed sitting up with her hand still resting on his ankle, keeping watch even in sleep. The others ate cold rations in silence, rationing not just food but words, saving their strength for whatever came next.

Silas climbed the bank alone and glassed the backtrail. Empty. For now.

He was turning to climb down when Mara appeared beside him, silent as ever, a pair of binoculars in her hand.

"Movement," she said, and handed them over.

Far upstream, tiny with distance, riders had reached the narrows. Even at this range Silas could read what they were doing: dismounting, spreading out along the rocks, picking through the wreck of the supply raft exactly as he'd predicted. One of them raised something to his mouth — a signal mirror flashing a message south.

Sallow would know by noon that his quarry had run the throat of the river and lived. And Sallow was not a man who forgave himself for being outmaneuvered.

"They gained on us anyway," Mara said quietly.

"They gained on a wreck," Silas said. "We're not there anymore." He lowered the glasses. "But you're right. We burned our trick. Next time we won't buy a day with a boat. Next time we'll have to buy it with something we can't replace."

Mara looked at him. "Like what?"

Silas handed the binoculars back and looked east, toward Corlissville, toward supplies, toward the company town where a shopkeeper named Elena Brandt might or might not remember a boy whose father had drowned in the river that built her town.

"Like a favor," he said, "from somebody who owes my family nothing — and owes Ada Corliss less."
