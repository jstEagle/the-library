## 16. Peter's Secret

Peter Liu had been rehearsing the sentence for three days.

He'd tried it a dozen ways in his head while hauling water, while mending the litter straps for Ferro, while lying awake in the dark of the box canyon listening to Cass Duval breathe through the fever that was starting to sound like wind through a cracked window. *I have something to tell you.* Four words. He'd said them to himself until they wore smooth as river stone, and every time he got as far as opening his mouth, the words turned to sand and blew away.

It wasn't fear of what Silas Vasquez would do. The ranger had proven over two weeks of hard country and one firefight that he didn't scare easy and didn't punish quick. It wasn't even Mara — though Mara Hollis looked at him sometimes with her father's eyes, and those eyes were the hardest thing in the canyon to face.

It was simpler than that, and worse. Peter had spent his whole life being the reliable one. The grad student who triple-checked the sample labels. The one who remembered the spare batteries, the backup maps, the extra water purification tablets. On the expedition, his job had been to be careful, and he'd been good at it, and being good at it was the only solid thing about him in a world where everything else — his funding, his advisor's approval, his place in the department — hung on a thread. Careful Peter. Dependable Peter. And dependable people don't do the thing he'd done.

The thing he'd done sat in his chest like a swallowed coal.

* * *

It started, as most of his mistakes did, with enthusiasm.

The night Tomás Reyes came back from the cave with the ledger wrapped in an oilcloth, the camp had gone strange and quiet. Peter remembered it now the way you remember a photograph: frozen, over-lit, everyone standing too still around the cook fire. Dr. Hollis turning the leather book over in his hands like it might bite him. Ines reading by firelight, lips moving, going pale page by page. Mara asleep in her tent, which was why she'd never known how the camp changed that night — why she'd woken to a father who was already dead inside, even before he was dead.

Peter had been the one Hollis called over.

"Liu. You're our document man." Hollis had held up the ledger, and the firelight had caught the embossing on the cover — some land office stamp from the 1890s, half worn away. "I want this recorded before anything happens to it."

"Recorded how? Scanning equipment's back at the university—"

"Hell with the university." Something in Hollis's voice then, something Peter hadn't heard from him in four years of fieldwork. Not excitement. Urgency. The urgency of a man who has just learned the ground under his feet is not ground at all. "Camera. Film. Whatever you've got. Every page, both sides, plus the covers, plus any insertions. I want a copy that can't be burned, can't be stolen, can't be drowned. You understand me?"

Peter understood. He'd understood so well that he'd worked past midnight, rigging the expedition's survey camera on its copy stand, shooting page after page under the lantern, checking exposure brackets, logging frame numbers in his neat hand in the site notebook. Forty-one pages. Sixty-three frames including retakes. He'd been proud of it. That was the part that shamed him most, later — not the mailing, but the pride. He'd felt like a real scholar for the first time in his life, holding history in a light meter instead of a footnote.

And then, because he was careful Peter, because he always followed protocol, because his advisor had drilled into him that primary-source material never stays in the field where a flash flood or a careless cook can take it out — he'd done the responsible thing.

There was a mail drop at the trading post forty miles downriver. The expedition's supply runner went twice a month. Two days after the photography session, Peter had sealed the film roll in a lead-lined pouch — radiation shielding habit, he almost laughed about it now — addressed it to Professor Gerald Whitmore, Department of History, and handed it to the runner with the rest of the outgoing mail. Field reports. A postcard to his mother. One roll of 120 film containing the only unburnable copy of a document worth, apparently, killing for.

He'd told himself it was procedure. He'd told himself Whitmore was discreet. He'd told himself a lot of things in the week between the mailing and the night the camp burned.

* * *

Now it was late afternoon, and the canyon light had gone amber, and Silas Vasquez was cleaning his rifle by the fire the way he did everything — without hurry, without waste — and Peter decided that if he didn't say it in the next thirty seconds he never would.

"I need to tell you something."

Silas didn't look up. "Go ahead."

"It's about how Sallow found us. At the canyon. How fast he found us." Peter's hands were doing something useless with a strap of the litter harness, braiding and unbraiding it. "We moved camps twice after the burn. We left no sign — Tomás made sure of that, he's better at it than anyone. There's no way they tracked us that fast. Not through that country. Not in that weather."

"No," Silas said quietly. "There isn't."

"They knew where we were." The words came out faster now, the dam cracking. "They knew exactly. And I know why, and I've known since the second night, and I couldn't — I kept telling myself maybe I was wrong, maybe it was a coincidence, maybe—" He stopped. Made himself stop. Made himself look at the ranger across the fire. "I mailed the film, Silas. Before the camp burned. I photographed the whole ledger like Dr. Hollis asked, and then I mailed the film to my advisor at the university. Professor Whitmore. Standard archival protocol, that's what I told myself, that's what I *told myself*, and either Corliss has someone inside the department, or Whitmore talked, or Whitmore was bought years ago and I handed the thing straight into her hands with a return address on it."

The fire popped. Somewhere up-canyon, Ruth Ann's voice murmured low over Cass, the rhythm of care, steady as water.

Silas set down the rifle. He did it slowly, the way he did everything, and when he looked at Peter there was no anger in his face. There was something worse. There was understanding.

"The mail drop at Hollister's Trading Post," Silas said. "Runner comes on the fifteenth and the thirtieth."

"You know it."

"Everybody knows it. Including Duke Sallow, who grew up twenty miles from it." Silas fed a stick to the fire. "When did it go out?"

"Two days after we shot the pages. Five days before Dr. Hollis died."

Silas nodded once, slow, and Peter watched him do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic Peter had done a hundred times, arriving at the same terrible sum. The timeline fit perfectly. Too perfectly. Hollis announces he's taking the ledger public; somewhere east, a telephone rings in a department office or a Corliss office; within days, Ward Pellis appears at the edge of camp wearing his brother-in-law's smile; within a week, Hollis is dead in the shallows and the radio is smashed and six people are running for their lives.

"I killed him," Peter said. His voice came out small, younger than he'd sounded since he was a boy. "Maybe not with my hands. But I mailed the map to the wolves. He told me to make a copy that couldn't be stolen and I — I stole it for them. I did their work for them. They didn't even have to search the camp. I packaged it up and put postage on it."

"Stop." Silas said it without sharpness, which somehow made it land harder. "You're going to sit there and tell me the story straight, all of it, and then you're going to hear me out. Understood?"

Peter nodded. His eyes were burning and he hated that, hated crying in front of this man who never seemed to need anything, but the tears came anyway, hot and fast, three weeks overdue.

"Whitmore," Silas said. "Tell me about him."

"He's — he was my whole career. My advisor, my reference, the chair of my committee. He got me onto the expedition. He wrote the letters. Everyone loves Gerry Whitmore, he's this avuncular old man, pipes and tweed, remembers your birthday. He taught me that archives are sacred. He used to say, *a document outlives its keepers, so be worthy of it.*" Peter laughed, and it came out broken. "I thought I was being worthy of it. Sending it somewhere safe. Somewhere permanent."

"And Corliss money in the university?"

Peter wiped his face with his sleeve. "The Corliss Chair of Regional History," he said. "Endowed 1931. Guess whose name is on it."

Silas closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them, there was something moving behind them, some cold bright calculation, and Peter realized with a lurch that the ranger wasn't thinking about blame at all. He was thinking about use.

"All right," Silas said. "Here's what you're not seeing, because you've spent three weeks staring at your own navel instead of the trail. Tell me if I've got this right. The film went to Whitmore. Whitmore, or whoever he answers to, got word to Corliss. Corliss sent Pellis. Pellis killed Hollis and burned the camp — but the ledger itself survived, because Tomás pulled it out of the fire and carried it. Yes?"

"Yes."

"So the photographs didn't save Corliss anything. She already had the original in reach and failed to get it. What the photographs did was tell her the ledger existed, and tell her where we were, and start the clock." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight carving his face into planes and shadow. "Which means the film is still sitting in a drawer in a history department two thousand miles away. Which means there are negatives, Peter. Physical negatives. Maybe prints by now, maybe not. Either way, there is a second copy of the proof that Ada Corliss's grandfather stole the water out from under three towns' grandparents — and it's in a place she doesn't control yet, guarded by a man whose salary she signs."

Peter stared at him. "You're saying my mistake might—"

"I'm saying nothing's wasted yet. Not even shame." Silas picked the rifle back up, ran the cloth along the barrel. "But here's the part you're not going to like. If Whitmore knows what he's holding, Corliss will move on that drawer eventually. Quietly. A filing error. A fire in the archive — universities burn documents all the time, happens every year, nobody asks questions. So that copy has a shelf life. And we can't get to it from here."

"We could wire ahead. Telegraph office in Corlissville—"

"Corlissville telegraph reads for Corliss. Anything we send goes through her hands first." Silas's jaw tightened. "No. If that film gets protected, it gets protected by someone walking into that department and taking it out loud, in daylight, with papers. That's a job for later. First job is getting us and this ledger to Judge Pell alive."

* * *

Mara found them there.

Peter saw her coming down from the watch rocks, and his stomach dropped, because of course — of course she'd come now, of course the universe would schedule it this way, the daughter arriving mid-confession. He didn't know how much she'd heard. Her face gave nothing away; grief had taught her that kind of architecture.

"What's wrong?" she said. "Both of you look like somebody died."

"Peter's got something to tell you," Silas said, "and I'm going to let him tell it, because it's his to tell. I'll be at the north watch." He rose, gathered his rifle, and paused beside Peter long enough to put one hand briefly on his shoulder — a weight, not a squeeze, there and gone — and then he walked up-canyon and left them alone with the fire.

Mara sat down on the rock Silas had vacated. She waited. She was good at waiting now; the trail had taught her that too.

So Peter told it again, all of it, beginning to end. The midnight photography session. Hollis's urgency. The lead-lined pouch. The mail runner on the fifteenth. The Corliss Chair. The timeline that fit like a bullet fits a wound. He told it worse the second time, because telling it to Mara Hollis was different from telling it to Silas Vasquez — Silas had lost a father to the Corliss machine slowly, over years, in ways nobody could point to. Mara had lost hers in a river shallows with a specific date on it, and Peter had just handed her a new candidate for the reason why.

When he finished, she was quiet for a long time. The fire settled. Up-canyon, Ferro coughed in his sleep.

"My dad trusted you," she said finally. "With the camera, with the record. Out of everybody in that camp, he picked you."

"I know."

"That wasn't an accident. He watched people. He used to say you could tell more about a person from how they handled equipment than from anything they ever said." She looked at him, and her eyes were dry and very tired. "He picked you because you were careful. Because you followed protocol. Because you were the kind of person who mails things to where they'll be safe." A breath. "He wouldn't blame you, Peter. He'd be furious at the world for making safety into a trap. But he wouldn't blame you."

"You don't know that."

"I know it better than anybody breathing." Her voice didn't rise; it went flatter, which was worse. "I spent three weeks blaming myself. For sleeping through the night he died. For not hearing the radio get smashed. For doubting Tomás. For every single thing I did and didn't do. And you know what I figured out?" She leaned forward. "Guilt feels like work. It feels like doing something. It isn't. It's just another way of hiding. Sallow's men are real. The ledger is real. Judge Pell is real. Those are the things we can act on. Everything else is just us, kneeling in the ashes, polishing our own chains."

Peter laughed — a wet, surprised sound. "Did Silas teach you that?"

"Silas doesn't talk enough to teach anything. I figured it out at three in the morning watching Ruth Ann fight an infection with willow bark." Mara reached over and took the strap out of his hands — the one he'd been braiding and unbraiding for an hour — and set it aside. "You photographed every page. Both sides. Covers. Insertions."

"Sixty-three frames."

"Then my father's last order was carried out. Completely. By the person he chose to carry it out." She stood, brushing off her jeans. "The delivery method was bad. The work was good. We keep the work, we fix the method. That's how everything works now — ask Cass, ask Ferro, ask any of us."

She started toward the fire ring where Ruth Ann was boiling water, then stopped and turned back.

"For what it's worth — I'm glad you told us. It would've been easier to keep carrying it. Easier and heavier every day." A ghost of something crossed her face that wasn't quite a smile. "That's the trap of secrets. They get lighter the moment you put them down, and you only find that out after."

* * *

That night Peter slept, actually slept, for the first time since the burn.

He woke near dawn to voices at the canyon mouth — low, urgent, Silas's cadence underneath someone else's. He crawled out of his bedroll into gray light and cold air and found the whole camp already awake, gathered tight, and Silas standing over something laid on a flat rock.

A dead quail. Or what was left of one — shot through, its feathers stiff with frost, and tied to one leg, a strip of cloth torn from a red bandana.

Sallow's colors. A message without words: *we see the canyon mouth.*

Silas crouched, examined the bird without touching it, and looked up at the rim of the canyon where the first light was just touching stone.

"He's not herding us anymore," Silas said quietly. "He's counting us."
