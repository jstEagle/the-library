## 17. The Town That Water Built

They left the horses in a stand of cottonwoods two miles out and walked the last stretch in the blue hour before dawn, when the road into Corlissville belonged to nobody but the milk trucks and the ghosts.

Silas had been to company towns before. He'd thought he knew what to expect: fences, foremen, the particular slump of men who worked land they'd never own. But Corlissville was something else. It wasn't a town that happened to have a company in it. It was a company that happened to have a town around it, the way a pearl forms around a wound.

The main street ran straight as a survey line from the river to the smelter hill, and every building on it faced the same direction — toward the Corliss house on its rise at the north end, white-pillared even in the dark, like a headstone waiting for its name.

"Charming," Mara said under her breath.

"Keep walking. Don't look at anybody longer than you have to."

"I grew up in university towns, Silas. I know how to be invisible."

"You've never been invisible in your life," he said, and she didn't argue, because it was true.

He'd dressed her down for it. Her expedition clothes were gone — burned with the camp or lost in the narrows — so she wore a canvas coat of Cass's and a man's hat pulled low, and still she moved through the world like someone who expected to be listened to. In a town like this, that could get you noticed. In a town like this, getting noticed could get you buried.

* * *

The store opened at six, and they were first through the door.

Elena Brandt's establishment sat mid-street, wedged between the scrip office and a barbershop whose pole hadn't turned in years. It was smaller than Silas remembered from a supply run three seasons back, shelves crowded close, everything smelling of coffee, lamp oil, and the particular dust of goods that passed through many hands without ever being owned by any of them.

The woman behind the counter was small and iron-backed, seventy if she was a day, with a face like a dry creek bed — cracked, but you could see where the water used to run. She looked up from her ledger, took them in with one pass of her eyes, and said nothing.

"Flour, salt, coffee," Silas said. "Lamp oil. Aspirin if you have it. And whatever's fresh."

"Nothing's fresh." Her voice was flat as the counter. "Company truck comes Thursdays. Today's Tuesday." She began pulling items anyway, moving with the economy of a woman who'd made the same motions forty years. "Cash?"

"Cash."

Something flickered behind her eyes at that. In Corlissville, cash was a foreign language.

Mara drifted along the shelves, playing browser, actually cataloguing. Silas watched her clock the back room door, the stairwell to the apartment above, the single window facing the alley. Good. She was learning country the way he read it — not terrain anymore, but architecture, sight lines, exits.

Then he saw what Mara saw next, and his stomach dropped.

On the wall behind the register, framed under glass gone amber with age, hung a photograph. A row of men in front of a ditch camp, shovels on shoulders, squinting into a sun thirty years dead. And second from the left, hat brim shadowing half his face but not enough, stood a young man with Vasquez's shoulders and Vasquez's jaw.

Silas's father.

His body went very still, the way it did when he was tracking something dangerous. Elena Brandt saw him see it. She set down the flour sack slowly, deliberately, and looked at him the way you look at a lock you're deciding whether to pick.

"Vasquez," she said. Not a question.

"That right?"

"Emiliano's boy." She said it like she was setting a stone on a grave. "You have his way of standing. Like the floor might charge you rent."

Silas didn't answer. There was nothing to answer. Twelve years of miles, and the miles had never once carried him far enough.

* * *

She counted out his change in coins, and then she didn't move away from the register. The store was empty except for them. Through the window, the street was filling — men walking to the smelter shift, women to the cannery, all of them passing the pay office where a clerk in a Corliss vest would trade their week's labor for paper scrip good only at the stores Corliss owned, priced at figures Corliss set.

"They can't leave, you know," Elena said, following his gaze. "The scrip. Man works ten years, he's got maybe forty dollars real money and a debt at the company store. His kids are born owing." She wiped the counter with a rag that was cleaner than it needed to be — a working motion, a thinking motion. "My father helped dig the main canal in '98. Corliss paid him in land he already owned."

Mara came back down the aisle, slow. "Ma'am," she said carefully, "we're just passing through—"

"I know who you are too." Elena cut her off without heat. "Girl from the Hollis expedition. Radio's been full of it for two weeks. Lost scientists, drowned guide, tragic accident." She snorted, and it was the most alive sound she'd made yet. "I'm old, not stupid. Ada Corliss doesn't fund expeditions out of love of science. Ada Corliss funds things the way a spider funds a web."

Silas turned fully to face her. "You knew my father."

"I knew your father." Something moved in the creek-bed face — grief, or its fossil. "He worked this valley before you were born. Good ranch, south bench, decent water rights his grandfather filed honest. Then old Marcus Corliss decided the river needed 'rational management,' and there was a hearing in a courthouse two hundred miles from here that nobody from this valley could afford to attend, and one morning Emiliano Vasquez's water ran through somebody else's ditch." She paused. "He came in here after. Bought a bottle of rye with his last cash money. I told him the territory was big. Plenty of other valleys."

"What'd he say?"

"He said, *'Elena, a man can run from a thief. Where does he run from a law that signs the thief's papers?'"* She folded the rag over once, twice. "Two years later he drank himself into the river. Half this town watched the search parties go out. Nobody was surprised."

The words landed the way he'd known they would — no new information, only confirmation, which is worse. You can fight a lie. Confirmation just sits on your chest.

Mara's hand found his forearm. Light. Brief. Gone. But he felt it the rest of the day.

* * *

They walked the town while the sun climbed, because Silas wanted to see it whole, and because leaving immediately after Elena's words would look like what it was — flight.

Corlissville in daylight was worse than Corlissville in the dark. The church sat at the town's center, white and neat, and above its door, carved into the lintel where most churches put a verse or a cross, was a single word: **GRATITUDE**. The pastor, Silas learned from a bulletin board, drew his salary from the Corliss charitable trust. The school's textbooks came from the Corliss educational fund. The doctor's clinic was the Corliss memorial clinic, and its bills were payable in scrip.

"It's not corruption," Mara said quietly as they passed the pay office queue. Men stood in line holding paper, and their faces weren't angry. That was the thing that got her — he could see it landing on her. They weren't angry. They were *patient*. "That's what I keep getting wrong. Corruption is a leak in a system. This is the system. The theft isn't hidden. It's load-bearing."

Silas looked at her. "Say that again."

"The theft is load-bearing. Pull it out and—" She stopped. He watched her arrive at it the way Tomás must have arrived at it in a cave somewhere upstream, holding a leather book. "And the whole town comes down. That's why they'll kill for it. It's not greed anymore. It's structural."

"Ada Corliss is dying," Silas said. "Everybody upriver says so. Dying women don't usually think structurally."

"Dying women think about what they're leaving." Mara's voice hardened. "My father used to say the worst crimes wear the face of providing for family."

They bought nothing else. They spoke to no one else. But the town spoke to them anyway, in the language towns speak: a boy hauling ice who stared at Silas's ranger badge and quickly looked away; two old men on a bench who stopped talking when they passed; the pay office clerk who stepped into the doorway and watched them the entire length of the street, unhurried, memorizing.

"We're marked," Mara murmured.

"We were marked when we crossed the county line. Sallow will know we came here by nightfall." Silas kept his pace lazy, a shopper's pace. "Question is whether he learns anything worth knowing."

"And does he?"

Silas glanced back once, at the white church with GRATITUDE carved over the door, at the queue of men trading sweat for paper, at the framed photograph in the dark window of the store — his father's young face, thirty years patient under glass.

"Yeah," he said. "He learns we're not running blind."

* * *

They doubled back to the store at dusk, when the shift whistles had blown and the street emptied, because Silas had spent the whole day turning over one detail: Elena Brandt had given them their change in coins. Real silver. In a town paid in scrip, a shopkeeper sitting on silver was a shopkeeper keeping an escape hatch, and women who kept escape hatches usually wanted reasons to use them.

The CLOSED sign was up, but the door wasn't locked.

Inside, Elena sat at the counter with a cup of coffee she plainly wasn't drinking, and across from her, to Silas's surprise, sat the barber from next door — sixty, hands scarred by decades of straight razors, with the build of a man who'd once been a mule packer.

"This is Hollis's daughter," Elena said to him, without preamble, "and Emiliano Vasquez's son. They're the ones the radio's lying about."

The barber looked at them both for a long moment. Then he nodded, slow, like a gate swinging open.

"My name's Ruthford," he said. "In 1903 I was nine years old. My family had a place on the east fork. I remember the day the Corliss riders came with the transfer papers. My father couldn't write. They held the pen for him and told him where to make his mark, and they told him if he refused, the ditch above our place would be 'maintained differently.'" He turned his scarred hands over on the table. "I've been cutting hair in this town for fifty years. Every man in that chair has a story like mine. Every single one. We just never had anywhere to put it."

Elena slid a sheet of paper across the counter. Handwritten, dense, dated in three places across forty years. Names. Dates. Section numbers. Water rights filings, transfers, the courthouse records of a theft committed in triplicate.

"I witnessed the seizures," Elena said simply. "I was a child, but children see clearly. I wrote down what I saw when I was old enough to understand it, and I've kept it, and I've waited." She looked up, and the dry creek bed of her face held, for one moment, actual water. "I always figured someday somebody would come up that river carrying the other half of the proof. Your father used to say the river keeps its own books."

Silas's throat was tight. "There's a ledger," he managed. "From before the turn of the century. Transfers signed under duress. We have it."

Elena closed her eyes briefly, the way people do when a prayer is answered and they're embarrassed to admit they'd been saying it.

"Then hear me," she said, and her voice changed — flattened, hardened, the voice of a woman who had rehearsed this moment for forty years and would not waste it. "Do not take that ledger to the county seat. Judge Harlan Voss owes his chair to Corliss money — his own brother manages the scrip office. Take it to a federal man. Take it to Judge Amos Pell, out of Bitterroot, two hundred miles east. He tried a Corliss case twenty years ago and lived, which is more than can be said for the plaintiff." She tapped the paper once. "And when you go, I go with you. I'll sign anything, swear to anything, in front of God and the territorial marshal both. I've been quiet long enough to be ashamed of it."

The barber grunted agreement. "Me too. And I can name you thirty more before Sunday."

Silas looked at Mara. Mara looked at the paper on the counter — names, dates, witnesses, a map of the wound drawn by the people who'd bled — and he saw her do the arithmetic that changes everything: they had walked into the enemy's town looking for flour and lamp oil, and walked out with an army forming.

"One condition," Elena said, rising, iron-straight. "Before you take me anywhere — tell me how Emiliano's boy came to be mixed up in finishing what Emiliano started."

Silas met her eyes. Outside, far up the darkening street, a truck engine started — the wrong engine, heavy, idling too long near the pay office.

"That," he said quietly, "is going to take longer than one night. And I think we just ran out of nights."
