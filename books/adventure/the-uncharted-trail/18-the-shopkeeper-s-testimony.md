## 18. The Shopkeeper's Testimony

The store smelled of coffee, gunpowder, and forty years of dust that had never once been disturbed by a broom.

Mara stood just inside the door of Brandt's General Merchandise while Silas lingered by the window, positioned — she was learning to read this about him now — where he could see both the street and the back room without turning his head. The morning light came through the front glass in long dusty bars, catching the tins on the shelves: peaches, lard, sardines, tobacco. Everything arranged with the precision of a woman who had spent decades making small things stay exactly where she put them.

Behind the counter, Elena Brandt was measuring out flour with the unhurried economy of someone who had heard every kind of customer in every kind of mood and could not be rushed or rattled by any of them. She was seventy if she was a day, small and square-shouldered, her white hair pinned so tight it looked structural. Her forearms were those of a woman who had lifted crates since girlhood.

"You're the Hollis girl." She didn't look up from the flour scoop. "Paper ran your father's picture two weeks back. *Drowning accident, expedition leader lost to the river.*" She set the scoop down with a click. "I've lived beside that river sixty years. It doesn't drown men who grew up on it."

Mara's throat tightened. "No," she said. "It doesn't."

"And you're Vasquez's boy." Now Elena looked up, and her eyes went to Silas at the window — not to his face but to something older than his face, some architecture of jaw or stance. "You have his shoulders. He came in here the last winter before they took the ranch. Bought flour, coffee, a box of shells he couldn't afford and I sold him anyway at cost because pride is expensive and poor men pay full price for it." She wiped her hands on her apron. "He stood right where you're standing, ranger. Right there. And he said, *Elena, if it comes to it, will you remember what happened?* And I said yes. And then it came to it, and I remembered, and remembering did nobody one damn bit of good."

The words landed in Mara's chest like stones dropped down a well. She glanced at Silas. He hadn't moved, hadn't blinked, but she saw his hand flex once against his thigh, and she understood that this shop, this counter, this old woman were part of a geography of grief he'd been carrying alone for twelve years.

"We need supplies," Silas said quietly. "And we need to talk. Not here in the open."

Elena Brandt looked at them both for a long moment. Then she walked to the door, turned the little sign in the window to CLOSED, and drew the shade halfway.

"Back room," she said. "Through the cold storage. If anyone asks, I'm doing inventory and you two are arguing about the price of nails."

* * *

The back room was cool and dim, stacked with crates and hung with cured hams, and it smelled of salt and sawdust. Elena lit a single lamp, turned its wick low, and sat down on an upturned barrel as though she'd been waiting thirty years for someone to come ask her the question.

So Mara asked it.

"Do you remember the water seizures?"

The old woman laughed — a short, dry sound with no humor in it anywhere. "Child, I don't just remember them. I *am* them. My father owned forty acres and a quarter-share of the Miller Ditch before Corliss Water & Power existed. My mother buried two babies out on that land. When the company came through offering scrip instead of cash, my father said no. Said it twice. Said it loud at the county meeting with old Amos Corliss himself sitting on the platform like a judge at a hanging." Her hands folded in her lap, knuckles like river stones. "Third time they came, they didn't ask. They just turned the headgate and closed it. Forty acres in July, no water. You ever watch alfalfa die? It doesn't die pretty. It dies angry, all brown and curled up like it's cursing God. Took three weeks. Then my father signed the transfer paper with an X because he never learned his letters, and we moved into town, and he took a job hauling freight for the same company that stole from him, because a man has to eat even when eating feels like swallowing glass."

Mara found she was gripping the edge of a crate hard enough to hurt her fingers. "The ledger," she said. "My father found a ledger. From the 1890s. Transfers signed under duress. Names, dates, acreage, what was promised and what was actually paid. It's all in there. Every theft, written down in the thief's own hand."

Something moved behind Elena Brandt's eyes — something old and banked and very much alive, like coals raked over after a fire everyone assumed was dead.

"I know that ledger exists," she said. "Or I knew it should exist. Old man Corliss kept two books — everybody knew that much, though nobody said it. One book for the county assessor and one book for himself. The second one told the truth." She leaned forward on the barrel. "Your father found it. In the caves upriver."

"Yes."

"And Corliss killed him for it."

Mara held the old woman's gaze. "We believe so. We have a witness. He saw the man who did it."

Elena nodded slowly, as if confirming a diagnosis she'd made years ago. Then she stood, crossed to a shelf of canned goods, and began moving jars aside with deliberate care until she'd cleared a space. From behind the row she drew out a flat tin — a tobacco tin, rusted at the corners — and brought it back to the lamp.

Inside was a folded page of newsprint gone soft and yellow as cloth, and beneath it, a photograph.

She laid the photograph on the crate top. Mara leaned in. It showed a line of perhaps thirty people standing outside a building with CORLISS WATER & POWER painted on its false front. Men in Sunday coats. Women in aprons. Children. Everyone holding signs too faded to read.

"That's 1911," Elena said. "I'm the small one on the end, third from the left, holding my mother's hand. We marched on the company office after they cut off the Herrera family. The Herreras had six hundred acres and eleven children and they held out longer than anybody. When their turn came, the company didn't even bother with the headgate trick. They sent men with rifles to stand at the ditch until the harvest rotted in the field." Her finger touched the photo, tracing the line of faces. "Every person in this picture signed a petition. Thirty-one names demanding the territory investigate the water transfers. You know what happened to that petition?"

Mara shook her head.

"The county clerk lost it. Officially lost. Unofficially, Ada Corliss's father bought the clerk a new house the following spring, and the clerk's widow still lives in it." Elena tapped the tin. "I've kept this photograph for fifty-three years. Kept the newspaper clipping too — the one about the petition being lost. Kept my father's copy of the transfer deed with his X-mark on it. I kept all of it in case someday somebody came through that door with the other half of the truth." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely steady. "You're telling me that day is today."

"It can be," Mara said. "If you'll help us."

"What do you need?"

Silas spoke from the shadows by the cold storage door. "An affidavit. Your testimony, witnessed and signed — what you saw, what was done to your family, the petition, the clerk. Written down, dated, sworn."

"On whose authority?" Elena asked sharply. "Because I'll tell you right now, if that paper goes to the county courthouse in Corlissville, it'll be lost faster than the petition was. Half the county officials owe their seats to Corliss money and the other half owe their seats to fear of Corliss guns. Judge Harlan down at the county seat played cards with old man Corliss every Thursday for twenty years. You take testimony there, it goes into a drawer, and the drawer goes into the river."

"That's why we're asking," Mara said, "and not just doing."

For the first time, the old woman smiled. It transformed her face — suddenly Mara could see the girl in the photograph, the small one with her fist closed around her mother's hand.

"Good answer," Elena said. "Now listen. There's exactly one man in this territory who can't be bought and can't be scared, and it isn't because he's brave. It's because he's *federal*. Judge Amos Pell, United States District Court, sits two hundred miles east in Belden. Corliss money stopped at the state line when it came to him — tried twice to get him removed, failed both times because removing a federal judge takes Washington, and Washington doesn't care about our little kingdom out here." She pushed off the barrel and went to a desk in the corner, unlocking it with a key from around her neck. "Pell investigated the scrip system fifteen years ago. Wrote a report nobody published. He knows where every body is buried in this valley, metaphorically speaking. He's old, he's mean, and he answers mail."

She drew out paper, a bottle of ink, and a pen, and set them on the desk like a surgeon laying out instruments.

"I'll write my affidavit now," she said. "All of it. What I saw when I was nine years old. My father's X-mark. The petition. The clerk's house. Names, dates, everything I've carried." She uncapped the ink. "But understand something, both of you. The moment I put my name on that paper, I'm a dead woman if this fails. Ada Corliss doesn't forget, and she doesn't forgive, and she owns enough men in this town that my store would burn with me in it and the fire report would say faulty wiring."

Mara felt the weight of that settle onto the room. "Then don't—"

"Hush." Elena didn't look up from the first line she was writing. "I've been waiting to be a dead woman for something worthwhile since 1911. This is the first offer I've liked." The pen scratched. "Besides. I'm seventy-three. The Corlisses have been threatening my whole life. At my age, dying on purpose beats dying of boredom."

Silas stepped forward into the lamplight. "There's more," he said. "We're not just carrying testimony. We're carrying the ledger itself. And photographs of every page. And a witness who saw the murder. We mean to put all of it in front of Pell at once, complete, undeniable."

Elena's pen stopped.

She looked at the ledger-shaped bundle wrapped in oilcloth under Mara's arm, and Mara watched sixty years of careful, rationed hope break its dam all at once — watched the old woman's chin tremble and her eyes flood and her spine, incredibly, straighten further.

"Well," Elena Brandt said, voice thick. "Then write fast, old woman. History's in a hurry today."

* * *

It took her two hours.

She wrote standing up, refusing the chair Mara offered, filling page after page in a firm, slanted hand that never wavered: the headgate closing in the summer of 1909; the alfalfa dying; her father's X-mark on the transfer deed; the march of 1911; the thirty-one signatures; the clerk who lost the petition and the house he gained; the rifle detail at the Herrera ditch; the name of every dispossessed family she could recall, and she recalled dozens, reciting them aloud half to herself as she wrote — *Vasquez, Okafor's cousins, the Duvals before they went to horse country, the Reyeses off the west bench* — and Mara started at that last list, because those were the surnames of the people camped in the box canyon forty miles north, the descendants of the very families in Elena's inventory, gathered now around a stolen ledger like iron filings around a magnet none of them had known was pulling.

When she finished, Elena signed each page at the bottom, blotted them, and then did something else. She took the photograph of the 1911 march, and the newspaper clipping, and her father's transfer deed with its trembling X, and added them to the packet.

"Evidence corroborates evidence," she said. "That's what Pell always wrote in his unpublished report — I got hold of a copy years back. One document is a story. Ten documents are a case." She tied the packet with string and held it out to Mara. "This stays with you, not him." A nod toward Silas. "No offense, ranger, but if Corliss men take you, they take everything. Split the cargo. The girl carries the affidavit; you carry the ledger. Different pockets, different horses, different fates."

"She's right," Silas said. "It's good fieldcraft."

"It's fifty-three years of thinking about nothing else," Elena said. "Now go. Out the back, through the alley, past the livery. And children—" She caught Mara's arm at the door, and her grip was stronger than her age, and her eyes were bright and terrible. "Two hundred miles east. Judge Amos Pell. Tell him Elena Brandt finally answered his fifteen-year-old question. He'll know what that means."

Mara tucked the packet inside her coat, against her heart, and hugged the old woman quickly and hard, smelling of lamp oil and flour dust and courage.

Outside, the alley was empty and the afternoon light lay long across the boards. But as they reached the livery, Silas stopped, head cocked, listening — and Mara heard it too: hoofbeats on the far side of town, several horses, moving fast, and a voice calling out along the street, asking questions of someone.

Asking, by the sound of it, about a woman matching her description.

Silas pulled her into the shadow of the stable door, his hand finding the revolver at his hip, and murmured, "Sallow's ahead of schedule. Back way out of town. Now."
