## 19. Ambush at the Crossing

The ferry crossing announced itself an hour before we reached it.

Not by sight — the river bent away behind a wall of tamarisk and the land flattened into a gray plain of alkali and greasewood — but by sound. A chain creaking somewhere out in the heat. The slap of water against a hull that hadn't been maintained since before the war. And under all of it, faint as a heartbeat through stone, the groan of the cable stretched bank to bank across the current.

I held up a fist and the column stopped behind me. Nine people trying to move quiet is like asking a piano to whisper, but they'd learned fast in three days on the run. Cass eased up beside me, squinting east through the shimmer.

"That's it?" she said. "Looks half-drowned."

"It is. Ferryman quit two years back when Corliss bought the toll rights and let it rot. Cable's still good, though." I studied the far bank, then the near one, reading country the way my father taught me before he taught me anything about grief. "Cross here or swim forty miles of narrows with a wounded man."

Ferro sat propped against a pack on the ground behind us, gray-faced, his boatman's hands useless in his lap since Sallow's men had opened his shoulder at the canyon. Ruth Ann knelt over him, changing a dressing that had soaked through too fast. She didn't look up when she spoke.

"He can't take cold water. Not for more than a minute."

"Then we ferry," I said. "And we do it careful."

Because something was wrong. I'd been carrying the wrongness since dawn like a stone in my boot. The crossing was the obvious route — the only route — which meant either Sallow was a fool, and three weeks of evidence said otherwise, or he *wanted* us here.

Mara came forward on silent feet, field glasses raised. She'd gotten good with them. Too good. There were things a girl learns watching her father's camp burn that no survey course teaches.

"Near bank's clear," she murmured. "Far side — there's a structure. Old toll house. Windows are dark but..." She lowered the glasses slowly. "Silas. The dust on the road past it. It's been disturbed. Fresh tire tracks going in, none coming out."

Nobody coming out. Vehicles going in and staying hidden. My stomach did what it always did before bad news — went very still and very cold, the way water does just before it freezes.

"Cass," I said quietly. "How many rounds you got left?"

"Seven. You?"

"Nine." I looked back down the line: Tomás with his rifle and his old man's patience, Ines carrying the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heartbeat, Peter white-knuckled around nothing, Ruth Ann kneeling in blood, Ferro dying by inches, Mara beside me with her father's eyes. "That's sixteen bullets between nine of us and whatever's waiting in that toll house."

Tomás spat, deliberate and slow. "Then we don't spend them unless we have to."

"We're not spending them at all," I said. "We're not crossing here."

"But you just said—"

"I said we cross *here*. I didn't say we cross *now*, and I didn't say we cross *on their schedule*." I turned to face them, all eight faces turned toward mine, and I felt the weight of it settle where it always settled — right across the shoulders, where a ranger carries everything he can't put down. "Sallow knows this crossing is our only way east without killing Ferro. So he's parked men in that toll house and he's waiting for us to walk onto that barge like sheep into a pen. He doesn't need to chase us. He just needs to be patient."

"So what do we do?" Peter's voice cracked on it. Kid had been holding together with spit and photographs, and both were running thin.

"We make him come to us," I said. "On ground we pick."

* * *

We pulled back two miles to a bend in the river where the bluff came down hard to the water and a stand of dead cottonwoods threw broken shade across a gravel bar. It wasn't shelter. It was geometry — high ground on one flank, the river sealing the other, and one narrow approach that any man coming at us would have to funnel through single file.

I set it up the way I'd have set it if I were defending it alone, because part of me still planned like a man alone even when I wasn't anymore. Tomás on the bluff with the long view. Cass low in the cottonwood root wad where the gravel met the bank. Mara with me at the treeline. The rest back among the deadfalls with orders to stay flat and stay silent, because Ruth Ann couldn't fight and Ferro couldn't stand and Ines was carrying the only thing in the territory worth more than all our lives stacked together.

Then I did the thing that felt most unnatural to me in all my twelve years of miles.

I waited for other people's opinions.

"Cass. If they hit us from the water instead of the road?"

She chewed it over, spitting sunflower shells into the dirt. "They won't. Boats are loud, and loud isn't Sallow. He'll come down the road slow, maybe send two men along the bank ahead as pickets. He likes a hammer and anvil." She glanced at me sideways. "You know how I know that?"

"Because you worked for men like him once."

"Because I *was* a man like him once." No shame in it. Cass Duval didn't carry shame; she carried arithmetic. "Question is what you do when the hammer falls. Run?"

"No."

"Fight?"

"Not first." I traced it in the dirt with a stick: the bend, the bluff, the gravel bar, the road. "First we let them show us their shape. How many, where, who leads. Then—" I broke the stick and dropped the pieces. "Then we give them something they don't expect."

"Which is?"

"Us leaving. Fast, downstream, on the water."

Cass stared at me. "In what? We got no raft. Ferro's boat burned at the canyon."

"The ferryman's skiff." I nodded toward the crossing, invisible now behind two miles of heat and brush. "Tied under the toll house dock. I saw it when we scouted. Flat-bottomed, fifteen feet, patched but sound. Sallow's men will be watching the barge and the road. Nobody watches a rotten skiff nobody wants."

"You want to go *back* to the ambush site."

"I want them to think we're still here while we're already gone. Tomás keeps a fire smoking on this bluff tonight. Big green-branch smoke, visible from the toll house. They'll hold position all night waiting for a dawn attack that never comes. Meanwhile we slip back along the bank in the dark, take the skiff, and float out on the current before first light."

Mara had come up quiet during the planning, the way she did, materializing at my shoulder like she'd been born to it. "And if they've got a man on the dock?"

"Then we handle him quiet, or we die quiet." I said it flat because saying it soft would have been a lie. "There's no version of this with zero risk. There's only versions where we choose which risk."

She looked at me a long moment. Something passed between us that neither of us named — the same thing that had been passing between us since the fever night, unspoken, banked like a coal. Then she nodded once. "I'll take the dock."

"No."

"Yes." Her chin came up. "You taught me to read country. That dock is country. I'm lightest, I'm quietest, and if it goes wrong, I'm the fastest thing on that bank. You know it's true."

I knew it was true. That was the worst part. Somewhere in the last hundred miles I'd stopped being her rescuer and started being her partner, and partners get the truth whether they want it or not.

"Dawn tomorrow," I said. "We move at first gray."

* * *

The night came down hot and starless, the kind of desert dark that swallows sound whole. Tomás built his decoy fire on the bluff crest and fed it green greasewood until the smoke rolled off it in fat silver columns, and from below it looked exactly like nine desperate people making ready to defend a position. Which, in fairness, we were. Just not the position anyone was watching.

We moved out at midnight, single file along the waterline where the sand held no tracks. I led. Mara came second, close enough that I could hear her breathing, steady as a metronome. Behind her Peter carrying the medical kit, Ruth Ann with Ferro's arm over her shoulders, Ines with the ledger, Tomás sweeping our backtrail with the rifle slung muzzle-down. Cass took point on the flank, ghosting through the tamarisk like she'd been paid to do it all her life, which at one point she had.

Two miles takes forty minutes on flat ground with healthy legs. With a wounded man and a medic and a botanist and a boy, it took two hours. Every minute of it I listened for the thing you can't plan for — a boot on stone, a rifle bolt, the click of a radio handset. The river talked the whole way, low and constant, covering us the way it had covered every secret in this valley for a hundred years. Stolen water. Buried ledgers. Dead men in shallow graves. The river kept it all.

The toll house rose out of the dark at 2 a.m., a black shape against blacker sky, the cable humming overhead like a struck wire. I could smell woodsmoke on the air — Sallow's men had a fire inside, careless, confident. Why wouldn't they be? Fourteen guns against nine tired people. The math was theirs.

I put my hand on Mara's shoulder and pointed. The dock jutted twenty feet off the bank, and beneath it, tied low in the shadow of the pilings, the skiff rode the current with a patience I envied.

One sentry. On the dock's landward end, seated on a crate, rifle across his knees, hat tipped down. Asleep or near it. Firelight from the toll house window touched him in orange slices.

Mara looked at me. I looked at her. We'd rehearsed it on the walk in, in whispers: she goes low along the pilings, I cover from the tamarisk, and if the sentry wakes before she reaches him, I put a bullet through the crate beside his head and we all run for the skiff under covering fire and pray. Sixteen bullets. One prayer. Terrible odds dressed up as a plan.

She went.

I watched her move and felt something in my chest turn over — pride and terror in the same breath, indistinguishable, like twin currents in one river. She flowed across the open sand in that low crouch I'd drilled into her, hugged the first piling, slid into the dock's shadow. Ten feet from the sentry now. Five. I could see the rise and fall of his chest, see the moment his head began to lift—

A coyote sang from the bluff. Long, wavering, perfectly timed.

Tomás. The old man could throw his voice better than a vaudeville act, and the sentry froze, head turning toward the hills, and in that exact window Mara rose behind him and laid the edge of her hand against his throat and said something I couldn't hear but recognized anyway — the words Silas had taught her, the ones that meant *be still or die*. The man went rigid, then pliant. She took his rifle. She zip-tied his wrists with a strip of inner tube and walked him backward off the dock and sat him down gentle in the sand, and he stayed there, smart enough to understand the arithmetic.

She reappeared at the skiff and gave me the signal — two fingers, palm out. Clear.

We loaded Ferro first, laying him along the bottom boards with Ruth Ann cradling his head. Then the packs, the water, the ledger pressed into Ines's arms. Then the rest of them, one by one, sliding over the gunwales into a boat built for six riding nine. I cast off the line and pushed us out with a boat hook, and the current took us the way the river takes everything — gently, absolutely, without negotiation.

For eleven minutes, floating downstream through the dark with the toll house shrinking behind us, I allowed myself the luxury of believing we'd beaten him.

* * *

The first shot came at gray light, from the bluff we'd abandoned.

It missed by thirty yards — a ranging shot, high and wide, the kind you fire to say *I see you*. I twisted in the stern and looked back and the world went wrong in a single breath.

Men on the bluff. Six of them, maybe eight, spread along the crest where Tomás's decoy fire still smoldered. And below them, on the road, kicking up dust, a truck — Sallow's truck, the one I'd seen at the canyon, engine laboring as it swung toward the crossing two miles upstream.

"They found the empty camp," Tomás said grimly, working the bolt. "Smoke fooled them till dawn. Then somebody walked up to check."

"No." I was doing the math and hating every number. "They didn't find it. They *let* us have the night." Because now I saw it, the shape underneath the shape, the thing I should have seen two days ago when fresh tracks appeared where no one should be. The sentry asleep on the dock. The toll house fire burning careless. The bluff left thinly held. Every door unlocked except the one behind us.

"Sallow didn't guard the crossing," I heard myself say. "He guarded the *river below it*. He wanted us on the water. Wanted us moving slow, in the open, in a boat we couldn't steer off of."

"Why?" Peter's voice, thin. "Why not just take us at the crossing? He had us boxed."

"Because boxes make martyrs." Cass answered before I could, and there was something new in her voice — not fear, exactly. Recognition. "You corner nine people and some of them fight like devils and somebody's cousin hears about it. But if they drown running, or catch lead in open water where nobody can count the bodies right—" she spat over the gunwale, "—that's just tragedy. Deniable. Same trick Corliss used on the ranchers in '09. Never kill a man at home. Kill him traveling."

The second shot came lower. Closer. The third clipped the water a foot off our beam and threw spray across Ruth Ann's back, and after that the morning opened up.

Rifles cracked from the bluff in ragged sequence — not a volley, a *herding*, shots walking us toward the outside of the bend where the current ran fastest and the water got mean. I hauled the sweep oar and dug for the slack water along the inside bank, and the skiff groaned and wallowed, overloaded, nine souls sitting lower in the river than any boat should.

"Ferro's bleeding again," Ruth Ann called. "Pulse is thready. I need him flat and I need this boat to stop rocking."

"Working on it!" I put my back into the oar. Fifty yards to the cutbank, forty, and the shooting behind us found its rhythm, disciplined now, professional, rounds snapping past close enough to feel the wind of them. Sallow's men weren't trying to hit us. They were trying to *move* us, and the current was their partner, shoving our stern toward the middle of the river where the water ran deep and cold and fast toward the narrows.

Then Peter stood up.

I don't know why. Maybe to bail — we were shipping water over the stern, an inch of it sloshing around our boots. Maybe just panic standing upright the way panic does. Whatever the reason, he rose above the gunwale into the open air at the exact moment a rifle spoke from the bluff, and I saw the shooter correct, saw the muzzle track from the boat's centerline to the tall thin silhouette, and I understood in one frozen instant that this shot was different. This one was aimed.

"Cass—"

She was already moving. Later I'd try to reconstruct it and fail — the way she crossed that boat, over packs and knees and Ferro's stretcher, faster than a woman her age had any right to move. She hit Peter square in the chest with both hands and folded him down behind the gunwale as the round arrived.

The crack of it was lost under the river noise. What I heard instead was Cass make a sound I'd never heard her make — a short, punched-out grunt, surprise more than pain — and then she sagged against the thwart, and her hand came away from her side red to the wrist.

"Cass is hit!" Ruth Ann was already crawling toward her, kit strap between her teeth. "Silas, get us to that bank NOW!"

The world narrowed to oar-blade and current and the count of yards. Thirty. Twenty. Rounds chasing us, closer, angrier — Sallow's men had stopped herding and started hunting, and I understood that too, late, the way I understood everything about Duke Sallow one beat after it mattered. The herding was over because the herding had done its job. We were exactly where he wanted us: mid-river, slow, exposed, bleeding.

Ten yards. I threw the skiff sideways with the last strength in my shoulders and we grounded hard on the gravel shelf below the cutbank, hull grinding, everyone pitching forward. "Out! Everybody out, drag her over the bar, get below the bank line!"

They poured out into knee-deep water. Tomás lifted Cass like she weighed nothing and carried her up the gravel while Ruth Ann shouted instructions — pressure here, kit there, boil water, *now*. Mara grabbed Peter by the collar and hauled him behind the cutbank, and the kid was shaking so hard his teeth chattered, staring at the blood on his own shirt that wasn't his.

"She took it for me," he kept saying. "She took the bullet for me. Why would she—"

"Because that's what family does," Mara said, "and you're it, whether you signed up or not."

I knelt over Cass in the lee of the bank. The bullet had gone in below her ribs, low, and Ruth Ann's hands were quick and certain over the wound, but her face told me what her voice wouldn't. Deep. Bad angle. This was not a wound the riverbank willow tea was going to argue with.

"Ruth Ann. Straight answer."

"She needs a doctor. Real doctor, real table, within a day. Maybe less." Ruth Ann didn't look up from her work. "The narrows are two days' float."

"Then we don't float." I stood up and looked upstream, where the dust of Sallow's truck hung in the morning air like a accusation, and downstream, where the narrows waited with their teeth, and I felt the fury come up through me like heat off rock — not the hot kind, the other kind, the kind that freezes into something you can build with.

Because now I finally saw the whole board. The canyon. The crossing. The skiff. Every trap we'd slipped had been a gate, and every gate had swung shut behind us, and we weren't running from Duke Sallow at all. We were being *driven* — mile by mile, choice by choice, down a chute he'd mapped before we ever reached the river. He wasn't chasing us.

He was herding us somewhere. And I meant to find out where, and be waiting when we arrived.

"Mara," I said. "Get me the map. We're done running his river."

Behind us, upstream, the truck engine died, and men began to move along the bank.
