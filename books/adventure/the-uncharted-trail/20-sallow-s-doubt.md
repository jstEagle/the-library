## 20. Sallow's Doubt

The fire had burned down to a red eye by the time Duke Sallow sent the last of his men out on watch. He told them he wanted the camp quiet, wanted to think. What he wanted was to be alone with the dead man's saddlebag.

It had come off Renny Coates two days ago, down at the ferry crossing, after the bullet went through him and the river took its toll of everything else. Sallow had ordered the bag kept. Nobody asked why. Men didn't ask Duke Sallow why about much — that was the reputation he'd spent twenty years building, and reputations were the only currency a man like him could never counterfeit.

He sat on his bedroll with the saddlebag across his knees and worked the buckle loose with his thumb.

Inside: a change of socks gone gray at the heels, a tin of tobacco, half a deck of cards soft as cloth from sweat, and a leather folder wrapped in oilcloth. The folder was the reason Renny was dead. The folder was the reason any of them might end up dead, though Sallow doubted most of his boys had done that arithmetic yet. They thought they were chasing a ranger and a pack of bookish runaways. They thought the money was simple.

Sallow had been in this business long enough to know money was never simple. Money came with a smell to it, and this money smelled like a river bottom in August.

He unwrapped the oilcloth.

Photographs. Pages photographed flat against a board, corners shadowed, some of them blurred where the hand holding the camera had shaken. And beneath the photographs, folded twice, three actual pages torn from something old and cracked and soft as a dog's ear. Taken off Coates, which meant somebody in the canyon party had been searched before they ran, or one of the survivors had been turned, or — more likely, knowing how these things went — the pages had changed hands in the dark somewhere between the ambush and the escape, passed along like a hot coal nobody wanted to hold.

Sallow held it now. He struck a match and lit the stub of candle he kept for map work, and he angled the first page into the light.

The handwriting was copperplate, the kind they taught schoolboys a hundred years ago and nobody since. *Transfer of Water Rights, San Rafael Drainage, 1893.* A name at the top — Hollis Holdings, no, wait. He squinted. Not Hollis. The ink had faded to the color of weak tea. *Corliss.* Of course Corliss. Everything upstream of money in this territory eventually said Corliss if you followed it far enough.

He read. He was not an educated man, but he could read a contract the way other men read weather, and what he read made the fire behind him seem suddenly colder.

*In consideration of certain considerations,* the document said, which was lawyer talk for nothing at all, *the undersigned party conveys all right, title, and interest in the waters of the San Rafael and its tributaries...*

Consideration. Two dollars an acre, the next line said. Two dollars an acre for water rights that had built three towns and a company store and a church with a steeple you could see from ten miles out. Sallow knew what land went for. His whole life was land, in a way — knowing it, crossing it, reading it like print. Two dollars an acre wasn't a price. It was a joke told with a gun on the table.

And there, at the bottom, the signature line. Most of the names were signed in that same careful copperplate. But scattered among them, maybe every fourth or fifth page, were marks instead of names. An X, witnessed by some clerk's flourish, *his mark.*

Sallow turned the page.

The second sheet was dated four months later. Same drainage. Different family name. Same two dollars an acre. Same X at the bottom, and this time the clerk's note beside it had survived the years well enough to read plain:

*Signatory illiterate; conveyed under protest; advised by undersigned that refusal may result in forfeiture of homestead entire.*

Under protest. Advised that refusal may result in forfeiture.

Sallow sat very still. Somewhere out on the perimeter, a horse shifted and a man murmured to it, and the ordinary sounds of a camp at night went on around him like weather.

He turned to the third page.

He knew before he looked. Some part of him, some animal part that had kept him alive through eleven years of hired guns and range wars and jobs that paid in cash because cash left no trail — that part knew what was coming the way a horse knows a storm before the sky turns. His hands were steady. That surprised him. He'd have expected them to shake.

The date was 1896. The family name was Salcedo.

His grandmother had been a Salcedo. Before she married, before she crossed the line and became a Sallow by way of a Texas boy with quick hands and no patience, she had been Esperanza Salcedo, and her people had run sheep along the San Rafael since before there was a territory, back when it was all just Mexico's leftover country that nobody with power wanted enough to steal.

Until somebody with power wanted it.

Two dollars an acre. The X at the bottom of the page was big and clumsy, drawn by a hand that had never held a pen, pressed so hard the paper had nearly given way. Beside it, the clerk's notation, in that same tidy copperplate: *signatory illiterate; conveyed under duress; witness present.*

Witness present. Meaning a man with a rifle had stood there while Esperanza Salcedo's father — Sallow did the math without wanting to, and the math came anyway — while Sallow's own great-grandfather had marked his X and signed away the water that fed his sheep and his children and his children's children, and had walked away with forty dollars and nothing else, because the alternative written in the clerk's careful hand was forfeiture of homestead entire.

Duress. The word sat on the page like a stone.

Duke Sallow had shot men for less than a word. He had burned a rancher's barn once, early days, for a man who paid even worse than Corliss, and he had told himself then what he told himself now, what he'd been telling himself for twenty years: everybody works for somebody, and the somebody is always dirty, and a man who wants clean work can starve clean. It was a good philosophy. It had kept him fed through winters that killed better men than him.

But a philosophy is a thing you build, and a fact is a thing you find, and what he had found, alone by a dying fire with a dead man's papers in his lap, was the fact that his own blood was written into this ledger. Not as the thief. As the robbed. His grandfather's father had put his mark on this page because a man with a gun stood behind him, and a hundred miles and sixty years downstream, Duke Sallow had grown up into exactly that man with the gun, working for the family that had held it, collecting wages from the theft of his own name.

He laughed. It came out wrong, low and dry, more cough than laugh, and he stopped it fast because sound carried in canyon country at night.

*You always were on the wrong side of your own story,* he thought. *You just never read far enough back to see it.*

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat by the coals until they went gray, and when the eastern sky started thinking about light he got up and walked the perimeter, checking his men the way he always did, and found himself looking at their faces differently.

Vance, twenty-three, off a dryland farm south of Corlissville, good with horses, better with a rifle, sending half his pay home to his mother. Reyes — no relation to the guide, but from the same valley, same stock, the families that had worked the river before the company owned the river. The Delgado brothers, whose father still took scrip at the company store because there was nowhere else to spend it. Boys. All of them boys, or close enough, sons of the same dispossessed country Sallow had come from, carrying guns for the family that had dispossessed them because a gun was the only trade the country had left them.

He'd known that, of course. He'd known it the way you know the sun comes up — as a fact too large and constant to mean anything. Tonight it meant something. Tonight every face on the perimeter looked like a page out of the ledger, waiting for its X.

At dawn he climbed to the high point above camp with his radio, the little shortwave set Ada Corliss had issued him along with the frequency and the call sign and the standing order: *report daily, Mr. Sallow. I do not tolerate silence.*

She answered on the second call. Her voice came through thin and precise, stripped of everything but intent, the voice of a woman who treated conversation as a form of accounting.

"Status."

"We hit them at the crossing two nights back." He kept his report level, professional, the report of a man with no doubts in him. "Took one casualty on our side. Coates. They lost supplies and gained nothing. They're running east now, toward the state line, moving slow because they've got a wounded man on a litter."

A pause on the line. Static, and beneath the static the faint rasp of her breathing, which he had noticed lately was worse, wetter, though he supposed a dying woman's breathing was none of his business.

"Slow," she repeated. "You're certain."

"Confirmed by sign. Litter poles, drag marks, a blood trail we picked up at dawn yesterday. They're making maybe ten miles a day. I can have them inside the week."

Another pause. When she spoke again her voice had dropped half a register, the way it did when she was pleased, which was worse than when she was angry. "Good. Mr. Sallow, listen carefully. There's been a development. I've retained additional personnel — outside men, professionals. They'll be operating independently in the field. You will not interfere with them, and they will not interfere with you. Your task remains pursuit and containment. Do not engage the main body unless engaged. The outside men will handle resolution."

Sallow looked out over the canyon country, the red rock going gold with morning, the river a brown thread stitching it all together. Resolution. She'd used that word twice before in this job, and both times it had meant graves.

"Understood," he said.

"And Mr. Sallow." The static crackled. Her voice came through it like a blade through cloth. "I'm told they may have divided. If a smaller party has split off with the documents, that party is the priority. The people are leverage. The documents are the war. Am I clear?"

"Clear," Sallow said.

"Then earn your money." The line clicked dead.

He lowered the radio and sat with it in his lap for a long moment, watching a hawk turn circles over the far rim, riding a thermal up out of the canyon like something escaping.

They may have divided. She knew things he hadn't reported. Which meant she had eyes he didn't know about, or ears, or both — and the outside men were already moving, probably already in the country, probably already ahead of him. He'd been running this operation for three weeks and he'd just learned, from his own employer, that he was scenery.

Or he had been scenery. A man could stop being scenery.

Sallow opened the radio case again, took out his map, and spread it on a flat rock. He knew this country the way a preacher knows scripture. Forty miles northeast, past the painted hills, there was a stretch of badlands called the Moki Steps where a party moving slow with a litter would leave tracks a blind man could follow — and where a party moving smart would leave none at all, because the ground there ate sign like the river ate bodies. If Silas Vasquez was half the tracker the territory said he was, the Moki Steps was where he'd go. No tracks, no trail, no war.

If Sallow reported the Moki Steps, Ada's outside guns would spend three days raking empty rock while the real party moved wherever it was actually moving. And if he reported the opposite — the southern route, the open flats, the easy ground — they'd flood south and find nothing but jackrabbits.

He unslung his pencil. His hand was steady again, steadier than it had been all night, and he understood why. Doubt, it turned out, didn't shake a man. Doubt settled him, the way a verdict settles a trial. He had spent twenty years unsure what side of his own story he was on, and now he knew, and knowing was a kind of peace he hadn't earned and couldn't refuse.

He marked the southern flats. He keyed the radio and gave Ada Corliss coordinates for ground that was forty miles from anywhere her enemies might be, delivered in the flat confident voice of a man reporting sign he had personally verified, and when she said *good, Mr. Sallow, very good,* he felt nothing at all, which was its own strange relief.

He packed the radio away. Then he took the ledger pages out of his coat, all three of them, and looked at the last one a final time — the date, the name Salcedo, the X pressed deep enough to tear the paper, the clerk's neat word *duress* sitting beside it like a headstone.

He couldn't keep them. If the outside men searched him and found them, he was a dead man, and worse, a useless one. But he couldn't burn them either. He'd watched enough evidence burn in twenty years of dirty work to know that burning was just another way of signing.

So he did the only thing left. He folded the pages small, wrapped them in the oilcloth, and tucked them into the lining of his saddlebag, under the wool blanket, sewn in with three stitches of waxed thread that would take a knife to find. Whatever happened next — whatever the outside men did, whatever Ada Corliss ordered, however this war ended — those three pages were going to survive it. Somebody someday was going to read his grandfather's X and know it for what it was.

Not a signature. A scar.

He mounted up and rode down into camp, and his men looked up at him the way they always did, waiting for orders, trusting the reputation, and Sallow swung down and started giving them — false trails, false directions, a search pattern designed to miss — and discovered that lying to his own men was harder than lying to the old woman, because their faces were young, and every one of them was a page out of the ledger too.

By noon they were riding south, hard, toward empty country, and behind them, forty miles north and closing on a box canyon Sallow had deliberately not named, the real war was walking toward dawn.

He just hoped — and it was the first time in twenty years he'd let himself hope anything on the job — that the man leading it was faster than the guns coming up behind him.
