## 21. Fever Hours

The willow bark had to come from the north bank.

I'd known that since the second day, when Cass's fever first climbed past the point where fever is just the body doing its work and becomes the body burning its own house down. North bank meant crossing the river at dawn, when the water ran lowest and coldest, and it meant Silas coming with me because nobody argued with him about who went where anymore — not since the ambush at the crossing, not since Cass took that bullet through the shoulder for a boy she'd known three weeks and would have died for anyway.

So I crossed at first light with the ranger walking point, and we cut willow from the stands where the seeps kept the ground dark, and I stripped bark with my thumbnail while he watched the ridgeline, and neither of us said what we were both thinking: that willow bark was a prayer, not a cure, and prayers were running thin in this canyon.

Back at camp, the world had contracted to twelve feet of canvas and one burning woman.

Cass Duval lay on the litter we'd lashed between two pack frames, and she was fighting us even unconscious — her good arm twitching against the blanket, her jaw set like she was still steering a raft through white water somewhere behind her eyes. The wound itself was clean. That was the irony I kept turning over like a stone in my pocket. Ruth Ann Okafor, field medic, eleven years of patching people in places where hospitals were rumors: I knew clean when I saw it. The bullet had gone through muscle, missed the bone, missed the artery by a finger's width of luck. Clean entry, clean exit. She should have been healing.

But the river water had gotten into it during the narrows run, and river water in this country carried things you didn't want to name out loud, and now the flesh around the wound was hot and tight and shining, and the red streaks climbing toward her armpit were the thing I named only to myself, at night, when I let myself do arithmetic I hated.

Infection. Advancing. And my entire pharmacopeia was willow bark, boiled instruments, honey I'd been hoarding since the supply cache, and stubbornness.

"Talk to me," Ines said, kneeling across the litter from me. She'd appointed herself my nurse without asking, the way she did everything — quietly, competently, as if competence were just another form of breathing. "Numbers."

"Pulse one-ten and thready. Temp — I don't have a thermometer, so touch." I took her hand and pressed it to Cass's forehead, then to the skin above the wound. Ines winced. "Yeah. That's how I know too."

"The willow?"

"Steeping. It'll bring the fever down maybe two degrees for four hours, then it climbs again. We're buying hours, not days." I said it flat, because flatness was a tool same as my forceps. "What we need is drainage, and I can't drain what I can't see, and I can't see without opening her up again, and if I open her up again with these instruments and this water—"

"You'll boil them," Ines said. "You've boiled everything twice already."

"I'll boil them a third time. Boiling doesn't make water sterile, Ines. It makes it less deadly. There's a difference and the difference has a body count."

She absorbed that the way she absorbed everything, filing it somewhere behind those calm eyes, and then she said the thing that undid me a little: "Then we keep her alive hour by hour until the hours add up to something. That's all any of us are doing right now."

* * *

The morning passed in the small liturgies of nursing.

I sponged Cass down with water we'd boiled and cooled, working fast before the heat of the day made evaporation a cruelty instead of a mercy. I forced willow tea between her lips a spoonful at a time, and when she surfaced enough to know where she was, she cursed me with a fluency that actually reassured me — Cass Duval insulting my tea meant Cass Duval was still in there, still angry, still herself.

"That stuff," she rasped, "tastes like a beaver died in it."

"It did. That's the active ingredient."

Her laugh turned into a cough, and I held the cup while she drank, and her eyes found mine and held on with an intensity that had nothing to do with fever. "Ruth Ann. Straight answer. Am I dying?"

Every medic learns the calculus of that question. Lie, and lose them forever when they find out. Tell the whole truth, and some of them lie down and die of the truth alone. I gave her the third answer, the honest one: "You're dying *slower* than you were yesterday. That's not nothing. That's the whole job right now."

She nodded, slow, and her hand came up and gripped my wrist with a strength that surprised us both. "Then keep doing it. I'm not done. I've got land waiting on me now." Her eyes drifted shut. "Reclaimed land. You heard Tomás. I'm going to put cattle on ground my granddaddy bled for, and no fever gets a vote."

She slept. I sat back on my heels and let my hands shake for exactly thirty seconds, which was the allowance I'd given myself since the crossing, and then I washed them in the boiled water and went back to work.

* * *

Afternoon brought the heat, and the heat brought the flies, and the flies brought Mara Hollis with a smudge pot she'd built from green brush and damp earth, because smoke was the only argument flies respected.

She set it upwind of the litter with a care that told me something about her — that grief hadn't burned the practicality out of her, only reshaped it into vigilance. Everything Mara did now was done twice: once for the purpose, once for the threat she imagined inside every purpose. I watched her place the smudge pot and then check the wind three times, and I thought: this girl learned to double-check the world because the last time she trusted it, it killed her father.

"How is she?" Mara asked.

"Alive. Angry. On schedule." I wiped my hands on my apron — I'd started wearing an apron, an old shirt of Ferro's split up the back, because ritual mattered in medicine more than people admitted. Ritual was how you kept your hands steady when your heart wasn't. "How's Ferro?"

"He ate half a trout and complained about the other half. Ruth Ann, whatever you did for that wound dressing—"

"Changed it. Dressings aren't magic, they're just changed often enough to seem like magic." I glanced toward the shade where Ferro lay propped against a pack, arguing politics with Peter Liu in the desultory way of men too hurt to mean it. "Keep him still. Every time he 'helps,' he pulls his stitches, and then I get to sew him again, and my thread is finite."

Mara almost smiled. Then the smile went somewhere else, softer and more complicated, and she looked past me to where Silas stood at the canyon mouth with binoculars, doing what he always did: reading the country like a man checking a ledger only he could see.

"He hasn't slept," she said. Not a question. An observation filed under a larger worry.

"Neither have you."

"I sleep."

"Two hours a night, sitting up, with your rifle across your knees. That's not sleeping, that's sentry duty with your eyes closed." I said it gently, but I said it, because somebody had to, and I was the one whose job entitled her to notice bodies. "Go sit with him. Not to talk about Sallow or Corliss or the ledger. Just sit. Both of you. Ten minutes where nobody's guarding anything except each other."

She looked at me like I'd suggested something indecent. Then, slowly, like a woman stepping onto ice she didn't trust, she walked toward the canyon mouth.

* * *

I want to record what happened next accurately, because I was watching, and because medics spend their lives around other people's most private hours and rarely write any of it down.

Silas heard her coming — of course he did; I think he heard heartbeats at forty yards — but he didn't turn until she sat down beside him on the flat rock, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched and deliberately not quite touching. They watched the ridgeline together. The light was going long and gold, the way it does in this country in late afternoon, pouring down the canyon walls like something poured from a jug.

"You should sleep," Mara said.

"So should you."

"I asked first."

A silence. Not an empty silence — a full one, the kind that has weight and temperature. I pretended to sort my instruments, which needed no sorting, and listened with the shameless attention of a woman who has spent eleven years learning that love stories happen mostly in pauses.

"There's a spring," Silas said finally, "two miles up the west fork. Cold year-round. When this is over—" He stopped. Restarted, the way a man restarts when the sentence he planned turns out to be the wrong sentence. "When Cass is on her feet. There's a trail from that spring over the divide that nobody uses. It comes out in country with more elk than fences. I could show you."

"You could show me," Mara repeated. Testing the words.

"If you wanted."

The pause stretched so long I checked Cass's pulse just to have something to do with my hands. One hundred and six. Holding. Good girl. Keep fighting.

"I don't know how to want things anymore," Mara said, very quietly, and I don't think she knew she'd said it aloud. "My father used to say the river teaches you that plans are just hopes with a current underneath. I had a plan. Finish the expedition. Come home. Show him the specimens. And now—" Her breath caught and steadied, the way breath does when someone has practiced catching it. "Now wanting anything feels like painting a target on it."

Silas was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had dropped into a register I hadn't heard from him — lower, rougher, the voice under the ranger's voice.

"My father had a saying too. Different lesson, same river." A dry sound that wasn't quite a laugh. "He said a man who measures everything in miles never has to measure anything in years. Twelve years I've been measuring miles, Mara. Miles to the next patrol. Miles to the next ridge. Miles between me and every place I ever lost anybody." He turned his head and looked at her, and even from twenty feet away I saw the force of it hit her. "And then you showed up on my trail with an arrow drawn, and somewhere between the cache and the canyon I stopped counting miles. I haven't figured out what I'm counting instead. But it isn't distance."

The gold light moved on the rock between them. Neither of them moved. The space between their shoulders — that deliberate inch — seemed suddenly like the widest gap in the territory, wider than the narrows, wider than the forty miles of dead ground ahead of us.

Mara's hand moved. Just slightly. Across the stone, toward his.

And that was when Cass screamed.

* * *

Later, I would be grateful for the timing, though gratitude felt obscene. Later, Mara would tell me she could have killed me for sending her over there, and then, much later, thank me. But in the moment there was only the scream tearing up out of the litter, raw and wrong, and everything else in the world — every almost-spoken word, every inch of golden air — evaporated like water on hot stone.

I was moving before the echo died.

Cass had arched off the litter, her back bowed, her teeth locked, and the wound — God, the wound. The swelling had changed while we weren't looking. The skin over it had gone from shiny to taut to something darker, and beneath my fingers I felt it, the thing I'd been dreading and praying for in equal measure: fluctuance. A pocket of pus, gathered and ripe, pressing outward like a fist under a sheet.

It needed to open. Now. On my terms, with my blade, before it opened on its own and dumped its poison into her blood all at once.

"Ines! Boiled water, the big basin. Peter — hold her legs. Tomás, I need your belt and your hands, and I need you not to faint, because you're going to hold her shoulder down and she is strong as a mule and twice as mean."

They moved. That was the thing about this ragged little tribe of scientists and wranglers and boatmen — they moved. Peter's face had gone gray but his hands were iron on Cass's ankles. Tomás wrapped his belt around her forearm for her to bite and murmured to her in Spanish, low and constant, words I didn't understand but recognized anyway, because every language has a register for *you are not alone.*

"Mara." She was already there, pale, steady. "Lamp. Here, here, and here. Wherever my hands go, the light goes. Don't blink."

"Yes, Doctor."

"I'm not a doctor."

"You are tonight."

I took up the lancet. I'd boiled it three times. I'd boiled my hands in spirit and scrubbed them raw. Somewhere beyond the canvas, Silas had taken up position at the canyon mouth with his rifle, giving us his back — standing watch over the one thing left worth watching, so that no man's shadow would fall across this work.

"Cass," I said, close to her ear. "Listen to me. This is going to hurt worse than the bullet. I'm sorry. I'd drug you if I had anything. Bite down."

Her eyes opened. Fever-bright, furious, alive. "Do it," she snarled around the belt. "I've been bit by worse things than knives."

I cut.

What came out of her was yellow-gray and reeking, and I drained it and irrigated it and packed it, and she screamed and fought and called me names in two languages, and I worked through all of it with my hands steady because Mara's lamp never wavered and Ines counted my instruments aloud like a rosary — *one, two, three, all present* — and because somewhere in the middle of it I understood, with a clarity that felt almost holy:

This was the defiance. Not guns. Not ledgers. Not courtroom vindication two hundred miles east. This — a woman with a boiled knife refusing to let death have a wrangler, in a canvas shelter, by lamplight, while her friends held her down and sang to her in Spanish — *this* was how you spit in the eye of everything Ada Corliss represented. Every life saved out here was a page torn out of the matriarch's cold arithmetic. Every hour Cass Duval breathed was an hour Corliss couldn't buy back.

Tenderness, performed with a blade and boiling water, was still tenderness. Maybe it was the only kind that counted.

When it was done, Cass sagged into sleep — real sleep this time, deep and slack, the fight gone out of her body because the fight had been won. I sat back. My apron was ruined. My hands, for the first time in hours, shook without permission.

Ines touched my shoulder. "Well?"

"Ask me tomorrow. Ask me every tomorrow for a week." I looked down at Cass's face, already losing its burnished fever-flush, already just a tired woman sleeping. "But tonight? Tonight I think we stole her back."

* * *

Near midnight I stepped out of the shelter to wash, and found Mara and Silas exactly where the emergency had left them — side by side on the flat rock, closer now than before, as if the interruption had decided something their courage hadn't. Mara's head was tipped against Silas's shoulder. His arm was around her, careful as a man handling something rescued from a fire. Neither spoke. Above the canyon walls, the stars were thick and indifferent and beautiful.

They saw me and didn't pull apart. That was new. That was its own kind of confession.

"She's sleeping clean," I told them. "Real sleep. The fever's broken for now — it'll try to come back, fevers are stubborn that way, but we bought her the night. Maybe the week."

Mara exhaled a long, unsteady breath. "Thank you, Ruth Ann."

"Don't thank me yet. Thank me when she's complaining about fence posts." I hesitated, then said the thing I'd been carrying since the rock, because medics deliver hard news and I'd decided this was the good kind: "For what it's worth — I watched you two this afternoon. Before. And I'll say what I tell every fool I've ever patched up: the country out here kills you quick or wears you down slow, and either way, the ones who make it are the ones who stop keeping watch alone."

Silas looked at me across the firelight. Something moved behind those tracker's eyes — the look of a man hearing a map read aloud for territory he'd already crossed in the dark.

"Noted," he said.

And then, from the darkness at the canyon mouth, a sound that froze all three of us mid-breath: the click of a rifle bolt, and Silas's voice, low and hard, transformed —

"Hold your fire. Hold it—" A pause. "It's a horse. One rider. No lantern."

Mara was on her feet. I was reaching for my bag. And Silas rose slowly into the starlight, rifle leveled at the black mouth of the canyon, as hoofbeats came on through the dark — unhurried, deliberate, and alone.
