## 22. Ada Corliss

The doctor had told her in March that she would not see the cottonwoods turn. Ada Corliss had thanked him for his honesty, paid his bill in full — cash, always cash, never a debt she couldn't call in later — and dismissed him from the study with the same courtesy she extended to the man who delivered her ice and the man who delivered her threats. Dying was not an insult. It was an appointment. She intended to keep it on her own terms, seated upright, with her affairs in order.

It was now nearly September, and the cottonwoods along the Corlissville riverfront had begun to show a thin gold at their crowns, as if they too were keeping her schedule.

She sat by the east window of the big house because the light came through there first and left last, and because from that window she could see the water tower, and the company store below it, and the church steeple her father had raised with money he'd wrung out of other men's rivers. Three towns, all of them drinking from water her family had taken the way you take a purse off a drunk man's belt: quickly, quietly, and without ever once looking him in the face.

Her hands lay folded in her lap. They had been beautiful hands once; men had written poems about them, or so they'd claimed, though Ada had long suspected the poems were about the land those hands would inherit. Now the knuckles stood up like fence posts in a drought, and the skin was paper-thin, and one good squeeze would bruise her for a week. She wore no rings. Rings caught on things. Her father had taught her that too, though he'd meant it about ledgers.

*Father.*

She thought of him more often now, which annoyed her. There was no time left to waste on a dead man who had wasted everything else.

* * *

Emmett Corliss had been neither a monster nor a fool, which was the worst combination a daughter could inherit from. A monster could be hated cleanly. A fool could be pitied. Emmett had been a careful man with a soft voice and a talent for arithmetic, and he had looked at the high desert in 1891 and seen what everyone else saw — rock, sun, distance — and then he'd seen what nobody else saw: that the whole country was worthless except for the river, and the river belonged to whoever held the paper.

He hadn't stolen the water. That was the family myth, the one the newspapers liked when they wanted a villain with a black hat. Emmett had done something far more respectable. He had *bought* it.

Ada had found the original ledger when she was nineteen, in the tin box beneath the floorboards of her father's study, wrapped in oilcloth like something holy. She'd expected deeds. She'd found signatures instead — pages of them, X-marks and shaky names, dated across three hard years of drought, each transfer witnessed by the same two men: the county clerk Emmett kept on salary and a deputy whose boots Emmett also paid for. The prices were obscene. Not obscenely high. Obscenely low. A man signing away forty acres and all its water rights for thirty dollars and a sack of flour, in the third year of a drought, while Emmett's ditches ran full and his cattle grew fat on hay grown with other people's inheritance.

She remembered sitting on the study floor with that ledger open across her knees, nineteen years old and already understanding that she was holding either a weapon or a wound, and that she would spend her life deciding which.

She had decided before sundown.

Not because she was evil. She wished people understood that — she wished someone, someday, would write it down plainly, though she knew better than to expect fairness from history. She had been a girl who loved her father, and her father had shown her the ledger, eventually, deliberately, the way a man shows his heir the wellhead. He had not apologized. He had explained. *Water doesn't belong to anybody, Ada-girl. It belongs to whoever can keep it moving where he wants it. The signatures are just how we keep it honest.* And then he had laid out the arithmetic that justified three towns: the jobs, the store, the school, the church, the ditch crews, the wages — scrip, but wages nonetheless — the whole breathing organism of Corlissville that existed because Emmett Corliss had made the desert drink on schedule.

Take away the theft, he said, and you take away the town. Take away the town, and there are four hundred families with nothing but sand and grievance.

He had believed it by the end. That was the remarkable thing. Somewhere between the first coerced signature and the last, Emmett Corliss had stopped stealing water and started *providing* it, in his own mind, and the ledger under the floorboards had become not evidence but embarrassment — an old scar he preferred not to look at. He'd left instructions that it be burned after his death.

Ada had burned something else instead. She'd fed the fireplace a stack of blank ledgers of identical age and binding, and she had put the true one back beneath the floor, and she had told no one, not even her husband.

Especially not her husband.

* * *

Martin Corliss had been a handsome, hollow man from a banking family in Denver, and marrying him had been the single coldest decision of her life, which was saying something, given the inventory. She had needed a name that wasn't hers — a name that could front the expansion into the southern valleys without every old grudge riding along on the letterhead. Martin had needed money and a wife who wouldn't ask where he went on Thursdays. They had suited one another the way two locks suit one key: precisely, mechanically, and with no warmth required.

He had died eleven years ago of a heart that gave out in a hotel room in El Paso, and Ada had wept at the funeral, genuinely, to her own surprise — not for the man but for the girl she'd been when she chose him, the nineteen-year-old on the study floor who had traded her whole future for a tin box. Grief, she had learned, was rarely aimed where you thought.

And Daniel —

Daniel was the only thing she had ever done that she could not justify with arithmetic.

Her grandson was twenty-four, broad-shouldered, quick to laugh, with his grandfather's coloring and — God help them both — none of Martin's hollowness. He had come home from two years east with ideas. Fair contracts. Open books. He'd stood in this very study at Christmas, flushed with whiskey and sincerity, and said, *Grandmother, half these families are working land their grandparents owned. Doesn't that ever sit wrong on you?*

And Ada had looked at him across the fire and felt something crack behind her ribs that had nothing to do with the tumor, and she had said, *Sit down, boy, and let me tell you how a town gets built,* and she had told him the clean version. The version with vision in it. Hard times, hard bargains, a strong hand — the story every fortune tells about itself. And Daniel had listened, because he loved her, and love is the most credulous thing in the world, and she had watched him choose to believe her, and she had known — known with absolute clarity, the way you know a storm is coming by the smell of it — that someday he would find out she'd lied, and that the finding-out would cost him more than any lawsuit could ever cost her.

So she had gone looking for the ledger herself, that winter, meaning to burn it at last. Meaning to give him a clean empire or nothing.

It was gone.

* * *

The Hollis expedition. Eleven fools with grant money and a benefactor's check, paddling up her river with academic instruments and academic innocence. She had funded them herself — that was the elegant cruelty of it, she'd insisted on funding them, because a patron sees everything and owes nothing. And Hollis had found the cave, and the cave had held the tin box's twin: the *originals*, the transfer records with the witness affidavits attached, the ones Emmett had hidden twice over because even he had feared what a court would do with them.

And then Hollis had died, and the camp had scattered, and the ledger had vanished into the country like blood into sand.

Duke Sallow's reports came by shortwave every evening at seven, and Ada took them standing, because she refused to take bad news sitting down. Tonight's report was short. The ranger — Vasquez, the name meant nothing to her until Sallow added that his father had been one of the dispossessed, a rancher named Joaquín Vasquez who'd signed in '94 and drunk himself into the river in '09 — the ranger had reached the survivors. Six of them alive, holed up somewhere north of the narrows. One gunman lost at the canyon. Sallow's voice, as always, was flat as a table: positions, ammunition, weather, next move.

"Next move," Ada repeated, when he finished. She lowered herself into the chair by the radio — slowly; the pain made courtesy impossible some hours, and this was one of them. "Your next move is nothing."

A pause on the line. Static, and the river talking somewhere behind it. "Ma'am?"

"You've lost a man and gained a ranger, Mr. Sallow, and the ledger is still in the wind. Every hour your boys spend kicking canyons is an hour somebody teaches those six survivors to hate me properly. No." She turned her wedding ring — no, she didn't wear one; she turned the plain band she wore instead, a habit, a metronome. "You will stop hunting the survivors entirely."

"Then what am I hunting?"

"Nothing. You're going to do something harder. You're going to be *seen*. In town. At the store. Buying coffee, tipping your hat, being Duke Sallow, foreman, a man with no concerns whatsoever." She let the silence work for a moment. "Meanwhile I want lists. Families. Tomás Reyes has a daughter in Corlissville — the schoolteacher, the pretty one. Ines Okafor has a brother who works the south ditch crew. Find out who the others belong to. Mothers. Sisters. Sons. Anyone whose absence would make a scientist pick up a telephone."

Another pause, longer. She could hear him deciding whether to ask. To his credit, he asked. "Ma'am — you want me to threaten civilians? Women and children?"

"I want you to *visit* civilians," Ada said, "with flowers and concern and a reminder that the company store extends credit, and credit is a flexible instrument, and so are employment terms. Whether anyone feels threatened is a matter of temperament." She leaned forward, and the pain flared white down her left side, and she spoke through it evenly, because that was the trick of pain — it obeyed the voice. "Mr. Sallow. Look at the board. Those six people in the canyon have nothing I want. Their families have everything they want. A man will surrender any document on earth to keep his daughter out of a room with your face in it. We don't need guns. We need *leverage*, and leverage is just love with the price marked."

Static. Then, reluctantly: "And the ranger?"

"What about him?"

"He's got Corliss reasons to hate us. His father—"

"I know his father's name," Ada said, sharper than she intended. She smoothed it flat again. "Every Vasquez in the territory has a reason, and none of them ever did anything about it but drink. This one became a ranger, which means he believes in rules. Good. Rules can be waited out. Put a tail on him if you must, but touch nothing. If he delivers those people home and files his report, he becomes a hero with a paperwork trail, and paperwork is the one animal I've never failed to run to ground."

"And if he doesn't file his report? If he goes loud?"

Ada looked out the window. The light was going amber on the water tower, and the cottonwoods were letting go of their first leaves, one at a time, unhurried, certain of the season.

"Then he goes loud," she said, "and we learn what my father learned in 1894, which is that noise frightens townspeople far less than empty shelves. But it won't come to that. Men like that always hesitate at the line between justice and revenge. It's the hesitation I'm counting on."

She signed off, and sat alone in the study while the room filled with dusk.

* * *

That night she could not sleep, and so she did what she did on such nights: she worked. The ledgers of the present, the living ones — payroll, freight, the irrigation assessments — spread across the desk under the green lamp, columns marching, everything balanced, everything *honest*, God help her, the current books were honest to the penny, because she had insisted on it for thirty years. As if honesty could be applied retroactively. As if a clean surface proved anything about the foundation.

Daniel's photograph watched her from the shelf. Him at fourteen, on the gray horse, grinning like the world was a place that gave things away.

She thought: *I am doing this for you.* And then she heard the sentence as it actually sounded, stripped of the lamp's warm light, and it frightened her more than the tumor did. Because it wasn't true anymore, and maybe it had never been true, and the difference mattered. She was not protecting Daniel's future. She was protecting her *story* — the one she'd built brick by brick out of her father's sin and her own silence, the one where Ada Corliss was a hard woman in hard country who held a hard town together, and the ledger under the floorboards was just an old man's shame and not the blueprint of everything above it.

If the ledger surfaced, Daniel wouldn't lose money. Money was nothing; she could bury him in money. He would lose the *version of her he loved*. And she had discovered, in the long arithmetic of her dying, that she would rather burn the valley than let him look at her the way he would look at her — the way you look at a person you've caught lying, all the love draining out through the eyes and leaving a stranger standing there.

Love curdles, she thought. It doesn't spoil all at once. It curdles, slow, from the outside in, and the center stays sweet longest, and you keep drinking from the middle telling yourself it's fine.

Downstairs, faintly, the shortwave crackled. Sallow, checking in with the night man. Somewhere north, six frightened people and a ranger with his father's name were deciding what kind of trouble to be. Somewhere in a drawer in this very house was the list she'd ordered — Reyes, Okafor, and the rest, wives and daughters and brothers, ordinary names, ordinary loves — and by Friday her men would be standing on those doorsteps with their hats in their hands and their voices full of concern.

It was, she told herself, the humane option. No bullets. No funerals. Just pressure, applied where flesh was thinnest.

She wrote the note to Sallow herself, in her own hand, because some orders should carry a woman's handwriting — it made them harder to refuse and easier to deny. *No violence. Families only. Credit, employment, reputation. Nothing that bleeds.*

She sealed it, set it on the tray for morning delivery, and blew out the lamp.

In the dark, the tumor pulsed behind her eye like a second heart, patient as a creditor. Ada Corliss sat with her hands folded and did her final accounting honestly, since no one was watching: she had taken a stolen river and built three towns on it, and she had loved exactly one person cleanly in seventy-one years, and she was about to use that person's classmates' mothers as hostages.

Somewhere out in the dark, the river ran on toward the towns, free as ever, asking nothing, belonging to no one — and carrying, whether she willed it or not, the truth downstream toward daylight.

By Friday, she promised the dark, it would be stopped.

The dark did not answer. It never had.
