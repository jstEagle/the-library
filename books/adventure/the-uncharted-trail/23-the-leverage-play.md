## 23. The Leverage Play

*Tomás*

The message came in on the shortwave at dusk, in a voice Tomás Reyes had known for twenty years and had never once heard sound like this.

*"Papa. They took me from the store. I'm not hurt. They say — Papa, they say if you don't bring the book to Corlissville by Friday, they'll put me on a train east and I won't—" The voice broke, steadied. A man's voice came over hers then, smooth as river-polished stone. "You heard the girl, Reyes. Mrs. Corliss is generous with time. Don't make her generous twice."*

Then static, and the hiss of the canyon night filling the space where his daughter's breathing had been.

Tomás sat with the handset in his hand long after the channel went dead. Around him the fire cracked and spat, throwing orange light up the walls of the box canyon where six people and one ranger had made their stand against an empire. He was aware of them all going quiet, one by one — Ruth Ann setting down the willow bark she'd been grinding for Cass's fever, Peter freezing with a map half-folded in his lap, Ines looking at him with the particular stillness of a woman who already knew.

"Say it," he said to the radio, though no one was there. Then, louder, to the camp: "They have Lucía."

Ines closed her eyes. "And mine," she said. "Emilio didn't come back from the water run yesterday. I told myself he'd found work downriver." She opened her eyes, and they were dry and terrible. "He found Corliss."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, a canyon wren gave its descending note, indifferent as God.

Tomás thought about Lucía at eight years old, standing on the running board of his truck, one hand on the mirror, shouting *faster, Papá, faster* as they raced the rain home across the flats. He thought about her at nineteen, telling him she didn't need him to approve of the boy she loved, only to meet him. He thought about every mile he had ever driven away from her and how each one had felt like a small, survivable theft.

Now the bill had come due, and the currency was a leather ledger full of other people's stolen water.

"The book isn't worth her life," he said. His voice came out flat, practical, a guide's voice, and he hated himself for how easily he found it. "No book is worth anyone's life. We give it to them."

"No." Ines again, immediate. "We give it to them and they kill us anyway and publish nothing, because that is what Ada Corliss does with problems. Ask my brother what she does with problems. Oh — you can't. He's in a room somewhere being one."

"We don't know that they'll—"

"I know it," Ines said, "the way you know weather. I've read this sky my whole life."

Mara Hollis stood up from the far side of the fire. She had her father's jaw, Tomás had noticed before — the way it set when she'd decided something — but tonight it looked less like resolve than like a door slamming. "Nobody is giving them anything. My father died for—"

"Your father died," Tomás said, and the words came out crueler than he meant, or exactly as cruel as he meant, grief always finding the shortest path, "because he wanted to be a hero with someone else's family standing behind him. I dug his grave. I get to say it."

The silence after that was total. Even the fire seemed to lean back.

It was Silas Vasquez who moved first — not toward Tomás, not toward Mara, just rising, slow, the way he did everything, and putting another stick on the fire as though the argument were a pot that needed tending rather than a fuse that needed cutting.

* * *

*Ines*

Ines Okafor had spent eleven years learning to read plants the way other people read faces, and she had learned something in those years that no botany program taught: everything living negotiates. The saguaro rations its water. the creosote poisons the ground around its own children so they'll strike out farther. Nothing survives by holding still and hoping.

So when the camp fractured — Tomás pacing the eastern wall with his hat crushed in both hands, Mara sitting apart with her arms around her knees, Cass gray-faced and feverish on the litter, insisting in a hoarse voice that nobody sacrifice anything on his account, which everyone politely ignored — Ines did not hold still and hope. She walked to where Silas stood at the canyon mouth, watching the dark come down over the badlands, and she said what she'd been assembling since the radio went silent.

"You're the only one here who hasn't lost anything yet," she said. "That's why they're all looking at you like you're a judge. But I want you to understand what you're arbitrating before you open your mouth."

Silas didn't turn. "Tell me."

"Tomás would trade the truth for his daughter and never forgive himself, and the truth would die with him, and Ada Corliss would water three towns with it for another fifty years. Mara would trade her own life before the ledger and call it her father's legacy. Peter is doing arithmetic about photographs and guilt and coming out wrong every time. And I—" Her voice caught, and she let it, deliberately; she had decided years ago that hiding a crack was how a whole vessel failed. "I have a brother in a room somewhere, and I am the only person alive who knows he wet the bed until he was nine and I swore I'd never tell, and I keep thinking, what good is a secret now? What good has any secret ever done us?"

Silas turned then. In the last light his face was all planes and shadow, a topographic map of a man.

"So ask me the real question," Ines said.

"What's the real question?"

"Whether we can invent a third option before Friday, or whether we spend the next four days tearing each other apart and hand Corliss the victory she hasn't lifted a finger to win." She hugged her arms against the cooling air. "Because that's the play, ranger. That message wasn't aimed at Tomás. It was aimed at us. You don't threaten hostages to get a ledger. You threaten hostages to break the alliance holding the ledger. She doesn't even want the paper anymore. She wants us to do her work for her."

For a long moment Silas said nothing. Then he looked back out at the dark, and Ines watched something move through him — not softening, nothing so simple, more like a blade being turned so its edge faced outward instead of in.

"Elena Brandt," he said. "Judge Pell. Two hundred miles east." He was talking to himself as much as to her, laying stones across a river. "Sallow's got maybe fourteen men left after the crossing. He can't watch two hundred miles of territory and a town full of hostages at once. Nobody can. That's not his weakness." He looked at her. "That's ours. We're the ones who think we have to choose."

"And if we don't have to?"

"Then somebody has to be willing to look like they chose wrong." He pushed off the rock. "Get some sleep, Dr. Okafor. Tomorrow I'm going to ask this camp for something worse than surrender."

* * *

*Mara*

Mara slept badly, which was to say she slept in the way of soldiers and new widows — in shallow, armed increments, surfacing at every shift of wind, every settling stone. Once she woke to find Silas crouched at the fire, feeding it by hand, twig by twig, as if the size of the flame were a problem a patient man could solve forever.

She got up and sat beside him. Neither of them spoke for a while. That was the thing she had come to treasure most about him, she realized, watching his profile against the coals: he did not fill silence the way other men did, pouring words into it like water into a hole. He let silence be a place two people could stand.

"They have Lucía," she finally said. "A girl who used to braid my hair when I was seven and she was twelve, at expedition basecamp, while our fathers argued about grant budgets." Her throat tightened. "And Emilio, and my father's name still buried under a lie, and a ledger that could burn down everything Ada Corliss ever built — and we have one boat, four days, and a man named Sallow who keeps herding us like he's getting paid per mile."

"That's because he is getting paid per mile," Silas said. "Or was."

"What does that mean?"

Silas reached into his coat and brought out the dead gunman's paybook, the one he'd taken at the crossing fight, and held it so the firelight caught the pages. Cash amounts. Dates. No names anywhere — not the paymaster's, not the recipients'. Just numbers marching in neat columns, deaths rendered as line items.

"Sallow's seen this," Silas said. "I showed him at the parley. Watched him read it. A man like Sallow works deniable for a reason — the deniability is the product. Corliss pays cash so she can cut him loose the moment he costs more than he's worth." He closed the book. "Somewhere between the narrows and the crossing, Duke Sallow stopped being her dog and started being a stray. Strays are dangerous. But they're also hungry, and hunger can be pointed."

"You want to recruit him." Mara stared. "The man who shot Cass."

"I want to know which way he'll jump when the floor gives way. There's a difference." Silas poked the fire, and sparks climbed toward the stars like seeds sown upward. "But that's tomorrow's problem too."

Mara pulled her knees up. "My father used to say a problem shared is a problem halved. Tomás says a problem shared is a problem doubled, because now two people are worried." She managed something that was almost a smile. "They argued about it constantly. It was the only thing they ever fought about, besides me growing up."

Silas was quiet a moment. "They were both right," he said. "Depends whether the sharing makes the load lighter or just spreads it thinner. Most days I'd tell you I've spent twelve years making sure my load stayed mine precisely so it couldn't be spread onto anybody." He glanced at her, and the firelight did something to his eyes that she decided not to name yet. "Lately the arithmetic's changed."

Before she could answer — before she could decide whether she wanted to answer, whether answering was a door she could close again afterward — a boot scraped on the canyon rim, and Peter Liu slid down the scree toward them, breathless, waving a piece of paper like it was on fire.

* * *

*Peter*

Peter Liu had been composing his apology for three weeks, revising it nightly the way he revised everything, and in the end none of the drafts survived contact with the actual moment, because the actual moment arrived wearing a telegram.

"It came to the Brandt store," he gasped, reaching the fire. "Elena sent it up with the supply runner — the one who brings Ruth Ann's herbs. It's addressed to *the party in possession of the Hollis materials*, which is us, obviously, and—" He thrust it at Silas, hands shaking. "It's from my advisor. Professor Wrenn. At the university."

Silas unfolded it. Read it once. Read it again, slower, and Peter watched the ranger's face the way he'd once watched instrument needles during a storm — waiting for the needle to swing, trying to predict the direction.

"Read it aloud," Ines said, arriving with Tomás and Ruth Ann in tow, the whole camp converging now, gravity pulling everyone toward the fire and the paper.

Silas cleared his throat. "*Wrenn to Liu. Your film arrived safe. Developed all thirty-six exposures. Recognized material immediately given my own family history — grandfather ran sheep on the Rio Seco until '09. Have shown pages to two colleagues I trust absolutely. Also regret to inform you a man identifying himself as a Corliss agent visited my office Tuesday asking after Hollis expedition correspondence. I told him nothing. I have mailed duplicate prints to Judge Amos Pell, federal district, care of the courthouse, marked personal and confidential, and kept a second set hidden. Do what you judge right. The truth travels better with company. — E.W.*"

Nobody spoke. The fire cracked. Down the canyon, Cass coughed in his sleep, a wet, worrying sound.

"He mailed them," Peter whispered. "He mailed them without asking me. Without—" He stopped, because the sentence he'd been building — *without knowing what he'd exposed* — collapsed under its own weight. What had Wrenn exposed? The photographs were already exposed. Peter had exposed them, weeks ago, in a fit of academic diligence, mailing undeveloped film to a mentor like a good student, like a boy who still believed institutions were where you took important things to keep them safe.

"I did this," he said. "I led them to us. The agent visiting Wrenn's office — that's how they tracked the expedition's network, that's how they knew where we'd run, that's—" His voice cracked clean through. "Cass got shot because I wanted a good grade in archival method."

Ruth Ann crossed the fire circle and put her hand on the back of his neck, firm as a splint. "Boy," she said, in her medic's voice, the one that had talked Ferro through a fever and Cass through infection, "if I had a dollar for every person in this canyon carrying blame that isn't sized to them, I could buy Corlissville and turn it into a dance hall. Sit down."

Peter sat.

But it was Tomás who leaned forward into the firelight, and it was Tomás whose face had changed — Peter saw it happen, saw the despair drain out of the old guide's expression and something harder and brighter flood in behind it, and understood, with a lurch, that the telegram hadn't been news to Tomás at all. It had been ammunition.

"Do you see it?" Tomás said. "Do any of you see what the old man just did?" He tapped the telegram. "Duplicate prints. Hidden. Already mailed to Pell. Corliss can take the ledger off us tomorrow, burn it in front of our eyes, put every one of us in the ground — and it changes nothing. The proof is already out of her reach. It's been out of her reach since a professor in a cardigan licked an envelope."

"She doesn't know that," Mara breathed.

"She can't know that," Ines said. "Wrenn told the agent nothing. As far as Ada Corliss sits tonight, the only copies of that ledger in the world are the ones we're holding." She laughed — a short, fierce, disbelieving sound. "She's threatening hostages for a document that's already worthless to her. We could hand it over and lose nothing except—"

"Except the moment we hand it over," Silas said quietly, "she learns there's nothing to fear from taking it. And then the hostages stop being leverage and start being witnesses." He stood, and the whole camp rose with him in some way — attention, weight, the sense of a compass needle finding north. "No. We don't hand over anything. But Tomás is right that we can't trade the original either, and Ines is right that the threat to Lucía and Emilio is real, and Mara's right that we're being herded, and Peter—" he looked at the young man, and something in his expression eased a degree, "—Peter just handed us the map out, same as he handed Corliss the map in. Fair trade."

"How?" Tomás demanded, raw. "Four days, ranger. My daughter has four days. Give me something better than a map metaphor."

Silas looked around the fire: the botanist, the guide, the wrangler, the medic, the grad student, the girl with her father's jaw, the injured man sleeping his fevered sleep. Ten names on a deed that didn't exist yet. He took a breath, and Peter, watching, thought: *he's about to do the thing he does, the impossible arithmetic, the third option.*

"Here's what we know," Silas said. "Corliss expects a cornered animal to do one of two things — bolt for the judge, or crawl to the town and bargain. Every plan she's built, every man Sallow's positioned, covers those two choices. So we stop choosing." He crouched and dragged a stick through the dirt, drawing fast: the river, the canyonlands, the long dead ground east, the town, the distant courthouse two hundred miles away. "Two parties. One loud, one silent. One carries something that looks like the prize and runs straight down her throat, buying days, pulling her guns east. The other goes nowhere near the judge — goes to Corlissville itself, into the teeth of it, because nobody guards the door they assume you'd never knock on. We get Lucía and Emilio out ourselves."

Tomás went white. "Into the town? With my daughter fifty feet from—"

"Fifty feet from the only place her guards will never expect us," Silas said. "Sallow learned our routes, our habits, our caution. He's never once seen us do anything stupid." A ghost of grim humor crossed his face. "Tomorrow we show him stupid."

Mara was already studying the dirt map, her mind visibly racing ahead along the lines Silas had drawn. "The bait party needs someone who can drive like a liar and read terrain fast," she said. "Someone you trained."

"Mara—"

"You said it yourself at the narrows. Student surpasses teacher. Put me on the bait run." She met his eyes, and neither of them blinked. "You lead the ledger party. Into town. You're the one Elena Brandt trusts. You're the one with the surname those workers remember."

Silas looked at her a long moment — and Peter, watching, saw the exact instant the ranger stopped arguing with her and started planning around her, the way water stops arguing with a boulder and starts moving past it.

"Cass stays with Ruth Ann and Ferro at the fallback camp," Silas said, the plan assembling itself aloud, gaining mass and speed. "Tomás comes with me — he knows the town's back ways better than anyone alive. Ines, Peter — you're the bait party's brains. Mara drives. You leave tomorrow at first light, loud as a parade, headed east like fools for the judge." He straightened, wiped the dirt map away with his boot, and looked around the fire one final time.

"Ada Corliss thinks she's playing chess with a cornered piece," he said. "Friday morning, she finds out the board's bigger than hers."

Above the canyon rim, the stars wheeled on, indifferent as God — but down in the firelight, ten battered people leaned toward one another like a single organism deciding, against all evidence, to live.

And forty miles west, in a ranch house lit by a single lamp, a dying woman's telephone began to ring.
