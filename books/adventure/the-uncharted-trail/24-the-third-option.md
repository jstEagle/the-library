## 24. The Third Option

The fire had burned down to a red eye by the time Silas laid out the map.

He'd taken it from the dead gunman's saddlebag two nights ago — a survey map of the canyonlands east of the river, marked in pencil with circles where Sallow's men expected them to be. The circles were wrong now. The circles assumed they were still running together, one herd, easy to drive.

Silas spread the map flat on a slab of sandstone and weighted the corners with four stones. Around him, in the blue dark beyond the firelight, sat six people who had every reason to distrust any plan that came out of his mouth. Tomás Reyes with his arms crossed and his jaw set like he was chewing on something bitter. Ines Okafor with her notebook open on her knee, writing even now, because writing was how she kept from screaming. Peter Liu hunched forward like a man trying to make himself smaller than his guilt. Cass Duval lying propped against her saddle, arm bandaged, gray-faced but listening with the flat attention of someone who'd been shot once already this month and didn't intend to be surprised twice. Ruth Ann beside Ferro, who couldn't sit up without help but insisted on hearing it anyway. And Mara, kneeling at the edge of the stone, her father's compass in her hand, turning it over and over without looking at it.

"Here's what we know," Silas said. "Sallow's got maybe fourteen men left after the crossing. He's not chasing us. He's herding us. Every move we've made since the narrows, he's been a half-step ahead, funneling us toward ground he chooses." He tapped the pencil circles one by one. "Dead Horse Wash. The Split Rock. Redrock Canyon. He wants us boxed somewhere with one way in and one way out, and then he wants it over quick."

"And Ada Corliss wants it over quiet," Ines said. "Which means she's done being quiet. Which means whatever comes next won't be four men at dusk. It'll be all of them."

"It'll also be sloppy," Silas said. "Quiet costs money. Desperation doesn't. She's spending faster than she planned, and people who spend fast make mistakes."

Tomás spat into the fire. "You've been saying that for two days. Mistakes don't dig graves, ranger. Men do."

"I'm not saying wait for mistakes." Silas looked up and let the firelight hit his face so they could all see there was nothing clever hiding behind it. Just a tired man with a plan he hated. "I'm saying stop giving him one target."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere down the canyon, water moved over stones, patient as it had been for ten thousand years, indifferent to everything these people were about to decide.

"Two parties," Silas said. "We split."

Cass laughed — one dry bark, no humor in it. "There it is. Knew you'd get there eventually. Split's the oldest play there is, and it's the one that gets everybody killed separate instead of together."

"Not split to run," Silas said. "Split to *lie*. One party goes loud. Vehicles, dust, radio chatter if we can fake it — driving straight east toward Judge Pell's courthouse like the whole ledger operation is riding in the back of that truck. Sallow can't ignore it. He can't risk letting the real thing reach a federal judge, so he takes every man he has and chases the noise."

"And the other party?" Ruth Ann asked quietly.

"Walking. Cross-country. Forty miles of dead ground north of the canyonlands — no water, no shade, no trails, nothing Sallow's trackers can read because nobody in their right mind crosses it." He traced the line with his finger, across the blank space on the map where the surveyors hadn't bothered to draw anything. "The ledger goes that way. On foot. Slow, hard, invisible. Four days if the weather holds. Five if it doesn't."

"The ledger party reaches Pell first," Ines said slowly. He could see her turning it over, testing the joints of it the way she tested a theory. "The bait party gets chased through hell for nothing—"

"Not for nothing," Silas said. "For time. For every hour Sallow spends chasing dust is an hour the ledger gets further from him. And when he finally runs the bait party to ground—" He stopped. Looked at the fire.

"When he runs them to ground," Cass finished, "what exactly does he find?"

Silas didn't answer right away, and the silence did the answering for him.

"No," Tomás said. Flat. Final. A door closing. "No. I've buried enough people on this trip. I'm not handing you a sacrifice and calling it arithmetic."

"That's not what I said."

"It's what the plan says." Tomás leaned forward, firelight carving the deep lines of his face. "I've known men like you my whole life, Vasquez. Men who volunteer for the dying part because they think it's the only honest thing left to offer. My friend Hollis was one. He stood up in front of all of us and said *I'll take it public* and three days later we put river stones on his chest." His voice cracked on the last word and he let it. "So no. Nobody drives that truck as a dead man walking. We find another way or we don't go."

"There is no other way," Silas said. "I've looked. Two days of looking. Sallow's too good and too many. If we stay together, we all die together, and the ledger dies with us, and Ada Corliss wins the same way her father won — by making sure nobody lives long enough to say otherwise." He spread his hands. "This is the only version where more than half of us see the judge's courthouse."

"More than half," Cass repeated. "Generous odds, ranger."

"Cass." It was Mara. She hadn't spoken since he'd laid out the map, and when she did, her voice came out low and steady and older than her years. "Shut up a second."

Cass shut up.

Mara set her father's compass down on the stone, next to the map, next to the blank country where the ledger would cross. She looked at Silas for a long moment, and he watched her do the thing she'd been doing since the day he'd found her tracks by the river: reading him. Reading the terrain of him. Looking for the lie or the flinch or the tell.

"You're planning to lead the bait run yourself," she said.

"Yes."

"Because you're the best driver, and the best shot, and you know the canyonlands better than Sallow does."

"Yes."

"And because part of you thinks it's what you're for." She said it without cruelty, which made it worse. "The man who measures his life in miles. The man whose father the river took. You've been volunteering for the dying part since before any of us met you."

"Mara—"

"I'll lead the bait run."

The words landed in the camp like a stone dropped down a well. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"No," Silas said.

"You don't get to say no. This isn't your expedition." She knelt up straighter, and he saw her father in her then — the same set of the shoulders, the same refusal to look away from a hard fact. "Think it through, ranger. Actually think it, instead of just deciding you're the one who gets to die. Who does Sallow want? Not you. You're an obstacle. He wants *me* — Ada's orders, the girl who saw the cave, the last Hollis. He wants me alive, probably, because a dead witness is a martyr and a captured one can be questioned. If I lead the bait run, he follows it with everything he has, because he thinks the prize is in the cab. If *you* lead it, he might smell it. He knows you'd never leave the ledger. He knows you'd stay with the real thing." She tapped the map. "Your face on that road buys us hours. Mine buys days."

"She's right," Ines said softly.

"Ines—"

"She's right, Silas. I hate it, and I'm going to argue with myself about it all night, but she's right. Sallow's intelligence is about targets, not terrain. He's hunting a girl and a ledger. Put the girl where he expects her and the ledger where he isn't, and every instinct he has works for us instead of against us." Ines closed her notebook. "It's better tradecraft. That's the whole ugly truth of it."

"Then I ride with her," Tomás said. "If she leads the bait, I'm in the truck."

"Papá's daughter is in town," Ruth Ann reminded him gently. "And Ines's brother. If the bait run fails, somebody who can talk to those families needs to be standing in front of Judge Pell when the warrants come down. You and Ines both. That's not negotiable either."

Tomás looked like a man being pulled apart by horses. Silas knew the feeling intimately.

"Then it's settled," Mara said. "I take the truck. Cass rides shotgun — don't argue, you're the only one of us who can drive eight hours on a busted arm and swear the whole time, and Sallow's men know your face, which helps sell it. Peter goes with us."

Every head turned to Peter, who flinched like he'd been struck.

"Why me?" he asked.

"Because you're the one who mailed the film," Mara said, and there was no accusation in it anymore — that debt had been paid and forgiven somewhere between the narrows and the crossing — "which means you're the one who knows what Corliss already has. If they intercept us, you're the one who can tell Pell's people exactly what's been compromised and what hasn't. The photographs are half our evidence. Their custodian rides with the decoy, because nobody hides the custodian with the treasure." She almost smiled. "Also, you're the lightest, and the truck will be carrying water for a chase."

Peter opened his mouth. Closed it. Nodded, pale but steady. Something had changed in him since the crossing — since Cass had taken a bullet meant for him. He carried it differently now. Not lighter. Straighter.

"So the ledger party," Ruth Ann said, counting on her fingers, "is you, Silas. Me — because Ferro can't walk forty miles and someone has to keep him alive doing it. Ferro. And Tomás."

"And me," Ines said.

Ruth Ann raised an eyebrow. "Forty miles of dead ground, Doctor Okafor. No shade. Your brother's in town."

"My brother is in town *because* of what I carry," Ines said. "The fastest way to get him released is to make the evidence worthless to hold him over. I'm not sitting at a courthouse waiting to hear whether my family bled for my convictions. I carry the ledger myself or none of this means anything." She picked up the oilskin-wrapped bundle from beside her bedroll and held it against her chest like a child. "Besides. I'm the only one who's memorized every page. If the worst happens, the ledger survives in here." She tapped her temple. "Corliss can burn paper. She can't burn what I know."

Silas looked around the fire at what was left of the argument and found there wasn't any. There was just the plan, ugly and necessary, wearing the faces of people who had volunteered for it — every one of them, volunteered, nobody assigned, nobody sacrificed by committee. It was the only kind of plan he'd ever have agreed to, and it was still the hardest thing he'd ever drawn on a map.

"Rules," he said, because rules were the last thing he had to give. "No fires after tomorrow night. No shots unless it's life or death — a gunshot carries five miles on dead ground and tells Sallow everything. Water rationing starts now; we cache a reserve at the north rim tonight and pick it up on the way through. If the ledger party makes contact with anything bigger than two men, we scatter and reform at the Brandt store in Corlissville — Elena Brandt knows faces, she'll collect strays." He looked at Mara last. "Bait run breaks for the courthouse the moment you clear the canyonlands. Don't be brave past the point of usefulness. Your job is to be *seen*, not caught. The second Sallow commits his whole force, you turn south and run for Pell. Let him chase empty desert."

"And if he doesn't commit?" Mara asked.

"He will." Silas held her eyes. "Because he thinks you're the ledger."

She nodded slowly. Then, quieter, just for him across the fire: "Come back. Both parties. All ten names, ranger. That was the deal."

"That was the deal," he said, and hated how much he wanted it to be true, and hated more that wanting things had started to feel possible again after twelve years of measuring his life in safe, lonely miles.

* * *

They broke camp in the last hour of dark, working by feel and starlight, packing in the silence of people who understood that sound traveled.

At the fork — the track east toward the canyonlands, the blank slope north toward the dead ground — they paused, engines off, breath steaming. Mara came to Silas's horse in the gray light. Up close he could see she hadn't slept, and neither had he, and neither pretended otherwise.

"If this goes wrong," she said, "if they catch the bait run — there's a copy of the page photographs sewn into my jacket lining. Peter made a duplicate set before he mailed the film. Pell needs to know it exists. Tell him it's under the left shoulder seam."

"You're telling me this like you're not coming back."

"I'm telling you this because prepared people come back." She reached up and gripped his forearm, ranger-fashion, the grip he'd taught her on the trail a lifetime ago. "Twelve years of miles, Silas. Time to find out what zero feels like."

She climbed into the truck. Cass turned the engine over. Headlights swept the canyon wall once, found the northern slope where four figures stood with a litter and a wrapped ledger and forty miles of nothing ahead of them, and then the taillights dropped away eastward, dust rising gold in the first sun, loud and deliberate and full of lies.

Silas watched until the dust settled. Then he turned north, shouldered his share of the litter pole, and led his people up into the blank country, toward the judge, toward the truth, toward whatever waited on the other side of the dead ground.

Behind them, far to the southeast, a second dust plume lifted — Sallow's whole force, exactly as promised, chasing the girl who wasn't running away at all.

She was leading.
