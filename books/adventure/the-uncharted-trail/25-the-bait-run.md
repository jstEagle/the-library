## 25. The Bait Run

The plan sounded simple when Silas said it out loud, the way all terrible plans sound simple. Drive loud. Drive visible. Let them see you.

"Let them *chase* me," Mara had said.

"That's the idea."

"And the ledger party walks forty miles of dead ground while every gun in the territory is looking at my taillights."

Silas had looked at her for a long moment across the fire, and she'd understood that the look was the whole conversation — that he knew exactly what he was asking, and that asking it of her specifically was not an accident. He could have given the bait run to Cass, who knew engines better than anyone alive. He could have driven it himself. Instead he stood there with his hat in his hands like a man at a funeral and said, "You know the canyonlands now. Better than they do. Maybe better than me."

That had been two days ago. Now she was doing forty-five miles an hour down a washboard track with three trucks behind her and dust boiling up like a signal fire, and she had time to think about how much she hated him for being right.

* * *

They'd built the convoy out of everything that still ran: her father's expedition truck, the supply rig they'd salvaged from the narrows wreck, and Cass's ancient flatbed with a tarp over its bed to suggest cargo worth chasing. Three vehicles, four people — herself, Cass riding shotgun in the lead truck because he refused on principle to let anyone else drive his flatbed, Peter in the middle truck, and Ruth Ann in the rear as their medic, though Mara prayed to nothing in particular that the rear would be the safest position and knew better than to believe it.

The decoy ledger sat in a lockbox under Mara's seat: Tomás's journal wrapped in oilcloth, weighted to feel right, sealed with wax from Ruth Ann's kit. If the worst happened, whoever opened it would find weather notes and a pressed wildflower collection. It was the best lie they had, and lies were currency now.

"You want to know the trick," Cass said beside her, one wrist draped over the wheel, watching the mirrors more than the road. "It ain't speed. Anybody can go fast out here. You go fast, you break an axle, you're done. The trick is making them spend more than you spend."

"Spend what?"

"Everything. Fuel. Patience. Parts. Every mile they chase you costs them something they can't get back, and every mile you choose costs you nothing, because you chose it." He tapped the dash. "Your ranger teach you that?"

"He taught me to read country. The spending part I'm learning now."

Cass grinned, gold tooth flashing. "Then you're ahead of where I was at your age."

In the side mirror, the dust plume behind them had resolved into shapes. Two trucks, maybe three, running wide abreast the way men do when they think numbers are the same thing as tactics. Sallow's main force. Exactly what Silas had promised: word had gone up the river overnight, planted through Elena Brandt's shop boy, that the Hollis girl was driving east with the ledger, alone but for a skeleton crew, trying to outrun the news. Ada Corliss couldn't let that reach Judge Pell. She'd throw everything at the road.

Everything except the four people walking north through dead ground with the real pages sewn into their coat linings.

Mara checked the mirror again and made herself look away before fear could set up housekeeping in her chest. Fear was a passenger. Her father used to say that — back when he was alive, back when the world made sense — *fear rides along, sweetheart, but it doesn't get to drive.* She hadn't understood it then. She understood it now, at fifty miles an hour, with bullets not yet flying but certainly loading.

"They're closing," Peter called from the middle truck, voice pitched high over the engine noise. "Half a mile. Maybe less."

"Hold your line," Cass said. "First lesson: don't run from them yet. A chase needs a chaser who believes he's winning. You give him that belief early, he commits. You give it to him late, he gets smart." He glanced at her. "Second lesson is yours. Where?"

She'd spent the whole night before with the topographic maps spread across the tailgate, Silas crouched beside her, tracing routes with a burnt stick. Not roads — the canyonlands barely had roads. Routes. Lines of country that went where wheels could follow, and lines that only pretended to.

"Waxwing Draw," she said. "Six miles northeast. It runs parallel to the east road for eight miles, then dog-legs south through the Narrows Cut. Anybody following has to commit to the draw or lose us entirely, and if they commit—"

"The floor floods in spring. Cobbles the size of skulls. Suspension-eater." Cass nodded slowly. "And after the cut?"

"After the cut it gets interesting."

* * *

Interesting began when the first shot came.

It cracked off the rocks to their left — not aimed well, just announced, a statement of intent — and Mara's whole body flinched toward the sound before her mind caught up. Then a second shot, closer, and glass starred in the middle truck's rear window, and Peter made a sound that wasn't quite a word.

"Now?" she asked.

"Now," Cass said, and she hauled the wheel left off the track and into the open mouth of Waxwing Draw.

The transition was violence. The truck dropped off hardpan into loose sand and the world became a shaking, roaring blur of tan walls rising on either side, close enough that she could have touched them, the sky reduced to a blue ribbon overhead. Behind her the other two trucks followed — she heard Cass muttering coordinates to them over the radio, short clipped calls, *left line, stay off the center cobbles, Peter watch your oil temp* — and above it all the engine screaming in a register she felt in her teeth.

The first pursuer followed. Of course he did. The second hesitated — she saw it in the mirror, saw the truck slow at the draw's mouth, its driver clearly weighing the math — and then committed too, because losing sight of the quarry was worse than any broken axle.

"Both in," Cass said, sounding pleased about it. "Now we make them pay rent."

Mara drove.

She drove the way Silas had taught her to walk country: eyes moving, never fixed, reading three hundred yards ahead the way other people read a sentence. The draw floor was exactly what the maps promised — flood-cobbled, rutted, treacherous — and she learned its rhythm inside a mile, found the smooth lanes between the skull-sized stones, felt the truck's weight shift and answered it with her hands before her head finished the thought. There was a moment, somewhere in the third mile, when the fear in her chest changed key. It didn't leave. It just stopped being in charge. What replaced it was something colder and cleaner, almost mathematical: *this line works, that line doesn't, they'll take the bad line because they don't know it's bad.*

The first pursuer took the bad line.

She heard it more than saw it — a bang like a rifle shot that wasn't a rifle shot, the unmistakable report of a front tire detonating on cobble edge, and then the grinding shriek of a rim plowing stone. In her mirror, the truck slewed sideways in a rooster tail of dust and stopped being a threat and started being scenery.

"One down," Cass said. "Don't gloat. Gloating costs attention."

"I wasn't gloating."

"Your face was gloating."

* * *

The Narrows Cut was where the draw turned mean. The walls pinched together until the truck's mirrors nearly kissed both sides, and the floor rose in a series of ledges that had to be taken at angles Silas had made her memorize — *left wheel high, momentum through, don't stop, whatever you hear underneath you, do not stop* — because stopping in the cut meant becoming a roadblock in a shooting gallery.

Something did hear underneath her. She felt it through the frame, a heavy scraping along the differential, metal on rock, a sound like the truck being eaten slowly. She didn't stop. She fed it more throttle and the sound became a scream and then, mercifully, became silence, and then they were through, popping out of the cut into a broad red amphitheater of a valley with afternoon light pouring into it like honey.

Behind them, the second pursuer's headlights appeared at the cut's far entrance. And stopped.

Mara watched the mirror, waiting. The truck backed up, turned around, and withdrew the way it had come.

"It quit," Peter radioed.

"No," Cass said. "It reported. We just cost them two trucks and they've still got our heading. That driver's going to find Sallow and tell him exactly where we are, and Sallow's going to stop chasing and start *herding*. He did it at the ferry crossing; he'll do it again. He won't try to catch us. He'll try to be where we're going."

"So we don't go where we're going," Mara said.

Cass looked at her sideways. "Meaning?"

She pulled the map from between her knees — she'd been studying it even while driving, folding it smaller and smaller until she could read it one-handed against the wheel — and spread it across the console. "Sallow knows the east road. He knows Judge Pell is east. Everything he does assumes we're pushing east. So." Her finger traced a line west of their position, up through a maze of canyons marked in faded contour lines. "We go north. Through the Labyrinth. There's a stock trail — old cattle route, pre-war, probably not on his maps. It comes out at Salt Fork Flats. From there we can swing east again on ground he won't have covered."

"The Labyrinth." Cass said the name the way other men said *cancer.* "I ran sheep through there thirty years ago. Lost two animals and half my hair. Water's poison in half those holes and the other half's dry."

"Silas says there's a spring at Anvil Rock. He marked it." She tapped the pencil cross on the map, small and precise, and felt something tighten in her chest that wasn't fear. He'd marked it for her. Before the split, before the bait run was even proposed, he'd sat by the fire and marked water sources along every possible route, and she understood now that he'd been planning for this since before anyone asked him to — that some part of him had always intended to hand her the country itself, like an inheritance.

"Cass," she said. "How much fuel?"

He checked the gauge without looking away from the trail ahead. "Enough for the flats if we don't idle. Not enough for mistakes."

"Then we won't make any."

He laughed, short and rough. "God help me, you sound just like him."

* * *

They ran the Labyrinth through the last hours of daylight, and it was everything the name promised and worse. The stock trail existed mostly as a rumor — a shelf of packed earth no wider than the truck, cut into canyon walls, switchbacking up and down formations that seemed to have been stacked by a drunk god. Twice Mara stopped and walked the next section on foot first, checking the shelf's edges, exactly as Silas had drilled into her: *if you wouldn't bet your life on the ground, don't bet the truck on it.* Once she found the shelf crumbled away entirely and spent forty minutes building the crossing back with stones and brush while Peter held the light and Cass watched the back trail and nobody complained, because complaining cost breath and breath was budgeted now.

At full dark they reached Anvil Rock — a black monolith rising out of a fold in the earth, unmistakable even by starlight, exactly where Silas's pencil cross said it would be. The spring ran cold and faintly mineral beneath a lip of stone. They filled every container they had. Ruth Ann made everyone drink on schedule whether they wanted to or not. Mara drank last, standing at the water with the map folded in her pocket, and let herself feel, for exactly sixty seconds, the full weight of what she was carrying: her father dead in the shallows, six friends scattered and hunted, a man she'd held at arrow-point five weeks ago now trusting her with the only plan that kept them all breathing.

Then the sixty seconds ended, and she capped the canteen, and went back to work.

* * *

Salt Fork Flats at dawn was a sea of pale grass under a sky turning pink at the seams, and for twenty minutes it felt almost like safety. Then Cass, who had slept in ninety-second increments with his hand on the keys, said quietly: "Dust. North-northeast. Moving to cut our line east."

Mara climbed onto the flatbed's bed rail and looked. Two trucks, maybe three, fanned out wide, running not at them but *ahead* of them — herding, exactly as predicted. Somewhere beyond that dust was Duke Sallow, and somewhere beyond Sallow was whatever Ada Corliss's money could buy on a day's notice.

"They're between us and the east road," Peter said, climbing up beside her, binoculars raised. "If we push east now we drive straight into them."

"If we push north we run out of country in sixty miles."

Ruth Ann appeared at the tailgate, calm as ever, hands stained with willow-bark tincture. "There's a third option," she said. "There usually is. Your ranger taught you that too, didn't he?"

Mara stared at the dust, at the pink sky, at the map in her hands, and felt the old grief and the new purpose settle into the same shape, one weight, carryable. Her father had died believing the truth was worth anything. She was starting to believe it too — but believing wasn't enough. It had to arrive. It had to reach a courthouse two hundred miles east, and every mile between here and there was currently owned by men paid to stop it.

"We split the difference," she said slowly, the idea assembling itself as she spoke. "We don't run east and we don't run north. We go to ground. There's an old line shack on the Salt Fork — Silas mentioned it, patrol cabin, abandoned. We put the trucks inside the wash below it, kill the engines, and let them spend the whole day searching empty flats. Every hour they search is an hour the ledger party walks. Come tonight, we move east through the low ground, quiet, no lights."

"And if they find the shack?" Peter asked.

"Then they find three people and a box of pressed flowers," Mara said, "and by the time they figure that out, it won't matter."

Cass was already turning the truck toward the river line, grinning that gold-toothed grin. "Pressed flowers," he said. "I have got to start lying better."

They dropped into the wash as the sun cleared the horizon, and behind them the dust plumes wheeled and searched and burned fuel over empty ground, and Mara Hollis sat in the shade of a dead man's patrol cabin with her father's map on her knees, teaching herself the last lesson Silas hadn't gotten to:

The best terrain in any fight is time.

And she meant to own all of it.
