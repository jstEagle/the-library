## 26. Cross-Country

The dead ground began where the mapmaker's ink gave out.

Silas had told them about it the night before, crouched over the survey chart by firelight, his finger tracing a blank stretch east of the narrows. "Forty miles," he said. "No springs marked. No shade worth the name. The old sheep herders called it the Tablas Secas — the dry boards. They crossed it in winter, at night, and they still lost animals."

"And we cross it in summer," Cass said from the litter, "in daylight, because I'm too heavy to carry in the dark."

"You're not heavy," Ruth Ann said, adjusting the sling beneath his leg. "You're load-bearing."

Cass laughed, then winced, then laughed again anyway, because that was who Cass Duval was — a man who treated pain like a rude stranger at the door, something to be acknowledged and then ignored until it went away or didn't. The fever had broken in the night, thanks to Ruth Ann's willow bark and boiled water and sheer stubbornness, but the bullet wound in his shoulder was still angry, still weeping, and every mile of litter-carry would cost him blood he didn't have.

Silas had done the arithmetic three times. It never came out comfortable. Forty miles at two miles an hour with a litter rotation of eight carriers — no, seven, because Ferro's ribs were still strapped and Peter's hands were soft as a scholar's — meant twenty hours of walking. Twenty hours in country where the temperature would hit a hundred and ten by noon and the nearest water was a mudhole forty miles behind them and a river forty miles ahead.

They'd filled every container they owned at the last seep. Eleven gallons, plus what Ruth Ann had boiled and cooled in the canvas water bags. For eight people and the long light of a summer day, it was enough if nothing went wrong.

Silas had been a ranger long enough to know that something always went wrong.

* * *

They started at four in the morning, in the blue-black hour when the desert gives back the cold it borrowed from the stars. Silas led. Mara walked at his shoulder, and he'd stopped pretending he minded.

The first ten miles were almost kind. Hardpan and gravel, the ground firm under the litter poles, the eastern horizon slowly kindling from charcoal to copper. Tomás took the first litter shift on the left pole with Silas, and for a long time neither of them said anything, which was its own kind of language. Men who had spent weeks not trusting each other didn't fix it with words. They fixed it with miles.

The sun came up the way it always did out here — not rising so much as arriving, all at once, like a door kicked open. By seven the heat was a hand on the back of the neck. By nine it was a weight on the chest.

They walked in silence because silence saved breath and breath saved water. Ruth Ann called the drinking schedule, and Silas enforced it, and when Peter tried to skip his ration — Silas saw him pour a little of his cup back into the jug, thinking no one noticed — Silas took the cup from his hand, refilled it from his own ration, and handed it back.

"Drink it all," he said. "You faint, we stop. We stop, we die. Your water is the team's water. That's not charity, it's logistics."

Peter drank it all.

By noon the country had turned against them completely. The hardpan gave way to a chaos of dry washes and sun-cracked clay, and the litter poles caught on every hummock, and the carriers rotated every half mile because shoulders gave out faster than legs. Ferro walked with one hand pressed to his strapped ribs and never once complained, which Silas noted and filed away. Ines had wrapped a strip of canvas around her head and under her chin, Bedouin-style, and marched with her eyes fixed on the horizon like a woman reading a book only she could see. Ruth Ann counted steps under her breath. Cass, fever-flushed and gray-lipped, insisted on walking the flats and only lying down for the rough ground, and they let him, because a man's dignity was a kind of medicine too.

It was on the fourth hour, in the worst of the glare, that Silas saw the bones.

They were cattle bones, mostly — a skull with the horn cores still attached, a ribcage collapsed into a wooden lattice, the desiccated hide still clinging like old wallpaper. A cow that had wandered off the herders' route, or been left behind, and had walked in circles until the circles closed.

Mara stopped beside it. "That's what's out here waiting for us."

"That's what's out here that gave up," Silas said. "Different animal."

She looked at him. Sweat had cut clean channels through the dust on her face, and her eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "You believe that, or you just say it?"

"I say it because it's true, and I believe it because I have to." He adjusted the litter pole on his shoulder. "Out here, the story you tell yourself is a supply. Same as water."

She almost smiled. "Then tell me a good one."

"Forty miles," he said. "That's the whole story. Everything after that is somebody else's problem."

* * *

The bad hour came at three in the afternoon, as the bad hours always did — not at the peak of heat but just after, when the body had spent everything it had budgeted for the climb and the summit turned out to be a lie.

Cass went down.

Not collapsed — he'd been walking — but his knees buckled on a slope of broken shale and he sat down hard, and when Ruth Ann got to him his eyes were unfocused and his skin was hot and dry as the ground itself.

"He's past heat exhaustion," she said, and her voice was flat in the way that scared Silas more than panic would have. "He's tipping into heat stroke. I need shade. I need water I don't have. And I need him horizontal for at least an hour."

"There's no shade," Peter said, looking around at the naked country as if accusing it.

"There's shade," Silas said. He'd been reading the ground all day, the way his father had taught him before his father had anything left to teach. "There." He pointed to a dry wash a quarter mile east. "The cut banks are six feet deep. The west wall throws shadow all afternoon. It's not comfort. It's survival."

They got Cass into the wash. Ruth Ann stripped his shirt, poured their precious water over his head and neck — Silas watched eleven gallons of arithmetic shrink and made himself not flinch — and worked the willow bark and the wet cloth and the low, steady murmur of a medic talking a man back from the edge. Ines and Ferro rigged a litter-awning from the canvas groundcloth, and Mara and Tomás took turns fanning him with their hats.

For an hour, the expedition did nothing but keep one man alive, and Silas understood that this was the real work. Not the miles. This.

He climbed the wash bank to keep watch — habit more than necessity; Sallow's men were miles behind and on the wrong trail, thanks to the bait run — and found Tomás already there, sitting with his back against the caliche, his hat over his eyes.

Silas stood a moment, then sat down beside him. The caliche was hot even in the shade. Below them, Ruth Ann's voice rose and fell like a psalm.

"You've been avoiding me," Tomás said, without moving the hat.

"I've been walking."

"Forty miles is a long time to walk and not talk." Tomás lifted the hat and looked at him. He was sixty-some years old, leathery as a saddle, with eyes that had guided rivermen and scientists and now fugitives through country that killed the careless. "You've had something to say to me since the grave by the camp. I'd rather hear it now than have it come out sideways later."

Silas looked out at the dead ground. The heat shimmered off it in silver sheets, and the horizon wavered like a mirage, and somewhere under all that shimmer was the truth he'd been carrying since the camp of stones.

"You dug that grave," Silas said.

"I did."

"River stones on top. Neat. Deliberate. A marker, not a hiding place." He turned to face the older man. "I've buried people, Tomás. I know the difference between a man hiding a body and a man burying a friend. But I also know the grave was shallow, and the records were burned, and the radio was smashed, and six people ran in six directions. From where I stood, it looked like a cover-up. And the only man at the center of it was you."

Tomás nodded slowly, as if Silas had confirmed something he'd already priced in. "And you decided I was the one who killed him."

"I decided I didn't know. Which out here is worse."

"It is," Tomás said. "It's worse." He was quiet a moment. Below them, Cass groaned, and Ruth Ann's voice steadied him. "You want to know why the grave was shallow."

"I want to know everything. But start there."

"Because I dug it in the dark, with a camp shovel, in ground that's caliche down to eighteen inches, and I dug until my hands bled and I couldn't go deeper." Tomás's voice didn't waver, but his hands, Silas noticed, had closed into fists on his knees. "And I put river stones on top because Hollis loved this river. He used to say it was the only honest witness in the territory. I wanted the river to have him."

"Then why run? Why burn the records? Why scatter?"

Tomás took off his hat entirely and turned it in his hands. "Because the man who found Hollis's body — the man who told us he'd died in a fall — was standing right there when I finished filling the grave in. And I realized, standing over that shallow hole with stones in my hands, that if he'd killed Hollis, I'd just buried the evidence where he could find it or destroy it. And if he hadn't killed him, then whoever did was still in camp, listening, watching, counting who believed the story." He looked up. "So I did the only thing a guide knows how to do. I got the living away from the dead ground. Fast, scattered, quiet. Burned what could be burned so the killer couldn't read what Hollis had found. Smashed the radio so nobody could call ahead and report where we'd gone. It wasn't a cover-up, Silas. It was a retreat. There's a difference, but I'll grant you it looks the same from the outside."

"Who told you it was a fall?" Silas asked. "Who found the body?"

Tomás's jaw worked. "That's the part I've been chewing on for three weeks," he said. "And I'll give it to you, all of it, tonight or tomorrow — when we're through this ground and I can say it out loud without my voice shaking. Because what I saw that morning, Silas — who I saw — it's going to change what you think about everything. About Hollis. About the Corlisses. About who's been hunting us." He put the hat back on. "It nearly broke me. I'm not going to hand it to you broken."

Silas studied him for a long moment. The old man's face was dust and sweat and sixty years of river sun, and behind the leathery calm there was something moving, something he was holding down with both hands.

"Tomás." Silas said his name the way you set a stone on a cairn. "I've spent three weeks deciding what you were. I'm done deciding. When you're ready, I'll listen. Until then, you carry the left pole and I'll carry the right, and that's enough."

Tomás looked at him, and something in the old man's face let go — not all at once, but the way a knot lets go, a little at a time, when the rope finally stops pulling. "Your father," he said quietly. "Emiliano Vasquez. I guided him up the Green twice, before the Corlisses took his water. He was the best tracker I ever saw, and I told him so, and he laughed and said tracking was just paying attention to what the country was trying to tell you." He paused. "He'd be proud of how you pay attention."

Silas's throat tightened. He looked away, out at the shimmering flats, and found he couldn't speak for a moment. When he could, all he said was, "Left pole in ten minutes. We're losing the light."

Tomás smiled, and it was the first real smile Silas had seen from him since the canyon. "Right pole," he said. "Always did prefer the left."

* * *

They made the last miles in the dark, by Silas's stars and Mara's stubbornness, the litter swaying between six sets of shoulders, Cass walking the flats again because he refused to be carried one yard farther than the ground required. The temperature finally fell. The stars came out in their terrible abundance, the Milky Way a river of its own running above the river they were walking toward, and somewhere around midnight Peter started humming, low and tuneless, and Ines picked it up, and then Mara, and it wasn't a song so much as a heartbeat the group made for itself, something to walk to, something that wasn't the wind.

Silas walked at the front with Tomás on the left pole and Mara on the right, and he let the rhythm take him. Twenty hours. Thirty-eight miles behind them. His feet had gone past pain into a numbness he recognized as the body's last courtesy. His water was a sloshing inch in the bottom of his canteen. And when the ground finally changed under his boots — when hardpan gave way to sand, and sand to the smell of cottonwood and wet silt — he almost didn't trust it.

But the river was there. Black and broad and murmuring in the dark, running free through the willows, and the party stumbled down the bank and fell on it like the starving, and Ruth Ann stood over them making them sip, sip, sip, because she was the only one among them who remembered that men dying of thirst could kill themselves drinking.

Silas knelt at the edge and let the water run through his fingers, cold and alive, and beside him Tomás did the same, and the old man said, quietly, "He was right, you know. Hollis. It's the only honest witness in the territory."

And then Tomás straightened up, wrung out his bandana, and looked at Silas with an expression Silas hadn't seen on him before — the look of a man stepping off a ledge he'd been standing on for three weeks.

"Build a fire," Tomás said. "Low, behind the cutbank, where the light won't carry. It's time I told you what I saw on the morning Hollis died. And who I saw standing over him."

Silas looked at the old man's face, and felt the night go still.
