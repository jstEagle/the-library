## 27. What Tomás Saw

They built the fire small, the way Tomás had taught all of them in the first week of the expedition — a fist of flame, a windbreak of stone, heat you had to sit close to share. Forty miles of dead ground lay ahead of them, and the ledger party had made camp in a fold of badlands that smelled of sage and cold sand, and now the whole of it came down to this: a fire the size of two cupped hands, and an old guide who had been carrying a story the way a mule carries a pack too heavy to shrug off.

Silas sat across from him. Mara sat beside Silas, close enough that their shoulders touched when one of them shifted, though neither of them seemed to notice they were doing it. Tomás noticed. He noticed everything now. That was the curse of what he'd seen — you couldn't unsee a thing, and after you'd seen it, you saw its shadow in every corner of the world.

"You don't have to do this tonight," Silas said. "We cross the flats tomorrow. You could tell it on the far side."

"The far side isn't promised to anybody." Tomás fed a stick of greasewood to the flame. "And I'm tired of carrying it. A man can carry a pack too long and forget he was ever without it."

Ines sat to his left with her notebook closed on her knee. Ruth Ann had Ferro settled and sleeping, his bandaged shoulder propped against a saddle, and she'd come to sit at the fire's edge with her hands wrapped around a tin cup she wasn't drinking. Peter hovered at the dark rim of the light, and Cass stood watch on the rise above them, though Tomás had seen the wrangler angle herself so she could hear.

"Start wherever it starts," Mara said. Her voice was steady. Her hands weren't. "Start with the cave."

Tomás looked into the fire. The fire looked back, the way fire does, with all its old indifference.

* * *

"The cave was three days' walk above the last supply cache," he said. "My father ran sheep through this country when I was a boy, and he told me once about a hollow in the limestone where the water ran cold even in August. Hollis knew about it too — I'd told him the story around a campfire, the way you tell stories, not thinking they're maps. But he was a scientist. He heard *map*. So on the fourth of the month we went up to look."

He could still see it. That was the thing nobody warned you about memory — it didn't fade like the old men said. It sharpened. Every year the details came back harder, brighter, until the past was more vivid than the present and you lived in a kind of double exposure.

"The entrance was behind a fall of rock, maybe four feet wide. You'd never find it unless you were looking. Inside it opened up — a chamber, big as the Brandt store, with a seep running down one wall and pooling in a basin the old-timers had lined with stones. There were markings on the wall. Not Indian markings. Chisel work. Dates. Names."

"Corliss names?" Silas asked.

"Corliss names going back to before there was a Corliss in this valley worth the name. And papers. A tin document box, rusted half through, and inside it a ledger. Leather-bound, this thick—" he held his hands apart, the width of a Bible, "—with a canvas cover stitched over it to keep the damp off. Somebody had gone to a lot of trouble to keep that book alive. Somebody had wanted it found, someday, by somebody who'd know what it meant."

"And Hollis knew what it meant," Ines said quietly.

"Hollis read it by headlamp for six hours. I know because I sat with him for four of them and couldn't sleep for the other two. He read it the way a man reads his own death warrant — slow, going back, checking." Tomás's hands had gone still in his lap. "You've seen the pages. You know what's in it. Transfers. Quitclaims. Assignments of water rights, 1891 through 1904. Signatures from men who couldn't write their own names — an X, witnessed by a Corliss clerk. Men signing away the water under their own ranches for a dollar and a debt already owed. And in the margins, in the clerk's hand, notes. *Signatory illiterate. Signatory indebted to company store. Signatory advised of consequences of refusal.*"

"Consequences," Silas said. The word came out of him flat and hard, like a stone dropped on stone.

"There's a page in there," Tomás said, "with a list of ranches that refused. And next to each name, a date. And next to each date, a note: *ditch diverted.* You understand what that means? They didn't buy the water from those men. They took it, and they let the land die, and then they bought the dead land for back taxes. Your father's place is on that list, Vasquez. The Vasquez ditch. Diversions noted in three different years."

Silas didn't move. But Tomás had guided men through enough country to read a body the way he read terrain, and he saw the stillness in the ranger that wasn't calm at all — it was the stillness of a river going deep and fast under ice.

"I'm telling you that so you understand what Hollis was holding," Tomás went on. "Not a grievance. A machine. Forty years of it, documented in the perpetrators' own handwriting. He understood it too. He sat back from that ledger near dawn and he said — I'll never forget the words, because I've turned them over every night since — he said, *Tomás, this doesn't ruin one family. This rewrites three towns.* And then he smiled. God help me, he *smiled*. He thought he'd found a treasure. He thought the truth was a weapon that only fired in one direction."

* * *

The fire had settled to a red eye in its bed of coals. Somewhere above them, on the rise, Cass shifted her weight, and the gravel whispered and went quiet.

"Tell them about the radio call," Ines said. Her voice was gentle, but her eyes weren't. She'd been waiting for this part longer than anyone.

Tomás nodded slowly.

"The next evening, Hollis made a call on the camp set. I was twenty feet away, breaking down the survey line, and I heard his side of it. He was talking to the expedition's sponsor's office. Not to Ada Corliss directly — to her agent. The man who handled the expedition's logistics, the permits, the money. Ward Pellis."

Mara's head came up. "Pellis? He was at our house. He came to Christmas dinner. He—" She stopped, and Tomás watched her do the arithmetic he'd done himself, weeks ago, in the dark, over and over until the numbers bled.

"He carried your father's gear at the trailhead," Tomás said. "I remember it. Big man, easy laugh, always first with a flask when the work was done. He'd been Corliss's man for years by then — I learned that after. But that night, all I knew was that Hollis told him what we'd found. Told him over an open radio channel. Told him he was coming out of the field in two weeks and taking the ledger to the territorial capital, and then to the newspapers, and that Ada Corliss could either settle with the dispossessed families or read about it in the morning paper with her coffee."

"He said that?" Peter's voice cracked in the middle. "He *announced* it? Over the radio?"

"He was an honest man," Tomás said. "That was his whole tragedy. He thought honesty was armor. He thought that because he meant no harm, no harm would come to him. He'd spent his whole life in a world where that was true — universities, funding boards, polite rooms where the worst thing anyone did was steal another man's research. He didn't know he'd walked into a country where the Corlisses had been solving problems like him for sixty years, and the way they solved them was quiet, and final, and never written down anywhere except in ledgers like the one he was carrying."

Tomás reached into his shirt and pulled out the little canvas-wrapped bundle he'd carried against his chest since the night of the fire. He didn't open it. He just held it, the way a man holds a wound.

"Two nights later, Hollis came to my tent. Past midnight. I was awake — I was always awake those days, something in my bones knew what was coming before my head did. He told me he'd gotten a reply. Pellis had radioed back that morning. The message was friendly. *Ada wants to talk. Bring the ledger to the ranch. We'll do this properly, family to family.* Hollis was pleased. He thought he'd won. He told me he'd sleep well for the first time in a week."

"But he didn't take it to the ranch," Silas said.

"No. Because I told him not to." Tomás's jaw worked. "I told him: you don't walk into the lion's house carrying the meat. I told him: send copies first, go yourself second, or don't go at all. And he laughed at me. He put his hand on my shoulder — he was the only man I ever let do that — and he said, *Tomás, you've been in the backcountry so long you've forgotten how civilized people behave.*"

Tomás looked up, and his eyes were wet, and he let them be.

"Three days later he was dead."

* * *

Mara had gone white. Ruth Ann set her cup down in the sand very carefully, as if the small noise of it might break something.

"Tell it," Mara said. Her voice was a thread. "All of it. Don't carry it anymore. Give it to us."

So he gave it to them.

"It was the night of the eleventh. I'd taken the last watch, midnight to four. The camp was asleep. The river was running loud — it does that in the dark, you can hear it half a mile off. And I heard a boat."

"A boat," Silas repeated. "At midnight. Above the camp."

"Coming downriver. Oars, muffled — wrapped in cloth at the locks. Whoever it was had done it before. The boat came in below the camp, on the far side of the gravel bar, and I saw one man step out and stand there, looking at the camp. I knew the shape of him before I knew the name. Big man. Shoulders like a door. He stood in the moonlight and he pulled off his hat and he looked at the tents the way a man looks at a field he's about to burn — not with hate. With *assessment*. Like he was pricing it."

"Tomás," Ines said softly. "You could have stopped him."

"I could have shot him," Tomás said. "I had a rifle in my hands. I told myself later I didn't know what he intended. That's a lie. I knew. I'd known since the radio call that something was coming for us, and I lay there behind that rock with a rifle and I did nothing, because some old coward in me said *not your fight, not your grave, old man, let it pass.*" He said it without flinching, offering it up the way a man offers his palm for a blade. "I have carried that alongside everything else. You should have all of it."

Nobody spoke. Above them Cass had come down off the rise without a sound and stood at the edge of the firelight, listening, her hand nowhere near her gun, which was its own kind of judgment.

"He went to Hollis's tent," Tomás said. "He knew which one it was. He didn't hesitate — he walked straight to it, like a man walking to his own barn. And Hollis must have heard him coming, because he came out. He came out in his long underwear with a lantern, and he said — I heard this, clear across the gravel, the river carrying his voice to me like it was a messenger that had changed sides — he said, *Ward? Ward, what are you doing here?*"

Mara made a sound. It wasn't a word. Silas put his hand over hers and left it there.

"And Pellis said, *You should have taken the offer, Ned.* Just like that. Conversational. Almost kind. And Hollis said something — I couldn't hear it, it was low — and Pellis said, *The old woman can't have it loose. You know that. She's got a grandson, and that boy's whole life is built on what's in that book, and she will burn this valley down to its bedrock before she lets some professor hand it to a newspaperman.* And Hollis said — this I heard, because he raised his voice, he was angry now, he was *scared* and angry and being a Hollis he got louder instead of careful — he said, *Then she can burn it down, because the truth doesn't belong to her.*"

The fire popped. A coal split and fell.

"Pellis had a gun out by then. I saw the moonlight run along it. And Hollis saw it too, and he stopped talking, and for a moment the two of them just stood there on the gravel bar, and the river went on being the river, like it always does, like it doesn't care what happens on its banks, and then—" Tomás's hands closed around the canvas bundle. "It was fast. One shot. Hollis went down on his knees first, like a man kneeling to pray, and then he went over sideways, and Pellis stood over him for maybe ten seconds. Ten seconds. I counted them because I couldn't do anything else. Then he took the lantern and went into the tent, and I heard him going through things, and he came out with the ledger — the *ledger*, Tomás, he had it — and he walked to the water's edge and he threw it in."

"But it didn't burn with the camp," Peter said. "And it's here."

"Because the river gave it back," Tomás said. "That's the joke of it. That's the joke God wrote. Pellis threw it in the river and the river carried it down a hundred yards and hung it up on the willow roots below the bar, and at dawn, when I went down — when I finally made myself go down, after I'd stood over Hollis and not been able to touch him — the ledger was there in the shallows, snagged, soaked, half the ink run but the clerk's hand had been iron-gall and iron-gall doesn't quit. I pulled it out and I wrapped it in canvas and I took it into the cave and I put it back in the tin box where it had slept for sixty years."

"And the fire," Silas said. "The camp burned."

"I burned it." Tomás met the ranger's eyes without apology. "I burned the records, I smashed the radio — Pellis had already wrecked the set, but I finished it — and I dug the grave and I put river stones on it, and when the others woke I told them Hollis had died in the night and I didn't know how, and then I told them we had to leave, and I didn't say why. I moved him because a grave with a bullet in the story would have brought the coroner, and the coroner in this county is elected with Corliss money, and whatever that man ruled, it would have been *accident*, and then we'd have had a paper saying *accident* that we could never get unpainted. I thought I was protecting the truth. I buried it in a tin box and I sat on it like a hen on a stone egg, and I told myself: when we're out, when we're safe, when there's a court that isn't bought. And then the guns came, and we ran, and every day I waited made the next day harder to tell it in."

"Why didn't you tell us it was Pellis?" Mara's voice had gone from thread to wire. "From the beginning. In the canyon. You said you didn't see."

"Because I did see, and what I saw was impossible, and I was afraid that if I said his name, I'd be the crazy old guide who accused a Corliss agent of murdering his own brother-in-law with no witness but a moonlit gravel bar." Tomás spread his hands. "And because of the other thing. The thing that broke the world."

"Say it," Silas said.

"Brother-in-law," Mara said. She'd gotten there first. She was staring at Tomás with an expression he'd seen once on a hawk — total, terrible focus. "He said *brother-in-law*. Pellis was married to my aunt. To my father's sister, Lorraine. He came to Thanksgiving. He taught me to skip stones. He held my mother's hand at my father's—" She stopped. Started again, quieter, which was worse. "He sat in the front row at the funeral. In the front row, Tomás. He put flowers on the—"

"He'd been Corliss's for years," Tomás said. "I learned it after, from the ledger itself, in a way. There's a transfer in it, 1919, the Pellis homestead on the east fork — signed over to Corliss Water and Power for less than a tenth of its worth. I went looking, after. Asked old men in towns we passed through. Ward Pellis's grandfather took the Corliss dollar when it was on offer and hated himself for it, and Ward grew up watching that hate eat his father, and when the Corlisses came to *him* — not with a gun, with a job, a salary, a place at the table — he took it. He took it and he told himself the same thing every bought man tells himself: *I'll change it from inside. I'm different.* And then one night the old woman said *Ned Hollis is a problem*, and Ward Pellis discovered he wasn't different at all. He was exactly the same. He was the end of a line sixty years long, and the line was always going to end on a gravel bar with a gun."

Silence. The river of it, the kind that runs under everything in that country, under every campfire, under every town.

"He was family," Mara said at last. "My father trusted him with—"

"With everything," Tomás said. "That's the ugliest part, and I won't soften it for you because you deserve the whole weight, you and no one else. Your father didn't die for the ledger. Plenty of men would kill for a ledger. Your father died because the man he'd have trusted with his own daughter's life had already sold the family name for a Corliss paycheck, and when the moment came, family didn't slow the bullet. It aimed it. Pellis knew exactly where the tent was. He knew your father's habits, his voice, the way he'd come out of a tent at midnight instead of shooting first. He used all of it. He killed him with trust, Mara. That was the weapon. The gun was just the delivery."

Mara didn't cry. Tomás had watched her hold herself together for weeks with a will that frightened him, and she held it now, but her hands had gone to fists in her lap and her knuckles were the color of the moon.

* * *

Silas stood. He walked to the edge of the firelight and stood looking north, toward the dead ground they'd cross at first light, and for a long time he said nothing, and nobody pressed him. When he turned back, his face had the look Tomás had learned to read on men who'd found something in the dark they'd been looking for all their lives and hated finding it.

"Then we don't just have a ledger," Silas said. "We have a witness. You saw the killing. You heard him say her name — *the old woman*. You heard him say *she can't have it loose*. That's not a ledger page, Tomás. Ledgers can be called forged. That's testimony, and it points uphill, all the way to Ada Corliss herself."

"Testimony from a man who watched and did nothing," Tomás said. "Whose word against the right hand of the richest family in the territory."

"Testimony," Silas said, "that a federal judge will hear under oath, with Peter's photographs and Elena Brandt's affidavit and a dead gunman's paybook sitting next to it. They built a wall, Tomás. We're not going to knock it down with one stone. We're going to walk up to it carrying all of them."

Tomás looked at the old man's face across the coals, and then at Mara, and at Ines with her closed notebook, and Ruth Ann, and Peter in the shadows, and Cass with her arms crossed and something in her eyes that wasn't contempt anymore, and he felt the pack shift on his back for the first time in weeks — not lighter, never lighter, but shared, the weight distributed across more shoulders than his own.

"Then I'll say it in front of a judge," he said. "And in front of a newspaperman, and God, and anybody else who'll stand still long enough. Ward Pellis killed Ned Hollis on the eleventh of the month, on the gravel bar below the camp, and I watched him do it, and I've been dead ever since. Time to see if the telling raises either of us."

Silas nodded once. Then he crouched and began raking dirt over the coals, killing the fire down to nothing, because dawn was coming and forty miles of dead ground didn't care about testimony.

It was Cass who broke the quiet, from the edge of the light, her voice dry as the flats ahead:

"One problem, ranger. Pellis knows we've got the ledger back. He's been hunting it this whole time. Which means he's not behind us anymore." She let it hang. "He's ahead of us. Waiting somewhere on the only ground between here and Judge Pell's courthouse."

Silas's hands went still over the dead fire.

"Then we'd best be faster than his waiting," he said.
