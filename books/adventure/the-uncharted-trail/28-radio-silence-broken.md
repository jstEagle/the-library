## 28. Radio Silence Broken

Ada Corliss had learned to read the desert the way other people read scripture — not for comfort but for instruction. From the east window of the big house on Corliss Hill, she could see forty miles of it: the river a brown thread stitching through gray-green scrub, the mesa country standing up in red shoulders against the sky, and far off, almost invisible at this hour, the smoke haze of Corlissville where two thousand people earned their bread from her family's name and paid for it in scrip.

She had watched that country for sixty-one years. She would not watch it much longer.

The doctor had been honest with her, which was more than most men managed. Six months, he'd said, and then had the decency to look away when she laughed. She didn't need six months. She needed four weeks — long enough to see the operation closed out, the ledger burned or buried, the witnesses scattered to the four winds, and Daniel standing solid on ground no one could shake.

She poured herself two fingers of whiskey she wasn't supposed to have and sat down at the shortwave set.

The radio was her father's habit before it was hers. Old Amos Corliss had run his empire from this same chair, this same frequency, his voice going out across the territory like weather. Ada had modernized nothing about it. There was something in the crackle of the set, in the discipline of scheduled check-ins, that kept a thing like an empire honest — or at least kept its keeper honest about what it cost.

"Sallow. Report."

Static, then Duke Sallow's voice, flat as the alkali flats. "Ma'am. Ranger Vasquez is still upriver. He's made contact with at least some of the Hollis party. We've got sign of them moving north through the box canyons."

"And the ledger?"

A pause. Sallow's pauses always had weight; she'd learned to price them years ago. "No confirmation they still have it. Could've gone in the river with Hollis."

"It didn't go in the river." Ada turned the glass slowly on the desk, watching the lamplight swim in it. "If it had gone in the river, you'd have found the body next to it. Men don't drown holding what they mean to destroy. They throw it first. Hollis died with his hands full, Duke. Which means somebody has those hands now."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Vasquez is a ranger. Rangers write reports. Reports go to courthouses. Courthouses are ours — mostly. But paper travels, Duke. Paper is the one thing in this territory I never fully owned." She let that sit. "I want the problem closed inside ten days. Quietly. No bodies where the law will find them, no fires visible from town, and nothing — *nothing* — that traces back to this house."

"Understood."

"One more thing." She looked out the window again, at the thread of river catching the last light. "The girl. Mara Hollis. If she's alive, I want her brought to me alive. Unharmed. I'll handle her myself."

Another pause, longer than the others. "That complicates things, ma'am."

"Everything worth doing complicates things. Ten days, Duke."

She signed off and sat in the quiet afterward, listening to the set tick as it cooled. Somewhere upstairs, the house creaked the way old houses do when the temperature drops — settling, her mother used to call it, though her mother had been dead thirty years and the house had never once finished settling.

Ada drank her whiskey and did the arithmetic she did every night now, the cold columns of it. One ledger. Eleven people who might have seen it, minus one dead expedition leader, minus however many Sallow had already put in the ground without telling her. A ranger with a rifle and, by all accounts, a stubborn streak a mile wide. Against all of it: four weeks of her life, a fortune built over three generations, and a grandson who thought the world ran on fairness because she'd raised him in a house where the water never stopped running.

It would hold. It had held before. Her father had buried worse than a ledger, and she had buried worse than her father.

She just had to live long enough to finish the job.

* * *

Daniel came down two mornings later, and Ada knew before he reached the study door that something was wrong, because Daniel never came down before noon unless something was wrong.

He stood in the doorway a moment — her daughter's boy, twenty-four years old, tall and loose-limbed with his grandfather's jaw and none of his grandfather's appetite — holding a sheaf of papers in one hand like a man holding a dead bird.

"You went into my office," Ada said. Not a question. She kept writing.

"The lock was changed last month. You changed it back." He crossed the room and laid the papers on the blotter in front of her, squaring the edges with a care that told her how angry he was. Angry people either threw things or tidied them. Daniel tidied. "Operation orders. Payment schedules. A list of names — hired men, most of them, and three of them I know, Ada. Three of them worked our east range last season. Their mothers shop at the company store."

"They're being paid well."

"They're being paid to kill people." He said it quietly, which was worse than shouting. Daniel had learned young that shouting got him sent to his room and quiet got him answers. "Eleven scientists. A botanist. A grad student. And a ranger whose only crime so far is doing the job we pay the county to let us ignore."

Ada set down her pen. She took her time with it, laying it parallel to the blotter's edge, because she had learned long ago that the slower you moved when the stakes were high, the more room the other person had to talk themselves into your position. Her father had taught her that too. Most of what she knew had come from him, and most of what he knew had come from taking things.

"Sit down, Daniel."

"I'll stand."

"Suit yourself." She folded her hands. "How much did you read?"

"All of it. Twice." His eyes were wet and furious and trying to be neither. "There's a line item here for 'disposal.' Disposal, Ada. Of human beings. And there's a memo in your handwriting saying the girl comes in alive because — I read it three times because I couldn't believe it the first two — because *she'll make a better example breathing.*"

"A hostage is not an example. A hostage is leverage. Leverage means nobody dies who doesn't have to." Ada heard her own voice go smooth and reasonable, the voice she'd used on bankers and county commissioners for forty years, and hated how easily it still fit. "You think I want blood? I want this finished. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

"Finished." He laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "The Hollis expedition was funded out of this house. Grandfather's money, your signature. Those people trusted us. Papa trusted them. And now there are men on our payroll hunting them through canyon country like coyotes, and you're sitting here calling it *finished* like it's a fence line."

"What do you imagine pays for this house?" She gestured at the room — the walnut paneling, the Persian rugs worn soft as moss, the window with its forty-mile view. "What do you imagine pays for your education, your horses, your fine ideas about how the world ought to work? Water, Daniel. Every drop of it in three towns runs through a Corliss gate, and every gate sits on paper my father wrote and I have spent my life defending. That ledger — if it exists — isn't a book. It's a match. And this whole valley is kindling."

"Then let it burn."

The words landed between them and neither moved.

Ada rose, slowly, one hand on the desk. The illness had taken weight off her faster than she'd expected; she felt light in a way that frightened her, like a tree hollowed from inside, still standing but no longer sure why. She crossed to the window so she wouldn't have to look at him while she said what came next.

"When I was nine years old," she said, "my father took me to the river diversion above Miller's Flats. There were families camped along it — ranchers, small ones, homesteaders. They'd been there two generations. My father walked the line with the sheriff and a dozen hired men, and they posted notices, and when the notices weren't enough they cut the headgates and let the ditches run dry, and by August those families were gone. Some of them signed transfers for pennies on the dollar rather than watch their cattle die. I stood behind my father the whole time, and I remember thinking he was the biggest man God ever made." She turned. "He wasn't big. He was just closest to the money. But I was nine, and I loved him, and I chose the story where he was right. That choice is the only inheritance I have ever truly guarded. Do you understand me?"

"No," Daniel said. "Because you're describing a crime and calling it a birthright."

"I'm describing the world as it is. You're welcome to describe a better one. People have been doing it since Eden and it has never once changed the price of water." She came back around the desk, and her voice dropped to the register she saved for the rarest occasions, the one that had ended partnerships and marriages and, once, a man's career in a single sentence. "Here is what happens now. You will take those papers back to your rooms, you will forget the names on them, and you will stay out of this. In four weeks it will be over, and you will inherit the cleanest version of this empire I can hand you, and you will spend the rest of your life pretending you don't know what it's standing on. That is what I did. That is what my father did. It is an ugly inheritance, but it is *yours*, and I am dying, Daniel, and I will not spend my last weeks watching you give it away."

"You're threatening me."

"I'm instructing you. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

He looked at her for a long moment. She watched him weigh it — she could see the scales working behind his eyes, love on one side and the papers on the other, and she had counted on the love because she had always counted on the love, because she had built him out of it the way her father had built the dams.

"If you go to the newspapers," she said, "if you breathe one word of this outside this room, I will disown you. I will rewrite the will tonight. I will leave every acre, every gate, every drop to a trust and let the lawyers feed on it, and your name will come off this house the way the homesteaders' names came off their land. Is that what you want? To be nothing?"

"That's the thing, Ada." He picked the papers up off the blotter, squared them once more against his palm. "You keep saying it like it's a threat. I've been nothing my whole life. I've just been nothing *richly.*"

He walked out. He didn't slam the door. Daniel never slammed doors; he closed them with the terrible gentleness of a man who has already decided something, and Ada stood alone in her study with the whiskey and the shortwave and the forty-mile view, and for the first time in sixty-one years she could not read the country at all.

* * *

She didn't sleep. At two in the morning she rang for Marta, and when the old woman came, Ada told her to get the car ready and say nothing to anyone, and Marta — who had served three generations of Corlisses and kept more secrets than the river kept bodies — nodded and went.

But Daniel was already gone.

She learned it at dawn from the empty garage bay and the telephone exchange, and she spent the morning doing what she had done all her life, which was to move pieces. She called the newspaper office in the territorial capital and asked, in her pleasantest voice, whether the editor had received any unusual correspondence lately, and the editor's assistant said no, ma'am, nothing unusual, and Ada hung up half relieved and half more afraid than she'd been all week, because silence from a newspaper is not innocence. Silence is a pressroom deciding how long to sit on a thing.

By afternoon she had Sallow on the shortwave again, tightening the timeline from ten days to five, and by evening she had drafted the letters — the ones she'd hoped never to send — to the families of Tomás Reyes and Ines Okafor, instructing her men in town to extend them the hospitality of the company guesthouse until further notice. Leverage, not violence. She told herself it was the same distinction she'd drawn for Daniel, and for the first time in her life the words tasted like ash on the way down.

She was at the window past midnight, watching the dark line of the river, when the telephone rang.

Not the shortwave. The telephone. Nobody called this house after midnight except bad news, and bad news had been calling all week.

"Yes."

"Ma'am?" It was the night man at the exchange, sounding young and scared. "There's a wire came through for the *Territorial Herald*. Capital office. It's — ma'am, it's marked urgent, and it's addressed to the editor personally, and the operator says it came from a public wire office down in Corlissville. Paid in cash. No name given."

Ada closed her eyes. The river ran on in the dark, patient as arithmetic.

"What did it say?"

"Operator wouldn't say, ma'am. Said it was sealed traffic. But—" the young man hesitated, and she could hear him deciding whether to risk his neck, and deciding, as everyone in this valley eventually decided, that Ada Corliss was the safer bet, "—the operator did say whoever sent it asked twice whether the *Herald* had a correspondent assigned to cover federal courts. Federal, ma'am. Not county."

Federal courts. Judge Pell's courts, two hundred miles east, beyond the reach of every favor and every dollar she had planted in this territory over sixty years.

"Thank you," Ada said, and hung up, and stood a long while in the dark with her hand still on the receiver.

So. The boy hadn't gone to the papers to sell them a scandal. He'd gone to point them east — toward the one courthouse she couldn't buy, the one direction she'd spent a lifetime making sure no one ever looked. He hadn't fired a shot. He'd simply opened a door and let the light in, and light, unlike lead, could not be recalled.

She thought about rewriting the will. She even took the pen out.

Then she thought about Daniel at nine years old, standing beside her at the headgate above Miller's Flats — because of course she had taken him, of course she had shown him the water and the gates and the country that answered to their name, wanting him to feel what she had felt, the size of it, the rightness of it — and she remembered that he had asked her one question that day, just one, looking out at the dry ditches and the empty homesteads:

*Who lived here before us?*

And she had said, *Nobody important.*

She put the pen down. Outside, the river kept moving, carrying everything toward the sea, the way it always had, indifferent to whose name was written on it — and somewhere upstream, in the black folds of the canyon country, a ranger and a ledger and a dying woman's only heir were all converging on the same crossing, and Ada Corliss, for the first time in her life, could do nothing at all but wait for the water to tell her what it knew.
