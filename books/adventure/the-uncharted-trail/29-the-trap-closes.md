## 29. The Trap Closes

We crossed the last ridge in the dark, and I let myself believe it for one more mile.

That was the mistake. Belief is a luxury out here, and I'd been spending it like a rich man since Sallow's men turned south two days ago. The ledger party — me, Tomás, Ines, Peter, Ruth Ann, Ferro on his own feet now but slow, and Cass on the litter between Tomás and me — had come off the mesa at midnight and dropped into the long shallow draw that ran east toward the dead ground. Forty miles of nothing between us and Judge Pell's courthouse. No water we hadn't carried. No shade taller than a man. If we made it across before word of our route reached anyone who mattered, we'd won.

And Sallow's riders had gone south. I'd watched their dust from the rim at dusk, a dirty ribbon unspooling toward the bait run, toward Mara's convoy of trucks rattling loud and obvious through the canyonlands with an empty strongbox painted up to look like it held a fortune.

She was leading them away. She was good at it. I'd taught her half of what she knew about reading country, and she'd taught herself the other half out of grief and stubbornness, which are better teachers than I am.

So why couldn't I stop looking over my shoulder?

* * *

We made camp at three in the morning in the mouth of a box canyon — not by choice. Ferro's legs gave out on the scree slope, and after that it was carry him or leave him, and nobody said *leave him* out loud because saying a thing makes it real. The canyon walls rose sheer on three sides, red rock going black in the starlight, and the only way in or out was the draw behind us. Bad ground to camp in. Ground you camped in only when your people had nothing left.

Ruth Ann made that call, and she made it right. "They're done," she said, kneeling beside Ferro, then Cass. "All of them. You push another ten miles tonight and you'll be carrying four instead of two."

Cass had been shot at the ferry crossing nine days ago. The wound had gone bad once already, and Ruth Ann had beaten it back with willow bark and boiled steel and a will that didn't recognize defeat as a category. But infection is patient. It waits in the blood like a claim jumper, and when it moves, it moves fast. Cass's fever had broken at sundown and come back at midnight, and she'd stopped complaining somewhere around the second day, which from Cass Duval was the worst sign there was.

"Get some rest," Ruth Ann told the rest of us. "I'll take first watch."

I should have argued. I should have taken the watch myself and put her down beside her patient, because she'd been running on coffee and duty for a week. Instead I sat with my back against the canyon wall where I could see the whole length of the draw, and I did what I always do when something feels wrong: I read the country.

The draw was quiet. Too quiet, maybe — no coyotes working the flats, no night birds off the river. Or maybe just desert being desert. Wind came down the canyon in slow breaths, warm and smelling of dust and creosote. Somewhere above us a slab of rock ticked as it cooled, the way rock does.

Tomás settled next to me without asking. He'd been doing that since the cross-country march, since the night he finally told me what he'd seen at Hollis's grave and I finally believed him. We weren't friends yet. We were something more useful than friends — two men who'd each spent years carrying a weight and had set it down within arm's reach of each other.

"You keep looking south," he said.

"I know."

"The girl's fine."

"I know that too." I turned my canteen in my hands. It was half full and I wasn't drinking, saving it the way you save the last match. "It's not her I'm worried about. It's how easy this has been."

Tomás grunted. He was sixty-some years old and built like the country itself, all weathered planes and hidden depth, and he'd been guiding expeditions through this territory since before I could ride. He knew the answer before I said it.

"Sallow pulled his men off us," I went on. "Eight hired guns, and he sends them chasing trucks through the canyonlands? Duke Sallow doesn't make mistakes like that. He's been herding us since the ferry crossing — cutting angles, pushing us east, funning us into open ground where he could see us. And then two days ago he just... lets go."

"Maybe he got tired."

"Duke Sallow doesn't get tired. He gets paid." I looked at the stars through the notch of the canyon mouth. "He let us go because somebody told him to let us go. And the only person who can tell Duke Sallow anything is Ada Corliss."

"So she called him off."

"Or she went around him." I said it quietly, but in the stillness of that canyon it might as well have been a gunshot. "Sallow's been having doubts. I showed him that paybook — cash payments, no names, deniable deaths — and I saw it land. A man like him doesn't quit, but he slows down. He starts making mistakes. Starts misdirecting his own riders." I shook my head. "Ada Corliss didn't build an empire by trusting one man with one job. If Sallow wavered, she'd have other guns. Outside guns. Men nobody in this territory knows, so nobody can warn anybody."

Tomás was silent a long moment. Down the line of sleepers, Peter Liu stirred and murmured something in his sleep — he'd been dreaming out loud ever since he confessed about the film, like the shame had loosened something in him that wouldn't stay shut even unconscious.

"If you believe that," Tomás said, "why are we sitting here?"

Because we had nowhere else to sit. Because Cass couldn't ride and Ferro couldn't walk and forty miles of dead ground doesn't care how motivated you are. But I didn't say any of that. What I said was:

"Because if outside guns are coming, they're coming from the north, over the mesa, and the mesa's twelve miles off. We'd hear vehicles on the hardpan before they got close. We'd have an hour's warning, maybe two." I patted the rock under us. "This canyon's defensible from the front. One narrow approach, high walls. Four of us can hold that mouth against twenty, given time to set up."

"And time is the one thing—"

The rock ticked again.

I froze.

Not the cooling-tick. That sound is irregular, arrhythmic, a stone settling by fractions. This was a click, clean and deliberate, and it came from the canyon wall above us — thirty feet up, where no man had any business being — and it was followed by a second click, and I knew exactly what it was because my father had owned a rifle with a bolt that made that same sound when you worked it slow and careful, the way you do when you don't want to wake anybody.

"Down!" I hit Tomás in the shoulder and dove flat as the first round cracked off the wall where my head had been and spanged away into the dark.

* * *

They were on the rims. Both rims.

I understood that in the first ten seconds, from the geometry of the fire — muzzle flashes high on the left wall and high on the right, staggered, overlapping fields, professional spacing. Not Sallow's men riding in off the flats. These had climbed in the dark, scaled the canyon walls on foot, and lain prone on the rims waiting for dawn or for us to move, whichever came first. Waiting, most likely, since before we'd arrived. Which meant they'd known where we'd be. Which meant someone had fed them our route — and there was only one man alive who knew it besides the eight of us.

Sallow. God help me, Sallow had sold us after all — or been made to sell us, or been bypassed and left holding the blame either way. It didn't matter now. What mattered was the arithmetic.

I crawled to the canyon mouth with rounds snapping overhead, kicking grit into my face, and took stock. Ines had Peter and Ferro pressed flat against the south wall. Ruth Ann had thrown herself over Cass, one hand splayed on the litter, the other dragging her medical bag by its strap. Tomás was beside me, rifle up, breathing hard but level. Four of us who could fight. Maybe five, counting Cass on her best day, and this wasn't her best day.

Against the flashes on the rims, I counted eleven distinct firing positions before they went quiet. Eleven, spaced like men who'd done this before, and probably more I couldn't see. Four to one. Worse than four to one, because they held the high ground and we held a dead-end box with no back door.

A voice rolled down off the north rim, amplified by the canyon walls until it seemed to come from everywhere. It wasn't Sallow's voice. It was younger, flatter, with the clipped vowels of a man from somewhere east of anywhere that mattered to us.

"Vasquez! You're a ranger, so I'll give you the courtesy of a count. Eleven of us. Two of yours can't stand. You've got one rifle apiece and maybe thirty rounds each, and we've got all night and all morning. You see the problem."

I did see the problem. I lay in the dirt at the mouth of that canyon and saw it with perfect clarity, the way you see things in the moment before everything breaks — every option laid out and weighed and found wanting. Run and die in the open. Stay and die on the walls. Surrender and die later, somewhere quieter, with the ledger burned and the witnesses scattered and Ada Corliss dying peacefully in her big house knowing the rivers were still hers.

"Here's my offer," the voice went on. "Send out the paper and the woman named Hollis, walk the rest of your people south with your hands empty, and everybody sees the sunrise. That's the deal. You've got until first light to decide, and I'd think fast, because my employer's patience is shorter than mine."

*His employer.* Not Sallow. Not even Ada by name. Outside guns, contract language, deniability stacked three layers deep. She'd learned. After fifty years of buying men whose names she knew, she'd finally bought men who didn't exist.

Tomás's hand found my sleeve. "Silas." His voice was low and rough. "The ledger. We split it. I take the pages north along the wall while you hold their eyes—"

"No."

"—and even if I fall, Peter's photographs—"

"*No.*" I said it harder than I meant to. "Nobody splits. Nobody plays hero. That's how she wins — one piece at a time, one brave fool at a time." I pressed my forehead against the cold sand and thought, and the thinking felt like drowning, like the river in the dream I'd had since I was twelve years old, the one where my father walked into the current and I stood on the bank measuring the distance instead of moving.

Twelve years of miles. Every mile chosen so I'd never have to stand still and face a thing like this. And here it was, standing still, facing me.

Ines Okafor crawled up through the dark and joined us at the mouth. She was a botanist, small and precise, and she'd crossed a desert on blistered feet without one word of complaint, and now she looked at me with eyes that had already done the arithmetic and refused to accept the answer.

"They won't honor any deal," she said. "You know that."

"I know that."

"Then we need a fourth option. You're good at those."

"I'm not." I almost laughed. "Every third option I've handed this group, somebody nearly died executing it. Mara took the bait run. Cass took a bullet. Sallow—" My throat closed on the name. "Sallow took worse."

"Then find a fifth," Ines said simply. "You will. It's what you do. You look at impossible country long enough and you find the line through it."

Above us, the voice called down again, bored now, checking its watch. "Thirty minutes, Vasquez."

And that was when I heard it — faint, far off to the south, past the canyon walls, past the mesa, out where Mara's convoy had led Sallow's riders into the maze. An engine. Then another. Not coming toward us. Coming *fast*, and wrong for the terrain, too many gears grinding, a vehicle being driven by someone who knew exactly where she was going and didn't care what the country thought about it.

Tomás heard it too. His head came up like a bird dog's.

"That's the south road," he breathed. "Nobody runs the south road at night. Nobody runs it at all — it's washed out past the fork."

"She knows it's not," I said, and something in my chest that had been clenched for nine days — since the ferry crossing, since the narrows, since the first arrow she'd drawn on me on a trail neither of us should have been walking — something in my chest let go, just a fraction, just enough to breathe. "Washed out for anyone who reads maps. Not washed out for anyone I taught to read country."

Mara Hollis was coming back. Against orders, against sense, straight into eleven rifles on the rims — because of course she was. Her father had died for a piece of paper, and she'd decided somewhere in the dark of the last week that she was done letting other people carry the thing her family bled for.

The engine noise grew. On the rims, the muzzle flashes shifted, gunmen rolling to new positions, the flat young voice snapping orders that no longer sounded bored. They'd heard it too. Their perfect trap, sprung at last after days of patient waiting, and now the whole shape of it was tilting, because traps only work on the animal you planned for, and we had never been just the eight of them counted.

First light was coming up gray behind the eastern mesa. Thirty minutes, the voice had said. I checked my rifle, kissed the last of my water, and passed the canteen down the line to Ines.

"Everybody listen," I said. "When that truck hits the draw, they'll have to split their fire. The second they do, we move — all of us, together, at the south wall, low and fast. Nobody stops for anybody. Nobody's a hero. We've buried enough heroes."

Tomás chambered a round. Ines nodded once. Behind us, Ruth Ann lifted her head from Cass's litter, and even Ferro dragged himself up onto his elbows, white-faced and grinning like a madman.

Overhead, the sky went from black to iron, and the engines roared closer, and the moment hung there — the whole trap balanced on its hinge, everything we'd fought for and everyone we'd lost gathered into one breath before the break.

"Hold," I whispered, to my people, to myself, to the coming light. "*Hold.*"

Then the truck hit the draw at sixty miles an hour with its horn wide open, and the world broke loose.
