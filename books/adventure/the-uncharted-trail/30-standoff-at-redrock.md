## 30. Standoff at Redrock

### Silas

Dawn came up the color of an old bruise, and with it came the guns.

Silas heard them before he saw them — the particular silence of horses being held back, riders who didn't want to be heard, which was itself a kind of announcement in country this still. He crouched behind the fallen slab of redrock that had given the canyon its name and counted muzzle flashes of early light off rifle barrels along the rim. Eight. Then a ninth moving on the east shelf where there was no cover worth a damn.

Four to one, and the four were tired, half of them wounded, and one of them — Cass — couldn't stand without Ruth Ann's shoulder under her arm.

"Rim's covered," Tomás said, sliding down beside him, breathing hard from the climb he'd made to check the back trail. "Sallow's men?"

"Maybe." Silas kept his voice low. "Ada bypassed him. These aren't his boys."

He could feel it in the way they moved. Sallow's crew worked like men who knew the ground and respected it. These ones spread out like men reading off a map somebody else had drawn, taking positions by the numbers, no feel for the country at all. Outside guns. Ada Corliss had gone over her foreman's head, which meant Sallow either didn't know or hadn't stopped her, and Silas didn't know which possibility worried him more.

Mara came low along the rock, Peter's film canister strapped inside her jacket against her heart like something living. Her face was gray with two nights without sleep but her eyes were clear.

"How long do we have?"

"Minutes." Silas checked the cylinder of his revolver out of habit, though six rounds against nine rifles was arithmetic even a preacher wouldn't call hopeful. "They'll want us to see them first. That's how this works. They show themselves, they offer terms, and when we refuse —"

"They shoot." Mara said it flatly, the way you'd read a weather report. Somewhere between the ferry crossing and here she'd stopped flinching from the shape of the thing hunting them, and Silas hated that he'd been the one to teach her its outline.

"They'll try to take the ledger intact," he said. "That's our only card. They can't just pour lead into this canyon until it stops — they need the paper whole."

"And us?"

Silas looked at her. He thought about lying and knew she'd hear it in his voice the way she heard everything now.

"Us, they don't need whole."

Below them, in the bowl of the canyon where the ledger party had made their cold camp — Ferro propped against a pack with his leg splinted, Ruth Ann boiling willow bark over a fire no bigger than a cupped hand, Tomás's daughter's name written on a scrap of paper in Ines's pocket like a prayer — the light strengthened. Somewhere on the rim a rifle bolt slid home with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Then a horse came down the draw.

One rider, walking slow, hands wide of his thighs, no rifle in the scabbard. A young man in a city coat gone dusty, riding like someone who'd learned recently and badly. Silas put the glasses on him and felt the bottom drop out of the morning.

"That's Daniel Corliss," he said.

* * *

### Mara

She knew the face from newspapers and from the portrait that hung in the Corlissville company store, larger than life and twice as flattering: the heir, the grandson, the clean future of the empire. In the photographs he looked like money. Up close, coming down into the canyon alone with the sun rising behind him and nine rifles tracking him from above, he looked like a young man walking toward something that scared him more than dying.

The gunmen on the rim didn't know what to make of him. She could see their confusion in the way the barrels wavered — because whoever these men were, they'd been paid to stop the ledger party, not to explain to Ada Corliss why her grandson had a bullet in him.

Daniel Corliss reined up twenty yards from the redrock slab and did not dismount. His hands stayed visible. His voice, when it came, cracked once and then steadied, and Mara realized he'd rehearsed this — standing in some dark room saying the words over and over until they'd hold their shape under pressure.

"My grandmother sent these men," he called, loud enough for the rim, for everyone. "I'm asking them to hear me out before anyone does anything nobody can take back."

Nobody shot him. The morning held its breath.

"I grew up on stories," he went on. "I grew up on my grandfather taming a dead river and my grandmother holding three towns together with her bare hands. That's the story I was raised on. It's a good story." He swung down from the horse, awkward, and pulled a folded sheaf of papers from inside his coat. "Yesterday I read a different story. And I'm going to read it out loud, right now, and every one of you is going to listen, because most of you are from towns this story built."

Mara's hand found Silas's sleeve. "Where did he get —"

"The first page." Silas's voice was barely air. "Peter mailed copies. Or he got hold of one of ours. Doesn't matter. What matters is what those men on the rim do next."

Tomás had come up beside them, and Mara saw the old guide's knuckles whiten on his rifle stock. "Boy's either the bravest Corliss that ever lived or the last one," he muttered. "Same thing, maybe."

Below, Daniel Corliss unfolded the paper and began to read.

* * *

### Duke Sallow

Duke Sallow heard the boy's voice carrying up the canyon and felt the day tilt sideways under him.

He'd ridden all night trying to get ahead of the outside guns Ada had brought in over his head — hired out of El Paso, strangers, men with no stake in the country and no names he could use on them. He'd failed. Now he sat his horse on the west rim with six of his own remaining crew and watched the Corliss heir stand unarmed in the kill zone reading from a piece of paper, and Duke understood with a clarity that felt like falling that everything he'd been paid for was coming apart in front of him.

"— dated June the fourth, 1891," the boy was reading. "*Received of E. R. Corliss the sum of forty dollars in consideration of which I do hereby quit and transfer all claim to water rights upon the Rio Salado pertaining to my land, executed under such circumstances as —*" The boy stopped. Looked up. "You know what 'such circumstances' means? My great-great-grandfather signed that with an X. He couldn't write. Forty dollars, and the river that watered his fields went to a man named Corliss. That X is right here on this page. Is anybody's grandfather's X on this page?"

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses down on a rim full of armed men like snow on a roof.

Duke looked at his crew. Reyes County boys, mostly. Hidalgo boys. Sons and grandsons of the very ranches whose names were inked down that page one after another after another — Quintero, Brandt, Vasquez —

Vasquez.

His eye snagged on the signature and stuck there. He'd known a Vasquez once. Everybody in the territory knew of the Vasquez place, the one that went dry the year the new dam went in, the one whose owner drank himself into the river while his boy watched. Duke had been nineteen that year. He'd stood in the Corliss store and taken his pay in scrip and told himself it was just how things were done, the way his father had told himself, the way his grandfather's X on a coerced page had been just how things were done.

The boy below kept reading. Names. Dates. Dollar amounts so small they were obscene — the price of a river, the price of a town, the price of a family, itemized in a dead man's ledger handwriting.

"Duke." One of his crew, Otero, had edged his horse close. Young, quick-tempered, the best shot on the payroll. His rifle was still leveled at the canyon floor, but his eyes weren't on the target anymore. They were on the paper. "My abuelo — he had a brand, the lazy V over the bar. It's —" He swallowed. "It's on there. Thirty-five dollars, 1893."

"I see it," Duke said.

"What do we do?"

And there it was. The question Duke Sallow had spent thirty years making sure nobody would ever ask him, because he'd always known he didn't have an answer that wasn't just another form of kneeling. He looked down at the canyon where the ranger stood — Vasquez, the son, the one whose father's name was on that same damned page — and at the girl beside him, and at the old guide, and at the boy Corliss reading truth into a canyon full of guns like it was scripture.

Ada's orders sat in his breast pocket. *Handle it. Quietly. All of them.* Written in her own hand, because she trusted no telegraph clerk. He'd carried those orders for two weeks like a stone.

Above him, one of the El Paso men — a hard case named Merrick with a scarred cheek and no history in this country — turned his horse and started picking his way down toward the canyon floor, rifle unslung, moving with the unhurried certainty of a man who'd been paid to finish a job and intended to finish it.

"No," Duke said.

Merrick didn't stop.

"Merrick." Duke put his heels to his horse. "I said *no.*"

### Ines

Ines Okafor pressed herself flat against the canyon wall with Peter's spare negatives in a tin against her ribs and listened to a Corliss read her neighbors' grandparents' names to a canyon full of guns, and she thought: *this is what it sounds like when history stands up.*

She'd spent her life believing history was made in courtrooms and print shops, in careful citations and peer review, in the slow accumulation of documented fact. She had argued for newspapers in the box canyon while Tomás argued for selling the pages and Mara argued for the dead man's name, and she had believed, with a scholar's arrogance, that the argument itself was the work.

But the work, it turned out, was a twenty-three-year-old boy with a dusty coat and a stolen page, standing in the open with his voice breaking, saying *my great-great-grandfather signed that with an X* — and watching armed men lower their rifles one inch, then another inch, because the truth had gotten inside them past the pay.

Otero, on the rim — she knew his people, Hidalgo Oteros, had interviewed his aunt two summers ago about the old irrigation ditches — Otero's rifle was pointing at the dirt now. She could see other barrels drifting the same direction, the way compass needles drift when the true north finally shows itself.

Then Merrick started down the slope, and the needle snapped back.

"Silas," she breathed. "The one in the gray hat —"

"I see him." Silas was already moving, stepping out from behind the slab into the open, hands away from his gun, putting his body between the descending gunman and the boy with the paper. "Corliss! Keep reading!"

And Daniel Corliss, white-faced, hands shaking, lifted the page again and read on — *"...and further transfers executed upon the persons of Juan Reyes and Miguel Otero, being at the time detained without consent..."* — his voice ringing off the redrock, and Ines understood suddenly why the old griots had sung instead of written, why the first thing tyrants burned was always the book.

Words were weapons. She'd written that sentence in a journal article once, as a flourish.

Down in the canyon, it was load-bearing.

* * *

### Silas

Merrick reached the canyon floor at a walk, rifle loose in his hands, professional and patient, and Silas stepped into his path with nothing but his badge and his nerve.

"That's far enough."

"Ranger." Merrick smiled with half his mouth. "This ain't your jurisdiction. This is private employment matter."

"This is a public reading." Silas didn't move. Behind him, Daniel Corliss's voice went on, steady now, finding its legs — the names coming faster, the crowd of ghosts on that single page growing — and Silas let the words stand at his back like a second gun. "You're welcome to stay and listen. Half these names are probably your neighbors' too."

"Ain't from here." But Merrick's eyes flicked upward, toward the rim, toward his employer's other guns, and Silas watched him do the arithmetic every mercenary does eventually: nine rifles, one canyon, and a story spreading through the shooters faster than any order could outrun it.

"You're from somewhere," Silas said quietly. "Everybody's from somewhere. And wherever it is, I'd bet the deed somebody's grandfather got a raw deal, and I'd bet you've spent your whole life pretending that's just weather." He nodded up at the rim, at the wavering muzzles, at Otero with his rifle in the dirt. "Look at them, Merrick. You're the only man in this canyon who doesn't know how this ends."

For a long moment the only sound was Daniel Corliss's voice and the wind moving through the canyon, and Silas felt the standoff balanced on a point finer than a trigger's sear — the whole rotten weight of the Corliss empire teetering above them, held up for fifty years by nothing but the agreement of everyone not to say it out loud.

A boy was saying it out loud.

Merrick's rifle came up an inch. Silas's hand drifted to his holster, calm as still water, twelve years of miles settling into his shoulders —

And on the rim, Duke Sallow stood up in his stirrups, loud enough to wake the dead of three counties:

"Merrick! Drop it, or by God I'll drop you myself — and I got six rifles up here that remember whose payroll they're actually on!"

Nine barrels on the rim. Six of them swinging toward the three El Paso men.

The standoff broke — or bent — or became something else entirely, something with no name yet, hanging in the dawn air like the moment after lightning before the thunder arrives.

And from the canyon floor, Daniel Corliss turned the page, and kept reading.
