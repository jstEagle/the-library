## 31. Sallow's Choice

Duke Sallow had spent thirty years learning that every man had a price, and the last three weeks learning his own.

He stood in the gray light before dawn at the mouth of Redrock Canyon, a pair of field glasses pressed to his eyes, watching four of Ada Corliss's outside guns take up positions on the rim above the box canyon where the ranger and his band of ghosts had bedded down. The men moved well. Professional. The kind Corliss money bought when it stopped buying locals and started buying strangers — men with no grandfathers buried under these mesas, no memories, nothing at stake but the wage.

That was the trouble with them, Sallow thought. That was exactly the trouble.

Behind him, Ward Pellis crouched over a map weighted with stones, tracing the canyon's throat with one blunt finger. Pellis wore a city suit gone soft with river dust, and he had the particular calm of a man who had killed before and found it agreeable work. "Positions are set," he said. "When they come out for water at first light, we close the box. Nobody walks out."

"And the ledger?"

"Corliss wants it burned. In front of them, if possible." Pellis smiled without warmth. "She wants them to watch it go. Wants them to understand that paper doesn't beat blood."

Sallow lowered the glasses. His hands were steady. They were always steady; that was the thing people misread about him, mistook for coldness, when really it was just the steadiness of a man who had stopped expecting anything from the world except its weight. He'd been Ada Corliss's foreman for eleven years. He'd cleared squatters off her land, run rustlers into the badlands, buried two homesteaders' complaints so deep even the courts forgot they'd existed. He'd told himself every time that it was work, honest work, the kind a man did to eat. And he'd mocked the ranger's badge often enough and loud enough — *a tin star and a prayer*, he'd called it, *the government's way of hiring a man to lose* — because mocking it was easier than admitting he'd once wanted one himself, back when he was young enough to believe authority could be clean.

Now here he stood, about to help murder a United States ranger and six civilians, and the only thing he felt was tired.

"You're quiet, Duke," Pellis said.

"I'm thinking."

"Don't think too long. Sun's coming."

Sallow looked east. The sky over the mesa was going the color of an old scar, pink bleeding into gray, and below him the canyon held its shadow like a cupped hand. Somewhere down there was Silas Vasquez, the man who'd shown him a dead gunman's paybook and said nothing at all while Sallow read it. No cash in the book. No names. Just numbers and dates and a column marked simply *handled*. Eleven entries in two years. Sallow had known four of those names personally. Good men. Men who'd eaten supper at his table in Corlissville, played cards with his crew, laughed at his jokes. Handled.

And last night, alone in his tent, he'd read something worse than the paybook. One of his own riders had been carrying pages torn from a leather ledger — taken off the gunman Silas killed at the canyon, though how the man had gotten them Sallow never learned. Corliss's orders, probably: know what you're guarding. Sallow had read them by lantern light, expecting dry legal language, and instead he'd found his own history staring up at him in faded iron-gall ink.

*Transfer of riparian rights, Section 14, 1893.* And there at the bottom, the signature line: an X, pressed hard enough to tear the paper, witnessed by a county clerk whose fee had been paid in Corliss scrip. Beside the X, in the clerk's marginal hand: *signator illiterate; signator protested; signator signed under notice of eviction.*

The X belonged to Sallow's grandfather. Tomás Sallow, sheep man, forty acres and a spring, the only thing the old man had ever owned in his life. Duke had grown up on the story — how the family lost the spring, how they'd drifted into Corlissville and worked company wages until the story wore smooth as river stone and nobody asked anymore why the Salloows had come to town with nothing. Duke had built a whole life on top of that smooth-worn stone. Foreman. Wage. Reputation. A man didn't dig under his own foundation if he wanted to keep standing on it.

But he'd dug. And now he couldn't unsee the X.

"Duke." Pellis's voice, sharper. "I said sun's coming."

"I heard you."

"Then give the order. Your men respect you. It'll sit better coming from you."

*My men.* Sallow almost laughed. Half the riders below him were Corlissville boys — sons and grandsons of families on those same coerced transfers, boys who'd grown up drinking company water and calling it luck. He'd recruited them because local men knew the country. He'd never let himself think about what else local men knew in their bones, without knowing they knew it.

He raised the glasses one more time. Below, at the canyon floor, movement: the survivors breaking camp, the wrangler woman Cass Duval pale and half-carried, the ranger moving point with that low-hipped watchfulness Sallow had come to grudgingly respect. And the Hollis girl beside him, scanning the rims — she'd spotted the picket line already, or sensed it. She was pulling the others back toward the rock.

They knew. Of course they knew. Vasquez would have heard the horses in the night; the man tracked like the country itself had adopted him.

Pellis stood, brushed off his knees. "Time," he said, and nodded to the signal man, who raised a carbine toward the dawn sky.

"Wait." Sallow's voice came out flat and final, the voice he used on greenhorns and drunks, and the signal man froze with the rifle half-raised. "Nobody fires yet."

Pellis turned slowly. "Excuse me?"

"I want to talk to them first."

"There's nothing to talk about. The old woman's terms are terms. Ledger burns, witnesses go in the ground, we ride home rich." Pellis's calm had developed a hairline crack in it. "Duke. You've done this work a long time. Don't get sentimental at the finish line."

"It's not sentimental." Sallow stepped forward, away from the map, away from the signal man, putting the rising sun at his back so Pellis had to squint at him. "It's arithmetic. You want to hear arithmetic? There are nine of us up here and maybe eight of them down there, and half my riders are town boys who've never shot at anything but jackrabbits. The ranger has already killed one of ours. You start a firefight in a box canyon and some of those boys die — boys whose mothers I know, whose fathers I drink with. And for what? So a dying woman can leave her grandson a fortune built on an X-mark?" He paused. "You ever read the ledger, Pellis?"

Something flickered across Pellis's face. "That's not your concern."

"That's what I figured." Sallow walked to the rim's edge, where the drop fell away sheer into the canyon's throat, and stood there a moment with the wind pulling at his coat. Then he turned around and did the thing he'd spent thirty years not doing.

He made a decision that cost him.

"Pellis," he said. "Ada Corliss is seventy-four years old and dying of something the doctors won't name in front of her. She'll be dead inside a year, and everything she's protecting will belong to a grandson who, if the newspaper man I spoke to last week is any indication, doesn't want it. We are eight hired guns about to commit murder for a corpse's vanity." He let that sit. "I'm not giving the order."

The silence stretched. The signal man looked from Pellis to Sallow and back, the rifle wavering between sky and shoulder.

"You're relieved," Pellis said quietly. "Go home, Duke. Collect what's yours and ride. I'll tell Mrs. Corliss you took ill."

"No." Sallow unbuckled his gun belt — slowly, deliberately, letting every man on the rim see it — and slung it over his saddle horn. Unarmed. In front of all of them. "I'm not going home. I'm going down there."

* * *

He rode down alone, hands empty and lifted where the canyon watchers could see them, and the strangest part was how quiet his head finally was. Thirty years of noise — the wage, the reputation, the smooth-worn stone — and now, walking his horse into the mouths of the people he'd been sent to kill, silence. He'd have laughed at the irony if he'd had the breath for it.

Vasquez met him fifty yards out, rifle leveled, Mara Hollis flanking with an arrow nocked. Behind them the survivors watched from the rocks, and Sallow saw Tomás Reyes recognize him, saw the old guide's face close like a door.

"Sallow," Vasquez said. "You picked a hell of a morning to visit."

"I did." Sallow dismounted, keeping his palms wide. "Ranger, I'm going to say some things, and I'd take it kind if you let me finish before anybody shoots anybody." He swallowed. The words tasted like copper and thirty wasted years. "Last night I read pages of that ledger. My grandfather's mark is on one of them. An X, because the county clerk didn't bother teaching him to write his own name before he took his spring away." His voice held. Barely. "I've been Ada Corliss's dog for eleven years, and this morning I found out I've been her dog since 1893. I'm done."

Mara's arrow didn't waver. "Prove it."

"Ma'am, proof is the one thing I've got left." He turned and pointed up at the rim, where Pellis's men were visible now against the brightening sky, small and hard-edged. "Eight guns up there. Four of them outsiders on Corliss gold. Four of them town boys — Hargrove boys, the Mendoza kid, young Pete Ellison. Their grandfathers are in that ledger too, whether they know it or not." He looked back at Vasquez, and here was the part he'd rehearsed in the dark and still couldn't make sound like anything but what it was: surrender. "Ranger. You've got territorial authority. I've spent weeks telling my crew your badge wasn't worth the tin it's stamped from. I'd admire it if you'd spend it now. Deputize me, arrest me, whatever the law allows — but use it. Because it's the only authority on this river those boys up top might listen to besides mine, and mine's about to run out."

Silas studied him for a long moment. Sallow had been read by a lot of men in his life — buyers, bankers, the old woman herself — and none of them had ever read him like this, quick and total, the way you'd read weather.

"You understand what you're asking," Silas said. "Once I swear you in, there's no riding away from it. Corliss owns judges. She'll come for you legal and illegal both."

"She's already come for everybody," Sallow said. "At least this way I'll be facing the right direction."

Silas looked at Mara. Something passed between them — Sallow saw it, the silent arithmetic of two people who'd learned each other's minds under fire — and then the ranger pulled the tin star from his pocket, spat on his palm, and held out his hand.

"Duke Sallow," he said. "By the authority vested in me as a ranger of this territory, I hereby swear you in as a deputy. Swear it true."

Sallow gripped the offered hand. The palm was rough and warm and human, and the oath was five words long, and it went through him like water through cracked ground. "Sworn true," he said, and his voice broke on the second word, and he let it break, because a man ought to pay something for thirty years of silence and that was the cheapest coin he had.

Then he mounted, turned uphill, and rode back toward the rim with a deputy's star pinned where his employer's pin had been.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way bad things do.

Pellis met him at the picket line, and Sallow saw the moment the man understood — saw it land behind his eyes, the arithmetic of a professional recalculating. Eight guns, minus one, plus a sworn lawman standing between them and their orders.

"You're joking," Pellis said.

"Afraid not." Sallow swung down. "Boys! Gather round!" He pitched his voice to carry, the parade-ground voice, the foreman voice, and the four town boys came, because they'd been coming to that voice since they were kids stealing apples. The four outsiders hung back, hands near leather, watching Pellis. "Here's the situation. Mrs. Corliss hired us to murder a federal officer and six civilians, including a girl whose only crime is her father's name. I've read the orders and I've read the ledger, and I'm telling you the ledger has your grandfathers in it. Every family in Corlissville. Every acre we thought was bought fair." He drew breath. "As of an hour ago I'm a sworn deputy of the territory, and I am placing Ward Pellis and his men under arrest for conspiracy to commit murder. Anybody who wants to stand with them, stand now. Anybody who wants to stand with Corlissville—" he nodded downhill, toward the canyon, toward the survivors, toward the future, "—stand with me."

For three full seconds nobody moved. The wind moved. A hawk circled somewhere above the mesa, indifferent.

Then young Pete Ellison stepped forward, and then the Mendoza kid, and then the Hargrove brothers, slow and uncertain as men crossing ice. Four town boys, standing at Sallow's shoulder. The four outsiders looked at Pellis, and Pellis looked at the odds, and Sallow watched him do the math and hate it—

—and then Pellis smiled, and Sallow understood, one heartbeat too late, that the man had never intended to win by odds.

"Mrs. Corliss anticipated you, Duke," Pellis said pleasantly. "She doesn't rely on hired loyalty anymore. She relies on hired obedience." He raised two fingers.

The outsiders opened fire.

It was not a battle; it was a butcher's minute. The town boys scattered for cover, and Sallow dove behind his horse and heard the animal scream and go down, and rolled clear as bullets chewed the gravel where he'd knelt. Down in the canyon, Vasquez and the survivors returned fire — he heard the ranger's rifle, deliberate as a clock, and Mara's bow singing — and the fight became a confused, roaring chaos of echo and dust, muzzle flash stuttering along the rim, rock spalling into shrapnel.

One of the outsiders broke for the horses and young Pete Ellison, nineteen years old and terrified, shot him off the saddle — his first man, and Sallow saw the boy's face and knew it would live behind his eyes forever. Another outsider got the drop on the Mendoza kid, hammer cocking, and Sallow was closest, and there was no decision in it at all. That was the thing he'd finally learned, in the last hour of his life: real choices don't feel like choices. They feel like gravity.

He crossed the open ground and hit the Mendoza kid with his whole body, knocking him flat behind the rocks, and the bullet meant for the boy came through Sallow's chest instead, high on the left side, punching out through the back of his shoulder in a spray of heat he felt more than heard.

He sat down hard. The sky tilted. Somebody was shouting his name — the kid, crying, hands pressing the wound the way Ruth Ann had drilled into all of them weeks ago — and Sallow waved the boy off, gently, because he could feel where the bullet had gone and he'd seen enough gut-shot animals to do the arithmetic.

"Sit tight, son," he said. "You did fine."

The firing was slackening. Through the red haze closing at the edges of his vision, Sallow saw the ranger working upslope, methodical, and saw Pellis break from cover for the canyon's throat — running, running toward the ledger party, toward the girl — and then go down hard as Mara's arrow took him through the thigh, and Vasquez was on him, and it was over, mostly, the way storms end: raggedly, with thunder still rolling.

Footsteps. Boots in gravel, hurrying. Then Ruth Ann's face above him, competent and grim, her hands already at his chest, and behind her the ranger kneeling in the dust, and the Hollis girl, and old Tomás Reyes, who took Sallow's hand in both of his own gnarled ones.

"Reyes," Sallow managed. Blood made his voice bubble. "Your daughter. In town. Corliss has her people—"

"We know," Tomás said. "We know, amigo. Save your wind."

"Won't need it." Sallow tried to laugh and it cost him dearly. "Listen. Listen, Ranger. Paybook's in my saddlebag. Names, dates, accounts — Corliss's private bank in Denver, the account number's written inside the cover. Everything she pays from. Judge can freeze it." His breath hitched. "And the ledger page. My grandfather's page. I want it — I want it entered in the record. Tomás Sallow, sheep man, forty acres. Put his name in the court papers. Not the X. His name."

"You have my word," Silas said, and his voice was rough in a way Sallow had never heard it.

"Good." The sky was very blue suddenly, bluer than it had any right to be over Redrock Canyon, and Sallow found he wasn't afraid, which surprised him, because he'd assumed he would be. "Thirty years I worked for that woman. Eleven years knowing better. You know what I figure out at the end?" He coughed, wet and long. "A man can't choose what side he was born on. Only what side he dies on." His grip tightened on Tomás Reyes's hand. "Tell the boy — the Mendoza kid — tell him it wasn't his fault. Tell him he stood up. That's all any of us ever had to do, and I waited thirty years to do it, and it turns out it takes about a minute."

His gaze drifted to the rim, to the sunrise laying gold along the mesa tops, the country he'd ridden his whole life and never once seen until today.

"Well," he said. "Would you look at that."

And Duke Sallow, deputy of the territory, closed his eyes with the sun on his face, having paid in full.

* * *

They buried him that evening where he'd fallen, above the canyon, facing the river. But the living could not stop to mourn, because Tomás Reyes arrived at a gallop from the picket line holding a scrap of paper torn from a dead outsider's coat, and his face was gray as ash.

"Silas," he said, handing it down. "You need to see this."

The note was brief, in a clerk's precise hand:

*If operation compromised, proceed to secondary targets. Families of Reyes (Corlissville), Okafor (town). Detain. Hold as leverage.*

Below it, fresh ink, newer: *Ward P. confirms compromise. Execute contingency.*

Silas read it twice, and when he looked up, Mara was already saddling, and the fear in the camp had changed shape entirely — no longer fear for themselves, which they'd learned to carry, but fear for the people they loved, hundreds of miles behind them in a town that water built.

"How long ago was this written?" Silas asked.

Tomás's jaw worked. "Judging by the ink. Two days."

Two days. A day and a half's hard ride to Corlissville. Silas closed his fist around the paper and felt the whole shape of the war change beneath him — because Ada Corliss had stopped hunting witnesses, and started hunting hostages, and the race was no longer to the courthouse.

It was to the town.
