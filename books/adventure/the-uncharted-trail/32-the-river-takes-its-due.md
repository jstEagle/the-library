## 32. The River Takes Its Due

The gunfight lasted ninety seconds and felt like a year.

When it ended, the canyon floor was smoke and ringing silence. Sallow lay on his side in the red dirt, one hand pressed to his chest, still breathing — barely. Two of Ada Corliss's outside guns were down for good. The rest had thrown their rifles into the dust and put their hands up when Daniel Corliss's voice cracked across the rocks like a whip made of paper.

Silas didn't watch any of it. He was watching Ward Pellis.

The man had gone still the moment the shooting started — still the way a snake goes still, not the way a stone does. Silas had spent twelve years reading country and the people moving through it, and everything in Pellis's posture said *calculating exits*. When Daniel stepped forward with the ledger held high and began to read, Pellis's eyes weren't on the page or the crowd. They were on the river.

So when Pellis broke left through the willows, Silas was already running.

"Pellis!" he shouted, more to mark him for the others than to stop him. It didn't stop him. The man could move for a soft-handed agent of a dying empire — long strides, elbows pumping, crashing downslope toward the water with the ledger's leather cover still clamped under his arm where he'd snatched it off the rock beside Daniel.

Behind Silas, boots on gravel. Mara. Of course Mara.

They came out of the willows together and the river opened before them, wide and brown-green and glittering with morning, running high with snowmelt from the eastern forks. And there — hip-deep already, wading hard for the far channel where the current braided around a gravel bar — went Ward Pellis, holding the ledger above his head like an offering.

Or like something he meant to drown.

"He'll sink it!" Mara gasped. "Deep water, mid-channel — we'd never—"

"I see it."

Silas hit the shallows at a dead run. The cold took his breath, then his knees, then his balance; he went down to his waist and kept going, dragging himself forward on raw will while the river shoved back like a living thing with opinions. Ahead of him Pellis glanced over his shoulder, saw them coming, and made his decision.

He raised the ledger higher.

"No you don't—" Silas lunged.

Pellis hurled it. The leather book turned end over end against the sky, a dark shape against the white glare, and for one suspended heartbeat Silas saw the whole future inside that arc: three towns, a judge two hundred miles east, Elena Brandt's affidavit, Peter Liu's photographs, Tomás Reyes's testimony, Hollis's name cleared or buried forever — all of it spinning in the air above stolen water.

Then Mara hit Pellis from the side.

She'd cut right while Silas went straight, wading the shallows where Hollis had died, and she took the man at the waist like Cass Duval taking down a steer. They went under together. Silas dove after the book instead of the men, arms slicing, fingers closing on wet leather just as the current tried to peel it away. He got both hands on it, hugged it to his chest, and let the river carry him ten yards downstream until his boots found bottom.

He stood up gasping, the ledger dripping against his ribs, and looked back.

Mara had Pellis by the collar in knee-deep water, and she was not gentle about it. She hauled his head up out of the current and drove him stumbling toward shore, her face streaked with mud and river spray, her eyes carrying something Silas recognized and would never forget: the look of a woman standing in the exact water where her father's body had been found, holding the man who put it there.

"Walk," she said. "Walk, or I drown you here and tell them the river did it."

"Mara." Silas waded toward them, book held high. "Not like this."

"Like hell not like this."

"Not like this," he said again, quieter. "He walks. He talks. That's worth more than his corpse."

For a long moment the only sound was the river working the gravel bars, patient as geology. Then Mara shoved Pellis forward so hard he nearly went down, and the three of them came out of the water together, dripping, muddy, alive.

* * *

The crowd at the camp had grown. Word had run up and down the canyon on foot and horseback faster than any radio: ranchers from the dispossessed parcels, Brandt store hands, two of Sallow's own men with their hats in their hands, Daniel Corliss still holding the first page of the ledger like a man afraid to set down something holy. Ines Okafor stood at the front with her notebook already open. Ruth Ann knelt over Sallow, doing what she could, which wasn't much and everyone knew it.

Tomás Reyes stood apart, rifle slung, watching Pellis with an expression Silas had last seen on men looking at old graves.

They dumped Pellis on a flat rock at the waterline. He was fifty-some years old, well-dressed even soaked, and he sat there shivering with his hands laced behind his head because Mara had tied them with the same rope she used on the supply rafts. He looked at the circle of faces and did the arithmetic Silas had watched him do his whole career: odds, options, exits.

There were none.

"Ward Pellis," Silas said. He crouched so they were eye to eye, and he kept his voice level, ranger-calm, the voice he used on panicked travelers and cornered animals. "You've got one job left in this territory, and it's talking. You're going to tell these people what happened to Nathaniel Hollis."

Pellis smiled. It was a thin thing, professional, the smile of a man who'd talked his way past harder rooms than this. "I don't know what you mean. I'm the sponsor's liaison. I was never even at the expedition camp. Ask anyone—"

"We are asking," said Tomás.

His voice came out low and rough, and it changed the shape of the whole circle. He stepped forward until he stood over Pellis, blocking the sun, and Silas saw the guide's hands trembling — not with fear.

"Three days before Hollis died, I saw you come into camp," Tomás said. "You wore a fisherman's vest and you told everyone you were checking on the sponsor's investment. I walked you to the cave myself, because Hollis asked me to. You saw the ledger. You spent forty minutes alone with Hollis at the entrance. And three nights later, I found my oldest friend facedown in the shallows right there—" his hand shook as he pointed at the water, at the exact stretch Mara had dragged Pellis through — "with the back of his skull stove in by a rock nobody could find afterward. Because somebody carried it away."

"A rock in a riverbed," Pellis said. "Accidents happen in country like this."

"Hollis swam that stretch since he was nine years old," Mara said. Her voice cracked but held. "He knew every stone in it."

"Then he slipped."

Silas reached into his coat and brought out the paybook — the dead gunman's, the one he'd shown Duke Sallow at the parley, the one with its columns of cash payments and no names. He flipped it open to the last filled page and held it where Pellis could read it upside down.

"Corliss pays cash," Silas said. "No names. Deniable deaths. But cash leaves a trail too, Mr. Pellis, if you know which ledgers to cross-check. This book has dates. The night Hollis died, there's an entry. A bonus entry. Double rate, marked *special service, completed*. And there's exactly one man on Ada Corliss's payroll whose special services require him to be near that expedition." He let the page hang in the air. "I pulled the dispatch records from the Corlissville office two weeks ago. Your name is in the margin. Pencil. Somebody wrote it there and thought better of it and didn't erase it hard enough."

Pellis's smile stayed on, but Silas watched it die behind the eyes. It was like watching a lamp go out in a window while the house still stands.

"You're bluffing," Pellis said. "A ranger. A girl. A bunch of—"

"A bunch of witnesses," said Daniel Corliss.

He came forward slowly, and the crowd parted for him the way it parts for weather. He was twenty-four years old and looked older this morning, and he held the ledger's first page in both hands like a verdict. He crouched next to Silas, facing Pellis, and his voice was quiet enough that everyone had to lean in to hear it, which was exactly the point.

"My grandmother sent you," Daniel said. "My family's money built every road you ever drove. And I have spent the last hour reading aloud, to these people, how that money was made. Transfers signed under duress. Ranchers starved off their own land. My name is on the other side of that history, Mr. Pellis, and I can't wash it out — but you can still be honest about your part in it. That's the only clean water left anywhere near you. Take it."

The river ran. Somewhere upslope, Ruth Ann murmured to Sallow, and Sallow groaned once and went quiet. Ines's pencil scratched. A dozen faces waited.

Pellis looked at the water. At the ledger. At Mara Hollis, standing over him with river mud drying on her arms and her father's grave two hundred yards upstream marked in stones.

And he broke. Not dramatically — no sobbing, no collapse. He just exhaled, long and slow, like a man setting down a load he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy, and started talking.

"He wouldn't sell," Pellis said. His voice was flat, reciting, the voice of a confession rehearsed in the dark for weeks without knowing it. "Hollis. I offered him anything. Money, publication under Corliss imprint with edits, a chair at the university, his daughter's schooling for life. He sat there in the cave with that ledger in his lap and he laughed at me. Said the numbers belonged to the people the numbers were taken from. Said he was going to mail copies from the next town regardless of what I offered or what I threatened." Pellis's throat worked. "Mrs. Corliss's instruction was simple. The ledger doesn't reach a courthouse. The man doesn't reach a newspaper. She didn't say kill him. She never says it. She says *handle it quietly*, and men like me hear the rest."

"You hit him," Tomás said. It wasn't a question. It was a wound being reopened on purpose, because Tomás Reyes needed to hear it said aloud or he'd carry the not-knowing to his own grave.

"At the camp. Night of the ninth. He'd moved the ledger to the cache and told the camp he was floating downriver at dawn to wire the newspapers. I followed him to the shallows. We argued. He turned his back on me — turned his *back*, like I was nobody, like I was furniture — and I picked up a rock." Pellis stared at his own bound hands. "One blow. He went down in the water and the current took him a little way and I... I made it look like a drowning accident. Took the rock with me and dropped it in the deep channel half a mile down. Smashed the camp radio on my way out so no one could call it in fast. Reported a tragic loss to Mrs. Corliss by shortwave from the ridge, collected my fee, and went home to sleep fine, which is the part that should tell you everything about what I am."

Nobody spoke. The river filled the silence, indifferent and enormous.

Mara had gone very still. Silas watched her take it in — the mechanics of it, the banality, the fact that her father had died not in some grand conspiracy but in a shallow argument between a proud man and a hired functionary — and he saw the moment grief and fury fused into something she'd have to carry the rest of her life. He stepped closer to her, shoulder almost touching hers, and she leaned into it, one degree, barely, and that was enough.

"The ledger," Ines said suddenly. "If you destroyed the evidence of the thefts, why not destroy the ledger itself that night? You had it in reach."

"Because it wasn't mine to burn," Pellis said, and for the first time he sounded tired instead of smooth. "Mrs. Corliss wanted it brought to her. Whole. Proof of her father's hand in it, in his own signature — she needed to know exactly what existed and where, so nothing could ever surface twice. I hid it back in the cave and reported its location. She sealed the cave story up tight, scared the expedition into silence, and planned to collect it once the search died down." He looked up at Daniel, and something almost like wonder crossed his ruined face. "She never counted on you."

"She never counts on anyone," Daniel said. "That's the whole empire."

Silas stood. His legs ached, his coat dripped, and the ledger against his chest weighed what it had always weighed and also ten times more. He looked around the circle — Tomás with his eyes closed and his lips moving in something that might have been prayer or apology; Ines writing fast; Peter Liu pale and steady with his camera bag, already photographing Pellis's face from three angles because evidence was a language Peter had learned to speak fluently; Cass Duval propped against a pack with a bandaged shoulder, grinning through the pain like a man watching a bull get what was coming to him; Ruth Ann rising from Sallow's side and shaking her head once, gently, at Silas.

Duke Sallow was dead. He'd bought them this morning with his life, misdirecting his own men into the wrong draw, and Silas filed the grief away where he kept all the others — a debt to be paid later, at a graveside, properly.

"Well," Silas said to Pellis. "You heard yourself. So did they." He nodded at the circle, at the ranchers, at Ines's notebook, at Peter's lens. "Every word. On the record. That confession travels east tomorrow along with the ledger, the photographs, the affidavits, and a federal warrant request addressed to Judge Amos Pell by name. You'll ride in a wagon, tied, in front of God and everybody. No more quiet."

Pellis laughed — one dry bark, joyless. "She'll send more men."

"She'll try," said Mara.

"She's dying, Mr. Pellis," Daniel said quietly. "And her empire just learned how to read."

* * *

They loaded Pellis at noon, and the column formed up along the riverbank — horses, wagons, a litter for Sallow's body wrapped in canvas, the survivors riding together for the first time since the night everything broke. Mara came to stand beside Silas at the water's edge while the others mounted up.

They looked at the shallows together. Ordinary water. Gravel bars. Light.

"He confessed right here," Mara said. "Where Dad died. In front of the whole territory, practically."

"Muddy," Silas said. "Imperfect. Late by three months and a corpse." He turned the ledger over in his hands, drying now, its spine cracked, its pages swollen from one dunking too many. "But real. Most justice isn't clean, Mara. It's this — wet, tired, everybody bleeding, and the truth finally said out loud where the right people could hear it."

Mara wiped her face with a muddy sleeve and managed something that was half laugh, half sob. "My father used to say the river keeps what it's given. Turns out it gives things back, too."

"It does," Silas said. "If enough people stand on the bank and ask."

Upstream, Tomás Reyes mounted his horse slowly, an old man climbing an old saddle, and looked back once at the water — at the spot, at the stones, at the place he'd dug a grave in secret to hide a murder he hadn't committed and couldn't prove. He caught Silas's eye and nodded, once, the nod of a debt acknowledged that would never fully be paid and didn't need to be.

Silas swung up onto his horse, the ledger wrapped in oilcloth and strapped tight behind the cantle, and pointed them east.

Two hundred miles. One judge. Sundown deadlines and warrants waiting to be born.

"Ride," he said.
