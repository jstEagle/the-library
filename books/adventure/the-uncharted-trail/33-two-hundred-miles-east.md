## 33. Two Hundred Miles East

The courthouse smelled like paper and floor wax, and Mara Hollis nearly wept at the smell of it.

Two hundred miles. It had taken them three days — the truck limping the last fifty on a cracked axle block, Cass riding flat in the bed with Ruth Ann monitoring a fever that finally, mercifully, had broken outside of Prewitt. They'd washed the blood off in a stock tank at a windmill Silas swore wasn't on any map. They'd buried Tomás's hat somewhere along the way, lost to the wind off a ridge, and nobody had stopped for it because stopping felt like something they could no longer afford.

Now Mara stood on the courthouse steps in a shirt stiff with dried river silt and someone else's blood, and the brass handle of the door was the most beautiful thing she had ever touched.

"Go on," Silas said behind her. "I'll bring the rest."

She pushed the door open.

* * *

Judge Amos Pell turned out to be a small man with enormous hands, the kind of hands that looked like they'd been swapped onto him from a blacksmith, and he was eating a sandwich at his bench when the clerk ushered them in. He didn't put the sandwich down. He looked at Mara, at Tomás Reyes behind her with his arm in a sling, at Ines Okafor holding the ledger wrapped in oilcloth like it was a sleeping infant, and he set the sandwich down.

"You'd be the Hollis girl," he said.

"Mara Hollis. This is Tomás Reyes, Dr. Ines Okafor, Peter Liu. The others are outside. Judge, we've come two hundred miles to put something in your hands, and I need you to understand that people have already died for it, and more will die if you put it in a drawer."

Pell's eyebrows went up, but his voice stayed level. "That's a hell of an opening statement, Miss Hollis. You been practicing law?"

"No. Just burying my father."

The room went quiet. Somewhere down the hall a clock ticked with a lazy, unhurried sound, the tick of a building that had never been shot at.

Pell stood. "Clerk, lock the front door. Then get me the court reporter." He looked back at Mara. "You've got until sundown, and then I go home to my supper. Convince me."

* * *

They laid it all out on the judge's bench like surgeons laying out instruments.

The ledger first. Ines unwrapped the oilcloth with hands that shook only a little, and Pell put on spectacles and bent over the leather-bound pages, and Mara watched his face as he read. She'd seen that face before — on her father, the night he'd first opened the thing in the cave. The slow change from curiosity to concentration to something colder. The moment a man realizes the ground under a town is hollow.

"Eighteen ninety-three," Pell read aloud, mostly to himself. "Transfer of riparian rights, upper basin, from the Vasquez holdings to Corliss Water and Cattle, consideration of... forty dollars." He looked up. "Forty dollars."

"Signed under duress," Ines said. "Read the marginalia. There's a note in the clerk's hand on the next page — *signatory illiterate, mark witnessed under threat of eviction, water diverted prior to execution.* They cut off his water before they even had him sign. Then they paid him forty dollars for a river."

"Vasquez," Pell said. His eyes went to Silas, who stood by the window with his hat in his hands, and something moved across the judge's face — recognition, or the shape of an old story arriving at its last page. "There were Vasquez ranches up there. Good ones. Gone by the time I was reading law."

"Mine was one of them," Silas said. No bitterness in it. Just fact, laid down like a stone.

Pell took off his spectacles and pinched the bridge of his nose. Then, "Go on."

Peter Liu went next. His hands were steadier than Mara had ever seen them, and his voice was quiet and precise, the voice of a man who had rehearsed his confession on a long drive and found it had gotten lighter with every mile. He told Pell about the photographs — every page of the ledger, shot with the expedition's survey camera before the camp burned. He told him about mailing the film to his advisor at the university. He told him, in a voice that did not quite stay flat, that he believed that mailing was how the killers had found them so fast, and that a grad student named Peter Liu might have signed eleven death warrants with a postage stamp.

"Your advisor's name," Pell said.

"Dr. Edwin Marsh. Department of Hydrology."

Pell wrote it down without comment, but Mara saw him underline it twice.

Then Elena Brandt's affidavit, notarized in Corlissville by a justice of the peace who had done the work with his door open and his coat on, ready to run. Mara read it aloud because Elena had asked her to, in the shop, pressing the paper into her hands with both of her own: *I was nine years old when the Corliss men closed the headgate above our place. I watched my father beg. I watched them laugh. I can name eleven families. I will name them under oath.*

And then the last item, the one Mara had carried in her breast pocket for two hundred miles, wrapped in a handkerchief because she couldn't stand to let it touch anything else.

Ward Pellis's confession.

She told it plainly, because Pell had asked her to tell it plainly. The ferry crossing. The ambush. The flight downriver. The box canyon at dawn, and the standoff at Redrock, and Daniel Corliss standing unarmed between two lines of guns reading his own family's founding theft out loud. And then the shallows — her father's shallows, the exact bend of river where Hollis had died — and Pellis wading in with the ledger page he'd torn out, and Silas and Mara closing on him from both banks, and Pellis, cornered in the water with a dozen armed men watching from the rocks, deciding that a confession was better than a bullet.

"He said he did it," Mara said. "He said my father told him the expedition was going public with the ledger, and Pellis knew Corliss would kill everyone in camp to bury it, so he decided to kill only one. He said it like math. Like he was doing my father a favor." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it. "Twelve people heard him say it. Tomás Reyes identified him as the man he saw at my father's tent that night. And Daniel Corliss — the grandson — heard it too, and he'll swear to it. He told me so on the drive out. He said, *I'm done inheriting lies.*"

Pell was silent for a long moment.

"Where's Pellis now?"

"Jail cell in Redrock," Silas said. "Sheriff there is a decent man named Cobb. He's holding him on the murder, and holding three of Sallow's gunmen besides, though two of those are more witness than prisoner now. Duke Sallow himself—" Silas paused, and Mara saw him choose the words carefully, the way you step carefully on scree. "Duke Sallow took a bullet for me. He's buried upriver. Whatever he was, he finished as something else."

Pell nodded slowly. He looked down at the ledger, at the affidavit, at Peter's contact sheets fanned out like a hand of cards, each one a photograph of a page that had cost a man his life.

"You understand what you're asking me," he said. "Ada Corliss owns the sheriff in the county where these crimes occurred. She owns two of the three newspapers. She owns the irrigation district, the bank, and half the legislature, if rumor serves. If I issue warrants and they get quashed — if this comes back on you as kidnapping, as theft, as anything at all — there won't be a jurisdiction in the territory that'll touch your case again. You get one shot at this. Maybe less than one."

"Judge," Tomás Reyes said. He hadn't spoken since they'd entered, and when he did, his voice was gravel and river-bottom. He stood, awkward with the sling, and Mara realized he was holding his hat — the new one, Cass's spare, that someone had scrounged for him in Prewitt. "I guided that expedition. I dug Hollis's grave with these hands because I was afraid, and I've been afraid every day since, and I am done with it. I am sixty-one years old. I don't have time left to be afraid in." He set the hat on the bench, over the ledger, like a man closing a book. "Issue the warrants."

* * *

They worked until sundown.

Pell was a different man once the decision was made — Mara could see it happen, could see the enormous hands stop hesitating and start moving. He dictated to the court reporter in a voice that carried through the open windows into the street, where a small crowd had gathered, because a locked courthouse door in the middle of a weekday was the most interesting thing to happen in that town since the rail spur went in. He issued a warrant for the arrest of Ward Pellis on the charge of murder in the first degree. He issued one for the Corliss agent in Redrock whose name they'd pulled off the dead gunman's paybook. He issued a subpoena for Dr. Edwin Marsh of the university, and — Mara held her breath when she heard it — one for Ada Corliss herself, to appear and answer questions in the matter of the Hollis expedition, returnable in thirty days.

"Thirty days," Ines murmured. "She'll run everything through thirty lawyers in thirty days."

"She's dying," Mara said. "Peter told me what the shopkeeper said. She may not have thirty days. That's not a mercy, Ines. That's just the truth. The empire doesn't die with her. It has to die in court, in the newspapers, in the water law. That's why we're here."

By the time the sun came through the west windows in long orange bars, the clerk was stamping the last of the warrants and Pell was writing — longhand, in a fine careful script that looked strange coming out of those blacksmith hands — a letter. When he finished, he sealed it and handed it to Silas.

"Territorial marshal's office," he said. "You'll pass through the capital on your way home, or close enough. Hand-deliver this. Tell them Judge Pell requests federal oversight of the Corliss water rights matter — *requests*, mind, because a district judge asking for federal eyes is either a coward or a man who knows how deep the rot goes, and I'd rather they think me the second." He took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, and for the first time all day he smiled, a small dry thing. "Also tell them I've ordered the ledger photographed and the original sealed in the court vault, with copies to three newspapers I happen to know aren't bought. If anything happens to this courthouse, the story survives. That's the whole trick of it, Miss Hollis. Truth is hard to kill if you make enough copies."

Mara thought of Peter, hunched in a tent by lamplight, photographing page after page while the man who'd be dead by morning slept ten feet away. Truth is hard to kill if you make enough copies. Her father had found the ledger. Peter had multiplied it. Elena Brandt had signed her name to it. And now a judge with blacksmith's hands was mailing it to the world.

It had taken all of them. That was the thing she hadn't understood out on the river, alone and grieving and ready to shoot the first man who tracked her. No one carries this alone. It's too heavy. That's not the design's flaw — it's the design.

* * *

They walked out of the courthouse into the last of the light, and the town had come to see them.

Not a crowd, exactly. Maybe forty people along the boardwalks, drawn by the locked doors and the dictation and the rumor that was already running up the street faster than any of them could walk it — *the Hollis girl, the Corliss ledger, warrants, the Corliss, actual warrants.* A woman in a feed-store apron stood with her hand over her mouth. An old man by the hitching rail took off his hat as Mara passed, and she didn't know him, would never know him, but she understood the gesture, and it undid her more than the warrants had.

She stopped in the middle of the street.

For eleven days she had been running — from the camp, from the grave, from Sallow's guns, from the thing her father's death had made of her. She had slept in arroyos and eaten cold beans and held an arrow to a stranger's throat, and every mile had been measured in fear. And now she stood on a quiet street two hundred miles from the river, and the running was over, and she found she didn't know how to stand still. Her body kept bracing for the next ridge, the next ambush, the next name on the next warrant.

Silas came and stood beside her. He didn't say anything. He'd gotten good at that — at the silence that wasn't empty, that was more like a hand on the shoulder than a hand over the mouth.

"They know," she said finally. Her voice came out rough. "It's out. It's in a vault and it's in photographs and it's in a judge's docket and half this street already knows. They can't bury it now. Nobody can bury it now."

"No," Silas said. "Nobody can."

She laughed — a short, cracked, astonished sound that was half sob and had no shame in it. "It's done. It's actually — we did it. We got it here. All of us." She turned and looked down the street at her ragged, battered, impossible family: Ines arguing happily with the court reporter about deposition dates, Peter asleep sitting up against a post with the camera case still strapped to his chest, Cass upright on the truck's tailgate for the first time in three days, pale as milk and grinning like the devil, Ruth Ann and Tomás standing together in the gold light, and Ferro leaning on the fender with his bandaged shoulder, whistling something low and off-key.

And Ada Corliss, somewhere east in a house full of dark wood and older money, reading — or soon to read — the first wire out of this town. Mara felt no triumph at the thought. Only a tired, steady certainty, like the river's pull. *You built it on stolen water, and the water is going home.*

The warrants would be served by morning. Pellis would stand trial. The newspapers — three of them, the unbought ones — would have the story by the weekend, and after the weekend there would be no putting it back in the cave, no burying it in the river, no killing it in the dark. Her father's name would come out of the ground clean. That was the part that made her press her knuckles to her eyes right there in the street, in front of forty strangers, and let it happen. *He didn't die an accident. He died a witness. And the witness won.*

Silas's hand settled on her shoulder, light as a bird landing.

"Supper?" he said.

She wiped her face and laughed again. "Supper."

They were halfway to the diner when the telegraph boy came running up the boardwalk, wire slip in his fist, calling out for Judge Pell — and Mara, turning, saw the clerk take it, saw him read it, saw his face change. He looked up the street, straight at her, and started walking fast.

"Miss Hollis," he called. "Miss Hollis — you'll want to hear this."
