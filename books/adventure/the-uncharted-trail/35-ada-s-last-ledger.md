## 35. Ada's Last Ledger

The newspapers arrived on a Tuesday, folded in a canvas mail sack that smelled of dust and axle grease, and Ada Corliss had them carried up to her room before the nurse finished changing the water in the pitcher.

She was past pretending she didn't know what they would say. She'd known since the shortwave went strange and quiet, since Duke Sallow's voice stopped coming through on schedule, since Daniel walked out of this house with his jaw set like a man leaving a church he no longer believed in. The words were already written. She only hadn't read them yet. There was a difference, she told herself, between knowing a thing and letting it into the room.

"Shall I open them, ma'am?" asked Ruthie, the day nurse — not to be confused with that other Ruth Ann, the medic, whose name now appeared in print beside words like *witness* and *survivor*. Ruthie was nineteen and had hands like a bird's nest, all quick small movements, and she was the only person left in the house who looked at Ada without calculation.

"I'll do it," Ada said. "You bring me my glasses and then you can go fuss at something else."

Ruthie brought the glasses. She hesitated at the door, and Ada knew what the hesitation cost her, because the girl had been reading the papers too. Everyone in Corlissville had been reading them. That was the nature of an empire — you built it so that everything inside its walls belonged to you, and then one morning the walls printed their own news.

"Ma'am," Ruthie said carefully, "the store closed today. Mr. Brandt's old store, I mean — the new one. Men came and took down the sign."

Ada looked at her for a long moment. "Did they now," she said, and it was not a question, and Ruthie understood she was dismissed, and went.

* * *

Ada read alone by the east window, where the light came in clean and did her no favors. Her hands shook — they had shaken for months now, the tremor working its way up from somewhere deep, some engine failing — but her eyes were still good through the glass, and she read every word.

**CORLISS WATER RIGHTS DECLARED VOID BY FEDERAL JUDGE**, said the territorial paper, the big one, the one her father had once bought three favors from and a fourth from its editor's widow. Below it: **SCRIP PAY OUTLAWED IN CORLISSVILLE DISTRICT — WORKERS TO BE PAID IN CURRENCY OR GRAIN AT THEIR CHOICE.**

Below that, smaller, in the kind of type reserved for obituaries and weather: **MRS. ADA CORLISS INDICTED; CHARGES INCLUDE CONSPIRACY, BRIBERY, AND THE DEATH OF EXPEDITION LEADER EDMUND HOLLIS.**

They had spelled her name right. That was something. Her father had always said that if a thing was worth doing, it was worth doing with your full name attached, though he had never once done it himself. Every document in the family vault bore his mark or a lawyer's, never his hand. He had stolen a river and signed nothing. She had spent seventy years learning the lesson wrong: she thought he'd taught her discretion. He had taught her cowardice dressed as caution, and she had upholstered it well.

There was a photograph accompanying the indictment notice. Not of her — of the courthouse steps, Judge Amos Pell standing at the rail with his hand raised against the wind, and behind him a cluster of people she studied longer than she meant to. The Hollis girl, thin as a whip stock, holding a leather ledger against her chest like an infant. The Reyes man, gray-bearded, hat in his hands. A tall ranger half a step apart from the rest, the way a fence post stands apart from a grove — near them, belonging to the picture, but planted in his own ground.

Silas Vasquez. She knew the name before the caption confirmed it. Of course she did. Miguel Vasquez's boy. She had signed — no, she had *approved*, which was how she'd learned to think of signing — the paper that took the Vasquez spread in '09, when Silas was nine years old and his father stood in her father's office with his hat crushed in both fists, saying *there has to be another way, there has to be*, and her father had said, kindly, the way you'd speak to a horse you were about to shoot, *there is always another way, señor, just never for men who wait until the water's already sold.*

Her father had laughed about it afterward at dinner. She remembered that. She remembered laughing too, because she was fourteen and wanted to be the kind of woman who could hear a thing like that and keep eating. She had succeeded. It had taken her whole life, and she had succeeded, and here was the bill.

She set the paper down. The tremor in her hands made the pages rattle like something trying to get out.

* * *

In the afternoon she had them bring the ledgers up from the vault.

Not the one everyone was talking about — the Hollis ledger, the 1890s book with its coerced X-marks and its river numbers that didn't add up unless you assumed theft. That one was two hundred miles east under federal seal, being read aloud by men who had never gotten their boots muddy in her valley. No. She wanted her own ledgers. The real ones. Forty years of them, bound in calfskin, her own fair hand in iron-gall ink, recording every transfer, every scrip issuance, every "adjustment" of a rancher's allotment, every payment made in cash to men with no names in the paybook.

Duke Sallow had kept such a book for her for eleven years. She wondered what had become of him. The papers said a gunman died at Redrock; the papers did not say which side of which gun. She found she could not make herself hope it was Sallow, and she found she could not make herself hope it wasn't, and she sat with that contradiction the way she'd learned to sit with all of them — upright, unhurried, letting it pass through her like weather.

The ledgers filled the long table by the window. The nurse fussed about drafts; Ada dismissed her until evening. Then she began at the first volume, 1911, the year her father died and she took the books over, and she read herself the way a judge would read her, page by page, line by line, and she did not flinch, because flinching was for people with time.

What surprised her was not the thefts. She had expected the thefts. What surprised her was the arithmetic of smallness. Entry after entry, the amounts were so modest. Four hundred acres here. A season's water allotment there. Eleven dollars owed to a widow named Brandt, marked *disputed — settled by pressure.* The empire that had swallowed three towns had been assembled not in grand strokes but in pinches, the way you'd bleed a steer a pint at a time so it never lay down. Nobody had ever been robbed all at once. They had each been robbed just enough, just slowly enough, to believe they were losing to weather.

That was the family genius, she thought. Not cruelty. Patience. Her father used to say God gave the desert two kinds of rain — the flood that washes everything away and the drizzle that soaks in — and that a smart man farmed the drizzle. She had farmed the drizzle for sixty years. And now a girl with a dead father's ledger had opened the sky all at once.

Ada turned to the last volume, the current one, and found the entry she'd been looking for. Three weeks old. *Disbursements, special: six men, cash, no names.* The order that had gone out before Sallow ever radioed her. She had written it herself, in her own steady hand, before the tremor. She looked at those six words a long time.

Then she pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward her, uncapped the pen, and began to write.

* * *

*Daniel,*

*By the time you read this I will be dead, and the lawyers will have told you what I've left you, and the newspapers will have told you what I've cost you, and neither account will be true, so I am writing the third one myself.*

*You told me you didn't want an inheritance built on this. You were right, and I want you to know I knew you were right while I was doing it, which is worse than not knowing. A woman who does evil in ignorance is a fool. A woman who does it knowing, and calls it love, is something else, and you may name it yourself because I have spent my whole life naming it and I never found a word that fit.*

*Here is what happened, all of it, in order.*

*My father stole the river. Not gradually, not entirely — he stole the headwaters first and let the rest dry up on its own, which is how theft looks when it wears a suit. The transfers in the Hollis ledger are genuine. I have seen them. My grandfather held men at gunpoint — politely, with witnesses, the way respectable people did it then — and made them mark X on paper surrendering water they had diverted with their own hands. One of those X-marks belonged to a Sallow. I learned that only recently, and I confess it gave me a bad night, which is the closest I have come in sixty years to paying for anything.*

*I continued it. Not because I loved the money — the money stopped mattering to me around 1930, when I realized I could not remember the last thing I had bought that I wanted — but because stopping felt like dying. An empire is a machine that must be fed, and I had become part of the mechanism. I told myself I was protecting the workers. I paid them in scrip so they could only spend their wages back into my store, and I called it stability. I foreclosed on ranchers during the drought years and I called it consolidation. When Edmund Hollis found the ledger in that cave, I did not order his death. I want that on the record, even the record nobody will believe. I ordered him handled. I hired Ward Pellis years ago for exactly this kind of handling, and I knew what handling meant, and I signed nothing, and I let myself not know. That is my confession, Daniel: not the crime, but the not-knowing, purchased deliberately, maintained daily, like a garden.*

*Your grandfather's fortune, my fortune, the house you grew up in, the school wing bearing our name — all of it sits on water that belonged to other people, taken a pint at a time. I am telling you this in writing, witnessed and sealed, to be delivered after my death, because I am a coward to the end and could not bear to watch your face while you read it. But I am also telling you so that you can give it to the judge. Every page. Let Pell have it. Let it stand beside the Hollis ledger and Elena Brandt's affidavit — yes, I know about Elena Brandt; I have known about Elena Brandt for fifty years, and I let her keep her store because a witness you leave alive is a witness you can pretend doesn't exist.*

*Give them everything, and settle the claims fairly, and do not fight the lawsuits, because fighting them will cost more than losing them and will make you the kind of man I spent your childhood afraid you'd become. Sell the house if you want. Keep the name or drop it. But whatever you do, do not let anyone tell you I was a great woman undone by bad luck. I was a careful thief undone by arithmetic. The numbers finally came due, and they were brought by a ranger's son whose father I ruined, and I find I cannot even hate him for it, which tells you something about how much hating was ever really in me, and how much was habit.*

*One more thing. I have enclosed the deed to the north pasture — the good water, the spring that never failed. It is deeded jointly to the ten names listed below. Yes, ten. I have been reading the newspapers too. If I cannot undo sixty years, I can at least choose what the last acre does. Call it restitution if that helps you carry it. Call it a bribe to history. I no longer care what it's called. Water should run to whoever stays.*

*Your grandmother,*
*Ada*

*P.S. — Your grandfather's vault key is taped beneath the drawer of my writing desk. There are things in that vault I could not bring myself to burn and could not bear to explain. Burn them yourself. That is my last order, and the only one I hope you disobey is none.*

She read it through twice, corrected nothing, and sealed it. Then she sat looking at the window a long time, at the light going amber on the mesa, at the irrigation ditches below the town running full and silver in the evening — her ditches, her water, running on schedule, obedient to the last.

* * *

Ruthie came in at eight with the tray: broth, tea, the little cup of laudanum the doctor had prescribed against the pain, which Ada took or didn't take depending on whether she wanted the night to have edges.

"Leave the cup," Ada said. "And sit a moment, girl. My hands are tired and I want someone to read to me."

Ruthie brightened — she was starved for any task softer than bedpans — and reached for the newspaper on the nightstand. "Which part?"

"The rancher claims. The ones filing on the old land. Read me the names."

So Ruthie read. Names upon names, families Ada had known all her life from the wrong side of a desk: Vasquez cousins reclaiming the east bench. A Duval filing on bottomland. A Brandt grandson, testifying before Judge Pell with his grandmother's affidavit in his fist. On and on, the dispossessed coming home across paper, and Ada listened with her eyes closed like a woman hearing music from a house she'd been locked out of.

"They say the river runs clearer already," Ruthie said, setting the paper down. "Since the diversions stopped. Down past the narrows. Fisherman told my brother."

"Do you believe that?"

"Everybody believes it. Whether it's true or not." Ruthie smiled, apologetic, young. "It's nicer believing it."

"Yes," Ada said. "That was always my business. Nicer believing."

Ruthie gathered the tray, hesitated again at the door — that same bird-quick hesitation — and this time Ada spoke first.

"Girl. A question, and answer me honest, because you're the last person left who will."

"Ma'am?"

Ada turned her head on the pillow. The window showed a strip of darkening sky and, below it, the first lamp lit in the company houses, tiny and yellow, going off one by one as families ate their suppers on wages that would soon, thank God or the courts, be real money.

"When a man steals a well," Ada said, "and drinks from it all his life, and builds his house beside it, and raises his children on it — when he dies thirsty, the way I'm dying, does the water he stole still quench? Or does it taste of the taking, all the way down?"

Ruthie stood in the doorway with the tray against her chest, and Ada watched the girl understand that this was not a riddle, that there might be a true answer, that she had been chosen to give it.

"I don't know, ma'am," Ruthie said slowly. "But my grandma used to say water doesn't remember who owns it. Only who's thirsty."

Ada considered that. Outside, the wind came down off the mesa and moved through the cottonwoods along the ditch bank, and the leaves sounded exactly like water, the way they always had, the way they would tomorrow and every day after, belonging to no one.

"No," she said at last. "I don't suppose it does. That was the whole trouble with us. We thought we'd bought something that couldn't be owned."

She waved the girl out. She did not take the laudanum. She wanted the night to have edges.

* * *

She wrote one more letter that night — a short one, a single page, addressed to no one and sealed anyway — and laid it atop the first in the desk drawer, beneath the vault key's hiding place, where Daniel would find both together. Then she slept, or something near it, and dreamed of the river as it had been when she was six: running high and brown in the spring melt, and her father lifting her onto his shoulders to watch it, saying, *Look at that, Ada-girl. All ours.* And the child on his shoulders had believed him, and the old woman dreaming had wanted, more than justice, more than forgiveness, simply to climb down.

She woke before dawn with the tremor in her hands gone still, which she recognized, from the deaths she had sat beside, as its own kind of message. She dressed herself — refused Ruthie's help, tied the buttons with fingers that obeyed — and sat at the east window to watch the light come up on the valley.

The ditches were running. Somewhere downstream, past the narrows, past the crossing where a man had died twice — once in the flesh and once in the newspapers — the river went on toward country she had never troubled to chart, carrying everything she'd hoarded back to the world.

Ada Corliss took out the small notebook she kept by the bed, the one with her private accounts, and made her final entry in a hand gone thin but level:

*Ledger balanced as far as it can be. Remainder owed to time.*

She dated it, blotted it, and closed the book.

Downstairs, faintly, the front bell rang — early callers, lawyers probably, or a newspaperman, or perhaps, though she smiled at the fancy, a rider with dust on his coat bringing word from two hundred miles east. Ada smoothed her skirt, folded her hands to stop their rattling, and waited to see who the day would send her — knowing, with the calm of a woman whose accounts were finally in order, that whoever it was, the door would answer before she did.
