## 36. Burials

The river ran high and green the morning we buried Duke Sallow.

It had come up overnight on snowmelt from the far ranges, and it moved through Redrock Narrows with a sound like a crowd talking low — that constant mutter of water over stone that I'd spent twelve years using as company. Now there were eleven of us standing on the bank above it, and for once nobody needed me to count heads or read tracks or decide which way was safest. We were just people standing in the sun, waiting to put a man in the ground.

Cass had insisted on being carried down. Ruth Ann wanted her flat on her back in the shade of the willows, but Cass Duval had never in her life done what a medic told her when she could help it, so she sat propped against a cottonwood with her bandaged shoulder and watched us dig. She'd taken a bullet meant for Peter Liu at the ferry crossing, and the fever had nearly finished what the lead started, and now she was thin as a fence rail and twice as stubborn. When Tomás handed her the first spadeful of earth to throw, she looked at it a long time before she let it go.

"He shot at us," she said. "Three weeks of shooting at us."

"And then he didn't," Tomás said.

"That's not how I'd want to be remembered. One good day at the end."

"Maybe it's not about how he'd want it," Ines said quietly. "Maybe it's about who we are when nobody's paying us."

Nobody had an answer for that, so we kept digging.

The ground went easy near the water — deep alluvial soil, dark and sweet-smelling, nothing like the caliche hardpan up on the mesa where the Corliss men would have preferred their dead. I dug most of it myself. Not because anyone asked. Because my hands needed something honest to do, and shoveling is the most honest work there is. You can't lie to a hole in the ground. It's either deep enough or it isn't.

Sallow lay wrapped in his own bedroll on a litter beside the grave. Ruth Ann had cleaned him up as best she could — closed his eyes, bound his jaw, folded his hands over the paybook we'd found in his coat. That book had turned him. Somewhere between reading his grandfather's X-mark on a coerced transfer from 1894 and hearing Ada Corliss order outside guns to finish what he wouldn't, Duke Sallow had crossed a river most men never find the ford for. He'd arrested his own employer's agents with a dead ranger's authority he'd spent a month mocking, and then he'd stood in front of me when Ward Pellis's man opened fire, and the bullet that should have gone through my chest went through his instead.

He'd lived four hours after that. Long enough to make me promise two things.

"Get them out," he'd said, gripping my wrist with fingers already going cold. "All of them. And tell Ada—" He'd coughed then, blood on his teeth, and laughed, which was worse than the coughing. "Tell Ada the ledger wins. Tell her I said so. She'll hate that more than dying."

I hadn't promised the second part yet. There hadn't been time. But I would.

* * *

We lowered him at midmorning, when the light came straight down the canyon and turned the river to hammered tin.

Tomás Reyes spoke first. He'd known Sallow longer than any of us — thirty years back, when Tomás was still guiding sportsmen up this stretch of water, Duke Sallow had been a boy wrangling horses for the Corliss outfit, all elbows and appetite, learning early that the world paid better for loyalty than for honesty.

"He wasn't born mean," Tomás said. "I want that said over him, because nobody else will say it. He was born into a machine, same as all of us, and the machine chewed him a long time before he figured out what it was made of." He took off his hat. "He helped poison this river. And then he helped unpoison it. If God keeps books, maybe those balance out. If God doesn't keep books, then it falls to us to remember both columns, and I'm willing."

Ruth Ann read something from a small Bible she'd carried through everything — through the burned camp, the narrows, the forty miles of dead ground with Cass on a litter. I didn't catch the words. I was watching the water.

Peter Liu came forward last, awkward in his too-big shirt, and set something on the mound of fresh dirt: one of his photograph prints, the only one he'd risked making in the field. It showed the first page of the Hollis ledger — the 1893 transfer, Elias Corliss's signature riding above nine X-marks, the marks of men who couldn't write their names on the paper that took their water away.

"For the record," Peter said. "In case anybody comes looking."

"In case anybody comes looking," Cass repeated, and spat ceremonially into the river, which was as close to a eulogy as Duke Sallow was ever going to get from Cass Duval, and probably more than he deserved.

We filled the grave by hand. Nobody hurried. The river talked its low talk the whole time, and I thought about how many men this water had buried without ceremony — my father among them, drunk or despairing or just tired enough to walk into the current one night and let it have him. At least Sallow got stones. At least somebody said his name out loud where the country could hear it.

When it was done, Mara touched my elbow. Just that. Her hand there for a second, then gone.

"There's one more," she said.

* * *

We climbed to the cave in the heat of the afternoon, the six of us who could make the climb — Tomás, Ines, Peter, Mara, Ruth Ann, and me. Cass stayed below with Ferro, whose ribs were still strapped, and the two of them sat in the shade of the truck running the longest conversation either of them had managed since the crossing, Cass doing most of the talking and Ferro doing most of the wincing, which was their way of being friends.

The trail up was one I knew now better than any trail in the territory. Forty days ago I'd cut sign here — a woman moving fast toward the river, alone, carrying grief like a pack she couldn't set down. I'd followed those tracks into the worst and best story of my life. Today I walked them again in reverse, and every switchback had a memory nailed to it: the arrow drawn to her cheekbone at the spring, the firelight in the box canyon, her voice steady over the radio while Sallow's bullets chewed the rocks around her during the bait run. She'd learned this country faster than anyone I'd ever taught. Some of that was me. Most of it was her father, and the stubbornness he'd left in her like an inheritance no lawyer could contest.

The cave mouth opened onto the canyon wall above the river bend, exactly where Nathaniel Hollis had found it, exactly where the ledger had slept for a hundred and thirty years under a slab of limestone that half the prospectors in the territory must have walked past and never thought to lift.

Mara stopped at the entrance.

She was carrying a small tin box, and I knew what was in it because I'd helped her carry it since Redrock — the ashes of her father, recovered from the camp where Pellis's men had burned everything they could burn. She'd kept them close through ambush and river and desert, sleeping with the box inside her jacket like a second heartbeat.

"You don't have to do this today," Tomás said gently. "There's no schedule on grief."

"Yes there is," Mara said. "There's always a schedule. You just don't get to see it until you're living it." She looked at me. "You told me once that the country keeps its own records. Tracks, bones, flood lines. That nothing out here really disappears, it just gets filed somewhere you haven't looked yet." Her voice caught, and she pushed through it. "I want him filed here. Where he found the truth. Where he died for it."

Inside, the cave was cool and dim, the air tasting of stone and old dust. The slab still lay where Tomás had pulled it aside that first terrible morning. The space beneath it was empty now — the ledger long gone east to Judge Amos Pell's courthouse, its copies scattered across every newspaper desk in the territory like seed thrown on ground everyone had assumed was dead. But the hollow remained, a shallow shelf in the living rock, and the afternoon light came slanting through the cave mouth and laid a bar of gold across it.

Tomás stepped forward first. He'd asked for this — formally, the night before, sitting by the fire with his hat in his hands like a man asking a father for a daughter.

"I buried Nathaniel Hollis badly," he said. His voice filled the stone room the way water fills a channel. "I buried him fast, in the dark, with river stones on top, because I was hiding what I saw and ashamed of why I was hiding it. For six weeks I let this whole territory believe a good man died in an accident, or worse — that he died guilty of something. His daughter believed it. I let her believe it." He turned to Mara. "That's the weight I've been carrying, and I'm setting it down now, in front of witnesses, in front of God if He's listening, in front of the country itself."

He straightened. Old guide, old friend, oldest liar in the valley for one bad season — and now the only man alive who could say what he was about to say with the authority of having seen it.

"Nathaniel Hollis did not die of exposure. He did not die of accident. He did not die of anything he did or failed to do. He was murdered in his sleep on the ninth of June by Ward Pellis, agent of the Corliss Water and Power Company, brother-in-law to the deceased, because Nathaniel Hollis found proof that the Corliss fortune was built on stolen rivers, and because Nathaniel Hollis intended to tell the world." His voice cracked on the last of it and he let it crack. "I saw Pellis leave the tent. I moved the body to hide the evidence of murder, not to commit it, and I have hated myself every day since for choosing silence over truth. I un-choose it now. Nathaniel Hollis died honest, and I bury his name clean, and may every courthouse in this territory read it back to itself until the shame lands where it belongs."

The words hung in the cool air. Ruth Ann had her eyes closed. Peter was writing it down, because Peter wrote everything down now, having learned what happens when the record gets burned.

Mara took her father's ashes from the tin with both hands.

She didn't speak at first. She just stood in the bar of gold light and looked at the hollow in the rock, and I watched her do the arithmetic that grief forces on you — that this was the last act, the final errand, the thing you'd been carrying toward since the day the world broke, and once it was done there was no more carrying, only the strange lightness afterward that feels almost like falling.

Then she tipped the tin, slow and careful, and the ash went into the stone hollow in a gray ribbon, fine as flour, catching the light.

"Some trails aren't charted," she said softly, "because the charters are all bought." A ghost of her father's own dry humor in it, borrowed for the occasion. "You found the one that mattered, Dad. I'll walk the rest of it."

She pressed the empty tin flat against her sternum for a moment, the way you'd press a wound, and then Tomás slid the limestone slab back into place over the ashes, gentle as closing a door on a sleeping child, and it was done.

Nobody spoke for a while. The river murmured below us, running free for the first time in three generations, its water rights voided by a judge two hundred miles east, its future belonging to whoever lived along it honestly. My father's river. Sallow's river. Hollis's river. Ours, now, in whatever way a river belongs to anybody, which is only the way a wild thing belongs to you — by choosing, daily, to stay.

* * *

We came down off the mesa in the long gold light of evening, and the others drifted ahead toward the trucks and the smell of Ruth Ann's coffee, but Mara hung back at the last switchback, and I waited, because some waits you don't have to be asked to keep.

"They're going to ask you tonight," she said.

"I know."

"At the fire. They've been circling it for days. Ines wants you in Boston for the publication — she says a firsthand account needs a firsthand witness. Cass wants hands for the ranch. Tomás won't say it outright, but he's already planning next season's guide trips around your calendar." She smiled a little. "And I haven't said anything at all, because I figured you'd run farther from me saying it than from all of them combined."

The river talked below us. Twelve years of mileage logs, twelve years of radio silence and solo patrols and a life measured out in miles because miles never ask you to stay. I looked at the woman who'd held an arrow on me at a spring and now stood close enough that I could see the dust of her father's ashes still on her knuckles, and I felt the old habit rising in me like water in a well — the habit of leaving, of counting distance instead of days.

"Mara," I started.

"Don't answer yet," she said. "Just — don't ride off before you answer. That's all I'm asking. Whatever you decide, decide it with us standing there, not from the saddle." She squeezed my arm once and walked down the trail toward the firelight, and I stood alone on the switchback with the whole valley going blue and gold below me, knowing that tonight, around that fire, they were going to offer me the one thing I'd spent twelve years making sure I'd never have to refuse —

a home —

and that for the first time in my life, I didn't know which way my feet would take me.
