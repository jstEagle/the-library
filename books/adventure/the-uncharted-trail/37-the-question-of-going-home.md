## 37. The Question of Going Home

The valley had gone green at the edges.

Silas noticed it first from the ridge above the Brandt store, the way he noticed everything — distance first, detail after. Three weeks since Redrock, and the river ran brown and full where it had run thin and rationed all his life. Irrigation ditches that had been dry scars on the land for two generations now carried water to fields whose owners were still learning their own names again. Men rode fence lines they'd only ever ridden as hired hands, and stopped sometimes in the middle of nowhere just to look at a corner post with their grandfather's name burned into it.

He'd come down off the ridge because he was due in town. That was the story he told himself. Due in town, papers to sign, a report to file with a territorial office that had finally started answering its radio.

The truth was simpler and he knew it: he couldn't stay away.

* * *

Ines Okafor had taken over the back room of the Brandt store like she'd been born to it.

Elena Brandt had cleared a table by the window, and Ines had covered it with ledger pages laid out in sequence, weighted at the corners with river stones — the same kind of stones, Silas thought, that had marked Hollis's grave. Peter Liu worked across from her with a magnifying glass and his photographs, matching image to original, initialing every verification in a hand so small it looked like insect tracks.

"You're staring," Ines said without looking up.

"Counting."

"Counting what?"

"Pages. Signatures. X-marks." He came inside, hat in hand out of habit, though Elena's store hadn't been Corliss property since the warrants went out. "Forty-one coerced transfers. I keep thinking if I count them enough times they'll make sense."

"They won't." Ines set down her pen and finally looked at him. She had the particular tiredness of someone who'd slept badly for weeks and decided to be productive about it. "That's what I've learned writing this. Numbers don't make sense. They make *accounting*. Sense is a different thing entirely."

She slid a thick manuscript across the table toward him. Handwritten, dense, the top page titled in her careful architect's lettering: *THE WATER PAPERS: A History of the Corliss Appropriations, 1891–1934.*

"University press in the east wants it," she said. "They wrote me before the judge even issued the last warrant. There's a woman there, an editor, who said — and I'm quoting — 'This is the most important document to come out of the territory in fifty years, and we will publish it even if you have to carry the manuscript here on foot.'"

"And you said?"

"I said I'd already walked far enough this year." Ines smiled, and it transformed her face — the siege-mentality face, the rationed-trust face, gone in an instant. "I'm going east, Silas. Train takes me as far as the rail head, then it's coaches and hotels and lecture halls. Me. A botanist who spent three years cataloguing lichen because lichen doesn't lie to anyone."

"You'll be good at it."

"At what? Lying?"

"Telling the truth loudly." He turned a page of the manuscript with one finger, careful of the ink. "Your father would—"

"My father sold water rights he didn't own to men who knew he didn't own them." Her voice stayed level but something moved behind it. "I used to think I'd spend my whole life being quietly embarrassed about that. Now I get to spend it being loudly accurate instead. It's better work."

She gathered the pages back toward her, and before she did, she looked at him a moment too long — the look of someone composing a question and deciding against asking it yet.

* * *

Peter found him at the corral an hour later.

The boy — man, really, though he still stood like someone apologizing for taking up space — leaned on the rail and watched Silas check hooves on a horse that wasn't his, because checking hooves gave his hands something to do when conversations got close.

"I leave Thursday," Peter said. "For the capital. Judge Pell arranged a federal escort. Apparently testimony about corporate murder gets you a train compartment instead of a boxcar when there are newspapermen involved."

"That'll take some getting used to."

"Everything will." Peter picked at a splinter on the rail. "I testified for six hours last week. Six hours. And afterward this reporter — young guy, excited, kept licking his pencil — he asked me why I did it. Why I photographed the pages, why I mailed the film, why I confessed when it nearly got us all killed." He shook his head. "And I told him the truth, which is that I panicked. Every step of it was panic. Photographing the ledger was panic. Mailing the film was panic. Telling you all in that canyon was the first thing I did on purpose, and it was only because I couldn't stand carrying it anymore."

"How'd the reporter take that?"

"He wrote it down." Peter almost smiled. "Then he said, 'Son, half the brave things in history were done by people too scared to do the cowardly thing twice.' I've been chewing on that for nine days."

Silas straightened, wiped his hands on his thighs. "You're not scared anymore?"

"Oh, I'm terrified." Peter said it cheerfully, which somehow made it worse and better at once. "I'm going to stand up in a federal courtroom and testify that Ada Corliss ordered the deaths of witnesses, and her lawyers are going to try to shred me, and my advisor — the one who sold us out — might be in the room. But it's a different terror than the canyon. Canyon terror says *run*. This terror says *stand there and say it again, louder.*" He pushed off the rail. "Cass says that's growth. Cass says a lot of things now. You should hear her."

"I heard some of it."

"Then you know." Peter hesitated at the corral gate, and Silas saw the question forming in him too — the same question Ines had swallowed, taking shape in a second mouth. Peter was braver about panic than most people were about anything, so he asked a piece of it: "What happens to you, when we all go?"

"Patrols. Paperwork. Miles."

"Yeah," Peter said softly. "That's what I figured." And he left it there, hanging in the dust between them, and walked back toward town.

* * *

Cass Duval didn't ask questions. Cass Duval issued invitations.

Silas rode out to the north parcel on a blue-sky afternoon and found her on horseback at the boundary of what had been, until three weeks ago, Corliss rangeland — and was now, per Judge Pell's restitution filings, Duval land, reclamation claim number four, filed by a wrangler whose grandmother had been run off the same ground in 1908 with nothing but a mule and a grudge.

Cass sat her horse like the horse was lucky to have her. She'd cut her hair short for the summer and it stuck out under her hat in black tufts, and when she saw Silas coming she didn't wave, just waited, because waiting was free and waving cost something.

"Well?" she called, when he was close enough. "What do you think?"

"Of what?"

"The ground, Vasquez. Keep up." She swept her arm across the valley benchland — grass going to seed, cottonwoods along the ditch line, a spring-fed stock tank that some dispossessed rancher had dug sixty years ago and some Corliss foreman had blasted shut, and which Cass and two hired hands had spent eleven days digging open again. Water stood in it now, maybe four feet deep, catching the sky. "Eight hundred acres. My grandmother's brand is in the county book, section twelve. Pell confirmed the title chain himself. First deeded water rights on this river that don't trace back through a Corliss signature."

"It's good ground."

"It's *my* ground." She said it like a woman testing whether the words held weight, and finding that they did. "You know what I keep doing? I keep riding out here at dawn and standing on this exact spot and saying it out loud. Mine. Feels like blasphemy every time. Feels like it ought to." She swung down and walked to the tank, crouched, dragged her fingers through the water. "Ferro's staying on through haying season. His shoulder's mended enough to drive a team, not enough to run boats, and he says rivers betrayed him anyway. Ruth Ann's taking the east road with Peter — she's got family in the capital and a state medical board that owes her a license review. So it's me and Ferro and eight hundred acres and more work than three people can do."

"Sounds about right for a start."

"Which is why—" She stood, flicked water off her fingers, and looked at him with the flat directness that had made her the expedition's best negotiator with horse traders and gunmen alike. "Come work it with us."

"Cass—"

"Don't say no yet, because I haven't finished the pitch, and the pitch is good." She ticked points off on her fingers. "One: you can read country better than any man I've met, including Tomás, and Tomás agrees, which nearly killed him to admit. Two: reclaimed land needs a man who knows where water goes and where it hides. Three: Ferro cooks like a criminal and I need someone to share the suffering. Four—" and here her voice changed, dropped its performance, went quiet and real, "—four, I watched you spend five weeks keeping seven people alive with nothing but stubbornness and terrain, and I have never in my life seen anybody so built for a place and so determined to live nowhere. So. That's the pitch."

Silas looked out over the water in the tank, at the sky lying in it, at the cottonwoods throwing shade on ground that had been sun-scalded and empty his whole life.

"I'll think on it," he said, which was what he always said, and they both heard the shape of the lie in it, worn smooth as a river stone from use.

Cass nodded slowly. "You do that. You think on it in your saddle, going your next hundred miles." She remounted. "But Silas — the offer doesn't expire. That's the difference between us and everything else that's ever asked you to stay. We're not going anywhere. Neither is the ground."

* * *

Tomás Reyes came to him at dusk, at the river.

That was fitting, Silas supposed. Everything between them had happened near water — the grave marked with river stones, the forty miles of dead ground where forgiveness had been slow labor, the night at the fire when Tomás had finally described what he'd seen at the cave mouth: Ward Pellis's silhouette, Hollis turning, the sound that Tomás had lived with every night since. They'd buried that conversation somewhere on the trail between the badlands and the crossing, and both of them had walked lighter after, the way you do when you put down something heavy that was never yours to carry alone.

Now Tomás sat on the bank with his boots off, feet in the current, a man of sixty letting a river touch him without flinching. He didn't turn when Silas sat down beside him.

"Mara leaves Monday," Tomás said. "East, with Ines. She's taking her father's ashes to the cave first — says the ledger slept there thirty years, he can sleep there until the trailhead's named. Then she's going to stand in front of the university and study surveying, of all things. Says she wants to map the valley properly. Says maps are how you keep a place honest."

"She'll be good at that."

"She'll be better than good. She's her father's stubbornness with your eyes." Tomás smiled at the water. "And me — well. You heard."

The territorial outfitters' association had written to Tomás the week before. Public apology, printed in the paper, for how quickly they'd believed the worst about a dead man's oldest friend. And beneath the apology, an offer: lead expeditions again. Their best routes, their best pay, their name on the brochures. *Reyes Guided Expeditions*, restored.

"A month ago I'd have laughed," Tomás said. "A month ago I was a fugitive standing over a grave I'd dug for a murdered man, wondering which of my friends had killed him. Now I'm a respectable guide with a letterhead." He flexed his toes in the current. "I said yes. Not for the money. For the river. I want to be on it, showing people what it is, while it's still free. While it belongs to everybody again. That's the whole eulogy Hollis gets from me — not words. Work. I'll guide this river until my knees quit, and every trip, I'll tell them the story of what it cost."

Silas nodded. Words felt insufficient and Tomás, of all people, understood that silence could be respect.

They sat a while. The light went copper, then gray. Somewhere upstream a heron lifted off the shallows on prehistoric wings.

"Silas." Tomás said his name the way he had on the trail, that night of reconciliation — carefully, like handling something that had been broken and repaired and would break again if gripped wrong. "All of us are leaving. Did you notice? Ines east. Peter east. Ruth Ann east. Mara east, eventually, after the mapping. Cass and Ferro north to the parcel. Everyone has a direction now. Everyone has a door walking toward them."

"I noticed."

"And you're the only one standing still, except standing still for you means moving — patrols, circuits, miles. Zero miles is death for you; you told Mara that yourself, around a fire, with your whole history laid out like bones on a tarp." Tomás pulled his feet from the water and turned to face him fully, and his old dark eyes were wet and unashamed of it. "So here is what I came to say, and I'll say it plain because I'm too old for anything else. We are not asking you to choose one of us. We are asking you to stop leaving. All of us. Together. Whatever shape that takes — the parcel, the town, the trail, the store — the shape doesn't matter. The stopping matters."

"Tomás—"

"No. Hear it all." His voice cracked and he let it. "I dug a grave for my best friend and suspected my own heart for a year. You forgave me on dead ground with no water and no shade. Do you understand what that bought? It bought a family. Ugly, improvised, shot-up, grieving — but a family. And families don't ask their people to visit. Families ask them to *come home*."

The word landed in Silas's chest like a boot heel.

Home. He turned it over. Twelve years of mileage logs. A father drunk into the river. A badge that meant motion was duty and duty was shelter. He had built a whole life on the principle that a man who kept moving couldn't be taken from anything, because he belonged to nothing — and now seven people sat in a green valley holding doors open, and every single one of them had asked, or was about to ask, or was silently asking right now.

"I don't know how," he said finally. It was the truest thing he'd said in years. "I don't know how to be in a place. I know how to ride through one."

"Nobody does, the first year," Tomás said. "That's why it's called home and not a camp. Camps you know how to make. Home you have to learn." He stood, groaning theatrically, joints announcing decades, and clapped Silas once on the shoulder — hard, the way you clap a man you intend to see tomorrow. "Monday morning, Mara walks to the cave. I'm riding with her. After that, everyone scatters to their futures." He paused. "There's one future nobody's claimed yet. It's got your name on it if you want it. Elena Brandt says the deed paperwork takes a week to draw up. She asked me who else should be listed."

Silas stared at the river. The river ran free, brown and loud and unowned, going wherever it pleased, answerable to nothing but gravity and rain — and staying, all the same. Staying in its banks. Making everything along them green.

"Tell Elena," he heard himself say, "to draw up ten names."

Tomás grinned like a man watching a foal stand for the first time. "Ten names. Whose tenth?"

"Mine." The word came out rough. "If the ground will have me."

"The ground's been waiting on you longer than any of us, muchacho." Tomás scooped up his boots and started up the bank toward the lamplit windows of town. "Think fast, though. Knowing that girl, Mara's already written to Elena herself. And knowing Elena, the deed's been drafted since before the warrants dried."

* * *

Silas stayed on the bank until full dark.

He took out his mileage log — the little oilskin notebook, twelve years of entries, columns of dates and distances, a life measured in departures. He clicked open his pen and, for a long time, wrote nothing.

Then, on today's line, where a number of miles belonged, he drew a single zero.

It looked strange there. It looked like a beginning.

He was still looking at it when hoofbeats came soft up the bank behind him, and a familiar horse stopped at the edge of the firelight, and Mara Hollis swung down with a lantern in one hand and a folded paper in the other, and the look on her face said she already knew everything, and had brought proof.
