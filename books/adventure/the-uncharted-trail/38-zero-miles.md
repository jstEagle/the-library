## 38. Zero Miles

The resignation letter was one sentence long, and it took Silas Vasquez three days to write it.

He sat at the dispatch desk in the district station with the form in front of him — an official document, cream-colored, stamped with the territorial eagle — and he wrote *I hereby resign my commission as ranger of this district* and then he stopped. The pen hovered. Outside the window the wind came down off the mesa carrying the smell of sage and cold stone, the same smell it had carried every morning for twelve years, and Silas understood that what he was really doing was not resigning a job but burying a man: the man who had measured his life in miles because miles could not leave him, could not die on him, could not drink themselves into a river or get shot shielding a stranger at a ferry crossing.

Miles didn't ask anything of you. That was their great virtue. A mile was honest. You walked it or you rode it, and when it was done it was done, and it never once looked back over its shoulder and said, *stay.*

He finished the letter on the third day. He signed it, folded it, and slid it under Supervisor Amos Hargrove's door before dawn, before the coffee was on, before anyone could catch him and ask questions he didn't have answers for yet. Then he saddled the bay mare, loaded her with everything he owned — which fit into two saddlebags and a bedroll, which had always fit into two saddlebags and a bedroll, that being the whole point of a life measured in mileage logs — and rode out of the station yard without looking back at the flagpole or the corral or the row of bunks where younger men slept between patrols.

Hargrove caught him anyway, of course. At the gate, wrapped in a coat against the early cold, gray stubble on his jaw, holding the letter like it might bite him.

"Twelve years," Hargrove said. "You're leaving me one sentence."

"You always said the best reports were short."

"I also said the best rangers stayed put." He studied Silas a long moment, breath steaming. "Where will I send your mail?"

Silas opened his mouth to say *nowhere,* because there had never been anywhere, and found the word wouldn't come out. It sat in his chest like a stone in a riverbed, too heavy to move and suddenly too obvious to ignore.

"I'll let you know," he said.

Hargrove almost smiled. "That's the first time you've ever lied to me, Vasquez. I'll count it as progress." He stepped back from the gate. "Ride easy."

Silas rode east.

* * *

The country between the station and Corlissville was forty miles of high desert he knew the way other men knew their own hands, and he let the mare take it slow, partly because there was no reason left in the world to hurry and partly because he wanted to feel every mile of it pass under him, one last time, like counting coins out of a purse.

The land had changed since autumn. Word traveled faster than water in this territory, and the water had changed most of all. Where the Corliss headgates used to stand — those iron jaws that had swallowed the river whole for sixty years — there were now crews of men with crowbars and cutting torches, ranchers working side by side, some of them grandsons of men whose names were on the coerced transfers in the ledger, opening the ditches that fed their own fields. Silas passed one crew at midmorning and an old man straightened up, shaded his eyes, and called out, "Ranger! You the one that rung the bell?"

Silas didn't answer. He touched his hat brim and rode on. But the old man's voice followed him for a mile, warm as sun on the back of the neck, and Silas found himself sitting straighter in the saddle without meaning to.

At the river crossing where Cass Duval had taken the bullet meant for Peter Liu, the water ran free and loud over the shallows, and someone had driven a fence post into the bank and nailed a board to it. On the board, in paint that hadn't fully dried, someone had written: *CASS DUVAL STOOD HERE.* Below it, smaller: *AND SO DID THE REST OF US.*

Silas stopped and read it twice. The mare drank while he did, her muzzle making rings in the current.

Cass was alive. That still felt like a thing the world owed him and might yet repossess. She'd come through the fever by way of Ruth Ann's willow bark and boiled steel and pure cussedness, and the last time Silas had seen her she'd been walking a reclaimed quarter-section with a surveyor's chain over her shoulder, limping, laughing at something Ines Okafor had said, looking like a woman who'd decided death would have to take her by appointment only.

He rode on. The miles came off the log. Thirty-one. Twenty-six. Nineteen.

And all the while, the thought he'd been outrunning since the courthouse in the east kept gaining on him, patient as dusk.

They had asked him. That was the trouble. Back in chapter after chapter of that long hard winter, around fires and in hospital rooms and once, memorably, in the back of a buckboard while Ferro snored, they had each asked him in their own way — Tomás with a hand on his shoulder, Mara with a look across a campfire, Ruth Ann by simply assuming he'd be there and handing him a bowl — asked him to stop leaving. And he had told them he needed time, and they had given it to him, because that was the kind of people they were, and now the time was spent and the question stood in the road ahead of him like a gate he couldn't ride around.

Because here was the arithmetic he couldn't escape: he had spent twelve years tracking other people home. Missing prospectors, lost children, drifters who wandered off the trail into bad country. He had found them all, or nearly all, and delivered them to doors and porches and waiting arms, and then he had turned his horse around and ridden back into the empty miles, alone, telling himself that solitude wasn't loneliness, that a man who measured his life in mileage logs had no need of a door with his name on it.

And then a woman on foot had crossed his patrol line, moving fast toward the river, and cracked that worn-smooth peace like ice on a stock tank, and everything after — the cave, the ledger, the gunfights, the narrows, the dead ground, the standoff at Redrock — everything had been the trail leading somewhere he'd never charted.

Toward a door with ten names on it.

Seventeen miles out of Corlissville, he made camp alone in a draw, and lay awake under more stars than seemed fair, and admitted it plainly, the way he admitted things to no one: he was afraid. Not of dying — he'd made his peace with that years ago, out on the dead ground, carrying Cass on a litter through forty miles of nothing. He was afraid of staying. Staying meant being known. Being known meant being losable. Every person he had ever been losable to was dead — his mother first, then his father, drunk into the river the Corliss family had stolen — and Silas had built his whole life on the simple, brutal logic that a man with no one to lose could not be gutted.

But a man with no one to lose, he thought, watching the stars wheel, has no one to lose *for*, either. And he had seen what losing-for looked like. He'd seen it in Sallow, dying in the shallows with a stranger's blood on his hands and something like relief on his face. He'd seen it in Ada Corliss, who had loved one grandson so hard she'd strangled everything else she touched. He'd seen it in Hollis, dead in the river for a piece of paper that told the truth.

The truth was worth dying for. Silas had learned that much. But so was breakfast with people who expected you.

He slept, finally, and dreamed of nothing at all, which was its own kind of mercy.

* * *

Corlissville had changed its name.

Not officially — the paperwork was still grinding through Judge Pell's office two hundred miles east — but someone had taken a ladder and a bucket of white paint to the water tower, and CORLISSVILLE was gone, and underneath, in letters visible from three miles off, it said BRANDT. Elena Brandt's store had burned in the old days and been rebuilt twice, and now it stood at the center of town with fresh lumber and new glass, and the street in front of it had been renamed by unanimous acclamation of everyone who mattered, which was to say everyone who shopped there.

Silas rode in at midafternoon with his collar up and his hat low, intending — he told himself — nothing more than supplies. Flour, coffee, cartridges, maybe a new set of horseshoes. In and out before dark. There was a line in his head, rehearsed somewhere on the ride without his permission: *I'll just see the place, and ride on.* He'd been saying it for seventeen miles. He didn't believe it anymore, but he kept saying it, the way a man keeps whistling past a graveyard.

The town was busy in a way it had never been in the scrip days. Men argued prices out loud in front of the store instead of whispering them. Kids ran the boards with paper windmills. A woman sold tamales from a cart and nobody checked whether she had a company permit, because there was no company anymore, just a county, and the county belonged to whoever lived in it.

Elena Brandt herself was on the porch, directing two carpenters with the serene tyranny of a woman who had waited seventy years to give orders. She was smaller than Silas remembered, or he was bigger; grief and distance do that. Her eyes found him before he reached the hitching rail, the way a hawk finds a mouse, and she smiled.

"Well," she said. "Look what the river washed up."

"Ma'am."

"Don't 'ma'am' me, Silas Vasquez. Your father stood on this exact board and bought flour off my husband in 1898, and he tipped his hat the same way you just did, like the hat owed him money." She came down the steps, unhurried. "I knew the moment I saw your surname on that affidavit you'd come back eventually. Men like you always circle. Question is whether you land."

"I'm just here for—"

"Supplies. Yes." She patted his arm, and her hand was light and dry and strong as a bird's foot. "Flour's inside. So is everything else." She leaned close, and her voice dropped to something almost sly. "Back room. They've been waiting since Tuesday. I told them you'd come today. Don't ask how I knew. Old women know weather."

Silas's mouth went dry. "Waiting for—"

"Go on," Elena Brandt said, and turned back to her carpenters, and that was that.

* * *

The back room of the Brandt store smelled of cut lumber and coffee and, faintly, of river water, though the river was half a mile off. Someone had cleared it out — crates stacked, a stove ticking, lamplight gold on whitewashed walls — and they were all there.

All of them.

Tomás Reyes sat nearest the stove, hat on his knee, silver-haired and straight-backed, and he rose when Silas came through the door, and the look on his face was the look of a man greeting a son he never had. Ines Okafor was beside him, spectacles pushed up into her hair, a newspaper open on the table in front of her — the *Territorial Herald*, headline half-visible: *WATER RIGHTS VOIDED IN THREE COUNTIES — CLAIMS OFFICE FLOODED WITH DESCENDANTS.* Peter Liu sat cross-legged on a crate with a camera in his lap, and when he saw Silas he grinned the grin of a man who had mailed his shame east and gotten forgiveness back in return. Ruth Ann stood at the counter sorting bandages into tins, because Ruth Ann sorted bandages the way other people breathed. Ferro sat propped in the corner, arm still in a sling but his color good, playing solitaire badly and cheating worse. Cass Duval leaned against the doorframe with her boot up, favoring the leg, and she looked Silas over head to heel and said:

"Took you long enough."

And Mara.

Mara Hollis stood at the table's far end, and the lamplight caught the scar along her jaw — the one from the narrows, from the night terror got transmuted into competence — and she wasn't smiling, exactly. She was watching him the way she'd watched him the first time, on the trail, arrow drawn, except now the bow was gone and what she held instead was a single sheet of heavy paper, and her hands weren't quite steady.

"We voted," she said.

"Voted."

"Ten names." She laid the paper on the table and turned it to face him. "Well. Nine, until tonight."

Silas came forward slowly, the way you approach a wounded animal or a live wire, and looked down at the deed.

It was real. Embossed seal of the county recorder, Judge Pell's signature countersigned below, the ink dry and official. And there in the description, in the flat legal language of surveys and sections, was the thing itself: *one hundred and sixty acres, more or less, bordering the north bank of the [river], water rights conveyed free and clear per amended territorial statute* — acreage along the free-running river, the river his father had drowned in, the river that had been stolen and given back, and beneath the description, in a column, the names:

*Tomás Reyes. Ines Okafor. Peter Liu. Cass Duval. Ruth Ann Mercer. Ferro Bastida. Elena Brandt. Daniel Corliss. Mara Hollis.*

Daniel Corliss. Silas blinked at it. "The grandson signed on?"

"He showed up with the deed already drafted," Tomás said quietly. "Said his grandmother's empire ought to end in something built instead of something guarded. Said he'd read the first page of that ledger aloud at Redrock and it was the truest work he'd ever done." Tomás's eyes crinkled. "We argued about it for two days. Then we voted. Nine to nothing."

"And the tenth name," Mara said. She tapped the blank space at the bottom of the column. It sat there white and open as snowfield. "Is yours, if you want it."

The room went quiet. The stove ticked. Somewhere out front Elena Brandt was bullying a carpenter about trim, her voice bright and ordinary, anchoring the world.

Silas looked at the blank space for a long time.

He thought about mileage logs. Twelve years of them, neat columns in a canvas-bound book, every day accounted for, every mile numbered, and not one entry in all that time that said *home.* He thought about his father, who had owned land once, and lost it, and let the losing kill him by inches in a bottle, and how Silas had sworn the opposite oath — never own, never stay, never give the country a chance to take anything out of you — and how the oath had kept him alive and hollow both.

He thought about the woman on foot, crossing his patrol line, moving fast toward the river. How he'd almost let her go. How curiosity — that small crack in the worn-smooth peace — had turned his horse.

Every trail he'd ever charted started exactly like that. One track. One decision to follow instead of ride past.

His hand went to his shirt pocket, out of habit, for the pencil he'd carried for twelve years to mark his mileage log. It wasn't there. He'd left it in the desk drawer at the station, under the resignation letter, on purpose, and hadn't realized until this exact moment why.

Some measurements you retire.

"The mare needs shoeing," he said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "And I'm near out of coffee."

Cass laughed from the doorframe. Ruth Ann set down her bandages. Ferro swept the cards into a pile and started dealing a new hand, wide open, room for one more.

Mara held out the pencil she'd brought — his kind, the flat carpenter's pencil, sharpened with a knife — and Silas took it, and knelt at the table's edge, and wrote his name in the last space of the column, the letters large and unhurried, a man signing not a document but a direction.

*Silas Vasquez.*

Tomás put his hand on Silas's shoulder, and it stayed there. Ines poured coffee nobody had asked for but everybody needed. Outside, the free-running river made its sound in the dark, steady as breathing, going where it pleased at last.

"There's one condition," Mara said, when the noise settled.

Silas looked up, wary. "There's a condition."

"In spring." Her eyes were bright and absolutely serious. "You're teaching a survey class. Trail-reading, water-finding, the whole country, the way you see it. Starting with me." A pause, and the corner of her mouth lifted. "Consider it tuition for the deed."

Silas looked around the room — at the found and the finding, at the ten names, at the door he had almost ridden past — and found, to his complete lack of surprise, that he was smiling.

"First lesson's at dawn," he said. "Be on time."

From the porch, Elena Brandt's voice floated in through the doorway, satisfied as a cat in cream: "I told you he'd land."
