## 1. The Last Contract

The name stitched on my shirt said *RUTH CALLOWAY*, and for three years I had answered to it the way a dog answers to a name it never chose — without complaint, without recognition. Ruth sold rope and diesel and freeze-dried stew to weekend fishermen out of Harrow's Outfitters on the Saltmere waterfront. Ruth kept her head down. Ruth had never guided anyone up anything higher than a barstool.

I was re-spooling a winch line behind the counter when the bell over the door rang at seven in the morning, an hour before we opened, and a woman walked in like weather.

She was maybe forty, dressed in city clothes that had already given up pretending to be city clothes — salt-stained hem, mud to the knee, a canvas satchel worn crosswise like a bandolier. Her eyes did the thing I recognized from a hundred trailheads: they swept the room once, catalogued exits and inventory, then settled on me with the terrible precision of someone who has spent eleven days looking and has finally stopped looking.

"You're Ada Galloway," she said.

The winch line slipped through my hands and sang off the spool. I caught it before it hit the floor, which took more composure than it should have.

"Ruth Calloway," I said. "You've got the wrong shop."

"I don't." She crossed to the counter and set her satchel down on it, which was either very rude or very deliberate, and unzipped it halfway. "Your hands are wrong for retail. Rope burns across both palms, healed wrong, set by yourself. You rack the spool left-handed because your right shoulder doesn't trust you anymore. And there's no Ruth Calloway registered anywhere in this county, because I checked, because I've been checking for nine days."

Outside, the tide was coming in over the flats, filling the channel where the fishing boats knocked against their moorings. Somewhere down the waterfront a gull screamed at another gull about something neither of them would ever have. Ordinary morning sounds. They felt very far away.

"Whoever you think I am," I said, "she doesn't work here."

"She works here." The woman's voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "My name is Elena Vasquez. My husband is Daniel Vasquez. He leads survey expeditions. Eleven days ago he took six people upriver to locate a wreck called the *Meridian*. Four days ago, all contact stopped. Nine days ago—" she paused, and I watched her decide how much truth to spend, "—nine days ago I started trying to find the one person in this country who knows that river better than the river knows itself."

"I don't know what you've heard—"

"I know exactly what happened at Kestrel Pass." She said it flatly, the way you'd read coordinates off a chart. "Four years ago. Three climbers went up in weather that every other guide in the province refused. Two came down. The third was Marcus Hale, son of Aldous Hale, who owns half the shipping concern on this coast. The inquiry blamed the guide. The guide lost her certification, her livelihood, and her name, and disappeared into a town small enough to forget her."

The shop was very quiet. I could hear the diesel pump ticking outside as it cooled, and my own pulse, which was behaving badly.

"That's a story," I said. "Stories get details wrong."

"Not this one." Elena reached into the satchel and drew out a folder, soft-cornered, travel-worn, and laid it on the counter between us like a card played face-up. "Because I didn't come here to ask you to trust me. I came here to show you something."

* * *

I should have thrown her out. Any sensible person would have. A stranger walks into your hiding place speaking your dead name aloud and you put her back on the street and lock the door and go back to being nobody. That was the whole architecture of the life I'd built — low walls, shallow water, nothing worth flooding.

Instead I opened the folder.

Photographs first. Survey camp shots, professional quality: a river the color of steeped tea winding through mangrove country I knew by its old names; men and women in expedition gear around a folding table; a chart weighted with stones, marked in red. Then documents. Elena watched me read them and had the grace not to speak.

The top sheet was a transcript excerpt. A hearing, four years old. Kestrel Pass Inquiry, Day Two. Testimony entered under the name **J. MARSH**, expedition meteorologist, independent contractor.

I remembered him. Of course I remembered him. Everyone remembers the knife; you remember the hand less than the blade. Julian Marsh had sat across the hearing room from me in a gray suit, glasses catching the light, and spoken in a calm, apologetic voice about pressure gradients and wind forecasts he claimed he'd issued and I'd ignored. He'd used the phrase *grossly negligent* twice. He'd looked at me exactly once, and even then not quite at my face.

What I held now was not the transcript everyone saw. It was a draft. Earlier draft, dated three days before the hearing, with whole paragraphs struck through in pen — paragraphs where Marsh wrote that his own forecast had been superseded by station data showing a window of stable pressure, that the guide's decision to climb was consistent with the information available, that responsibility for the storm's early arrival lay with a malfunctioning remote sensor array maintained by—

By Halcyon Marine & Salvage. Aldous Hale's company. The same company whose insurance syndicate had funded the inquiry.

"He tried to recant," Elena said quietly. "This draft proves his original testimony was coerced — Hale's people leaned on him, threatened his contract, his family. He wrote the correction and then he buried it himself, because they made sure burying it was cheaper than telling the truth. This copy came from his personal files. Daniel trusted him. Daniel was the only person Marsh ever showed it to."

My hands were not steady. I set the paper down on the counter and looked at it for a long time, at the struck-through sentences that would have saved my career if any court had ever seen them, and felt something move in my chest that I had spent four years convincing myself was dead.

"Why are you showing me this?"

"Because I need you to say yes," Elena said. "And I know you won't do it for money, and you won't do it for a stranger, and you may not even do it for Daniel, who is a good man and my whole life. So I'm doing it for spite. Take the job because the man who ruined you is the man who can help me find my husband, and because the company that buried your report is about to get away with something much bigger."

"Bigger how?"

She pulled a printed notice from the folder — official letterhead, embossed, recent. A claim registration. *Halcyon Marine & Salvage Consortium, exclusive salvage rights, wreck site designated Meridian Channel, effective immediately.*

"They filed it eight days ago," she said. "Before Daniel even went silent. Think about that. They filed claim on a wreck site they hadn't located yet — unless they already knew exactly where it was, and the survey expedition was never meant to find it. Only to confirm it. Or to disappear."

"And the fleet?"

"Six vessels. A demolition barge. They're staging at Port Anselm now. Their advance cutter reaches the wreck channel in—" she checked her watch, an old habit from someone who understood deadlines, "—call it five days, depending on tides. After that, whatever's down there belongs to them, legally, and they intend to blow the hull open rather than raise it. Whatever evidence is aboard goes to the bottom of history."

I stood there with the rope in my hands and the ghost of my own name in my ears and the morning light coming gold through the dusty window, and I thought about all the reasons to say no. There were many. They were excellent reasons. They had kept me alive and invisible for four years.

Then I thought about Julian Marsh writing *grossly negligent* in a gray suit while Hale's lawyers watched from the gallery, and about the version of him that had written the truth first and let it be buried, and about a wreck full of heirlooms — stolen heirlooms, colonial blood-money, the kind of cargo a company like Halcyon would dynamite a historical site to keep quiet — sitting in black water while a salvage fleet steamed toward it with legal paper and high explosives.

Spite, Elena had said. Fine. Spite was a fuel I knew how to burn. Hope was a luxury; hope was what had gotten two climbers killed above Kestrel Pass. But spite — spite had gotten me down the mountain alive.

"The river's in flood season," I said. "The channels shift every week. Your husband's charts will be wrong by now."

"Then you'll need someone who can read water," said a voice from the doorway.

We both turned. A kid stood there — sixteen, maybe seventeen, grease to the elbows, holding a wrench like a sidearm. Tomas Okafor, who rebuilt the fishing boats' outboards for pocket money and owed Harrow's owner four months of rent on a room behind the shop. He'd been eavesdropping with the total shamelessness of the young.

"Tomas," I said, "this is a private conversation."

"It is now." He stepped inside and let the door swing shut behind him. "You'll need the *Pelican*. She's shallow-draft, forty horsepower, and I'm the only one who can keep her running. And you'll need to leave tonight, on the ebb, because Brandt's scouts are already flying the estuary and every boat in this harbor gets logged."

Elena looked at him, then at me. "You know him?"

"He fixes what I break," I said. "Unfortunately for me, he listens while he does it."

Tomas grinned. It was a reckless, hungry grin, the grin of someone with debts and nowhere to spend his courage, and I recognized it too, God help me, from the mirror of twenty years ago.

I looked down at the counter. At the draft testimony with its murdered paragraphs. At the claim notice with Halcyon's seal. At the photograph of Daniel Vasquez's survey team, six faces squinting into river sun, four days silent.

Five tides until the cutter reached the channel. Maybe fewer, if Brandt ran ahead of schedule. The flood would make the upper river suicide until Thursday; after that, a narrow window, then nothing.

"Ruth Calloway quits," I said, and untied the apron.

Elena exhaled — a long, shaking breath, eleven days of fear leaving her body all at once — and pressed a key and a rolled chart into my hands. "There's a skiff moored at the north pilings. Provisions, radio, everything Daniel packed for me in case I found you. There's one more thing in the bag you should hear before you decide anything else."

"What?"

She lifted a battered handheld receiver from the satchel and thumbed the power switch. Static filled the shop, river-hiss and interference. Then, threading through it, thin and broken but unmistakable, a voice — repeating a position, a bearing, a plea, over and over on a dead expedition frequency.

I knew that voice. I had spent four years hearing it in my sleep, saying *grossly negligent* in a gray suit.

Julian Marsh was still alive. And he was calling for help on a frequency only a dead man's enemies would be listening to.

I took the chart, and the key, and the radio, and walked out into the morning to steal my own boat back.
