## 2. Six Tides

The tide tables were pinned above the outfitter's counter for eleven years before Ada ever looked at them properly. Now she stood at three in the morning with a grease pencil and a cup of cold coffee, marking the paper like a woman planning a heist.

Six high tides. That was the arithmetic Elena Vasquez had bought her.

The fleet's salvage cutter — the *Corvus*, out of Port Meridian, corporate registry, all chrome and litigation — was still two hundred miles downcoast taking on equipment. Harbor gossip said five days. Harbor gossip said a lot of things. Ada worked it backward from the wreck channel's draft requirements: the *Corvus* drew too much water to enter on anything but a spring high, and the next spring tide that gave her clearance came on the sixth rise after this one. Six tides to cover ninety miles of river that bent like a drunk's signature, past shoals and deadfalls and whatever had swallowed nine surveyors whole.

She circled each high tide in red, then wrote beneath them in small block letters: **NO SECOND CHANCES**.

The outfitter smelled of tar, rope, and the ghost of every fisherman who'd ever argued about bait at this counter. Ada moved through the dark aisles by memory, pulling stock without lights. A hand-crank radio. Two hundred feet of poly line. A case of flares she'd have to sign out under a name that wasn't hers — she used R. Calloway, the alias she'd been hiding behind for three years, and hated how easily the lie fit now, how it slid off the tongue like it belonged there.

*Ada Galloway doesn't exist anymore,* she reminded herself, loading the last crate into the skiff. *Ruth Calloway sells boat parts.*

But Ruth Calloway was provisioning a shallow-draft river boat at three in the morning for a run upriver after a vanished expedition, and there was no version of that story which ended quietly.

* * *

The boat was called the *Mudlark*, and she was beautiful in the way only ugly boats are: eighteen feet of welded aluminum with a flat bottom, a push-pole bracket, and an engine Tomas Reyes had rebuilt twice from scrap. Ada found him where she always found him, curled in the engine well behind the marina shed, sleeping in a nest of rags with a wrench still in one hand.

"Tomas."

He came awake the way strays do — all at once, no transition, eyes already tracking exits. Seventeen years old, all elbows and oil stains, with a debt to the Ferreira brothers that he talked about the way other kids talked about weather. Constantly. Cheerfully. Like it might change if he mentioned it enough.

"You're stealing your own boat," he said, sitting up. "That's a new one."

"I'm borrowing it urgently."

"Urgently." He looked past her shoulder at the crates stacked on the dock, the fuel drums, the tide table folded in her jacket pocket. His face did the thing it always did when he smelled trouble — brightened. "Upriver?"

"Ninety miles. Past the falls channel."

"The fleet's going up there." He said it carefully, watching her. Everyone in the harbor knew about the fleet; it was the only news bigger than the missing surveyors. "The *Corvus*. My cousin loads freight for them. They've got divers and a demolition barge and lawyers. Real ones, with ties."

"I know what they've got."

"So what have you got?"

Ada crouched so they were eye level. In the sodium light his face was all sharp angles and stubbornness, and she thought — not for the first time — that he reminded her of herself at that age, before the mountains taught her humility and a hearing room taught her everything else.

"A boat, six tides, and a bad feeling," she said. "I need someone who can keep the engine running when the mud tries to eat it. I can pay two hundred. I can't pay more."

"I owe the Ferreiras four hundred."

"Then you'll owe them two hundred after."

He grinned, and it was such a young grin, so unguarded, that she almost told him no. Almost sent him home. But home for Tomas was a shed behind a marina, and the Ferreira brothers collected debts with crowbars, and the truth was she'd already built this trip around him in her head somewhere between the second coffee and the third.

"We leave tonight," she said. "Before dawn. We go quiet past the point, no running lights until we're clear of the patrol line."

"And if the patrol stops us anyway?"

"Then you're my nephew and we're fishing."

"I'm a terrible fisherman."

"Then pray they don't ask questions." She stood, tossed him the engine key. "Prep the *Mudlark*. Fuel, water, spare prop. Nothing that rattles."

Tomas caught the key against his chest like something holy. "Boss," he said, and the word landed warmer than she wanted it to.

* * *

They slipped the harbor at 4:40 in the morning, riding the last of the ebb.

The patrol line sat across the harbor mouth like a held breath — two rigid inflatables with running lights, sweeping their beams across the black water in slow, patient arcs. Ada killed the engine a quarter mile out and let the current carry them, Tomas in the bow with the push-pole, walking them sideways through the dark in short silent strokes. The tide did most of the work. The tide usually did.

A beam swept toward them and Ada lay flat against the deck plates, cheek against cold aluminum, counting heartbeats. The light passed over the bow rail six inches above her head, painted the sky behind her eyelids red, and moved on.

"Coast is—" Tomas started.

She put a finger to her lips. He nodded, chastened, and poled them through the gap between the patrol boats while both crews stared intently at nothing, drinking thermos coffee, dreaming of shift change. Then the harbor mouth opened onto open water and the first gray hint of dawn was bleeding into the east, and Ada let herself breathe.

"Engine," she whispered.

Tomas lowered the *Mudlark*'s little outboard into the water and pulled the cord once, soft as a kiss. The engine caught on the first try, because of course it did; he'd rebuilt it. They ran south along the shadow of the headlands until the sun came up proper, and then Ada opened the throttle and the river took them.

* * *

It was midday, running the long straight reach below the old ferry crossing, when the radio made its noise.

Not static exactly. Static with a shape inside it. Ada had been scanning channels out of habit — half listening for fleet traffic, half for weather — when channel 16 coughed and produced a human voice, thin and shredded by distance and interference.

*"—any station, any station—"* A pause, thick with crackle. *"—Meridian survey, do you copy—position—"*

Ada's hands went still on the wheel.

The voice was male, hoarse, clipped at the edges like it was being spoken through gritted teeth or a broken jaw. It repeated itself the way drowning men repeat things, precise and terrible: position coordinates, a request for any vessel, then — cut off by a squall of interference — a fragment that didn't sound like distress protocol at all.

*"—he lied to us, all of it was—"*

Static swallowed the rest.

Tomas had come aft without being called. He stood beside her, radio handset in his white-knuckled grip, turning the gain dial with the delicacy of a surgeon. "That's them," he said. "That's the survey expedition. That's — boss, that's a live signal."

"I heard it."

"They said a position."

"I heard that too." The coordinates were burned into her now, a set of numbers floating in the middle of the flooded country upstream where the chart showed nothing but green mangrove and brown water. She keyed the transmit button. "Meridian survey, this is vessel *Mudlark*, do you copy? Meridian survey, come in."

Nothing. She tried again. Nothing but the river hissing back at her, and somewhere far downstream, faint as a headache, the fleet's working channels chattering about cranes and cargo manifests.

"He sounded hurt," Tomas said quietly.

"He sounded angry." Ada set the handset down. Her hands weren't quite steady, and she looked at them with clinical dislike. There was something in that voice — she'd turned it over three times since the transmission, the way you turn over a stone to see what's underneath. Something familiar. Not the words. The timbre. Some cadence underneath the static and the damage, some way the man shaped his vowels, that her memory kept reaching for and failing to close around.

"You know him?" Tomas asked. He missed nothing; it was his best and worst quality.

"No."

"You hesitated."

"I hesitated because someone is alive up there, and alive means I have to find them before the tide does or the fleet does." She pushed the throttle forward and the *Mudlark* surged, bow rising, the green banks beginning to slide faster. "Both of those are on a clock."

The afternoon aged around them. The river narrowed and darkened, folding into the drowned forest country where the charts went vague and the water went tea-brown and slow. Egrets stood in the shallows like punctuation marks. Twice they passed abandoned fish camps, roofs collapsed, pilings furred with moss — the river eating back everything people tried to leave on it.

At dusk Ada anchored in the lee of a dead cedar snag and spread the tide table on the deck, weighting the corners with fuel caps. Six circles in red grease pencil. She crossed out the first one — this morning's, spent, gone forever — and studied the five remaining.

Five high tides left. Ninety miles minus forty covered. Fifty miles of winding channel, plus whatever waited at the coordinates, plus a fleet cutter coming the other way with a demolition barge in tow and lawyers on retainer.

Tomas cooked rice on the single burner without being asked, and they ate in the blue twilight watching the water slide past, and neither of them spoke until the stars were fully out.

"Boss," Tomas said finally. "That voice on the radio. When it said *he lied to us* — lied about what?"

Ada looked upriver, where the darkness was total and the coordinates floated like a lit window in a burning house.

"I don't know yet," she said. "But I've been lied about before, and I know what it costs."

She rolled the tide table, stowed it, and lay back against the gunwale with the engine key in her fist. Sleep came slowly, and when it came, it brought the hearing with it — the long table, the company men in their good suits, and a witness at the stand reading testimony that hadn't happened, in a voice she almost recognized.

Somewhere upstream, that voice was still alive. Still repeating itself into the static.

Four days to reach him. Five tides before the fleet.

The clock ticked whether she listened or not.
