## 3. What the River Keeps

The river changed its mind about them a dozen times between the last beacon and the camp. It widened into brown lakes where the current forgot itself, then pinched through channels so narrow the mangrove branches scraped both gunwales like fingers testing for purchase. Ada ran the shallows by eye and memory — she'd guided this delta in another life, under another name, before the hearing, before the report, before the word *unqualified* followed her out of every office on the coast.

Tomas crouched at the bow with the depth pole, calling numbers in a voice that tried too hard to be bored. "Four feet. Three and a half. Mud, not rock."

"Keep it coming."

The sun sat low and coppery over the water when the channel bent north and the trees opened onto a shelf of black mud, and there it was: the expedition's camp, exactly where Elena's coordinates said it would be, and exactly as wrong as Ada had been dreading since dawn.

Three dome tents. Two of them collapsed inward, slashed — not cut open carefully to search, but stabbed through, the fabric hanging in ribbons. The third still stood, its rain fly half unzipped, one sleeve of a sleeping bag trailing out like something trying to escape. A folding table lay on its side in the mud. Papers everywhere, weighted down by nothing now but silt and rain, most of them pulped past reading.

No bodies. That was the first thing she made herself confirm. No blood she could see from the boat, no dark stains in the mud, no smell of it. Whatever had happened here had happened fast, and at least some of these men had walked away from it.

"Kill the engine," she said quietly.

Tomas killed it. The silence that came down was enormous — the river's own sound filling back in, water ticking against the hull, birds starting up again in the canopy, cautious, one voice at a time.

Ada let the boat drift the last twenty yards and stepped off into shin-deep muck, rope in hand, and hauled them up onto the shelf. She stood a moment just looking, because looking was the job. Anyone could walk a site. Reading it was the work.

* * *

She started at the landing, where the mud held prints best, and worked inland the way she'd been taught — by a man whose name she no longer said aloud, in a range she no longer climbed.

Six sets of boot tracks left the bank toward the tents. Five sets left again, heading downstream, hurried, overlapping, some of them half-filled with rainwater already — two days old, maybe three. She knelt over the sixth set and felt her stomach tighten before her brain caught up.

The sixth set didn't leave.

It walked from the tents toward the treeline on the north side, unhurried, even-spaced, a stride that belonged to someone who knew he was being watched or didn't care. It went into the mangroves and the mangroves kept it.

One man, walking away alive, alone, into country that swallowed people whole.

She followed the prints as far as the roots allowed, marking each one without touching anything, and found what she expected to find and had been praying not to: drag marks beside the last few steps. Not a body being dragged — a pack, heavy, its corner plowing a thin furrow every time the walker paused. He'd stopped twice near the treeline. Both times the furrow deepened, as if he'd set the weight down, turned, looked back at the camp.

Looked back at whatever he was leaving behind him.

"Ada." Tomas's voice cracked on the second syllable. "You should see this."

* * *

He was inside the standing tent, on his knees, holding a chart the way you hold a bird with a broken wing. It was a hydrographic survey sheet of the lower channel, professional stock, the kind the fleet's own cartographers used — and it had been torn clean in half, then torn again, then stuffed into a gear bag that had itself been ransacked and abandoned.

"I found it in here." Tomas's hands were shaking slightly. "It's torn, but I think— look, the edges match if you—"

"Give it here."

He gave it to her and she carried it out into the last of the light, laid it flat on the overturned table, and fitted the pieces together while he hovered. The tear ran diagonally through the channel markings, but the important part survived: a bend in the river six miles upstream, and penciled over it in a hard, impatient hand, a cross and a string of figures. Depth readings. Tide corrections. And beneath them, underlined twice:

**MERIDIAN — 11 FT AT LOW WATER. HULL INTACT. DO NOT ANCHOR OVER.**

"Do not anchor over," Tomas read aloud. "Why would you write that?"

"Because they weren't planning to dive it." Ada traced the pencil line with one finger. "They were planning to go inside it. You don't anchor over a hull you intend to enter — your ground tackle comes down through the deck and everything below shifts, silts, collapses. Whoever wrote this knew the wreck's condition before anyone officially located it."

"So they found it first. Before the fleet filed claim."

"Somebody did." She stared at the handwriting — quick, slanted, confident, the letters of a man who wrote standing up. "And then somebody else wanted this chart destroyed badly enough to slash three tents over it, and missed one piece."

Tomas was quiet a moment. Then: "The guy who walked into the trees. You think he took the other half?"

"The other half isn't here. So either he took it, or whoever tore it up took it, and I know which one I'm betting on." She folded the fragments along their tears, careful, and put them inside her jacket. "We're going upstream at first light."

"In the dark we're sitting ducks," Tomas said, and then, because he was sixteen and couldn't help himself: "For what? What happened here?"

Ada looked at the slashed tents, at the five sets of prints fleeing downstream, at the single line of tracks walking calmly into the green dark carrying a heavy pack.

"A fight," she said. "A short one. Somebody came upriver after these men, took what they needed, and left the rest alive to carry a message — *go home, stop looking.* Professional work. Which means our widow's husband wasn't lost. He was run off." She paused. "Or worse than run off, somewhere we haven't found yet."

"And the one who walked into the mangroves?"

"He wasn't taken by surprise. He saw it coming, packed what mattered, and left." She heard herself say the next part flatly, the way you read a verdict. "That's not a victim's stride, Tomas. That's a man who planned his exit."

* * *

They made camp aboard, anchored mid-channel where the mud couldn't be crossed quietly, and ate cold rice and tinned fish while the light died. Tomas rigged the little paraffin lamp low behind the wheelhouse coaming so its glow wouldn't carry, and spread the salvaged papers out to dry — bills of lading, a supply manifest, a page of tide calculations in the same slanted hand as the chart.

Ada turned the pages without much hope until she turned one that stopped her cold.

It was an equipment inventory, standard expedition format, columns of items and serial numbers. Her eyes ran down the list automatically, the way they ran down any gear list, and snagged on line fourteen.

**Rope, static, 9mm — 200m — BlueWater Dynamics — lot BW-4471.**

BlueWater Dynamics. Lot 4471.

She knew that rope. She'd bought two hundred meters of it with her own money, the year before the hearing, because the outfitter's budget rope had kinked and she hadn't trusted it with clients' lives. Same manufacturer. Same lot number — she'd written it in her own logbook, checked it against the batch certificate, because that was the kind of guide she'd been. Careful. Thorough. The kind of guide whose thoroughness got documented, timestamped, and then cited in a safety report as evidence of *nonstandard equipment practices.*

Her rope. Or rope from the same shipment, bought through the same supplier, ending up on a survey expedition led by—

By Julian Marsh.

The name rose in her like floodwater. She'd known it intellectually since Elena slid the file across the outfitter's counter — *expedition leader, J. Marsh* — had read it and told herself it was a common name, that the world was full of Marshes, that the universe was careless but not cruel enough for this. Now she was holding his handwriting. The same slanted, standing-up script she'd last seen scrawled across the margin of a deposition exhibit, the sentence that had ended her career: *Guide deviated from approved route; equipment substitutions noted.*

Equipment substitutions. Her carefulness, weaponized.

"He signed this," she heard herself say.

Tomas looked up. "What?"

"Nothing." She put the page face-down on the pile, deliberately slow, because her hands wanted to crumple it. "Get some sleep. I'll take first watch."

He didn't argue. He was learning. He rolled into his bag in the wheelhouse and within minutes his breathing went long and even, the way only the young and the deeply tired can manage in a place like this.

Ada sat in the stern with the rifle across her knees and the river sliding black past the hull, and thought about a man walking into the mangroves with a heavy pack and an unhurried stride. *A man who planned his exit.* If it was Marsh — and the handwriting said it was, and the rope said it was, and the sour taste in her mouth said it was — then he hadn't been run off. He'd walked away from his own crew the night they were attacked, carrying whatever mattered, and left five men to scatter downstream into the dark.

Just like he'd walked out of that hearing room eleven years ago without meeting her eyes.

The moon came up late, thin as a paring. Around midnight, Tomas stirred and muttered something about carburetors and slept on. Ada drank tepid coffee from a thermos cup and watched the northern channel, the direction of the wreck, the direction the tracks pointed if you extended them past the mangroves in your mind.

She almost missed it, because she was watching the water instead of the sky.

First it was a sound — a thin drone, far off, wrong for a night bird, too steady for wind. Then a light, high up over the tree line to the south, sweeping laterally across the stars. Running lights. Aircraft running lights, red and green, moving without hurry.

Then the searchlight snapped on beneath it and the world went white.

The plane came down the channel at maybe three hundred feet, slow, deliberate, its beam raking the banks, the mudflats, the standing tent on the shore — lighting it up stark and skeletal for two full seconds before moving on. Ada was flat on the deck before her conscious mind finished the decision, one hand clamped over Tomas's mouth, waking him, her other hand killing the lamp. The boat rode dark. The beam swung across the water fifty yards astern of them, close enough that she could see the mud boiling where fish fled it, and held there, and held—

And moved on. Upstream. Toward the north bend. Toward the cross on the chart.

Tomas's breath was ragged under her palm. She lifted it slowly.

"Fleet scout," she whispered. "Sweeping the river at midnight. They're not looking for a wreck anymore, Tomas. They know where it is. They're looking for *people*."

The plane droned on, a fading insect whine, its light strobing once against the clouds above the north bend and going out.

Ada sat back against the transom, heart slamming, and understood with total clarity what the next forty-eight hours were. Not a search anymore. A race — against a fleet with aircraft and a demolition schedule, toward a wreck that held either her salvation or her grave, and somewhere between here and there, in the mangroves, a man was walking with a heavy pack who owed her eleven years.

She found she was already checking the tide tables by feel in the dark.

High water at 3:40. They'd ride it north.
