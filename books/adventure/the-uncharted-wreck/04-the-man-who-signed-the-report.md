## 4. The Man Who Signed the Report

The stilt hut sat where the mangroves thinned, perched on blackened poles over a channel of slack brown water, its tin roof furred with moss. Ada saw the smoke first — a thin gray thread rising from a rusted stovepipe, too steady to be abandoned, too careful to be careless. Someone was living here. Someone who wanted to be found by no one.

She signaled Tomas to kill the outboard and let the boat drift. The current carried them the last hundred meters in near silence, the hull whispering through reeds, and she stepped off onto the mud with the rope in her fist before the boat touched the ladder.

"Stay with the engine," she murmured. "If I shout, you go downstream and you don't come back."

Tomas's jaw tightened, but he nodded. The kid had learned in three days that Ada Galloway did not repeat herself.

She climbed the ladder slowly, testing each rung, her knife already loose in its sheath. The hut's door hung open an inch. Inside: a cot, a crate for a table, a kerosene lamp burned down to a thumb of oil. Survey equipment stacked in a corner — theodolite, brass-cased charts, a radio with its antenna bent double. The expedition's gear. The expedition's ghost.

And on the cot, propped against the wall with a splinted leg stretched out before him, a man who looked up at her with eyes she had spent four years trying to forget.

"You," she said.

Julian Marsh. Thinner than the man from the hearing, bearded, gray coming in early at the temples, but unmistakable. The voice from the radio fragment. The signature on the report. The man who had stood in a wood-paneled room in Port Calloway and said, *I observed Ms. Galloway disregard the turnaround protocol, and I observed her leave the client's son behind at the high camp* — and with those sentences had taken her license, her reputation, her name.

Ada's knife was out before she knew she'd drawn it.

* * *

The hearing had been small. That was the insult of it — not a courtroom, not even a formal tribunal, just a conference room at the outfitters' guild with a long table and three men who wouldn't meet her eyes. Rain on the windows. Her hands folded so tightly her knuckles ached, because she'd been told that fidgeting read as guilt.

Julian Marsh had sat across from her in a pressed shirt, composed, sympathetic. He had been the expedition's surveyor then, second-in-command, the man she'd trusted to hold the rope on the descent. When Daniel Voss died in the whiteout — when she'd made the call to descend without him rather than lose the whole party — Marsh had been there. Marsh had seen everything. Marsh had agreed with her call at the time, had said so on the mountain, had gripped her shoulder and said, *You did what you could, Ada.*

Then he'd signed the report.

"I observed Ms. Galloway disregard the turnaround protocol." His voice in her memory was calm, regretful, rehearsed. "I advised against the descent. She overruled me. She left Mr. Voss at the high camp against his expressed wishes."

Every word a knife laid flat against the truth. There had been no protocol to disregard — the storm had erased the schedule. There had been no expressed wishes — Voss had been unconscious with hypothermia, and carrying him would have killed four people instead of one. And Marsh had never advised against anything. She had the memory of his nod, his *go, go, we're losing the light*, burned into her like a brand.

She'd stood up in that conference room and called him a liar to his face, and the three men had looked at her anger and seen confirmation. *Emotional. Unstable. Exactly the judgment the report describes.* Her license revoked. Her name passed from outfitter to outfitter on a list she was never allowed to see but always felt, like weather.

She had spent four years working under other people's names, and now the man who put her there was lying on a cot in front of her, alive, and her knife was shaking in her hand.

* * *

"Ada Galloway." He said her name like a man identifying his own executioner, and there was no surprise in it. He'd known someone would come. Maybe he'd even known it would be her — Elena's desperation had a long reach. "I'd say I'm glad to see you, but you'd probably use the knife."

"Give me one reason."

"I've got several. None of them will matter until you've heard all of them." He didn't flinch from the blade. His hands stayed where they were, open on the blanket, and she noticed the tremor in his left one, the fever-sheen on his face. The leg under the splint was bad. Badly set, badly infected. He'd been dying out here by inches. "You came eleven days upriver for the expedition. I'm what's left of it. Ask your questions."

"Where's your crew?"

"Gone. Three days ago. They took the longboat, the sat phone, the medical kit, and the chart case." His mouth twisted. "They left me the radio, broken, and the opinion that a man with a broken leg was a fair trade for a lighter boat. Voss's cousin was among them. He had opinions about me, too. Older ones."

"You signed the report." She took a step closer, the knife steady now, her voice low and flat. "Daniel Voss is dead because you lied. I lost everything because you lied. So before I ask you one more question, you're going to tell me why I shouldn't open your throat and let the river have you."

"Because I recanted." He said it fast, the words tumbling like men escaping a burning building. "Fourteen months ago. I wrote a full retraction — every detail, the pressure the company put on me, the payments, the threats. Voss's death was an accident of weather, and your descent was the only correct call anyone made on that mountain. I signed the original because Halcyon Marine owned my contract and my debts, and they told me that if I didn't, they'd bury me the way they buried you. I was a coward. I was twenty-six and I owed them eleven thousand dollars and they made it very clear what happened to people who crossed them." He dragged a breath. "I sent the retraction to the guild, to the licensing board, to Halcyon's own legal department. And then I found out what happens to recantations at Halcyon. They go into a drawer. The drawer goes into the sea."

"Convenient," Ada said. "A retraction no one's ever seen."

"It's not the only copy." His eyes held hers, fever-bright and unflinching. "The original report was drafted from the expedition's field logs. The logs that would have contradicted it — my own survey notes, the weather readings, the timestamps — were pulled from the archive. But Halcyon kept duplicates of everything in the days before they went digital. Paper. Filed. And the files that matter, the ones that would hang them —" he nodded toward the corner, toward the brass-cased charts and the theodolite, "— aren't in an office in Port Calloway. They're in a strongbox in the hold of a ship at the bottom of this river. The *Meridian* wasn't carrying heirlooms, Ada. That's the story they let people chase. It was carrying evidence. Their own captains' evidence. Records of everything Halcyon trafficked, salvaged, stole, and burned for sixty years. My survey found the wreck because I was looking for it. Not for gold."

The knife didn't lower. But her breathing had changed, and he saw it.

"You expect me to believe that."

"I expect you to want to believe it. That's better." He shifted on the cot, winced, and she saw the full damage of the leg — swollen, the skin dark above the splint. "Elena hired you. Elena has my letters. She knows what I found, and she knows the fleet that's coming up this river isn't coming to excavate. They're coming to dynamite. Halcyon's successor company holds the salvage claim, and the first thing a company does with a witness at the bottom of a river is make sure it stays at the bottom of a river."

"Your own crew left you for dead," Ada said slowly. "Who told them to?"

"Brandt's advance man bought them at the last supply camp. Cash and a cut of whatever the fleet pays for silence." He laughed, a dry, cracked sound. "I led those people. I chose them. And they sold me for less than you were blacklisted for."

Something moved behind her ribs — not forgiveness, nothing like forgiveness, but the terrible arithmetic of recognition. She knew what it was to be discarded by the machinery of other people's money. She had been a line item once too.

She kept the knife up. "Say I believe the strongbox exists. Say I believe the retraction. You still signed the report. You still stood in that room and lied while they took my name."

"Yes."

"Say it like you mean it."

"I stood in that room and lied." His voice didn't waver. "I was scared, and I was bought, and I let them take you apart to save myself, and I have thought about that room every single day for four years. I won't insult you by asking you to forgive it. I'm asking you to use me. I know where the wreck is. I know what's in it. I know how Brandt's people work, because I used to work for the same company that owns them. Hate me afterward. Hate me the whole way upriver. But I'm the only map you've got, and the fleet's cutter is two tides behind you."

Ada stood there a long moment, the lamp hissing, the river muttering beneath the floorboards. Four years of anger sat in her arm, in the knife, in the memory of rain on the hearing-room windows. And underneath the anger, unwelcome as a stone in a boot, was doubt — not doubt of his guilt, but doubt of everything she'd built her life on top of it. If he was telling the truth, then the world had been more rotten than she'd let herself believe, and her exile had been cheaper for them than a bribe.

"You're fevered," she said at last. "You'll slow us down."

"I'll slow you down," he agreed. "And I'm the only map you've got."

She sheathed the knife. It felt like a defeat. It felt like a beginning.

"Tomas," she called down through the door, "bring the med kit. We've got a passenger."

She was halfway down the ladder when Tomas's voice cracked across the water, sharp with alarm:

"Ada — boat coming downstream. Big one. Flying a company pennant."

She turned. Far up the channel, around the bend, a white hull gleamed through the mangroves — the fleet's advance team, a full tide early, and Captain Ilsa Brandt's flag snapping at its stern like a promise.

Marsh, watching from the doorway, went very still.

"That's faster than they said," he murmured. "They know I'm alive."
