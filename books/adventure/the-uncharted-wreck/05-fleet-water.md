## 5. Fleet Water

The scout plane came back at dawn, and this time it didn't pass over.

Ada heard it first as a change in the river's voice — a low thrum under the birdsong, the water shivering in rings against the hull. She was already awake, had been since the third watch, lying in the bow of Tomas's patched-together boat with the chart spread across her knees and her knife loose in its sheath. The plane banked once over the mangrove line where Marsh was hidden, circled twice, and climbed away north with a sound like a swallowed word.

"They saw something," Tomas said from the stern, not looking up from the outboard he'd half-disassembled. His hands were steady but his jaw wasn't.

"Or they wanted us to think they did." Ada folded the chart and stowed it inside her jacket, against her skin, where it had lived for two days now like a second heartbeat. "Keep working on that carburetor. If we have to run, I want it to actually start."

They were anchored in a side slough three miles below the wreck channel, tucked under a canopy so thick the morning came down green. It was good hiding water. It was also dead-end water, which meant that when the fleet came — and Ada had known since the plane that they would come today — there would be exactly one way out, and it would be behind them.

She'd spent the night doing what she always did before trouble: counting exits and counting lies. The lies were hers. She had told Elena Vasquez she'd bring back proof or bodies, whichever the river gave up first. She had told Tomas this was a survey job, clean and simple, salvage-adjacent, nothing that would put his name back on anyone's list. And she had told herself, standing in the outfitter's back room with the evidence of her betrayal spread across a widow's table, that she was taking this contract for justice.

That one she almost believed. Almost.

The sound reached them an hour after sunrise: diesel engines, more than one, running heavy and unhurried. Not sneaking. The fleet didn't sneak. They came up the main channel in plain view because they owned every legal inch of it, and legality, Ada knew better than anyone, was just violence with better paperwork.

Through a gap in the mangroves she watched them arrive. A work barge first, gray and slab-sided, cranes folded along its spine like sleeping insects. Then a shallow-draft tender riding low with gear, and behind both, cutting the water clean, a white-hulled patrol cutter with a company pennant snapping at its masthead. Meridian Recovery Group. The letters were new paint. The name underneath them was old money.

A launch detached from the barge — small, fast, six figures aboard in matching orange vests. It turned toward the slough mouth without hesitation, without searching, straight toward them, and Ada felt the cold arithmetic of it settle in her chest. They hadn't found her. They'd been told where she was. Someone upstream had talked.

Someone with a ruined face and a liar's tongue.

"Tomas." Her voice came out flat and calm, the guide's voice, the one she'd used on ledges and in white water and once, years ago, across a hearing room full of men in suits. "Tools down. Hands visible. Whatever happens next, you say nothing unless I nod at you first."

"What happens next?"

"I'm about to be offered something," Ada said, watching the launch grow, watching the woman at its bow — tall, silver-haired, hands clasped behind her back like a woman reviewing troops rather than riding a river. "And I need to know exactly how much it costs before I decide whether to spit on it."

* * *

Captain Ilsa Brandt stepped onto the mud without waiting for the launch to fully ground, boots finding the root tangle like she'd been born to it, and Ada understood immediately that everything else about the woman — the pressed uniform, the corporate lanyard, the pen clipped to her breast pocket — was camouflage. This was river people wearing a boardroom's skin.

"Galloway," Brandt said. "Or should I say *Marren*? The outfitter in Puerto Cielo knows you as Marren. Your license file says Galloway, revoked, pending review, four years running." She smiled without warmth. "I've read your file. All of it. The Kestrel Ridge rescue. The inquiry. The testimony."

"You've wasted a trip, then. You already know everything worth knowing about me."

"I know you're the best guide this coast has produced in twenty years, and I know a piece of paper is keeping you from being paid like it." Brandt stopped ten feet away, close enough for conversation, far enough for respect. Behind her, four fleet crew stood in the shallows with the studied boredom of men who'd done this before. "I also know you're sitting on a chart that my expedition needs, and harboring a man my expedition wants. I'm here to make that problem go away. For both of us."

The river moved around their ankles of shadow. Somewhere overhead a heron complained and went silent, as if even the birds understood negotiations like this one ended badly for the wildlife.

"Go on," Ada said.

"A clean record. Not pardoned — *cleared*. The inquiry reopened, the testimony re-examined, the blacklist dissolved by the same people who built it. Your license reinstated with seniority. Back pay, if you want to be petty about it." Brandt's eyes were pale gray and utterly patient. "In exchange: Julian Marsh, alive and reasonably intact, and the survey chart he stole when his own crew abandoned him. That's it. That's the whole price."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you're a trespasser on a licensed salvage site, obstructing a court-sanctioned recovery operation, in the company of a fugitive who faked his own death." Brandt shrugged, a small economical motion. "You've seen how fast paperwork moves when someone important wants it to. It moves just as fast the other direction."

Ada let the silence stretch. She'd learned that trick from rivers — most people couldn't stand still water; they'd fill it themselves. Brandt filled it.

"You're wondering why we want him," the captain said. "He's a disgraced hydrographer. He signed a report once that ruined you, and then, when it suited him, he claims he recanted — though curiously, no one can produce the recantation. You're wondering what makes a man like that worth a clean record plus whatever I'm authorized to add if you haggle." She tilted her head. "It's not sentiment, Galloway. It's liability. Dead men can't sign affidavits, but neither can missing ones. My employers prefer him *found*. What happens after 'found' is above my pay grade, and frankly above yours."

"And the chart?"

"The chart is property. Company property, commissioned by the company, drawn by a company employee. He took it. We want it back." Brandt's tone didn't change, but something behind it did — a hairline fracture of emphasis, the way ice sounds before it gives. "You've seen it. You know what's marked on it. So let me save us both the afternoon: yes, we know where the Meridian sits. Yes, we could find it without him or the chart, given time. But time is the one thing my employers didn't buy enough of, and you —" the pale eyes flicked to Tomas, to the gutted outboard, to the boat riding light on stolen hours — "you are spending time you don't have either."

Ada crossed her arms. "Say the rest. The part you came all this way to say carefully."

Brandt studied her for a long moment, and Ada watched the decision move through her like weather. Then the captain said, simply: "We're not excavating. We're demolishing."

The word landed in Ada's chest like a dropped anchor.

"The hull's degraded past safe entry. Colonial iron, two centuries of silt load, tidal stress fracturing the frames. A conventional recovery would take eighteen months and kill divers. So we drop shaped charges, collapse the structure in a controlled sequence, and lift the cargo off the bottom in pieces." Brandt said it the way a surgeon describes an amputation — competent, clinical, final. "The heirlooms come up. Everything else comes up as rubble."

"Everything else," Ada repeated slowly. "You mean the ship itself. The manifests. The captain's strongroom. Two hundred years of paper sealed in anaerobic mud."

"I mean the parts of the wreck nobody has filed a claim on, because nobody knows they exist." Brandt held her gaze, and to her credit she didn't flinch from it. "You're thinking about documents. I can see it. Some story Marsh fed you about proof, about evidence, about the company that ruined you both. Let me tell you what I know about stories like that: they require the paper to survive, and paper doesn't survive demolition. So whatever truth you think is down there, Captain Galloway — in eleven days, give or take a tide, it becomes chum."

The tide. Six high tides, Elena's broker had said, before the fleet's cutter reached the channel. They were ahead of schedule. Of course they were. Contracts, like testimonies, were only binding until someone powerful decided otherwise.

"Why tell me this?" Ada asked quietly. "You could have lied. Made it easy."

"Because you'd have found out anyway, and angry people do stupid things with dynamite barges nearby." Brandt glanced back at her crew, then returned. "And because I read your file twice, Galloway, and I don't think you're the woman they made you into. I think you're the woman who went back into a collapsing snowfield for two strangers and got blamed when only one came out. I think you want your name back more than you want revenge, and I'm offering you the legitimate version of it. Signed, witnessed, irrevocable." She extended her hand, palm open, over the brown water. "Marsh and the chart. Walk away whole. Or stay in the mud with a dead man's secrets and drown with them when the charges fire. Those are the options. I'll be at the barge until slack water tomorrow."

Ada looked at the offered hand and did not take it.

* * *

The launch receded upriver, engines fading, and the green silence closed back over the slough like water over a stone.

Tomas spoke first, from the stern, very carefully. "Eleven days. She said eleven days."

"She said eleven days. The contract said six tides." Ada stood at the gunwale, watching the wake of the barge flatten against the current. "Which means either the fleet's timeline moved, or Elena's information was wrong, or somebody wanted us hurrying scared into exactly the position we're in right now."

"Do we give him up?" The boy asked it plainly, no judgment in it, and she loved him a little for that. "Marsh. For the record. It's — I mean, it's everything you lost."

Ada was quiet for a long moment. Four years of blacklisted mornings ran through her: the outfitter job under a false name, mending other people's gear, reading tide tables at night like scripture, the slow erosion of being excellent at something no one would let you do. One signature. One sentence spoken by Julian Marsh into a microphone while she sat with bandaged hands and a dead client's mother in the front row.

*The guide proceeded despite conditions she was warned were beyond margin.*

One sentence. And now the man who said it was rotting in a stilt hut two miles upstream, left for dead by his own crew, claiming — *claiming* — that he'd tried to take it back, that the recantation existed, that the same company backing Brandt's fleet had buried it along with her career.

"He stays," Ada said finally. "Not for him. Because if there's paper in that wreck, Brandt's dynamite turns it to pulp, and pulp clears no one's name. The record she's selling me is a favor from the people who broke me. Favors get revoked. Evidence doesn't."

Tomas nodded slowly, then frowned. "Then we've got a problem. She knows where we are. Someone told her."

"Yes." Ada pulled the chart from her jacket and looked at it — the torn corner, the faded ink, the wreck channel marked in a hand she now knew too well. "And there's only one person on this river who could have."

She started the engine. It caught on the second pull, Tomas's overnight work holding, and the boat swung out of the slough into the main current, turning upstream, toward the stilt hut, toward the man whose testimony had drowned her and whose recantation might raise her again.

Behind them, far downstream, faint as a headache, she could hear the fleet beginning to unload its dynamite.
