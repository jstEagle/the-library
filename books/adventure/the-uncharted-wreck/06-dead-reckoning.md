## 6. Dead Reckoning

The side-channel opened off the main river like a wound, a green seam of water threading between mangrove roots where the chart said no channel should be. Ada killed the engine and let the boat drift, reading the surface the way she used to read snowfields — for the lie underneath.

"You're sure about this," Tomas said. Not a question. He'd been asking it in different shapes since dawn.

"The fleet's cutter draws two meters. This draws one." She nodded at the water sliding past the hull, tea-dark and slow. "If the old survey notes are right, this channel cuts four hours off the run to the wreck site. Four hours is the whole game."

"And if they're wrong?"

"Then we spend the next tide pulling the boat off a sandbar and Brandt wins without firing a shot." She took the tiller. "So let's not be wrong."

Marsh sat amidships with the torn chart spread across his knees, weighing its corners with two brass shell casings he'd found in the abandoned camp. He hadn't spoken much since the stilt hut. He didn't need to. Every time Ada glanced at him she saw the hearing room instead — the fluorescent light, his clean hands folded on the table, his voice saying *the guide exceeded her mandate* while her license died on the floor between them.

"Current's against us past the second bend," Marsh said. "We'll want to pole through the narrows rather than burn fuel fighting it."

"I know how to read water."

"I know you do." He said it quietly, not as flattery. As fact. And that was somehow worse.

She pushed the throttle forward and the bow swung into the channel, and the river closed behind them like a door.

* * *

Marsh watched her hands on the tiller and thought: *she hasn't changed.* Three years since he'd seen her, and Ada Galloway still steered like the water owed her money. He remembered that from before — from the expedition upcountry, before the report, before he'd signed his name to a lie because Halvard Shipping's man had slid a folder across a table and told him what would happen to the families of anyone who refused.

He had told himself, then, that it was survival. That the truth was already buried too deep to dig out alone. That a live coward could someday do more good than a dead hero.

Three years of telling himself that, and the sum total of his courage was a recantation letter hidden in a strongbox at the bottom of a colonial wreck, waiting for a woman who hated him to raise it.

The channel narrowed. Mangrove branches raked the gunwales, and the light went green and underwater-colored, and the smell of the river thickened — mud and rot and something older beneath it. Ada stood at the helm with one hand on the throttle, reading depth by eye, calling adjustments over her shoulder in a voice stripped down to bare function. *Hard left. Slow. Watch the snag.*

Tomas crouched at the bow with a push-pole, a wiry seventeen-year-old who flinched at loud noises but handled an engine like a surgeon. The kid kept looking back at Marsh when he thought nobody noticed. Fear, Marsh recognized. But curiosity too. Kids always wanted to know what kind of man was worth hating that much.

"Sandbar," Ada said suddenly. "Tomas, pole us left — *now*—"

The boat shuddered. Grounded. The current grabbed the stern and swung it sideways, and the hull groaned against the bar with a sound like a bone breaking.

* * *

They lost the supplies in eleven minutes.

Ada directed it like a disaster drill — Tomas on the pole trying to walk the stern around, Marsh hauling crates toward the bow to shift weight, the river taking everything that wasn't tied down. A case of drinking water went first, then half their fuel, then the spare parts bin, its lid popping open and spilling carburetors into the brown water like coins from a torn pocket. Ada watched a crate of food slide off the gunwale and vanish without surfacing, and something cold settled in her chest: three people, one tide window, and now barely enough provisions to make the trip home even if everything else went right.

Which it wouldn't. It never did.

"Off!" she shouted, feeling the hull lift a hand's width as the load lightened. "Everyone off the stern — push!"

They shoved the boat free into deeper water and climbed aboard dripping, and the arithmetic of the loss sat among them, unspoken. Marsh wrung out his shirt and started inventorying aloud, calm as a man counting change, and Ada wanted to hit him for it — for being useful, for being steady, for being exactly the competent leader the hearing testimony had proven him not to be.

"We have enough fuel to reach the wreck and drift back on the ebb," he said. "Food for two days if we're careful. Water's the problem."

"There's rain ahead," Tomas said, pointing at the bruised sky upstream. "There's always rain ahead here."

"Then we catch it." Marsh looked at Ada. "Your call. Push on or turn back?"

Every instinct she owned said turn back. Cut losses, regroup, come at it again with better numbers. That was the doctrine she'd taught a hundred clients. But the doctrine belonged to a guide with a license, and the license was gone, and the only thing left was the clock — six tides, and this one already bleeding away.

"Push on," she said, and hated how much it sounded like hope.

* * *

The narrows came an hour later, and the river tried to kill them there.

It happened fast, the way drowning always does. The channel kinked around a drowned tree, the current funneled hard against the outside bend, and Tomas leaned into his pole at the wrong moment. The pole caught between two roots. The river took the shaft, took the boy's balance, took him over the side before anyone could shout.

Ada saw him go under — saw the brown water close over the white flash of his shirt — and her body was already moving, hand reaching for the throw line, mind running the rescue math she knew by heart: current speed, downstream hazards, seconds of breath.

She never got the line thrown.

Marsh went in without taking off his boots.

No hesitation, no shout, nothing so dramatic as a dive — just a controlled slide off the gunwale into water moving faster than any man should swim, angling across the current toward the spot where Tomas had gone down. Ada steered after them both, engine screaming in the shallows, and for ten seconds that lasted a year there was nothing on the surface but debris and the churn of the drowned tree.

Then Marsh surfaced with an arm locked around the boy's chest, dragging him, and Ada brought the boat alongside close enough to break ribs and hauled them both over the gunwale by fistfuls of clothing.

Tomas came up coughing, alive, furious at himself. Marsh lay on the deck plates and breathed like a bellows, boots streaming, a cut on his forearm welling red where a root had caught him.

Nobody spoke for a long moment except the river.

"You went in boots and all," Ada said finally. Her voice came out strange. Rough.

"Heavier feet sink slower." Marsh wiped blood off his arm without looking at it. "Is he all right?"

"He's fine." She checked anyway — pulse, pupils, the works — because checking was easier than thinking. Because thinking led to the place she couldn't afford to go, which was that the man whose signature had ended her life had just spent his own without pause to save a stranger's kid. Heroes didn't sign false reports. Cowards didn't dive into floodwater. And Julian Marsh kept refusing to be either one, and every hour he refused made the hearing room in her memory a little less certain, and she hated him for that most of all.

"Tomas. Take below. Get dry or get sick." She turned to the tiller. "We've lost an hour. Move."

* * *

They ran the rest of the channel in a silence that wasn't empty. Marsh bound his arm with a strip of shirt, steering when Ada slept twenty minutes at a stretch against the gunwale — she fought it and lost, and woke each time furious at herself and found the boat still on course, the chart still weighted with shell casings, the coffee tin of fresh rainwater Tomas had rigged under the canopy filling drop by drop. Small competences, accumulating. Trust assembling itself out of parts the way Tomas rebuilt engines, without permission and against specifications.

Near dusk the mangroves fell back and the channel widened, and the air changed — salt coming in ahead of the tide, the river tasting of the sea it hadn't reached yet. And then the trees parted entirely, and the estuary opened before them, wide and pewter-gray under the last light, and there it was.

The Meridian.

Dead-low tide had done what a century hadn't: laid the ship bare. The old brig lay heeled over in the mud like a felled animal, her starboard side exposed from keel to rail, hull planking gone silver-black with age, ribs showing through the broken sections like the carcass of something enormous. Her masts were gone but the stub of the foremast still stood, leaning drunk against the sky, and the evening sun caught the exposed hull and lit it copper, and for a moment none of the three of them said anything at all.

"She's bigger than the charts said," Tomas whispered.

"They always are," Ada said. "That's how they kill you."

Marsh stood at the bow with the wind pulling at him, and Ada watched his face do something complicated — grief and hunger and fear braided together. Whatever he'd hidden aboard that ship, whatever letter waited in whatever strongbox, it was down there in the mud looking up at him. A verdict with a hull around it.

"High tide comes at midnight," he said quietly. "We go aboard at slack water, work the hold while she's dry, and be out before the water returns. After that—" He stopped. Downriver, faint as a match strike, a light blinked twice. Then again. Deliberate. Rhythmic.

A signal.

Ada raised the binoculars. Two miles down-estuary, framed in the last gray light, rode a shape she knew from the harbor charts and dreaded in her bones: a flat-decked barge, cranes silhouetted against the sky, riding at anchor in the deep channel.

Not the advance team. The demolition crew.

Brandt hadn't waited for the sixth tide after all.

"Then we don't wait for slack water," Ada said, slinging her pack. "We go now."
