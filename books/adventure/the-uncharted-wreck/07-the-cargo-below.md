## 7. The Cargo Below

The Meridian's hold was a drowned cathedral.

Julian Marsh went down the ladder first, because Ada Galloway would not have trusted him to go last, and he could not blame her. His boots found the rungs slick with silt and something soft that might once have been rope. Below him, the water lay black and still as poured ink, holding the light of their three lamps in trembling coins on its surface. The air tasted of iron and old wood, of two hundred years of river rot compressed into a single breath.

"Careful," he said, and heard how the word cracked in his throat. "The deck beams may be false. Colonial shipwrights doubled them over cargo spaces."

"You'd know," Tomas said behind him, not unkindly. The boy's voice still shook from the side-channel, from the moment the river had taken him under and Marsh had gone in after him without thinking — without *allowing* himself to think — and hauled him out by the collar while the current tried to peel them both off the world. Ada had watched that from the boat. He had felt her watching. Something between them had shifted by one degree, like a compass needle finding north again after years of lying.

Now they stood in the lower hold, waist-deep where the tide had already begun its slow return, and Marsh played his lamp across the dark.

The Meridian had been a merchantman of perhaps three hundred tons, and she had died hard. Her keelson was sprung, her frames fanned open like ribs of some enormous animal that had starved on its feet. Silt had buried everything to knee height in places, and from the silt rose the shapes of what the salvage fleet had come for: crates split open long ago, their contents scattered — porcelain shards catching lamplight like fish scales, a silver candlestick fused to a coral lump, coins fused into plates of green corrosion.

Treasure. Enough of it to make men lie, kill, dynamite a piece of history rather than let anyone else touch it first.

"It's real," Tomas breathed. "It's all actually here."

"It was never the point," Marsh said.

Ada turned. In the lamplight her face was all angles and suspicion, and he understood that he had maybe one chance left, standing chest-deep in a dead ship with the woman whose life he had broken, to say the true thing before the tide or the fleet or his own cowardice closed over it.

"The manifest," he said. "Elena's husband wasn't hunting gold. He was hunting paper."

He waded forward, following the memory of the torn chart — the one they'd found at the ransacked camp, the one Tomas had spent half a night deciphering. Herrera had marked it. Not the wreck itself; a point *relative* to it. The mainmast step. Every colonial merchantman kept its most valuable papers in an iron strongbox bolted beneath the mast step, where fire couldn't easily reach and mutineers couldn't easily pry. Marsh had known this the way he knew tide tables and harbor regulations: because eight years ago, he had signed a report saying otherwise. Saying whatever Corvane Shipping had paid him to say.

"There." His lamp caught it — a square of darker shadow against the silt, iron-rimmed, furred with rust but whole. The mast step above it had collapsed into a tangle of oak, and the box sat beneath it like a heart under a ribcage.

Ada came to stand beside him. She didn't speak. She just looked at him with those eyes that had haunted every bad night since the hearing, and Marsh understood that she was waiting for him to explain why a man who had destroyed her would lead her to the thing that might save her.

So he told her. There in the water, with the wreck groaning around them like something dreaming, he told her everything.

* * *

"I was thirty-one," he said. "Junior surveyor, Corvane Shipping, drowning in debt I'd made trying to look like the kind of man who belonged there. They gave me the Galloway inquiry because no one senior wanted it. A mountain guide had lost a client on a Corvane-chartered expedition — a client worth more dead than alive, thanks to an insurance policy nobody was supposed to read too closely."

"Weather killed him," Ada said flatly. "I made the call to descend. Weather killed him."

"That's what you said at the hearing. It's also what the meteorological logs said. I read them." Marsh's laugh came out wrong, a dry rattle. "That was the problem. I read them, and then I wrote a report that contradicted them, because my director handed me a draft and told me my career was in it. And I signed it. That's the whole story, Ada. No conspiracy in my heart. Just a frightened man with a pen."

"And the recantation?"

"Written six months later. Never filed." He looked down at the black water. "Because by then I'd learned what happened to people who crossed Corvane. The captain before me on this very survey — Reyes — tried to report the heirloom trafficking. Stolen estate pieces moving through colonial ports under legitimate manifests. He was framed for the thefts himself, court-martialed by reputation, ruined. Died poor. The company learned it could do that. To captains. To guides. To anyone." He met her eyes. "To me, if I spoke. So I didn't speak. I took the survey job when they offered it — mapping the Meridian site so the fleet could claim it — because refusing would have looked like disloyalty, and loyalty was the only currency I had left."

"But Herrera knew."

"Herrera knew everything. He'd found Reyes's private log in an archive in the capital. He recruited me for the survey not despite my history but *because* of it — because I knew where Corvane hid its bodies. We were going to raise the strongbox ourselves and go public. Then our own crew sold us out to the fleet's advance money, and Herrera—" Marsh stopped. Swallowed. "They left me for dead in the mangroves and took him upriver. Or he ran. I don't know which. I've been living in that stilt hut for eleven days eating raw oysters and rehearsing this conversation, and in none of the versions did I deserve your forgiveness."

The water lapped against his chest. Somewhere above, through the collapsed decks, daylight was failing.

"So the letter," Ada said slowly. "Your recantation. Where is it?"

Marsh smiled, and it hurt his face, because it was the first honest smile he'd worn in eight years.

"Where else? I hid it aboard."

* * *

The strongbox fought them.

The padlock had surrendered to two centuries of rust — Tomas broke it with a pry bar and a grunt — but the box itself had settled into the silt like a foundation stone, and the collapsed mast step pinned one corner. They worked in shifts while the tide crept up their ribs, Marsh digging with a spade they'd salvaged from the camp, Ada bracing the fallen timber with a jack from Tomas's tool roll, the boy bailing and hauling and watching the water line with the professional anxiety of someone who understood boats.

"Rising faster than the tables say," Tomas reported. "Spring tide plus the river's up. We've got maybe forty minutes before this hold floods to the beams."

"Then dig," Ada said.

At the twenty-minute mark the box came free with a sucking groan, and they hauled it onto a shelf of deck plating above the waterline, and Marsh knelt before it the way he imagined men knelt before altars in centuries he didn't believe in.

Inside: oilcloth, wrapped tight, tarred at the seams. Inside that: tin. Inside the tin — dry. After two hundred years, *dry*. Herrera had chosen the spot well.

Documents. A ship's true manifest in faded brown ink, listing cargo that matched no official record: estate silver from a plantation burned for insurance, reliquaries pried from mission churches, a dowry set reported stolen in a burglary that had killed two servants. Letters of instruction from Corvane's founding partners, discussing the traffic with the breezy cruelty of men discussing weather. And copies — *copies*, someone aboard had been keeping receipts — of the framing of Captain Reyes, including the forged ledger entries and a note in a director's hand: *Let him hang with his own rope. The guide business will keep.*

"The guide business," Ada read aloud. Her voice didn't shake, which somehow made it worse. "They were already doing it. Before me. It was a *method*."

"It was a policy," Marsh said. "And here—" He reached past her, careful, reverent, and lifted out a final packet, smaller, newer, wrapped in modern plastic. His hands were steady until they weren't. "This is mine."

She took it. Unwrapped it. Read.

Her lamp held steady on the page for a long time. Marsh counted his heartbeats and lost count twice.

"*I, Julian A. Marsh,*" she read quietly, "*being of sound mind and unsound career, do hereby swear that my report of 14 March in the matter of the Corvane charter expedition was composed under direction of Corvane management and is false in every material particular. The meteorological record exonerates the guide A. Galloway entirely. I signed what I was told to sign. I am sorry. I have been sorry every day since, which is not absolution, and I do not ask for it.*"

She lowered the page.

"You carried this aboard the Meridian."

"I hid it in the strongbox the night before the crew turned. If I disappeared, I wanted it somewhere no one would ever look — inside the company's own skeleton closet, sealed with its crimes. Poetic." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him more than the confession had. "Herrera thought it was melodrama. Maybe it was. But it seemed right that the truth should live in the same box as the lie's ancestors."

Tomas made a small sound. Both of them turned. The boy was at the hatchway, lamp raised, and his face had gone the color of the silt.

"The water," Tomas said. "It's coming in fast now. And there's—"

They heard it together, then: a sound rolling down the river channel, deep and diesel-heavy, vibrating through the Meridian's bones until loose corrosion rattled off the frames like sleet. Through the flooded hatch, framed in dying light, the silhouette of a barge slid past the wreck's exposed stern — cranes folded, deck crowded with men and equipment, and along its rail, stenciled in fresh white paint, the name of the fleet.

And stacked in plain view on its aft deck, glinting dully under work lights: crates of demolition charges.

"They're not waiting for the claim to clear," Ada said. She was already moving, shoving the documents back into the tin, sealing it against her chest. "They're going to blow her tonight."

Marsh looked around the drowned cathedral — at the ribs, the silt, the small stubborn island of dry plating where two centuries of evidence had waited for exactly this hour — and felt the tide close around his throat like a hand.

"Then we'd better be off her," he said, "before the witness is silenced."
