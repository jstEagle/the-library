## 8. Fourth Tide

The tide turned at 3:17 in the morning, and Ada knew it before she saw the water move. She felt it in the Meridian's bones — a long groan through the hull as the river shifted its mind, the wreck settling deeper into its own grave. She was in the hold, shoulder-deep in black water, prying at a corroded deck plate while Marsh held the lantern above her and Tomas kept watch at the breach.

"Anything?" Marsh asked.

"Rust and regret." Her fingers found the seam again. "Give me the crowbar."

The strongbox sat behind that plate — they were sure of it now. The chart's cipher had led them to the master's cabin first, then down through a collapsed companionway into what had once been the purser's stores, a low brick-lined room where the river had been patient for two hundred years. Ada could feel the box through the gap: iron edges, a padlock swollen shut, and beneath her palms something that made her breath go shallow — the crisp corner of paper, sealed in oilcloth, untouched by water.

Evidence. Not gold. Elena's husband hadn't died hunting treasure. He'd died because he'd found the truth, and someone upstream wanted it drowned.

Tomas's whistle cut the dark — three short notes, the signal they'd agreed on. *Boat.*

Ada froze, crowbar half-lifted. Through the breach overhead, where the hull had opened like a wound, she could see stars, and then she couldn't, because a searchlight swept across them and painted the mangroves white.

"Fleet," Tomas hissed from above. "Barge. Two skiffs coming off her side."

*Too early.* Brandt wasn't due for another tide. Unless Brandt had never intended to keep her schedule — unless the whole negotiation in Fleet Water had been theater, a way to fix their position while the demolition crew loaded charges.

Marsh's hand closed on her arm. "The documents—"

"We get them out." She hauled herself up through the breach, water streaming off her, and took stock in the searchlight's glare. Their boat was grounded on the mudflat fifty yards out, half-sunk, useless. The fleet's skiffs were cutting across the channel, engines muffled, six or seven shapes aboard each. And downstream, riding at anchor with her running lights doused until this exact moment, the barge — flat-decked, crane-armed, a floating machine shed with a name stenciled on her bow: *Vigilant Star*.

"Tomas." She grabbed his shoulder. "Skiff. Ours is dead — take the inflatable, the one lashed under the tarp. Get it in the water on the far side of the hull, quiet as you can."

"What about you?"

"I'm going to be loud." She pulled the flare gun from her belt. "When I am, you paddle."

"And Marsh?"

"Marsh takes the box."

He stared at her. Sixteen years old, all elbows and engine grease, with his father's debts around his neck like chain — and he looked at her then the way no one had since the hearing, since the last time she'd put herself between other people and the cost. "You're not coming."

"Not yet." She forced a grin she didn't feel. "Somebody has to teach Brandt some manners."

The first skiff grounded on the mudflat with a crunch of gravel. Boots hit water. Ada counted five, then four more, spreading into a line, moving well — these weren't dock thugs, they were hired professionals, and Captain Ilsa Brandt stood in the bow of the second skiff with a megaphone she clearly didn't need, her voice carrying flat and clear across the slack water.

"Galloway! I know you can hear me. You have something that belongs to my employers. Give it up and we'll discuss that clean record again — I'm feeling generous tonight."

Ada stepped onto the exposed keel of the Meridian, silhouetted against her own flashlight beam, and fired the flare straight up. Red light bloomed over the river, and in its glow she saw the boarding party flinch, saw Brandt's jaw set, saw — behind the wreck, on the far side, a pale shape sliding into the current with two men aboard, low and silent.

Go, she thought. *Go.*

Then she started shouting, waving the flashlight, making herself the only thing on the river worth looking at.

* * *

They took her at the breach, three of them, and she made it cost. A boot to the knee, an elbow she drove into someone's throat, a moment when she nearly went free — and then a rifle butt across the back of the skull and the world went white and sideways, and when it came back she was face-down in the mud of the mudflat with zip ties biting her wrists and Brandt crouched beside her, immaculate even at four in the morning, rain jacket buttoned to the throat.

"The strongbox," Brandt said. "Where is it?"

"You're standing on a national heritage site," Ada said, spitting mud. "Technically, everything here belongs to everyone."

Brandt nodded to one of her men. It wasn't much of a blow — a backhand, almost casual — but Ada's ears rang with it. "I offered you a clean record. That offer has expired. What I'm offering now is that you walk away from this river instead of being part of what happens to it." She glanced at her watch. "Demolition is scheduled for slack after next. Charges are already being placed. If your friends are still aboard when they fire—" She shrugged, a small, economical motion. "Well. The company prefers its history compacted."

"They're gone," Ada said, and hated how true it might be. "You're too late."

"Am I?" Brandt straightened, scanning the far bank, the mangroves, the black water. One of her men came jogging up along the mudflat and spoke low; Ada caught fragments — *inflatable*, *far side*, *two sets of prints heading inland*. Brandt's expression didn't change, but something behind it did, a recalculation. "One of them has the box."

Ada said nothing.

"One of them," Brandt repeated, softly, "has the box, and the other one is a child." She crouched again, close enough that Ada could smell coffee and diesel on her. "Here's what happens now. My men will find them by dawn — this isn't difficult country, and a man carrying forty pounds of iron doesn't move fast. When they do, I'll have both the documents and a hostage exchange. Unless." She let it hang.

"Unless what?"

"Unless you tell me exactly which channel Marsh will use to reach open water, and I let the boy go when we take him." Brandt tilted her head. "You've been trading yourself for other people your whole career, Galloway. I read your file. The Kestrel Ridge rescue — you went back up for a client who was already dead, and it cost you your partner's life and your certification. You can't help it. It's not nobility. It's a defect." She stood. "So. Defect or deal?"

Ada lay in the mud and thought about Tomas's face in the flare light. Thought about Elena Vasquez's hands shaking around a photograph of her husband. Thought about Julian Marsh, who had signed her ruin with a fountain pen and then, impossibly, spent three years trying to unsign it, and who was right now somewhere in the dark with the truth in his arms.

She thought about the fact that Brandt was lying. Of course she was lying. People like Brandt always lied, and the lie worked because people like Ada always said yes.

But saying yes bought time. Time was the only currency left on this river.

"He'll run the S-curve past the deadfall," Ada said. "It's the only deep water left before the bar. But he won't run it until full dark tomorrow night — he'll hole up in the day. You'll never find him by dawn."

Brandt studied her for a long moment, then smiled without warmth. "Thank you for your professional opinion." She turned to her men. "Put her on the barge. Cabin, not the deck — I want her where she can see the wreck."

* * *

The cabin of the *Vigilant Star* smelled of chart ink and cold coffee. They zip-tied her to a pipe bracket and left her alone, which was either arrogance or a message: *you are not a threat anymore*. Through the salt-fogged window she watched the fleet work the Meridian under floodlights — divers going down into the breach she'd widened, demolition crews hauling canvas-wrapped bundles along the deck on lines, the whole wreck lit up like a stage set for a play about erasure.

Her head throbbed. Her wrists bled where the ties had chewed them. Somewhere out there, Marsh was moving through mangrove tunnel-dark with forty pounds of iron and two hundred years of proof, and a sixteen-year-old kid was trusting her to have a plan, and she had nothing. No plan. No radio. No knife — they'd taken even the one sewn into her collar, which meant they'd read her file too.

Duty to others costs you everything. That was the lesson the hearing had taught her, the lesson Kestrel Ridge had carved into her, and here it was again, written in zip-tie marks: every time she chose someone else, the river took the payment out of her hide.

And she would do it again. That was the defect. That was the whole rotten truth of her — she'd trade herself for Tomas a hundred times, and hate herself for the arithmetic every single time.

Through the window, the floodlights flickered. Once, twice. Then steadied.

Ada frowned. The *Vigilant Star*'s generator hummed steady beneath her; the flicker wasn't the barge's power. It was the wreck lights — the portable array rigged on the Meridian's deck. Flickering like something loose. Like a connection being made and remade.

She leaned forward against the ties, straining, and saw it: a small figure moving along the wreck's exposed keel in the strobing light, quick and low, ducking between the demolition bundles. Not a diver. Not a fleet man. A familiar silhouette, all elbows, moving with the particular desperate grace of a boy who'd grown up fixing things he wasn't supposed to touch.

*Tomas.* Aboard the wreck. Free — or hiding — and doing something to the light array, or the charge lines, or God knew what, because she'd taught him exactly one thing on the river: *when you can't win, break their equipment.*

And below him, visible through the breach in the hull, divers' bubbles rising in strings — and among the floodlit debris on the deck, a canvas bundle that did not look like dynamite, dragged halfway to the rail, as if someone had been interrupted mid-theft.

The strongbox. Still aboard. Never left.

The cabin door banged open. A fleet tech, young, harried, a clipboard in his hand. "Captain wants the prisoner moved to the foredeck," he said, not looking at her, untying the bracket. "Something's wrong with the array feeds. She thinks there's a fault in the wreck's old wiring — says the Meridian's copper's still live somehow."

Ada let him haul her up, let him march her out into the wet wind, and kept her eyes fixed on the flickering lights and the small moving shadow, and began, very quietly, to count the seconds between flashes.

The pattern wasn't random.

Three short. Three long. Three short.

SOS — sent backward, from the wreck to the barge, in a code only two people on this river had ever used.

*Tomas had a plan after all. And he was asking her to trust him one more time.*
