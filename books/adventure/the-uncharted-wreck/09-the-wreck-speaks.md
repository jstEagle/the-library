## 9. The Wreck Speaks

The barge's hold smelled of diesel and wet rope, and Ada sat in it with her wrists zip-tied to a stanchion, listening to the demolition charges tick somewhere below her like a second heart.

Brandt had left two men to watch her. They were bad at it. One kept checking the radio because the fleet channel had gone strange for the last ten minutes — not static, not silence, but a voice. Calm. Measured. Reading numbers.

Ada knew that voice. She had spent three years trying to forget it and one week failing to.

"—lot forty-one, bill of lading stamped with the Halcyon Shipping mark, contents listed as 'machinery' —" A pause, the sound of paper turning. "Contents were not machinery."

The younger guard shifted his weight. "What is he doing?"

"He's reading," the older one said flatly. "He's reading cargo manifests on an open frequency."

"Not just manifests." Marsh's voice carried the particular patience of a man who had rehearsed this in the dark of a stilt hut for eleven days, alone, with nothing but his guilt for an audience. "Insurance claims. Court deposition transcripts. Letters between Halcyon's directors and a magistrate in Port Calloway — signed, dated, and witnessed. I have the originals in my hands right now. Photographic copies are already transmitting to three newspapers, two maritime boards, and the Admiralty archive via the relay buoy at Gannet Rock. You can't shoot a radio wave, gentlemen. Though I imagine someone is going to try."

Ada closed her eyes. *You magnificent, impossible liar.* There was no relay buoy. There was no transmission. But there didn't need to be — not if Brandt's backers believed there was.

The older guard keyed his handset. "Bridge, this is hold security. The man on the wreck is broadcasting. He's got — he says he's got documents. Company documents."

Static. Then Brandt's voice, tight as wire: "Jam him."

"With what? He's on our frequency. He's using *our* mast."

There was a silence on the bridge end of the line that Ada could feel through the deck plates, a silence like a man realizing the water has reached his knees.

Marsh went on, unhurried, merciless. "For the record of anyone listening: Captain Ilsa Brandt was not informed of the full nature of her charter. Her orders specify salvage. Her contract's rider specifies demolition. Demolition, of course, destroys evidence — and evidence is the only thing aboard the *Meridian* worth anything now. Whoever ordered those charges knew exactly what they'd be burying." A pause. "I'd suggest, Captain, that you ask yourself why you weren't told."

* * *

On the *Meridian*'s tilted deck, Marsh crouched behind the broken rail with the strongbox open across his knees and the fleet's handheld radio pressed to his mouth, and let himself feel — for exactly four seconds — the thing he had not allowed himself to feel since the hearing six years ago.

Not triumph. Triumph would come later or never. This was something smaller and stranger: the sensation of his voice coming out of his own throat and meaning what it said.

He had lied once, in a courtroom, from a chair, with a lawyer's hand on his shoulder and a director's promise in his ear. *Read the statement, Mr. Marsh. Sign here. Your expedition will be funded for a decade.* He had read the statement. It had cost Ada Galloway everything — her certification, her clients, her name — and it had bought him four years of quiet funding and one night in the mangroves with a bullet crease in his ribs, when the same company decided a recanting witness was worth less than a dead one.

Now he read the manifests aloud, item by item, heirloom by stolen heirloom, and every name he spoke was a nail pulled out of a wall.

The radio crackled. A new voice — clipped, expensive, the accent of a boardroom rather than a bridge. "Mr. Marsh. This is Aldous Renwick, Halcyon Maritime. I'm told you have certain materials."

"I have all of them, Mr. Renwick."

"You should understand your position. You're alone on an unstable wreck with armed contractors under my authority—"

"Under your *former* authority." Marsh watched the fleet cutter below, saw figures moving on its deck without purpose, officers arguing, a crane operator climbing down from his cab. "I've been listening to your channel for an hour, Renwick. Your own lawyers are on it. They're telling Captain Brandt to stand down. They're telling you to shut up. Because every minute you keep talking, you're adding to a transcript somebody, somewhere, is already saving."

Silence. On the cutter, a searchlight swung wildly and went dark.

"The charges," Renwick said at last, and his voice had lost its polish. "The demolition charges are armed. If we can't have the site—"

"They're on a timer set by your own demolition crew, and your own crew has abandoned them, and Captain Brandt knows the abort code, and so do I, because I made her first officer very generously drunk on brandy two nights ago in exchange for it." None of which was true except the last part, which was true enough. "Stand down, Renwick. Or go down in history as the man who blew up his own evidence while the whole coast listened."

The channel stayed open for nine more seconds. Long enough for Marsh to hear breathing, and then a click, and then nothing.

* * *

In the hold, the guards heard it too — the order, relayed down the chain in fragments: *stand down, stand down, all units stand down* — and something in the discipline of the fleet came apart like wet rope.

The younger guard lowered his rifle. "They're pulling everyone off the site."

"We're still holding the prisoner," the older one said, but without conviction, and then the radio spat again — not orders this time but the sound of the cutter's engine room being secured, men shouting about the tide, about getting clear before dark, about lawyers.

Ada worked the zip-tie against the stanchion's rusted edge. She had started the moment the guards stopped looking at her and started looking at each other; the metal had already sawed halfway through the plastic. She thought about Tomas, locked somewhere forward on this barge, and about the ticking below the deck, and did the arithmetic she always did: tide, time, distance, water.

The tie snapped.

She took the younger guard's wrist and turned it, put him down against the bulkhead before he understood he was in a fight, and had the older one's pistol out of its holster before he finished turning around. She did not shoot either of them. She pointed the gun at the deck and said, "Where's the boy?" and the younger guard, nose bleeding, hands up, said, "Forward cabin. Door's not even locked, lady. Nobody's guarding anything anymore."

She found Tomas behind a door held shut with a hook-and-eye latch, furious and unharmed, and the look on his face when she opened it was worth every zip-tie and every hour in that hold. "Took you long enough," he said, voice cracking in the middle, and hugged her hard enough to bruise, and neither of them mentioned it after.

"Charges," she said. "How long?"

"Saw the timer through the porthole. Forty minutes when they marched me past. That was—" He checked a borrowed watch. "Twenty-five ago."

*Fifteen minutes.* Ada looked up through the hatch at the sky going violet over the river, at the *Meridian*'s black hull listing against the last of the ebb, at the small figure still crouched on her deck with a radio in his fist.

"Tomas. Skiff. Now. I have to get the abort code to the wreck."

"You don't know the abort code."

"No," Ada said, already running. "But he does. And he's standing on a bomb."

* * *

Brandt found Ada halfway up the *Meridian*'s flank, hand over hand on the old mooring chains, and for a moment the two women simply looked at each other across ten feet of rotting timber and rising water.

Brandt stood on the barge rail below, alone. Her crew had melted away to the cutter; her orders had dissolved; her career was, at that moment, being read aloud on an open frequency by a dead man. She had her sidearm drawn. She did not raise it.

"Galloway." Her voice was hoarse. "You heard the broadcast."

"Every word."

"Then you know I'm finished either way. If the wreck blows, I buried it. If it doesn't, I failed to." The gun barrel dipped toward the water. "Renwick offered me a way out an hour ago. Bring him the strongbox, sink the evidence quietly, and the company makes my problems disappear. Their kind of disappearing." She laughed, short and bitter. "I told him no. First order I've ever refused. Felt good. Feels terrible."

"There's an abort code," Ada said. "Marsh has it. Fifteen minutes, Brandt. Help me get to him or swim clear — but decide now."

Brandt holstered the pistol. It was, Ada would think later, the fastest decision she ever saw the woman make.

They climbed together, two enemies sharing a chain, and the river rose around the *Meridian*'s flanks with the patience of something that had waited two hundred years and could wait fifteen minutes more.

Below them, in the flooded dark of the hold, the charges ticked on — and somewhere above, Marsh turned at the sound of their boots on the deck, saw Ada coming toward him out of the dusk with Brandt a step behind her, and shouted the only thing that mattered:

"The code! I can't remember the last digit — Galloway, I can't remember it!"

The water reached the first charge.

And Ada, hauling herself over the rail, remembered — suddenly, absurdly, from a drunken first officer's slurred bragging two nights she had never witnessed — that Tomas had heard it too, and that Tomas was thirty yards away in a skiff, and that the last digit was a number, and the tide was coming in, and there was no time left at all.
