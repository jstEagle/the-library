## 10. Slack Water

The Meridian went down for the second time on the morning of the fifth tide, and this time she took her secrets with her — not into silence, but into the world.

Ada watched it from the deck of Tomas's patched-together skiff, anchored a quarter mile off in the channel's deep water. The demolition charges Brandt had armed had done their work too well and not well enough: the hull had split along a seam two centuries of salt water had already opened, and instead of shattering, the old ship had simply sighed — a long exhalation of mud and black river water and bubbles that rose like applause — and settled into the channel bed with her masts still standing. One of them broke the surface at slack water, a bare spar leaning against the sky like a man resting his shoulder against a doorframe.

Around the wreck, the fleet was in chaos.

It had happened fast, the way real collapses always do. Marsh had found the fleet's radio room aboard the barge — or rather, he'd walked into it soaked to the waist, bleeding from a cut over one eye, carrying a waterproof case against his chest like a man carrying an infant through fire — and he had put the documents on their own frequency. Not a summary. Not a threat. He read them aloud, page by page, in the flat, careful voice of a man who understood that the words would be recorded, that they would outlive him, that every syllable was a nail in a coffin he was building with his own hands. The shipping manifests. The insurance filings. The letters between directors discussing which captains could be trusted to carry stolen heirlooms and which ones would have to be ruined when they asked questions. And then, last, his own testimony from nine years ago — the deposition Ada had heard read aloud in a hearing room while her career died quietly in front of her — followed by his recantation, written in a stilt hut with a dying pen, dated three weeks before anyone came looking for him.

He read until the frequency went dead. They cut his transmission twice. Both times he switched bands and started again from the page where they'd silenced him.

By dawn, the corporate backers' lawyers were on satellite phones telling Captain Ilsa Brandt, in language that left no room for interpretation, to stand down. By mid-morning, the story was everywhere — picked up first by a fishing-radio operator who recorded forty minutes of it, then by a coastal news service, then by everyone. The Meridian's cargo wasn't gold. It never had been. It was paper, sealed in a strongbox and sunk with the ship deliberately, by a captain who had decided that if the company was going to use the sea to hide its crimes, the sea could keep them until someone honest came asking.

The wreck itself was the witness. All those years, all those salvage claims and legal maneuvers and quiet disappearances — everyone had been fighting over a grave, and the grave had been holding the confession.

* * *

They arrested Brandt at noon, on the barge, in front of her own crew.

Ada stood on the skiff's deck and watched the police launch come alongside — real police, marshals flown upriver on the fleet's own helicopter, which was somehow the most fitting detail of the whole affair. Brandt didn't fight it. She stood at the rail in her salt-stained uniform coat with her hands cuffed in front of her, and when the launch pulled away she looked across the water and found Ada, and held her gaze for a long moment.

There was no hatred in it. That was the strange thing. There was something almost like recognition — two women who had both been used as instruments by the same machine, one of whom had figured it out too late.

Brandt said something. Too far to hear. But Ada read the shape of it, or chose to: *You knew what you were carrying.* Maybe. Or maybe she'd said nothing at all, and the river just moved the way rivers do.

Then the launch rounded the bend, and Ilsa Brandt was gone from the story, and the Meridian's masthead leaned against the afternoon light, and Ada Galloway stood alone on a borrowed boat with a name that belonged to her again.

* * *

The blacklist collapsed faster than she'd expected, and somehow that hurt worse than building it ever had.

The calls started within hours. A licensing board official whose voice she remembered from the hearing nine years ago, suddenly warm, suddenly full of *Miss Galloway* and *of course* and *any misunderstanding, fully rectified*. An outfitter in the capital offering her a partnership. A mountaineering federation representative who had once refused to return her letters, now reading a formal apology down a crackling line while Tomas grinned at her from the wheelhouse and mouthed every word in exaggerated pantomime.

She let them talk. She wrote down two names and hung up on the rest.

"That's it?" Tomas said, after the third call. "That's all they say? Sorry, sorry, welcome back?"

"That's how it works." She coiled rope on the deck, hands moving without her. "Nobody kicks the door open. They wait to see if it opens on its own."

"Nine years."

"Nine years." She cinched the coil tight. "And then one morning it's embarrassing to them that it was ever closed. Like weather. Like it happened to them."

Tomas leaned on the rail beside her, sixteen years old and scuffed from head to toe, a bruise blooming yellow-green along his jaw where the boarding party had grabbed him. He'd stopped flinching when he touched it sometime yesterday, which Ada took as its own kind of graduation.

"They're going to want you to lead things," he said. "Big things. Expeditions. You'll be gone half the year."

"Probably."

"I can run the shop."

She looked at him. He shrugged, elaborately casual, ears going red.

"The shop," she said. "Our shop."

"Somebody's got to fix the engine, and it sure isn't going to fix itself." He turned back to the water, hiding whatever his face was doing. "Besides. Somebody's got to be here when you come back."

Ada said nothing. Some things you didn't answer out loud, because answering them made them fragile. You just let them sit there in the slack water between tides, holding.

* * *

Marsh was waiting at the harbor master's office in the estuary town, two days later, when they brought the skiff downriver for the last time.

He looked terrible. He looked better than he had in the stilt hut, but that was a low bar — the man had been left for dead by his own crew, shot at, drowned nearly twice, and had spent a night reading a company's sins into a radio microphone. Someone had given him dry clothes. They hung off him like flags on a windless day. His hands were wrapped where the ropes had burned them, and there was a new scar over his eyebrow that he was going to carry for the rest of his life, and he stood very straight, the way men stand when they've decided exactly what they deserve and are prepared to receive it.

The marshals had released him pending formal review. Everyone knew what that meant. The story had made him something between a whistleblower and a ghost, and institutions didn't know what to do with either, so they did nothing, and waited.

Ada tied off at the dock and climbed up onto the planks and stood in front of him, and for a moment neither of them spoke. Behind her, Tomas made a great show of inspecting the engine cover.

"You could press charges," Marsh said. Quietly. No performance in it. "Assault. False testimony. There are lawyers now falling over themselves to represent you. You'd win. Whatever you wanted — restitution, prison time, all of it. You'd win."

"Yes," Ada said.

"It would be justice." He said the word carefully, like a man handling something that might still be sharp. "Real justice. The kind you earned. The kind I stole from you."

The harbor gulls wheeled and screamed over the fish market. Somewhere a diesel engine idled. The tide was turning; she could feel it under the dock, the slow reversal of everything that had been running one way all week.

Nine years ago, in a hearing room with bad lighting, a man's voice had read testimony that ended her life as she knew it. She had built a whole self out of the wreckage of that day — a false name, a counter job, a hardness she wore like armor plate. She had carried the weight of it up every mountain and down every river, and it had gotten heavier, not lighter, because that's what injustice does when you can't set it down: it doesn't fade, it accretes.

And now here was the man, standing in the sun, offering her the one thing she'd wanted since the day it happened. Permission to hate him forever. A trial. A sentence. A clean, lawful ending, signed and stamped.

She thought about the masthead breaking the surface at slack water. About a ship that had held its truth for two hundred years, not because it wanted revenge, but because the truth keeps. Because eventually somebody comes asking honestly.

"If I press charges," she said, "it's over. Really over. I get my justice and I lose—" She stopped. Started again, steadier. "I spent nine years being right about what you did. It kept me warm. It's a cold kind of warm, Julian. I'm tired of it."

His eyes closed. Opened again. "Ada—"

"You recanted. You wrote it down and hid it aboard a wreck and got left for dead trying to make it public, and then you read it aloud knowing what it would cost you. That's not nothing. That's not even close to nothing." She held out her hand — not to shake. Palm up. Open. "I'm not saying it's forgiven. I don't know yet what it is. But I'm not handing you to them, and I'm not spending the rest of my life collecting on it either. Take it or leave it."

He took her hand. Held it in both of his bandaged ones, like something rescued from water.

"Where will you go?" she asked.

"There's a survey vessel heading south next month. Legitimate outfit — Elena vetted them personally, which should tell you something about how thoroughly they'll be checked." A ghost of a smile. "Deep-water work. Months at a stretch."

"Months at a stretch," Ada repeated.

"I'm told the coast has good harbors." He met her eyes. "I'm told some of them are worth coming back to."

Behind them, Tomas slammed the engine cover with unnecessary force and announced, loudly, that the engine was fine, that it had always been fine, and that if certain people were going to take forever at the dock, certain other people were going to go find lunch.

* * *

They sailed on the sixth tide, as the tables said they would, though by then no one needed the tables anymore. The clock had run out. Everything it had been counting toward had happened.

The channel was glass-still at slack water, that brief suspended hour when the sea forgets which way it was going. They came past the point at easy throttle, Tomas at the wheel because he'd earned it, Ada standing at the bow with the wind finally — finally — just wind, nothing else, no deadline in it, no claim, no fleet.

The Meridian's masthead broke the surface ahead of them, black against the morning, streaming weed and light. Below it, sixty feet down, the old ship lay in the mud with her secrets published on every continent, her hold empty, her debt paid. Salvage crews would argue over her for years. Divers would visit her. In time she'd have a name on charts and a paragraph in histories, and schoolchildren would learn that the treasure of the Meridian was paper, and that paper, sometimes, is worth more.

Ada raised one hand as they passed. Not a salute, exactly. More like a acknowledgment between two things that had survived.

"Tide's turning," Tomas called from the wheelhouse.

"I know."

"We going home?"

Ada looked back once — at the masthead, at the water closing smooth behind it, at the whole drowned length of the story they'd raised and lived and let go. Then forward, down the broad bright reach of the estuary, where the current had begun to move again, gentle and certain, running the way it always ran: out.

"Yeah," she said. "Home."

And the sixth tide carried them in.
