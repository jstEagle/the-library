## 1. The Map That Wouldn't Burn

The trouble with Port Moresby in the wet season was that it smelled like everything you'd ever tried to forget. Salt and diesel and rotting fruit, the sweet-sour breath of the harbor at dawn, and underneath it all, for a man with Theo Brightwater's particular conscience, something like smoke. Like paper burning. Though he hadn't burned anything. That was the whole problem.

He sat on the seawall at Ela Beach with a warm beer he wasn't drinking and watched the fishing boats come in out of a sky the color of wet slate. Three weeks he'd been in the city now. Three weeks was too long. He'd learned that about himself somewhere on the coast of Queensland, and again in Darwin, and again in a dozen towns whose names he made a point of forgetting: three weeks was the limit. Past that, places started to recognize him. Landlords started asking when the rent was coming. And his own reflection, in bar mirrors and shop windows, started asking worse questions.

He'd left his last job — hauling freight manifests for a shipping agent who watered down his crews' pay — by simply not going back after lunch one Tuesday. Before that there had been a survey stint outside Lae that ended when the money did. Before that, other things, each one smaller than the last, a career shrinking in his hands like a map folded too many times until the creases wore through.

He was an engineer. He kept having to remind himself of that, because nothing in his current life suggested it. A man who had once laid grades through mountain country, who could read a river's temper from the color of its water, who had signed off on bridge loadings that carried a thousand tons a day — that man was sitting on a seawall with four kina and change, letting a beer go warm because even warm beer cost money he'd soon need for something else.

And in the inside pocket of his jacket, against his ribs where it had lived so long he sometimes thought he could feel its edges printing themselves into his skin, was the reason he couldn't stop moving.

* * *

It hadn't felt like stealing at the time. That was the lie he told himself on bad nights, and the truth he admitted on worse ones: it had felt like nothing at all. A Tuesday in the drafting office at Corrigan & Vale, eleven years of his life stacked in drawing tubes around him, and old Harrow the archivist out sick, and Theo alone with the fireproof cabinet that held the master copies of every line the firm had ever run or abandoned.

He'd been cleaning out his desk. That was all. He'd been dismissed — *restructured*, they'd called it, though everyone knew what the word meant when it came attached to a man who had asked one question too many about the Kikori extension — and he'd been putting forty years of pencils and parallel rules into a cardboard box, and the cabinet had been open, and the folder had been right there.

**CLOSED LINES — UPPER FLY & HIGHLANDS APPROACHES — DO NOT CIRCULATE.**

He'd taken it the way a man takes an umbrella left behind in a café. Absently. As if it belonged to him already, as if some part of him had been reaching for it for months without knowing why.

He'd known why, of course. He'd known since the day he'd stood in front of the wall map in the main office and noticed what no one else seemed to notice: that the closed lines didn't end anywhere. A line gets closed because the traffic dies, or the ground fails, or the money runs out. These lines stopped dead at coordinates in the middle of country that had never been surveyed — and then, thirty miles on, other lines began again, drawn in a different hand, on different sheets, filed under different project numbers. Rerouted. Bent around something the way a river bends around a stone it doesn't want to show.

He'd asked about it exactly once. He could still feel the temperature of the room drop. *"That's above your grade, Brightwater."* And then, six weeks later, the restructuring.

So he'd taken the folder home, and he'd meant to look at it properly, and he'd meant to do something — write to someone, anyone, a minister, a newspaper — and instead he'd spent eleven years not doing any of those things, carrying the folder from town to town like a man carrying a debt he couldn't remember contracting but couldn't stop paying interest on.

Twice he'd tried to burn it. Once in a brazier in Darwin, once in a camp stove on the Sepik. Both times he'd gotten as far as striking the match. The paper was good stock, heavy, the kind that doesn't catch easy. That's what he told himself. But he knew the truth, standing there both times with the flame trembling in his hand: he didn't want it gone. Burning it would mean deciding. Deciding would mean either admitting he'd been a coward for a decade, or finally acting, and both of those were heavier than the folder itself. So the folder stayed, and the matches went back in the tin, and Theo moved on before the questions could catch up with him.

* * *

The courier job found him the way jobs always found him now — through the back room of a trade store where men who needed things moved spoke quietly over warm beer.

"You're Brightwater? The one who knows rivers?"

The man was called Anis, a fat, quick-eyed broker with a mosquito coil burning at his elbow and a ledger of his own, though nothing like the kind Theo carried. "I move parcels," Anis said. "Documents, mostly. Nothing you need to look inside of. There's a government party working the Upper Fly — survey team, road alignment, very hush. They need resupply papers ferried up-country, and the regular runners won't take it this time of year."

"Why won't they?"

"Monsoon's coming early. River'll be a brown snake inside two weeks." Anis shrugged. "Also, the last runner came back saying the upper reaches are strange this season. Quiet. Boats that don't answer hails. You know how stories get."

Theo knew exactly how stories got. He also knew that four kina bought nothing, and that the advance Anis slid across the table — real money, folded soft with handling — bought a boat ticket, gear, and three weeks of not thinking.

"Where exactly am I taking these?"

"Government post at D'Albertis Junction. From there, whatever direction their survey party went, someone local will know." Anis tapped the sealed oilskin packet without opening it, the way a man taps a sleeping dog. "You don't read them. You don't lose them. You deliver them, you collect a signature, you come back downriver richer than you left. Simple work for a simple man."

"I'm not simple," Theo said, "and I'm not coming back."

Anis smiled with most of his face. "They're all not-coming-back, the ones who go up there. Then the monsoon ends and here they are again, buying beer." He pushed the packet across. "Boat leaves tomorrow, high tide. Don't be late. The tide won't wait and neither will the captain, and he's meaner than me."

* * *

That night, in a room above the trade store with a ceiling fan that turned like it was apologizing for something, Theo took out the folder.

He hadn't meant to. He'd meant to sleep, honestly meant to, the way he always meant to. But the room was hot and the fan was useless and the harbor sounds came through the louvers — chain rattling, a winch groaning, someone singing low in a language he didn't know — and his hands went to the jacket pocket the way they always did, checking, confirming, the old ritual.

He spread the drawings on the bed under the bare bulb.

Eleven years, and he still knew every line by heart. The Upper Fly approaches. Grades he'd helped draft in another life, contour lines flowing like water around the highlands, and then the gaps — those clean, deliberate absences where the ink simply refused to go. He'd measured them once, drunk, in a room much like this one: thirty-one miles of blankness across country that every other map in the cabinet treated as ordinary. Thirty-one miles where the railway didn't just close. It looked away.

And in the margin of the oldest sheet, in pencil so faint he'd needed the bulb angled just right to find it, a note in a hand he'd never identified:

*Valuation contingent. See K. Holdings. Do not file.*

See K. Holdings. Eleven years, and he still didn't know what K. Holdings was. But he knew what the note meant, the way an engineer knows when a span is overloaded — not by calculation first, but by the sound it makes. Something in those thirty-one miles was worth enough to make a railway lie about itself. Worth enough, maybe, to make other things disappear too.

He rolled the drawings tight, sleeved them in oilskin, and sat holding the cylinder across his knees while the fan apologized overhead.

Here was the arithmetic he'd been avoiding for a decade. The courier job went up the Fly. The Fly ran toward the highlands. The highlands were where the blank miles lived. Somewhere ahead of him, if he let the current carry him far enough, was the thing the map wouldn't say — and the thing he'd been running from and running toward, he'd long ago stopped being able to tell which, since the day he'd walked out of that drafting office with stolen paper against his chest.

Or he could get off at the junction, sign for nothing, hand the packet to whoever wanted it, and take the next boat anywhere. Darwin again. Queensland again. Another three weeks, another seawall, another warm beer going flat while a man rehearsed the speech he'd never give.

The bulb flickered. Out past the harbor, thunder muttered along the horizon like a wagon train of something heavy coming inland — the monsoon, early, just as Anis said. The whole coast would drown soon. The rivers would rise and take whatever they wanted and give it back rearranged.

Theo slept maybe two hours. In the morning he packed the folder deep in his kit, beneath spare clothes and a tin of biscuits, wrapped twice in oilskin, and went down to the wharf while the tide was still turning.

The boat was worse than he'd expected, which took effort. Forty feet of weathered timber with a diesel engine that sounded like a man clearing his throat before every difficult sentence, deck cargo lashed under tarpaulins, and a captain — Anis hadn't lied — with a face like a closed fist. Two other passengers, a trader and his boy, already aboard. Room for one more.

"Upper Fly?" the captain said, looking Theo over with the frank appraisal of a man pricing a beast. "You're paying to go up there this season?"

"I'm being paid."

"Worse." The captain spat over the rail, unhurried, a man who'd seen every kind of fool the sea sends upstream. "Get aboard, then. And if you've got anything you don't want the river to have, lash it down good. She leaks."

Everything I've got, Theo thought, stepping onto the deck with the kit heavy on his shoulder, is something I don't want the river to have.

The engine caught on the third try. Lines came off the pilings. The wharf slid away, then the trade stores, then the whole gray sprawl of Moresby with its rusted roofs and its smell of everything forgotten, and the boat leaned into the current and began the long climb inland.

Theo stood at the stern and watched the coast go. Behind him, the storm light built along the southern horizon, patient as a creditor. Ahead, the water ran brown and strong toward country he'd only ever known as ink on stolen paper.

Three weeks, he reminded himself. Just deliver the packet. Just keep moving.

But his hand had already gone to the kit strap, checking the folder's shape beneath the canvas, and the gesture felt less like habit now and more like a compass needle settling north.
