## 2. Gear Without Ghosts

The trading post at Wawoi Bend announced itself by smell before sight — kerosene, dried fish, the sweet rot of sago left too long in the humidity — and then by sound: a diesel generator coughing somewhere behind the trade store like an old man clearing his lungs of river water. Theo brought the boat in against the current with the particular care of a man who had learned that engines fail exactly when you trust them most, and tied off to a pilings scarred with decades of mooring rope.

He had been three days on the water since Port Moresby, and the courier job had turned out to be what most jobs upriver were: a crate of medical supplies and a bundle of mail for a mission station, plus whatever else could be wedged between the crates without shifting. The pay was thin. The pay was always thin. But it was money that arrived without questions attached, and questions were the one cargo Theo Brightwater refused to carry.

Except, of course, for the one sewn into the lining of his duffel.

He thought about it the way he thought about a bad tooth — probing it with his tongue when he should have been sleeping, tasting the ache of it. Six sheets of drafting linen, folded once each, showing the closed lines. The Sepik extension. The Fly highlands spur. Grades surveyed, costed, approved, and then struck from the company's published maps as though they had never existed. He had stolen them on his last night in Lae, sliding them out of the dead file cabinet while the office fan ticked overhead, and he still could not have told anyone — not honestly, not even himself — whether he'd done it because he meant to expose something or because he simply could not bear to leave the evidence of his own ruin behind. A man who has been blamed for a bridge collapse wants witnesses. Even paper ones.

The trade store was a long low building on stilts, its veranda crowded with netting, outboard spares, and a hand-painted sign advertising *COLD DRINKS* with the optimism of a place where cold was a rumor. Behind the counter sat a Chinese trader named Lau who had been upriver so long he spoke four languages and trusted none of them.

"You're the boat from Moresby," Lau said. Not a question. "You're late."

"River's late," Theo said. "I just follow it."

Lau counted out the delivery fee in creased notes, then paused, holding the last one. "There's a man looking for you. Or for someone like you. District officer. Came down two days ago, went back up this morning. Said he'd be at the government rest house." Lau slid the note across. "He asked about engineers."

Theo kept his face still, which was a skill he'd practiced for two years now, ever since the inquiry board had read out the findings in that flat administrative voice while his career came apart on the table like wet cardboard. "I'm a boatman."

"Mm," said Lau, who had eyes like a ledger. "That's what I told him you'd say."

* * *

The government rest house stood on the rise above the post, a prefabricated box with a wide veranda and a flagpole flying nothing. The district officer was waiting on the steps with a thermos and the patient posture of a man who had been told Theo would come and had budgeted his boredom accordingly.

He was younger than Theo expected — mid-thirties, sunburned at the nose, khakis pressed despite everything the climate could do about it. He stood when Theo reached the bottom step, which put Theo immediately on guard. Bureaucrats who stood for boatmen wanted something.

"Brightwater? Mr. Theo Brightwater?" The man extended a hand. "Hollis. District officer, Western Division. Sit, please. There's tea, such as it is."

"I didn't tell anyone my name was coming upriver."

"No. You didn't." Hollis poured tea into enamel mugs anyway, an act of hospitality performed regardless of consent. "The shipping manifest did. Cargo manifests list the master's name. It's one of those small tyrannies of paperwork." He smiled briefly, without much warmth in it. "Also, you have the hands of an engineer. I watched you tie off at the pilings. Boatmen cleat. Engineers knot. You made a stopper hitch I haven't seen since university."

Theo took the mug and said nothing. Above them the sky was doing its afternoon performance — building towers of cloud over the western ranges, blue-black at their bases, the monsoon still weeks out but rehearsing daily.

"I'll come to it," Hollis said. "A government survey team came through here eleven days ago. Six men. Route survey for the proposed highlands extension — the line everyone talks about and no one funds. They were due back here on the ninth day. On the eleventh, a patrol going up to meet them found their camp."

"And?"

Hollis looked out at the river for a moment, and when he turned back, the administrator's polish had thinned enough for Theo to see something underneath it. Something that might have been fear wearing a uniform.

"The camp was intact," Hollis said. "Tents pitched. Instruments laid out on the folding table — theodolite, levels, chain, notebooks under a stone to keep the wind from taking them. The kettle was warm. Not warm-ish. Warm. And there were six bedrolls, six mess kits, six men's kit bags, and no men. No tracks leaving the camp in any direction. No blood. No signs of struggle. The patrol waited a day, searched downstream and up, found nothing, and came back to report to me."

"Crocodiles take people," Theo said. "But they leave a mess."

"Feral men take people. They leave a mess too, usually, and they take the useful things. Nobody walked out of that camp naked and barefoot and left a theodolite worth more than my annual salary sitting on a table." Hollis set down his mug. "And nobody — nobody — leaves a warm kettle unless they left in the last half hour. Which means either the patrol's timing is a lie, or the kettle is."

"Or the whole story is," Theo said quietly. "Staged. For whoever comes asking next."

Hollis studied him. "Yes," he said. "That occurred to me too. That's why I'm talking to a stranger instead of sending my own constabulary. My own men know me. Know my habits, my routes, my family. If someone wanted to make a government survey team disappear and leave no loose ends, the loose ends would start with the people sent to look." He leaned forward. "You're a stranger. You have no family upriver, no clan obligations, no reason to lie to me and every reason to want triple pay. I checked your manifest name against the engineering registry before I ever came down here, Mr. Brightwater. T. J. Brightwater, civil engineer, Lae. Discharged from the firm of Marsh & Kellow two years ago following the Wewak trestle inquiry. Acquitted of negligence. Not rehired."

The name landed on Theo like a dropped spanner. He had spent two years being nobody, and hearing himself pronounced aloud — full initials, full history — felt like being stripped in public.

"There was a fault in the survey data," Theo said. The old defense, worn smooth as river stone. "Not mine."

"I'm not offering you absolution. I'm offering you work." Hollis reached into his shirt pocket and produced a folded paper — a route map, government issue, the survey team's planned line inked in red climbing away from the river into the highlands. "Follow their route. Find out what happened to them. If they're alive, bring them out. If they're dead, bring back proof. If—" he hesitated, and the hesitation was the most honest thing he'd done yet, "—if you find nothing, find out why there's nothing. Triple pay, paid on return, plus expenses. And Mr. Brightwater — if you don't come back, I will report you lost in the course of duty, and no one will ever ask what a disgraced engineer was doing on a closed government route."

It was well done, Theo had to admit. The offer and the threat wrapped in the same paper, so you couldn't take one without the other. He looked at the red line on the map. It followed the old grade — he could see it even at this scale, the way the route hugged contours that only a rail engineer would have chosen, switchbacks cut for locomotive gradients, not foot traffic. Someone had surveyed that line years ago and buried it, and now someone else was surveying it again, and then six men and a warm kettle had stopped existing.

His hands wanted the work. That was the shameful truth of it, the thing he'd been running from longer than the inquiry: give him a problem of grades and loads and water, and the guilt went quiet. The map in his duffel lining seemed to press against his back like a hand.

"Why me?" he said. "Really. You could hire any boatman with a rifle."

"Because any boatman would look at that camp and see ghosts," Hollis said. "You'll look at it and see engineering. Whoever staged that scene — if it was staged — knew how people expect a camp to look. I need someone who knows how camps are actually built." He stood, and held out the map. "Boat leaves whenever you decide. Storm season's coming early this year. Every day matters."

Theo took the map. The paper was damp at the edges already, drinking the air.

* * *

He told himself he'd sleep on it, and instead he walked down to the campsite the patrol had described, because of course the patrol had camped at the same bend coming back, and of course some part of the site remained — a fire ring, a scatter of tin, the trampled grass of too many boots. The river ran brown and heavy past it, carrying the smell of rain fallen somewhere far upstream.

He stood there as the light went copper and then grey, and let himself do the thing he was best at: read ground. Fire ring built by men who stacked wood the way surveyors stack wood, teepee-fashion, efficient. Tin ration labels from the government stores in Moresby. Boot prints gone soft-edged with nine days of weather, overlapping, heading everywhere and concluding nowhere.

And beneath all of it, faint, almost erased — a single line of prints, larger, deeper-heeled, leading up from the landing toward the camp and then back down again. One man. Walking in, walking out. No load carried either way.

Theo crouched over those prints for a long time, feeling the dread sharpen into something with an edge to it. A camp doesn't empty itself. A kettle doesn't stay warm on its own. And a man who walks into a vanished camp and walks out again, unhurried, empty-handed, is either the ghost who took them —

— or the messenger sent to make sure the stage was set correctly.

Behind him, up at the rest house, a lamp came on in the window. Triple pay. A route into the highlands along lines that had been closed for reasons no official map would admit. And somewhere ahead of him, six men's worth of absence, arranged with care.

Theo Brightwater had spent two years learning that stopping meant answering for what he'd done. But standing there in the failing light with the red-lined map in his hand, he understood that this time the choice wasn't between moving and stopping.

It was between walking away clean and walking toward the thing that had been waiting for him all along.

He went back to his boat, pulled the stitching loose on the duffel's lining, and spread six sheets of drafting linen across the thwart. The closed lines caught the lamplight, running inland like veins.

In the morning, he'd need a stronger boat.
