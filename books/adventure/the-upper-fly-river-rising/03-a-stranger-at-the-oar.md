## 3. A Stranger at the Oar

Kari had watched the white man for two days before she decided to hate him properly, and even then the hatred kept slipping its moorings.

He was easy enough to find. Port Moresby had a hundred men like him — sunburnt, broke, drinking their last coin slowly so it would last until some new scheme found them — but this one was different in ways that mattered. He didn't drink to forget. He drank like a man doing arithmetic, one finger of rum at a time, eyes moving over the room the way a surveyor's eye moves over country: measuring, noting, filing away. And he carried a leather tube everywhere he went, slept with it against his ribs, and had once, when a dockside thief brushed too close, moved with a speed that told Kari he had been afraid before and had learned what fear cost.

His name was Brightwater. Theo Brightwater. She'd heard it from the shipping clerk who sold him passage up the Fly on a leaking longboat called the *Marianne*, whose owner was happy to take a stranger's money and happier still not to ask why a railroad engineer wanted to go where there were no railroads.

Kari knew why. Or she guessed, and her guess was close enough to keep her awake.

She stood at the edge of the wharf in the grey hour before dawn, when the river breathed fog and the moths still worked the lanterns, and watched him load his gear. He packed like an engineer: everything squared, everything lashed twice, the leather tube wrapped in oilcloth and stowed not with the baggage but under the thwart, where a man's hand could find it in the dark. A compass hung at his neck. A revolver sat low on his hip, worn honest, the holster darkened by years of the same hand drawing and reholstering out of habit rather than danger.

*You're running*, she thought. *But you run in straight lines. That's the trouble with you.*

Her brother Tamu had been the same way. Straight lines all his life, right up until the government survey took him into the highlands and nothing came back but his instruments, laid out neat as Sunday on a table no one had touched.

The *Marianne* cast off at first light. Kari let it get half a mile downriver, then walked the bank path to the old ferry landing at Wabo bend, where the channel narrowed and every boat going upstream or down had to hug the eastern shore to dodge the snag that lay just under the surface there, black and patient as a crocodile's back.

She waited on the landing with her pack between her feet and her paddle across her knees, and when the longboat came sliding through the mist with its engine coughing like a sick man, she stood up and raised one hand.

Brightwater saw her. She watched him weigh it — the calculation running behind his eyes, visible as fish under ice. A woman alone on a landing at Wabo bend meant one of three things: trade, trouble, or knowledge. He cut the engine and let the boat drift in, which told her he wanted knowledge more than he feared trouble.

"You're losing water," she said, before he could speak. "The *Marianne*. She's been holed since the wet season and patched with tar and prayer. There's a seam below your waterline, port side, about amidships, that opens when you carry weight forward. You've been sitting too far aft to notice."

He looked at her for a long moment. Up close he was younger than his hands, older than his face, and tired in the marrow-deep way that sleep doesn't touch. "And how would you know that?"

"Because I know this river the way you know your rails. Every boat that works the upper Fly stops at my father's post for repairs, or doesn't stop and is found later." She stepped to the edge of the landing. "I'm coming aboard."

"No."

"I haven't asked yet."

"Then don't." He was already reaching for the throttle. "I travel alone."

"Alone," Kari said, and let the word sit in the fog between them. "That's why you bought a boat with a hole in it from a man who laughed after you paid him. That's why you're running the closed reach above D'Albertis Junction without a pilot, in monsoon season, following grades that were abandoned before I was born." She crouched, unhurried, and picked up her paddle. "You'll die alone somewhere around the second gorge, and the river will keep your compass, because the river keeps everything eventually."

The engine died in his hand. She heard him breathe once, slow, the breath of a man counting something.

"What do you want?" he said.

"Passage. As far as the highlands."

"Passage costs money."

"I'm not paying money." She stood, shouldered her pack, and looked him full in the face, letting him see that she wasn't afraid of him, which was true, and that she wasn't hiding anything, which was not. "I'm paying knowledge. The snags above the Junction shift every flood. The company charts are nine years old. Your government charts are worse. Without me, you'll spend three weeks learning the river's temper by breaking on it. With me, four days."

"And what's in the highlands for you?"

"Work," she said. It came out smooth. She had rehearsed it on the walk down from her father's post, and rehearsal held. "Guides are paid. Guides eat. My father has six daughters and one boat, and the boat leaks worse than yours."

It was a good lie. It had the shape of truth — the poverty was real, the family real, the guiding real. Only the reason underneath was false, and she felt it settle into place inside her like a stone dropped into deep water, sinking and sinking without reaching bottom.

Brightwater studied her the way he'd studied the thief on the docks, and she made herself stand still under it. Let him look. Looking was free; understanding would cost him.

"The survey team," he said suddenly. "You know them."

The stone in her chest turned over. "Everyone knows them. Government men come up the river, government men go down. It's talk."

"It's more than talk." He wasn't accusing her. That was the strange thing — he said it quietly, almost to himself, watching the fog burn off the water. "Their camp was found intact. Kettle warm. No tracks leaving. District office is offering triple pay for anyone willing to follow their route." His eyes came back to hers. "You didn't ask me what I was doing upriver. Most people would."

"Most people mind their own fires."

"Yes," he said. "Most people do." He stepped aside then, clearing the gunwale, and jerked his chin toward the bow. "Forward third only. You bail, you watch the water, and you tell me about every snag before we hit it, not after. If I say turn back, we turn back."

"We won't turn back," Kari said, stepping aboard, "because you can't. Whatever you're carrying in that tube, it's heavier than your gear, and heavy things don't float."

Something flickered across his face — quick, quickly buried, the ghost of a man caught. He turned to the engine without answering, and she took her place in the bow with her paddle across her knees and her back to him, which suited them both. Two people facing the same direction, each watching the other in the reflection of the water.

* * *

The first day taught her his measure.

He was better than she'd expected, which annoyed her. He read water like text — could see a submerged bar by the way the current wrinkled over it, could hear the change in the engine's note when the prop bit shallow gravel. Twice he corrected course before she called the hazard, and the second time she caught herself almost admiring him and shut the feeling down hard, the way you'd trap a rat in a food store.

But he was wrong about people, and wrong in a way that would get them killed. At midday they passed a fishing camp on the south bank, and the fishermen stood up in their canoes to watch them pass, and Brightwater's hand went to his revolver and stayed there until the camp fell astern.

"They're fishermen," Kari said.

"They were counting us."

"They count every boat. Boats mean trade, means news, means whether the mission truck has come with salt." She turned in the bow to look at him. "Not everyone on this river is hunting you, Brightwater."

"Enough of them are." He said it flatly, without drama, and that flatness frightened her more than any story would have. This was a man who had been hunted, recently, by someone competent. "Where does the channel go past the next point?"

"East of the green rock. West is a sandbar since the March flood." She paused. "Why did you leave the railways?"

The question landed harder than she'd intended. She'd meant it as probing, a small knife slipped between his ribs to see what was there, but his face did something complicated — guilt and pride fighting for the same ground — and she understood she had struck nearer the truth than either of them wanted.

"I didn't leave them," he said finally. "They left me. There's a difference, though nobody downstream believes it." He wiped oil from his hands with a rag, methodical, not looking at her. "Ask your next question somewhere else."

So she did, silently, all afternoon, while the banks unspooled past them — rainforest standing thick and green and indifferent, hornbills crossing the river in pairs like thrown axes, the water brown and muscular and carrying whole trees along like matchsticks. *What did you do? What's in the tube? Who taught you to flinch at counting eyes?* And underneath those questions, the one she never let surface, the one that burned: *Did you know my brother? Did your world send him up that river to disappear?*

Because that was the thing she hadn't told him, the twin of his own secret lying in the bilge beside his leather tube. Tamu had written her one letter from the survey camp, dated eleven days before the district officer found the tents empty. In it, a single line that she had read perhaps four hundred times:

*Sister, if anything happens, the numbers are what matter. Men can be replaced. Numbers cannot. Guard the numbers.*

Tamu had been a chainman, a measurer, a boy who counted paces for a living. He had never in his life used a phrase like *guard the numbers*. Someone had put those words in his mouth, or put fear there, and fear had dressed itself in his plain voice.

Kari bailed the boat at dusk, as agreed, and found what she already knew she'd find: a slow seep coming through the port seam, a cupful every ten minutes, patient as arithmetic. Brightwater watched her work and said nothing, and she understood that he knew too, and that they were both pretending the boat was sound, the way they were both pretending she was aboard for wages.

* * *

They made camp that night on a shingle spit below the second gorge, where the river ran wide and shallow and the fire could be small. He cooked rice and tinned meat and split it evenly without being asked, which surprised her, and then he did something that surprised her more: he took out a notebook and drew, by firelight, the day's channel from memory — every bar, every bend, every reading of current — in clean engineer's script, annotating depths in fathoms.

"You're charting," she said.

"Somebody has to. These reaches aren't on any map worth trusting." He didn't look up. "A river that isn't mapped is a river anyone can lie about."

Kari fed a stick to the fire and thought about her brother's letter, and about numbers, and about lies drawn on official paper in official ink. "Who lies about rivers?"

"Companies," he said. "Governments. Anyone who wants land to be worth less on paper than it is in fact, or more." He closed the notebook. "Get some sleep. We run the gorge at first light, and I want you fresh."

She lay back on her mat and listened to the river working the shingle, endless, patient, and watched him across the dying fire. He sat up long after she pretended to sleep, the leather tube across his knees, turning it over in his hands like a man holding something both precious and cursed. Once he looked toward the dark line of the forest and stayed looking, and Kari, watching through her lashes, saw his hand move to the revolver and rest there, not drawing, just resting, the way you'd touch a wound to check if it was still bleeding.

Someone was out there. Or he believed someone was. Either way, the fear in him was not imagination; it was memory, wearing the present like a mask.

Near midnight she heard him speak, so softly she might have dreamed it, addressed to no one, to the fire, to whatever ghosts a man like him carried:

"I didn't know. I swear I didn't know what they were doing with it."

Then he wrapped the tube in its oilcloth, laid it under the thwart within reach of his sleeping hand, and lay down with his back to the water.

Kari stared up at the stars through the gap in the trees and let herself feel the full weight of what she had done, boarding a stranger's boat with a stranger's secrets, telling her own smooth lie, carrying her brother's dead voice in her chest like a second heartbeat. Four days to the highlands. Four days of shared fire and shared water with a man who said *I didn't know* in his sleep.

In the morning, running the gorge, she would learn something that changed the shape of everything: the first sign, faint as a smell of smoke on wind, that the *Marianne* was not the only boat moving upriver — that behind them, quiet and deliberate, something else was climbing the Fly through the mist, keeping their pace, keeping their wake, and keeping its distance exactly.
