## 4. Closed Lines

The grade began where the jungle thinned, a long green scar running northeast into the hills like an old wound the country had decided to keep.

Theo saw it first from the water, before Kari did—or before she let on that she had. A straightness in the treeline. Nothing in the Fly's country grew straight; the rainforest was all elbows and argument, vines crossing vines, canopy shouldering canopy. But there, cutting through it, ran a line so level and so deliberate that his eye went to it the way a tongue goes to a missing tooth. He killed the outboard and let the boat drift, listening to the bilge pump churn its losing battle against the leak, and felt something in his chest turn over that he hadn't felt in three years.

Recognition. The shameful, sweet kind. Like hearing a language you'd sworn off spoken in a market crowd.

"That's ballast," he said. "Under the scrub. You can tell by the color—see how the green changes? Crushed rock doesn't rot the same way soil does."

Kari sat in the bow with her paddle across her knees, watching him with that flat patience she wore like armor. "You love this," she said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't kind.

"I hate it," Theo said, and pushed the throttle forward. Both things were true, which was the problem with him generally.

* * *

They left the boat tied in a side channel under a pandanus whose roots went down into the water like the legs of some patient wading bird, and they walked the grade inland. Theo had expected ruin. That was what *closed* meant on the maps he'd stolen—closed meant rusted rails torn up for scrap, meant culverts collapsed and bridges burned for their iron, meant jungle swallowing the right-of-way whole within a decade of abandonment. He'd walked dead lines before, in another life, in another hemisphere, and he knew their anatomy: the embankment slumping soft at the shoulders, the cuttings filling with wash, the whole thing dissolving back into the country like sugar into tea.

This wasn't that.

The embankment was sound. Not maintained-sound—*engineered*-sound, built once and built well, its slopes pitched at exactly the angle he'd have specified himself. The drainage ditches along both sides ran clear, graded true, emptying into culverts that still carried the hillside streams beneath the bed without a silted throat among them. Somebody had built this to last a hundred years without a hand laid on it after. And the rails—

He crouched at the first length of rail he found, half-buried in leaf litter, and put his palm flat against it.

"No rust," he said quietly.

"Everything here rusts," Kari said behind him. "Boats. Machetes. My brother's fishing hooks."

"Not steel like this." He scraped the railhead with his thumbnail, then with a stone, and the metal came up bright and silver-grey under the brown film. "This is weathering steel. Copper alloy. It forms its own patina and stops corroding. You use it when—" He stopped. He heard his own voice doing the thing it did, the lecture voice, the competent voice, the voice of the man he'd been before Port Moresby and the bottle and the letter that ended everything, and he hated how good it felt. "You use it when you're building something you intend to leave in place for decades without maintenance crews."

"Or," Kari said, "when you don't want anyone coming back to look at it closely."

Theo looked up at her. She stood with the sun behind her, arms folded, and her face gave away nothing except a kind of grim satisfaction, as if he'd finally arrived somewhere she'd been waiting for him to reach since the trading post.

"Show me," he said.

* * *

She led him off the main grade onto a spur he would have missed—a branch line, single track, angling northwest away from the surveyed corridor. And here was the thing that made the skin crawl along his forearms, made the engineer in him sit up and pay attention with his whole body: the spur was not old.

The main line's ballast was grey-green with age, its ties blackened and softened, ferns growing in the four-foot. The spur's ballast was pale. Its ties were hard, dark but intact, creosote still bleeding faintly at the bolt holes when the sun warmed them. The rails sat proud on their plates, gauge true—he checked it with his hands, span after span, because checking was the only prayer he knew—and the gauge did not wander more than a sixteenth of an inch in fifty yards.

"This is new work," he said. "Ten years old at most. Maybe five."

"The maps say closed," Kari said. "All of them. Government survey sheets, company atlases, everything. Closed lines, abandoned corridor, no rail service north of the confluence."

"So who lays fresh track on an abandoned corridor?" He was walking faster now, reading the ground the way other men read faces, and the thrill was rising in him despite everything—that hot clean joy of a puzzle yielding, of competence confirmed, of being for one hour the man he'd trained to be instead of the man he'd become. "Nobody builds like this for scrap value. Nobody builds like this for a mine access road—you'd use trucks, you'd cut a haul road, cheaper by an order of magnitude. This is rail-grade engineering. This cost real money. This was built by people who wanted it *hidden*, because if you're going to spend this much, you spend it openly unless—"

"Unless the land itself is the point," Kari said.

He stopped walking.

The spur curved ahead of them around the shoulder of a ridge, following the contour with the easy economy of a route surveyed by someone very good at their job, and beyond the curve he could see the valley it served: a broad green bowl of forest, unbroken, unlogged, no smoke rising anywhere in it. On the government map folded in his jacket—the one the district officer had given him, the official one, printed with the confident hatching of *closed line, abandoned*—this valley appeared as nothing. Blank space between two dashed lines. No villages marked. No trails. No names.

But he'd been in the country nine days now, and he'd learned to read what cartographers left out. There were gardens on those lower slopes—he could see the lighter mosaic of planted ground through gaps in the canopy. There were fish weirs in the creek, pale sticks in rows. People lived in that valley. Had lived there, he'd have bet his last kina, for longer than any company had held a lease anywhere on earth.

And every map he carried said the valley was empty, because every map said the line through it was dead, because a dead line draws no inspectors, no surveyors, no eyes.

"They didn't close the railway," Theo said slowly. The words came out of him like something being set down carefully on ice. "They closed it *on paper*. Rerouted the actual traffic around this land and let the paper version die. Officially there's nothing here. Unofficially—" He gestured at the immaculate spur, the tended culverts, the living valley. "Unofficially this is the best-built mile of railroad in the territory, and it exists so that nobody will ever come look at what's inside it."

Kari said nothing. But her jaw changed, some muscle setting hard, and Theo understood suddenly that she had known—not the engineering, not the specifics, but the shape of it. She had known since the trading post, maybe since before, and she had hired herself onto his leaking boat with a lie about passage knowledge because she needed someone who could read exactly this: steel, grade, gauge, intent.

"You knew," he said.

"I knew my brother walked into this country with a government survey team and didn't walk out," she said. "I knew the camp was found with the kettle warm. I knew the company men who came asking questions afterward weren't police, whatever papers they carried." She turned and started up the grade ahead of him. "Now you know too. Walk faster."

* * *

The fear arrived an hour later, and it arrived the way fear always arrived for Theo: late, and all at once, and wearing the face of his own competence.

Because competence was the trap. For an hour he'd been gloriously, stupidly happy—reading the line, calling grades and curves, showing Kari how to spot a hidden siding by the change in vegetation where spilled ore killed the understory, feeling the old machinery of himself turn over and run smooth for the first time since the disgrace. He'd forgotten, in the pleasure of it, the first rule of every job site he'd ever stood on:

If you can read the ground, so can the next man.

It was Kari who caught it, and she caught it not by looking at the ground but by looking at the birds. She stopped mid-stride, head cocked, and raised one hand, and Theo froze behind her with his heart already climbing into his throat, because he'd spent nine days learning what that hand meant.

"What is it?"

"Hornbills," she whispered. "Over there. They flew up twice from the same place." She nodded toward a bend in the spur ahead, where the line passed through a deep cutting between two ridges. "Something down in that cutting keeps startling them. Something that moves and stops and moves again."

Theo listened. The afternoon held its usual noise—cicadas, distant fruit bats, water somewhere below—but underneath it, or perhaps only in his imagination underneath it, he thought he caught it: a rhythm. Not continuous. Metronomic. Footfalls, spaced evenly, spaced *professionally*, pausing when the hornbills flushed and resuming when they settled.

Someone was walking the line behind them at patrol pace.

His mind did what trained minds do under threat: it went cold and fast and precise, and the precision showed him everything at once. Showed him that the cutting ahead was a kill zone, walls on both sides, one way in, one way out. Showed him that he and Kari had been walking a perfectly engineered corridor for an hour, a corridor designed to move men and material invisibly, which meant it moved pursuers invisibly too. Showed him that the same qualities that had filled him with pride—the soundness of the embankment, the clear sightlines, the absence of deadfall—meant there was nowhere to hide within a quarter mile in any direction.

He had read the ground like an engineer. Someone else had read it like a hunter.

"Off the line," he breathed. "Now. Slowly. Don't run—running tells them we know."

Kari was already moving, sliding downslope into the fern brake with a silence that shamed him, and he followed, crashing far less quietly than he liked, his pack snagging, his boots finding no purchase on the wet humus. They went to ground beneath a fallen trunk furred with moss, and Theo pressed his cheek to the earth and watched the cutting through a screen of fronds.

For a long time, nothing. His own pulse. The cicadas. The leak in his boat, miles behind, still faithfully drowning itself.

Then, at the head of the cutting, a figure stepped out onto the grade.

Distant—two hundred yards, maybe more—but unmistakable in the way that mattered. The man paused at the crown of the embankment and did not look around the way a lost man looks around, casting wide. He looked *down the line*. Straight along the corridor, the way a tracker reads a road, the way a supervisor walks a right-of-way checking the work. Then he lifted one hand, and two more figures came up over the lip behind him and spread out with parade-ground economy, one to each shoulder of the line.

Theo knew the stance. He knew the spread. He had stood in it himself a hundred times, on a hundred grades, in a cleaner life.

He knew the lead man's silhouette the way you know a voice on the telephone.

"Kari," he whispered, and his mouth had gone dry enough that the name barely made it out. "That's my old chief engineer. That's the man who fired me."

Kari turned her head, and in the shade under the log her eyes were very bright and very steady.

"Good," she said. "Then he knows your face too."
