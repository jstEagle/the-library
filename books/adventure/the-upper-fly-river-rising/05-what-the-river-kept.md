## 5. What the River Kept

Kari heard the man before she saw him.

It was a sound the river made sometimes at dusk—a thin, broken keening that could have been a bird, a child, or something worse—and every guide on the Upper Fly learned early to paddle past it without looking. Looking meant stopping. Stopping meant stories. But this sound had words in it, and the words were English, and one of them was *ledger*, repeated over and over like a prayer worn down to its beads.

"Stop," she said.

Theo shipped his oar. "That's not—"

"I know what it's not." She was already turning the boat toward the bank, toward the tangle of sago and pandanus where the water went shallow and brown and the air hung thick enough to chew. The leak in the hull had been gaining on them all afternoon; Theo bailed with a cut-down kerosene tin between strokes, and the boat rode low and sullen, like an animal that had decided to die and resented being argued with. They needed the bank anyway. That was what she told herself. Not the voice. The bank.

The man lay in the root buttress of a fig tree like something the river had tried to swallow and given up on. He was dressed in what remained of a government survey uniform—khaki gone green with mold, the brass buttons furred with corrosion—and his left leg was wrong from the knee down, swollen to twice its size and striped black where the infection had drawn its maps. Flies had found him. So had the leeches. He was maybe thirty years old and he looked carved from driftwood, except for the eyes, which were still burning.

When Theo knelt beside him, the man's hand shot out and closed on Theo's collar with a strength that seemed impossible, dragged from some reserve below the bottom of him.

"The ledger," he rasped. "You have to tell them. Tell Port Moresby. It's real. I saw it. Pages of it. He carried it against his chest—" His voice cracked and climbed. "The men came at night. Company colors. Blue and white. You understand? Not police. *Company.* They took the others up the old grade. They said there was no survey. They said we were never here."

"All right," Theo said, low and even, the way you'd talk to a spooked animal or a river about to turn. "All right. You're safe now."

"No one is safe." The man laughed, and it was the worst sound Kari had heard in a year, worse than her brother's name spoken by strangers. "He hid it. Tamasa hid it before they came. If they find me they'll find out I know. Water. Please. Water first."

Theo moved to get the canvas bag, and Kari crouched where the man could see her face, because sometimes that was the only medicine that worked—the shape of another person, close, refusing to leave.

"What is your name?" she asked in Motu, then again in English.

"Hollis." He drank from the tin Theo held to his lips like a man who had forgotten everything else but this. "J. Hollis. Assistant surveyor. Third party, Upper Fly traverse." His eyes wandered, fixed on something above the tree line. "We mapped a valley that isn't on any chart. Tamasa said the coordinates kept coming back wrong. Deliberately wrong. Someone had paid for the wrongness. He wrote it all down. Every payment, every signature, every village that wasn't supposed to exist."

Kari's hands went still on the man's shoulder.

*Every village that wasn't supposed to exist.*

She had grown up in one of those villages. She had carried water along paths that the company's maps showed as empty bush, and she had watched, three years ago, when men in blue and white came upriver with papers and smiles and a district officer's stamp, and by the end of the season her grandmother's house was a government depot and her people were scattered like seed thrown on hard ground. Her brother Tamasa had gone to work for the survey team that same year, saying he would make the maps tell the truth. Saying: *if the land isn't written down, little sister, it can be taken. So I will write it down.*

She had not known until this moment, kneeling in the mud with a dying stranger, whether Tamasa had succeeded.

"He hid it," Hollis said again. His fever was climbing; she could feel the heat coming off him like a stone left in the sun. "Before they took us. He gave it to—" The eyes rolled, lost their focus, found hers again with sudden terrible clarity. "You have his face. Around the eyes. You have Tamasa's face."

The river kept talking behind them. The monsoon light was failing, bruise-colored, and somewhere upstream thunder cleared its throat.

"Yes," Kari said. "I am his sister. Kari."

Hollis began to cry. There was no drama in it; it was simply what the body did when it could finally stop holding, the way a fist opens. "He talked about you. On the traverse. Every night. Said you could read water better than any man he'd met. Said if anything happened, if anyone ever came asking—" He gripped her wrist. "He said his sister would come. He said it like knowing a thing makes it true."

"Where is the ledger?" she asked. Gently. The way you ask a question you are afraid of.

"Gave it to Ropi. Second party. Downstream past the falls, at the old kiln site—he'd know why, he'd know—" The sentence drowned. Hollis shuddered, surfaced. "They're following the river too. Men in blue and white. A big one, with a scar through his eyebrow. He asked me questions for a long time. Then he left me. He wanted me to die slow, so the others would hear how I died." His grip tightened. "Don't let them hear how I died."

* * *

They built a shelter of palm fronds while the last light went out of the sky, and Theo worked in silence, competent and quick, lashing frames with vine, rigging a fly sheet against the rain that hadn't started yet but was gathering itself somewhere north like an army. Kari did what could be done for Hollis: cleaned the leg as best she dared, packed it with the bitter pulp she kept for such wounds, forced quinine and water into him until he slept in shallow, thrashing increments.

Twice in the night he woke calling for Tamasa. Twice Kari answered, *I'm here,* and let him believe it, and hated herself a little, and did it anyway.

Theo sat apart, sharpening the machete neither of them had yet needed, and she watched the firelight move on his face and thought: *this man is also running from something. This man also has a name he doesn't say straight.* She had known it since the trading post, since the way he looked at the abandoned camp—with recognition, not surprise. Since the map she'd glimpsed once, folded in his jacket, marked with rail lines that officially did not exist. She had climbed aboard his leaking boat with her own lie buttoned to her throat, and she had told herself the lies balanced out.

They didn't. Lies never balance. They only accumulate interest.

"You knew him," Theo said, not looking up from the blade. "Your brother. Before I hired you."

It was not a question, and she was too tired to build a wall out of deflection. "Tamasa was on the survey team. Assistant chainman. He sent letters downriver for eight months and then the letters stopped." She fed a stick to the fire. "I told you I knew the passage past the falls. That part was true. I told you I was guiding you for the money. That part was not."

Theo set the machete down. In the firelight his face did something complicated—not anger, not quite hurt. Recalculation. "Why come to me?"

"Because you had a boat going where I needed to go. Because you had a map with closed lines on it, and closed lines are where secrets get buried." She met his eyes across the fire. "And because you look like a man who owes a debt he can't remember the amount of. I thought maybe we could pay them together."

For a long moment the only sounds were the river and the small wet breathing of the wounded man and the first fat drops of rain hitting the palm fronds overhead, tentative, like fingers testing a door.

"My firm drew those closed lines," Theo said finally. "Brightwater—that's my name, my whole name, Theo Brightwater, engineer, third class, dismissed for falsifying load reports. Or so the story goes. I signed off on reroutes I never surveyed. I told myself it was paperwork. Lines on paper. Nobody lives on a line." His laugh had no floor under it. "Except people live on lines. Don't they."

"Yes," Kari said. "People live on lines."

Rain arrived properly then, drumming the shelter, filling the silence neither of them wanted to fill further. She watched Theo look at Hollis—at the ruined leg, the mold-eaten uniform, the government issue compass still hanging around the man's neck like a crucifix—and she watched him arrive somewhere, visibly, the way a boat arrives at a landing after a long time out of sight of shore.

"We should take him with us," Theo said. "Or leave someone. We can't carry him and outrun whoever left him here."

"He'll die if we move him."

"He'll die if we don't, once they come back to check their work." Theo rubbed his face with both hands. When he lowered them he looked older and somehow steadier, like a structure that has been tested and found true. "Turn back, take him downriver to the mission station, report to the district officer, let the government sort it out. That's the sensible thing. That's what a sensible man does."

"And?" she said, because she could hear the *and* coming like weather.

"And the sensible thing takes four days, and Ropi is two days ahead of us with a ledger full of dead men's evidence, and there are men in blue and white between here and there who are very good at making witnesses stop existing." He picked up the machete and slid it home in its sheath. "I spent six years telling myself I didn't know what the lines were for. I'm done not knowing. We go upstream. We find your brother's ledger. And we make somebody read every page."

Kari felt something crack open in her chest—not grief exactly, though grief lived in the neighborhood, but the thing grief had sealed shut: hope, uninvited, badly timed, alive.

Above the shelter, the rain thickened. Far to the north, beyond the black ridgelines, the sky flickered without thunder, a long silent stutter of light, and Kari—who could read water, who could read cloud, who had been reading the sky since before she could read—felt the hair rise on her arms.

"That's not ordinary storm light," she said quietly.

Theo followed her gaze. "What is it?"

"The monsoon. It's coming early, and it's coming angry." She stood, listening to the river, which had begun, very softly, to rise. "And Theo Brightwater—we are about to be on it."
