## 6. The Company's Men

The trestle ruin announced itself before they saw it — a smell of old creosote rising off the water, and then the pilings themselves, black and splintered, marching out of the jungle on the left bank like the vertebrae of something enormous that had lain down to die a century ago and never finished.

Theo cut the engine and let the boat drift. The river here ran wide and slow, braided around gravel bars the color of old bone, and in the hush after the motor died he could hear the country breathing: birds somewhere back in the green, the tick of cooling metal, Kari shifting her weight in the bow without a sound. She had gone still in a way he'd learned to read over four days on the water. Stillness meant she was listening.

"What is it?" he asked quietly.

"Trestle," she said. "The map you carry says the line crossed here."

"It does." He unfolded it against his knee, careful of the wet, and traced the grade with one finger. The closed lines ran up the valley like scars, and this crossing was marked in his own firm's drafting hand — *Trestle 14, decommissioned 1911* — though the timber below them looked older than that, older than anything his firm had ever touched. "We should pass under the near span and put a mile between us and it before we camp. Ruins draw people. People draw trouble."

Kari didn't answer at once. She was looking at the pilings with an expression he couldn't name, her jaw set in the particular way it set when she was deciding how much truth she could afford him today.

"Or," she said, "we stop under the far span. There's dry ground above flood line. My brother wrote of it in his letters — the survey team camped there their last night on this stretch." Her voice did not change when she said *brother*. It never did. That flatness was its own kind of grief, and Theo had learned not to touch it. "If anyone from the team came back downriver looking for us, they'd look for a camp at the trestles. It's where they'd expect men following their route to be."

"And if the wrong men are looking for us?"

"Then we're already found," she said simply. "I heard a boat an hour ago. Behind us. Engine idling, then nothing. Someone is drifting the way we drift."

The skin along Theo's arms tightened. He had felt followed since the second morning — a cigarette butt floating past their anchorage, too fresh; a flash of movement on a cutbank that might have been a bird or might not; once, at dusk, a thread of woodsmoke where no village stood on any chart. He had told himself it was nothing. He had gotten very good, over six years, at telling himself things were nothing.

"Under the far span, then," he said. "And we keep watch in shifts."

* * *

They made camp in the shadow of the ruin as the light went copper and then gray. The far span leaned overhead like a fallen cathedral arch, its trusses furred with moss, orchids growing out of joints where iron bolts had rusted to lace. Dry ground rose behind a screen of pandanus, enough for two shelters and a fire pit ringed with stones that some earlier traveler — perhaps the surveyors themselves — had laid in a neat square.

Theo cooked rice and tinned fish while Kari checked the boat. Their supplies were lashed under canvas: fuel for three more days, food for five, the medicine kit, the survey instruments they'd recovered from the first camp, wrapped in oilcloth because Theo could not bring himself to leave them even though they were useless to him now except as evidence. And beneath everything, in a waterproof wallet against his ribs, the map of closed lines, and folded inside the map, the page he'd copied from the district officer's file — the route the vanished team had been walking when they walked out of the world.

He was dishing the rice when Kari said, from the edge of the bank, "Light."

One light. Then another. Lanterns, moving along the ridge above the near span, unhurried, spaced like men who knew exactly what they were doing and had done it before.

Theo killed the fire with a sweep of sand and took her arm, drawing her back into the pandanus. His heart was going hard but his hands were steady, and that steadiness surprised him — six years of running had taught his body one useful trick, which was to go calm precisely when it should have panicked.

"How many?" he breathed.

"At least four. Maybe more behind the ridge." Her eyes were huge in the last light, fixed on the lanterns. "They've split. Two coming down to the water, two holding high. That's a search pattern, Brightwater. That's how you flush game."

"They're not looking for us specifically. They can't know—"

"Theo." She almost never used his name. When she did, it landed like a stone dropped down a well. "A government team disappears. A district officer hires a stranger with river experience to follow their route. You think the men who made that team disappear don't have someone watching the officer's office? Watching every boat that goes upriver? They let us come this far because they wanted to know how much we'd find. Now they know."

The logic of it settled over him with terrible weight. He thought of the warm kettle at the empty camp, the instruments laid out as if the men had stepped away for a moment and meant to return. He thought of the delirious first surveyor they'd pulled from the reed beds two days ago, babbling about a ledger and men in company colors before the fever took him back under. Company colors. Theo had assumed the man meant some local trading concern. Some faceless thing.

The lanterns reached the waterline below the near span. In their glow he could see figures now, moving with a discipline no poacher or crocodile hunter possessed — checking sightlines, covering angles. One of them raised a hand and the others stopped, and the man who had raised it stepped forward onto the gravel bar, lifting his lantern higher, and the light caught his face.

Theo's whole body went cold, colder than the river air, cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature.

He knew that face. He had sat across from it in a draughty office in Port Moresby for three years, taking instructions, signing waybills, laughing at its jokes at the firm's Christmas dinners. Broad, ruddy, heavy-browed, with the small neat mustache he'd always privately thought looked like something glued on. A voice like gravel poured slow.

*Aldous Renwick. District operations supervisor. The man who had signed off on every closure order for every line on the map against Theo's ribs.*

Renwick turned his head, scanning the water, and for one absurd moment Theo was transported — a memory of Renwick standing at a site office window, saying, *Brightwater, the numbers only work if nobody asks where the land went. Don't ask where the land went.* He had been joking. Theo had laughed. God help him, he had laughed.

"That man," Kari whispered. "You know him."

"Yes."

"Who is he?"

"He was my supervisor." The words tasted like ash and river mud. "Six years ago I worked for the company that built these lines. The closures — the rerouting — the land deals. His signature is on half the documents I stole." He swallowed. "He didn't just sign them, Kari. He *wrote* them. I carried the files. I carried them to his office and I never once asked what was in them, because asking would have meant knowing, and knowing would have meant—" He stopped. There was no version of that sentence that ended anywhere but in shame.

Kari looked at him for a long moment. Whatever she saw in his face seemed to decide something for her. "Then he knows your face too," she said. "Which means we cannot be taken alive and talking. Come."

* * *

They went downstream on the dark side of the island bar, paddling, engine cold, Kari in the bow reading the water by feel and Theo hauling on the spare oar with his shoulders burning. Behind them the lanterns multiplied — five now, maybe six, spread along both banks, and once a flashlight beam swept the water so close that Theo felt it brush the back of his neck like a hand.

Then the sky broke open ahead of schedule.

It did not begin as rain. It began as a pressure change, a dropping in his ears, and the birds going silent all at once, and then the first wind came down the valley like a door thrown open in heaven, flattening the pandanus, tearing the river into whitecaps where whitecaps had no business being. The first drops hit like flung gravel. Then the sky simply fell.

They ran the boat into a mangrove chute on the leeward side of the bar and got the canvas half-lashed before the real rain arrived and made the world a single roaring gray thing. Lightning walked the ridgelines. Thunder came not as sound but as concussion, in the chest. Theo crouched in the bilge bailing with a tin pan while Kari held the painter and shouted things he couldn't hear, and through the curtain of water he watched the lanterns on the shore wink out one by one — not abandoned, he was sure, but shuttered. Men like Renwick did not stop hunting in weather like this. They simply hunted blind, and waited.

When the worst of the squall passed and settled into the steady monsoon drumming that would not stop for days, they took stock, and the taking of the stock was its own quiet catastrophe. The canvas had ripped. Half their rice was paste in its sacks. Two fuel drums had shipped water; the third was gone entirely, torn loose somewhere in the chute. The medicine kit had survived, and the instruments, and the map against his ribs, and that was nearly all.

"Three days' food," Kari said at last, wringing out her shirt with hands that did not shake. "Half a drum of fuel. We cannot go back and we cannot go fast."

"We go forward."

"Forward is the highlands. Forward is where they want us to go." She said it without accusation, which somehow made it worse. "You understand that, yes? They let the storm do half their work. Now they follow our wake at their leisure and take whatever's left of us at the place where the ledger is."

Theo looked upstream, into the black, into the rain. Somewhere up there was a dying man with a ledger full of names — villages bought for nothing, erased from maps, erased from the earth. Somewhere up there, too, was Aldous Renwick's employer, and Renwick himself, patient as rot.

He had spent six years telling himself he hadn't known. Standing in the flooded mangrove chute with the ruins of his supplies around him, Theo understood at last that this had always been the lie at the center of his life: not that he hadn't known, but that not-knowing had been a choice, renewed daily, purchased with silence. Renwick was coming up that river because Theo had finally stopped choosing it.

"Kari," he said. "The man who wrote those closure orders — the land they rerouted around. What was on it?"

She was quiet long enough that the rain filled the silence for her.

"You'll see it tomorrow," she said. "We pass it at first light. My family's ground." She picked up the bailer and handed it to him, and her eyes in the lightning-flash were not grieving anymore. They were lit. "Row, Brightwater. Row like the past is behind you. Because it is, and it's gaining."
