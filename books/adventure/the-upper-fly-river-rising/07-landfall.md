## 7. Landfall

The sky had been lying to them all afternoon.

Kari had watched it from the stern, one hand on the tiller, the other bailing with a dented tin pan because the pump had died two bends back and Theo's hands were needed on the engine. The clouds over the highlands had gone the color of wet slate, stacked in towers that leaned east like men listening at a door. Her grandmother used to say that before the big rains, the sky gathered itself the way a gathering crowd does—quiet first, then all at once too loud to argue with.

Theo kept glancing back at her. She could feel it between her shoulder blades, that particular attention of his, careful and guilty, like a man checking whether he'd left a stove burning.

"Bank," she said, pointing left. "There's an overhang past the fig trees. We can't run this water when it comes."

He didn't argue. That was something she'd learned about him in four days on the river: he argued with himself constantly, out loud, in fragments, but with her he only listened, as if her word were a rail spike he could trust to hold.

The rain arrived not as rain but as a wall. One moment the far bank was visible; the next it was erased, and the sound came down on them like gravel poured onto tin roofing. Kari hauled the boat toward the overhang while Theo killed the engine and they drifted the last thirty meters on brown, surging water thick with leaves and broken cane. The world went white with noise. Then the stone roof closed over them and the roar dulled to something almost bearable, and there was only the boat knocking against rock, the lantern Theo lit with hands that shook more than the cold explained, and the water rising inch by patient inch inside their shelter.

They bailed through the night.

It was stupid work and endless work—the river climbing into the cave mouth in slow pulses, each pulse higher than the last, the two of them passing the pan and a cracked bucket back and forth until Kari's shoulders burned and her palms went soft as candle wax. Theo worked beside her without complaint, shirtless, hair plastered flat, and she hated how steadily he did it, hated the competence of him, the way even now, soaked and hunted and half-drowned, he moved like a man who understood load-bearing things.

Somewhere past midnight the water stopped gaining. They wedged the boat between two rocks, dragged their soaked swag up onto a shelf of dry-ish stone, and sat with their backs against the cave wall, listening to the monsoon hammer the country outside like a fist on a locked door.

Kari couldn't hold it anymore. It had been building since the delirious surveyor by the cutbank, since the ledger, since her brother's name spoken aloud for the first time in weeks—*Tomasi carried it, Tomasi carried it*—and here in the dark with the storm erasing everything else, it came up her throat like floodwater.

"You signed it," she said.

Theo turned his head. In the lantern light his face looked older than it had in Moresby, or maybe she was only finally seeing it straight.

"The agreement," she said. "The land transfer. Your company. Your name would have been on the engineering certification—I've seen the copies. *T. Brightwater, P.E.* You drew the lines."

"I certified a route," he said quietly. "A corridor survey. I never—" He stopped. She watched him reach for the excuse the way a drowning man reaches for a floating branch, and she watched him find it, and she watched him know, even as his fingers closed around it, that it wouldn't hold his weight.

"Three villages," Kari said. "Dawara. Sese. And mine." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it; she was done being careful with him. "You want to know what your corridor looked like from the ground? It looked like graves. They moved us with paper and policemen. No compensation ever came—there was no one left to pay it to, on paper. The land was transferred to a holding company before we were even off it. My father stayed to argue the title and—" She made herself stop, breathe. "The river took the rest during the last flood season, because the new embankments you people built pushed the water onto us. I pulled my mother's body out of a sago swamp in March."

Theo said nothing. The lantern hissed. Outside, the rain kept up its work of unmaking the world.

"I didn't know," he said at last, and it came out small, and then, hearing it, he flinched at his own words like a man who'd stepped on a snake. "No. That's not—that's the whole disease of it, isn't it. I knew *something*. I knew the closure orders came down wrong. I knew the grades I surveyed didn't match the alignment on the final drawings. I told myself it was clerical. I told myself a lot of things." He put his head back against the stone. "I ran because stopping meant reading the documents properly. And if I read them properly, then I wasn't innocent anymore, and I'd spent eleven years being innocent. It was the only thing I had left."

Kari stared at him. Fury was still in her, hot and living, but underneath it something else stirred—recognition, unwelcome as a stranger's face across a fire. She knew what it was to keep moving so you never had to look at what you'd lost. She'd been doing it since March.

"Tomasi read them," she said. "The documents. He was the survey team's clerk—he copied everything, the transfer deeds, the payment schedules, the names. He said the same names kept appearing. Company men. Government men. Men who eat at the same table." She looked at him hard. "One of the names was Brightwater."

"That's why you came aboard." Not a question. "Not passage knowledge. You wanted to see what I was."

"I wanted to see if you were worth drowning for," she said. "I still don't know."

He laughed—a short, broken sound with no humor in it—and then pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "The supervisor. Marlowe. He was on the trestle with a rifle, Kari. He wasn't chasing surveyors. He was chasing *paper*, and the paper is the only thing in this country that can hang him, and I helped build the thing he's hiding." His hands dropped. "Every mile of that closed line is a lie I laid out with my own instruments."

"No," Kari said. "Every mile of that line is evidence *you* can read better than anyone alive. That's why Tomasi sent for an engineer. That's why the map mattered." She leaned forward, and the lantern threw her shadow huge against the cave wall, arms spread like wings. "You think running made you clean? Nothing makes you clean. But some things make you *useful*. Right now, tonight, in this hole in the rock, you are the most useful man in the province, and Marlowe knows it, and that's why he'll come through this storm after us."

The water pulsed again at the cave mouth—higher. She saw Theo register it, the engineer in him measuring rise per hour behind his eyes even as the rest of him sat gutted open.

"How long do we have?" he asked.

"If the rain holds like this? The shelf floods by midmorning. Maybe sooner." She glanced at the boat, riding its rope between the rocks, taking green water over the bow with every swell. "And the second surveyor is a day upstream. Wounded. Alone. In this."

Theo was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed—flatter, harder, the guilt burned off it like slag tapped from a furnace, leaving something denser underneath.

"Then we go at first light. Storm or no storm."

"It's not a question of storm," Kari said. "It's a question of whether the river lets us."

She lay back against the stone and did not sleep. Beside her, Theo sat awake with the lantern turned low, and once, thinking she couldn't hear, he said her brother's name to himself—*Tomasi*—the way a man tests a knot he means to trust his whole weight to.

Outside, the monsoon walked on the land, and the river rose, and rose, and rose.

* * *

An hour before dawn, the water found them.

It came not gradually but all at once—a single great surge that lifted the boat, snapped the mooring line, and drove the hull against the cave wall with a crack Kari felt in her teeth. She was on her feet and into the water before she'd decided to move, catching the bow, Theo beside her hauling on the painter stub, both of them fighting to drag the boat onto the shelf as the cave floor vanished beneath brown flood.

They got it half up. Enough. The lantern drowned and died, and in the dark they crouched on the last dry stone with the boat grinding beneath their hands and the river breathing at them like something enormous asleep.

"We can't stay," Theo shouted over the roar. "Another hour and the shelf goes under!"

"So we leave now, in the dark, in flood stage!" Kari shouted back. "Or we stay and drown politely!"

He laughed again—that same broken sound—but this time there was something under it, something that might, in another life, have been joy. He found her hand in the dark and gripped it once, hard, engineer to guide, and let go.

"Then we run the river," he said. "Together."

Kari swung herself over the gunwale into black, boiling water, and reached for the oars, and did not tell him that the stretch ahead—the Narrows, where her brother's boat had been found empty—was the one place on the Upper Fly that even she feared.
