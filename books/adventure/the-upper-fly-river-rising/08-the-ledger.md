## 8. The Ledger

The cave mouth opened onto a world gone white with rain.

Theo had stopped thinking of it as weather somewhere in the night. This was not weather; this was the sky coming down to reclaim the land, a solid roaring curtain that turned the river below them into something brown and muscular, hauling whole trees past at the speed of a galloping horse. He stood at the lip of the overhang and watched the water climb the banks in visible increments, each minute adding another hand's width of flood, and he thought: *the surveyor is up there in this.*

Kari was behind him, wrapping her brother's compass in oilcloth with hands that did not shake, though her jaw was set like poured concrete. She hadn't slept. Neither of them had. The night of bailing water out of the boat, then the scramble up the scree to the cave when the engine finally drowned for good — it had left them wrung out and hollow-eyed, and still she moved like a woman with a schedule written on the inside of her eyelids.

"He'll be at the old cut," she said, without looking up. "Wesley told me once — if anything goes wrong, go to ground at the old cut. The rail cutting above the second falls. High enough to stay dry."

"High enough," Theo said, "if we can reach it."

The route Wesley's memory had given them ran along the shoulder of the valley, a contour path the surveyors themselves had cut, and then up through the abandoned grade where the closed line had once been graded and ballasted and then, on paper, erased. Theo knew what that erasure meant now. He'd known since the trestle, since he'd seen the fresh-cut timber and new spikes gleaming under the rust on a line that was supposed to be dead. Someone was building a railroad to nowhere on official maps, and three villages were the nowhere it was built through.

His firm's name was on the papers. His supervisor's signature. And his own hands had carried the original survey drafts into that office, never asking why the grades didn't match the terrain.

*You didn't ask,* said the voice that had ridden with him from Port Moresby. *That's not innocence. That's just a slower way of saying yes.*

He shouldered the pack. Kari doused the fire they'd risked, and they went out into the rain.

* * *

The old cut announced itself before they saw it — a change in the sound of the water, the river's roar doubling back off rock instead of scattering down-valley. Then the trees thinned and there it was: a corridor sliced through the hillside forty years ago by men who believed in the future, its floor now a streambed, its walls dripping curtains of green. Halfway along, wedged into a recess in the rock like a swallow's nest, was a lean-to of survey stakes and torn fly sheeting.

And beside it, propped against the stone, a man.

Theo saw him from thirty yards and knew already. There was a way a body arranges itself when it has stopped expecting rescue — a finality to the posture, the head tipped back against the rock, the hands open on the thighs. Not resting. Finished.

"Wesley." Kari was past him before he could speak, sliding down the last stretch of grade on her heels, mud to her knees.

The man's eyes found her with difficulty, tracking like a compass needle near iron. He was young — younger than Theo had expected, younger than the beard and the gray lips suggested — and his survey shirt had been washed clean in patches around wounds that had not been cleaned at all. A rifle lay across his lap with the bolt removed, and the bolt lay in his palm, held out slightly, an offering or a warning.

"Kari." The name came out cracked, half-swallowed. "You shouldn't— the road. They're watching the road."

"I know." She knelt, took his hand, folded the bolt back into his fingers. "I'm here now."

Theo crouched at the edge of the lean-to and kept his eyes moving — down the cut, up the walls, back along their own trail where the rain was already erasing them, which was mercy and menace both. Water sheeted off the lean-to's broken edge and pooled around the man's legs. He was shivering in a way that had nothing to do with cold.

"You're Brightwater." It wasn't a question. "The engineer. She said—" A cough bent him double, and when he straightened there was blood on his teeth and something bright and terrible in his eyes, the lucidity of the nearly gone. "She said you'd come. I told her you would. An engineer can read a line. Nobody else could follow it the way we laid it."

"Laid it how?" Theo asked, though he was beginning to understand.

"The closure order." Wesley fumbled at his breast pocket, and Kari helped him, peeling back wet cloth. "The official one. It routes the line east — around the plateau. But we surveyed the real alignment. Through the valley. Through the villages." His breath hitched. "The company filed the eastern route for compensation purposes. Clean map, no villages, no claims. Then they build the real line through everything the map says isn't there. The land costs them nothing because officially no one lives on it." He looked at Theo, and there was no accusation in it, which was worse. "Your firm drew both maps. You carried the drafts."

Theo's throat worked. "I didn't know."

"No." Wesley almost smiled. "Nobody knows. That's the design of it. That's why they needed us erased — not because we found the fraud. Because we *surveyed the truth*, and a true survey is evidence that walks around on two legs." He pressed his hand flat against his pocket. "It's all here. Every station, every chainage, every village boundary overlaid on the real alignment. Signatures. Dates. The compensation schedule that pays for land on a line that will never be built, while the real line takes the villages for free. It's arithmetic, Brightwater. Ugly, simple arithmetic, and it doesn't work unless people are subtracted."

Thunder rolled down the valley and the river answered, deeper now, hungrier. Theo glanced upstream and felt his stomach drop — the flood had taken the low bank while they talked. The path they'd come by was gone.

"The ledger," Kari said. "Give it to me, Wes. We'll take it downriver. Magistrate Okafor at Wabo — he's honest, Papa vouched for him years ago—"

"No." Wesley's hand closed over hers, and the strength in it surprised them both, a last reserve spent all at once. "Not you. Listen to me. They let me live four days because I was bait. They wanted whoever came for me. They've been following *you* since the trestle — the tall one, the one who limps, he wants the engineer alive." His eyes went to Theo. "He knows your name. He said it like he was tasting it. *Brightwater.* Like an old debt."

Cold walked down Theo's spine and settled in his gut. Halloran. It had to be. The man whose signature sat beneath his own on every draft he'd ever carried, come upriver personally to close accounts.

"The ledger goes with him." Wesley pushed himself forward off the rock, and Kari caught him, lowered him gently. "Because he's the only one who can get it through. Because they'll chase a boat, but they won't expect—" Another cough, longer this time, and when it passed he was quieter, smaller. "Kari. Look at me."

She looked at him. Rain drummed the fly sheet. Somewhere above, a slope of sodden earth let go with a long wet sigh and slid into the flood.

"Mama named me after the river," Wesley said. "Did you know that? She said rivers always find the sea. Whatever road you take them down—" His hand found the ledger inside his shirt and drew it out, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with survey twine, heavier than any object Theo had ever seen. "Take the sea road. Find the sea."

He put it in Theo's hands.

For a moment nobody moved. The rain filled the silence with static. Then Wesley smiled at his sister, a real smile, brief as lightning, and said, "Tell Mama the river found the sea," and closed his eyes, and did not open them again.

Kari made a sound Theo had never heard from her — small, stripped, animal — and bent over her brother, and Theo turned away to give her the dignity of the moment and stood in the rain with the ledger against his chest and understood, finally and completely, what he was carrying. Not paper. People. Three villages' worth of names and boundaries and claims, the whole weight of the erased, bound in oilcloth and survey twine.

When he turned back, Kari had closed Wesley's eyes and folded his hands over the empty bolt. She was humming something, barely audible under the rain — a mourning song, three notes rising and falling like water finding its level.

"We have to go," Theo said softly. "I'm sorry. We have to go now."

She rose. Her face was wrecked and calm, the way a flooded street is calm. "I know," she said. "The high bank. Before it goes too."

* * *

They lost the high bank anyway.

It went twenty minutes later, a whole terrace of it sloughing into the brown torrent while they watched from the next rise, and after that there was only the ridge, and the ridge funneled them down toward the river whether they liked it or not, because the alternative was the company's men, and Theo had seen movement on the backtrail twice — figures in oilskins, patient as leeches, letting the flood do the driving.

The boat was where they'd left it, half-swamped, wedged between two tree trunks that the river had deposited like receipts. Theo bailed her out with the hat off his head while Kari rigged the tarp over the stern, and when he looked up she was swaying on her feet, one hand pressed to her side.

"What is it?" He was beside her in two steps. "Kari. What happened?"

"Trestle," she said. "When we ran. I hit a beam. It's nothing."

It was not nothing. When he pulled her hand aside, the flesh below her ribs was darkening, and when he touched it she went white and bit down on a cry. Cracked rib, maybe worse, and she'd climbed a ridge and hauled a boat and buried her brother on it without a word.

"You should have said."

"And you'd have done what?" Her eyes came up, furious and clear. "Turned back? Left Wes alone? No. It changes nothing. Get us downriver."

But it changed everything, and Theo stood in the rising water holding a ledger and a choice, and the choice had teeth.

Here was the arithmetic Wesley had taught him, ugly and simple: downstream, the river ran fast and straight through the gorge, and a light boat with one strong oarsman might beat the pursuers to Wabo. Alone. Him and the proof, lashed to his chest, running like he had run from everything his whole life — quick, clean, survivable. Kari couldn't row with those ribs. Couldn't even sit upright without help. Carrying her meant a slower line, the eddies, the portage at the falls, hours added to a lead measured in minutes.

Alone, the ledger probably lived. Together, maybe neither did.

*They're coming,* said the rain. *Choose.*

Theo looked at the ledger in his hands, and then at Kari, who was already dragging the boat free of the driftwood, already refusing, with her whole body, to be a variable in anyone's calculation. And he thought about the man he had been on the train out of Port Moresby, the man who kept moving because stopping meant answering, the man who had signed nothing and therefore done nothing and therefore been no one.

He lashed the ledger to his chest, tight, under his arms, knot after knot until the twine bit.

Then he took Kari's arm and put it over his shoulders, and took her weight, all of it, without flinching.

"Together," he said. "We're slower together. So we'd better be smarter. There's a maintenance track on the west bank past the falls — it shows on my map and not on theirs. We walk it tonight, paddle the slack water at dawn, and we arrive at Wabo with the ledger and with you, or we don't arrive at all."

Her breath came ragged against his neck. "Brightwater. If they catch us—"

"They won't." And for the first time in six years, he found that he meant a promise. "I built lines, Kari. I know where the ground gives way and where it holds. Trust the engineer."

Downstream, around the bend, an oilskin figure stepped out onto the shattered remains of the old landing, and raised one arm, and behind him in the rain others gathered like crows on a fence.

Halloran had found the river.

Theo lifted Kari fully into his arms, turned his back on them, and started walking into the storm.
