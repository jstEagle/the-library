## 9. High Water

*Theo*

The bridge announced itself before they saw it — a groan rolling down the valley like something enormous clearing its throat.

Theo heard it through the rain, through the roar of the river that had swallowed its banks and was now eating the hillsides in slow, greedy bites. He had been carrying Kari for six hours. Her leg was worse; she'd stopped pretending otherwise an hour ago, her arm locked around his neck, her breath coming in short controlled pulls against his ear. The ledger rode against his chest inside the oilskin wrap, lashed tight with the same cord he'd used to secure cargo on a hundred flatcars in another life. Every jolt of the trail pressed it against his sternum like a second heartbeat.

Then the trees broke open and there it was: the span.

It rose out of the flood mist like the ribcage of some drowned leviathan — iron trusses furred with rust and vine, deck planks gone to rot, one whole section sagging toward the water as though the river had already begun pulling it down by the ankle. Theo knew it at once. He'd known it for years without knowing it, because this was one of the closed lines, one of the grades his firm had struck from every official map, and now he understood why they'd wanted it forgotten. A bridge like this, left unmaintained over a river like this, wasn't infrastructure. It was a confession waiting to be read.

"Theo." Kari's voice was thin but level. "The far side. Look."

He looked. Through the sheets of rain, on the eastern abutment, movement — four figures, maybe five, dark against the grey water, working their way along the old rail grade toward the bridgehead. His old supervisor would be among them. Brightwater could picture him perfectly: that unhurried walk, the way he checked his watch even in a downpour, as if time itself were on the company's payroll.

They were cut off. Behind them, the pursuers; ahead of them, two hundred feet of dying iron over a river running brown and furious, carrying whole trees in its grip like a man carrying kindling.

"We can't go around," Kari said. It wasn't a question. She'd seen the same thing he had — the grade funneled here, the cliffs closing in, the water high enough to drown any path along the banks.

"No," Theo said. "We can't go around."

His mind did what it always did when everything else failed. It went quiet and technical. He stood in the rain holding a wounded woman and felt the engineer come back into his hands like an old tool finding its familiar groove. The span was a Warren truss, probably eighty years old, built for loads that no longer ran. The middle section had already failed partially — he could see the buckled lower chord, see where the deck dipped. The upstream pier was taking the full force of the flood, scouring out its footing with every hour.

The bridge was going to fall. The only question was whether it fell *now*, under his hand, or later, after men with rifles had walked across it.

"Kari." He turned so she could see his face. Rain ran off his chin. "I need you to listen very carefully, because I'm only going to say this once and then we're both going to be too busy to talk."

She looked at him. Even soaked, even grey with pain, her eyes had that flinty patience he'd come to rely on more than he'd ever admitted.

"The deck will hold us if we move fast and stay on the main chords — the load path runs through the top members here, so we keep to the centerline and we don't stop. When we reach the far tower, I'm going to drop the midspan behind us."

Her eyes went to the buckled section, then back to him. "You're going to bring it down while we're still on it."

"I'm going to bring it down *after* we're past the failure point. There's a difference, and the difference is about forty feet and about four seconds."

"And if your arithmetic is wrong?"

"Then we find out together." He shifted her weight higher on his back. "Hold on to me and not to the bridge. The bridge is a liar. I'm not."

Something moved in her face — not quite a smile, but its cousin. "My brother said engineers were arrogant."

"Your brother was right." Theo's throat tightened around the name he didn't yet know how to carry. "Let's go."

* * *

*Kari*

The bridge sang under them.

That was the word for it — sang, a low iron moan that rose through the planks and into her palms where she gripped Theo's shoulders, a sound like the whole structure was mourning itself in advance. Below, the river had become a living thing, brown and muscular, hauling entire trees sideways, and every time she looked down the world tilted and her leg screamed and she made herself look up instead. Up was Theo's neck, wet and steady. Up was forward.

Halfway across, the wind came off the gorge in a fist and the whole span swayed — a long, sickening lean, the cables overhead thrashing — and Kari heard herself make a sound she hadn't made since she was a child. Theo didn't stop. He didn't speak either. He just walked, each step placed with a precision that was almost ceremonial, and she realized he was counting. His lips were moving. He was counting his own footsteps, measuring the bridge with his body the way other men measured it with chains and instruments.

At the buckled section the deck dipped and the planks beneath his boots gave a crack like a rifle shot. One plank split and dropped away, spinning down into the flood, gone between one heartbeat and the next. Kari pressed her face against Theo's shoulder and thought of her brother walking this same iron in the dry season, ledger buttoned inside his shirt, believing maps were honest things. Believing the government was a real thing and not just a word men in company colors hid behind.

"I've got you," Theo said. It was the first thing he'd said since the bridgehead. His voice didn't shake. "Twenty more steps. Then you get off my back and hold the tower, and I do the last part alone."

"You said four seconds."

"I lied about the four seconds. It's going to be closer to ten, and I need you somewhere safe while I count them."

She wanted to argue. She had been arguing with men her whole life — arguing them out of her village, arguing them out of their lies, arguing her brother out of going on that survey — and she knew the taste of an argument she couldn't win. This was one. Not because he was right, but because his arms were shaking and he was still walking, and there are arguments you lose to stubbornness and there are arguments you lose to love, and she could no longer tell which kind this was.

The far tower rose up through the rain. Theo took the last steps onto the solid abutment stone and set her down against the iron column, and for one moment his hands stayed on her shoulders, gripping hard, as if she were the only fixed point left in the world.

"Whatever happens," he said, "don't come back onto the span."

Then he turned and walked back out onto the bridge, toward the falling part, and Kari screamed his name into the storm and he did not turn around.

* * *

*Theo*

The midsection was worse up close. Up close it wasn't engineering anymore; it was anatomy — a body with broken bones, held together by habit. Theo knelt at the edge of the sound deck and put his palm flat against the lower chord, feeling the vibration of the flood come up through the steel like a pulse. The failure point was exactly where he'd read it from the bank: the buckled panel, its rivets weeping rust, its geometry already surrendered. All it needed was permission.

He had brought the tools without deciding to. The pry bar from their lost supplies, salvaged from the wreckage at the trestle, had ridden in his belt all day like a promise. Now he worked it into the joint at the base of the critical diagonal member, and the metal gave a shriek that the wind tore away and scattered downstream.

Behind him — across the span, small in the rain — the first of the company's men stepped onto the bridge.

Theo's heart did something complicated. Fear, yes, but underneath it a cold clean anger he hadn't felt in years, the anger of a man who finally understands that running was never the same thing as escaping. He thought of his supervisor's voice in the Port Moresby office, five years ago, explaining the rerouted lines with that patient smile: *Some land is better left unmapped, Brightwater. Maps make promises.* He thought of three villages that existed now only in a ledger lashed against his chest.

He leaned his weight into the bar.

The joint fought him. Rust had welded it into a single stubborn mass, and for three long seconds nothing happened except the screaming of iron and the burning in his shoulders and the flood rising below, and then — with a sensation like a great animal deciding — the member let go.

The sound was enormous. The diagonal member folded inward, the buckled chord followed, and the midspan dropped — not all at once but in stages, a terrible accordion collapse, deck planks cartwheeling, truss members twisting as they fell, the whole hundred-foot section folding down into the river with a detonation of white water. The shock ran through the remaining span like a whipcrack. Theo threw himself backward off his knees as the deck heaved under him, the far end of the surviving section kicking upward, and he slid ten feet on his back across wet planks until the abutment caught him.

When he got his breath back, he looked up.

The bridge ended twenty feet in front of him in a ragged mouth of torn iron. Beyond it, air. Beyond that, the far tower, the abutment, Kari — visible through the rain, clinging to the column, her mouth open in a shape that might have been his name.

And beyond *her*, on the far bank, the company's men stood at their own bridgehead, watching their route die in the flood. One of them raised a rifle. The shot cracked past Theo's ear and went out over the water, and then the supervisor's hand came down on the barrel, pushing it low, and even at this distance Theo could read the gesture: *No. Let the river have him.*

Theo got to his feet. His legs were shaking. His hands were shaking. Everything about him was shaking except the place behind his sternum where the ledger sat, dry and intact, and he found that he was laughing — a short, wild, disbelieving sound torn out of him by the wind.

"Hey!" he shouted across the gap, across the ruin of the span, and his voice cracked and he shouted again, louder, with everything he had. "You want to know what I kept? Ask me what I kept!"

The supervisor's figure went very still.

Theo beat his fist once against the oilskin wrap over his heart, then turned his back on them — turned his back, deliberately, the way a man closes a door — and limped to the tower where Kari waited.

She was crying and hitting him at the same time, fists landing soft against his chest, and he caught her wrists and held on.

"You counted wrong," she said.

"Ten seconds," he said. "I told you it'd be close."

Below them the river carried the bridge away in pieces, and above them the monsoon kept falling, and somewhere downriver — a day's travel, maybe less, if the flood allowed — there was a magistrate who needed to read what Theo had carried across a dying country lashed to his chest.

He lifted Kari onto his back again, settled her weight, and started walking.

The way out was gone. That was the truth of it, and he felt it settle over him not as despair but as something almost like relief: there was no line left to run along. Whatever came next, they would have to make it themselves.

Ahead, through the rain, the old grade climbed toward the ridge — and on the ridge, faint but unmistakable, a thread of woodsmoke rose against the grey.
