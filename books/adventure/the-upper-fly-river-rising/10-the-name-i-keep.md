## 10. The Name I Keep

The storm broke on the fourth morning after the bridge, the way a fever breaks—suddenly, and leaving everything behind it changed.

Kari stood at the rail of the government launch as it worked its way down the last reach of the Fly, watching the river run brown and swollen but no longer angry. Debris still came down from the highlands: whole trees, a roof torn from some mission station, a canoe paddled by no one. The water had taken what it wanted. Now it was only water again, going about its ancient business, and she found she could look at it without her chest tightening.

Behind her, under an awning rigged over the stern, Theo slept. He slept the way men sleep who have not slept properly in weeks—all at once, completely, one arm flung out as if reaching for something even in dreams. The ledger was gone from him now, surrendered two days ago to the magistrate at D'Albertis Junction, but Kari noticed his hand still curled sometimes around the shape of it, as if his body hadn't yet learned that he could let go.

She had carried that ledger herself for part of the journey downriver. She knew its weight—not just the paper weight, though it was heavy enough, water-stained and swollen, its covers warped from the flood—but the other kind. Her brother's handwriting was in it. Ano's careful figures, columns of measurements he'd never trusted anyone else to take, notes in the margins in his small precise script: *chainage disputed — re-survey required — this line does not match filed plans.* He had known. He had known what the company was doing, and he had written it down anyway, knowing what it would cost him, because that was who Ano was. The steady one. The one who stayed.

She looked at Theo sleeping and thought: *and that is who you are becoming.*

* * *

The magistrate's office at D'Albertis Junction was a low building of weathered timber with a veranda sagging at one corner, and it smelled of mildew, ink, and the particular staleness of rooms where important decisions are made slowly. Kari had expected nothing better. What surprised her was the magistrate himself—a thin, grey man named Okuk with reading glasses mended with wire, who turned the ledger's pages for four hours without once looking up, while constables stood in the doorway and rain dripped off the eaves into tin buckets.

Theo sat across the desk the entire time, hands flat on his knees, answering questions in a voice gone quiet and level. Kari watched him give his name—his real name, the one from before, the engineer's license number, the firm, all of it—and watched him do it without flinching. That was the thing she kept returning to, afterward. Not the courage on the bridge, dropping the span behind them with the river roaring through the gap like something unleashed from a cage. Anyone might find that kind of courage; it comes fast and burns hot and is over quickly. But this—sitting still, saying *I am the man who did this, I signed those drawings, I rerouted that survey line because my supervisor told me to and because I wanted to keep my position*—this was the slower courage, the kind that has to be chosen every morning.

When Okuk finally closed the ledger, he took off his wire-mended glasses and looked at them both for a long moment.

"The company's men were taken at Wabo yesterday," he said. "Six of them, including your Mr. Voss." He said it to Theo, and Kari saw the old supervisor's name land on Theo like a physical weight—the man who had taught him, praised him, used him, and hunted him down a flooded river with rifles. "They will be tried here. There will be convictions." A pause, and the grey eyes grew tired. "The men above them—the directors, the minister who signed the grant—I cannot touch. Not yet. Paper travels slowly upward, and the people it must reach have every reason to read it slowly."

"I know," Theo said.

"You know." Okuk almost smiled. "You are learning how the country works."

"It's why I stopped running," Theo said. "Running only works if you don't care where you end up."

Kari looked away then, out the window, because there was a tightness in her throat she didn't want either of them to see. Outside, the sun had come properly out for the first time in days, and steam was rising off the veranda boards, and somewhere a child was laughing at something near the landing stage. The world, indifferent and continuing.

Three of the company's local laborers were released that same afternoon—men who had been paid to burn the survey camp's records and say nothing, and who had talked within a day of their arrest. One of them, a boy hardly older than Ano had been, wept openly when they brought him past the window. Kari did not feel triumph. She felt the way the river looked: full, heavy, carrying everything downstream toward some sea where accounts might or might not be settled.

But it was a beginning. She held onto that. It was a beginning, written in her brother's hand.

* * *

They buried Ano's effects—or rather, Kari did, and Theo stood beside her—at the mission cemetery above the junction, where the ground was high enough that even the flood had not reached the stones. There was no body. There never would be. She buried his field notebook instead, the second one, the personal one, with its sketches of birds and its lists of songs their mother used to sing, wrapped in oilcloth against the wet earth.

Theo spoke at the grave. Kari hadn't asked him to. He simply stepped forward when the moment came, cleared his throat, and said, in his careful, accented way:

"He was the best surveyor I never met. His lines are true. Every measurement in that ledger is true, and because of that, three villages are going to be on the map again. That's—" His voice caught, and he steadied it the way he'd steadied himself on the bridge, jaw set, eyes fixed on some point beyond the present trouble. "That's more than most of us manage. More than I managed, for a long time."

Afterward, walking back down through the mission garden, he said quietly, "I keep thinking about the day I signed the reroute order. It was a Tuesday. I remember because it was raining and I remember thinking, *just paper, just a line on a map.*" He shook his head. "A line on a map is never just a line. It's houses. Gardens. Graves. I drew three villages out of existence with a ruler and didn't lose a night's sleep over it."

"And now?" Kari asked.

"Now I don't sleep much at all," he said, and there was something in it that was almost humor, and she laughed despite herself, despite the graveyard behind them, and he smiled—that crooked, unpracticed smile that was getting less crooked the longer he stayed in one place.

* * *

He stayed.

That was the thing Kari kept turning over in the weeks that followed, the way you turn a stone in your hand looking for the flaw in it. He stayed. When the launch went back downriver to Port Moresby, he stood on the bank and watched it go, and Kari saw the pull of it in his face—the old restlessness, the itch of the horizon, the belief that the next place would be the place where the past couldn't follow. She'd seen it in a hundred drifters who passed upriver and kept passing. She waited for him to name a destination.

Instead he turned to her and said, "Okuk says the villages need a proper resurvey before the land titles can be restored. Official lines, filed plans, the whole machinery." He rubbed the back of his neck, embarrassed. "It needs an engineer who isn't afraid of the company finding out, because the company already knows everything there is to know about me. And it needs someone who can pay me, which is nobody, so I suppose I'll be working for rice and stories."

"There are worse wages," Kari said.

"I've had them," he said. "Mostly."

So he stayed, in a house lent by the mission at Kavaira—one of the three villages, hers, the one whose gardens the company's map had erased—and began the slow work of putting her people back onto the face of the earth. Kari watched him do it, and understood that she was watching a man rebuild himself along the same lines.

He started with the boundaries. He walked them with the old men of the village, pacing out distances in the early cool of the morning, marking trees with blazes that would be measured and recorded and filed where no company clerk could ever redraw them. He learned the names of every creek in their language and wrote them into his field book exactly as they were given to him, refusing the district officer's suggestion that they be anglicized for convenience. "A name is a title deed," she heard him tell the man. "You don't get to simplify what you're stealing."

He rebuilt the transit stand he'd salvaged from the drowned survey camp, machining a new pivot from scrap brass with tools borrowed from the mission workshop, working late by lamplight with his sleeves rolled up and a smudge of grease permanently on his left cheekbone. He argued with the district officer about chainage standards until the man gave up and conceded the point. He taught two village boys to read a staff and level, patiently, over and over, the way Ano must once have been taught.

And every evening, when the light went gold and then violet over the river, he would sit on the step of the mission house with tea, and Kari would come and sit beside him, and neither of them would say very much. It was enough. The silence between them had changed since the cave, since the night of bailing water and truth-telling. It was no longer the silence of two people concealing things. It was the silence of two people who had seen each other clearly and found the sight survivable.

One evening, late in the dry season's beginning, she asked him about the map. The old one, the stolen one, the closed lines that had started everything.

"Burned it," he said. "Night before we reached the trestle. Couldn't sleep, so I got up and fed it to the stove page by page." He looked at his hands. "You know what's strange? It was the first time since I took it that I felt clean. All those years I told myself I'd stolen it because I needed proof, insurance, leverage. Truth is I stole it because I couldn't bear to be the man who'd drawn those lines and have nothing left to show for it. The map was my confession, and I carried it everywhere hoping someone would arrest me for it." He glanced at her sideways. "Nobody ever did. So I confessed myself another way, I suppose. Down a river, at the wrong end of a rifle, on a bridge in a flood."

"Confession accepted," Kari said.

He laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him—and then went quiet, looking down at the village below: the smoke of cooking fires, children running along the levee path, the new blaze-marks bright on the boundary trees. Somewhere a drum was starting up for a dance that would go long into the night.

"They're going to ask me to stay permanently," he said. "Okuk mentioned it. Chief surveyor for the restoration, if the titles come through. A salary. A house. A name on a government roll." He turned the cup in his hands. "I haven't said yes."

"Why not?"

"Because every time I've ever had a name worth keeping, I've done something to lose it." He looked at her then, fully, and there was no deflection in it, no engineer's careful distance. "I'm afraid of being trusted, Kari. That's the honest, ugly bottom of it. I've spent six years being exactly as reliable as a leaking boat."

Kari considered this for a while, the way she'd consider a stretch of water before taking a boat through it.

"When my brother was small," she said at last, "he was afraid of deep water. Our father took him out to the middle of the river and made him swim, and Ano cried the whole way, and our father said: *the fear doesn't leave. You just learn to carry it and swim anyway.*" She stood, brushing off her skirt. "You dropped a bridge into a flood to save a woman you'd known three weeks. You're swimming, Theo Brightwater. The fear is just coming along for the ride."

She walked down toward the drums, and did not look back, because she didn't need to. Some things you know are following you, and some things you know are staying, and she had learned the difference on a river that had tried very hard to teach her.

Behind her, after a long moment, she heard the mission-house door open, and footsteps come down the steps, unhurried, certain of the ground.

In the morning there would be chains to check and boundary trees to blaze and a letter to write to the magistrate, and the powerful men far away would still be powerful, and the fight would go on for years. But tonight there were drums, and a village back on the map, and a man walking toward the firelight who had finally stopped running—who had, at the end of all that brown water, chosen a name and decided to keep it.

Kari smiled, and went down to dance.
