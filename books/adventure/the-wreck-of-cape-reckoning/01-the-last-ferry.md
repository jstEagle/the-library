## 1. The Last Ferry

The river woke before he did.

Theo Vane had learned to sleep in the shallow hours, the way a man learns to sleep in any bed that isn't his own—lightly, with one ear turned toward the world. So when the first light came down the Reckoning Valley, gray and cold and smelling of wet stone, he was already awake inside his sleeping bag, listening to the water work at the shingle bar where he'd beached the boat. The river talked all night. It never said anything new.

He crawled out into air that bit through his flannel shirt and stood on the gravel with his hands jammed under his arms, watching the mist lift off the current in long slow ribbons. Upstream, the valley narrowed toward mountains that hadn't decided yet whether they were blue or black. Downstream, behind him, lay Salt Landing—six hundred people, two bars, one church, and a fuel dock that opened at seven because its owner believed God kept regular hours.

His name here was Tom Marsh. It was written on the manifest clipped to the boat's wheel: T. MARSH, FREIGHT CONTRACT, RECKONING RIVER SURVEY SUPPLY RUN, 14 DAYS. He'd been Tom Marsh for eleven months now, which was longer than he'd been some of his other names. There had been a stretch, three years back, when he'd gone through identities the way other men went through socks. Daniel Reyes in one country. A man called only Anders in another, up north, where the winters were honest about what they intended to do to you. Names were cheap if you didn't get attached. That was the whole trick of exile, Theo had learned: never let anything cost you too much. Not a name, not a town, not a face you passed on the street twice.

He'd broken that rule once or twice anyway. He tried not to think about it before breakfast.

The boat was a converted survey skiff, twenty-two feet of riveted aluminum with a mud-brown paint job and an outboard that ran rough until it warmed, then ran like something forgiven. He'd bought it from a fisherman who was leaving the country for reasons he hadn't shared and Theo hadn't asked. It handled heavy loads well. It drew eighteen inches empty, maybe two feet loaded, and it could read the Reckoning's moods better than most men could—which mattered, because the Reckoning's moods were the whole employment problem. The river dropped out of the high country in a series of braided channels and riffle bars that shifted every spring flood, and the charts were five years stale, and the men who knew the water by eye could count themselves on one hand.

Theo counted himself among them without false modesty. Reading water was the last thing anyone had ever paid him for honestly.

He loaded by headlamp while the mist still hung. Crates of diesel for the survey camp's generator, boxed in slats and stenciled FUEL—HANDLE WITH CARE by someone who had never met a river. Rice and flour in fifty-kilo sacks stacked on pallets. A crate of radio batteries. Two cases of tinned peaches, which struck him as extravagant for a geological survey, until he remembered that geologists were people too, more or less. Rope coils. A drum of chain lubricant. Everything lashed down with trucker's hitches he tied without looking, his hands moving through the old patterns while his mind wandered off ahead of him, the way it did, checking the day's water against the season's.

Late September. The dry season's tail end. The glacier-fed reaches would be running low and clear, which made the bars visible but the shallows treacherous—you could see exactly where you were going to hit. The lower gorge would still hold its permanent bad temper. And somewhere above all of it, past the last navigable reach, the survey camp sat on a bench of gravel at the mouth of the Corrie Fork, nine people who had come to this valley to look at rocks, and who depended on a man named Tom Marsh to bring them their peaches.

Theo cast off at first proper light, poling off the bar with the boat hook, dropping the current take the bow, and swinging downstream into the cold running dark of the early channel. The outboard caught on the second pull. He throttled up just enough to make way and settled behind the wheel with his collar up and his cap low, and for a while there was nothing in the world but the sound of the engine and the river and the gray light coming up over the eastern ridge like something being slowly remembered.

* * *

He made the lower riffles by feel as much as sight, standing at the wheel with his weight forward, reading the water the way other men read faces. There was a language to it. A smooth green tongue of current meant depth; a line of standing waves meant a bar underneath, growing or shrinking depending on how the water curled around it; a boil on the surface meant a boulder doing its patient work below. The Reckoning spoke it fluently, and it lied sometimes, and Theo liked that about it. A river that lied kept you honest.

By midmorning the sun had cleared the ridgeline and the valley had turned gold and green, cottonwoods along the banks shedding their first yellow leaves onto the current, the water carrying them downstream in little flotillas. He passed a heron standing in the shallows with the stillness of a thing that had never once doubted itself. He passed an abandoned dredge, half-sunk, its bucket line rusted into a sculpture of somebody's failed dream. He passed no people at all. That was the arrangement he'd made with this stretch of river: ninety miles of nobody.

It was on the third day, camped at the halfway eddy under a leaning spruce, that the radio check came through.

The survey team called in on schedule—every Tuesday and Friday evening, six o'clock, a routine so fixed it had become almost liturgical. Base station in Salt Landing relayed to the freight contractor, which was Theo, because the camp's own set had a cracked antenna mast and they couldn't raise anything beyond the next bend. Theo carried a handheld unit in a waterproof bag and answered when they called, mostly to confirm deliveries, occasionally to pass along weather. In four months he had exchanged perhaps forty minutes of total conversation with these people. He knew the cook's voice—warm, wry, a man named Aldous who complained about the peaches even though he ordered them. He knew the expedition leader's voice, brisk and clipped, a woman named Dr. Ferrant who said *copy* the way other people said *no*. He knew the geologist's voice only faintly—a woman, Ines Okafor, who rarely came on the net except to ask for things like acetone and more batteries, in an accent that put soft edges on hard consonants.

At six o'clock sharp, the handheld crackled.

*"Marsh, Marsh, this is Corrie Fork base, do you copy?"*

Aldous's voice, but wrong. Thin. Running fast, the way voices run when the mouth is trying to keep up with something the mind hasn't finished. Theo set down the pan of rice he'd been burning and picked up the handset.

"Corrie Fork base, this is Marsh. Copy you. Go ahead."

Static. A long wash of it, like wind through wire. Then fragments, arriving torn:

*"—say again, we have—"* Hiss. *"—not survey personnel—"* Hiss. *"—if you're hearing this, do not—"*

"Do not what?" Theo said. "Say again, Corrie Fork. You're breaking up. Say again your last."

More static, and beneath it, faintly, something else—not words, just a texture of sound that might have been shouting, might have been an engine, might have been nothing but the mountains interfering with themselves. Then Aldous's voice again, closer to the microphone now, close enough that Theo could hear breathing behind the words:

*"—they're here, they came up the—"*

And then nothing. Not static fading, not a sign-off. Nothing—the particular nothing of a carrier wave with no one left to speak into it. Ten seconds of dead air. Twenty. Theo held the handset against his ear and listened to the hiss the way he'd listen to a heartbeat, waiting for it to resume.

"Corrie Fork base, this is Marsh." His voice came out level, which surprised him. "Radio check. Over."

Nothing.

"Corrie Fork, radio check. Over."

The hiss went on, indifferent as rain. He keyed the set another dozen times over the next hour, walking away from it and coming back, telling himself each time that it was the antenna, the cracked mast, the weather front building over the divide—he'd seen squalls kill a signal for days. At dusk he raised Salt Landing's base station instead, and the operator there, a bored teenager named Petey whose uncle owned the fuel dock, confirmed he hadn't heard from Corrie Fork either. "Probably their set," Petey said. "That mast's been garbage since August. You want me to log a fault?"

"Yeah," Theo said. "Log a fault."

He ate his burned rice without tasting it and sat by the fire until it burned down to coals, looking upstream into the dark, where the valley bent away toward mountains he could not see and a camp he could not hear.

* * *

Here is what a reasonable man would have done: nothing.

Here is what the contract required: deliver supplies to the landing below the Corrie Fork trailhead, leave them under canvas, return downriver. If no contact from the client within seventy-two hours, notify the broker in Salt Landing and stand down. That was the paper of it. That was what Tom Marsh, freight contractor, a man with no history and no obligations, would do. Leave the crates on the gravel, turn the boat around, and let whatever was happening nine miles up the Corrie Fork trail happen to whoever it was happening to. Not his people. Not his name. Not his problem.

Theo lay in his bag that night and conducted the argument he'd been conducting with himself since dusk, and he knew both sides of it too well for either to win.

Because here was the other thing, the thing under the thing: he knew what silence on a mountain meant. He had professional knowledge of it, the kind that comes stamped into a man. Silence was not neutral. Silence was either equipment failure—and equipment failure was common, ordinary, the kind of thing that resolved itself by morning—or it was the space where something had happened, the negative impression left behind, the way snow holds the shape of what fell into it. He had spent years learning to tell those silences apart, and everything in his trained instincts told him this one was the second kind. You didn't lose a whole camp's voice because of a cracked mast. Nine people didn't all stop talking at once.

And underneath even that, deeper than instinct, older than training: guilt. Though he'd have shot anyone who called it that. Guilt wearing the mask of pragmatism, whispering its familiar arithmetic—*you owe the mountains a debt, Vane, you've been paying it in installments for years, here's one more payment, just due diligence, just checking the mail.* He knew the voice. He'd been listening to it so long it sounded like his own thinking.

He got up before dawn, colder than the night before, and stood on the gravel bar looking upriver. The mist was thick, and the mountains beyond it existed only as a suggestion, a darkening in the gray. Somewhere up there, at the end of a trail he'd walked a dozen times, was a camp full of strangers who had done nothing worse than order tinned peaches and trust a supply man named Tom Marsh.

"Just due diligence," he said aloud, and the river took the words and gave him back nothing.

He broke camp fast, the way he did everything—efficiently, without ceremony, a man dismantling a life in fifteen minutes flat because he'd had practice. He loaded the remaining fuel drums, the ones destined for the final delivery, and pointed the bow upstream into the current, and the Reckoning accepted him the way it accepted everything, without comment.

The run up to the Corrie Fork landing took the better part of two days. He worked the boat through the braided middle reaches where the channels split and rejoined like arguments, picking his lines by memory and by the color of the water, and both nights he stopped at the scheduled times and keyed the radio, and both nights the answer was the same beautiful, terrible nothing. By the second evening he had stopped telling himself it was the antenna.

On the afternoon of the second day he rounded the last bend before the Fork and killed the engine and let the boat drift, because there was a thing he wanted to see before the noise of him arrived. The landing came into view the way it always did—a shelf of pale gravel at the mouth of the side valley, a steep trail switchbacking up through the trees toward the bench where the camp sat, invisible from the water. He looked at the trailhead.

There were boot prints in the mud of the landing. Several sets. They came down from the trail, crossed the gravel, and ended at the water's edge—ended, all of them, facing the river, as if a number of people had stood here recently, looking at the water, deciding something. There was no boat. There was no sign that anyone had ever gone back up.

Theo drifted past the landing without putting ashore, and the current carried him around the next bend, and the camp was a mile of trail above him, and the radio in its waterproof bag said nothing at all.

He tied up to a root ball a quarter mile downstream, out of sight of the landing, and sat in the drifting evening cold with the engine ticking as it cooled, and understood, with the flat certainty of a man reading his own chart, that the life he had built on movement—the names, the rivers, the ninety miles of nobody—had just gotten thinner than it could bear. Whatever was waiting up that trail, it had his name on it. Not Tom Marsh. The other one.

He should turn the boat around. Every survival instinct he owned, every lesson of seven years of exile, said turn the boat around.

Theo Vane pulled the tarp off the supplies, checked the load on his pack frame, and started up the trail in the last of the light.
