## 2. Blacklist

The river had a voice, and Theo Vane had learned to sleep inside it.

That was the trick of the Reckoning—no other water he'd known talked so constantly, so insistently, as if it were rehearsing an argument it never meant to finish. In Salt Landing they said the river was named for a shipwreck two hundred years gone, some brig that had come apart on the coastal rocks and spilled its cargo of timber and salt pork into the current, and ever after the water had carried the memory downstream like a debt no one could pay. Theo didn't care about the name. He cared that the sound covered other sounds: his own breathing, the creak of the tarps, the small dishonest noises a man makes when he is lying to himself.

He lay in the bottom of the ferry boat with his boots off and his hands laced behind his head, watching the stars wheel over the gorge walls, and told himself—for the fourth night running—that the silence from the survey camp was nothing.

Radios failed. That was the whole truth of radios. Batteries died in the cold, antennas iced, connections corroded in the damp. He had watched a hundred thousand dollars of equipment go dark for reasons as mundane as a mouse chewing through insulation. The survey team upriver was six people with a satellite phone and a weekly check-in schedule, and they'd missed one check-in. One. Men had been shot for less in the borderlands south of here, but this wasn't the south. This was the Reckoning watershed, where the worst thing that happened to you was weather.

Except that it hadn't always been only weather. He knew that better than anyone.

He closed his eyes and the river kept talking, and underneath its voice, as it did every night at this hour, came the other sound—the one from Kestrel Face.

* * *

It began, as those things do, with a decision that seemed reasonable at the time.

March, four years ago. The Kestrel Face expedition, nine clients and three guides, ten days into a traverse of the northern massif. Theo was lead guide, thirty-one years old, at the absolute top of his form—the kind of guide who could read a snowpack the way illiterate men read faces, who had walked clients across slopes that made other guides turn back, who had a reputation in two countries for bringing everyone home. Not fast. Not flashy. Home. That was the whole of his creed, learned from his father, who had guided out of the same valley before him: *The mountain doesn't care if you're brave. It cares if you're patient.*

The storm on the eleventh day was forecast to arrive Thursday. It arrived Tuesday.

Theo remembered standing at the col below the Face at dawn, the light coming up gray and grainy, like the world seen through dirty ice, and feeling the wrongness before he could name it. Wind loading on the upper slopes all night. A temperature swing that had turned Tuesday's powder into Wednesday's slab. The snow under his boot when he probed it had a sound he knew—a dense, dead sound, the sound of a thing holding its breath.

His partner, Callum Reyes, had wanted to push through. Nine clients, most of them mid-pack skiers who'd paid a year's savings for this traverse, a weather window that would close behind them if they waited even half a day. Callum was younger, hungrier, the kind of guide whose confidence was a currency he spent freely because he'd never yet been asked to cover a withdrawal. "We beat the front by six hours," Callum said, breath steaming, eyes bright. "It's eight hundred vertical of low-angle. We've done it in worse."

"We haven't," Theo said. "And it's not low-angle anymore. It's loaded."

They stood there in the wind and argued for eleven minutes. Theo would count them later, in the inquiry, in the dark, in the years after—eleven minutes, forty seconds by his watch, though the watch had stopped working sometime during what came next and he'd never been able to look at it again without doing the arithmetic.

In the end he made the decision that ruined him, and the terrible thing, the thing he could not explain to any tribunal or priest or 3 a.m. ceiling, was that the decision itself had been defensible. He had weighed the evidence. He had dug pits, tested columns, read the layers. The data said marginal. Marginal meant go-slow, spacing out, one at a time, avoid the convex roll beneath the serac band. It did not mean turn around—not by the book, not by any book he'd been trained on. And turning around meant nine disappointed clients, a season's income, Callum's contempt, and a two-day retreat through terrain that had its own hazards.

So he went. Slowly. Carefully. By the numbers.

The avalanche released at 9:47 in the morning, from the serac band, four hundred meters above the route—a crown face two meters deep and three hundred meters wide, the kind of slide the textbooks call unsurvivable and the mountains call Tuesday. It took the middle of the rope team. Six people. Two clients dug themselves out. One client, a retired schoolteacher named Alma Brandt, did not.

Alma Brandt, sixty-three, who had climbed with Theo twice before and trusted him absolutely, who had written him a card once that said *You make me feel safe in places where I shouldn't be*, and which he had kept folded in his wallet until the paper wore soft as cloth. They found her eleven meters downslope of her skis, buried under a meter and a half of set-up debris, and the probe line found her at 10:31, and she had been alive under there for perhaps twenty minutes, and every one of those minutes belonged to him.

That was the arithmetic he could never stop doing. Eleven minutes of argument. Forty-four minutes of burial. One life.

* * *

The inquiry lasted five weeks and felt like being flayed slowly, by polite people.

It was held in a hotel conference room in the valley town, because the regional authority had no building large enough, and the room had that particular hotel smell of carpet shampoo and coffee urns, and Theo sat at a table under fluorescent lights while men in good jackets explained avalanche science to each other over his head. There were diagrams. There was a meteorological reconstruction. There was Callum, composed and sorrowful, testifying that he had raised concerns about the route timing and that lead guide Vane had overridden them—a statement that was true in the way a photograph is true, capturing one frame of an argument and calling it the whole.

What Callum never mentioned—what Theo, to his lasting shame, never forced him to mention—was that the concerns Callum claimed to have raised had been about speed, not safety. Callum had wanted to go faster. When the lawyers got hold of the transcript of the col conversation—which existed, because Theo's radio had been recording on the open channel per protocol—the record showed Theo saying *it's loaded* and *we space it out* and *one at a time*. But transcripts are bloodless. Nobody reading them cold could feel the wind, see the grainy light, know that *marginal* on a day like that was a coin toss dressed up in data. What the transcript showed, to a panel looking for someone to blame, was a lead guide who acknowledged danger and proceeded anyway.

Which was also true. That was the unbearable part. Both things were true. He had been careful, and he had been wrong, and Alma Brandt was dead, and the difference between tragedy and negligence turned out to be a matter of paperwork.

The findings came down in April. Negligent route assessment; failure to exercise appropriate caution under deteriorating conditions; contributing factor, overconfidence. His certification was suspended. Then the second country—the one on the far side of the range, where he'd held a reciprocal guiding permit since he was twenty-five—reviewed the findings and revoked his permits too. Two bans. Two countries. The word that followed him out of both hearings and into every valley afterward was *blacklisted*, spoken in the flat administrative tone of people closing a file, and it stuck to him like burrs.

There had been an offer, in the end. The consortium that ran the big heli-ski operation south of the range needed experienced mountain men and didn't much care about inquiries conducted in foreign jurisdictions. They'd heard about the Kestrel Face business and sent a man to find him, a smooth man with a leather portfolio, who laid it out plainly: sign here, run our backcountry program, your past stays past. Theo had read the contract twice and understood exactly what he'd be signing—not employment but ownership, his knowledge and his silence bundled together, and somewhere in the fine print the understanding that whatever the consortium did to the mountains he loved, he would be contractually obligated to look away.

He'd burned the contract in a camp stove and taken the ferry work instead. Low visibility. Cash. False names. Moving water, moving seasons, no slope steep enough to bury anyone.

* * *

Theo opened his eyes. The stars had shifted west. The river went on arguing with itself.

Four years, and he still did the arithmetic. He'd gotten good at hiding it—at wearing the exile like a coat that fit well enough that strangers couldn't tell it wasn't his skin. Ferry runs up and down the Reckoning, supplies to survey camps and fishing lodges and the occasional party of researchers who paid in cash and didn't ask questions, because a man with a false name and a scarred boat attracted exactly the clientele who preferred not to be asked questions either. It was a living. It was barely a living. It was, most days, enough.

But the silence from the survey camp had gotten under that coat.

He sat up in the dark and made himself go through it again, item by item, the way he'd once gone through snowpit data. The Halvorsen survey—six people, geological team, contracted by some mining concern out of the capital to assess the upper Reckoning basin. He'd ferried for them three times: core drills, sample crates, a crate marked FRAGILE that clinked like glassware and that the geologist woman—Okafor, sharp-eyed, tired, the only one of them who treated him like a person rather than a conveyance—had handled like it held the actual bones of the mountain. Their radio checks had come in regular as tide tables, every Sunday at eighteen hundred, relayed down through the landing office. Cheerful, boring transmissions. *All quiet. Samples progressing. Send more coffee.*

Then last Sunday: garble. Thirty seconds of static with a voice inside it like a face behind frosted glass—he'd caught maybe four words, and two of them had been *not* and *here*—and then nothing. This Sunday: nothing at all.

The landing office manager, a fat comfortable man named Dreesen who had never been above the flood line in his life, had shrugged. "Radios die. Boats sink. People quit. Not your problem, Vane." Except Dreesen called him Voss, because that was the name on the papers Theo had bought in the port city, and the falseness of it sat strangely tonight, like a shoe on the wrong foot.

Because here was the thing Theo kept circling and refusing to land on: the survey area was in the high basin below the Kestrel massif. Below the Face. Whatever they were drilling for, they were drilling in the shadow of the slope that had eaten Alma Brandt, and the mining concern that hired them was the same class of smooth-portfolio outfit that had once offered to buy his silence, and none of that proved anything except that the world was small and money went where money went.

Still. Still.

He thought about Okafor's face the last time he'd dropped supplies—she'd looked over her shoulder at the treeline twice while talking to him, quick flicks, the way animals check when they've sensed something they can't identify. He'd filed it then as nerves, altitude, the isolation. Tonight, lying in the bilge-smell dark of his boat with the river talking and the stars indifferent, he filed it differently.

If something had happened to them—if men had come up that valley and done what men sometimes did to small parties of witnesses in remote places—then the distance between himself and it was not comfort. It was just luck. And luck, he had learned on Kestrel Face, was not a plan; it was a debt, and debts collected.

He could hear himself already, composing the justification the way he used to compose route plans, clean and logical and deniable: *Due diligence. I'm already positioned. I'm going upriver anyway on the supply run; checking on a silent client camp is standard practice, any operator would do it. If they're fine, I've lost a day. If they're not—*

If they're not, then what? What exactly did a blacklisted ferryman with a false name and no standing intend to do about it?

Nothing, probably. Look, confirm, come back, report to Dreesen, let the authorities handle it. That was the sane version. That was the version he'd say out loud, to Dreesen, to himself, to anyone who asked.

But underneath the sane version, in the place where the river's voice couldn't reach, another motive stirred that he refused to examine too closely—the old guilt, Alma's forty-four minutes, the sense he'd carried for four years that he owed the mountains below Kestrel Face a debt of attention, that he had once looked at a slope and chosen to see what the data permitted instead of what the wind was telling him, and that a man who had made that mistake once did not get to look away twice.

Due diligence, he told the dark. Just due diligence.

The lie fit so well he almost believed it, and he was almost asleep when the landing office's generator coughed awake down the bank, and he heard Dreesen's voice crackle over the old PA they used for river traffic, flat and bored:

"Voss. You awake up there? Got a message came in late. Survey camp. Says they're—" static chewed the words, then gave them back whole, and Theo sat bolt upright in the cold. "—says they're evacuating. Says men came. Says don't come upriver, Vane. Says—" and here Dreesen paused, puzzled, reading it again, "—says *they know your real name*."

The river went on talking. Theo didn't hear it anymore at all.
