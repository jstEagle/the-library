## 3. Empty Tents

The river told him before he saw it.

Theo Vane had spent eleven days poling the supply scow up the Reckoning, and in that time he had learned its moods the way a man learns a lover's breathing—the shallow stutter of shallows, the deep slow pull of the bends where the current folded under itself. This morning, three miles below the survey camp's landing, the river went quiet. Not calm. Quiet. The kind of quiet water makes when something upstream has changed its shape.

He shipped his pole and let the scow drift, listening. Above the usual morning chorus of thrushes and the tick of frost letting go of the alder brush, there should have been the generator. The survey team ran a Honda generator from six to eight every evening and again at dawn; he'd heard it faintly on the wind for two days now, a thin insect whine that meant people were alive up there, boiling coffee, charging radio batteries, complaining about the cold in four languages. This morning the wind carried nothing but water.

He grounded the scow at the landing just after seven. The mist was still peeling off the current in long gray ribbons, and the landing itself—three pilings and a plank platform someone had built with more enthusiasm than skill—emerged from it piece by piece as he tied off. His breath hung around him. He stood for a moment with one hand on the rope, doing the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do since the garbled transmission two nights ago.

*Static, then Halvorsen's voice saying something about—"not right, the readings are not"—then static, then nothing. Two scheduled check-ins missed. The company that hired him didn't pay enough to care, which was exactly why they'd hired a man with no name worth checking.*

"Just due diligence," he said aloud. The words sounded like what they were: a coat pulled over guilt. He'd told himself the same thing at Kestrel Face, standing at the edge of the debris field while the rescue crews worked below. *Somebody has to look. Might as well be me.* It had never once been true.

He climbed the bank trail with the rifle slung across his back—he'd started carrying it at the gorge, another thing he wasn't examining—and came out of the trees into the camp clearing.

The tents were still standing. That was the first wrongness, and it took his breath worse than the climb had. Four dome tents in a row, guy-lines taut, rain flies on, the cook shelter at the end with its stovepipe still in place. A camp that looked lived-in. A camp that looked like everyone had just stepped away to look at an outcrop.

But the details were all wrong. The mess table under the cook shelter was bare—not cleared, *stripped*, down to the screws holding the lantern hook. The supply crates stacked along the treeline had been opened and emptied, lids tossed aside in a way no careful expedition cook would allow. The fire pit held ash from a fire burned hot and fast, and scattered through the ash were things that don't burn: a zipper pull, a scrap of tent fabric, the melted spine of a notebook.

And there were no footprints going anywhere. Just churned ground, many boots, days old, half-healed by frost and drizzle, all of them pointing the same direction: downriver. Toward him.

Theo stood at the edge of the clearing for a long time, letting the camp speak. He'd read avalanche slopes this way, crime scenes this way, once, in another life. Snow doesn't lie and neither does disturbed ground, if you're patient enough to listen.

The generator was gone. The radio was gone. The satellite phone case by the cook tent door was open and empty. Somebody had taken every means of calling the outside world, and then taken the people who would have used them.

He moved through the camp slowly, touching nothing at first. The geologists' tents: sleeping bags rolled or not, a paperback swollen with damp, a pair of reading glasses folded on a crate. Nobody packs reading glasses and leaves. Nobody leaves a tent pitched unless they left fast, or were made to leave fast, or never left at all.

At the third tent—the one Halvorsen used, the expedition lead, a big deliberate Norwegian who'd shaken Theo's hand at the landing two weeks ago and said, *You pole that boat like a man who's done harder things*—there was blood.

It was on the forward pole, low, at knee height, where a man might be struck if he were kneeling or already down. It had dried black-brown and weathered to the color of old rust, but there was a lot of it, smeared in an arc, and below it, pressed into the trampled mud, a handprint. Large. Halvorsen-large. Sliding, the fingers dragged, as if he'd reached for something—or been reaching when they took him.

Theo crouched beside the print for a while without touching it. The cold had gotten into his knees and stayed there. He found he was thinking, absurdly, about the last time he'd seen Halvorsen, how the Norwegian had stood on the landing platform and watched him load the scow and said, "You'll come up for coffee next run? A man shouldn't work alone on a river." And Theo had said sure, maybe, next run, the way you say things you mean and never do.

"Next run," he said now, to nobody. His voice cracked in the middle of it, and he let it.

* * *

He searched the camp the way he'd been trained to search a scene, back when training was something done to him rather than something he did. Systematic. Outside-in. He catalogued what was missing because the missing things tell you who your enemy is.

Missing: all communications equipment. All firearms—the team had licensed a shotgun and two rifles for bear country; the rack in the cook shelter held three empty brackets and cut zip-ties. Missing: all sample cases, the aluminum core boxes, the field laptops, the GPS units, the paper field notebooks—even the trash bag of discarded packaging behind the cook tent had been gone through. Whoever came had wanted data. Every gram of it.

Not missing: personal effects. Cash in Halvorsen's tent—four hundred dollars in an envelope marked FUEL, untouched. Boots. Watches. A gold ring in a dish by someone's cot.

That was the detail that settled it, if settling was needed. Thieves take rings. Men sent to erase an expedition leave the ring, because the ring isn't evidence of anything except a person, and persons can be dealt with elsewhere. You strip the science and you take the scientists, and you leave the camp looking almost normal so that anyone who wanders past thinks: *they've moved on ahead, fine,* and wanders past.

Except they hadn't counted on the floor.

It was the cook who'd hidden it—Theo was sure of that within a minute of finding it. Her name was Amara Deng, and she'd cooked for survey crews for twenty years, and she was the only member of the team who'd ever looked at Theo like she knew exactly what a false name on a manifest was for. She'd fed him stew on the second run and said, "You keep your eyes open on this river, boatman. Pretty valley. Ugly business." He'd thought she was making conversation.

The cook shelter's floor was rough planking over joists, and under the stove—under the one place a floorboard would be warm, dry, and never casually lifted—there was a board cut slightly shorter than its neighbors. Theo found it because the sawdust in the joint was fresh, and because Amara Deng struck him as a woman who fixed things properly and hid things the same way.

Under it: a strongbox. Steel, military green, the size of a bread loaf, locked, and heavy enough that his forearms burned lifting it into the light. No key anywhere in the camp—he checked, all afternoon, while the light went long and amber and the cold came down off the ridge like a tide. He checked Halvorsen's pockets, Amara's tin of sewing supplies, the toolbox, the bottom of the flour bin. Nothing.

In the end he didn't pick the lock. He broke the hasp with a rock and a length of pipe from the collapsed drying rack, kneeling in the cook shelter as the dusk came blue through the canvas walls, and the sound of it was very loud in the empty camp, and he flinched, and hated that he flinched.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth in careful layers, was paper.

Geological surveys first—dozens of pages, core logs in tight engineer's handwriting, assay reports, stratigraphic sections drawn in colored pencil with the precision of icons. Theo didn't understand half of it, but he understood structure, and these documents had it: dates, sample numbers, chain-of-custody forms signed and countersigned. Real work. Months of it.

Then the other papers. And the cold that came over him then had nothing to do with the hour.

Memoranda. Internal correspondence, printed from email, some of it clearly not meant to leave the office it was printed in. Letterhead he recognized from the news, from the inquiry hearings he'd listened to on a borrowed radio in a hostel in another country while his own name was being dismantled: **Meridian Consolidated Mining Group**. And threaded through the memos, again and again, in language smooth as river stone:

*...revised stability assessment for the Kestrel East face, per client request...*

*...the original 2019 slope analysis overstated fracture density; recommend we proceed with the corrected figures...*

*...given regulatory timelines, the independent verification of the upper bench readings may need to be... expedited...*

Kestrel East. The Kestrel Face. The mountain that had come down on a Tuesday morning in March and buried eleven people, and buried Theo Vane's name under them.

He read it twice, three times, kneeling on the floorboards with the pages shaking in his hands—not his hands shaking, he noticed distantly, the pages, as though the paper itself were afraid. The surveys in this box weren't new work. They were *verification* work. The Reckoning survey team hadn't been mapping this valley for minerals. They'd been re-sampling the old Meridian sites, pulling cores from the same benches, running the same assays Meridian had run before the face failed—and their numbers didn't match. Didn't match at all. The fracture densities Meridian had reported as marginal were, in these logs, catastrophic. The "corrected figures" were falsifications, bought and paid for, and eleven people had died inside the margin between the truth and the lie.

And here it was. In a bread-loaf steel box under a cook stove, hidden by a woman who'd said *ugly business* and kept her eyes open, waiting for someone to carry it out.

Theo sat back on his heels. Through the canvas wall he could see the last light dying on the ridge above the camp, the same ridge, more or less, that had stood over Kestrel Face, forty miles north. Somewhere downriver, men with radios and rifles and a payroll signature were moving toward a boat landing, covering their tracks, tying off loose ends. Loose ends with names like Halvorsen. Like Amara Deng. Like, eventually, whoever found this box.

Like Theo Vane, if anyone ever learned his real name.

Every instinct he had honed in seven years of exile said the same thing, in the same flat voice: *put it back. Board the floor. Walk to the scow. Pole downriver past the landing, past the town, past the border, and never have seen any of this.* He'd survived this long precisely by being nobody who'd seen anything.

But the handprint in the mud wouldn't leave him. Halvorsen's hand, sliding. *A man shouldn't work alone on a river.*

And underneath that, older and colder, the thing he'd carried since the inquiry: the knowledge that the report had blamed his judgment, his route, his call to move the team—and that he'd spent seven years half-believing it, because the alternative was believing the mountain had been murdered and he'd been made to carry the body.

Now he knew. Now he was holding the proof in his hands, and it weighed less than a sleeping bag and more than anything he'd ever carried.

He repacked the box. Wrapped the oilcloth the way Amara had wrapped it, layer on layer. He didn't put it back under the floor—if the men who did this came back, the floor was the first place they'd look, and anyway, the box was leaving with him. Instead he stowed it in the false bottom of his gear duffel, beneath the spare rope and the tinned heat, and sat in the cook shelter in the dark with the rifle across his knees, listening to the river.

Around midnight, frost furred the canvas. Around one, he heard it—a sound upstream of the camp, in the timber. Not a branch. Not an animal. The particular soft double-fall of boots on frozen ground, moving carefully, stopping, moving again.

Theo killed his headlamp and waited in the black, breathing through his mouth, one hand on the stock.

The sound passed below the clearing, angling down toward the landing. Toward the scow. Whoever it was, they weren't coming to search the camp.

They were tracking someone. Someone who'd come through here ahead of Theo—someone small enough, and desperate enough, to have left the camp alive.

The footsteps faded downriver, unhurried, patient as the water. Theo stood in the dark of the empty camp, and felt the dread in his chest finish hardening into something else—something with edges, something that pointed.

Whatever had survived this place was still moving. And so, now, was he.
