## 4. The Survivor

The trail began where the blood ended.

Theo found it forty minutes downstream of the camp, half-hidden in the duff beneath a stand of hemlock: a boot print, pressed deep at the heel, the tread of a woman's hiking boot by its narrowness, and beside it a smear on a fallen log where someone had caught themselves and kept moving. Whoever it was had gone into the trees instead of downriver. Downriver was the sensible choice—downriver led to Salt Landing, to boats, to people. Into the trees meant hiding.

He crouched over the print for a long time, longer than the tracking required. The morning fog was burning off in ragged sheets, and the river talked to itself below him in that low, constant voice he'd been listening to for three weeks without hearing. He told himself what he'd been telling himself since the strongbox: look around, mark what you see, get back to the boat, report nothing to no one. He was a ferryman with a false name and a cash contract. Due diligence. That was all this was.

But his eyes were already following the trail upslope, reading it the way other men read a page. A drag of moss disturbed at knee height. A snapped fern, wilting but not yet browned—hours old, not days. And here, pressed into the mud near a seep, a second print with the toe dug in hard, the kind of impression a person leaves when they're climbing on a leg that doesn't want to carry them.

Wounded, then. Alone. Moving uphill because downhill was where the men had come from.

Theo stood, and his knees made the small complaints of a man thirty-eight years old who had spent too many of those years carrying weight they weren't designed for. He looked back down at the river. His boat was tied there, twenty minutes' walk through the alder, loaded with the expedition's resupply—flour and fuel and radio batteries for people who were gone. He could untie it. He could be in Salt Landing by dark, and by tomorrow he could be someone else entirely, which was the one skill he had never stopped practicing.

The wind moved through the hemlocks with a sound like a long exhale, and Theo Vane, who had run from two countries and one name and one face he saw every time he closed his eyes, stood on the bank of the Reckoning River and discovered that his feet had already decided.

He went up.

* * *

The forest thickened as he climbed, old growth giving way to a snarl of slide alder and devil's club that tore at his sleeves and left the air sharp with green bruise-smell. He tracked slowly, the way he'd learned before he ever had a license to do it—his father's way, back when tracking was just how you found your way home to supper. Weight on the outside edges of the feet. Rest stops against living wood, never dead. Twice he found where she had doubled back on her own trail, laying false direction, and both times the instinct behind it was good enough that he felt something like respect, and something like worry. People who laid false trails were people who expected to be followed.

By early afternoon the terrain steepened toward a granite shoulder where the trees thinned, and he smelled woodsmoke before he saw anything else—a thread of it, thin and careful, the smoke of a fire built small and hot and shielded. Someone who knew fire drew searchers like blood drew sharks. Someone lighting one anyway was either desperate or stupid, and the false trails said not stupid.

He came off the rock shoulder into a fold of the hillside where a leaning slab of granite made a shallow overhang, and there she was.

She had a pistol pointed at him before he heard her move, which frightened him more than the pistol did. She was propped against the slab with her left leg stretched out stiff and black with dried blood from mid-thigh to boot, a parka wrapped around her shoulders that had once been expedition red and was now mostly mud. Younger than he'd expected—thirty, maybe. Dark skin gone gray around the mouth. Her hands shook holding the gun, but her eyes didn't, and her eyes were the thing he catalogued first, because eyes were the last part of a person to stop working.

"Don't," she said. Her voice was scraped raw, like a file dragged over rust.

"I'm not with them." He kept his hands visible, open, away from the knife at his belt. "I came up from the river. I run supplies. Ran supplies," he corrected himself, because whatever the camp had been, it wasn't a client anymore.

"Nobody runs supplies up here." The barrel didn't waver. "There's nothing up here."

"There's a camp," Theo said. "Or there was. Tents cut open. Cook shack stripped to the studs. Blood on the tent pole—enough of it that I don't think whoever bled walked far." He paused. "You walked far."

Something moved behind her eyes—not softening, exactly. Recalculation. He watched her do the arithmetic he'd done a hundred times in his own life: what does this man know, what will it cost me if he's lying, what will it cost me if he isn't.

"You went inside the cook shack," she said.

"There was a floorboard that didn't match."

Her breath came out slow through her nose. The pistol lowered an inch, then another inch, the way a drawbridge comes down grudgingly. "Ines Okafor," she said finally. "Geologist. Meridian Survey Group, contract number—" She stopped herself, and a ghost of something bitter crossed her face. "It doesn't matter. None of the paperwork matters now."

"Theo." He gave the false name out of habit—*Aldous Ren*, the name stenciled on his supply manifests—and hated it even as it left his mouth, because it sounded thin here, under this broken slab, in front of this woman with a hole in her leg and a dead camp behind them both. "I have water. I have a med kit on the boat, and food, if you can walk."

"I've been walking." She said it like a dare.

"On that leg?"

"On that leg," she confirmed, and something in the flatness of it told him she'd been walking on worse than the leg.

* * *

She let him clean the wound because the alternative was infection, and infection was the one enemy that didn't negotiate. It was a gash more than a puncture—something had opened her thigh along six inches of muscle, maybe shrapnel, maybe a blade, maybe the edge of the world falling on her; she didn't say, and he didn't ask yet. He flushed it with boiled water, packed it, bound it tight enough to hold and loose enough to keep the tissue honest. She watched his hands the whole time with an expression he recognized because he'd worn it himself in field clinics after Kestrel Face: the expression of someone deciding whether competence could be trusted, or whether competence was just another kind of trap.

"You've done this before," she said.

"A long time ago I was a mountain guide. WFR certified. That was another life."

"What happened to it?"

"The life or the certification?"

"Either."

He tied off the bandage and sat back on his heels. Around them the afternoon light was going amber and long, filtering down through the canopy in dusty columns. Somewhere above, a raven said something rude to the sky. "Both," he said. "There was an accident. There was an inquiry. There were findings." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him more than he wanted it to. "Two countries decided I wasn't welcome in their mountains anymore. So now I move boxes up rivers under a name that isn't mine."

Ines studied him for a moment. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed—a single dry note with no humor in it at all, the laugh of a person whose sense of irony had survived everything else. "An inquiry," she repeated. "Findings. God. You should hear yourself. You sound like the reports."

"What reports?"

And there it was—the question he hadn't known he was carrying until it left him, the hook going in deep before he understood there was a line attached.

Ines leaned her head back against the granite. For a while she didn't answer, and he thought she might have passed out, her color was so bad. But her eyes were open, fixed on the underside of the overhang where water had stained the stone in long brown curtains, and when she spoke again her voice had changed—flatter, more careful, the voice of a scientist reading data aloud because reading it aloud was the only way to make herself believe it.

"Meridian Survey Group is a shell," she said. "You probably figured that from the strongbox. We were contracted to do baseline geological assessment on the upper Reckoning drainage. Core samples, slope stability, hydrology. Standard pre-development work for a mining consortium—the Karst-Veyl group, though you'll never find that name on anything you can subpoena. Three layers of subcontractors between them and us." She turned her head and looked at him directly. "Except our team lead wasn't corrupt. Old man Sorensen. He'd been doing this forty years and he still believed the numbers belonged to everybody. When our cores came back showing the east face of the Kestrel massif was shot through with fracture zones—unstable, unmineable, catastrophic if you put blasting charges anywhere near the headwall—he wrote the honest report. Sent it up the chain with the raw data attached."

Theo had stopped breathing. He knew it only distantly, the way you know weather changing. The word *Kestrel* had gone through him like a spike through the palm.

"Six months later," Ines continued, "Karst-Veyl files a development application citing a stability assessment for that same east face. Different numbers. Clean numbers. Numbers that said the face was sound." Her jaw tightened. "Sorensen started pulling threads. Found the falsified assessment. Found that it had been authored using survey data from four years ago—data from before the disaster."

"The avalanche," Theo said. His voice came out strange to him, sanded down to almost nothing.

"The avalanche," Ines agreed quietly. "Kestrel Face. Fourteen dead, if you count the village below. They blamed guide error. A man named—" She stopped. She had seen his face now; there was no missing it. Whatever she read there made her go very still. "Vane," she finished softly. "Theo Vane."

The river was a mile below and he could hear it anyway, filling the whole world, saying his name over and over in its low indifferent voice.

"That's not possible," he said. "That inquiry closed eight years ago. The findings are sealed. Nobody reopens—"

"Somebody kept copies." Ines's hand found the pocket of her ruined parka and rested there, not dramatically, just resting, the way a hand rests on a wound. "Sorensen kept copies. The raw cores, the assay logs, the comparison tables, correspondence. Everything Karst-Veyl buried, everything the original inquiry never saw because the consortium's lawyers got to the commissioners first. He hid it in the cook shack floor because he trusted the camp, and the camp was family." Her voice cracked, just once, and she mastered it. "Then three nights ago men came up the river who were not family."

* * *

She told it plainly, which was the worst way to hear it. No drama, no trembling set pieces—just facts laid out in order, the way she'd lay out core samples on a table, each one labeled, each one true.

They had come at night, seven or eight of them, professional, quiet, moving like men who had done this before in places where no one would ever ask questions. Not robbers—they took almost nothing personal. They took equipment, they took the data drives, they took the satellite phone, and they took people. Sorensen first. Then the others, one by one, zip-tied and hooded and marched down to a boat that idled black on the river. She had been on latrine duty at the treeline when it started—she heard the first shout, and instead of running down to help, which would have meant joining them, she ran up. Deeper. She'd torn her leg open on the same devil's club that had slowed Theo all afternoon, and she'd crawled the last hundred meters, and she'd lain under a windfall listening to her team call each other's names in the dark until, one by one, they stopped calling.

"How many?" Theo asked.

"Five. Sorensen, Devi, Marcus, Chen, and the new kid, Okonkwo. Five." She stared at the fire she'd let die down to coals. "They didn't kill anyone at the camp. That's the part I keep turning over. If they wanted us dead we'd be dead. They took us somewhere, which means we're worth more alive, which means there's time, which means—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "Which means every hour I sit here with my leg open is an hour I'm spending their lives."

"Who leads them? The men who took them."

"A fixer. The others called him that like it was a title. Corliss." She pronounced it carefully, the way you pronounce the name of a disease. "He wasn't there the first night—I think he came after, when the cleanup crew couldn't account for everyone. One of them said his name on the radio. Said, *Corliss wants the site swept and the woman found.*" She looked at Theo. "*The woman found.* That's me. That's the whole assignment. Not killed—found. Because I'm the loose end that knows where Sorensen hid things, and because dead geologists raise questions that disappeared ones don't."

The light was failing. Theo fed a few sticks to the coals and watched the fire take them, and inside his chest something vast and cold was rearranging itself, iceberg-slow, and he could feel the shape of it but not name it yet.

Eight years. Eight years he had carried the findings of that inquiry like a stone sewn into his sternum—*guide error, delayed response, failure of judgment*—and he had carried it because some part of him believed it, or needed to believe it, because the alternative was that fourteen people died for a spreadsheet. He had frozen on the face. That part was true; he'd confessed it to himself a thousand times in a thousand lonely rooms. But the ice had come off the headwall because someone upstream had decided a fracture zone was a rounding error, and no inquiry had ever looked higher than the man roped to the last anchor.

"They'll come for you," Ines said into the silence. "Corliss's men. Maybe tonight, maybe tomorrow. When they find the camp empty and the strongbox gone, they'll sweep the drainage. You should go. You have a boat. You could be—" She caught herself. "You could be gone."

"I could," Theo said.

It was true. Every practical cell in his body affirmed it. He was a ferryman named Aldous Ren with a cash contract and a clean—if borrowed—record, and the smart play, the survival play, the play he had executed flawlessly for eight years across two borders and a dozen names, was to walk down to the river right now and row until this valley was a story he never told.

But he thought of the tents cut open like bellies. Of Marta—no, not Marta, that was later, that was a town he hadn't reached yet—of the cook shack stripped to the studs, of five names he'd never heard spoken until an hour ago and already couldn't unknow. He thought of Sorensen, a man he'd never met, who had written the honest report and hidden the truth under a floorboard because he believed the numbers belonged to everybody. He thought of the word *found*, and what it meant that a company could buy a human being the way you bought diesel.

And underneath all of it, older and colder than any of it, he thought: *they used my disaster.* They had mined his ruin for cover the way you drove a tunnel through a mountain, and he had spent eight years apologizing for standing in the blast zone.

"The nearest radio that works is in Salt Landing," he said. "Three days downriver, if the gorge lets us past. After that it's a day to a road." He looked at her leg, at the gray around her mouth, at the pistol in her lap that she'd held steady with shaking hands. "Can you walk three days?"

"I can walk three days," Ines Okafor said, in the voice of a woman who would have said it with both legs broken.

Theo nodded once and began kicking dirt over the coals. Above the trees the first stars were coming out, indifferent and bright, and somewhere down the darkening valley—though he couldn't hear it yet, wouldn't hear it for two more days—a boat with no lights was moving up the Reckoning River, and a man named Corliss was asking after a woman who was supposed to be found.
