## 5. What the Mountain Keeps

Ines Okafor had spent eleven years learning to read rock the way other people read faces, and she had never once been wrong about what stone could tell her. Stone did not lie. Stone did not flatter, did not hedge, did not round off an inconvenient number to please a board of directors. When she pressed her thumb against a seam of fractured quartzite and felt the looseness in it, the way a tooth feels loose before it goes, she knew what she was touching: a mountain holding its breath.

She was thinking about this now because the man who had pulled her out of the fern brake was sitting three feet away with her stolen strongbox open between them, and he was not reading it the way she read it. He was reading it like a man reading his own death warrant, which, in a sense, he was.

The hollow they'd reached by dusk was a fold in the hillside above the river, floored with pine needles and walled on one side by a slab of granite that held the day's heat like a banked stove. Theo Vane — though she hadn't known his name yet, only *the ferryman*, the man with the boat and the burned-looking eyes — had built a small fire in a pit lined with stones, screened from the water by a curtain of alder. He'd done it without being asked, without wasted motion, and Ines had watched him do it and understood that this was a man whose body had memorized survival so thoroughly that his mind no longer needed to attend.

Her leg throbbed where the branch had opened it. She'd cleaned it with river water and the iodine from his kit, and he'd stitched it for her — four stitches, quick and even, hands steadier than his face suggested. Now he sat with the documents spread across his knees, and the firelight moved on his features, and he said nothing at all.

He had been silent for a long time.

"You should say something," Ines said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. Three days in a hole in the ground will do that to a voice. "You've been quiet since the second page."

"I'm reading."

"You've read it twice."

"Then I'm checking my work." He turned a page. The paper made a small dry sound, very loud in the dark under the trees. "I want to be sure I'm understanding what this says."

"And are you?"

He didn't answer immediately. He lifted one of the core logs instead — her own handwriting, her own careful columns — and held it toward the light as if distance would change what it said. It wouldn't. She had written those numbers herself, crouched at the drilling rig with mud to her elbows, and she had known while writing them that they were the most dangerous numbers anyone on that expedition had ever produced.

"The Kestrel Face report," he said finally. "The official one. The one the inquiry used." His voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a man carrying something heavy across ice. "It cited slope stability assessments. Seepage analysis. A hazard classification of *moderate, manageable*." He looked up at her. "These are the raw numbers underneath that report."

"Yes."

"These aren't moderate."

"No," Ines said. "They're not."

* * *

She had known about Kestrel Face before she ever met him, the way everyone in the field knew about it — the way you know about a plane crash or a bridge collapse, as a story told to make you careful. Seven dead. A guided party caught under a slide that the assessments had called unlikely. One guide survived, and the inquiry decided the guide was the reason everyone else hadn't. Two countries had agreed on almost nothing in forty years, but they had agreed on Theo Vane: banned, blacklisted, name struck from every permit registry from the coast to the interior ranges. *The guide who froze*, someone had called him once within her hearing, and the phrase had stuck because it was so clean, so satisfying, so exactly the shape of a story people wanted. One man's failure. Not the mountain's. Not the paperwork's. His.

She had believed it too. That was the thing she could not get past now, sitting here watching him hold the evidence of his own ruin in his hands. She had believed it, and she had been one of the people whose job it was to know better.

"Tell me what you found," he said. "All of it. From the beginning. I want it in order."

So she gave it to him in order, because ordering things was the only discipline she had left tonight.

"We were contracted for a mineral assessment along the upper Reckoning — the consortium's land claims, the ones they were preparing to develop. Standard work. Core samples, stability mapping, hydrology. I ran the geotechnical side." She shifted against the granite; her leg complained and she ignored it. "The first anomaly showed up in week two. The consortium's original survey data — the baseline we were supposed to verify our results against — didn't match the terrain. Not slightly. Not margin-of-error. The fracture densities in their reports were half what we measured. Their seepage figures assumed drainage paths that don't exist. They'd mapped the water table two hundred meters lower than it actually sits."

Theo had gone very still. It was a particular stillness, she noticed — not relaxation but arrest, the stillness of a man listening for something under the sound.

"That's not sloppiness," he said.

"No. Sloppiness scatters. This was systematic. Every dangerous number had been adjusted in the same direction, by roughly the same factor, across six years of filings. Someone sat down and built a mountain that wasn't there." She took a breath. "And then I cross-referenced the coordinates, because the falsified surveys covered more than the consortium's current claims. They covered the whole headwall region. Including—" she nodded at the papers in his lap, "—Kestrel Face."

The fire popped. Somewhere down the valley a bird made one complaint and went quiet.

"The report that cleared the Face for guiding traffic," Theo said slowly, "was built on their data."

"It *was* their data. Same survey firm, same lead geologist, same filing numbers. The inquiry commissioned an independent review, but the review used the consortium's archives as its source material. Garbage in." She watched his face. "Garbage out. Seven people died on top of a number somebody changed in an office in the capital."

He set the papers down. Carefully, the way you set down something breakable when your hands want to crush it. Then he stood, walked to the edge of the firelight, and stood with his back to her looking at the dark shapes of the trees.

Ines let him stand there. She knew what it was to need a minute inside your own skull with a fact that had rewritten everything behind you.

* * *

Here is what she had learned in three days in a hole, waiting for men with rifles to give up on finding her: fear clarifies. It burns away everything decorative and leaves you alone with what you actually believe. Crouched under a windfall with her leg bleeding and Corliss's men moving through the trees speaking in low, unhurried voices — men treating murder as logistics — she had stopped pretending to herself about anything.

She had pretended for months, back at the camp. That was the truth of it. When the numbers first came in wrong, she'd told herself error. When the pattern emerged, she'd told herself she needed more data. When she'd finally assembled the full picture and shown Dr. Amara, the expedition lead, and seen his face go grey — when he'd said, quietly, *we have to notify the ministry, all of it, the whole chain* — she had still pretended, right up until the night the trucks came, that institutions would handle it. That there was a machinery for this. That you handed the truth to the proper authorities and the proper authorities did the rest.

The trucks had come at 0300. Six men, professional, polite, terrifyingly organized. They had taken the radios first, then the satellite phone, then Dr. Amara and Yusuf and the drill crew, walking them into vehicles with their hands zip-tied, and the man in charge — Corliss, though she hadn't learned his name until later, hearing it spoken by the men who came back to sweep the camp — had gone through the tents with a list, collecting notebooks like a tax collector. He had not raised his voice once. That was the detail that haunted her. The whole operation had the courtesy of a dental appointment.

She had survived because she'd broken protocol. Because some animal instinct had gotten her out of her tent twenty minutes before the trucks arrived, into the trees with the strongbox and a water bottle, and she'd lain in the ferns and listened to her colleagues beg, and then stop begging, and then the engines leaving.

Three days. And in those three days she had understood that there was no machinery. There never had been. There were only people, and what they were willing to carry, and how far.

"Their data killed them," Theo said to the trees. He hadn't turned around. "The inquiry had their archive. The permitting offices had their archive. My own lawyers—" a sound that was almost a laugh, "—my own lawyer subpoenaed the same survey firm to defend me. Used their numbers to argue the Face was stable. Argued *against* myself with fabricated rock."

"I'm sorry," Ines said, and meant it, and knew it was worth nothing.

"Do you know what they asked me? At the inquiry?" He still hadn't turned. "Eleven hours over four days. And the question they kept coming back to, the question the commissioner asked me last, leaning forward like it mattered — was whether I'd *hesitated*. Whether there was a moment, on the approach, when I saw something and chose not to turn the party around." Now he did turn, and the firelight showed her his face, and it was worse than anger. It was the face of a man doing arithmetic. "They wanted to know if I froze. And I couldn't prove I hadn't. Because the entire evidentiary basis of the hearing — the slope data, the seepage history, the hazard class — was fiction. They interrogated my judgment against a mountain that never existed."

"And the real one—"

"The real one was telling anyone who could read it that it was coming apart." He crouched suddenly and picked up the core log again, and his hand shook — just barely, just enough that she saw it. "This says the Face was saturated. This says the fracture network was connected and loading. This says a party of eight on that route in late season was—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "It was a coin toss. They sent us up a coin toss and then spent four days asking why I didn't call heads."

Ines looked at the fire. Fury has a physics, she thought. It wants somewhere to go, a direction, a target, and when it can't find one it turns inward and starts eating the person carrying it. She could see that happening in him in real time — seven years of fury that had never had anywhere to go, that had been forced to eat itself in silence in river towns under false names, and now here, at last, was the target, the proof, the *reason* — and it was a consortium in the capital, a filing cabinet, a signature. Something he could not reach. Something with lawyers and ministers and a fixer named Corliss between it and justice.

"They'll bury this," she said. "That's what Corliss is for. The samples, the logs, the witnesses. If the falsification surfaces, it doesn't just embarrass them — it unwinds every claim they hold. The liability alone would end them. So they'll spend whatever it costs." She met his eyes. "Which means the two of us, holding this box, are currently worth more dead than any two people on this river."

"I know."

"And you still came upriver for me."

"I came upriver to check on a silent camp," he said. "Due diligence."

"That's what you're calling it."

"That's what it is." But he said it without conviction, and they both heard the lack, and after a moment he looked down at the strongbox and exhaled through his nose, long and slow, like a man setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying. "Seven years," he said. "Do you understand what seven years is? I have slept in ninety towns. I have used four names. I stopped reading newspapers about the range because my own name in print made my hands shake. I built a life out of not-existing, and it turns out the floor of that life was printed by a mining company."

"Yes."

"And nobody—" His jaw worked. "Nobody will ever print a correction big enough. You understand that. Even if this goes to a ministry, even if it's true in every line, the world already finished the story. *The guide who froze.* Stories like that don't get revised. They just get retold."

Ines pulled the blanket tighter around her shoulders. Her leg had begun a deep, rhythmic ache that meant infection was circling, looking for a way in. She catalogued it and set it aside, the way she catalogued everything now.

"Then we don't ask the world to revise," she said. "We make it impossible not to."

"How?"

"Physical evidence. Paper burns. Testimony dies conveniently. But I kept duplicates of everything — sealed core samples, logged and dated, cached at the high station hut below the Serrano col. Rock in sealed tubes. You can't edit rock. It's the only witness Corliss can't buy." She paused. "It's a hard climb. In season it's a guide's route. Out of season, with weather moving in—"

She stopped, because he was looking at her strangely.

"What hut," he said.

"The old survey station. Below the col. Grey roof, bolted to the ridge—"

"I know the hut." His voice had changed. Something had come into it that hadn't been there all evening — not hope exactly, but its raw material. "I helped build that hut. Summer I was nineteen." He looked at the fire for a long moment, and when he spoke again it was quieter. "There's a route to it from this side that isn't on any map. Gorge, then the scarp, then the bench. Four days if the weather holds. Five if it doesn't."

"And the weather?"

He tilted his head back and read the sky the way she read rock — and she recognized the gesture, recognized the fluency, and understood suddenly that everything the inquiry had taken from him, they had not managed to take this. The mountain still spoke to him. He still answered.

"Five days," he said. "Maybe less. The light on the far peaks is wrong. Dry season's ending early." He began gathering the papers, stacking them with quick precise movements, squaring the edges. "We move at first light. You'll need to learn to walk on that leg by tomorrow, because I'm not carrying you and Corliss isn't waiting."

"Corliss," Ines said. "You keep saying the name like you know it."

"I don't." He fed the documents back into the strongbox and closed the lid, and the click of the lock was very small and very final. "But a man who runs a sweep that clean doesn't leave loose ends. He knows the camp had a geologist. He'll assume she ran downhill — toward towns, toward phones. Everyone runs downhill." He looked up at her, and the firelight made his eyes look like water over stone. "So we go up. We go the way nobody goes, toward the thing nobody wants found, and we let the mountain keep what it keeps."

Down in the valley, faint but unmistakable, an engine started.

Theo's head came up. Ines felt the night reorganize itself around the sound — distance, direction, the slow grind of a vehicle taking the logging spur on the far bank, headlights sweeping the treetops a half-mile off and climbing.

"He's not assuming downhill," she whispered.

"No," Theo said, already killing the fire with practiced hands, pressing dirt over the coals until the last orange light drowned. "He's coming up."
