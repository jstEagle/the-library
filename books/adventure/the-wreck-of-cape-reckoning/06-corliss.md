## 6. Corliss

The satellite phone weighed almost nothing in Corliss's hand, and that was the thing he liked least about it. A tool that light should not carry the weight it carried. He stood on the gravel pad behind the consortium's field office at Blackpine Junction, watching the last of the daylight drain out of the western ridgeline, and dialed the number he was not supposed to have written down but had written down anyway, because a man who kept everything in his head was a man who could be erased with his head.

Three tones. Then the voice, dry as old paper.

"Report."

"Six accounted for," Corliss said. "Five secured, one dead—accident during transport, or close enough to call it one. The geologist, Okafor, is unaccounted for."

A pause on the line. Not long. The man at the other end never wasted time on pauses longer than necessary; his silences were measured like doses.

"Unaccounted for," the voice repeated. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning she wasn't in the camp when we arrived, she wasn't in the vehicles, and she isn't in the river. We found blood inside the second tent—not hers, we think, or not only hers—and a trail heading northeast into the timber. My men worked it until dark. Rain came in around four and took the rest."

"And the materials?"

"The strongbox was empty. The camp cook's floor had been cut up and put back down—badly. Whatever they were keeping under there, someone got to it before we did." Corlliss turned a slow half-circle, scanning the tree line out of habit rather than expectation. "Either Okafor took it, or someone else did. There was a boat at their landing stage. One of the expedition's own, a freight canoe. It was gone."

"Gone where?"

"Downriver, most likely. Or up. I've got men covering both banks at the narrows tonight. By morning we'll know which way the current did our work for us."

Another silence, this one longer, and Corliss understood it for what it was: the sound of a careful man recalculating. He had worked for careful men his whole career and had learned early that the dangerous ones were never the loud ones. The loud ones wanted results by Friday. The quiet ones wanted certainty by whenever certainty became possible, and they paid for patience the way other men paid for speed.

"You understand what's in that box," the voice said. It was not a question, so Corliss did not answer it. "Fifteen years of drilling reports. Fifteen years of assays from the Kestrel drainage. If those numbers leave this valley, the company doesn't lose a mine. It loses everything upstream of the mine. Every permit. Every settlement. Every signature."

"I read enough of it to understand," Corliss said.

"Then you understand that a missing geologist is not a loose end. She is the end. Everything else can be explained, delayed, litigated, denied. She cannot. She ran the cores herself. Her initials are on the logs." The paper-dry voice paused again, and when it resumed it had dropped half a register, the way it did when the arithmetic turned ugly. "Find her. And find whoever helped her off that mountain, because she didn't walk out of a secured camp on a shattered ankle without help."

Corliss looked at his own boots. Good boots. Eight hundred grams of insulation, resoled twice, because a man who worked in mountains learned quickly that his feet were the only part of the operation that never lied to him.

"There may already be a complication on that front," he said.

"Name it."

"The landing stage log at Salt Landing shows a supply run upriver three days ago. Freight only, no manifest names beyond the operator. But the harbormaster's clerk remembers him. Says he didn't talk much, handled a loaded canoe like it was empty, and took the Reckoning gorge route north on his return instead of the channel—which nobody does, because the gorge route kills people who don't know it." Corliss let that sit for a moment. "The clerk also said he'd seen the face before. Years back. Couldn't place it."

"And now you can."

It wasn't a question either, but this time Corliss answered it anyway, because the name needed saying aloud, needed to exist between two people before it could become work.

"Theo Vane."

* * *

He heard the breath go out of the man on the other end of the line—not surprise exactly, but the particular exhalation of a man finding a familiar coin in an unfamiliar pocket.

"Vane," the voice said. "You're certain."

"The clerk dug out an old ferry license after we left. Photograph attached. It's him. Older, harder, hair going grey at the temples, but it's him. Running supplies under the name Aldous Wren, out of Salt Landing, cash only, no forwarding address, no next of kin listed anywhere in three registries I can find. He's been living like a ghost with a work ethic."

"Theo Vane," the voice said again, slower now, tasting it. "Kestrel Face."

"Kestrel Face," Corliss confirmed.

There was a sound then that might have been a laugh, if laughter could be done on paper. "Do you know, I sat in on the tail end of that inquiry? Second year. The consortium retained outside counsel and I attended two sessions as a note-taker. I watched them take that man apart." A pause. "He struck me as honest. That was the problem, of course. An honest guide who froze on the face—that's a story the public can digest. Guilty of hesitation. Guilty of being human. It let us hang the disaster on human error instead of on the reports that told everyone the face was stable when we knew—" The voice stopped itself, the way careful men do. "Well. You've read the file."

"I've read the file."

"Two countries banned him. Canada first, then the Chilean agencies followed the Canadians' lead within the year. No formal charges—there was nothing to charge, that was the elegance of it—just a name passed quietly between licensing boards until no insurer would touch him and no outfitter would hire him. He lost everything without ever standing trial. Which means," and here the voice acquired something almost like pleasure, thin and cold as frost forming on wire, "he has no standing. No counsel. No record to appeal. If he surfaces in connection with this, he isn't a witness. He's a convicted man interfering with an investigation. Do you see the gift here, Mr. Corliss?"

"I see it."

"He discredits himself just by existing near the evidence. We couldn't have designed a better shield if we'd tried. A disgraced mountain guide and a frightened geologist—the press will write it as a fugitive and his accomplice before we lift a finger. All we have to do is make sure neither of them ever gets the chance to correct the record." The pause that followed was brief and businesslike. "And if correction becomes impossible in the more permanent sense, then it should become impossible in every sense. I trust that's understood."

"It's understood," Corliss said.

"Bury him twice," the voice said. "Once in the ground if you must. Once in the story, regardless. Make sure that whatever happens on that mountain, the world hears *disgraced guide Theo Vane* and stops listening. That name has been doing our work for fifteen years. Let it finish the job."

The line went dead without a goodbye. It always did.

* * *

Corliss stood a while longer on the gravel pad, phone in his pocket, watching the dark come up out of the valley like water filling a basin.

He had been a fixer for nineteen years and a soldier before that, in a war whose name changed depending on which government was currently embarrassed by it, and in all that time he had developed only one rule worth keeping: know the man before you hunt the man. Dossiers told you what a person had done. They rarely told you what a person would do. And the difference between those two things was where fixers went to die.

So he did what he always did. He built the man from the pieces.

Theo Vane. Forty-one, probably forty-two. Raised in a mining town that no longer existed—Corliss had driven through its replacement, a strip mall and a memorial cairn, on his way into the valley two weeks ago. Apprenticed at seventeen under a legendary guide named Aldous Wren, hence the alias: men who hid behind borrowed names usually borrowed the name of the one person they still trusted. Certified at twenty-three. Ninety-odd guided ascents without a serious incident. Taught avalanche forecasting at a seasonal school in the Kestrel range. Married briefly, divorced amicably, no children. The wife had remarried a fisheries biologist and lived, as far as Corliss's morning's work showed, in a town with a good bakery and no idea her first husband was alive thirty miles north of her.

And then Kestrel Face. Corliss had read the inquiry transcript twice, once for content and once for tone, because tone was where the truth usually leaked. Six clients and a junior guide dead under four meters of slab. Vane had taken the party up the east couloir against a forecast he later claimed he hadn't seen—a claim the consortium's lawyers had spent a great deal of money making sure stuck. The final report used the word *negligence* eleven times. It used the word *geology* zero times, even though the face that killed those people had been drilled, sampled, and declared stable by a report whose raw data Corliss had personally burned in a fifty-five-gallon drum behind the Blackpine office nine days ago.

That was the irony sitting in the middle of all of it, fat and patient as a toad. The consortium had ruined Theo Vane to bury its own fraud, and now the ruin itself was the threat. Because a man with nothing left had nothing to protect, and a man with nothing to protect was the single most dangerous animal in any mountain range.

Unless he was tired. Tired men were manageable. Corliss had managed many.

He walked back inside, past the field technicians pretending not to watch him, into the room they'd given him—a desk, a cot, a wall map of the upper Reckoning drainage pinned with red flags. His three best men were due back from the narrows by midnight. He had six others rotating through the downstream towns, buying drinks, showing a photograph, asking after a woman with a broken ankle traveling with a quiet man who carried too much rope.

On the desk lay the photograph from the ferry license. He picked it up and studied the face the way he studied faces: not for identity—he had the identity—but for character. The eyes gave him what he wanted. Not hard eyes, not soft ones. Watchful ones. The eyes of a man who had learned, expensively, that the world would hand him nothing, and who had decided to keep moving anyway.

"Mr. Vane," Corliss said to the photograph, conversationally, the way he spoke to all of them before the work began. "You spent fifteen years learning how to disappear. Unfortunate timing. What I need right now is a man who knows these mountains better than my people do—and you're the only one for a hundred miles."

He set the photograph face-down on the map, directly over the Serrano Pass, and weighted it with an empty cartridge casing.

Because that was the other thing dossiers taught you, if you read them the way Corliss read them. A man like Vane wouldn't run downriver toward roads and telephones; roads and telephones were where his enemies lived. He'd run up. Into the high country, over the pass, out the far side—taking the geologist and whatever she carried with him. Four days' climb, maybe five with a wounded woman. Five days of weather window before the season closed and the snows made the pass a grave for anyone on it.

Which meant Corliss had five days too. Better equipment. Radios. Fresh legs. And the luxury of knowing exactly where desperation would drive his quarry, because he had read the same maps, and desperation always chose the same door.

He checked his watch, gathered his men's route cards, and began, with the unhurried precision of a man setting a table, to plan the hunt.

Upstairs, the satellite phone sat silent in its cradle, and somewhere above the tree line, in the cold coming down off the icefields, the snows were already deciding when to start.
