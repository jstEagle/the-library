## 7. Downriver Men

The gorge began where the map gave up.

Theo had known that before he said it aloud. He'd stood at the lip of it three days ago with Ines Okafor leaning on a birch trunk behind him, her wound dressed in strips of his spare shirt and a paste of yarrow he'd chewed like a man who'd done it before, and he'd looked down into the slot where the Reckoning River dropped four hundred feet in white noise and thought: *the map stops here because no one who draws maps has ever needed to come here.*

"They'll expect us to follow the river trail," he said. "It's the only sane route to Salt Landing. Wide enough for horses, sightlines every hundred yards. If Corliss has men between us and the town, they'll be strung along it like beads."

"And we're not taking it."

"We're not taking it."

Ines came forward until she stood beside him, close enough that he could feel the heat coming off her fever. She was three days out from a bullet graze along her ribs, and she walked like a woman carrying a full pail of water in each hand—careful, level, refusing to spill. He'd seen guides with worse wounds quit at the trailhead. He'd seen better men than her die of less.

"Show me," she said.

He crouched and drew in the dirt with a stick: the river bending south, the gorge cutting straight through the shoulder of the mountain, a jagged line he scratched twice to make it look as bad as it was. "There's a route. Kind of. It's not a route so much as a series of mistakes I made when I was twenty-two and being paid by the day."

"You've taken clients through this?"

"No client would survive me bringing them here. I scouted it once, alone, in low water, because a man I was guiding told me there were gold claims upriver and I wanted to know if he was lying." He paused. "He wasn't lying about the gold. He was lying about everything else. But I learned the gorge."

"How long does it take?"

"River trail, two days. Gorge—" He looked at the drop, at the mist rising off the falls like breath off a wound. "Three, if nothing goes wrong. Four if something does. And something always does."

Ines studied the dirt map the way she must have studied core samples, he thought—looking for what was buried under the surface story. "You're telling me all the reasons not to do this."

"I'm telling you the truth. You've had enough people lie to you for one month."

Something moved across her face then—not quite a smile, too tired for one—but she nodded once, and that was the whole of the agreement. That was how they'd been making decisions since the forest: few words, no votes, a kind of grim arithmetic performed by two people who had each already calculated that the other was the only variable left worth trusting.

Theo shouldered the pack. Below them, faint on the wind, he heard—or imagined he heard—a sound that didn't belong to the river. A whistle. Two notes, high, repeated.

Searchers.

* * *

They went down into the gorge in the last hour of light, when the shadows pooled so deep in the slot that the walls seemed to rise around them like a closing hand.

The first mistake—the first of his old mistakes—was a ledge no wider than a coffin, running forty feet along the north wall above a chute of churning green water. Theo went first, back to the rock, testing each foothold with his heel before committing his weight, talking without turning around because he'd learned long ago that frightened people need voices more than they need help.

"Left foot on the gray flake. Not the brown one—the brown one's frost-cracked, it'll go like biscuit. There's a root at chest height, dead but sound, grab it and swing your hips across. Don't look down. Looking down is free, and everything free costs you."

Behind him, Ines's breathing came in short controlled pulls. Once her boot skated on wet stone and he heard the small involuntary sound she made—not a scream, just a syllable of fear bitten in half—and then her weight came solid against the wall again.

"Still with me?" he called.

"Where exactly would I go?"

He almost laughed. It surprised him, the almost. He couldn't remember the last time laughter had even knocked.

They made the ledge and then the talus field beyond it, hopping stone to stone while the river roared below them and the walls narrowed until the sky was a ragged blue ribbon overhead. Theo moved through it all with a fluency that felt less like skill than like memory in the muscles—the body remembering a younger man's routes the way a tongue remembers a language you swore you'd forgotten. Here was the slab where he'd nearly lost the pack. Here the overhang you could walk beneath if you kept your head down and your opinions to yourself. Here, wedged in a seam ten feet up, the rusted iron ring some prospector had driven in thirty years ago, still holding, still waiting for someone mad enough to use it.

He used it. He anchored their single rope to it and lowered Ines down the final pitch into the gorge floor, her boots finding the shingle bank, and then he followed, and when he reached the bottom the walls rose a thousand feet on either side and the river ran fast and shallow over pale stone and the world above was gone.

They camped on the shingle bar. No fire. Cold food. The stars came down into the slot with them, filling the gap overhead so thickly it looked as though the sky had spilled.

Ines sat with her back against a boulder, arm cradled against her side, watching him check the rope by feel in the dark. He was re-coiling it, loop over loop, and she realized he did it with his eyes half-closed, the way other men might pray.

"You've done this a lot," she said. "Lowered people down things."

"A lot."

"And before the gorge? Before all this?" She gestured—at the pack, the rope, the exile-shaped shape of him sitting by a cold camp. "What were you?"

He kept coiling the rope. "A guide. A good one. Kestrel Face, the northern range—I had a reputation. People paid waiting-list money to have me take them up."

"What happened?"

The question hung there. Downriver, somewhere in the dark, the water worked at its stones without ceasing. Theo set the coiled rope down between them like a thing being surrendered.

"There was an avalanche," he said. "Four years ago. Eleven clients, two assistant guides, me. We were on the face when it cut loose above us. I got six people into a terrain trap that turned out to be the one safe pocket on the whole slope. Six lived." His voice stayed level, which took work; she could hear the work in it. "Seven didn't. Including a nineteen-year-old girl whose father had begged me—begged me—to talk her out of the trip, and I hadn't, because she was strong and she'd trained hard and I liked her, and liking her seemed like reason enough."

Ines said nothing. She had learned, in the forest and since, that Theo Vane talked only when nobody asked him to.

"The inquiry found I'd read the snowpack wrong. Or read it right and ignored it—one report said careless, another said reckless. The two countries that license guides both banned me. Her father wrote letters. Newspapers ran my photograph next to the word *disaster*. And after a while I stopped arguing, because arguing required standing still, and standing still was the one thing I couldn't do anymore." He looked up at the ribbon of stars. "So now I ferry supplies under false names and sleep in a different bed every week and tell myself movement is the same thing as freedom."

"Is it?"

"No." He said it flatly, without self-pity, the way you'd report the weather. "But it's faster than grief, and I never learned to walk that slow."

Ines pulled her blanket tighter. The fever was back behind her eyes—he could see it, a brightness that didn't belong—and he made himself note it, because noting things was the only discipline he had left.

"The consortium falsified those reports," she said quietly. "The snowpack studies. The ones the inquiry relied on. They'd been logging and blasting above Kestrel Face for two seasons and they paid a lab to say the slope was stable. Your reading of the snowpack might have been wrong, Theo. But the mountain was rigged. You were blamed for a crime someone else committed against you."

"I know what you're doing."

"Telling you the truth. You said you'd had enough people lie to you."

He was silent a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice had changed—lower, rougher, like a blade drawn partway.

"When I found those documents in the strongbox, do you know what I felt? Nothing. That's the truth. I looked at the name of the company that ruined me printed on a survey cover sheet and I felt *nothing*, and that scared me more than Corliss does. Because a man who feels nothing can be pushed anywhere." He turned to look at her, and in the starlight his face was all planes and shadow. "I don't want justice, Ines. Justice is a courtroom word. What I want is for the running to stop. Those aren't the same thing. Don't confuse them for me."

"They might become the same thing," she said. "If you let them."

He lay back on the shingle and put his hat over his eyes, and the conversation was over, and yet neither of them slept for a long time, and the river carried their silence down toward Salt Landing like cargo.

* * *

On the second morning in the gorge, the searchers found them anyway.

Not their bodies—their smoke. Theo had risked a fire in a hollow beneath an overhang where the draft pulled the smoke up a crack in the rockface, scattering it thin, and he'd judged the risk acceptable because Ines's fever had climbed through the night and cold food was no longer enough. He was right about the smoke. He was wrong about the crack. Somewhere above, the thread of it must have risen into open air, a gray signature written on the sky for anyone on the rim to read.

He heard them before he saw them: a shout, flattened by distance, then another answering it, and the particular cadence of men spreading out along a line, which he knew the way a deer knows the gait of wolves.

"Up," he said, and was already moving.

They couldn't go back—the way behind was a day of exposed ledges, impossible at Ines's pace. They couldn't go forward along the gorge floor; the searchers held the rim, and the rim commanded everything. Theo stood in the middle of the shingle bar with the water roaring past his boots and did the arithmetic of the terrain the way other men breathe, fast and total, and the answer arrived ugly and immediate.

"The narrows," he said. "Half a mile ahead the walls come together and the river fills them wall to wall. There's a shelf above the flood line—two feet wide, maybe three, carved by the water when it ran higher thousands of years ago. We go through there."

"In the water?"

"Above it. Mostly." He was repacking the rope as he talked, cinching straps, moving with a speed that had nothing calm in it. "Can you swim?"

"Yes."

"Don't plan on it. If you go in the narrows, the river will fold you into the rock like laundry. Stay on the shelf."

They reached the narrows as the shouts above grew organized, converging. The walls leaned together overhead until they nearly touched, and the river ran through the gap in a single compressed muscle of white, loud enough that Theo had to press his mouth to Ines's ear to speak.

"Hand on my shoulder. Three points of contact at all times. If I stop, you stop. If I fall, you anchor to whatever I anchored to and you keep going east. You don't come back for me. Swear it."

"I won't swear that."

"Ines—"

"I won't," she said, and her voice had a hardness he hadn't heard from her before, the hardness of a woman who had watched seven colleagues marched away into the trees and had crawled out of a grave of ferns afterward. "I've buried enough people by surviving them. Find another oath."

For a moment, in the roar and the spray, he simply looked at her. Then he nodded, once, the way he'd nodded at the gorge rim, and turned, and led them in.

The shelf was worse than he remembered—or rather, he remembered it honestly and his body had spent four years editing the memory down to something survivable. It ran along the true left wall, slick with spray, sometimes vanishing altogether into seams he had to bridge with a shoulder pressed to wet stone and a foot extended onto holds that existed mostly as suggestions. Twice the river leapt at them, spray hitting like thrown gravel. Once the shelf pinched to nothing and Theo crossed a gap of eight feet on a fallen log jammed between the walls, rotten at one end, and he went across first, tested it with his whole weight, and then talked Ines across with his voice pitched low and even under the thunder, describing each step before she took it, building the crossing in her mind a half-second ahead of her body.

And Ines—clinging to the wall, ribs screaming, fever cooking her thoughts into strange bright shapes—watched him.

She watched the way his fear moved through him and came out the far side as precision. She watched him place her safety ahead of his own with the unthinking constancy of a heartbeat. And she saw, with a clarity the fever sharpened rather than blurred, what the exile had cost him: not his skill, which was intact, enormous, almost frightening—but everything around the skill. The warmth. The jokes she'd been told good guides made. The ease. He guided like a man defusing a bomb, every movement necessary, none of them alive. Somewhere in the last four years, Theo Vane had turned himself into an instrument, and instruments don't get to be held.

*We can fix that,* she thought, absurdly, halfway across a log above a drowning river. *That's the one thing left that can be fixed.*

Then the log shifted, a half-inch of settling rot, and there was no more time for thinking.

They came out of the narrows an hour later, into a widening of the gorge where birch grew from the shingle and the light fell whole and yellow through the opening canopy, and Theo sat down abruptly on a stone as if his strings had been cut. His hands, she noticed, were shaking now—only now, only after. He laughed at them, a short exhale with no humor in it, and shoved them under his arms.

"That," Ines said, lowering herself beside him, "is the hardest thing I have ever done. And I once extracted a core sample from a crevasse wall during a storm to win a bet."

"You climbed into a crevasse for a bet?"

"I was younger and dumber and surrounded by people who loved me badly." She looked at him sideways. "You're very good at that. Keeping people going. Talking them across things."

"It's the job."

"It's more than the job, and you know it."

He didn't answer. Above them, faint now, fading eastward as the search party read the empty gorge and moved on toward easier country, the whistles sounded one last time and went quiet. Theo listened until he was sure, then tipped his head back against the birch and closed his eyes.

"Why did you come for us?" Ines asked. "The camp. The radio went silent and you came. Nobody hired you. Nobody would have blamed you for turning around. Why?"

The question sat between them in the yellow light. Theo was quiet so long she thought he wouldn't answer.

"Because I recognized the silence," he said finally. "After Kestrel Face, everyone went quiet around me. Not cruel—quiet. The kind of quiet that means *you're already gone to us*. And I swore that if I ever heard that silence around somebody else, I'd—" He stopped. Started again, plainer. "I know what it is to be left in it. That's all. That's the whole noble speech."

Ines looked at him—this ruined, careful, stubborn man who had carried her across a river of knives and called it a job—and felt the thing settle in her that had been settling since the forest: trust, forming in motion, the only kind that ever lasted.

"Salt Landing by tomorrow night?" she said.

"If the rest of the gorge behaves. It won't. But yes."

She smiled, and this time it survived the tiredness. "Then let's go meet your friend Marta before Corliss's money gets there first."

Theo opened his eyes. Somewhere downriver, past the last bend of the gorge, the town was waiting—and he found himself wondering, with a dread he couldn't shake, whether the reward posters had already beaten them to it.
