## 8. The Town That Forgot Him

They came down out of the gorge in the last hour of light, and Salt Landing opened before them the way it always had—low and crooked along the river's bend, a scatter of tin roofs and drying nets, smoke standing straight up from a dozen chimneys into windless air. From this height, on the old game trail above the sawmill, the town looked like something left behind by the water: a place the river had considered carrying off and then, in its laziness, decided to keep.

Theo stopped at the edge of the trees and looked at it for a long time.

He had told himself, all the way down the gorge—two days of wet stone and cold spray, Ines's arm over his shoulder, her wound going from angry red to a duller, more dangerous color—that he was bringing them here because it was the only place he knew where no one would ask questions. That was true. It was also not the whole truth, and he knew that too. The whole truth was that some part of him had been walking toward this town since the day he'd left it, the way a man walks toward a door he swore he'd never open again just to stand outside it and feel the handle.

"You've been here before," Ines said. It wasn't a question. She was leaning against a pine with her weight mostly on her good leg, gray-faced, watching him watch the town.

"Four years ago. Maybe five." He shifted her arm higher on his shoulders. "Under a different name."

"What name?"

"Doesn't matter. Nobody used it anyway." He started down through the trees. "They called me the ferryman."

* * *

Salt Landing had forgotten him, or had never bothered to remember, which amounted to the same thing and was, in Theo's experience, the safer condition. The town remembered weather. It remembered the year the salmon ran thin and the year the mill burned half down and the winter the ice took the footbridge. Men came and went through Salt Landing like water through a sluice; the town let them pass without holding any of them.

He came in from the north end, past the fish racks, keeping his hood up and his head down out of habit rather than necessity. Ines walked beside him with a stick cut from alder, refusing his shoulder now that there were eyes to see them refuse it. She had insisted on washing the blood off her jacket in the gorge, and she moved with the deliberate steadiness of someone who has decided that appearing weak is more dangerous than being weak.

The streets smelled of pitch and woodsmoke and the low-tide stink of the river flats. A dog followed them for two blocks, lost interest. A woman hauling a basket of split fish nodded at them the way people nod at strangers here—not unfriendly, just incurious, incuriosity being the town's chief civic virtue.

"The hotel," Ines said quietly.

"No hotels."

"Theo."

"There's one," he admitted. "The Anchorline. It's fine. It's also the first place anyone looking for us will look, because it's the only place." He turned them down a side street, between a chandlery and a shuttered cooperage, toward a lane that climbed away from the water. "There's someone else."

Marta Reyes lived at the top of the lane in a house she had built herself, or built herself twice—the first version had gone into the river during the flood year, and she had hauled the salvaged timber back up the slope and put it up again, higher, farther from the water, as if daring the river to try. She was fifty-something and built like the pilings she used to drive, and she had run the ferry landing's supply post back when Theo had run the ferry, and between those two facts there had grown the closest thing to friendship he had known in exile.

He knocked the way they used to knock—three, pause, two—and heard her boots cross the floorboards before the second knock landed.

She opened the door with a lamp in her hand and looked at him for three full seconds without a flicker of recognition, and Theo felt something in his chest go very quiet and very cold, because if Marta didn't know him—

Then her face broke open. "You old fool," she said. "You look terrible."

"Marta."

"Don't 'Marta' me on my own doorstep. Get inside before somebody sees you, and bring your friend, who is about to fall down."

Inside, the house smelled of cedar and coffee and the particular warmth of a place kept by one stubborn woman against the damp of the world. She put Ines in the chair by the stove, put coffee in front of both of them, and stood over Theo with her arms crossed while he drank, studying him the way she used to study manifests—for discrepancies.

"Ines Okafor," Ines said, offering her hand. "Geologist."

"Marta Reyes. Retired from everything." But her eyes stayed on Theo. "I heard about the survey camp upriver. Whole town heard. Boats went quiet, radios went quiet, then men came downriver I never seen before—quiet ones, well-armed ones, asking questions nobody wanted to answer. And now you walk in my door with a wounded woman and a face I haven't seen in four years." She pulled up a stool and sat, heavily, facing them both. "So. Start anywhere. But start."

He gave her the shape of it, not the details—the camp stripped, the men who took the others, Corliss, the papers, the gorge. Marta listened without interrupting, which was her gift; she could listen the way other people talked, fully and with her whole body. When he finished she was quiet a long moment, turning her cup in her hands.

"And the papers," she said finally. "The ones that say what really happened on Kestrel Face. You have them."

"They're safe."

"They're a death sentence, is what they are." She said it without drama, the way she'd have said *the bridge iced over*. "Theo. Sit down, because you're going to want to be sitting. There's a reward."

Ines looked up sharply. Theo did not move.

"Word came down on the packet boat ten days ago," Marta went on. "Handbills, first, then talk. A man and a woman, traveling together, the woman injured. Five thousand for word of where they're staying. Twenty-five thousand for the pair delivered to certain gentlemen who'll be arriving shortly." She watched him. "That kind of money in this town doesn't buy silence. It buys the opposite of silence. It buys every ear in Salt Landing."

"Who posted it?" Ines asked.

"A company name. Halvard Mineral Group, or some such respectable nonsense." Marta spat into the stove, an old habit that expressed her entire opinion of companies. "The handbill had no signature on it worth trusting. That's how you know it's dirty. Honest rewards sign their names."

Theo sat looking into his coffee. The fire ticked. Somewhere below the lane, the river worked at its banks in the dark, patient as ever.

"How long," he said, "since the packet boat?"

"Ten days. And the gentlemen it mentioned arrived yesterday. Two of them, staying at the Anchorline, polite as church deacons. Been buying drinks and asking after strangers." She leaned forward. "Now here's the part I need you to hear, and I need you to actually hear it, because I know how you get. My brother Emilio drives a hack for them when they ask. He says they're paying cash for information, no questions. Emilio's got debts. Emilio's got a mouth. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That your brother might sell us."

"I'm telling you my brother already asked me if I'd seen anyone. Tonight. At supper. With that look he gets." She reached across and gripped Theo's wrist, her hand rough and strong as rope. "One night. You sleep here tonight, behind a locked door, and at first light I put you both on the mail skiff down to Tern Bay, and you're gone before these vultures finish their breakfast. That's the offer. That's the only sane offer."

* * *

They slept—or lay—in the loft above Marta's kitchen, under the low rafters where cured fish hung in rows like sleeping bats. Ines dozed fitfully, her fever coming back with the dark. Theo lay awake listening to the house settle around them, to the river, to the small honest sounds of a home.

It was the sounds that undid him.

Four years ago he had lived eleven months in this town under the name Daniel Marsh, ferrying supplies upriver, drinking one beer at Marta's counter each evening, fixing her roof each autumn, learning nothing about anyone and letting no one learn anything about him. And somewhere in those eleven months, without noticing, he had come to love the place—not dramatically, not the way you love a person, but the way you love a room you wake in slowly, day after day, until one morning you realize the room knows you. The way the light came down the lane at six. The sound of Marta's broom on her porch at dawn. The smell of the racks in September.

He had left because leaving was what he did, because the name Daniel Marsh had begun to feel warm, and warmth, in his experience, was the thing that got people hurt. He had told himself it was caution. Lying in the rafters now, listening to Ines breathe, he understood it had been cowardice wearing caution's coat—the same coat he'd been wearing for years.

Below him, faintly, he heard Marta moving in her kitchen even at this hour, banking the stove, setting out things for morning. Keeping watch. Nobody had kept watch over him since—his mind flinched from the thought, from the mountain, from the faces—and yet here it was happening again, unasked, unpaid.

*I could stay,* he thought. The thought arrived whole and shocking, like a bird through a window. Not forever. Just—one season. Let Ines heal. Let the hunt move on. Take the ferry work again, the false name, the low visibility. Marta would allow it; Marta had already, in her way, offered it. The town forgot everyone; it would forget him too, and he could live inside that forgetting like a man living in a cave, warm enough, alive, small.

It would cost the others. It would cost whatever truth lived in Ines's papers. It would cost the dead of Kestrel Face their names back. And it would buy him a room that knew him.

He lay in the dark and wanted it so badly his teeth ached.

Beside him, Ines stirred. "You're thinking loudly," she murmured.

"Go back to sleep."

"Can't. Fever." A pause. In the dark her voice came thinner, younger. "What's below us? I keep hearing someone walk."

"Marta. She does that. Checks the doors."

"She checks the doors at midnight?"

"She checks the doors whenever she feels like it. She owns the doors."

Ines made a sound that might have been a laugh. Then, quieter: "You love her. This place."

"It doesn't matter."

"It matters enormously. It's why you almost didn't come, and it's why you came." She shifted, winced. "Tomorrow, when we leave—you'll look back at this town from the water, and you'll think about staying, and you'll hate yourself either way. I know the shape of it. I left Lagos the same way." Her breathing slowed. "The difference is what you carry out. Carry nothing out, and you were never here. Carry something—" she was sliding under, words blurring, "—carry something worth the leaving..."

She slept. Theo lay staring at the rafters until the window over the eaves went from black to iron-gray, and he did not decide anything, because he already had, and knowing it changed nothing about how much it cost.

* * *

First light came up the lane the color of pewter. Theo was dressed and packing the papers tight against his chest when he heard it: voices below, in the kitchen, Marta's low and hard, and another voice answering—a man's, familiar in the wrong way, the way a tune is familiar when you can't place where you learned it.

He moved to the top of the stairs. Through the gap in the boards he could see the kitchen: Marta at the stove with her back to the room, arms folded, and in the doorway a lean man in a rain-dark coat, hat in hand, courteous as a knife laid on a table. One of the deacons from the Anchorline. Behind him, at the gate, a second man waited beside a hired hack—and the hack's driver sat hunched and not looking at the house.

Emilio.

"—last night, ma'am, that's all we're asking," the man in the doorway was saying pleasantly. "Two travelers. Woman favoring a leg. Somebody fed them, somebody bedded them. Towns like this always know more than they say, and towns like this are always sorry later when they don't say it." He set something on the table with a soft clink. The sound of coin. "My employer pays for gratitude."

"My employer," Marta said, "is the Lord, and He's not hiring."

The man smiled. He turned to go—and paused, his eyes drifting upward, casual as weather, to the ceiling. To the loft hatch, closed but not latched.

To the thin line of gray dawn showing around its edge.

Theo stepped back from the gap, silent, his heart going slow and enormous. Below, the front door clicked shut. Boots crossed the yard. The hack rolled away down the lane, wheels loud in the still morning—

And then, faintly, from the direction of the Anchorline, a bell began to ring. Not the church bell. The hotel bell, hammered fast and hard, once, twice, again—the sound of a message being sent, of men being woken, of a hunt that had just been given its bearing.

Marta was already on the stairs, face white, keys in her fist. "Back door," she whispered. "Now. Both of you. Go."
