## 9. Betrayal at the Landing

The rain came in off the river at dusk, and with it the smell of the fish market—brine and diesel and the sweet rot of the day's leavings hosed toward the gutters. Theo stood in Marta's kitchen window with a chipped mug of coffee he wasn't drinking, watching the lamplighters work their way down the waterfront road, and tried to remember why this place had ever felt like safety.

Salt Landing hadn't changed. That was the trouble with towns built on mud and fish: they couldn't afford to change, so they didn't. The same crooked boardwalk, the same cannery with its broken windows boarded like eye sockets, the same church steeple listing east as if it were tired of listening to the sermons. He had lived here for eleven months under the name Daniel Reyes, mending nets and guiding duck hunters up the sloughs, and he had almost—*almost*—believed that a man could be someone else permanently.

Behind him, Ines slept on the daybed, her wounded leg propped on a rolled blanket, her breathing shallow but steady. The stitches Marta had put in her thigh were holding. The fever had broken sometime before dawn. She would live, and she would walk, though not well and not fast, and every hour they stayed in Salt Landing was an hour Corliss's men gained on them.

Marta came up the outside stairs with her boots squelching, shaking rain from her shawl. She was sixty now, gray shot through the black hair she still wore long, her forearms still rope-hard from thirty years of hauling crab pots. She looked at Theo the way she always had—like she was taking inventory of everything wrong with him and deciding, again, how much of it she could forgive.

"You're still here," she said.

"One more night."

"You said that last night." She set down her wet shawl and took the mug from his hands, drank from it herself, grimaced. "You said it the night you arrived, too. *One more night, Marta.* You know what my mother used to say about men who need one more night?"

"I can imagine."

"She said they're already gone. They just haven't told their feet yet." She glanced toward the daybed, lowered her voice. "The girl's leg is bad. Another two days and she'd travel better."

"We don't have another two days."

"No," Marta agreed quietly. "You don't." She turned the mug in her hands, and Theo saw something flicker across her face that he had never seen there before—not in all the years he'd known her, not through the inquiry, not through the years after when she'd fed him and lied for him and never once asked what he'd done. It looked like fear, and it was aimed past him, out the window, at the town.

"Marta. What is it?"

"Nothing." Too fast. "There's been strangers around. Down at the Cannery Row end. Asking questions about the river traffic, who's come up from the Reckoning lately. I told them nothing. But—" She stopped herself, pressed her lips together. "But my brother has been drinking with them."

The floor seemed to tilt a degree, the way a boat tilts when something below the waterline gives way.

"Emil," Theo said.

"Emil," she confirmed, and her voice was flat and hard, which meant she was afraid. Marta only went flat when she was afraid. "He's been telling me for a year I'm a fool to keep the room over the boathouse empty. Rent's rent, he says. And now there's men in town flashing money like it's carnival, asking after a woman with a hurt leg traveling with a man nobody knows, and Emil's sitting with them buying rounds, and he keeps looking at our lights like he's doing arithmetic."

Theo set the mug down. His hands were steady, which surprised him; inside, something old and cold was turning over, the way ice turns over on a river in spring, showing its black underside.

"How much is the reward?"

"I don't know. Enough." She met his eyes. "Enough that Emil wouldn't have to fish again. You know what that means to him. He hates the water. He's hated it since we were children. He only does it because I make him, because somebody has to, and here's a chance for him to buy his way off the boats forever, and all it costs him is—" She stopped.

"Two people he's never met," Theo finished.

"And me," Marta said. "It costs me too. Because if he sells you, and they come, and there's shooting, and anyone dies—the law will come to Salt Landing, and they'll ask whose house sheltered fugitives, and they'll find out about *you*, Danny Reyes, and then everyone I've spent twenty years protecting will know what I've been hiding." She laughed, short and bitter. "You see? Even betrayal has overhead."

Theo crossed to the daybed and crouched beside Ines. Her eyes opened halfway—she surfaced from sleep the way she did everything now, warily, in stages, one hand drifting toward the knife she kept under the pillow even half-conscious.

"What is it?" she murmured.

"We're leaving tonight. Now, before the tide turns."

She blinked at the dark window. "In this?"

"In this. Can you walk to the water?"

Her jaw tightened. He watched her take inventory of her own body the way he'd taught her on the gorge route—honestly, without flinching. "I can walk to the water," she said. "Whether I can walk away from it is tomorrow's problem."

"That's the spirit," Marta said dryly, and began pulling oilskins from a chest.

* * *

They went down the back stairs into the rain, and the town received them the way it received everything—with indifference and a certain damp hostility. Theo carried most of Ines's weight without making it look like he was carrying her; they moved like drunks, two fishermen the worse for drink, stumbling along the boardwalk toward the boathouse where Marta kept her skiff. The plan was simple, the way plans made by desperate people are simple: run the skiff downriver through the dark, past the cannery, past the bar mouth, and put ashore ten miles south where the coast trail started. From there, the range. From there, the pass. Four days' climb if the weather held and five if it didn't, and the dry season ending whether they liked it or not.

Marta walked ahead with a lantern, unashamed, daring the empty street to notice her.

At the corner by the net shed, Theo stopped.

Down the hill, at the Cannery Row end, lanterns were moving. Not the lamplighters—they worked alone, methodical, top of the street down. These lanterns moved in a group, and they were coming up from the water, and behind them, faint but unmistakable, came the sound Theo had hoped never to hear again in his life: the oiled click of a pump shotgun being readied under a slicker.

"They're early," Ines whispered.

"They're not early," Theo said. His mind was already running the map of the town, the way it ran maps the way other people ran sentences. "They're coming from the boathouse. They've already been to the boathouse." Which meant Emil had sold them hours ago, and the men had waited, patient, letting the net close before drawing it. "Marta—"

"I see it." Her voice had gone very quiet. "Back way. Through the market."

"The market's locked at dusk."

"It's locked for customers," Marta said, and there was something in her face now that Theo recognized from the mountain—a guide's face, a person deciding. "Not for family. My key still works. Stalls, gutters, the loading doors on the river side. Go through, come out at the fuel dock, take the skiff from there—no, they'll have watched the skiffs. Take the *Marisol*, old Pelayo's boat, he's drunk by now and won't miss it till morning." She was talking fast, laying it out, and Theo realized with a cold drop in his stomach that she intended to stay behind.

"Marta—"

"Somebody has to be home when they knock," she said. "If the house is empty, they'll tear the whole landing apart looking for you, and they'll find Emil, and Emil will tell them everything because Emil tells everyone everything. If I'm home, I'm a foolish old woman who knows nothing. I've been a foolish old woman for twenty years. I'm good at it." She pressed the market key into his hand and closed his fingers over it. "River side, third loading door. Don't stop for anything. Don't come back for anything. Promise me."

He promised. It was the second-worst promise he ever made.

* * *

The fish market at night was a cathedral of ice and shadow. The stalls stood empty under their awnings, tables scrubbed white, scales hanging like strange fruit. Their boots rang on concrete slick with meltwater from the drains, and the smell was overwhelming—iodine and salt and blood, the whole ocean's history of the town compressed into one long hall. Ines limped between stalls, one hand on Theo's arm, her breath coming in short controlled pulls. Behind them, muffled by walls, voices rose in the street—Corliss's men, spreading out, kicking in the net shed door.

Then the first gunshot.

It cracked flat in the rain, and glass broke somewhere, and a man shouted—not pain, just command—and then another shot, and this one was followed by silence, the particular terrible silence of a small town hearing something it will spend decades retelling.

Theo froze with his hand on the third loading door's bar.

"That's the house," Ines breathed. "That's her street."

"I know."

"Theo. *Theo.*" Her hand closed on his wrist, iron despite the fever's residue. "You go back there, we both die, and the samples die, and everyone on Kestrel Face stays dead for nothing. You know it. I know it. *Go.*"

Every instinct he owned was screaming the other direction. Every instinct he owned had screamed the other direction once before, on a mountain, and he had listened, and men had died, and he had spent nine years paying for listening. He stood in the loading doorway with rain blowing in and the town burning noise behind him and understood, with total clarity, that whatever he chose tonight he would carry forever, because that was the actual weight of being alive: you chose, and then you carried.

He pulled the door open and shoved Ines through into the rain.

* * *

Pelayo's *Marisol* smelled of diesel and herring and sat low in the black water like something sleeping. Theo got the engine on the third pull, and Ines collapsed against the gunwale, and the fuel dock slid away behind them, and Salt Landing fell astern in pieces—first the market roofline, then the church spire, then the orange bloom of fire where the boathouses stood, light rippling on the river like something drowning.

He did not look back. He made himself not look back, and then at the bar mouth, where the river met the sea and the swells came in long and green, he looked back anyway, because he owed her that. The whole waterfront was lit. Figures moved on the boardwalk, small with distance, and one of them stood apart from the others, motionless, at the top of Marta's stairs, and even at this distance Theo knew the shape of her—shawl, lantern, straight-backed, refusing.

The wind took the smoke out to sea. The engine hammered. Ines was crying without sound, tears cutting lines through the grime on her face, and Theo found he could not cry at all, that grief had gone somewhere below speech, down where the pressure lives.

"She knew," he said finally. The words came out of him like splinters. "She knew what Emil would do, and she stayed anyway. She stayed to cover us."

"Yes."

"He sold her too. He sold his own sister for—" Theo's voice failed, restarted, lower. "For enough money to stop fishing."

Ines reached over and gripped his forearm, her hand freezing and fierce. "Then we make it cost more than money," she said. "You hear me? We get these samples over that pass, and we put them in front of the inquiry, and we burn the consortium down to the waterline, and every time they count what it cost them, they count Marta Okafor's name in the ledger. That's the only payment left to collect. So we collect it."

Ahead, the coast trail began where the beach ended, a pale thread climbing into black timber, and above the timber, invisible in the cloud but utterly present, the range waited—the pass, the snows, the four days they no longer had. Theo put the wheel over and ran the *Marisol* aground on the shingle below the trailhead, and helped Ines over the side into the surf, and stood one moment longer in the wash with the burning town reflected in his eyes.

Stopping cost this. He would not stop again.

He shouldered the pack with the samples against his chest and started uphill into the dark, and behind them, far behind, Salt Landing burned like a signal fire marking exactly where he could never go home.
