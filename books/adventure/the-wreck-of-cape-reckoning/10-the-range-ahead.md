## 10. The Range Ahead

Theo woke before dawn with the map already in his mind.

He had spread it out the night before on the flat stone beside their fire—a surveyor's sheet Ines had pulled from her pack, soft as cloth from years of folding, marked in the expedition's own hand with contour lines and elevation notes in three colors of pencil. The Serrano Pass sat at its upper edge like a door drawn shut: a notch in the granite wall of the range at 3,400 meters, the only gap in two hundred kilometers of ridgeline that a man on foot could hope to force. Everything else was cliff and icefall and summits that shrugged off climbers like weather.

Four days' climb from where they lay. Maybe five, if the weather turned.

And the dry season had six days left in it. Maybe fewer.

He lay on his back under the alder scrub and watched the sky go from black to iron to a thin, bruised gray, and he did the arithmetic again because it hadn't changed overnight and he needed it to have changed. Four days up. Six days of season. That left no margin for rest, none for injury, none for the kind of storm that came off the high ice without warning even in the best weeks of the year. It left nothing at all for a woman who could barely walk.

Behind him, Ines was breathing in the shallow, careful rhythm of someone who had learned in the last two days to sleep around her pain. Her ankle had swollen through the boot by evening; he'd cut the laces rather than pull them, and she'd made a joke about it that neither of them laughed at. The gash along her forearm had held its stitches. The bruising across her ribs worried him more than either—she breathed like a man carrying water uphill, guarding one side, and when she thought he wasn't looking she pressed her hand flat against her side as if holding something closed.

He got up quietly, fed the fire down to coals, and looked north.

The range stood against the first light like something carved and waiting. He had spent eleven years in these mountains—first as a boy running traplines with his father, then as a guide who knew every goat track and hanging valley between the Reckoning headwaters and the coast—and he had never once looked at them the way he looked at them now: not as home, not as work, but as a wall between himself and everything he wanted. Or as a road. He couldn't decide which, and the not-deciding sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

The mountain had always been the one thing that never lied to him. People lied. Institutions lied. Reports lied—the thought arrived with its usual heat, the surveys in the strongbox, the falsified readings that had put an unstable snowpack under forty paying clients and a name on a blacklist, and he pushed it down where he kept it, banked like the coals, hot enough to use later. But the mountain only ever told you what it was going to do to you, plainly, in advance, and whether you lived depended entirely on whether you listened.

He was listening now.

* * *

Ines woke to the smell of melted snow and the sight of Theo crouched over the map, weighting its corners with stones, drawing a line with a charred stick.

"Show me," she said. Her voice was rough with sleep and cold. She'd stopped apologizing for slowing them down somewhere on the second morning, which he took as a good sign. Apology was a luxury. So was shame.

He turned the map so she could see it. "We're here." The stick touched a bend in a nameless creek below the last stands of timber. "Serrano's here." The stick moved up through a maze of contour lines to the notch. "The obvious route follows this drainage—" the stick traced a valley climbing northeast, "—which is what anyone following us would expect, and it funnels into a choke point at the tree line where four people with rifles could hold it all winter. So we don't take it."

"Then what?"

"We climb out of the drainage tonight, cross this shoulder—" the stick moved west, over lines packed tight enough to mean steepness, "—and come at the pass from the south flank. There's a bench system above the moraine. Old game trail, mostly. It adds half a day."

She studied the contours, and he watched her read them—not the way a hiker reads a map but the way she'd read the core samples, patiently, looking for what the rock was actually saying. "Half a day we don't have."

"Half a day Corliss doesn't know about. If he's coming up the drainage, we'll hear him across the valley and be gone by morning." He paused. "If he's smart, he's already sent men up the flank. Then we're in trouble either way, and I'd rather be in trouble on ground I know."

"You know this ground."

"I guided tourists through that bench for three seasons. Honeymooners. A banker from the capital who cried at the tree line." Something almost like a smile crossed his face and didn't survive it. "It's good ground. It just isn't easy ground."

Ines looked at the pass again, at the little V of white between the peaks, and Theo saw her do the same arithmetic he'd been doing since midnight, and arrive at the same empty answer. "Five days of dry season," she said. "Four and a half now."

"Then we move like the season's already ending."

* * *

They broke camp in twenty minutes, which was fifteen minutes longer than he wanted and ten minutes faster than yesterday, and then he did the thing he had been turning over all night, the thing that felt less like a plan than like a confession.

"Ines. Before we start—I need to teach you how to walk."

She stared at him. "I've been walking since I was a year old."

"Not like this." He crouched, drew in the dirt with his finger. "You're walking like a town person carrying a pack. You plant your heel, you roll, you push off. That's fine on a road. On scree and wet roots and old snow it's how ankles break. From here on, you walk flat-footed. Whole foot down at once, weight centered, knees soft. Like you're stepping on eggs you don't want to crack but can't afford to drop." He demonstrated, awkwardly exaggerated, feeling like a fool and doing it anyway. "Short steps. Never reach for a foothold—if you have to stretch, the footing's bad, find another. And when it's steep, you go diagonally, switchbacking, even when the direct line looks shorter. The direct line is how people die tired."

She was quiet a moment. "You're saying I'm going to get us killed."

"I'm saying the mountain doesn't care whose fault anything is. It kills the unprepared and the unlucky in equal numbers, and it doesn't check names first." He heard the edge in his own voice and didn't soften it. Softening it would be a lie, and lies were what had gotten everyone on Kestrel Face killed. "I need you alive at 3,400 meters. That means starting now, not at the tree line."

"All right." She stood, wincing as the ankle took weight, and looked at him steadily. "Teach me."

So he taught her, and they moved.

The first hour was the creek bottom, and he made her stop every hundred meters while he pointed things out—not because she needed all of it, but because some of it she might, later, when he was hauling rope or lying broken or simply too far ahead to shout instructions. Where the water ran milky, the banks were glacial silt and would slide. Where the alders grew tallest, the ground beneath was hollowed by meltwater and would give. Crows lifting off a slope meant something had disturbed them—animal or man, and either way worth knowing. He taught her to listen to the river's sound change when a tributary entered it, because in these valleys sound carried farther than sight, and a party moving up the opposite drainage would announce itself hours before it appeared.

She absorbed it the way she absorbed everything: silently, completely, asking questions only when the question mattered. Why did he test the snow bridge with the pole twice instead of once. What the different cracks in the ice meant, which ones were settling and which ones were warnings. Once, near midday, she stopped without being told and pointed at a smear of disturbed gravel on the slope above them, and he felt something turn over in his chest that was almost pride and refused to examine it.

By afternoon she was limping differently—still favoring the ankle, but moving with it instead of against it, her steps shorter and flatter, her weight lower. Not healed. Nothing would heal that ankle in time. But organized. He found himself thinking of her the way he thought of weather systems: here was a condition to be managed, not a problem to be solved, and managing it well was its own kind of respect.

They climbed out of the drainage as the light went long and gold, switchbacking up through the timber on a slope steep enough that conversation became breathing. Twice he caught her counting her steps under her breath—something he did himself without noticing, a metronome against exhaustion—and said nothing, because there are things a guide pretends not to see.

At dusk they came out on the shoulder, and the whole southern flank of the range opened below them, ridge after blue ridge falling away toward the Reckoning country, and above them the bench system he'd promised, pale shelves of grass and talus stitched together by game trails, climbing toward the pass like stairs built for something much larger than goats.

Ines stood at the treeline with her hands on her hips, breathing hard, and looked at it for a long time.

"It's beautiful," she said finally, and there was something in her voice he couldn't place until he realized it was grief. She was a geologist; she read time the way he read weather. "Do you know how old this rock is? Two hundred million years, give or take. It was seafloor once. Everything we're standing on was ocean, and it rose, and it keeps rising, faster than the erosion can wear it down." She shook her head slowly. "And we get four days to cross it."

"The mountain's been patient," Theo said. "We haven't got the luxury."

* * *

That night, camped in the lee of a boulder field with the fire small and screened, he laid out the next four days hour by hour, because a route you can't say aloud is a route you don't really know.

"Tomorrow we take the benches to the moraine camp—here. Water's reliable, wind's predictable, and if anyone's behind us we'll see their fires from there. Day after, we cross the moraine and gain the glacier tongue. That's the hard day. Crevasses start at the tongue's upper end, and we rope up." He glanced at her ribs, her wrist, the ankle she was icing with a cloth full of snow. "Can you travel roped? If you fall, I have to catch you, and if I fall, you have to arrest me. No third option."

"Yes," she said, without hesitation, and then, honestly: "I don't know. But yes."

"That's the right answer. Day three is the icefall below the col—there's a route through it that shifts every season, so we read it fresh, and if it's gone, we lose time finding another, and that's the day the schedule breaks if it's going to break. Day four is the col and the pass itself. We want to hit the pass in the morning light, before the sun softens the slopes above it. Afternoon avalanches. Every day of the year, but worse now."

"And after the pass?"

"After the pass it's downhill for two days to the mining road on the far side, and downhill is its own punishment, but it's downhill." He fed a stick to the fire. "There's a hut at the col. Station hut, old survey service. Might have fuel, might have a stove, might have mice and disappointment. We'll see."

Ines was quiet for a while. The firelight moved on her face. When she spoke, her voice had changed register—lower, more careful.

"Theo. If the season closes early. If we're on the glacier when the storms start." She didn't finish it, which was its own kind of mercy.

"Then we dig in and wait it out, and Corliss freezes along with us, and in spring we walk out thinner and angrier." He met her eyes across the coals. "But that doesn't happen. I'm not going to let it happen."

"You can't promise that."

"No," he agreed. "But I've been promising the mountains things for thirty years and mostly they've let me keep them." He looked past her, up at the pass, a black notch now against the stars, closer than it had been that morning and still impossibly far. "There's a version of me that would have taken the drainage. Fast route, direct, done in three and a half days, roll the dice on the choke point. That version of me is dead, or should be. He's the one who trusted the reports."

Ines reached over and put her hand briefly on his forearm—just pressure, just presence, taken away again. "Get some sleep," she said. "You're teaching again at dawn."

He took first watch anyway, sitting with his back to the warm stone, the rifle across his knees, watching the darkness below the shoulder for fires that didn't come. And somewhere in the second hour of it, with the glacier wind beginning its nightly descent and the stars wheeling over the notch of the pass, he noticed what had changed and almost laughed at how long it had taken him to see it.

For eleven years the mountains had been the place he fled *to*. Every contract, every ferry run, every winter alone in a cabin with a false name—they'd all been ways of getting higher and farther from roads, from records, from the inquiry and the blacklist and the photograph of Kestrel Face that followed him through two countries' worth of guide-houses like a debt collector. He'd told himself he loved the range. Some nights he'd even believed it.

But love wasn't what this was. This was what a man felt for the only witness who'd stayed with him. The mountains had seen everything—the disaster, the exile, the fear—and had never once used it against him. They'd simply kept being themselves: honest, indifferent, demanding everything and offering nothing except the chance to be competent inside them. He had built his whole ruined life on that offer.

Now the range stood between him and freedom, and it was also the only way to freedom, and both of those things were true at once, and that was the mountain all over. Threat and home. It had never pretended to be anything gentler.

He checked the wind, checked the stars, checked the dark valley where a fixer's men might be climbing with better gear and radios and no doubt at all about what they intended. Then he woke Ines for her watch, gave her the bearing to watch and the sound of the wind to listen against, and lay down with the samples' weight—her papers, the duplicate cores, the whole buried truth of Kestrel Face—strapped inside his jacket against his chest, where it rode like a second heartbeat.

Four days. Five at most. The season was closing like a door.

He slept, and dreamed of snow, and woke at first light already moving.

* * *

They gained the first bench by mid-morning, and that was where he saw it: far below and east, at the mouth of the drainage they'd refused, a thread of smoke rising straight up in the still air. One fire. Deliberate. The kind of fire a man builds when he wants to be seen—or wants you to think you're seeing him.

Theo crouched at the bench edge and looked at it for a long time, doing the arithmetic one more time, and this time the numbers had changed.

"They're in the valley," he said quietly. "Which means the flank might be clean. Or it means they want us to think it's clean." He straightened, shouldered his pack, and looked up the long pale stair of the benches toward the pass. "Either way—we climb."
