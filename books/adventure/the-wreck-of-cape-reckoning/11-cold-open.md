## 11. Cold Open

The snow came an hour before it should have.

Theo had been reading the sky all morning—the bruised light over the western peaks, the way the wind had gone from steady to fitful, the smell of cold iron in the air that every mountain man learns before he learns anything else. He'd given them until midafternoon to reach the tree line. The squall gave them eleven o'clock.

It arrived not as snow at first but as a sound, a low hiss moving up the valley behind them like water filling a basin, and then the world went white at the edges. Within minutes the pines above them were ghosts of themselves. Within ten, Ines's tracks were filling in behind her faster than she could make them.

"We need shelter," Theo said. "Now."

"I can keep walking." Her voice was thin. Three days of training her on the move had built something into her legs, but not enough, and they both knew it. She was leaning on her pole the way a tired man leans on a fence.

"You can't. Not in this. You'd walk in a circle inside twenty minutes and we'd both die looking for you." He said it without cruelty, the way you'd report the weather. That was the trick he'd learned about fear: state it plainly or it grows teeth. "There's a cave. Half a mile, maybe less. I used it years ago."

She looked at him through the thickening white, and he saw her do the arithmetic she always did now—weighing his word against her life. He didn't blame her for it. He'd have done the same. But something in her face had changed since Salt Landing, since the fire, since Marta; whatever trust existed between them now was no longer given freely. It was spent, like coin, on necessity.

"Lead," she said.

* * *

The cave was where he remembered it, though the memory had worn the entrance larger than it was. It sat in a fold of granite above the last stand of trees, half-hidden by a curtain of roots and stone, and you could walk past it a hundred times and never see it—which was the point. He'd found it his first season guiding, when he was twenty-three and still believed the mountains were a career rather than a country you could be exiled from. Tour companies used to pay well for a "wilderness overnight" that was really a cave with a fire pit and a view. He'd carried clients' packs up here, cooked them freeze-dried stroganoff, told them stories about the range while they pretended to be pioneers.

The fire pit was still there, ringed with stones gone black with old smoke. Somebody else had used the cave since—there was a scatter of foil, a broken ski pole—but the space itself held its shape, deep enough for two, dry enough to matter.

Theo got Ines inside, got her wet outer layer off her shoulders, got the small stove going under the overhang where the flame wouldn't show. Then he stood at the mouth of the cave and watched the storm erase the world.

It was a proper squall, not the polite kind. The wind came down off the col in long shoves, driving the snow horizontal, and the tree line below vanished as if it had never existed. Somewhere under that white was the route they'd climbed that morning. By morning it would be a different route entirely.

"We're pinned," Ines said behind him. "Aren't we."

"For tonight. Maybe part of tomorrow. Squalls like this burn out fast usually, but this one's got weight behind it." He turned. She was sitting against the wall with her arms around her knees, and in the stove's small light her face looked older than it had a week ago. Everything about her did. Everything about him, probably. "We're alive, warm, and invisible. There are worse places to be stuck."

"There are worse things to be stuck with," she said, but there was the ghost of a smile in it, and he took it.

He melted snow, made them both drink, forced half a ration bar into her hands and watched until she ate it. Small tyrannies of care. Then there was nothing left to do, which was the problem with caves. On the move you could outrun conversation. Pinned in the dark with a storm howling at the door, there was nowhere to put yourself except forward, into what came next, and what came next—in her eyes, patient and unblinking—was the question she had been carrying since the gorge.

She hadn't asked it outright. She was too smart for that. She'd asked everything around it instead: why he knew routes no guide used, why he moved through these mountains like a man walking through his own house with the furniture rearranged, why a supply ferryman with a false name read avalanche slopes the way priests read scripture. She'd assembled the shape of him piece by piece, and now, in the orange flicker, she sat holding the finished puzzle and waiting for him to name it.

"The Kestrel Face," Theo said.

Ines went very still.

"That's what you want to ask me. You've wanted to ask since the gorge. So ask."

"All right." She unfolded slowly, turning to face him fully. "What happened on the Kestrel Face?"

Outside, the wind rose a register, and for a moment neither of them spoke, as if giving the storm its due.

* * *

"First, you should know what they say happened," Theo began. "The official version. Because I've heard it so many times I almost believe it myself."

He sat down across the fire pit from her, close enough that the heat reached them both, far enough that he couldn't see her face too clearly. That helped. Some things you couldn't say to a face.

"Six years ago I was lead guide on a commercial expedition. Four clients, one assistant guide, me. The Kestrel Face is the north wall of Mount Serrano—you've seen it, the big icefield with the seracs hanging over the route. Beautiful line. Popular. Guided parties ran it every season for a decade without incident." He paused. "The consortium's survey reports said the icefield was stable. Stable for another thirty years, their geologists wrote. That's what everyone climbed on top of—that paper. My company's decision to run the route rested on it. The park authority's permit rested on it. Everyone trusted the science because trusting the science is easier than hiring your own."

"And it wasn't stable," Ines said quietly.

"It wasn't stable. They knew. Or someone knew and someone else decided not to know. We found that out later—found it out the hard way, the way you're finding it now." His hands had closed around each other in his lap, knuckles white, and he made himself loosen them. "On the third morning we were high on the face, just below the icefield, when the weather turned. Fast. Faster than forecast. I had a choice. Push for the summit—we'd have made it by noon—or turn back down through terrain I didn't like with weather coming in."

"What did you choose?"

"I chose to push." The words came out flat, rehearsed, which was the only way he could get them out at all. "Every guide makes that call sometimes. Weather windows, client expectations, the sunk cost of being ninety percent of the way there. I told myself the window would hold. I told myself the icefield had held for ten years. I told myself a lot of things."

The stove hissed. In the silence between gusts, the quiet inside the cave felt enormous.

"At eleven-forty the serac field let go. The whole upper shelf, a section the size of a cathedral. It came down the face in three pulses. The first missed us. The second took Elena."

"Elena."

"My assistant guide. Twenty-six years old. She was roped forty meters below me, coaching the slowest client through a traverse, doing my job because I was above her chasing the summit." His voice didn't break. He'd had six years to sand the breaking points smooth. But something underneath the flatness trembled, the way ground trembles after an avalanche has already passed—aftershock, not collapse. "The third pulse buried the lower route. We dug for nine hours. We found her at midnight."

Ines's hand had come up to her mouth. She lowered it slowly. "Theo. The inquiry blamed *you*?"

"The inquiry blamed the guide who chose to push. That was me. And they were—" He stopped. Started again. This was the part, the true part, the part he'd never said aloud to anyone, and it came out of him like a splinter working loose. "They were partly right. That's the thing nobody wants to hear. The report wasn't a smear. The route choice was mine. The timing was mine. If I'd turned at dawn, Elena would be alive. The consortium's falsified surveys created the conditions, yes—they loaded the gun. But I pulled the trigger. I chose the summit over the descent, and I'll carry that until I'm dirt."

"But you didn't know the surveys were false."

"No. But knowing doesn't unmake the choosing. That's what I learned in that hearing room. They asked me one question that mattered—*did you have reason to believe the route was unsafe beyond normal hazard?*—and the honest answer was no, and the honest answer didn't help, because the deeper question underneath it was *would you have turned anyway if you'd been the guide you thought you were?* And I didn't know the answer to that. Neither did they. So they banned me from two countries and called it closure, and I took a false name and started hauling other people's cargo through mountains, because moving was easier than standing still anywhere the question could find me."

He looked up. Ines was watching him with an expression he couldn't parse at first—not pity, thank God, he'd have walked out into the whiteout rather than take pity—and then he understood it. Recognition. She was looking at a man doing to himself what she'd spent her whole career watching institutions do: refusing to hold two truths at once.

"Say it," he said. "Whatever you're thinking. I've earned worse."

"You're punishing yourself for the wrong crime." Her voice was steady, the geologist's voice, the one that read cores and strata and refused to flatter the sample. "You made a bad call in good faith based on data that was deliberately corrupted. That's not murder. That's negligence committed against you *first*. They forged the foundation, then blamed the house for falling. And you've spent six years agreeing with them."

"The report was partly true."

"A stopped clock is partly right twice a day." She leaned forward, and the firelight caught the scar along her jaw where the rope had burned her during the capture—another thing the consortium had given the world. "Listen to me. I've read the original Kestrel Face surveys. I've read them more carefully than anyone alive, because I needed to understand what kind of people we were dealing with. The stability assessment wasn't conservative-with-an-error. It was fabricated. The core logs were copied from a different site. The dates don't match the drilling season. Someone wrote *stable* knowing the shelf was rotting, because a closure order would have cost the access road eighteen months and millions of dollars. They didn't make a mistake, Theo. They made a decision. And then, when the mountain corrected them, they fed the inquiry a scapegoat with a clean conscience and a guilty face, and everyone went home satisfied."

"So I'm innocent."

"You're *wronged*. Those aren't the same thing, and I'm not going to lie to you and tell you they are." She held his gaze. "You chose to push. Own that. I own the fact that I stayed at that camp arguing about sample protocols while Corliss's men were driving up the valley—I could have run sooner and maybe others would have gotten out. We all own our real choices. But there's a difference between owning your choices and wearing theirs. You've been wearing theirs for six years. The blacklist, the exile, the false name—all of it built on a verdict that was purchased. And the men who bought it sent Corliss into the mountains after us, which means they think the purchase is still worth protecting. Which means—" she spread her hands, "—it can still be undone."

Theo sat with that. Outside, the squall slammed the cave mouth and withdrew, slammed and withdrew, like something testing the door.

"You want to know the worst part?" he said finally. "I froze. Up there. After the second pulse, when Elena was hanging and the clients were screaming—for maybe thirty seconds, maybe longer, I don't know, time went strange—I froze. Stood on the ice with my tools in my hands and did nothing. The assistant guide died forty meters below a man whose entire job was to act. That's the truth underneath the truth. The inquiry never even got to that part. I never gave it to them. I gave them the route choice, the weather, the data—all the defensible pieces—and kept the thirty seconds for myself. So don't tell me I'm innocent. I don't even know what I am. I've been running so long I'm not sure there's a man left under the movement."

The words hung in the cold air, and having said them, Theo felt strangely lighter and emptier at once, the way a room feels after the furniture's been carried out.

Ines was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost its argumentative edge entirely.

"In the gorge," she said, "when the search party was above us and you took us down that chute no sane person uses—you didn't freeze. On the glacier crossing yesterday, when the bridge collapsed under the sled, you didn't freeze. You've been making a hundred correct decisions a day since the moment you found me bleeding under those trees. Whatever happened in those thirty seconds on the Kestrel Face, it isn't the whole of you. It might not even be most of you. A man is not one moment. Even a bad one."

"Some moments weigh more than others."

"Yes. But weight isn't identity." She pulled her blanket tighter. "You asked me once, on the river, what justice means when the law failed you. I never answered properly. Here's my answer: justice isn't getting your name back. Your name won't bring Elena back, and you know it, and that's exactly why you've never fought for it. You think wanting your name back means saying her death mattered less than your reputation. It doesn't. It can't." She shook her head slowly. "But there are four other families from that expedition, Theo. And there's the next expedition, and the one after that, climbing on whatever the consortium writes next. Justice isn't yours alone. It never was. That's the trap you've been living in—thinking the verdict was about you, so the fight has to be about you too. It isn't. It's about the paper. It's about the next mountain full of people standing on lies."

Theo stared into the stove flame. Something was shifting in him, some load redistributing after six years, and it hurt the way thaw hurts.

"Elena had a sister," he said. "I've never contacted her. Six years. I told myself it was mercy—what could I say that wouldn't be ash in her mouth? But maybe I just couldn't stand the look I imagined on her face." He laughed, short and without humor. "If we get out of this range, I'm going to write to her. Tell her all of it. The route choice and the thirty seconds and the forged surveys and all of it. Let her decide what I am. That's more than the inquiry ever did. More than I ever did."

"That's the first honest plan I've heard from you," Ines said. "The samples and the testimony will handle the consortium. But that letter—that's the one that matters. Write it."

The wind dropped suddenly, the way squalls do, and in the lull the silence was so complete they could hear snow ticking against the stone outside. Theo crawled to the cave mouth and looked out. The world was still white, still churning, but lower, calmer, the storm settling into its long night shift.

Behind him, Ines spoke again, and her voice had changed—tightened, sharpened, the field voice, the one that meant data.

"Theo. Before you come back inside. Look at the temperature."

He frowned, held his bare hand out into the dark. The air had gone colder—not squall-cold, not altitude-cold. A different quality of cold altogether, dry and biting, the cold that comes down off the high icefields ahead of a front.

"That's not the squall," he said slowly. "That's coming off the glacier. That's arctic air."

"Which means?"

"Which means the dry season just ended early." He turned back to her, and the firelight showed her his face going grim. "That front isn't a day of bad weather, Ines. It's winter arriving. Serrano Pass closes when a system like this parks itself over the col, and this one's parked. We just lost our margin. Every hour we sit in this cave, the pass is closing a little more."

Ines looked at the storm, then at him. "How many hours do we have?"

Theo did the calculation he'd been avoiding since the cold first touched his hand, and didn't like the answer.

"If this breaks by dawn—if it breaks *at all*—we climb tomorrow. No rest day. No training pace. We go straight up, straight through, whatever it costs." He reached for the stove to bank the flame low. "Get some sleep. Both of us. Tomorrow we race the winter."

And far below them, unseen in the white dark, other fires were burning on the mountain—small, shielded, moving upward.
