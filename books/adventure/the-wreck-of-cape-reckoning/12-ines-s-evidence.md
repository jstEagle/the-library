## 12. Ines's Evidence

The storm had passed in the night, and morning came down the valley like something rinsed—light so clean it hurt to look at, snow on the boulders glittering as if the mountain had seeded itself with glass. Theo broke camp first, as he always did, working by feel while Ines slept inside the fly of the tarp he'd rigged against a granite shelf. Her ankle had swollen less than he'd feared overnight, which was its own kind of warning. Swelling meant blood still moving. Blood still moving meant she'd try to walk on it like nothing had happened, and they had eleven miles of broken moraine before the tree line.

He was measuring out the last of the oats when she spoke.

"Theo." Not his name, exactly—he'd given her "Vane" three days ago at the gorge and she'd worn it down to that single syllable since, the way water wears stone—but the tone underneath it stopped his hands. She was sitting up, the sleeping bag pooled around her waist, and she was looking at him with an expression he hadn't seen on her before. He'd catalogued her expressions carefully these past days: pain, stubbornness, the flat gray grief of someone who'd watched colleagues loaded into a truck. This one was new. It looked almost like hope, and hope, in his experience, was the most dangerous thing a person could carry above the tree line.

"Say it," he said.

"There's more than the papers."

He kept his face still. Below them the Reckoning ran high with snowmelt, loud enough that he'd have to raise his voice to be heard over it, but he didn't. He crouched instead, closer, and waited.

"The core samples," she said. "The ones that matter—the ones from Kestrel Face itself, the pre-quake borings. I pulled duplicates. Every critical interval, I pulled a duplicate and sealed it myself, because I've been in this business long enough to know what happens to data that inconveniences people. The originals went back with the expedition's main shipment. The duplicates—" She paused, and he watched her decide how much truth to spend. "I cached them. Station hut, the old survey shelter below Serrano Col. You know it?"

He knew it. Everyone who'd ever guided this range knew it—a tin-roofed box bolted to the rock at nine thousand feet, built by a mining survey forty years ago and abandoned when the seam played out, kept stocked with emergency fuel and stale chocolate by a tradition nobody enforced and everybody honored. He'd put tourists in it once, a pair of bankers who'd wanted to say they'd slept above the clouds. That felt like another man's life.

"You sealed them yourself," he said. "Chain of custody. If anyone challenges the samples, you can testify you handled them end to end."

"I can testify I drilled them, sealed them, logged them, and cached them, and there are photographs with timestamps to prove all four." Something fierce moved through her voice, under the hoarseness. "The papers can be called forgeries, Theo. Anyone with a printer and a grudge can make a document. But you can't fake rock. You can't fake the gas ratios in a frozen core, or the fracture patterns, or the isotopes. The consortium's own lab did those assays. Their equipment, their technicians, their seals. All I did was keep a second copy of the truth in a place where their fixer couldn't burn it."

Theo sat back on his heels. The oat pot ticked as it cooled between them, and somewhere downslope a ptarmigan exploded out of the willow scrub in a whir of white.

He understood immediately what she was asking, and he understood immediately what it would cost, because he'd already run the arithmetic of the route a dozen times in the night, lying awake listening to her breathe. The direct line to Serrano Pass from here was four days if the weather held and Ines's ankle held, which it would not, which meant five, which meant they'd cross with perhaps a day to spare before the season closed. The station hut lay east of the line, up a side valley, half a day's climb each way over ground that would be slow even for a healthy party. Add a day. Maybe two, if the squalls came early, and the squalls always came early now; he'd smelled the season turning in the wind off the glacier two mornings ago, that faint mineral coldness underneath the pine, the smell of the year closing its accounts.

"We don't have a day," he said.

"I know."

"Ines. We don't *have* a day. We have maybe thirty-six hours of margin, and every hour of that is weather we haven't seen yet. If the pass closes with us on the wrong side of it, there's no inquiry, no testimony, no justice. There's just two more names on a list of people the mountain ate, and Corliss doesn't even have to work for it."

"I know what it costs." She said it quietly, without flinching, and that was the thing about her that kept undoing him: she never bargained, never dramatized, never tried to make the price look smaller than it was. She simply named it and stood next to it. "But think about what happens if we go through with only the papers. We get across. We testify. And the consortium's lawyers stand up in that hearing room with a straight face and say the documents were fabricated by a disgraced guide and a compromised geologist seeking to deflect from her own negligence. They'll produce their own copies of the original reports—the falsified ones—and they'll be *cleaner* than ours, Theo, because theirs were typed in an office and ours were carried through a war zone. Paper against paper is a fight they win. Rock against paper is a fight they lose. I didn't cache those samples because I'm sentimental about rocks. I cached them because they're the only thing in this whole rotten story that cannot lie."

The wind shifted, coming up the valley from the north, and Theo turned his face into it out of habit, reading it the way other men read a page. Cold, but dry. High cloud building over the western peaks, thin as smoke, hours away from meaning anything. A window. A small one.

"And if the weather turns while we're sidetracked," he said, "you'll die on a slope you didn't need to be on, carrying rocks for a hearing you'll never attend."

"Yes," Ines said. "That's the trade. I'm telling you I'll make it. I'm asking you to make it with me."

* * *

They walked, and the argument walked with them, though neither of them spoke it aloud again. Some decisions announce themselves once and then live in the silence afterward, growing roots.

The moraine was brutal going. Boulders the size of houses, tumbled by some ancient collapse into a chaos that no map bothered to represent honestly, and between them scree that slid like water whenever weight came down wrong. Theo set a pace calibrated to Ines's ankle rather than to the daylight, wrapping the joint each morning in a compression bandage cut from the hem of her shirt, teaching her—without quite admitting he was teaching her—to place her feet the way he placed his, testing each hold before committing, keeping her weight over her boots instead of behind them. She learned fast. She learned the way people learn when the alternative is dying, which was the only way anyone ever really learned anything on a mountain.

By midday they'd gained the shoulder where the valley split, the main route bending west toward the pass and the side valley opening east toward the hut, and Theo stopped without deciding to stop, because his body had arrived at the fork before his mind had signed anything.

Ines stood beside him, breathing hard, her face raw with wind and altitude, and she did not say a word. She just looked at the eastern valley and then at him, and waited, and the waiting was worse than any argument would have been.

He thought about Kestrel Face. He thought about it the way he thought about it every day, which was not as a memory but as a room he lived in—the white roar of it, the sound the slope made before it let go, a sound like the mountain clearing its throat. He thought about the inquiry, the polished table, the men in good coats reading findings aloud that had been written before he ever walked into the room. He thought about eight years of false names and ferry decks and rented rooms with other people's curtains, the long gray exile of a man who had been convicted not of what he did but of what it was convenient to believe he'd done.

And he thought—if they crossed with paper alone, and the lawyers won, then Kestrel Face stayed closed forever. The dead stayed guilty-adjacent. He stayed a ghost. And somewhere in a glass tower a man who had signed off on falsified reports poured himself a drink and slept fine.

"East," Theo said.

Ines closed her eyes for a moment. When she opened them she nodded once, and said nothing, and he loved her a little for that—not romantically, or not only; it was larger and stranger than that, the love of a man watching someone refuse to make him say a hard thing twice.

He turned up the eastern valley without being asked.

* * *

The side valley climbed steeply through the last of the forest and then out onto bare rock, and by late afternoon they were walking through country that felt older than the world—glacially carved benches, tarns holding sky, lichen in continents of orange and black on stone that had not been warm since the ice left it. Theo had guided here twice, years ago, in the other life. He found he remembered it with his feet more than his eyes: where the trail bent around the hanging meadow, where the stream crossing ran shallow over gravel and deep over slab, where the angle steepened and the air began to cost something.

He also found, with a sensation like a key turning in a lock he'd thought rusted shut, that he was enjoying it. Not the danger, not the fear—those were constant, a low hum beneath everything, the awareness that Corliss's men might be a day behind or an hour. But the movement itself, the reading of ground, the small continuous competence of putting one foot in front of the other in a place that punished error: that part of him had never died in the exile, however much he'd starved it. The mountain had never banned him. Only men had.

"You're different up here," Ines said, at the point where they stopped to drink. She'd said little all afternoon, conserving breath, but she was watching him with that geologist's habit of looking at surfaces and thinking about what was underneath. "Down in the valleys, at Salt Landing—you moved like a man trying not to be seen. Here you move like—"

"Like what?"

"Like the terrain belongs to you and you're checking on it."

"It doesn't belong to me." He capped the bottle. "That's the whole problem with me, Ines. I belong to it. There's a difference, and the difference is that it can do whatever it wants to me and I can't leave it."

"That's not a problem. That's just what it's like to love something that can kill you." She said it lightly, but her eyes went, for a moment, eastward and downward, toward the burned gap in the world where Salt Landing had been, where Marta had been, and Theo understood that she carried the town too, that everyone who'd escaped it carried it, and that grief was the third member of their party and would climb every step with them.

They made the hut at dusk.

It stood on a shelf of rock above the tarn, a low rectangle of timber and corrugated iron, dwarfed by the walls rising behind it into the last red light. From a distance it looked like nothing, a toy abandoned by a giant child. Up close it had the particular dignity of all mountain shelters: battered, functional, indifferent to everything except the task of keeping human heat inside human skin. Theo circled it out of habit before approaching, checked the door seal, checked the lee side where drifts would form, and then got Ines inside and onto the bunk platform and had the little stove lit before full dark, because dark up here was not a condition but a substance, total and immediate and cold.

She slept almost at once, the way the exhausted sleep, falling between one breath and the next. Theo sat by the stove with the door open a hand's width, feeding it fuel sparingly, and looked out at the night.

The stars up here were not decoration. They were a fact so large it pressed on the chest, the whole spilled immensity of it, and below the stars the glacier below the col glimmered faintly, a river of pale light lying in the dark like something asleep. Tomorrow they would go up onto that ice. Tomorrow they would dig into the cairn behind the hut—or wherever she'd cached them; she'd tell him in the morning, and he found he trusted her to have chosen well—and tomorrow the math of the pass would tighten by another day, and the weather would either hold or it wouldn't, and none of it was in his hands, which was, he realized with something close to wonder, exactly where he'd finally decided to leave it.

He had spent eight years believing that safety was distance—that if he just got far enough from Kestrel Face, far enough from the inquiry and the names and the whispering, the avalanche would somehow stop following him. Tonight, nine thousand feet up, one day off the safest route, voluntarily carrying the very evidence that could drag him back into the hearing room he'd fled across a border to avoid, he felt safer than he had in eight years. Because safety had never been distance. Safety was having a reason to be exactly where you were.

Behind him, Ines murmured something in her sleep and turned toward the stove's warmth.

Outside, far to the south, down the long dark throat of the valley they had climbed out of, Theo saw a light. One point of it, steady, moving slowly along the lower slopes where no honest traveler would be at night—too low for stars, too steady for a lantern blown by wind, and moving with the deliberate, unhurried pace of men who knew where they were going.

He closed the stove door until only a thread of orange showed, and sat very still in the dark, counting seconds, watching the light walk the mountainside like a patient finger tracing a map.

They knew about the hut. Somehow, they knew.
