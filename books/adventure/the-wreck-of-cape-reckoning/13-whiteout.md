## 13. Whiteout

The storm did not announce itself. That was the first cruelty of it.

One hour the glacier lay open beneath a sky the color of pewter, and Theo could read its surface the way he'd once read print—crevasse shadows like handwriting, sastrugi ridges running in long sentences toward the col. The next hour the light simply died. Not darkened: died. Cloud base dropped to meet the ice and erased every distinction between them, and suddenly there was no up, no down, no horizon, only a white so total it pressed against his eyes like wool.

"Flat light," he said, and heard how flat his own voice had gone. "Ines. Rope up."

She was thirty meters behind him, following his boot prints by faith alone. He heard her stop, heard the small sounds of her working at the rope with fingers that hadn't been right since Salt Landing—two of them still wrapped, still stiff, though she'd never once complained about them, which was its own kind of information. She clipped in without argument. Four days on the mountain had taught her that much, at least. Four days, and she'd stopped asking why for everything, which Theo understood was either trust or exhaustion, and he'd decided he didn't want to know which.

They moved. Or they moved in the way that whiteout allows—a shuffling, blind man's walk, each step tested with the pole before weight committed, Theo counting paces against the map in his head because the map itself was useless now, folded away somewhere inside a jacket that had soaked through to the skin an hour ago. The wind came off the icefield in long pulses, not strong enough to be dangerous on its own but steady enough to strip what little heat they made, and Ines's breathing had developed a ragged catch on the inhale that he didn't like at all.

He should have called a halt. He knew that. The arithmetic was simple: they were maybe two hours from the station hut, two hours that would become four or five in this, and behind them—somewhere below, somewhere in the same white blindness—Corliss's men were climbing with radios and down suits and the particular patience of people being paid by the day. Every hour they stopped was an hour the gap closed. So he kept them moving, and told himself the crevasse field was behind them, crossed yesterday in clear weather, mapped in his memory, safe.

The mountain does not care what you have mapped.

The rope went slack.

Not gradually. There was no cry, no skid of boots, none of the warnings the books describe. One moment the line between them hummed with Ines's weight moving behind him; the next it lay dead in the snow like something cut. Theo turned into the white and saw nothing—no figure, no hole, no edge—and for one full second his mind refused the obvious answer, offered him instead a dozen reasonable explanations, she's stopped, she's crouched, she's—

"Ines!"

Nothing. Wind. His own pulse, enormous.

He threw himself flat, spread-eagled, driving his axe and both forearms into the snowpack, and only then let the rope run through his hands until it caught—caught with a jolt that slammed the breath out of him and dragged him half a meter downslope before the snow took his weight. Below him, far down in the blue throat of the crack, a voice. Small. Shaped like his name.

"I'm here." His voice came out steadier than he felt, which was the first useful thing about it. "Ines. I'm here. Talk to me."

"I—" A cough. The sound of her breath bouncing off ice, coming back wrong, echoing. "I can't—I don't know how deep. My head hit something. I can't feel my left arm."

*Don't panic. Don't you dare panic.* He said it to himself in Marta's voice, which was strange, because Marta had never climbed anything steeper than the stairs to her shop, and because Marta was dead, and because grief apparently kept whatever parts of a person you needed most. *You've done this. You've done this a hundred times.*

But that was the lie underneath everything, wasn't it. He'd done it a hundred times before Kestrel Face. Since Kestrel Face he had gone out of his way, deliberately, methodically, to never again be the man on the end of a rope with someone else's life running through his hands. Ferry work. Supply runs. Solo travel. Jobs where if someone fell, no one could say it was his fault, because no one else was there.

And now the rope was taut, and the snow under his chest was softening as his body heat worked into it, and forty meters below the surface a woman who couldn't feel her arm was waiting to find out whether the guide who froze had finally stopped freezing.

* * *

"Listen to me." He made himself slow down, made each word land separately. "First thing. Can you move your legs?"

A pause long enough to grow things in. Then: "Yes. I think yes. It's my shoulder—the wall caught me. I'm hanging a little. On the rope."

Hanging on the rope. Which meant his anchor was holding, which meant the snowbridge he'd just learned existed was holding, which meant every second he spent thinking was a second the bridge eroded. He looked at his gear laid out in his mind with terrible clarity: one ice axe, two poles, three screws, and rope—rope that had started the trip at sixty meters and was now, after the gorge, after the fish market, after cutting lengths for anchors and sled hauls, perhaps thirty-five. Thirty-five meters of rope between him and a woman hanging in a hole the depth of which neither of them knew.

"Can you prusik? Can you climb the rope?"

"I don't—" Her voice cracked, and he heard her fight it back down. "Theo, I can't feel my arm. I can't grip."

"Then I'm coming down."

"No." Fast. Frightened-fast. "No, your anchor is me, if you come off the anchor—"

"If I stay on the anchor we're both dead when the bridge goes." He was already moving, uncoiling the rope, measuring it out through his hands. "I'm going to build a second anchor, equalize it, then descend to you. You're going to talk to me the whole time. If you go quiet, I'll assume the worst, and I need you to not make me assume the worst. Understood?"

Silence. Then, smaller: "Understood."

He built the anchor the way his instructor had beaten into him twenty years ago, in another country, another life: two screws, staggered, tied off with the cord he'd been saving for emergencies, load-tested with everything he dared give it. The whole time the whiteout pressed down and the wind spoke its one syllable over and over and somewhere below him Ines kept talking—about her sister in Lagos, about the taste of dust in the dry season, about nothing, about anything, filling the silence because he'd asked her to and because she understood, without ever having been told, exactly what the talking was for.

Then he went over the edge.

* * *

The world inside the crevasse was another country. Blue. A blue so deep and lit-from-within it seemed to generate its own weather, falling in shafts where the surface cracks admitted the dead white light and turning it glacial, submarine, ancient. The walls rose sheer on either side of him, streaked with the black lines of years, dripping somewhere below in a rhythm too slow to be water and too regular to be anything else.

He descended hand over hand, boots against the wall, and the cold down here was different—not the wind's cold but the ice's own cold, patient, geological, the cold of a thing that had been waiting ten thousand years and considered a human lifetime a rounding error. Halfway down he found the blood. A smear on the blue wall, bright as paint, marking where her shoulder had struck.

"Ines. Talk."

"Still here." Wrecked voice. "Still talking."

He reached her at the limit of the rope. She hung against the wall, one arm above her wrapped in the sling of the line, the other hanging wrong, her face gray under the frost in her eyebrows, her lips moving around words he couldn't hear until he got close enough. When he saw her properly—saw the angle of that left arm, saw the blood frozen along her jaw—he felt something in his chest turn over and settle into a new position, the way ballast shifts in a hull.

"Hey," he said. "Geologist."

Her eyes found his. Even now, even here, there was something in them that refused the situation's terms—some dry, furious intelligence taking notes. "You came down," she said. "Your anchor is a dead man's trick and you came down anyway."

"Yeah, well." He got his sling around her chest, cinched it, checked it twice. "I'm told I have poor judgment."

"You're told correctly." But her good hand came up and gripped his jacket, hard, and held.

The physics were ugly and he walked himself through them twice because ugly physics forgive nothing. He was anchored above; she was slung to him; the haul would be his arms, his back, his legs braced against the wall, lifting her body weight plus the friction of the rope plus the margin the mountain always takes. Thirty-five meters of rope meant he'd rigged it short, meant the last stretch would be him climbing with her weight added to his own, and there was no version of the arithmetic where that was comfortable, and one version where it was impossible, and he declined to think about which version the day had chosen.

"On three," he said. "You push with your legs. Whatever your arm does, ignore it."

"Theo." She stopped him. Down there in the blue, with the drip keeping its slow time below them, she looked at him with an expression he'd learn later she saved for core samples that contradicted the accepted record. "Kestrel Face. What actually happened. Tell me while you pull."

"What?"

"You heard me. You want me to climb? Give me something to climb toward. Truth works. I'm a scientist. I collect it."

For a moment he almost laughed, and the laugh would have broken something, so he didn't. He set his boots against the wall. He took the rope in his ruined, burning hands.

"It was my call," he said, and hauled. "The route. I chose it. Everyone said wait a day for the snow to settle and I said go, because the clients had paid for the window, because I wanted the summit record, because—" haul, breathe, boot, hold "—because I was twenty-eight and stupid and I thought the mountain owed me. And when it broke, when the face let go—" haul "—I froze. Four seconds. Maybe six. People below me screaming and I stood there holding a rope I could have cut to swing them clear, and I froze, and Daniel Reyes died because of those seconds. That's the true report. Everything else was decoration."

He hauled. She pushed with her legs. The rope burned through his palms and the wall gave them both footing and inch by inch the blue country released them upward.

"And the inquiry?" she gasped.

"They needed a story that fit. Guide error fits. Consortium money had already bought the geology report that said the face was stable—it said that, Ines, your surveys prove it said that—and so when it collapsed they needed the failure to be human, not geological. They needed a man to blame. I was convenient. I'd frozen. I was guilty of something, just not the thing." Haul. Breathe. The light above them thickening. "So we're both liars' leftovers, you and I. They falsified the mountain and they falsified me, and the two lies held each other up for eleven years."

"Like a bridge," she whispered.

"Like a bridge." He looked up. Above them the snowbridge they'd fallen through showed as a ragged star of white against the blue, thin as paper at its edges, and beyond it the whiteout waited, and beyond that the hut, and beyond that Corliss, and beyond that a hearing room in a country that had banned him. "Now push. We're going through."

The last five meters he climbed with her strapped to his chest, her good arm locked around his neck, her breath sobbing against his collar, his shoulders screaming, his hands past pain into some white-hot country of their own. At the lip the rope ran out and he drove his axe into the surface snow and walked himself up the shaft of it, one step, another, the weight of two lives on a tool's worth of purchase, and the guide who froze held—held, and held, and held—until his knee found solid snow and he rolled them both onto the surface and lay there in the roaring white with her heartbeat hammering against his ribs like something rescued.

* * *

He destroyed the rope getting them to the hut.

There was no elegant way to tell that story, so he never tried. The truth was that the last anchor cord went into the haul system, and the last sound length of rope went into the improvised litter for her arm, and by the time the hut's black shape materialized out of the murk—two hours later, an eternity later, found by dead reckoning and prayer and Ines navigating off rock striations visible only to her—Theo Vane was carrying thirty centimeters of rope in his pack and nothing else, and both of them knew what that meant. No second rescue. No margin. From here to the pass, any fall was final.

Inside the hut, with the door wedged shut and the little stove ticking, he splinted her arm properly and fed the stove their last reserve of fuel and watched color come back into her face by degrees. She sat against the wall with the splinted arm cradled like something borrowed, and studied him across the small orange room.

"You could have cut me loose," she said quietly. "Early. Before the descent. Do the math, save yourself. Any sensible person—"

"I'm not sensible. I'm a man who froze once and has been paying interest ever since." He fed the stove another block. "Today the debt moved."

Outside, the wind changed its note—dropped, steadied, took on the low freight-train register he knew too well. He crawled to the window slit and looked north, into the white, and what he saw there made the cold in the room seem generous.

Lights. Far down the glacier, faint as stars fallen into the ice, but moving. Three of them. Climbing.

Headlamps. Someone was ascending through a whiteout that would kill an experienced party, on radios, on a schedule, closing the gap he'd bought with a rope and nearly his life.

Corliss hadn't stopped for the storm.

Theo turned back to Ines, and the look on his face must have told her, because she was already reaching for her boots.

"How long do we have?" she asked.

"Less than the night," Theo said. "Maybe less than that."
