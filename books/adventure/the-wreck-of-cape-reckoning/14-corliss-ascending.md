## 14. Corliss Ascending

The fire at Salt Landing had been visible from the water, a smear of orange on the black shoulder of the coast, and Corliss had watched it burn from the deck of the *Marta's Pride* with the particular stillness of a man doing arithmetic.

Three buildings, not the whole town. The boarding house where the woman Marta kept her rooms. The chandlery where the brother worked. A shed that had been standing too close to the chandlery to matter. The fire was not the point. The fire was a message, and messages were cheap; the point was what the brother had given them before the fire, and what the brother had given them was a name, a bearing, and a day.

*Serrano Pass. The high station hut. She said she had something cached at the hut.*

Corliss stood at the rail while the town's smoke leaned out over the water and thought about the shape of the thing he was hunting. Two people. One of them a geologist with a bullet-grazed shoulder who had spent four days in the forest eating what she could dig. The other was Theo Vane.

He turned the name over the way a man turns a stone in his palm, feeling for the crack.

Vane. Kestrel Face. The inquiry that had stripped him of his certification in two countries. Corliss had read the file twice on the flight north, and the file told him two things: that Theo Vane had been a very good guide, and that Theo Vane had been a very frightened one. The inquiry had found a man who froze. Men who freeze are the easiest kind to bury, because the ground is already dug. His employer had said as much, in the flat voice he used for costs: *He's already discredited. Nobody will believe him. Bury him twice and the second grave costs nothing.*

But Corliss did not work from files. He worked from weather, and the weather had changed.

* * *

He gathered them at dawn in the chandlery's scorched yard, the nine men who remained of his party, standing among the wet black timbers with their breath smoking. He had sent two downriver to close the water route and one to the telegraph office in the next town with money and instructions, and that left nine, and nine was enough. It had been enough for the survey camp. It would have been enough for Salt Landing if the Landing had been the objective rather than the errand.

"The informant says the woman told her brother about a cache at the high station hut," Corliss said. "Below Serrano Pass, on the western approach. The samples she kept out of the strongbox—the ones that matter, the ones that would hold up—are there, or she believes they are. Either way, she'll go for them. She has to. Papers burn. Stone doesn't."

He let that sit. The men were good in the way of hired men: they listened for what affected them and let the rest slide past like water around pilings.

"Vane is taking her over the pass. He has maybe a day on us, less if her shoulder slows her, and it will. We go up the eastern approach. It's steeper, but it holds snow later in the season, which means it holds a trail, and it puts us above them by the col." He unfolded the survey map on a crate, weighting the corners with stones, and drew the line with a charcoal stub. "We don't need to catch them on the ice. We need to be standing at the hut when they arrive, or between them and the pass when they leave it. High ground and patience. That's the whole trade."

One of the men—Doyle, broad and slow-talking, who had been a miner before he'd been other things—spat into the ashes. "And if they've already passed the hut?"

"Then we follow them onto the glacier and we take them on the ice, where there is nowhere to run and nowhere to hide and the cold does half our work." Corliss rolled the map. "But they haven't. The woman can't move fast enough, and Vane won't leave her. That's not a guess. That's the shape of the man."

He said it with certainty, and the certainty was real, but underneath it ran a colder current he did not share. Because the file said *frightened*, and the file said *froze*, and yet the report from the gorge said a man had taken two of his party down a route no living guide used, a route with a name the local boatmen wouldn't say aloud, and had brought a wounded woman through it in the dark. The report said the two men had come back quiet and would not talk about it except to drink. Frightened men did not do that. Frightened men did not burn a strongbox's worth of their own past to keep a stranger warm, which was what the informant swore she'd seen in the boarding house—the guide feeding the fire from a leather satchel of papers, old ones, his own, feeding them in page by page like a man paying a debt.

Corliss had learned long ago that the file was the story the world told about a man. The weather was the story the man told about himself. And the weather over Theo Vane had turned.

* * *

They went up through the forest in a loose file, and Corliss climbed the way he did everything, without hurry and without mercy, metering his breath, letting the young ones burn themselves out ahead of him and fall back sullen. The eastern approach was ugly going—deadfall from some old blowdown, then a scree fan that moved underfoot like loose teeth, then the treeline, ragged and final, where the trees gave up all at once as if a line had been drawn and agreed upon.

Above the trees the world went white and enormous. Serrano Pass hung in the distance like a door left ajar in the sky, and below it, a dark speck on a shoulder of rock, the station hut.

Corliss stopped there and let the men catch up, and while they caught up he read the mountain.

It was not reading he'd been born to. He'd grown up in flat country, in a town whose horizon was a fence line, and he had come to mountains late and without affection, the way a man comes to a language he needs for business. But he had learned to read them the way he'd learned everything else—by paying for the lessons. He could see now what the season was doing. The light had that hard, thin quality it gets when the air is drying out of it, and the peaks above the pass wore their snow like old paint, patchy, thin, the rock showing through. Dry season. Another week of it, maybe less. Then the first storms, and after the first storms, the pass would close like a mouth.

His employer's timetable and the mountain's timetable had converged, which was the kind of luck Corliss did not believe in and therefore did not trust.

"Radio check," he said, and the man named Pell, who carried the set, unwrapped it from its oilcloth and cranked it, and the set hissed and spat and found its voice.

They were on a frequency the consortium's people used across three districts, and the check went out, and the answer came back thin through the static: *Copy, Corliss. Position?*

"Treeline, eastern approach. We move on the hut tomorrow, camp tonight at the moraine." He paused. "Anything on the water?"

*Boat went downriver two days ago. Nobody's seen them since the Landing.* A pause, and then, because the man on the other end had been drinking or was simply careless: *Word is the guide burned his own papers before they left. Marta's brother watched him do it.*

Corliss looked at the smoke-stained yard below them, invisible now under the trees, and felt the cold current move again.

A man who burned his own papers was a man who had decided the past was a weight, and men who decided that were men who had decided to stop running. Corliss had buried runners before. Runners were simple. You cut the road behind them and waited, and they came apart on their own like wet rope. But a man who had stopped running was a different animal. A man who had stopped running had picked a place to stand, and a man who had picked a place to stand would die there before he gave up the woman or the stone in her pack.

He found, examining himself with his usual honesty, that he did not mind. It made the work cleaner. Sentiment was the expensive part of this trade, and Vane's refusal to run removed most of the sentiment. There would be no chase to the last. There would be a place, and a time, and two people in it, and nine men coming down on them from the high ground with the weather closing like a fist.

"Tell the boat to stand off the river mouth," he said into the set. "If they come down, we take them at the road. If they don't—" he looked up at the pass, at the thin snow, at the door ajar in the sky— "they won't."

* * *

They made the moraine by late afternoon, a grey rampart of old glacier debris below the hut's shoulder, and Corliss called the camp into the lee of a boulder the size of a chapel and set the watch. The men were tired and did not hide it well. Doyle sat rubbing his feet and complaining about the altitude in the low voice of a man who had complained about every altitude since the coast, and Pell fussed the radio like a hen, and the two who'd come back from the gorge—Rask and the boy, Ansel—sat apart from the others, the way they had sat apart since the gorge, and said nothing.

Corliss went and sat near them, because that was a thing he did: he went to the quiet ones.

"The gorge route," he said. No question in it.

Rask looked at the fire, or at the place where a fire would have been if Corliss had allowed one. "It's a bad place," he said.

"I know it's a bad place. I want to know what it did to you."

Silence. Then the boy, Ansel, younger, still with river mud dried in the seams of his cuffs: "He knew it. That's the thing. He knew every turn of it in the dark. He'd put his hand on the rock and know where the next hold was without looking. Like he'd been there before. Like the rock was—" the boy searched— "like the rock was a house he used to live in."

Corliss nodded slowly. In the file, the inquiry had noted that Theo Vane had guided the Reckoning gorge commercially for six years before Kestrel Face, that he had run it in conditions that got other guides to turn back. The file had filed this under *recklessness*. Corliss filed it under *knowledge*, and knowledge was heavier than recklessness and harder to bury.

"And the woman?"

"Quiet," Rask said. "Hurt, but quiet. He carried her pack and half of mine when mine went in the water. Didn't say ten words the whole time." Rask's jaw worked. "He looked at us once. At the landing. Just once. I've been looked at before, in this line. Men look at you to see how scared you are." He shook his head. "He wasn't checking. He was measuring. Like we were terrain."

Corliss sat with that for a while, watching the light die off the pass.

The employer had said *easy to bury twice*, and the employer was a man who read files the way other men read scripture, completely and without comprehension. The employer had never stood on a moraine at the end of the dry season with a radio in his lap and a boy's testimony in his ear. The employer thought Theo Vane was a document. Corliss knew better. Theo Vane was a mountain that had been mis-surveyed, and men who mis-surveyed mountains ended up in them.

It didn't change the work. That was the thing people got wrong about him, that he enjoyed the work or hated it or feared it. He did none of those. He did the work. But it changed the arithmetic, and arithmetic was the whole of the trade, and so he sat up past his watch, in the cold, redoing it.

Nine men. High ground. A hut they could hold or burn or simply wait inside, because the woman's shoulder would fail before her will did, and when it failed Vane would stop, and a man who stopped on the ice in the last days of the dry season was a man negotiating with the weather, and the weather always won.

And yet.

And yet the boy's voice, *like the rock was a house he used to live in*, kept returning, and Corliss kept turning it, because in eleven years of this work he had learned that the detail you wanted to discard was always the detail that killed you.

* * *

Near midnight, Pell woke him.

"Signal," Pell said. "Weak. It's the survey frequency—somebody's keying it. Short bursts. No voice."

Corliss took the headset and listened. Through the static, faint as a pulse under a wrist: three clicks, a pause, three clicks. Again. Again.

"Directional?" he asked.

"Too weak. Could be the woman's set, half-broken, could be anything. Could be nobody."

Could be a signal to someone. Could be a signal between the two of them and some third party on the water. Could be nothing at all—a broken switch, a dying battery, the mountain talking to itself. Corliss held the headset to his ear and listened to the small patient ticking out of the dark, three and three and three, and thought: *a man who burned his papers, a man who knows the rock like a house, a man who measures instead of fears.*

He thought: *what would I do, if I were him, and I knew I was followed?*

He would watch the fires. He would count the men. He would know nine was too many to fight and too many to evade on a glacier, and so he would not fight and he would not evade. He would do what a guide does. He would change the ground. There were old survey charges in the abandoned workings below the col—Corliss had passed the magazine himself that afternoon, rusted shut, and had thought nothing of it, and now he thought of it, and the thought went through him like cold water through a boot.

He took off the headset.

"Pell. In the morning, before we move on the hut, I want two men down at the old workings below the col. Quietly. If any of the survey explosive is missing, or the magazine's been opened, I want to know before we're on the ice."

Pell blinked. "You think they'd—"

"I think," Corliss said, settling back against the boulder, pulling his hood up, "that we are not hunting a runner anymore. Get some sleep. Tomorrow we take the hut."

Above them the pass stood open against the stars, thin snow on its shoulders, the dry season spending its last days, and somewhere below and ahead, in the dark between the moraine and the door ajar in the sky, three clicks and a pause, three clicks and a pause, patient as a man counting the steps of someone climbing toward him.
