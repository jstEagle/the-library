## 15. The Hut

The storm that had chased them off the glacier spent itself in the night, and morning came down the valley like a held breath finally released. The air was scoured clean, so clear that Theo could see the station hut from two miles out—a brown speck wedged into the shoulder of the ridge, no bigger than a thumbnail against all that white. It looked harmless. Everything at a distance did.

Ines walked behind him on the rope, and he could hear her breathing in the way he'd taught her to breathe—measured, deliberate, each exhale pushed out like she was wringing something from herself. Three days of it now. Her frostbitten fingertips wrapped in strips of his spare shirt, her hip bruised black from the crevasse fall, and still she climbed. He had stopped asking if she could go on. Asking was an insult to what she was already doing.

"There," she said, when they paused at a rock rib for water. Her voice came out flattened by wind and exhaustion. "That's it? That's really it?"

"That's it."

"It's smaller than I remembered."

"Everything is." Theo screwed the cap back onto the flask and handed it up the rope. "Huts are built small so they survive. Big things come apart in wind."

He didn't say what he was thinking, which was that the hut sat wrong. He'd been reading mountains long enough to know when a landscape felt inhabited—the particular stillness of a place where someone was watching from cover. Birds told you. Choughs would mob a hidden man; ptarmigan went silent in a way that wasn't natural silence. And there was another thing, simpler: the hut's door. From this distance he couldn't be certain, but he thought it stood a few inches open, and nobody who'd sheltered here last night—not them, not anyone—would have left it that way.

He weighed telling Ines. Decided against it. Fear traveled downhill faster than rockfall, and if there was nothing, he'd have spent her courage for nothing.

They climbed the last mile in the long blue shadow of the ridge, Theo picking a line that kept them below the skyline, using a shallow gully that approached the hut from the blind side. His hands remembered this ground even though his mind didn't want them to. He had guided tourists here eleven years ago—soft-handed accountants and honeymooners from the coast, all of them gasping at the altitude, all of them delighted by the iron stove and the bunk frames and the view down onto the icefield. He'd boiled tea for them on that stove and told them the mountain was safe as houses.

The Kestrel Face had been visible from the hut's front window. He'd pointed it out with his arm around a stranger's shoulders.

* * *

The door was open four inches. Up close, there was no mistaking it.

Theo stood with his back against the hut's outer wall, snowshoes off, boots silent on the packed timber of the porch boards. Ines crouched beside him, and when he put a finger to his lips she nodded once, her eyes going wide and then deliberately narrow, the fear being folded away somewhere and replaced with something harder. She'd learned that trick fast. He hated that she'd had to learn it at all.

He eased his head past the doorframe.

Inside: the single room, twelve feet by sixteen, the stove cold, the bunks stripped of their mattresses years ago by some previous party or by weather. Two windows, one facing the glacier, one facing the pass. A table bolted to the floor. And under the third bunk from the wall, half-hidden by shadow—

A man.

Not hiding well. Hiding adequately, which was worse, because it meant he wasn't trying to be invisible. He was waiting to be found late rather than never. He lay on his side with a rifle cradled against his chest, and he was awake—Theo could see it in the eyes, open and tracking—and he made no move at all as Theo's head appeared in the doorway.

"Gonna come in slow," the man said. His voice was flat, unhurried, the voice of someone being paid by the day. "Slow, and empty your pockets on the table. I know you got the papers on you. Boss says the girl's got samples cached here too. We'll take both and everybody walks home happy."

Theo's mind moved with terrible clarity, the way it always did now when things went wrong—the old curse of the disaster-trained, the ability to think perfectly while everything burned. Rifle. Bolt-action, older model, scope gone or removed. Muzzle currently angled toward the door but not shouldered—if the man fired from lying down through a doorway at this angle, he'd have maybe a forty percent chance, and men paid by the day did not gamble forty percent on the first shot. The room was small. The table was between them. The stove sat three feet from the bunk, cast iron, and the poker—

The poker was gone. Of course it was gone. Corliss's people swept terrain before they occupied it.

"Ines," Theo said quietly, without turning his head. "Walk back down the gully fifty meters and wait."

"No," Ines said.

"Ines—"

"I said no." Her voice cracked across the room like a whip, and the man in the bunk shifted, just slightly, the rifle coming up an inch. "I've run from enough of you people. I'm done running. Get the samples and let's finish this."

The man laughed softly. It was not a cruel laugh. That somehow made it worse. "Boss said the guide might be trouble. Said the geologist definitely would be." He rolled up onto one elbow, unhurried, keeping the rifle's line steady. "Name's Dekker. Nothing personal about any of this. You understand how it is."

"I understand how it is," Theo said.

And he stepped inside.

* * *

Later—in the years after, in the nights when sleep came thin—he would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and find that memory had taken most of them for itself. What remained was fragments, sharp and unconnected as broken glass:

The rifle swinging toward him and Theo already moving left, because right was where the man expected him to go, because everyone went right.

The shot going past him close enough to feel the air move, the report enormous in the little room, splinters jumping off the doorframe where his head had been.

Getting inside the rifle's arc—that was the whole thing, that was everything, a rifle was a club at three feet and the man knew it and dropped the gun's weight low to swing it and Theo took the stock on his forearm instead of his skull, heard something in the arm go wrong, felt the pain arrive a half-second later like news from a distant country.

The two of them going down together onto the floorboards, and the smell of the man—tobacco and gun oil and wool—and his knee driving into Theo's chest, and Theo's own hands doing things he had never trained them to do, never needed to, finding the man's collar and the edge of the bunk frame and using the frame, hammering the back of the man's head against the iron corner once, twice—

Dekker making a sound Theo had never heard a human being make and hoped never to hear again.

Then the man's hand, gloved, finding the knife at his belt, and Theo's hand finding it first, and the world narrowing to a single hot point where the blade was, and the understanding arriving not as thought but as fact, the way gravity arrives, the way cold arrives:

One of us stops moving here.

* * *

It was over in less time than it takes to tell. It was over, and Theo knelt on the floorboards of a hut he'd once filled with tourists' laughter, and his hands were red to the wrist, and Dekker lay still beneath him with his eyes open at the ceiling and his chest barely lifting, and then not lifting at all.

The silence afterward was total. Even the wind seemed to hold itself outside the walls.

Theo stayed kneeling. He was aware, distantly, of Ines in the doorway, of her breath going ragged, of her saying his name twice before the sound reached him. He was aware of the pain in his forearm, deep and structural, the bone probably cracked, the arm beginning to swell inside his sleeve. None of it mattered. What mattered was that he had killed a man, and that it had not felt like anything, and that this—more than the blood, more than the body—was the thing that frightened him.

He had expected horror. Horror would have been proof he was still somebody who shouldn't do this.

What he felt instead was arithmetic. Cold, simple, finished. One of us stopped moving, and it wasn't me, and tomorrow I will climb higher because of it, and I cannot find the part of myself that mourns.

"Theo." Ines was beside him now, kneeling, her bandaged fingers hovering near his shoulder without landing. "Theo. Look at me."

"He was waiting for us," Theo heard himself say. His voice sounded like someone else's. "Corliss sent him ahead. Which means Corliss knows about the cache, which means—" The sentence found its ending on its own, and the ending was very bad. "Which means the informant talked. Marta's brother gave up more than our location. He gave up everything."

Ines went still. Then, with a steadiness that shamed him, she reached past him, under the third bunk, and drew out the oilcloth bundle that had waited eleven years in the dark—no, five days, only five days, though it felt geological—and unwrapped it on the table. Core samples in sealed tubes, labeled in her careful hand. Physical truth, drilled out of the mountain itself. Beside them she laid the documents from the strongbox, the falsified reports, the consortium's signatures, the paper trail that led backward through the years to Kestrel Face and to the inquiry that had ended Theo's life and called it procedure.

"This is why," she said. "Whatever it cost to get here—this is why. They can't bury this. Not now. Not with both of us carrying it."

Theo looked at his hands. He wiped them slowly on his trousers, though the red had already soaked through, and stood, and tested his arm, and set the pain aside the way you set down a pack you'll have to pick up again anyway.

"We have maybe two hours before Corliss's main party reaches the col," he said. "Less, if they're moving light. We take the samples and we climb."

"And you?" Ines asked quietly. "Are you climbing with us, or are you still back there on the floor?"

Theo looked at the body, and made himself keep looking. He would not look away from it. Looking away was how men like Corliss lived—everything ugly pushed out of sight, handled by other hands, deniable. If this was what justice cost outside the law, then he would pay it with his eyes open.

"I'm climbing," he said.

They worked without speaking. He searched the body methodically—ammunition, a radio, a map with the col marked and a route line drawn in confident pencil. The radio was the real prize: Corliss's own channel, their own positions, if the codes were simple enough. Theo turned it over in his good hand and felt the shape of the next hours assembling themselves, terrible and necessary.

Outside, the light on the glacier had turned the color of brass. Somewhere beyond the ridge, faint as a heartbeat, he could almost believe he heard engines—or men, or wind, or only the mountain getting ready.

He slung the sample bundle across his chest, where it rode against his heart like a second pulse, and led Ines out of the hut without looking back.

Behind them, the door stood open to the cold, and would stand open until the snows came and closed it for the winter, over everything.
