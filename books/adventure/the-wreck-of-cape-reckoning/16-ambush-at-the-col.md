## 16. Ambush at the Col

The col announced itself before they saw it—a notch of pale sky between two shoulders of black rock, wind screaming through it like something caged. Theo had been listening to that wind for an hour, and what it told him he did not like.

He stopped where the glacier bent around a rib of moraine and let Ines catch up. She was moving better than she'd had any right to four days ago; the training had taken, her body remembering what her mind had never learned to quit. But she was still limping on the bad ankle, still breathing in threes when the grade steepened, and they were behind schedule by half a day.

"What is it?" she asked. She'd learned to read his silences the way sailors read water.

"Wind's wrong." He turned his face into it, letting the cold press his eyes to slits. "Should be spilling down off the pass from the northwest. It's coming across the col laterally. Means there's a gap in the ridge somewhere I don't know about, and air finds gaps the way water finds cracks."

Ines looked up at the notch, squinting against the glare. "Meaning someone could be waiting there."

"Meaning someone *is* waiting there." He said it flatly, without drama, because drama cost calories and they were running low on everything. "Corliss lost six men to the avalanche at the icefall. That leaves him eight, maybe nine, plus himself. He knows we can't go back down—the lower route's sealed behind us, same as we sealed it. He knows the only way out is over Serrano Pass. And he knows the only approach to the pass funnels through this col."

"So we're rats in a pipe."

"We're rats who chose the pipe." Theo unslung his pack and knelt, working the straps with fingers gone clumsy in the cold. "Which means we get to choose where the trap springs."

* * *

They'd found the charges two days earlier, cached in a rusted ammunition box beneath the old survey station—blasting gelatin and detonators left behind by the consortium's first survey teams, years out of date but packed in wax and grease the way men do when they expect to come back for them. Ines had wanted to leave them. *We're not soldiers,* she'd said. Theo hadn't argued. He'd simply packed them, and she'd watched him do it, and neither of them had spoken about it again.

Now he laid them out on the moraine: eleven sticks of gelatin, sweating slightly even in the cold, and the coil of det cord, and the mechanical timers, crude brass things that ticked audibly if you held them to your ear. He'd been carrying them distributed through both packs since the hut, wrapped in spare clothing, and every step since had felt like walking with a sleeping animal against his spine.

"Talk me through it," Ines said. She crouched beside him, breath streaming sideways in the wind. "I want to understand what you're doing."

He glanced at her. Four days ago she would have flinched at the word *bomb*. Now she asked to be taught. Something in his chest turned over—not pride exactly, something older and sadder.

"The col sits in a bowl," he said, tracing it in the snow with one finger. "The approach from our side climbs through this gully here—steep, maybe forty degrees, wind-loaded. Above the col, on the far side, the slope rises another three hundred meters to the pass itself. Corliss will hold the high ground. He'll let us climb into the bowl, into the kill zone, and then he'll close the top."

"And your charges?"

"Are buried—" he moved his finger along the flank of the western shoulder, a steep face of rock and old snowpack above the gully's mouth, "—there. Where the cornice has been building all season. The wind's been loading that face for weeks. It wants to come down. All it needs is permission."

Ines studied the face, then him. "You've done this before."

"Not like this." The words came out before he could stop them, and once out, he made himself finish. "Not on people. On slopes. On snow that needed convincing. It's the same physics either way." He looked down at the gelatin sticks, pale as tallow candles. "That's what nobody tells you about mountains. Everything up here is a weapon already. Rockfall, seracs, avalanches, cold. A mountain doesn't need us to make it dangerous. We just need to know how to ask."

"And you're asking it to kill men."

"I'm asking it to scatter them." He met her eyes. "There's a difference, and I'm going to hold onto it as long as I can. But I won't lie to you—if any of them are standing where the slide runs full, no one survives that. Not even a man like Corliss deserves to pretend otherwise."

Ines was quiet a long moment. Wind worried at their backs, plucking at their hoods. Then she said, "My father was a doctor in the war. He used to say the worst thing wasn't the killing. It was getting good at it." She reached out and picked up one of the timers, turning it in her glove. "Show me how these work."

* * *

They climbed the western shoulder in the last hour of light, moving fast and low, using the rock ribs for cover. Twice Theo froze, certain he'd seen movement on the far rim of the col—a flicker of dark against dark—but each time the wind covered whatever sound there might have been and nothing came of it. That frightened him more than certainty would have. Corliss was not a man who wasted movement. If his men were up there, they were being quiet about it, which meant they were patient, which meant they thought they had all the time in the world.

Theo meant to take that time away from them.

The burial site was a shallow bench below the cornice line, sheltered from direct view of the col by a fin of granite. He dug with the small aluminum shovel, cutting blocks the way he'd cut them ten thousand times—for snow caves, for wind walls, for science—and stacked them neatly to one side so the refilled pit would read as nothing more than disturbed drift. Ines placed the charges as he directed, tamping them deep into the hard old snowpack at the base of the cornice, packing the det cord in a protected channel back toward the trigger point, thirty meters down-slope behind the granite fin.

"Timers or wire?" she asked.

"Wire." He paid out the thin cord, testing the connections with a circuit tester salvaged from the survey kit. "Timers fail. Timers run early, run late. Wire does what you tell it, when you tell it." He paused, then added, quieter: "And I want to be looking at that face when it goes. I want to see it with my own eyes. If I'm going to do this, I'm not doing it blind."

She didn't ask what he meant by *this*. She was learning that too.

They finished in the blue hour, when the snow went the color of ash and the sky burned copper in the west. From the fin's shadow they could see the whole bowl of the col, the gully climbing toward it, the notch of sky beyond. Somewhere up there, men were settling into the cold, watching the approach, waiting for two figures to appear in the gully's mouth tomorrow morning.

"Sleep in shifts," Theo said. "First light, we show ourselves. Just long enough."

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless, which was wrong, and Theo hated it, because calm air meant the cornice was stable, meant the slide would need every gram of force his charges could give it. But there was no help for it. Waiting a day meant Corliss might send scouts down the gully and find the cache site, find the disturbed snow, find *them*.

At first light they moved down to the mouth of the gully and made themselves visible—two small figures on the white slope, deliberately slow, deliberately exposed. Theo counted to sixty in his head. Somewhere above, he knew, a man with binoculars was speaking into a radio, and other men were checking weapons and smiling the way men smile when the work has become easy.

Then they ran.

Not up the gully—that was the trap, and they gave it only the appearance of flight. They cut hard left, behind the granite fin, scrambling the thirty meters to the trigger point, and Theo dropped behind the rock with the firing leads in his hands while Ines pressed flat beside him, her breath ragged, her eyes wide and fixed on his face.

"They're moving," she whispered. She'd taken the rear watch during the sprint, and she'd seen them: dark shapes pouring off the far rim of the col, dropping into the gully's upper reaches, fanning out to close the top. "Six—I counted six, maybe seven. They think we're below them."

"They're right. For about ninety seconds." Theo checked the leads one final time. Copper terminals, clean crimps. His hands were steady, and he noticed that, and noticed himself noticing, and put it away. There would be time to be afraid later, or there wouldn't. "When I fire this, the cornice goes first, then the slab below it. The whole face comes down into the gully. Anyone caught in the runout—" He stopped. Started again. "It won't be survivable in the track. Along the margins, near the edges, people live. That's where I want them. Scattered, buried to the waist, alive enough to dig themselves out and think very hard about going home."

"And Corliss?"

"Corliss doesn't stand in avalanche tracks. He stands behind them." Theo wound the lead wires around his fingers. "That's the thing about men like him, Ines. They spend other people's bodies. It's the whole architecture of what they do. Which means today we make bodies expensive."

He looked at her one last time. She nodded once, small and definite, and pulled her hood tight, and turned her face away from the blast.

Theo touched the leads together.

The mountain answered.

There was no dramatic pause, no cinematic rumble of warning. One instant the face stood silent under its burden of winter; the next it split along a seam invisible from outside, and the cornice folded, and then the whole slope was in motion—a sound like the world tearing cloth, vast and intimate at once, and the gully below filled with white violence moving faster than anything living. Theo felt the shockwave through the rock under his chest, felt it in his teeth, in the roots of his teeth, and above the roar he heard—or imagined he heard—shouting, human voices, tiny against the immensity they had summoned.

The slide ran the length of the gully in perhaps twenty seconds. It hit the opposing wall and climbed it, deflected, spread, and settled, and the silence that followed was enormous, ringing, absolute. Snow dust hung in the air like smoke after a battle, drifting gold in the new sun.

Through it, Theo watched the slope settle. Watched the debris field smooth itself into that terrible innocent blankness avalanches always wore afterward, as if nothing had ever happened, as if the mountain had merely exhaled.

"Ines." His voice sounded strange to him, flattened by the ringing in his ears. "Look at the edges. Tell me what you see."

She raised the binoculars with hands that shook only a little. A long moment. Then: "Movement. Far right edge—two figures, digging. Another one further down, near the gully mouth—he's on his feet, he's... he's just standing there." Her voice tightened. "Theo, the middle of the track—"

"Don't look at the middle of the track."

She lowered the binoculars anyway. Looked at him instead. Whatever she saw in his face made her reach out and grip his arm, hard, through all the layers. "You did what you said. You scattered them. The rest is the mountain's."

"No." He got to his feet, legs unsteady, and looked up at the col, where the notch of sky now showed unbroken and empty. "The rest is ours. Every part of it. That's the price of pulling the trigger—you carry what falls."

But even as he said it, some older, colder part of him—the part that had lived on Kestrel Face, the part that had frozen while the world came down—was whispering something else. That the slope had run true. That the fracture line had gone exactly where he'd read it. That for the first time since the Face, since the inquiry, since the blacklists and the false names and the ferries up dead rivers, his hands had known precisely what they were doing, and the mountain had listened.

Mastery, he thought. This is what it feels like. It feels like grief with better aim.

* * *

They crossed the debris field at midmorning, moving along its margins where the snow was churned but firm, boots punching through crust into the dense set beneath. Here and there the surface bore evidence of what had passed through it: a rifle stock protruding like a broken branch. A single glove. A radio handset, cracked open, its battery spilled into the snow like a black egg. Theo collected the radios without comment—three of them, waterlogged, almost certainly dead, but dead equipment could still mislead a searching party if planted right.

Near the gully's lower mouth they found the man.

He was buried to the chest at the track's extreme edge, where the flow had barely kissed him—one of the lucky ones, if luck was the word. Young, younger than Theo had expected, his face bloodless with cold and shock, his breath coming in quick white gasps. When he saw them his eyes went wide and he began, absurdly, to apologize—to them, to the mountain, to no one.

"Please," he said. "Please. I can't feel my arms."

Theo knelt in the snow beside him. Up close the man was hardly more than a boy, frost in the stubble of his beard, a wedding band glinting dully on one buried hand. Behind him, higher on the slope, two other survivors were digging themselves free with the slow, mechanical movements of the half-frozen.

Ines crouched on Theo's other side. "We can't dig him out," she said quietly. "It would take hours, and they'd see us from the col the moment we started."

"I know."

"He'll freeze before nightfall unless he gets loose."

"I know that too." Theo looked at the boy—at the young man—and the young man looked back, and something passed between them that had no language. Then Theo drew his knife, cut the man's pack straps where they emerged from the snow, and worked the shovel from his own pack. He began to dig—not deeply, not for long, just enough to free the man's arms to the elbow, enough that he could work at the rest himself.

"Why?" the man rasped.

"Because I'm not him," Theo said. He didn't explain who *him* was. He didn't need to. "Dig toward the edge. Follow the sun. There's a valley on the other side of the pass, and if you move now, you might live to complain about my cooking someday."

He stood, shouldered his pack, and turned upslope. Ines fell in beside him. Neither spoke until the debris field lay below them and the col rose ahead, its notch of sky wide open and undefended, the whole high route to the pass lying bare under the morning light.

"You gave him a chance," Ines said at last.

"I gave him what I have." Theo climbed. The wind was returning, filling in from the northwest at last, righting the world's crooked grammar, and it pushed at their backs like a hand between the shoulder blades. "Corliss will have heard the slide. He'll know his party's broken. Men like him get more dangerous, not less, when they're alone—they've got nothing left to spend but themselves."

Ahead, the pass gleamed, still impossibly far, still white and silent and indifferent. Two days' climb, if the weather held. Less, if it didn't.

Behind them, faint across the ruined slope, came a sound that stopped them both mid-stride: a single shot, then another, deliberate and unhurried, echoing off the walls of the col.

Not aimed at them. Aimed at the sky. A signal—or a summons.

Or a promise.

Theo adjusted his pack straps and kept climbing, and did not look back.
