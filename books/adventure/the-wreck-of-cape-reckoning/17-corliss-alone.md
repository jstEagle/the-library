## 17. Corliss Alone

Corliss had buried four men in the mountain and felt nothing he could name.

Not grief—he had never been built for grief. Not anger, either, though the avalanche that had swallowed Vasquez and the two brothers had cost him a quarter million in equipment and a reputation he would need to rebuild from zero. What he felt was closer to arithmetic. He stood at the edge of the debris field below the col, where the snow lay in slabs like broken crockery, and he did the sum. Three days behind schedule. One radio. One rifle with a cracked stock and eleven rounds. Two hundred meters of rope, half of it stiff with ice. And above him, somewhere on the shoulder of the peak, a disgraced guide and a limping geologist carrying a steel case of core samples that were worth more than everything else on this mountain combined.

The sum came out simple. He went up alone.

He had considered turning back. That was the honest version of it, the version he would give no one: for perhaps a full minute, standing in the wind with his gloves off and his fingers going wooden as he studied the route, he had let himself imagine the descent. The valley. A bed that wasn't frozen ground. A telephone line to his employer, a voice saying *the witnesses are dead, the evidence is buried under forty feet of snow, write it off.* No one would have questioned it. Mountains eat people; that was their function. He could have walked away clean, poorer but whole, and let the consortium's lawyers do what lawyers did.

But there was the matter of Vane.

Corliss had read the file twice on the flight north, once out of diligence and once out of something he refused to call interest. Theo Vane, blacklisted twice, blamed for nine deaths on the Kestrel Face, a man who had spent seven years moving like water through the cracks of two countries—ferrying supplies under false names, guiding rich tourists through unpatrolled wilderness, leaving no address, no trail, no self. On paper he was exactly what Corliss's employer had called him: already discredited, easy to bury twice.

On paper. But paper had not triggered an avalanche with survey charges and sealed the lower route while three armed men stood on it. Paper had not hauled a wounded woman up a glacier through a whiteout. Paper did not explain why every man Corliss sent ahead of him had stopped sending word back.

So Corliss climbed. Not because he believed he would catch them—he gave himself even odds, and Corliss did not gamble—but because the alternative was to go down the mountain carrying the knowledge that a man he had dismissed had beaten him, and Corliss had discovered somewhere in the last ten years that he could survive losing money, losing men, even losing his employer's confidence, but not that. Not being *dismissed.*

He climbed through the afternoon, following a trail only he could have read: a boot print half-filled with snow, a scrap of orange tent fabric snagged on rock, the faint chemical smell of a stove burned at altitude. The mountain did its work on him. It took the feeling from his fingers first, then his toes, then the skin of his face, until he was climbing with a body that reported nothing and a mind that reported everything, cold and clear as the ice around him. By the time the light went long and amber across the western faces, he had stopped feeling like a man at all. He was a calculation still executing itself.

And near dusk, on the final ridge—the knife-edge of snow and shattered granite that ran the last four hundred meters to Serrano Pass—he saw them.

Two figures, small against the burning sky. One upright, one leaning hard on the other's shoulder. They had maybe an hour of light left, and the pass was close enough now that he could see the notch of it, the saddle where the wind tore the snow into banners. Close enough to touch, almost.

Corliss took the rifle off his shoulder and began to climb faster.

* * *

Theo felt him before he saw him—that old animal sense, the one that had kept him alive through seven years of exile and had failed him exactly once, on the Kestrel Face, when it mattered most. He stopped mid-stride on the ridge, Ines sagging against his side, and turned his head into the wind.

"What," Ines said. Not a question anymore. She'd learned what that tone meant.

"Behind us. Don't stop walking."

"He's alone?"

"I think so." Theo scanned the slope below without turning his head fully, using the periphery, the way you watched for rockfall. "Which means either he's stupid or he thinks we're finished."

"Which is it?"

Theo didn't answer, because the honest answer was *both*, and because at that moment the light shifted on the slope below and he saw the figure clearly for the first time—a dark shape moving up the ridge with a steadiness that made Theo's stomach drop. Not stumbling. Not desperate. Climbing the way Theo climbed, reading the snow, conserving, patient. And carrying a rifle.

"Ines." He turned her gently, put her back against the rock wall on the lee side of the ridge. Her face was gray with exhaustion, lips cracked, frostbite whitening the tips of two fingers despite everything he'd done. Four days of training her on the move, and she had made it further than he'd had any right to expect—but she couldn't run, and they both knew it. "Listen to me. There's a gully fifty meters ahead, on the north side. You can descend it solo if you're careful—it's steep but it holds snow all the way to the pass approach. I marked it yesterday."

"No."

"You can't outrun a bullet and I can't carry you and fight him. This isn't a discussion, it's arithmetic."

Her hand closed on his sleeve with surprising strength. "You told me on the glacier—" Her voice cracked, and she steadied it. "You told me you froze once. On the Kestrel Face. You told me you spent seven years running because you couldn't be the person who stayed. So stay. Stay and finish it, but don't you dare send me down that gully so you can die alone on a ridge proving something."

The wind screamed over the crest. Below them, unhurried, the dark shape gained.

Theo looked at her—at this woman who had crawled out of a forest with a bullet graze and a steel case and had never once, in eleven days of hell, asked him to make it easier—and something in his chest that had been clenched since the Kestrel Face finally, quietly, let go.

"Then we do it together," he said. "Get behind me. When he's close, you take the case and move for the pass. Don't look back. Whatever you hear, don't look back."

* * *

Corliss came over the crest of the ridge at the last light, and found the guide waiting for him twenty meters along the spine of it, standing between him and the two small shapes near the rocks—one of them already limping away, burdened by a steel case, moving with the grim economy of someone who has been told not to look back.

"Mr. Vane." Corliss set the rifle butt on the snow, casual, conversational. The wind tried to take his words; he pitched his voice under it. "You've cost me a great deal of money."

"Put the rifle down," Theo said.

"I don't think I will." He raised it—not fast, nothing wasted—and sighted along the ridge at the retreating figure of the geologist.

Theo moved before the shot. Not away—*in*, closing the distance, because at twenty meters a rifle beats a man and at two meters a man beats a rifle, and some part of him that had been asleep for seven years woke up knowing this without being told. The first round went wide, chewing snow, and then Theo hit him.

They went down together onto the ridge, and the world became very small and very loud. The rifle skittered, lost. Elbows, knees, the crack of a head against stone. Corliss fought the way he did everything—economically, without heat, looking always for the efficient line—and he was strong, stronger than Theo, and fresh where Theo was six days hollowed out. A fist found Theo's cheekbone and the ridge tilted sideways. A knee drove into his ribs and something in there creaked and slid.

They rolled. For one lurching second the world was sky, then rock, then sky again, and Theo understood with terrible clarity that the edge of the ridge was somewhere beneath them and neither of them knew exactly where.

Corliss got on top. His hands found Theo's throat, and his face, inches away, was calm—the calm of a man doing paperwork. "You should have stayed buried," he said, almost kindly, and squeezed.

Black flowers bloomed at the edges of Theo's vision. His own hands clawed at Corliss's wrists and accomplished nothing. And in the roaring dark, absurdly, he thought of the cave, and the confession, and Ines's face when he'd said *I froze, and nine people died, and I let them say it was the mountain's fault because the truth was worse*—and he thought: *not again. Not this time. This time I hold.*

He stopped pulling at the wrists and dropped his hands, let his body go slack for one heartbeat—enough that Corliss's weight shifted forward, confident—and then Theo brought both arms up inside the other man's guard and shoved, hard, at the chest, at the moment the balance was committed. Corliss rocked back. Theo bucked his hips and rolled them, once, twice—

The edge arrived.

It announced itself as absence: Corliss's shoulder meeting nothing, the sudden wrongness of gravity changing its mind. For a suspended instant they were both half over it, Corliss's fingers hooked into Theo's jacket, Theo's boots scrabbling on the crest, the drop yawning below them—a hundred meters of air and then the blue shadow of the glacier.

Corliss looked up at him. There was no fear in it. That was the worst thing, the thing Theo would carry afterward: the fixer's face held no fear at all, only a kind of furious accounting, as if the mountain itself had entered the ledger against him and he could not make the numbers work.

Then his grip slipped on the ice-glazed fabric, and Theo—God help him, some part of him reaching, some part still the man who carried people off mountains—caught his wrist.

For three seconds, four, Theo held him. Corliss's full weight swung below the edge, boots finding no purchase on the sheer face, and Theo's arms burned and his torn ribs screamed and the wind pushed at his back like a hand helping the fall along.

"Theo." Ines's voice, ragged, close—she had come back, of course she had come back, she had never once done what she was told. She was flat on the snow beside him, one arm locked around his harness, the other reaching past him. "I have him—I have your arm—let go, Theo, you can't lift him, *let go*—"

"If I let go he dies."

"Yes." No hesitation. No cruelty either. Just the arithmetic, returned to him in his own voice. "Yes. And if you don't, you both do."

Below him, Corliss stared up with those unafraid eyes, and said nothing. Did not ask. Would not ask. That, too, Theo would carry.

Theo's grip opened finger by finger, not because he chose it—in the end his body chose, the way bodies choose, strength simply ending—and the last thing Corliss's hand did was slide free, deliberate, almost dignified, releasing *him* rather than being released.

The sound the mountain made afterward was very small. Just wind.

* * *

They did not celebrate. There was nothing in it to celebrate, and both of them knew it, and for a long time neither spoke.

Theo sat on the crest of the ridge with his head in his hands, shaking—not from cold, or not only from cold—and Ines knelt beside him with her hand on his back, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't either a lie or a wound. Somewhere below them in the blue dark, a man who had hunted them across a hundred miles of ice was part of the mountain now, and Theo Vane had killed a human being with his hands, and the justice they had climbed toward tasted like copper and snowmelt.

"He would have killed us both," Ines said at last. Quietly. Not absolution—she was too honest for that—but fact.

"I know."

"And you caught his wrist anyway."

"I know."

She sat down beside him then, shoulder against his ruined ribs, careful, and together they watched the last light die off the western faces. Below them the glacier glowed faintly, holding its dead. Above them, close enough now to see the wind-scoured saddle of it, Serrano Pass waited under the first stars.

"We have maybe two hours of dark to cross it," Theo said eventually. His voice sounded like someone else's. "After that the snows come, and the pass closes until June."

Ines pulled the steel case against her chest. Inside it, wrapped in her spare shirt, were the samples that would break open the consortium, overturn the Kestrel Face findings, and give a blacklisted guide his name back—if they lived to deliver them.

"Then we'd better not waste the dark," she said.

Theo stood. Every part of him objected, and he stood anyway, and turned his face to the pass.
