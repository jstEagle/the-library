## 18. The Pass

They came up out of the col in the blue hour before dawn, when the mountain was neither night nor day but something older than both, and Theo Vane stopped believing he would ever be warm again.

The wind had turned sometime in the dark. It came off the ice fields to the north now, straight down the throat of the pass, and it carried with it a fine granular snow that hissed against their shells like sand poured on paper. Not falling snow—driven snow, the kind that didn't accumulate so much as erase, scouring the rock bare in the lee and burying everything else. The dry season was ending not with weather but with subtraction. The world was being taken away from them piece by piece.

Ines had not spoken in two hours.

He checked on her the way he'd learned to check on her over nine days of climbing: without stopping, without turning, just a glance at her rhythm. She was still moving, still placing her feet where he placed his, still following the line he'd cut across the wind-scoured slope. But her rhythm had gone mechanical, the rhythm of a body that had stopped negotiating with itself and simply obeyed. He'd seen that rhythm before. On Kestrel Face, he'd seen it in himself.

"Ines." His voice came out ruined, a rasp scraped raw by nine days of cold air and shouting into wind. "Talk to me."

"What do you want me to say?"

"Anything. Tell me about the samples again."

A pause long enough that he thought she hadn't heard. Then, behind him, her breath sawing: "The cores are sealed in wax-impregnated canvas. They'll survive anything short of fire. I told you that at the hut."

"Tell me again anyway."

"You're checking if my mind is going." A sound that might have been a laugh, or might have been a cough. "It's not. It's just... quiet in here. Like a room after everyone's left."

Theo understood that too well to answer. He kept climbing.

The pass revealed itself the way Serrano always did, according to the old guidebooks and the older guides: all at once, as a lie you'd been telling yourself for four days suddenly becoming true. One moment there was only the slope and the wind and the grey smear of the sky; the next, the ridge line simply ended, and beyond it was nothing—a white void with a floor of cloud, the eastern valleys drowned under an inversion layer, the sun not yet risen but already staining the underside of the clouds a deep bruised gold. The gap between the northern buttress and the southern tower was perhaps eighty meters wide. It looked, from here, like a doorway cut by something enormous and careless.

They had made it. That was the fact of it, plain and unadorned: they had climbed out of the gorge, past the hut, through the whiteout, across the glacier where the crevasse had nearly taken her, above the col where Corliss's men had pinned them and where Theo had brought half a mountainside down on their heads—and they were here, at the top of the world, with the samples strapped against his chest inside his shell, riding the rise and fall of his breathing like something alive.

He should have felt triumph. He searched for it the way you search a dark room for a light switch, patting along the walls of himself, and found nothing. What he found instead was emptiness—not despair, which at least had shape, but a vast flat quiet, like the plateau of a high glacier. They were alive. Corliss was dead. The proof was intact. And none of it meant anything yet, because meaning was a thing that happened on the far side of mountains, in rooms with tables and microphones, among people who had never been cold.

"Theo." Ines's hand closed on his shoulder. Her glove was stiff with ice; he felt it as a tap of wood. "Look."

She was pointing south, along the ridge they'd have to traverse to reach the descent gully. The light was coming up now, the sun clearing the cloud deck below them, and the whole eastern sky had caught fire. But that wasn't what she was pointing at.

Snow spindrift was lifting off the southern tower in long banners, streaming east like smoke. The wind was rising. And along the leeward edge of the pass, in the sheltered hollows where the season's first snows had already begun to settle, the surface was moving—subtle, continuous, a slow creep of crystals migrating downslope like something crawling.

"It's starting," she said.

"Not yet." He said it with more certainty than he felt. "We've got hours. Maybe six. Maybe less if the wind keeps building. The gully is two hours' work if we move well." He looked at her hands—at the way she held them curled against her chest, the early frostbite in her fingertips having gone past pain into a dull absence. "Can you move well?"

"I can move," Ines said. "That's the only verb I've got left. Use it or lose it."

* * *

They crossed the pass in the full blaze of morning, and it was terrible and beautiful in equal measure, and Theo could not tell the difference anymore.

The light came off the snow in a hard white flood, bouncing up into their faces from below, and even behind glacier glasses the world was reduced to contrasts: the black of rock, the white of snow, the blue-black of shadow. The wind hit them halfway across, funneling through the gap, and it took his breath away bodily—he had to turn his back to it, hunching over Ines, shielding her with his own mass while she fought to get her balaclava sealed against her face. For thirty seconds the world ceased to exist. There was only the roar, and the pressure of the air trying to peel him off the mountain, and the small warm weight of the sample case against his sternum, and Ines's fists knotted in his jacket.

Then it eased, the way these gusts did, dropping from violence to mere hostility, and they went on.

At the midpoint of the crossing stood a cairn. Theo knew it without knowing it—the way you know a face from a photograph you saw decades ago. The old guides had built it, the ones who'd worked this route before the consortium, before the disaster, before the bans and the blacklists and the long slow machinery of money grinding a mountain into ore. Someone had topped it with a slab of greenstone, and someone else, years later, had scratched initials into it that the weather had almost erased.

He stopped there, just for a moment, with one gloved hand resting on the stones.

"What is it?" Ines asked.

"Nothing. An old marker." He lifted his hand. "Guides used to bring clients through here in summer. Before my time, mostly. There was a saying—" He stopped. He hadn't thought of the saying in fifteen years, and hearing it come back to him now, in this place, at this hour, was like hearing a dead man clear his throat. "*The pass doesn't care why you came.*"

"That's grim."

"That's the mountains." He looked east, toward the descent, toward the green country he couldn't see yet but knew was there—valleys, rivers, roads, towns with telephones. "They don't care what you're carrying or who's chasing you or whether you're guilty. They just ask if you can walk. Everything else is weather."

Ines stood beside him a moment longer, looking at the cairn. Wind pulled at her hood. When she spoke, her voice was very quiet, almost lost in it. "My father used to say something like that. About the sea. He was a harbor pilot in Lagos." She paused. "I haven't thought about him in days. Isn't that strange? Nine days of trying not to die and I forgot my own father existed."

"He's not forgotten. He's just been put somewhere safe until there's room for him again."

She looked at him then, really looked, her eyes narrow ovals of frost-crusted skin above the balaclava, and whatever she saw in his face made her nod once and turn away. "Let's walk, then. While we're still verbs."

* * *

The descent gully was worse than he remembered from the survey maps, because maps don't carry wind.

It dropped away northeast from the pass in a series of steep steps—rock ribs alternating with snow slopes, each snow slope a potential slide path now that the season's first accumulation lay over smooth summer ice. Theo led the whole way, placing each step, testing each slope with his axe before committing his weight, and talking constantly, a low running patter of instruction that was half teaching and half prayer: *weight on the uphill foot, plunge the shaft before you step, if it whumps sit down immediately*. Ines followed in his prints, silent, obedient, saving every calorie for the simple brutal arithmetic of putting one foot below the other.

Twice the mountain tested them. The first time was small—a sluff of surface snow that broke loose above them on the third step and came down in a soft white rush, filling their tracks, stinging the backs of their necks. It carried no weight. It meant nothing. But they both stood motionless while it passed, listening to it go, and neither of them said what they were both thinking.

The second time was not small.

They had reached the fourth step, a broad snow ramp forty meters wide, when Theo felt it—a deep, subsonic shudder through his boots, felt rather than heard, the mountain clearing its throat somewhere above. He froze. Turned. Looked up the fall line to where the ramp narrowed toward a hanging slope beneath the southern tower, a slope he'd noted yesterday and hated and hoped to descend before the new snow bonded.

"Harness," he said quietly. "Now. Axe in the snow. Don't look up, look at me."

Ines moved fast—nine days had taught her that much, that when Theo's voice went quiet you obeyed it faster than when it shouted. She had the harness cinched and the pick seated when the slope above let go.

It didn't roar. That was the horror of it—it *whumped*, a soft compression, like a giant pressing a hand down on a mattress, and then the whole face above the ramp began to move, a slab maybe half a meter deep and fifty meters wide, sliding as one solid thing, and it was coming directly down the fall line at the ramp where they stood.

"Down-slope!" Theo was already moving, hauling her by the arm, cutting sideways across the ramp toward the rock rib at its edge. "Run! Run *now*!"

They ran. The snow ran better. He heard it coming behind them with that sound he knew from another life, another mountain, another man—that low gathering hiss, the sound of the world deciding your weight didn't matter—and he threw Ines behind the rib and dove after her as the slab arrived.

It hit the ramp where they'd stood and broke apart around the rock rib in two white torrents, hammering the stone beside them, filling the air with powder, burying their tracks, burying the cairn of their passage, burying everything except the two of them pressed flat against cold granite with the avalanche thundering past close enough to touch. Rocks came with it. One struck the rib a meter above Ines's head and shattered, and she flinched into Theo's shoulder and stayed there, shaking, while the sound rolled away down the mountain and faded into the ordinary silence of altitude.

Then it was done. The ramp below was scoured to bare ice. Their route was gone—but so, he realized slowly, sitting up against the stone with his heart slamming, was any trace that anyone had ever crossed here. The mountain had wiped the slate clean behind them. If Corliss's employers sent anyone up the pass after them, they would find a fresh slide, a closed season, and nothing else.

Ines pulled her balaclava down. Her lips were cracked and bleeding and she was smiling, or something adjacent to smiling.

"We're alive," she said, as if reporting a discrepancy in the data.

"For now." Theo got his feet under him. His legs were shaking and he let them. "The gully's open below the slide. Two hours to the tree line. Can you give me two hours?"

Ines Okafor looked down the mountain, at the green haze beginning to show through the thinning cloud far below—the first color either of them had seen in days—and then at the sample case strapped to his chest, and lastly at her own hands, which she flexed slowly, wincing.

"I can give you two hours," she said. "After that you're carrying me, and I warn you, I'm heavier than I look. Dense bones. Geologist."

"Deal."

* * *

They came down out of the snow at midafternoon, and the world changed its mind about them.

First the snow gave way to grass—actual grass, bent flat and gold under the weight of the early storm but unmistakably alive. Then grass gave way to scrub, scrub to krummholz, krummholz to the first stunted pines, and with the trees came silence, real silence, the wind dying among the branches, and with the silence came birdsong, absurd and impossible, a thrush somewhere off to their left singing as though avalanches and fixers and frozen men were things that happened on other planets.

Ines sat down against a pine trunk and cried. Not loudly. Just sat there with her forearms over her eyes and her shoulders moving, and Theo stood nearby and pretended to study the valley and gave her the privacy of his bad acting.

Below them the valley opened in long green folds, a river stitching through it, and along the river—there, where the haze thinned—a road. A dirt road, unpromising, rutted, but a road, which meant wheels, which meant engines, which meant a town at the end of it with walls and telephones and people who had never heard of Kestrel Face or Serrano Pass or the name Theo Vane.

He felt the pull of it like gravity. Disappear. It would be easy. Walk down, buy passage with the cash sewn in his jacket, put an ocean between himself and the consortium, mail the samples to some journalist from a border town and never learn how it ended. Nine days ago that plan had been the whole of him, worn smooth as river stone. Now he stood under the pines holding the most dangerous documents in two countries against his chest, and the plan felt like a coat that no longer fit.

Because the samples proved the surveys were falsified. And the falsified surveys had triggered the decision that put eleven climbers under the Kestrel Face cornice. And the inquiry that followed had needed someone to blame, and had chosen him—the guide with the debts, the guide with the temper, the guide who froze. Which meant the truth didn't just clear his name. It indicted everyone who had built that name for him, and they would spend anything to keep it buried, and the only place that spending could be stopped was in a hearing room, under oath, in the country that had banned him.

Going back meant facing the inquiry. Facing the inquiry meant giving them his true name at a border post, standing still in a line, surrendering the last thing he owned—which was his freedom to run.

"Vane." Ines had recovered. She was looking up at him with red-rimmed eyes and the ghost of her usual ferocity. "You've got the face again."

"What face?"

"The one where you're deciding something and hating it." She pushed herself upright against the trunk, swaying, catching herself. "Whatever it is—you should know the samples survive without us. Mail them, scatter them, whatever you need. You don't owe the mountain a testimony."

"No," Theo agreed. "But I owe eleven people one."

He started down through the trees toward the road, and after a moment he heard her follow, her boots crunching through the frost-bent grass, slower than his, steadier than yesterday. Behind them the pass stood open in the evening light, a doorway of gold and shadow, already closing. By tomorrow it would be sealed under winter, holding its silence, keeping whatever secrets had crossed it and whatever ones hadn't.

The thrush sang on. Somewhere ahead, a truck ground along the dirt road in a low gear, and Theo Vane walked toward the sound of it, carrying the mountain's evidence against his heart, and for the first time in nine days he was not running from anything.

Which meant, he understood with a strange calm, that tomorrow he would have to decide what he was walking toward—and the answer frightened him more than Corliss ever had.
