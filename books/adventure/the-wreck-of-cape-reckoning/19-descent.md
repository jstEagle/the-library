## 19. Descent

The snow stopped at the tree line.

It didn't fade or thin the way weather usually did, easing itself out in ragged curtains. It simply ended, one step to the next, as though Theo had walked through a door. Behind him: white, wind, the blue glare of the glacier they'd crossed with their fingernails. Ahead of him: green.

He stood a long time at that line, letting it happen to him.

The valley on the far side of Serrano Pass fell away in long folds of forest—larch and pine going gold at the edges, meadow grass still stubbornly green in the hollows where the season hadn't reached yet. A river ran down the middle of it all, braided and bright, chattering over stones like something that had never heard of winter. The air smelled different here. Warmer. Wet. It smelled like the world before everything, like the country he'd grown up in on the other side of the range, and Theo's throat tightened around it until he had to swallow twice.

Ines came up beside him, leaning on the ice axe he'd cut a walking stick from three days ago. Her left boot was wrapped in strips of tent fabric over two pairs of socks; her frostbitten toes were a rumor she refused to discuss. She was gray-lipped, hollow-eyed, twenty pounds lighter than the woman he'd pulled out of the forest a lifetime ago. She was also standing upright, which by the arithmetic of the last four days made her the strongest person he knew.

"You're staring," she said.

"I'm looking."

"At what?"

"Trees." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "I forgot how many there could be."

She followed his gaze down the valley, and after a moment she laughed—a small, cracked sound, half disbelief. Below them, threading through the green, was a road. Not a track, not a survey cut. A road, graded and gravel-topped, running the length of the valley floor beside the river, and along it, strung like beads on wire, power poles. And past the second bend, where the valley widened, rooftops. Chimneys. The flat gleam of windows catching afternoon light.

A town. An actual town, with a church spire and what looked like a fuel depot and, God help him, telephone lines sagging between the poles, each one a thread stitched back into the world of people who answered when you called.

"Vane," Ines said quietly. "That's Chile."

"That's *out*," Theo said.

* * *

They came down off the mountain the way water comes down—without decision, without dignity, following the path of least resistance because it was the only kind of strength either of them had left. By late afternoon they reached the road. Theo made Ines sit on the gravel shoulder while he stood in the middle of it, boots planted on man-made flatness for the first time in eleven days, and felt the strangeness of it climb up through his legs. Ground that someone had leveled. Ground that expected to be used.

The samples rode against his chest in a dry bag lashed under his jacket, the way they had since the hut. Four core cylinders and a sealed data drive, wrapped in Ines's spare shirt and a failed expedition's thermal blanket. He'd slept with them. He'd hauled them out of a crevasse field with his last rope. Somewhere in the last two days the weight of them had stopped registering as weight and started registering as heartbeat—he was aware of them the way he was aware of his own pulse, constantly, beneath everything.

A truck came up the valley at dusk. An old flatbed, farm-plate, driven by a woman in her sixties with a dog on the seat beside her. She took one look at the two of them—bearded, frost-scalded, Ines barely ambulatory—and pulled over without asking questions, which told Theo something about the kind of valley this was.

"Los Robles," she said, when Ines asked where the town was. "Twenty minutes. There's a clinic. There's a phone at the co-op." She looked at their faces, at the state of them, and her eyes went narrow and kind at once. "You come off the pass?"

"We came off the pass," Theo said.

"In October." She said it the way you'd say *you swam here*. Then she shrugged, patted the seat, and drove them down into the green.

* * *

Los Robles smelled of woodsmoke and diesel and bread. That was the thing that undid Theo—not the lights, not the telephones, but the smell of a bakery working the evening shift, yeast and heat drifting down the main street, ordinary as gravity. He stood outside the co-op while Ines used the phone, breathing that smell, and understood that he was shaking.

Not from cold. From the sheer proximity of safety. His body had been braced so long—against weather, against pursuit, against the next bad thing—that now, with nothing visible to brace against, it didn't know how to stand down. He kept checking the street. Kept cataloguing faces. A man loading crates. Two boys kicking a ball against a wall. Nobody watching them. Nobody hunting. But Corliss had radios and money and a head start on ruthlessness, and somewhere behind them on the mountain—

No. Corliss was gone. Corliss had fallen. Theo had watched it happen, had felt the man's weight tear loose from his grip at the ridge edge, had heard the silence after. Whatever else lived inside him from these eleven days, that silence would live there too.

But men like Corliss were hired, not born. Somewhere south of here, in offices with good coffee, the people who'd paid him would be learning tonight that their fixer had stopped reporting in. And they would know, by now, exactly who had walked out of the Reckoning country carrying proof that could bury them.

Inside the co-op, Ines set the phone down gently, like something that might bite.

"There's an inquiry office in Puerto Serrano," she said. "And a federal police post. I called the police. They're sending a car tomorrow morning." She paused. "I also called my sister. She thought I was dead. So did I, a little."

Theo nodded. Outside the window, the streetlights had come on, buzzing sodium-orange, moths already gathering. People moved along the sidewalk with groceries, with dogs, with the unhurried certainty of people whose evenings contained nothing worse than dishes.

"I'm going to walk," he said.

"Theo—"

"Ten minutes. I'll be ten minutes."

* * *

He walked to the end of the main street and then along the river road, and the whole way he argued with himself in two voices, and both voices sounded reasonable, and that was the trap.

The first voice said: *look at this.* Look at the valley. Look at the bakery, the lit windows, the old woman watering geraniums in a pot by her gate who nodded at him as if strangers nodding back were normal. This was a place where a man could be no one. Not exiled-no-one, not wanted-poster-no-one—just a man, with a face nobody knew, in a country that had never heard of Kestrel Face or the Vane inquiry or the consortium's tidy falsified reports. He had cash sewn into his jacket lining, enough to live quiet for a year. He had skills any valley in the Andes would pay for and never question. He had, pressed warm against his chest, the physical proof that could clear his name—and proof, unlike testimony, could be mailed. Anonymous envelope. Federal office. Let the documents do the work; let the name stay dead.

*You could stop,* the first voice said. *Eleven days ago you'd have traded your hands for this street. You could have a room above the bakery by Friday. You could learn the river. You could grow things.*

And the second voice—the one that had been getting louder since the crevasse, since the hut, since the ugly close-quarters fight that had put another man's blood on his hands—said: *and then what?*

Then what. Then he'd be Marta. That was the thought that stopped him at the rail bridge, mid-stride, his breath going out of him. Marta, who had sheltered him once before, who had fed them and lied for them and asked nothing, and who was dead now because kindness near Theo Vane had a cost other people paid. Because stopping wasn't safe. Stopping was just slower. The consortium didn't need to find him if he stayed foundable—they needed only to wait, to send the next Corliss, the one after that, patient men with patient money, until the loose ends frayed apart one by one. Ines. The sister on the phone. The old woman with the geraniums, if it came to that.

There was no disappearing with this proof. There was only carrying it, forever, looking over his shoulder in every green valley on earth—or ending it.

And ending it meant one thing, and he had known what it was since the border of the pass, and he had been walking away from knowing it all evening.

He turned around and went back.

* * *

Ines was sitting on the co-op steps in the yellow light, her wrapped foot propped on a crate, eating bread the shopkeeper had given her. She watched him come up the street and read his face the way she read rock cores—with patience, looking for the fracture lines.

"They'll want you to testify," she said, before he'd even sat down. No preamble. She'd been waiting to say it.

"I know."

"Do you?" She set the bread down. "Because I've been watching you look at this town for an hour, Vane, and I want to be fair to you, so I'm going to say the whole thing out loud, once, and then never again." She held up fingers. "Testimony means going back. Not mailing the evidence—*going*. Sitting in front of the same inquiry board that banned you, in the same city, with the same lawyers in the room, and saying your name out loud while men who called you a murderer take notes." Second finger. "It means the bans get tested before they get lifted. You'd be in custody, probably. Some of it administrative, some of it not. Weeks, maybe months." Third finger. "It means the consortium knows exactly where you are the entire time. Walls don't stop money. Corliss is gone, but—" 

"I know what Corliss was," Theo said quietly.

"—but he was replaceable, yes." She lowered her hand. "And fourth. Fourth is the one I actually care about." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and her voice lost its list-making crispness and became just a tired woman talking to a friend in the dark. "If you testify and it goes wrong—if they find some way to twist it, bury it, discredit you a third time—you won't survive it. Not legally. *Personally.* You've spent seven years being the man the mountain broke. If you hand them the truth and they throw it back at you again, I don't think there's a valley on this continent far enough."

The moths ticked against the window glass. Down the street, the bakery was closing, its warmth spilling out and fading.

"And if I don't," Theo said. "If I mail it. If I vanish."

"Then it might work." She said it honestly; that was the worst part. "Documents are documents. The samples are unambiguous—any lab in the country reads those cores and the falsification falls apart. You might clear your name by post. You might bring the whole thing down without ever showing your face." She paused. "And you might watch it happen from a hillside and feel nothing, ever again, for the rest of your life. Because everyone who knows what it cost will be dead or gone, and the record will say the evidence was received anonymously, and you'll have saved yourself and lost the only version of the story that was ever actually yours."

Theo looked at his hands. They were wrecked—rope-burned, split, one nail blackened, the knuckles of his right hand scabbed twice over. Hands that had frozen once, on Kestrel Face, seven years ago, while a man died. Hands that had held an anchor line in a whiteout and not let go. Hands that had done a terrible necessary thing at the hut and would carry it.

"When I crossed the pass," he said slowly, "I kept thinking about the border. The real one, south of here. There's a manned post on the far side of Puerto Serrano. I've crossed borders illegally more times than I can count—passes, river fords, once through a drainage tunnel. It's the only way I've known how to do it. You go where there's no one to see you." He turned his palms up, the ruined hands open in the lamplight. "Going back means walking up to the booth. Giving my true name. To a man with a stamp and a ledger and absolutely no reason to be gentle about it."

"Yes," Ines said.

"It means the inquiry gets to ask me anything they want. Including the parts that are true. Including the part where I froze." His jaw worked. "I told you that in the cave because we were going to die and it seemed cowardly to die with it unsaid. Saying it in front of a hearing room, with the consortium's lawyers circling, waiting for exactly that—waiting for the moment I admit the original report wasn't entirely wrong—"

"They'll wait for it," Ines agreed. "And you'll give it to them. And here's what they won't understand, Vane, because men like that have never once been on a rope: admitting you froze and held anyway isn't a confession. It's the strongest thing in the whole file. Anyone can be brave on a good day. The inquiry needs to hear what you did on the worst one." She picked the bread back up, tore it, handed him half—an offering, an anchor, a small ordinary act in a night full of enormous ones. "But it's your name. I've carried my piece across a glacier for it. I'll carry it into any hearing room they build. What I won't do is decide for you."

Theo ate the bread. It was the best thing he had ever tasted, and he tasted almost none of it.

He thought about the green valley, and how badly he wanted it, and how the wanting was real and honest and not enough. He thought about Marta. He thought about the ledger at the border post, and his name spoken aloud in a public building for the first time in seven years, and how terrified he was of it, and how that terror felt—finally, unmistakably—different from the old fear. The old fear had been of being caught. This was the fear of being *seen*. Of standing still in the open and letting the whole world look at exactly what he was: a guide who froze, who ran, who killed, who carried, who came back.

Running had a shape he knew. Standing still was the blank page.

"The car comes in the morning," he said.

"Six o'clock. The police post is sending it."

He nodded. He looked down the darkening street, at the lit windows, at the life he could have had by Friday, and let it go—not easily, not cleanly, but completely, the way you release a rope under load: controlled, deliberate, both hands.

"Then I'll testify," Theo Vane said, and heard his true name settle back onto him like a coat that fit. "All of it. Everything they ask."

Ines exhaled, long and slow, and for the first time since the forest she smiled with her whole face. "Good," she said. "Because I already told the police your name."

He stared at her.

"You gave yours at the co-op phone," she said, entirely unrepentant, tearing off another piece of bread. "I figured one of us should sleep tonight."

Outside, far down the valley, thunder rolled off the high peaks—the mountain closing its season behind them, sealing the pass, shutting the door on everything they'd been. Theo finished the bread, checked the street one last time out of seven years' habit, and then, deliberately, stopped checking.

Tomorrow he'd walk into a police station and say *my name is Theo Vane*, and the world would begin deciding what that meant.

Tonight, for the first time in seven years, he let himself imagine staying.
