## 20. Two Countries

The valley opened beneath them like a held breath finally released.

For four days they had come down out of the Serrano in stages—Theo setting a pace that Ines's frostbitten feet could bear, camping low in the timber where the wind couldn't strip the heat from their bones, following a drainage that widened by degrees until it was less a river than a rumor of one, braided and shallow and running the color of old pewter. The snowline climbed away behind them as they descended, retreating up the peaks like something defeated. By the third morning there were birds again. By the fourth, the smell of cut hay.

Theo had forgotten that valleys smelled like this. Not pine, not stone-dust, not the cold iron breath of glacier ice—but grass, drying in windrows; woodsmoke from a stove lit against the morning chill; manure and diesel and ripening apples, all of it layered over the green wet sweetness of irrigated ground. Civilization, he thought, was mostly a smell. You could live years without it and not know what you were missing until it reached you on the wind and made your chest hurt.

Ines stopped at the edge of the first fenced pasture and stood very still, her pack hanging off her shoulders by one strap because the other had torn out two days ago and neither of them had the thread to fix it. Her hands were wrapped in fingerless wool gloves, her face scabbed along the cheekbone where Corliss's ridge had taken its toll of both of them. She looked at the fence—the wire, the cedar posts, the crooked gate with its loop of baling twine—as if she'd never seen such a thing before.

"A road," she said.

"Gravel," Theo said. "But a road."

A tractor moved along the far hedgerow, trailing a plume of dust, and Ines watched it go with an expression Theo recognized from his own mirror, years ago, in another valley: the look of a person re-entering the world and finding it both smaller and more miraculous than memory had kept it.

They followed the road. There was nothing else to do with a road but follow it, and after eleven days of choosing routes by slope angle and snow bridges and the lie of the terrain against the weather, it was a strange luxury to simply walk where the walking had already been decided for them. The road took them past the pasture, past a stand of poplars turning early gold, past a shrine of some kind—a wooden post hung with faded ribbons and a tin cup nailed at eye level, an offering to whatever saint watched over travelers—and down toward the river, which here ran slow and wide and unhurried, as if it too were glad to be out of the mountains.

The town appeared around a bend in the valley: two dozen rooftops, a church spire, a grain elevator standing against the sky like a monument to patience. Smoke rose from chimneys. Somewhere a dog was barking at nothing, or at everything, with the tireless conviction of dogs.

Ines stopped again. "There'll be telephones."

"There'll be telephones."

"And police."

"Probably." Theo looked at the town for a long moment. "And a border post."

She turned to him. He felt her looking before he saw it—that steady, unflinching attention she'd been giving him since the day he'd found her bleeding under a deadfall, the look of a woman who had learned in the worst weeks of her life exactly how much a person's choices weighed.

"The line's the river," she said. "Isn't it? We've been on the far side since the pass. This is—" she gestured at the valley, the town, the whole soft green world of it, "—this is the other country. The one that never banned you."

"I know which side we're on."

"Then you know you don't have to cross." Her voice was careful, the way it always was when she approached this subject, circling it like a guide approaches a cornice—testing, probing, ready to retreat. "You have the samples. You have my testimony. We could find a lawyer in this town, or the next one, or the one after that. We could send everything to the inquiry by post and stay on this side of the river until it's done. They can't extradite a man for a crime he didn't commit, Theo. They can't even prove he was ever here."

"They don't need to extradite me," Theo said. "They just need me to keep being nobody. That's been working fine for six years."

"Then keep being nobody for six more months. Until it's overturned. Then walk back across at your own time, on your own terms—"

"On my own terms." He said it quietly, without anger, and Ines stopped talking, because she heard what was underneath it. He turned from the town and looked back up the valley, at the mountains they'd come down out of, white and remote and utterly indifferent, holding their secrets the way they always had. Kestrel Face was somewhere in that massif, sixty miles north, invisible from here, but he could feel it anyway, the way you feel an old fracture in cold weather.

"You asked me once," he said, "in the cave, why I told you the truth about that day. About freezing."

"I remember."

"Because I was tired," he said. "That was all. Six years of carrying it, and it turned out the carrying was heavier than the thing itself. And I thought—I thought if I ever got clear of all this, if I ever actually got free, I wasn't going to spend my freedom doing the same thing I did while I was hunted. Hiding. Being a name that isn't mine. Because then what did any of it cost? What did Marta—" His voice caught on the name and he let it, deliberately, the way you let a wound air. "What did she die for, if I just build a quieter box to live in?"

Ines said nothing. Below them, the dog had stopped barking, and the silence of the valley was enormous.

"The inquiry doesn't need me there," Theo went on. "You're right. Your samples will break it open whether I'm sitting in the room or not. But there's a difference between being cleared and clearing yourself. One of them is something they do to you." He turned back to the town, to the road, to the river beyond it where the border waited, marked on no map he'd ever seen except by a change in the color of the mailbox flags. "The other one you have to do yourself. And it starts with saying my name out loud to somebody who has the power to make me regret it."

"It might mean jail," Ines said. "Crossing like that. They banned you from both countries. Even with the consortium exposed, the original charges haven't been formally withdrawn. You'd be handing them a man who ran."

"I'm done being handed things," Theo said. "I'd rather hand myself over."

* * *

They slept that night in a hay barn on the outskirts of town, having traded a story about a hunting trip gone long for the farmer's discretion and two plates of stew, and Theo lay awake under the sweet-smelling rafters listening to the horses shift below and understood that this was the last night of his old life, and that he should savor it, and that he couldn't. Sleep came anyway, eventually, dreamless and deep, the sleep of a body that had crossed a mountain range and was done asking questions.

In the morning he washed in the stock trough, shaved with the farmer's razor, and put on a clean shirt the farmer's wife pressed on him without being asked, and stood in the farmyard feeling like a stranger wearing his own skin correctly for the first time in years.

The border post sat where the gravel road met the bridge over the river: a low building of pale timber, a striped pole across the road, a flag he hadn't stood under since the inquiry. Two officers, judging by the vehicles. Maybe three. It was, Theo thought, absurdly small for the size of what he was about to do inside it.

He and Ines walked the last mile side by side. She carried the core samples now—he'd insisted, and she'd refused, and they'd compromised by splitting them between the packs, each of them carrying half the proof, so that no single fall or theft or arrest could end it. The tubes rode against his spine, light and terrible, six inches of drilled stone and sealed documentation that had cost eleven days and a burning town and a man's life at a frozen hut, and would, if the world was capable of justice, cost a consortium considerably more.

At fifty meters from the post, Ines stopped.

"I can go first," she said. "Give my statement, get them focused on me, and you turn around and walk. They won't stop you. They won't even know to look."

"No."

"Theo—"

"Ines." He said her name and she went quiet, and he realized it was the first time he'd said it plainly, without urgency, without blood or snow attached to it. Just her name, in a farm lane, in morning light. "Whatever happens in there, you walk through that door and you tell them everything. Everything you told me on the mountain. Don't soften it, don't manage it, don't protect me. If they ask about me, tell them the truth: that I came back on purpose. That matters. Make them write it down."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded once, the sharp economical nod of a woman who had argued her case fully and lost cleanly, and who respected the losing more than she'd have respected a win.

"Together, then," she said.

"Together."

* * *

The border post smelled of paper and floor wax and coffee reheated too many times. A ceiling fan turned with the lazy skepticism of machinery that had never once been needed. Behind a scarred wooden desk sat a woman of perhaps fifty, gray-streaked hair pinned back, reading glasses pushed up into it, a radio murmuring static and cattle prices at her elbow. Her nameplate read OFFICER BRENN. She looked up as they entered with the expression of someone who had processed ten thousand crossings and expected nothing from the ten-thousand-and-first.

"Purpose of entry?"

Theo set his pack on the floor. Carefully, because of the samples. Then he stood straight, put his hands loose at his sides, and did the thing.

"My name is Theodore Vane," he said.

The words came out steady. That surprised him. He'd rehearsed them all morning, all night, all the way down the valley, and in rehearsal they'd always snagged, always stuck in his throat like a fishbone—but aloud, in the warm paper-smelling room, they came out clean, and hearing them in his own voice made the room seem to tilt slightly and right itself, the way a boat does when you step aboard.

Officer Brenn's pen paused above the ledger.

"I'm entering voluntarily," Theo continued, "to surrender to the authorities of this country and to place myself at the disposal of the inquiry currently investigating the Kestrel Face disaster and the conduct of the Meridian Consolidated survey program. I am the Theodore Vane named in the 2019 findings. I understand there are outstanding charges. I'm here to answer them."

The fan turned. The radio murmured. Somewhere behind the partition, a kettle began its climb toward whistling.

Brenn took off her glasses. She looked at Theo—not at his papers, not yet, at him—with the unhurried appraisal of a woman who had spent thirty years watching people try to be other than they were, and who knew every species of lie that walked through a border door: the smuggler's blandness, the refugee's desperation, the criminal's rehearsed calm. What she saw, apparently, fit none of the categories, because she looked for a long time.

"Vane," she said. "The guide. The one from the papers." It wasn't a question. Everyone in these valleys remembered; the disaster had happened close enough that the ash had dusted windshields two counties over. "You're supposed to be dead. Or in Patagonia. Depends who tells it."

"Both versions had their appeal."

Her mouth twitched, almost a smile, and died. She pulled a form from a drawer, squared it on the desk, and picked up her pen—and then set the pen down again, and laced her fingers, and gave him her full attention, which Theo understood suddenly was worth more than any kindness.

"You understand what you're doing," she said. "Once I log this name, it goes up the chain. There are people who've had six years' interest in that name, and not all of them work for the government."

"I'm counting on the ones who do."

"And if the inquiry goes the other way? If the samples don't break open like your geologist thinks?" A glance at Ines, who stood with her chin up and her wrapped hands folded, radiating the particular authority of a scientist in possession of facts. "If the old findings stand? You'll have walked into the room that banned you and handed them the keys."

"Then they'll ban me from a third thing," Theo said. "Myself. And I'll still know what I know, and I'll still have done what I did, and none of it will have been done hiding in a valley under a false name." He paused. "I froze once, on that mountain. On Kestrel Face. It's in the report, and it's true, and I've spent six years pretending the rest of the report was true too, because it was easier than finding out. I'm done with easier."

Brenn studied him a moment longer. Then she picked up the pen, uncapped it, and wrote, in a firm schoolroom hand, in the ledger that had waited six years for the entry:

**VANE, THEODORE. Voluntary surrender.**

"Sit down, Mr. Vane," she said. "This takes a while, and the kettle's nearly up. You'll want tea. Both of you." She lifted the radio handset, keyed it, and spoke into six years of silence with the mildness of a woman ordering feed stock: "Brenn at the Serrano crossing. I need the district office. Tell Superintendent Achebe—" she glanced at the form, at the name, at the man sitting very straight in the hard chair with half a mountain range's worth of truth in his pack, "—tell him the Vane file can come out of storage. He's just walked in the front door."

Outside, the fan turned, and the flag snapped in the valley wind, and Theo Vane sat in the country that had erased him, under his own name, waiting—for the tea, for the superintendent, for the inquiry, for whatever came next—and found that his hands, for the first time in six years, were perfectly still.
