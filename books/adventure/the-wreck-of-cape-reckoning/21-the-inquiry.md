## 21. The Inquiry

The hearing room in Meridian had been a courthouse once, before the consortium bought the city's better angles and the government moved its tribunals into rented offices above a bank. Theo noticed that first—the way the room tried to look like something it wasn't, the wood paneling too new to have heard an argument, the brass fittings polished by hands that had never gripped anything colder than a pen. He sat at the witness table with his hands flat on the surface and made himself breathe the way he taught clients to breathe at altitude: slow, deliberate, each one a small act of will.

Three months since the pass. Three months of depositions, of lawyers who spoke about him in the third person while he was in the room, of hotel rooms where he lay awake listening for boots on stairs that never came. The frostbite had healed badly in two toes; he could feel them now only as a dull argument between nerve and memory. Ines sat two chairs down, her arm still in a brace from the crevasse, her spine straight as a survey stake.

The chairman was a woman named Aldous-Prent, silver-haired, with the patient face of someone who had spent thirty years watching men lie in comfortable chairs. She did not look at Theo the way the inquiry board had looked at him seven years ago. That board had looked at him like weather—something to be explained, filed, moved on from. Aldous-Prent looked at him like a man.

"Mr. Vane," she said. "Before we proceed, I want to be clear about the record. You are here under your own name. You came to this hearing voluntarily. Is that correct?"

"It is."

"You understand that testimony given here can be used against you as well as for you."

"I understand." He almost smiled. "I've been living with what people use against me for a long time. I'd like to try the other thing."

A murmur went through the gallery. The consortium's counsel—a soft-spoken man named Herrick whose suits probably cost more than Theo's last three years of wages combined—shifted his papers with the delicacy of a man rearranging cards he believed were marked.

* * *

Ines watched Theo from two chairs away and thought about how little she had understood him on the river.

She had met him as a ferryman, a hard-eyed man who hauled crates and asked no questions, and she had thought: this is what fear makes. Then the gorge, then Salt Landing, then the fire, then the glacier where he held her over four hundred feet of blue ice on a rope he couldn't afford to lose, and she had thought: this is what guilt makes. It had taken the cave, and his voice breaking on the words *I froze, Ines, I stood there and I froze*, for her to understand that both answers were wrong, or rather that they were the same answer wearing different coats. Fear and guilt were just the names other people gave to a man carrying weight he hadn't chosen.

Now he sat in a hearing room in a borrowed jacket, and she watched the tribunal read the documents she had carried out of the mountains against her own blood, and she felt something she had not expected to feel in this place: calm.

The core samples lay in their case on the presentation table, sealed, catalogued, witnessed. Beside them, the surveys from the strongbox under the cook's floor—Kestrel Face, seven years ago, the slope stability analysis that had been altered. She had watched Herrick's face when the originals were laid beside the consortium's published report. Most people would have missed it. Ines had spent her career reading rock faces for the hairline fracture that meant everything, and she saw it: a flicker, there and gone, like a stone shifting somewhere high on a mountain in the dark.

"The published analysis," Aldous-Prent said, "indicates a shear strength figure of forty-one kilopascals along the failure plane. The field data in your possession indicates twenty-six."

"Twenty-six point three," Ines said. "The difference matters. At forty-one, the face reads as stable under forecast loading. At twenty-six, it fails. Someone took the field numbers and raised them until the mountain looked safe."

"And you can authenticate these documents?"

"They're my surveys. My handwriting, my sampling protocol, my core logs. I ran the Kestrel Face assessment. I was told my raw figures were 'recalibrated' by the senior review office. I signed off because I was thirty-one years old and I wanted to keep my career." Her voice didn't shake. She had rehearsed this confession on the glacier, in the whiteout, in the hours when staying alive seemed less certain than telling the truth. "That is the part I am most ashamed of, and it is also the part that proves the documents are real. No forger invents his own complicity."

Herrick rose, unhurried. "Dr. Okafor. These documents came into your possession through circumstances we'll address shortly—an expedition, I understand, that ended in considerable tragedy. Isn't it true that you cannot establish a chain of custody? That these materials passed through the hands of Mr. Vane, a man twice banned from professional mountaineering, whose judgment in exactly this kind of terrain has already been called into question?"

There it was. Ines felt Theo go still beside her—not tense, but quiet, the quiet of a man standing aside to let weather pass.

"He means," Ines said, "can you trust evidence carried by a disgraced man."

"I'm asking about chain of custody, Dr. Okafor."

"So am I answering." She turned to face the tribunal directly. "You've heard the story of Kestrel Face. A guide named Theo Vane led a party onto a slope that failed. Nine people died. The inquiry found his route decision reckless, found his pre-trip assessment inadequate, and banned him from guiding in this country and the next. What you were not shown—what was buried in the same office that buried my figures—is that the slope was already failing. That the consortium's own engineers knew it. That the hazard report that should have closed that face for the season was falsified six weeks before Vane ever tied into a rope."

She let it sit. Let the room hear the shape of it.

"Chain of custody," she said, "is a question about whether evidence has been handled honestly. These documents were handled dishonestly from the moment they were created. That is precisely why they exist. The consortium didn't lose control of the truth. They manufactured a false version and hid the real one, and the real one stayed hidden until men with guns came to burn it—and even then, it survived, because two people neither of you trusted happened to be stubborn enough to carry it over a mountain range ahead of the snows."

* * *

Theo listened to her and thought: *she's better at this than I will ever be.*

When his turn came, Aldous-Prent's questions were simpler, and harder.

"Mr. Vane. Seven years ago, this body found you responsible for nine deaths. Today you sit here claiming those findings were built on falsified data. I want to ask you about the day itself. Not the data. The day."

He had known this question was coming. He had climbed toward it for seven years, through two countries and a hundred false names, and it had been waiting at the top the whole time, patient as weather.

"The morning of the ascent," he said, "I had a bad feeling about the face. Not data. A feeling. The snowpack sounded hollow under my probe in two places. I noted it. I didn't act on it, because the official assessment said stable, because I had a client roster full of people who'd paid a year's savings, because turning back meant being the guide who turned back." He paused. "And then, when it started—when I heard it start—I froze. Four seconds. Maybe five. Long enough that my second rope team was still in the runout when I should have been moving them. I've done the arithmetic ten thousand times. If I'd moved immediately, I think six people live instead of three."

The room was very quiet.

"The falsified report doesn't excuse that," Theo said. "I want that in the record. The consortium stole the truth, but the four seconds were mine. I've paid for them every day since. What I object to—what I crossed a mountain range to object to—is that I was convicted of the whole disaster when I was guilty of five seconds of it, and the men who wrote the numbers that put all those people on that slope have never spent one night lying awake at all."

Aldous-Prent regarded him for a long moment. "Thank you," she said, and it sounded, for the first time all week, like it cost her nothing to say.

* * *

They broke for the afternoon session, and in the corridor outside, Ines found Theo standing at the window looking down at the street, at the ordinary traffic of a city that had forgotten him so thoroughly it had never bothered to remember.

"Nervous?" she asked.

"No." He considered. "Yes. Not about the verdict. About—" He gestured vaguely at himself. "After. Everyone keeps asking what I'll do if they clear me. Like clearing me is the finish line. But it doesn't give me back seven years. It doesn't give Marta her—" His jaw tightened. He still said her name carefully, like setting down something breakable. "It doesn't un-burn the town."

"No," Ines said. "It doesn't. Justice isn't restoration. It's just justice. It's the floor you build the rest on."

"That's a very geologist thing to say."

"We think in strata." She smiled faintly. "Everything rests on everything else."

* * *

The afternoon belonged to the documents, and the documents did their work with the terrible patience of evidence.

Herrick fought. He fought the authentication, brought his own experts, produced affidavits from consortium engineers whose signatures trembled slightly on the page—or perhaps that was only the photocopying. He fought the chain of custody, the methodology, the sample integrity. And then, at four o'clock, Aldous-Prent set before him a single page: an internal memorandum, recovered from the strongbox, dated eleven days before the Kestrel Face ascent, addressed to the consortium's operations director, containing the sentence *recommend continued client operations; revised figures support stability through season end.*

"Mr. Herrick," she said, "does your client wish to contest the authenticity of a document bearing its own letterhead, its own distribution codes, and the initials of its own operations director—who, I will note, currently sits on your client's board?"

Herrick looked at the page for a long time. When he sat down, he did not rise again.

The indictment came the following morning, read aloud in a room so silent Theo could hear the radiators ticking. The Meridian Consortium for Resource Development, three named officers, the former senior review office director—all of it, conspiracy, falsification, negligence causing death. Warrants issued. Assets frozen. The chairman of the board, a man named Ellery Vance whom Theo had never met and had hated for seven years without knowing his face, was taken into custody at his home at six that evening, according to the evening news, while his security detail stood aside and let it happen.

Ines watched the footage in the hotel bar with Theo, and neither of them cheered. It was strange, she thought, how anticlimactic justice was when it finally arrived—just men in coats walking a rich man past cameras, just words spoken in a rented room above a bank. All that weight, all that distance, all that ice, and at the end it came down to paperwork and handcuffs.

But then she looked at Theo—at the man sitting very straight on the barstool, staring at the screen with an expression she had seen on summiteers looking back down at the ground they'd crossed—and she understood that the paperwork was not the point. The point was that a man who had spent seven years being a rumor, a cautionary tale, a name on a blacklist, had walked into a room and said *my name is Theo Vane* and the world had been forced to write it down correctly.

* * *

The formal findings were announced on the last day. Aldous-Prent read them in a voice that never hurried:

The Kestrel Face findings of seven years prior were overturned in their entirety. The finding of recklessness against Theodore Vane was vacated. The professional bans, both of them, were lifted. A formal apology was entered into the record—read aloud, witnessed, archived—which was not the same as the seven years returned, and everyone in the room knew it, and Aldous-Prent said so herself, which was why Theo decided he believed her.

Then she paused, and looked at him over her glasses.

"Mr. Vane. This tribunal has one further matter. The consortium's settlement offer remains open—substantial compensation, no admission required, discretion assured. Counsel informs me you have declined to respond. Do you wish to state your position for the record?"

Theo stood. His borrowed jacket suddenly fit him better than it had all week.

"I decline it," he said. "All of it. I didn't cross that mountain for money. I crossed it because the truth was up there and somebody had to carry it down." He looked around the room, at the lawyers, the stenographers, the gallery. "Put in the record that the families get whatever compensation exists. Every cent. I'll sign whatever's needed to make that happen."

He sat down. Ines, beside him, did not say anything, because there was nothing to say that the mountains hadn't already said better.

Outside, the afternoon light lay long and gold across the courthouse steps, and for the first time in seven years, Theo Vane stood in public under his own name, cleared, unbanned, free—and felt the strangest sensation, which was that freedom, after so long, weighed almost as much as the chains had.

He turned to Ines on the steps. "So," he said. "What does a man do with a life he stopped expecting to get back?"

Ines smiled, and reached into her bag, and handed him an envelope with a postmark he wouldn't understand until he opened it.
