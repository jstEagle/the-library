## 1. The Mood of the Water

A river will tell you everything about itself if you're willing to shut up and listen. Most men aren't. Most men stand at the wheel with their opinions rattling around like loose coins in a tin cup, and the river takes one look at them and decides to drown them out of spite.

I'd spent twenty-two years learning to keep my mouth shut.

The *Maren's Mercy* — named for no Maren in particular, bought off a bankrupt widow in Corvane who cried when she signed the bill of sale, which should have been my first warning — was a hundred and ten feet of bad decisions held together with oakum and stubbornness. Her boiler coughed like a consumptive uncle. Her paddle wheel had a wobble you could set a watch by, if your watch was broken. But she drew four feet even loaded heavy, and on a river running low as this one was, four feet was the difference between a working captain and a man explaining to his creditors why his boat was now a reef.

Low water. Late season. The kind of autumn where the mountains hoard their rain and the river runs thin and mean, showing its bones. Every sandbar that had spent the summer hiding under six feet of brown water was out now, sunning itself like a snake on a flat rock, waiting for some fool to come along and step on it.

I was not that fool. Not today, anyway.

"Bar coming up on the left," I said to the wheelhouse in general, because talking to yourself is only madness if nobody answers, and the *Mercy* always answered — in groans, in creaks, in the particular complaint her sternwheel made when I asked her for anything resembling speed.

Tam Osei looked up from where he was coiling line by the starboard window. Sixteen years old, all elbows and appetite, with the kind of face that made old women want to feed him and customs officers want to search him. "Left? You sure, Captain? Looked like it swung right past Miller's Bend."

"It did swing right past Miller's Bend. Then it thought better of it." I eased the wheel over a spoke. "River changed its mind sometime in the last week. See how the current's folding? That crease in the water, there — where it goes dark and then light again?"

Tam squinted. He was a good boy about squinting. Half the deckhands I'd hired over the years treated my lessons like weather — something that happened to them whether they liked it or not, to be endured until it passed. Tam actually watched. That was going to get him killed someday, or made him worth something. With boys his age it usually went one way or the other and there was no telling which until afterward.

"The dark part's deep," Tam said slowly. "The light part's shallow."

"The dark part's *moving* deep. Different animal entirely." I pointed with my chin, hands staying on the wheel where they belonged. "Still water can be thirty feet down or three inches over a bar. Moving water tells you what it's doing. Dark fold means the channel's cutting along the left bank. The bar's pushed out from the point."

"And the birds?"

"What about the birds."

"You always look at the birds first."

Because the birds know things, is why. Gulls work the seams where current stacks up baitfish, and baitfish don't stack over sand. A line of gulls sitting quiet on a gravel spit meant the spit was dry enough to hold their weight, which meant somewhere below it the channel had wandered off to visit its other family. But I wasn't going to hand the boy the whole catechism in one morning. A man who gives away all his knowledge at once has nothing left to feel superior about.

"Birds are lazy," I said. "Trust the water. Water never lies. It exaggerates, it misleads, it'll show you a mirror where there's a hole — but it never lies outright. People lie. Birds just want fish. Water's the only honest thing on this river besides me."

Tam grinned. "You lied to the harbormaster at Vell's Landing so hard he wrote it down."

"That was diplomacy. Different category entirely."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about running a river for a living: it isn't the river that gets you. Rivers are predictable. Give me any stretch of water between here and the headwaters and a day to look at it, and I'll tell you where it wants to kill you and how much warning it'll give. No — what gets you is the towns. The landings. The places where the boat stops and people come aboard carrying their troubles like luggage, looking for somebody to haul those troubles upriver or downriver, anywhere but here.

Stopping costs. That's the whole philosophy, if I have one, and I suppose after twenty-two years I ought to. Every hour tied to a landing is an hour the river drops, an hour the cargo spoils or the buyer finds another seller or the law finds another reason. Every friend you make in a river town is a debt with a smile on it. Every night you spend ashore is a night somebody learns your schedule, your route, the name of your boat, the color of your eyes.

I'd stopped too long exactly once in my life, back when I was young enough to think a place could hold me. Cost me everything I'd had, which wasn't much, and taught me the lesson at a fair price, which was lucky. Since then: load, run, unload, run. The *Mercy* and I kept moving the way sharks do, and for the same reason.

It wasn't loneliness. People assumed it was loneliness, because people assume everything sad is lonely and everything else is fine. It wasn't that. It was arithmetic. Staying put anywhere too long cost more than it paid. I'd done the sums. I'd done them every year for two decades, and they came out the same every time, and I trusted them the way I trusted the water — completely, and with the understanding that someday they'd lie to me.

* * *

The customs launch found us an hour past Miller's Bend, which was rude of it, because I'd specifically arranged my morning to avoid that.

She came off the government dock at Tarrow Cut — white hull, blue stripe, a little brass whistle that she loved to blow for no reason whatsoever, the way small dogs bark at carts. Two men aboard. One at the tiller wearing the expression of a man who'd wanted to be a soldier and settled for paperwork. The other standing at the bow with a clipboard, and I want to be clear that the clipboard told me more than the launch did. Customs men carry ledgers. Inspectors carry manifests. A man with a clipboard is hunting something specific, and he hasn't found it yet, and he's annoyed about both facts.

"Customs!" the tiller man bellowed, unnecessarily, across maybe forty yards of open water. "Heave to for inspection!"

Now. There are two ways to handle a customs launch. The first is to heave to, be inspected, lose an afternoon, and possibly lose whatever they decide looks suspicious, which on my boat could be anything from the brandy under the pilothouse floor to the brandy in my own personal flask. The second is to not be where they are, which requires either speed I didn't have or water they couldn't follow.

I had water they couldn't follow.

"Hold on to something," I told Tam, and put the wheel hard left.

The *Mercy* came around with all the grace of a drunk aunt changing her mind mid-dance, and I took her straight at the point of the bar I'd been reading since dawn. The one the gulls were sitting on. The one with the dark fold of current sliding along its shoulder.

"Captain—" Tam started.

"Watch the lead line. And trust me."

"I'm watching the lead line. I'm trusting you less."

That's the spirit, I almost said. Didn't. Needed my breath for the wheel.

The bar's shoulder ran out maybe forty yards before it dropped into the cut, and the channel along its edge was honest water — five feet, maybe five and a half, deep enough for the *Mercy* if I threaded the needle, far too skinny for a government launch whose pilot had never once read a river in his life and steered by the map, and maps on a low river are historical fiction.

I came in tight. Tighter than I liked. The *Mercy*'s hull whispered over the bar's shoulder — I felt it through the deck boards, that faint shudder of water going thin beneath the keel, the river saying *this close, Gideon, this close is what you get* — and then the fold took us and we dropped into the cut and I had forty yards of deep water and a bend between me and the blue-striped launch.

Behind us, the whistle blew. Angry, now. Then a grinding crunch that was music to my ears, which tells you something about my ears.

"Did they—" Tam leaned out the window.

"Ran the bar. Exactly where the map says the channel is." I eased the wheel back amidships and let the *Mercy* settle into her run. "Map's two years old. River moved. River always moves."

"They'll be stuck for hours."

"They'll be stuck until the survey boat comes, which won't come until tomorrow, because the survey boat's crew is currently drinking in Tarrow Cut." I allowed myself the luxury of a whole breath. "By the time they're free, we'll be through the Narrows and their jurisdiction ends at the county line. Which is that willow stand, there."

Tam looked back at the willows like they were a border crossing, which technically they were. "You knew they'd run the bar."

"I knew they'd steer by the chart. Charts are how the government proves it knows things it doesn't." I rolled a cigarette one-handed, which is a skill I recommend to anyone planning a long career of having only one hand free. "Boy, write this down in whatever ledger you keep behind your eyes: the law moves on paper. The river moves on water. If you're ever caught between them, stand on the water."

"That's not a real lesson. That's a fortune cookie."

"We don't have fortune cookies on this river."

"No," Tam admitted. "We have you."

* * *

We made the Narrows by midafternoon, and I'll say this for the Narrows: they don't care about charts, customs men, captains, or God. They're a twelve-mile throat where the valley pinches down and the Kessler River remembers it used to be a mountain stream and still has opinions about it. White water at the top end in spring. In late autumn, low water, it was a maze of gravel bars and deadfall and current that switched sides every half mile like a politician.

I ran it slow, which is the trick nobody believes. Fast water rewards patience. You idle through, you read each seam twice, you let the current do the rowing. The *Mercy*'s engine stayed at half-throttle and my hands stayed light on the wheel and for twelve miles neither Tam nor I said a word, because the Narrows deserve silence the way churches do.

Coming out the bottom, the valley opened wide and green-gold, and there it was.

Mount Kessler.

Every river town pretends its landmark is famous. Kessler actually was, though mostly for being in the way. It stood up off the eastern bank like a fist through a table — nine thousand feet of granite and pine with its shoulders already dusted white, holding the sky up whether the sky asked or not. The river bent around its western foot, lazy and broad here, spreading out into shallows and gravel bars the way rivers do when they've finally got room to breathe.

Below the mountain, the light was doing that thing it does in October — coming in long and amber and forgiving, making even the wrecked and rusted look painted. There were ruins along the near bank, half-swallowed by brush. Old foundations. A chimney standing alone in a field like a man waiting for a train that stopped running twenty years ago. Nobody built there anymore. Nobody talked much about why, which on a river full of talkers is its own kind of answer.

I'd been meaning to ask about that for twenty years. I hadn't. Some questions you leave in the water.

"Captain." Tam was at the rail, forward, pointing. "There's something on the bar. Down there. Off the port bow."

I saw it before he finished pointing, and I want to say I saw nothing, if that makes sense. A pale shape snagged on the long gravel bar below the mountain's shadow, where the current spread thin and the water went glass-clear over stone. Driftwood, probably. A dead fish, belly-up. A piece of someone's lost laundry, bleached by sun.

Except driftwood doesn't sit square. Dead fish don't hold corners. And laundry doesn't glint — just once, just briefly, as the afternoon light swung — with the particular dull flash of metal under water.

I brought the *Mercy* around in a long, easy arc, the way you approach anything you're not sure you want to meet. The bar came closer. The pale shape resolved and refused to resolve, playing coy in the ripples.

"Drop the hook," I said.

"Here?" Tam was already moving. "Captain, that's not a landing—"

"I know what it isn't. Drop the hook."

He dropped the hook. The *Mercy* swung and settled, her wheel slapping the water in that lazy rhythm she fell into when I wasn't asking anything of her. I stood at the rail and looked down at the bar, at the pale thing lying in eighteen inches of clear water with the mountain standing over it like a witness who'd seen everything and intended to say nothing.

Twenty-two years on this river, and the river had given me cargo, trouble, money, scars, and one very memorable case of fever. It had never once given me a gift. Rivers don't give. They only lend, and the interest is ruinous.

Whatever that was, pale and square-cornered and glinting, it wasn't a gift.

It was a delivery.

And some cold, patient part of me — the part that reads water, the part that keeps the sums — understood, standing there in the amber light with Mount Kessler's shadow reaching out across the shallows toward my boat, that the river had finally decided I'd stopped running long enough.

"Get the grapnel line," I heard myself say. "And Tam — bring the strong hooks. Whatever that is, it's heavy."

It was heavy. Lord, it was heavy. But I didn't know that yet. All I knew was the mood of the water, and the mood of the water, that evening, below the mountain, was the mood of a thing that has been waiting.
