## 2. What the River Gives Up

The pale thing on the gravel bar turned out to be a chest, and I want it on the record that my first instinct was to leave it there.

That's not callousness. That's arithmetic. Twenty years of running this river has taught me that anything the water gives up for free, somebody downstream is already paying to get back. Salvage law is simple: finders keep, until the losers arrive with guns and paperwork, in whichever order their lawyers prefer. A man who stops for every interesting thing on a riverbank dies of curiosity long before old age gets its shot at him.

But Tam was already wading.

"Captain! Captain, it's got bands on it. Iron bands. And it's *heavy*, look how it sits in the gravel—"

"Everything heavy sits heavy, Tam. That's what heavy means."

He wasn't listening. He never listens when his voice goes up like that, which is most of the time, which is why I keep him—because a deckhand who doesn't listen is useless, but a deckhand who doesn't listen about the right things is worth three of the obedient kind. He'd spotted the pale shape from the wheelhouse while I was still reading the bar itself: the way the current split around it, the silt fan spreading off its downstream shoulder, the shallow draft of water going glassy where it should have been riffled. Something big enough to change the river's mind about its own course.

I brought the *Maren*—no. Bad habit. I brought the *Kestrel* in against the bar with her bow upstream and dropped anchor in two feet of water, which is the kind of thing you can only do in late summer, when the Kessler runs shy and apologetic, showing bones it spends the rest of the year hiding. Low water is honest water. It was low water that had given up the chest at all; come spring flood, that bar would be six feet under and rolling.

Tam stood hip-deep in the shallows, hands on the thing, grinning like he'd found a saint's fingerbone.

"It's lead," he said. "Feel it. Under the wood—it's lined with lead."

"I'll take your word."

"No, feel it." He knocked a knuckle against the lid, and the sound came back wrong—dead, dense, the sound of something built to keep things *in*, or keep things *out*. "Who lines a chest with lead?"

"Men who don't trust the river," I said, "or men who don't trust the people downriver of the river."

The chest sat maybe four feet long, oak gone gray as driftwood, iron straps furred with rust but still doing their job. No lock hasp that I could see—the front face was smooth except for one detail, pressed into a plate of some darker metal set flush into the lid:

A crest. A tower over crossed keys, ringed by nine stars.

I knew it before I finished looking at it. That's the part I keep circling back to, even now. There was no slow recognition, no puzzled squinting. My eyes went to it and my stomach dropped through the bottom of the boat like a stone through thin ice, all in the same half-second, the way your body knows a stair isn't there before your mind catches up.

"Gideon?" Tam said. "You've gone funny."

"I've gone thoughtful. There's a difference."

I hadn't seen that crest since I was small enough to hide under tables. Wax on documents my father burned without reading—or read first, then burned anyway, I never learned which was worse. Pressed into the soft lead sealing a crate in a cellar we weren't supposed to enter, the night men came to our street with lanterns and lists. Tower and keys and nine stars, stamped on everything, and then stamped out of everything, until the maps had a blank space where a country used to be and schoolmasters taught the blank space like it had always been blank.

Vhaloria.

Nobody said the name anymore. Not because it was forbidden—I think it was actually forbidden once, briefly, in the first years after—but because saying it invited questions, and questions about Vhaloria had a documented tendency to end badly for the asker. The war that erased it wasn't called a war. It was called a *resolution*. You resolve a debt. You resolve a dispute. Vhaloria got resolved so thoroughly that its rivers were renamed, its roads renumbered, and its survivors issued new papers with new names in a new alphabet, if they got papers at all.

And here was its seal, sitting in eighteen inches of Kessler water, waiting for me like a bill twenty years overdue.

"We should open it," Tam said.

"We should absolutely not open it."

"But it might be treasure."

"It might be trouble with a paint job. Treasure and trouble are the same animal, boy. One's just been fed better."

He looked at me with that expression he does, the one that means he's filed my answer under *things Gideon says instead of explaining* and will retrieve it later at the worst possible moment. He's seventeen. Everything goes into storage for later retrieval. I used to be the same, back when I stored things under tables.

Here's what I didn't tell him, standing there with the water pulling at my boots: I recognized more than the crest. I recognized the *workmanship*. The dark metal plate, the way the stars were struck rather than cast, the fine double-line border—that was archive work. Chancery work. My father had hands that made things like that. He'd been a scrivener before whatever he became after, and I remembered—God help me, I remembered sitting on a cellar floor at seven years old, watching those same nine stars take shape under his stylus, wax pooling gold around them, and him saying, *a seal is a promise wearing armor, Gideon. Never stamp anything you wouldn't say aloud.*

Then the lanterns came up our street, and I stopped collecting memories from that cellar permanently.

"Help me get it aboard," I heard myself say.

So much for arithmetic.

* * *

It took both of us and a block and tackle and most of an hour, because lead-lined oak full of river water weighs roughly what regret weighs, which is to say more than you planned for. We rigged it amidships under the cargo awning, lashed it down, and Tam immediately wanted to pry at the lid with a marlinspike.

"Touch it and you're swimming home."

"You always say swimming like it's a punishment. I grew up on this river."

"Then you know what's in it. That's the punishment."

He backed off, but he kept circling the chest like a gull around a fish cart, and I let him, because watching him circle told me things. The seal plate faced up. The rust was worst on the underside. The wood had been underwater a long time—years, not months—but the iron bands were newer than the oak, replaced at least once. Somebody had maintained this chest. Somebody had cared about what was inside it right up until the moment they lost it, or were made to lose it, or lost themselves.

Upstream of the bar, Mount Kessler stood with its shoulders in cloud, white already above the timberline though it was barely past midsummer up here in the high country. The mountain doesn't care what washes down off it. Landslides, dead men, sealed chests, whole nations—it takes them all and grinds them fine and feeds them to the river, and the river carries them down to people like me, who make their living on other people's leavings.

I've never liked the mountain. This is relevant later, so note it.

We ran downriver through the afternoon, and I did something I almost never do, which is run the boat badly. Twice I let the *Kestrel* drift wide coming around bends because I was below, turning the chest over in my hands, finding nothing to turn it over *with*. No keyhole. No hinges visible—the whole rear face was one smooth welded seam, which meant it opened from somewhere else or opened never. Lead-lined, seam-welded, banded twice, sealed with chancery work. That's not a strongbox. Strongboxes are meant to be opened by their owners. That's a coffin for paper.

Because it would be paper. Whatever else it held—and I made myself stop guessing, and failed—something had been sealed in there by men who feared fire, water, rot, and readers, in that order.

At dusk I tied up at a bend I know, a cutbank with cottonwoods and no neighbors, and cooked rice with dried fish and too much salt, and Tam talked the entire time about salvage shares and what lead sells for and whether I'd ever heard of the Vhalorian crown jewels, and I said the crown jewels were a story for children, which is true, and also that the children's stories were mostly lies, which is truer.

"You know about them," he said suddenly. "About Vhaloria. You knew that seal."

"I know seals. It's a hazard of the trade."

"You went white. I've seen you read your own death in the water twice and you didn't go white either time."

I looked at him across the cookfire. Seventeen, all elbows and questions, orphaned by the same flood that orphaned half the river towns that year, taken on as ballast and promoted to crew through sheer refusal to be jettisoned. I have spent five years making sure he asks exactly as many questions as he wants, because I remember being a boy whose questions got slapped down, and I have spent those same five years hoping he'd never ask *that* question.

"The country stopped existing," I said. "Before you were born. There's nothing to know."

"There's a chest on your boat with its name on it."

"There's a chest on my boat with nobody's name on it. That plate is a crest, not a signature."

He let it go, which frightened me more than pushing would have. Tam letting go of a question is Tam setting it down carefully in dry storage, labeled, dated, waiting.

* * *

I waited until he was asleep, because of course I did. Some doors you don't open in daylight.

The moon was up, half-full and unhelpful, and the river talked to itself along the hull the way it does at night, that low endless mutter, and I knelt beside the chest with a cold lamp and a cold feeling and worked the flat of my knife around the seal plate until it rocked loose.

Underneath, where the plate had covered the wood, there was a keyhole after all. Small, brass, corroded green. And scratched into the oak beside it, in a hand I would have known anywhere, in any century, in the dark, at the bottom of the sea—

Not a name. A number. A date, actually. And beneath the date, three words, cut deep enough to survive the river:

*Deliver it whole.*

My father wrote left-handed and pressed hard. I know the pressure of his letters the way you know a voice. I sat back on my heels in the moonlight and listened to the river muttering and felt twenty years fold up like a chart, all the blank spaces suddenly filled in with one line of his handwriting, and understood that the chest hadn't found me by accident, because the river doesn't deal accidents to men who grew up on its floor.

There was no key in my pocket. There was no key anywhere on the boat. But the lock was old brass and older spite, and I had a knife, a marlinspike, and a childhood of learning locks I wasn't supposed to learn, and none of that mattered, because my hands were shaking too hard to use any of it.

Gideon Vasquez does not shake. Gideon Vasquez reads sandbars, dodges customs launches, keeps moving, owes nothing, fears nothing he'll admit to. Gideon Vasquez knelt on his own deck at midnight and could not make his hands behave, because inside that box—behind his father's handwriting, behind a seal from a country that officially never happened—was the reason his father walked out one October night with a leather satchel and never came back, and the reason the men with lanterns came to our street a week later asking after him with a politeness that smelled like lamp oil.

I put the plate back. I covered the scratch with it, exact, careful, the way you'd close a dead man's eyes.

Then I sat against the rail with the cold lamp and watched the mountain hold its cloud, and thought about the pass over Kessler closing with the snows, weeks away yet, and about how far upriver a determined man could get in those weeks, and about how many kinds of people might be looking for a lead-lined coffin of paper, and how each kind would have a different idea of what to do with it, and how every single one of those ideas would cost somebody everything.

Behind me, the chest sat lashed under the awning, patient as a debt.

In the morning, Tam would ask questions. In the morning, I'd have to decide how much truth a seventeen-year-old can carry without it breaking his stride, and whether I could carry the rest alone.

And downriver, though I couldn't have known it then, a well-dressed man was already asking after a boat answering to the *Kestrel*'s description—which is the trouble with rivers. They carry everything to everyone eventually, and they never, ever lose the address.
