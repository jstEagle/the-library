## 4. The Man Who Buys Silence

I've been offered bribes exactly twice in my life, and both times the man doing the offering wore better boots than me. That's not a rule of the universe, but it's close enough that I've started keeping an eye on footwear.

The first time was a customs officer's clerk in Harrow Bend who wanted me to look the other way at a cargo of untaxed tobacco. He offered forty dollars. I took it, because forty dollars was forty dollars and the tobacco belonged to a widow who'd earned it, and I've never lost much sleep over the arithmetic of small sins.

The second time was three days after I pulled the chest out of the shallows below Mount Kessler, and the man doing the offering sat down across from me in the Blue Heron like he'd been assigned the chair at birth.

He was maybe fifty, dressed in a gray coat that cost more than my boat's engine, with a face like a ledger page — everything recorded, nothing revealed. He ordered the good whiskey without looking at the menu, which told me he'd been in the room before, or rooms exactly like it. Then he smiled, and the smile was the kind you'd put on a fence: decorative, and meant to tell you where the property line ran.

"Captain Vasquez," he said. "I'm told you found something interesting in the low water."

I'd been drinking alone, which is how I prefer to drink, and I'd been careful for three days. The chest sat under a tarp in my forward hold, weighted with chain, and I'd told Tam it was salvage we'd sell in Kessler City to a scrap dealer. Tam had looked at me with those bright, hungry eyes of his and said "Sure, Captain," in the tone of a boy who believes exactly as much of what you tell him as suits him.

"I find a lot of things in low water," I said. "Bottles. Boots. Once, a piano. Don't ask me how the piano got there, because I never did work it out."

"A lead-lined chest," Corliss said — and that was the first time I heard the name, though he offered it the way a card player shows you one card so you won't count the deck. "Corliss. I represent parties with an interest in historical recovery."

"Historical recovery," I said. "That's a fine phrase. Sounds like something a man says instead of what he means."

"What I mean," he said, "is that the chest you pulled from the gravel bar below the mountain is the property of certain interested parties. Its contents are, frankly, worthless — old paper, waterlogged, of sentimental value to collectors and no one else. But the parties are sentimental men. They'd like it back. And they'd like it back unopened."

"Unopened," I said.

"Unopened," he agreed, and set a bank draft on the table between us, face down, like a man laying a card he already knew was going to win.

I didn't touch it. I looked at my whiskey instead and did some fast figuring. Triple a season's wages — I could read the number through the back of the paper, because money has a way of showing through anything, even good paper, even good intentions. It was more than the *Marianne* had earned in her best year, and she'd had some good years back when the river was younger and I was too.

Here's the thing about a number like that. A number like that isn't payment. It's a question. It's asking: *what do you think is in that chest, and how much is your curiosity worth?* And every man has a price on his curiosity. Most of us sell it cheap and call the transaction something else — a wife, a mortgage, a drink, a night's sleep. The trick of a man like Corliss is that he doesn't buy the chest. He buys the not-opening of it, and the not-opening is worth more to him than the opening would be to me, which meant the contents were worth more than the draft on the table, which meant the draft was a lie of arithmetic, and I have never trusted a lie that came with a number attached.

"Waterlogged old paper," I said. "Sentimental value to collectors."

"That's correct."

"Then why the chain on the draft, Mr. Corliss? Why not just offer me scrap price and a receipt?"

The smile adjusted itself by a degree. Somewhere behind it, gears turned, and I got the sense of a very patient man revising a very short list.

"Because sentimental men," he said, "pay sentimental prices. You're a practical man, Captain. Your reputation says so. You move up and down this river, you keep your head down, you don't ask questions in taverns, you don't make friends you can't leave. I admire that. It's a rare discipline." He turned the draft over so the number faced me, and it was worse than I'd guessed. "All I'm asking is that you keep being who you already are. Hand me the chest. Take the money. Run your boat. Nothing about your life has to change at all."

And that — that was the sentence that did it. Not the money, not the lie about waterlogged paper, not even the careful way he said *your reputation says so*, which meant he'd bought my reputation the way he meant to buy everything else. It was *nothing about your life has to change at all.* Because I looked at him, sitting there in his gray coat with his fence-post smile, and I understood that he was right. That was exactly what I'd been doing for twenty years. Keeping my head down. Not asking questions. Not opening chests, literal or otherwise. And a man had died — a man with my own jaw and my own temper — without anyone ever asking him a single question, and I had spent two decades telling myself that was the same thing as peace.

"Mr. Corliss," I said, "I'm going to tell you the truth, which I understand is not customary in this line of work."

"I'd appreciate it."

"I don't know what's in that chest. And the fact that I don't know is the only thing in my life right now that's worth more than your draft."

He studied me for a long moment. I'll give him this: he didn't try again. A man who tries twice doesn't believe his own first offer, and Corliss believed every word he said, which made him more dangerous, not less.

"The parties I represent," he said, standing, "are patient men, Captain. But they are not *infinitely* patient. I'd think about which kind of patience you'd rather be the object of — theirs, or the river's. The river at least drowns you quickly."

He put a card on the table — heavy stock, no name, just an address in Kessler City and a number — and he left the draft where it lay, which was its own kind of message. *Keep it or don't. Either way, we know where you drink.*

I left the draft on the table too. I'd like to tell you that was principle. Mostly it was because I didn't want my fingerprints on it, and partly because I wanted him to know I'd seen through the arithmetic. But I took the card, because a man would have to be a fool to throw away the address of the wolf, and I have been many things but never that particular fool.

I walked back to the *Marianne* in the dark with my collar up and my hand nowhere near the pistol I wasn't carrying, and I told myself the feeling in my stomach was the whiskey. It wasn't the whiskey. It was the sound a trap makes when it closes — which is no sound at all, just the sudden knowledge that the walls were always there and you only now noticed them.

* * *

Corliss did not go to his hotel.

He went to the telegraph office at the edge of the rail yard, where the clerk knew better than to look up from his newspaper, and he wrote out his message in a hand so small and even it might have been printed:

*VESSEL MARIANNE. CAPTAIN V. DECLINED. ITEM INTACT, UNOPENED, IN FORWARD HOLD. HE IS NOT A GREEDY MAN. THIS IS INCONVENIENT.*

He paid for the wire, tipped the clerk a coin he couldn't have named the denomination of, and stood a moment in the yard among the sleeping boxcars, considering.

The reply, when it came the next morning, was four words long, and the clerk who handed it to him noticed — because clerks notice everything, it's the whole of their profession — that Mr. Corliss read it twice and that his face did not change at all, which in a man is more frightening than any change could have been.

The wire read:

*THEN HE IS NOT SAFE.*

Corliss folded it into his breast pocket, next to his heart, which was the nearest thing to sentiment he permitted himself, and went to hire some men whose discretion could be purchased by means other than bank drafts. Drafts left records. Records were untidy. There were other currencies — fear chief among them — and fear, unlike paper, could not be traced, could not be refused, and could not be left sitting on a tavern table by a stubborn riverboat captain with his father's jaw and his father's talent for being exactly where he shouldn't be.

* * *

I slept badly, which was nothing new, but I slept badly in a new key. Around two in the morning I gave up, lit the lamp in the *Marianne*'s cabin, and went and sat with the chest.

I didn't open it. I want that on the record, whatever record there is. I sat on the deck plate with my back against the tarp and my boots in the bilge water and I listened to the river go by, and I thought about the draft on the tavern table, and I thought about a man I'd met once in Harrow Bend who said that every debt gets collected eventually, the only variable being who does the collecting.

The trouble with refusing money, I decided somewhere around three, is that it's still a transaction. Corliss had offered to buy my silence and I'd refused, which meant my silence was now on the market with no price marked, and there is nothing in this world more dangerous than goods without a price, because then the buyer sets the terms. I'd told him I didn't know what was in the chest. I'd told him the not-knowing was worth more than the draft. Which meant I had just informed a patient man, in writing as it were, that I intended to open it.

Well. I'd always been quick in the shallows and slow on the land.

Tam came up on deck at dawn, the way he did, all elbows and questions, and stopped dead when he saw me sitting against the tarp with the lamp burned down to a stub.

"You been up all night, Captain?"

"Thinking."

"About the salvage?" And there it was — that bright, hungry look, the one I'd been pretending not to see for three days. "Because I been thinking about it too. That seal on the chest, the one with the tower and the two stars? I asked around some. Down at Ferro's, and the chandlery, and—"

"Tam." I said it quietly, which is how I say the things I mean most. "Who did you ask?"

He blinked. "Just — folks. River folk. Why?"

I looked past him at the gray morning coming up the water, at the mist sitting on the shallows below Mount Kessler like something waiting its turn, and I thought: *too late. It's already too late.* Somewhere in a rail yard, a wire had gone out with my name in it, and my deckhand had been walking the waterfront asking questions about a country that didn't exist, and every one of those questions had been a flare lit over open water.

"How many people," I said, "have you told about that seal?"
