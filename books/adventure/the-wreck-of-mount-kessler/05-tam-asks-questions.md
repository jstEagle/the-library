## 5. Tam Asks Questions

I want it on the record, before anything else, that I am not a fool. Reckless on occasion, curious to a fault, and possessed of a mouth that runs about half a length ahead of my sense—yes. But not a fool. A fool wouldn't have noticed the seal at all.

I'd noticed it three days before I started asking. That's the part nobody believes later, when they tell the story of what happened to the *Magpie's Rest* and the two men who crewed her. Three days I sat on it. Three days I watched Captain Vasquez scrub the deck like a man trying to scrub a hole through the planking and down into the river itself, and three days I said nothing, because I'd learned that much from him: the river rewards patience and punishes appetite.

But three days is about my ceiling. I'm nineteen. My ceiling is low.

* * *

It started at the Saltcellar, which is the kind of tavern that smells like boiled mutton and old rope, where the beer is honest and the company is mostly honest too, and where a boy with a deckhand's wages can buy a round for the old pilots if he flatters them right. Old pilots are my favorite people in the world after my grandmother, who is dead. They know everything and they'll tell you all of it for the price of listening, which is the only currency I've never run short of.

There was a pilot named Doss that night, half in his cups, telling the table about the year the customs men burned a smuggler's wharf at Gray's Landing, and I saw my opening the way you see a gap between sandbars—there and then, and if you hesitate it's gone.

"Doss," I said, "you ever run cargo out of Vhaloria?"

The table went quiet in a way I felt in my teeth. Not the quiet of a man deciding whether to answer. The quiet of six men all deciding at once whether to pretend they hadn't heard.

Doss took a long pull of his beer, which gave the room time to remember how to breathe. "Vhaloria's a story mothers tell," he said finally. "Keep your boots dry, or the Vhalorians'll get you."

"That's not what I asked."

"Boy." He set the mug down and looked at me with the flat gray patience of a man who has watched the river eat better men than me. "There is no what. There's a stretch of coast where maps used to say something, and now the maps say nothing, and the nothing has teeth. You don't ask questions about the nothing. You especially don't ask them loud, in a room, with your face where everyone can see it."

I laughed. I want to be clear about that. I laughed, because I was nineteen and I'd just helped drag a sealed chest out of the shallows with a dead nation's crest stamped on the lead, and I thought the danger was a grand joke that hadn't caught up to me yet.

"I only ask," I said, "because we pulled something out of the water below Kessler with the—"

"Boy." Doss's hand came down on my wrist, and his grip was still pilot's-strong even after all the beer. "Stop talking."

I stopped talking. But it was too late, and I knew it was too late the way you know a sandbar has shifted under you—by the feel of the room, not by anything you can point to. At the far end of the bar, a man in a gray coat had turned on his stool just enough to watch us in the back-bar mirror, and he wasn't drinking anymore. He'd been drinking when I came in. I remembered that much. He'd been drinking, and now he wasn't, and his mug sat there full and sweating like it was embarrassed for him.

I finished my beer slow and left early, and I did not look at the mirror, and I thought about the gray coat the whole walk back to the wharf.

* * *

Here's the thing about me and questions: I come by it honest. My grandmother raised me on stories, and every one of them had a hole in the middle where the interesting part should've been. *That's not for children,* she'd say, and I'd lie awake filling in the hole myself, and I got so good at filling holes that by the time I was twelve I could spot one in a grown man's conversation at forty paces.

The captain is a hole with a boat around it. That's what I'd have said, if you'd asked me that week, before I understood what it costs to say things like that out loud. Six months I'd crewed for Gideon Vasquez and I could count on one hand the facts I had about him: he read water like scripture, he paid fair, he never talked about the war, and he never, ever talked about his father, though once, drunk on two fingers of plum brandy—which is a very small amount for a man his size—he'd said the words *he chose them over us* and then gone to his bunk and not come out till morning.

I didn't know who *them* was. But I knew the crest on that chest was a *them*. I'd seen the shape of it in the firelight, that night on the gravel bar—a crowned river, coiled like a serpent, biting its own tail. I'd seen that shape before, scratched on a lockbox my grandmother kept under her bed and never opened, back when I was small, back in the river town where I was born, which is a thing I had never thought about until that chest came up out of the water with my whole childhood stamped on its lid.

So you see. Not a fool. Just a boy with a hole in his own story and a brand-new hole floating in the shallows, and the two holes shaped the same.

* * *

Two nights later I went back out, because of course I did. I told myself I'd be careful. I told myself I'd only listen, not ask. I told myself a lot of things, and the beer told me other things, and the beer and I have a complicated relationship.

The Ferryman's Rest is a rougher house than the Saltcellar—ferry crews and wreckers and the women who take their wages—and I figured rough was safe, because rough men don't care about politics, they care about pay. I sat with a wrecker named Cully who had hands like tree roots and a scar that started at his eyebrow and went somewhere under his collar, and I bought him beer, and I let him talk about salvage for an hour, and then, casual as a man asking the time, I said:

"You ever hear of a crest like this?" And I drew it on the table with my finger, in spilled beer. The crowned river. The serpent biting its tail.

Cully looked at the drawing for a long time. Then he looked at me. Then he wiped it out with his sleeve, slow, like he was wiping something off a window so the neighbors couldn't see in.

"Where'd you see that?"

"River salvage."

"Below Kessler?"

I should have lied. I want that written down too, in whatever ledger they keep on boys like me: *he should have lied, and he knew better, and he didn't.* "Maybe," I said, which is the same as yes to anyone who's listening, and someone was listening. I could feel it, that barroom weather-change again, the way the air goes still before the front comes through. I turned my head slow, casual as anything, and there he was. Gray coat. Different tavern, same man. Sitting by the door with a full mug he wasn't drinking, watching me in the glass behind the bottles like I was a show he'd paid for.

I finished my night in a hurry after that.

* * *

He followed me home. I want to be honest about how I figured that out, because it wasn't cleverness. It was my grandmother's voice, dead these six years, saying *check behind you, Tamri, the world is full of men walking your direction.* I cut through the cooper's yard, which is a maze of stacked barrels and bad light, and I took the little footbridge over the millrace, and I stopped at the far end and pretended to tie my boot.

And he crossed the bridge behind me.

Not hurrying. Not hiding. Just walking, steady as a tide, close enough now that I could see he was big through the shoulders and had a face like a closed door, and that he wanted me to see him. That was the part that turned my stomach cold. He wasn't following me the way a thief follows a purse. He was following me the way a man walks behind a horse to let it know he's there.

I walked the rest of the way to the wharf fast, not running, because running tells a man like that he's won, and I went up the *Magpie's Rest*'s gangplank with my heart going like a paddlewheel in flood, and the gray coat stopped at the foot of the plank and just stood there. Looking at the boat. Looking at me. Then he touched two fingers to his hat brim—not a salute, exactly, more like a receipt—and walked off into the dark, unhurried as Sunday.

I stood on the deck shaking for I don't know how long.

* * *

Gideon was in the cabin, awake, because he was always awake now. He slept like a cat since we'd hauled the chest aboard—light, and in pieces. I came through the door and he took one look at my face and set down the chart he wasn't reading.

"Tell me."

So I told him. All of it. The Saltcellar, Doss's hand on my wrist, the Ferryman's Rest, the drawing in spilled beer, the gray coat at both places, the bridge, the hat brim. I told it fast and I told it all, because I've learned that with the captain you get no credit for a partial confession. He listened without moving, which was worse than if he'd shouted.

When I finished he was quiet a while. The lamp hissed. Somewhere out on the water a night heron made its sound like a saw going through wet wood.

"Describe him again."

"Big. Gray coat, good wool, not river wool. Full mug, never drank. Face like—" I hunted for it. "Like he'd already decided about you. Whatever you did, it wouldn't change anything. You were just a fact he was collecting."

Gideon nodded, slow, and there was something in his face I'd never seen there before, and it took me a moment to name it, because it didn't belong on a man like him. It was fear. Not for himself. I understood that a second later, when he spoke.

"He touched his hat brim."

"Yes."

"That's Corliss's people. That's how they mark you." He said it flat, the way he read soundings off the water. "It means you've been priced."

"Priced."

"Everything's priced, Tam. That's not cynicism, that's arithmetic." He got up and went to the stern windows and stood looking out at the dark water, and I watched his back and understood, with the slow cold horror of a boy watching his own mistake come up over the horizon like weather, that I had done something that could not be undone.

"I'm sorry," I said. "I only asked about—"

"I know what you only asked about." He didn't turn around. "You asked in two taverns in three days. You drew the crest. In beer, on a table, in a room full of strangers." Now he did turn, and his voice wasn't angry, and that was the worst part, because I could have survived angry. "Do you know what I was going to do with that chest, Tam? I was going to wait for the customs launch to make its circuit, and on a dark night I was going to row it out to the deep channel and let it go. Lead-lined. It would've been in the mud before it finished sinking, and in a hundred years it would've been a story, and in two hundred nothing at all. That was the plan. That was the whole plan. Quiet, and gone."

"Then why didn't you?"

He looked at me for a long moment. "Because I kept not doing it," he said, and there was something raw under the words, some old wound the chest had torn the bandage off of. "Because it has my father's name in it, and I'm a weak man in exactly one direction." He rubbed his face with both hands, and when they came down he was the captain again, all business, and somehow that was scarier than the fear had been. "Now listen. Tomorrow we're going to have a visitor, or a note, or a fire. Men like the gray coat don't follow you home to chat. They follow you home so you know they can. The message is the following. The next message is worse."

"What do we do?"

"Tonight, we do nothing, because anything we do tonight tells them we're frightened, and frightened men make mistakes, and they're waiting for us to make one." He sat back down at the chart table. "Go get some sleep."

"Captain—"

"Tam." And here his voice changed, just slightly, and I heard under it the thing he almost never let me hear, which was the man himself, tired and forty-some and alone in a way I was only beginning to understand. "You asked about Vhaloria because of the chest, and you asked about the chest because of me, and that means whatever comes through that door tomorrow comes because of me. So let me carry this part. You've carried enough."

I went to my bunk. I lay there listening to the river slap the hull and the heron sawing away out in the reeds, and I did not sleep, because my grandmother had another saying, and it came back to me now with the full authority of the dead: *the first cost of caring about something, Tamri, is that it can be taken from you. And the second cost is that you'll pay the first one again and again, and call it worth it.*

I cared about the boat. I cared about the captain. And somewhere out there in the dark, a man with a face like a closed door had priced us both.

Toward morning I heard the captain's boots on the deck above, going to the stern rail, and I heard him stand there a long time, and I heard him say one word to the water, so quiet I nearly missed it, and the word was *father.*

And then, just as the sky went gray, a lantern came down the wharf. Unhurried. Steady as a tide.

It stopped at the foot of our gangplank.
