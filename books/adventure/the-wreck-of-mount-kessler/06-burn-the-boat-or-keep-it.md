## 6. Burn the Boat or Keep It

I've scuttled two boats in my life, and I remember both of them the way a man remembers the dogs he's had put down.

The first was the *Merry Alice*, back when I was twenty-two and too green to know that a river will forgive almost anything except carelessness. She took on water at the Kestrel Narrows and I ran her aground on purpose rather than watch her go down with all my gear aboard. I stood on the bank and watched the current take her apart over three days, plank by plank, like it was eating her. The second was a flat-bottomed skiff I sank outside Corvane rather than let a customs man find the brandy in her hold. That one didn't hurt. Boats you sink for cargo are just boats.

The *Vespera* would be neither of those things.

I stood on her deck at first light, three days after prying open the chest, and did the arithmetic every captain does when the water starts feeling crowded. Scuttle her here, in the deep channel off Miller's Bend, where the mud would swallow her whole and the customs divers wouldn't find so much as a nail head by spring. Take the chest upriver overland—no, not the chest, just what mattered in it—and vanish into the hill country where a man with no boat is just a man, and nobody follows a man who's already nothing.

It was a good plan. It was the kind of plan I'd have admired in somebody else and called cowardice in myself, which is how you know a plan is honest.

The trouble was Tam, asleep in the pilothouse with his boots on and my spare coat bunched under his head, snoring like a man who'd never once considered that the world might end before breakfast. And the other trouble was the ledger, sitting in my cabin under a false floorboard I'd laid down myself years ago for exactly this kind of cargo, and the third trouble—the one I kept trying not to look at directly, the way you don't look straight at the sun or at your own funeral—was the name inked inside the lid of that chest.

*Elias Vasquez.*

My father had been dead to me for twenty years before he was dead to anybody else. A man can carry that kind of thing a long way if the road stays smooth. The river had been smooth for a long time.

* * *

I spent the morning doing things that needed doing whether I sank her or sailed her: greasing the winch, re-caulking a seam aft that had been weeping since June, arguing with the fuel man at Miller's Landing about the price of diesel as though the price of anything still mattered to me. It's strange, the rituals that hold a man together. You keep paying for fuel because paying for fuel is who you are, right up until the moment you decide you're someone else, and then you don't know who's steering.

Tam found me at noon on the foredeck, coiling lines that were already coiled.

"Cap'n," he said, which meant he'd been working up to something. "You been standing there an hour."

"I'm thinking."

"You think loud." He sat down on the hatch cover, uninvited, the way he did everything. "We going upriver?"

"Why would we go upriver?"

"Because you've got that face. The one from the narrows, when you're picking a line." He squinted at me. "Except there's no narrows here. So either the line you're picking isn't in the water, or you're crazy."

The boy was nineteen and had been reading me for two seasons now, and I'd made the mistake of teaching him how, because a deckhand who can read water is worth double wages and a deckhand who can read *you* is worth trouble.

"We're staying put through tomorrow," I said. "Then we'll see."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I've got that doesn't lie to you."

He chewed on that, and I could see him deciding whether to push, and then—mercifully—deciding not to, because he went below instead, and I heard him rattling around in the galley making the kind of noise a boy makes when he wants you to know he's disappointed in you but hasn't figured out why yet.

I looked upriver. The Vhal ran north out of sight toward the hill country, and past the hills, past the timber and the scree, Mount Kessler stood with its shoulder already whitening—the first dusting comes early up there, always has, and the pass over its saddle closes sometime between late autumn and whenever the mountain feels like it, and stays closed until thaw. Six weeks, maybe eight, if the weather was generous. The mountain is not famous for being generous.

And beyond that pass, somewhere, were people whose names filled forty pages of a dead man's ledger. People who'd paid a man named Elias Vasquez to do something dangerous with paper, twenty years ago, and whose country had been erased so thoroughly that even the word *Vhaloria* sounded made-up now, like something out of a child's story about kingdoms under the sea.

I'd told myself for three days that I was holding the chest because dumping it would raise questions. That I was keeping the ledger because burning it felt like letting them win twice. All true, in the way that most of the stories men tell themselves are true—technically, uselessly, missing the point entirely.

The real truth was simpler and stupider: my father's handwriting was inside that lid, and I hadn't seen his handwriting since I was eleven years old, and some part of me that I thought had drowned years ago had come up gasping.

* * *

That evening I opened the floorboard and read the ledger again, properly this time, the way you'd read a chart before running a stretch of water you'd only ever heard about.

Names. Pages of them. Some marked with a small cross—dead, or presumed, or erased along with everything else. Others clean, untouched, waiting. A schoolteacher in a town I couldn't find on any map anymore. A ferryman. A woman named Maren Ostrova, whose entry wasn't just a name but a title: *custodian*. As if the names themselves were cargo, and she was the one licensed to carry them.

And tucked against the treaty fold, a letter in that same hand I knew better than my own. I'd read it four times now and it hadn't gotten easier. My father hadn't run during the collapse. He hadn't sold Vhaloria out, the way the river towns told it, the way *I'd* half believed it in the ugly years when believing the worst about him was easier than grieving. He'd tried to move the proof of what had been done to them—out of the country, over the mountains, to whoever was left to hold it. And he'd failed, or been caught, or drowned; the letter ended mid-sentence, the way letters do when the man writing them runs out of time instead of words.

Twenty years I'd built a man out of absence and called it my father. Now I had forty pages of evidence that the real one had been braver than the ghost, and I didn't know what to do with that except sit with it until it stopped hurting, knowing it wouldn't.

Tam knocked. I covered the floorboard with my boot.

"Supper," he said. Then, quieter: "There's a launch come up from Corvane. Black hull, no markings. Been sitting across the bend since dark."

I felt it in my stomach before my head caught up—that cold drop, the pilot's instinct, the same feeling you get when the water ahead goes glass-smooth over a bar that should be boiling.

"How long?"

"Since full dark. Running no lights." He paused. "Cap'n, nobody runs no lights on this river unless they're hiding or hunting."

"Or both," I said, and blew out the lamp.

* * *

We sat in the dark galley and ate cold beans and listened to the river, which is a thing you learn to do the way farmers listen to wind. The black launch stayed where it was. Not approaching. Watching. Whoever was aboard had learned patience from somebody professional, and professional patience on this river meant Corliss, and Corliss meant the men who'd paid him, and those men wanted my father's chest badly enough to spend money on hulls without lights.

"They know we're here," Tam whispered.

"They've known since Miller's Landing. Maybe since before." I turned my cup in my hands. "Question is what they're waiting for."

"Reinforcements?"

"Or for us to make a choice they can predict." I looked at him across the dark, this boy I'd fished out of a dockside riot two seasons ago because he reminded me of nobody and everybody, and I said the thing I'd been circling for three days. "If I scuttle her tonight and we walk, they'll expect it. A man with contraband sinks his boat and runs for the hills—it's the oldest play on the river. They'll have eyes on every trailhead south of the pass."

"So we go north."

"North is the mountain. North is six weeks of river with a launch faster than us and a customs service that owes Corliss favors." I rubbed my eyes. "North is a pass that closes with the snows, and if we're caught on the wrong side of that closing, there's no coming back till spring. For anybody."

Tam was quiet a while. Then: "The people in the book. The ones with the clean marks. They're past the pass, aren't they."

It wasn't a question, and I didn't insult him by treating it like one.

"The ferryman might be on this side," I said. "The schoolteacher, maybe. But the custodian—" I heard myself say the word and hated how much it sounded like hope. "She'll be where they went. Over the saddle. There's a valley up there the maps stopped mapping, same time they stopped mapping everything else."

"And you want to take it to her."

"I want to be rid of it," I said, fast and flat, the way you say a lie you've rehearsed. "There's a difference."

"Sure there is," Tam said, and the little rat smiled in the dark.

* * *

I didn't sleep. Around two bells I went out on deck and stood at the rail and had the conversation I'd been avoiding, the one captains have with their boats, which is really a conversation with themselves conducted at a volume low enough to preserve dignity.

Here's what the *Vespera* was: thirty-one feet of patched oak and stubbornness, bought used with my first command wages, repaired with my own hands through two groundings and one fire and more winters than she deserved. She was the only thing I owned outright, the only place I'd ever lived longer than a season, and the only witness to the last fifteen years of my life who'd never once lied to me. Every mile of river I knew, I knew from her wheel. Sinking her would be like sinking the version of myself that had survived this long—the one who stopped nowhere, owed nobody, answered to nothing heavier than the current.

And here's what the ledger was: forty pages of names that somebody had died moving once already, and a treaty that could start a war or resurrect a nation depending on whose hands held it, and a letter that proved my father had chosen the losing side on purpose because it was the right one.

One of these things could be replaced. I'd been telling myself for fifteen years it was the boat.

The black launch sat across the bend, patient as a heron. They expected me to run. They expected a man who stops nowhere to keep stopping nowhere, all the way to the grave. And the moment I understood that—really understood it, down where decisions actually get made—I realized the trap wasn't the launch. The trap was being predictable, and I'd been predictable for fifteen years, and predictability is just fear wearing a work shirt.

So fine. Let them watch the boat.

I woke Tam at gray dawn. He came up on deck with his hair flat on one side and his eyes already sharp, because the boy sleeps like a cat and wakes like one too.

"Get the mule feed and the mountain kit out of the hold," I said. "Rope, the good oilskins, cold-weather blankets. Leave the rest."

He stared at me. "The rest meaning—"

"Meaning the boat stays here, tied up pretty as Sunday, with enough gear aboard to look like we're just laying over. Let them stare at her all week." I looked upriver, at the hills, at the white shoulder showing above them like a warning. "We go north. Overland. Today."

"To the pass." He said it carefully, testing the weight of it. "Cap'n, that's weeks on foot. That's the Kessler trail. Men die on that trail in *summer*."

"Men die everywhere. At least up there it's honest about it." I swung my pack onto the deck and started filling it with the economy of a man who'd packed light his whole life and never once liked it. "There are people past that pass waiting on what's in that chest. Twenty years they've waited. I figure the least I can do is be twenty years late instead of never."

Tam grinned, and I scowled at him, because if I didn't scowl he'd think I'd gone soft, and a captain who's gone soft gets his crew killed.

"For the record," I said, "this is about being rid of it. Clean hands. Nothing else."

"Sure, Cap'n."

"And stop smiling."

"Yes, Cap'n." He didn't stop smiling.

We worked through the morning, stripping the *Vespera* down to what a layover would explain and loading what a mountain crossing would demand, and every hour I expected the black launch to make its move, and every hour it didn't—which worried me more than movement would have. By noon we had two packs, a coil of rope, and a canvas-wrapped chest small enough to break a man's back and heavy enough to break his heart.

At the gangway I stopped. Turned around. Looked at her sitting there in the bright water, paint peeling at the waterline, my handprint worn into the rail where fifteen years of steering had polished the wood smooth.

"I'll be back for you," I told her, and then laughed at myself, because boats don't wait and rivers don't keep promises and I'd learned both lessons twice already.

But I said it anyway. And then I stepped off onto the dirt, which is the hardest water a riverman ever crosses, and Tam fell in beside me, and we started walking north toward a mountain that closes its doors for the winter and doesn't leave word where it's gone.

Behind us, the black launch finally moved—but not toward the boat.

Toward the trailhead.
