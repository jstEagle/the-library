## 7. An Old Friend's Badge

The last river town before the mountains was called Harrow's Bend, which was a lie of a name, because the river didn't bend there so much as it gave up—spread itself thin and shallow across a mile of gravel and silt, as if it had heard about the mountains ahead and decided it wanted no part of them. I knew how it felt.

I tied up at the Bend because I had to. The *Maren's Luck* — no, that wasn't her name; her name was the *Sable Tern*, and I'd have to stop thinking of other women when I looked at her — needed wood, water, and a new pin in the starboard rudder fitting that had been complaining since Vicks Landing like an old man asked to climb stairs. And I needed to decide what in God's name I was going to do with a lead-lined chest full of dead country that sat under my bunk like a sleeping dog with bad dreams.

Three days out from pulling it off the gravel bar below Kessler, and I still hadn't told Tam what was in it. He'd seen the crest on the lid — hard not to see it, that crowned tower stamped into lead the size of a dinner plate — and he'd gone quiet in the particular way boys go quiet when they've decided a question is dangerous and are saving it up anyway. I'd caught him twice looking at me sidelong over the tiller, working up his nerve, and both times I'd given him something to do: coil a line, scrub the scum line, count the cordwood. A busy boy doesn't ask questions. Mostly.

Harrow's Bend smelled like every last town on every river I'd ever run: pitch smoke, tar, fried onions, horse, and the sweet rot of fruit nobody would buy before it spoiled. The kind of place where the saloon had two names painted on it because the first owner got shot and the second one couldn't afford new glass. I liked these towns the way you like a knife you've carried a long time — familiar, useful, and liable to cut you if you forget what it is.

I sent Tam for the rudder pin at the smithy and went to find the fuel office, and that's where I saw him, and my stomach did the thing it does when the river drops out from under your hull without warning.

He was standing at the freight counter with his back half-turned, arguing pleasantly with the clerk about a drayage rate, and even after twenty years I'd have known that silhouette anywhere. Hale Brannic stood the way he'd stood in the war: planted, patient, taking up exactly as much room as he needed and not an inch more. Big through the shoulders, going soft now at the middle the way honest men go soft, hair gone iron at the temples but still wearing that same regulation-short cut he'd kept through two campaigns and one court-martial hearing. When he turned and our eyes met across the freight office, he didn't startle, didn't grin, didn't do any of the things a surprised man does.

He just nodded, slow, like we'd had an appointment.

"Gideon Vasquez," he said. "They told me you were dead."

"Who told you that?"

"Everybody." He crossed the floor and took my hand, and his grip was the same grip, warm and heavy and entirely without apology. "Glad they were wrong. You look terrible."

"You look prosperous."

"I look *employed*." He said it easily, but I watched his eyes while he said it, and his eyes weren't easy at all. "Drink?"

Now here is the thing I want set down plainly, because a man ought to be honest somewhere, even if only in his own head: I knew. Before he said another word, before the whiskey, before any of it — some animal part of me that reads water and sandbars and weather by instinct looked at Hale Brannic standing in a freight office three days' run below Mount Kessler and said, *he's here for the chest.* Rivers don't put men in places by accident. Men put men in places.

And I said yes to the drink anyway. Because he was Hale Brannic, and there are debts that don't show in any ledger, and twenty years ago at a place called Cerna Ford he'd dragged me out of a flooded culvert by the collar of my coat and told me, spitting water, "Don't make me regret the effort." I owed him a drink or six against that collar. Even if the drink was bait.

* * *

Brannic's side of the table, and I'll tell it straight because somebody has to:

I'd been on the chest for eleven days when I walked into Harrow's Bend, and I'd been on it angry for ten of those. The commission came through the Recovery Office in the capital — licensed recovery agent, third class, which is a polite way of saying bounty man with paperwork — and the brief was thin in the way briefs get thin when the people writing them don't want to know too much themselves. Recover property. Lead-lined strongbox, Vhalorian manufacture, lost in transit during the evacuation period. Contents: documents. Do not read contents. Deliver intact to the client's representative.

No name on the client. Just a seal I wasn't cleared to identify and a payment schedule that made my pension look like a beggar's cup.

Twenty years in the army teaches you to smell a job like this. It's the ones where they tell you *don't ask* that end with somebody hanging, and usually not the officers. But I'd taken the commission, because a man gets tired of choosing between his pride and his rent, and I told myself the same thing I always told myself: papers are papers. Somebody's old treaty, somebody's old land claims, somebody's old war fought and settled and buried. My job was the box, not the history.

Then I'd traced it downriver — a customs launch report, a dockhand at Vicks who remembered a deckhand asking fool questions about Vhaloria in a tavern, which told me the captain hadn't kept his mouth shut either — and I'd walked into the freight office at Harrow's Bend to arrange mule transport for a recovery I assumed would take me another week upriver, and Gideon Vasquez had been standing at the counter like the river had delivered him to me gift-wrapped.

I want to say I was glad to see him. That part was true, God help me. Twenty years, and the sight of him hit me somewhere under the ribs that had nothing to do with commissions. We'd slept in the same mud for two winters. I'd watched him pull four men out of the water at Cerna Ford and then steal a boat to do it, because regulations said nobody goes back. He was the best man I ever served beside, and when he deserted — when he just *left*, after, no goodbye, no forwarding anything — it left a hole in me shaped like a question I never got answered.

So when I saw him, two things were true at once, and I drank with him knowing both: I was happy, and I was hunting him.

A man can carry both. That's the ugly lesson of my whole life. A man can carry almost anything.

* * *

We took a corner table at the Two Names Saloon, away from the window, away from the door — he chose it, I noticed, and I let him think I hadn't. The whiskey was local, which meant it was terrible, which meant it was honest. Everything else at that table was going to be lies.

"To Cerna Ford," Brannic said, raising his glass. "To the worst swim of my life."

"Your swim was fine. It was my drowning that was the problem."

"You never thanked me, you know. For the collar."

"I'm thanking you now." I touched my glass to his. "Twenty years late. Interest accrued."

He laughed, and it was the same laugh, big and unguarded, and for about ninety seconds I let myself believe we were just two old soldiers in a river town, and I hated myself for how good it felt.

"So," he said, settling back. "Still running the river. Still alone on it, near as anyone can tell. What happened to you, Gideon? After. One day you're the most decorated boat officer in the flotilla, and the next day you're a rumor."

"I got tired of being decorated."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I give free." I turned my glass on the scarred wood. "What about you? Sergeant-major Brannic, licensing himself out as a recovery agent. Chasing boxes for money. Your mother would weep."

"My mother wept when I enlisted. She got it all done early." He smiled, and then the smile did something complicated at the edges. "It's steady work. There's a lot of lost property in this world, Gideon. Wars make it. People pay good money to get their things back."

"What kind of things?"

"Boxes. Ledgers. Papers." He said it lightly, watching me over the rim of his glass, and I watched him right back, and for a moment we were both perfectly transparent to each other and both pretending otherwise, the way we used to play cards. "Funny thing. The item I'm currently engaged on — it came off the river. Below Kessler. Gravel bar, low water. You'd know the stretch."

"Would I."

"A fisherman saw a boat nosing around there three days back. Older steamboat, patched rail, a captain who reads water like a priest reads scripture." He set the glass down. Gentle. Almost tender. "That's you, Gideon. That's always been you."

Here's where I should tell you what I said. I'd like to report that I lied brilliantly, that I spun him a tale about salvage and driftwood and a chest full of rotted uniforms that I'd already reported to the customs house. But the truth is I did what I always did with Hale Brannic: I played it lazy, because lazy was the only game he couldn't beat.

"Fisherman sounds like he needs glasses," I said. "There's nothing on that bar but gravel and the bones of everybody's bad decisions."

"Gideon." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped into the register he'd used on green lieutenants and stubborn mules, the one that meant *this is the part where I'm your friend*. "I'm going to say a thing once, and then we're going to keep drinking and be glad to see each other, agreed?" I said nothing. He took it for agreement. "There's a warrant out. Not for you — for the item. But warrants travel, and the men paying for this one don't much care whose hands it comes out of, so long as it comes out. If you've got it, walk it to me tonight and walk away clean. There's a finder's fee. It's obscene. You could retire the *Tern* on it and buy her a sister."

"And if I don't have it?"

"Then you don't, and we finish this bottle like gentlemen, and I go on upriver and bother some other poor bastards." He held my eyes. "But you've got it. I've known you thirty seconds past forever, and you've got the look of a man sleeping on top of something."

"The look."

"Same look you had at Cerna Ford, sitting on that stolen boat with four drowned men wrapped in canvas. Like the river handed you something and you haven't decided yet whether to thank it."

I drank. The whiskey burned all the way down and sat in my gut like a coal, and I thought about the ledger under my bunk, and my father's name inked inside the lid in a hand I'd have known blindfolded, and I thought: *Hale, old friend, you have no idea what you're asking me to sell you.*

"Give me a day," I said.

His face didn't move, but something behind it did — a flicker, quick as a fish turning. "A day for what?"

"For me to decide whether I've got it or not."

* * *

From Brannic's chair, watching Gideon walk out of the Two Names into the lamplight:

He lied to me. Of course he lied to me. He lied warmly, the way he always did, with that lazy riverboat drawl that made you feel like the two of you were in on a joke together, and I sat there and let him, because I was lying too, and between two liars who love each other there's a kind of honesty that doesn't need words.

*A day.* He'd asked for a day, and I'd given it to him, and we'd shaken hands on it like it meant something, and maybe it did. Maybe it was the last honest transaction either of us would manage this week.

I went back to my room above the livery and took out the commission papers and read them again by candlelight, and for the hundredth time the thin places in the brief snagged at me like a burr. *Do not read contents.* Who writes that? Every legitimate recovery in the book comes with a manifest, an appraisal clause, an insurance bond. This one came with a payment schedule and a silence. And the client's representative — a soft-spoken man named Corliss whom I'd met once in the capital, gloves and cologne and eyes like a clerk counting someone else's money — Corliss had said one thing to me that had been sitting wrong in my chest for eleven days.

*"Sergeant, the contents are worthless. It's the box that matters. The seal, the provenance. Sentimental value to certain parties."*

Worthless contents. Obscene fee. Don't read. In twenty years of soldiering and five of recovering, I had learned exactly one law that never failed: *nobody pays a fortune for nothing.*

I blew out the candle and lay in the dark listening to the river, and I thought about Gideon's face when he'd said *give me a day.* He hadn't been deciding whether he had the chest. He'd been deciding something else — whether to trust me, maybe, or whether I was worth betraying, or whether the boy I'd seen waiting for him at the dock, young, dark-eyed, watching me with the flat caution of a dog that smells wolf — whether any of us were worth what was coming.

Because I'd seen the soldiers. Two of them, plainclothes, bad at it, drinking tea across the street from the freight office since noon. They weren't mine. My commission ran solo — that was the deal, that was why the fee was fat. Which meant Corliss had hired a second team, and hadn't told me, and a man who hires two hunters and tells each one he's alone isn't planning to pay either of them.

I lay in the dark and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding for eleven days.

If the contents were worthless, why soldiers? If the fee was honest, why a second team? And if Gideon Vasquez — the straightest man I ever served with, the man who stole a boat under fire rather than leave four bodies in the water — if *he* wouldn't hand it over clean, then whatever was in that box wasn't worthless, and whatever Corliss's people intended for it wasn't sentimental.

Somewhere down the street a door banged. I listened to the river going by in the dark, thin and cold and headed for the mountains, carrying everything it carried toward the sea without caring what it was.

One day, I'd promised him. I'd meant it. God help me, I'd meant it.

But I got up before dawn anyway, and saddled my horse, and rode for the trailhead at Kessler's shoulder — because a day was a courtesy between friends, and I had just understood that I was probably the only man in this valley who considered us friends at all.

And behind me, in the grey light, the second team broke camp and followed.
