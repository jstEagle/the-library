## 8. The Ledger's Living Names

I didn't sleep. That was the honest truth of it, though I'd have told Tam I'd slept fine, the way a man tells a lie so small it barely counts against his soul.

The lamp burned low on my cabin table, and the chest sat open between us like a confession neither of us wanted to finish. The ledger lay where I'd left it, its leather cover cracked along the spine, swollen from its years in the river but stubbornly whole. Lead lining keeps water out and secrets in. Whoever built that chest had wanted both jobs done properly.

I turned pages by lamplight until the candle guttered, then lit another and kept turning. It was slow work. The ink had run in places, names bleeding into one another like drowned men holding hands. But most of it held. Columns of them—names, trades, last known places. A census of the dead, or near enough.

*Alaric Venn, wheelwright, Kessany district.*
*Dorotea Lindqvist, midwife, the Vhalmar quarter.*
*Petru Sall, ferryman, Lower Vhal crossing.*

Page after page. Hundreds of names. Some had annotations in a second hand, smaller and sharper: *deceased*, *relocated*, *unaccounted*. And then, here and there, in fresh ink that hadn't been underwater at all—a third hand, careful and current:

*Alive. Contact through the miller at Brenn's Ford.*
*Alive. Last seen south road, autumn past.*
*Alive.*

That word did something to me that no amount of river work had ever done. I set the pen down—I hadn't even noticed I'd picked mine up—and pressed my palms flat against the table until the wood stopped seeming to move.

Because here was the thing I'd been circling all night like a hawk over a drowned calf: the ledger wasn't a record of the dead. It was a roll call of the living. Somebody was maintaining it. Somebody had been maintaining it for years, quietly, in ink that never touched water, while the rest of the world agreed Vhaloria was a story mothers told to frighten children about trusting governments.

A schoolteacher. *Yeva Brandt, schoolteacher, alive.* A ferryman. *Petru Sall, ferryman, alive, working the crossing under a bought name.* Names with trades attached, because whoever kept this book understood that a name without a trade is just a sound, but a name with a trade is a life you can find again.

And then, on the inside back cover, in that same careful current hand, a signature and a title:

*Maren Ostrova — custodian.*

Not author. Not owner. *Custodian.* The way you'd sign yourself keeper of a lighthouse or warden of a graveyard. Somebody who holds a thing in trust because it belongs to people who can't currently hold it themselves.

I sat back and looked at the ceiling beams of my cabin, the ones I'd been meaning to replace for six years, and I thought: somewhere out there is a woman who has spent twenty years writing *alive* next to names in a book nobody was supposed to find. And now her book was sitting in the belly of a dead man's riverboat, in the keeping of a captain who'd spent twenty years making sure he belonged to nothing and no one.

The irony wasn't lost on me. Rivers rarely lose anything; they just hold it until they're ready.

* * *

Tam found me at dawn, which meant he'd slept maybe four hours himself and had come looking for me the way boys do when they sense a grown man is carrying something too heavy. He knocked once and came in before I answered, which should have annoyed me and didn't.

"You look terrible," he said.

"I've been reading."

"Reading." He looked at the ledger, then at me, and I watched him make the decision not to ask. It cost him. His eyes went to the open chest, to the folded treaty still lying where I'd left it, sealed with wax that had survived two decades of riverbed better than most marriages survive two decades of dry land. "Anything good?"

"Names," I said. "Mostly."

"Dead ones?"

"Some." I closed the ledger. "Some not."

He waited. He'd learned that from me, God help him—the patience of a man who knows the river gives up what it gives up on its own schedule. Then he nodded and said, "Coffee's on," and left me alone with the dead man's chest and the living woman's book.

I should have let it go at that. Should have burned the thing right there, or buried it, or sold it to Corliss and bought myself a new boat and a long retirement somewhere the maps are accurate. Any sensible man would have.

Instead I picked up the treaty.

It was heavier than paper should be. Folded in thirds, tied with a ribbon gone stiff as wire, the wax seal unbroken. Official documents travel well; they're built by people who expect them to be carried into courts and wars. I turned it over twice, feeling the weight of it, and that's when I felt the thickness inside that wasn't treaty.

There was a letter tucked between the folds.

Not part of the document. Added after, by someone who'd had access and a reason. I broke the seal—my hands were steady, which surprised me—and unfolded both papers on the table.

The treaty itself I read the way you'd read a snake's belly: carefully, knowing something in there could bite. It was a surrender instrument, or close enough. Terms of occupation, terms of dissolution, signatures of ministers agreeing to hand their nation over piece by piece in exchange for guarantees that were, even to my untrained eye, worth exactly the paper they were written on. Dated three weeks before the collapse. Three weeks before the maps were redrawn and every atlas in the world got a blank space where a country used to be.

But the letter—

The letter was in my father's hand.

I know this the way you know your own reflection in dark water. Twenty years, and I'd have known it upside down in a burning building. The hard downstrokes. The way he crossed his t's like he was striking matches. I'd learned my letters copying that hand, sitting across a galley table from a man who wrote the way he piloted—fast, certain, leaving no room for argument.

*To whoever finds this,* it began, and I had to stop there, because whoever finds this was supposed to be an enemy, and my father had spent his whole life being careful about enemies.

I made myself keep going.

*If you are reading this, then the treaty has failed to do what it was meant to do, which was to buy time. It was never meant to be honored. It was meant to be believed. There is a difference, and the difference is everything.*

*They will tell you I signed it. I did. They will tell you I betrayed my country. I did not. I bought three weeks with my name, and in those three weeks, four hundred and eleven people crossed the mountains who would otherwise have crossed into graves. The ledger records them. Check the dates against the exodus if anyone ever asks you to. The arithmetic will speak for me when I cannot.*

*I am sending this chest downriver because downriver is the one direction they will not watch. Everyone looks up. Nobody looks at what the water carries away.*

*My son pilots these waters. If the river is kind, it may carry this to him, and if it does, tell him—*

And there it stopped. Not faded, not torn. Stopped. The rest of the sentence had been cut away, cleanly, with a blade, sometime in the last twenty years by somebody who knew exactly what those remaining words would be worth and to whom.

I don't remember standing up. I remember the cabin wall against my palm, and I remember the river sounds through the hull, patient as ever, and I remember thinking with perfect clarity: *someone cut this letter, and they cut it recently, because the paper around the cut hasn't yellowed.*

Someone had opened this chest before me. Recently. On the river. And they'd taken the ending of my father's last words and left me the beginning, like a meal served to a starving man with the meat removed.

I stood there a long time. Long enough for the coffee Tam had made to go cold in the pot outside my door. Long enough for the light through the porthole to move from gray to gold.

Here is what twenty years of believing does to a man: it builds a house in him, and he lives in it, and he stops noticing the walls. For twenty years I had lived in a small cold house whose foundation stone read *my father abandoned us, my father signed the treaty, my father sold a country and vanished.* I had furnished that house. I had hung pictures in it. I had raised myself in it, alone, learning to pilot rivers because rivers were the only thing he'd left me that couldn't lie.

And now a letter in his own hand was telling me the house was built on a forgery. That the man I'd hated since I was old enough to hate had spent his final weeks buying lives with his signature, and had died—or worse—for it, and had trusted the river, of all things, to deliver his apology to the son who'd needed it for two decades.

The past doesn't rewrite itself gently. It rewrites itself the way a flood rewrites a bank—all at once, taking everything you thought was solid, leaving you standing on ground that moves.

* * *

I found Tam on deck, coiling lines that didn't need coiling. He took one look at my face and put the rope down.

"What happened?"

I told him. Not all of it—I'm not built for all of it—but the shape of it. The letter. The cut. The living names. He listened the way he listens, which is completely, which is the rarest kind of listening there is.

"So your father—" he started, carefully.

"Wasn't what they said he was." I looked out at the water. "Or wasn't only. Men are rarely what they're said to be, Tam. That's the first lesson of the river and the last lesson of everything else."

"And someone cut the letter."

"Recently. Paper doesn't lie about age any more than water does." I turned to him. "Which means whoever sealed this chest up for me—or for whoever they thought would find it—wanted the names but not the proof. Or wanted the proof but not the names. Either way, they handled this chest after my father died, and they wanted something from it badly enough to take a knife to a dying man's last words."

Tam was quiet a moment. Then, in the way of the young, who ask the questions grown men have trained themselves not to: "Do you think Maren Ostrova knows? About the letter being cut?"

I hadn't thought of that. It landed in me like a dropped anchor.

"If she's the custodian," I said slowly, "if she's kept that book alive for twenty years—then either she sent this chest, or she's been waiting for it, or someone stole it from her. And I don't know which of those three possibilities scares me most."

"Because?"

"Because two of them mean she's in danger. And one of them means she's desperate." I picked up the ledger, weighed it in my hand. Four hundred and eleven names, give or take the bleeding ink. A schoolteacher. A ferryman. A woman who signs herself custodian of a country that exists now only in a book and a boy's questions and a captain's guilty conscience. "And because either way, this book is worth more than my boat, my life, and probably yours, and we're the only two fools on this river who know it."

Tam grinned at me, and it was the grin that would get us both killed someday, the one that meant trouble sounded like a fine idea. "So what do we do?"

What do we do. As if it were settled. As if I hadn't spent twenty years perfecting the art of doing nothing, going nowhere, belonging to no cause that could cost me.

I looked at the ledger. Somewhere beyond the mountains, a woman named Maren Ostrova was missing a book. Somewhere behind me, a man named Corliss was missing a payment. And somewhere in between, a sergeant I used to trust with my life was probably already asking questions in river towns about a boat called the *Kestrel* and the two fools aboard her.

"We do what the river does," I said finally. "We keep moving, and we decide later what we're carrying."

It was a good answer. It was even mostly true. But as I watched Tam coil his lines and hum his tuneless song, I caught myself doing something I hadn't done in twenty years: I caught myself checking the mountains. North, where the pass over Kessler's shoulder threaded up through the timberline and over, into country I'd never had reason to enter.

The snows wouldn't close it for weeks yet.

Weeks was enough.
