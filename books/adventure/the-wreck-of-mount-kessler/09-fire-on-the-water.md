## 9. Fire on the Water

I want it on the record, whoever ends up reading this, that I did not set out to get anybody killed. I asked questions. That's all. Questions are supposed to be free—that's what my mother always said, back when she was saying anything at all—and I believed her right up until the night a man with a face like a closed fist followed me home from the Salt Lark and tried to burn me alive for asking them.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Gideon says I do that. He says a riverman tells a story the way he reads water: start where the trouble starts, not where you wish it started.

Fine. The trouble started with the seal.

* * *

You can't pull a chest out of a river and expect nobody to notice. That's the thing about rivers—they're towns that move. Every dockhand, every fisherman, every kid with a line in the shallows between Kessler Bend and Harrow's Landing saw the captain of the *Marrow's Grace* dragging something heavy off the gravel bar, and every one of them had a theory by sundown. Most of them were wrong. Mine was wrong too, but at least mine was interesting.

The crest on the lid was a mountain over crossed keys, stamped in wax the color of dried blood. I'd seen it before—once, years ago, on the underside of a plate an old woman was selling at the Harrow's Landing flea market. She'd wanted two coppers for it. I'd haggled her down to one because I was fourteen and stupid about money, and then I'd gone home and looked through every atlas in the schoolhouse and found nothing. No Vhaloria. Not in the index, not in the appendix, not in the faded margins where some bored student had inked sea monsters. A whole country, gone like breath off glass.

So when Captain Vasquez told me to stop looking at the chest, I did what any sensible boy does.

I stopped looking at it where he could see me.

* * *

The Salt Lark sits three doors up from the Harrow's Landing wharf, and it has the particular genius of taverns everywhere: nobody remembers your face if you're buying. I bought. Two rounds for the dredge crew, one for the customs pilot who liked to feel important, and patience for the rest. Patience is cheap when you're seventeen and curious. It's the only thing I owned plenty of.

"Vhaloria," I said to the customs pilot, casual as spitting. "That ring a bell?"

He went still in a way I didn't have the sense to read. Men who've been paid to forget things go still like that. "Old country," he said. "Burned out before you were born. Why?"

"Just heard the name."

"From who?"

"A book."

He laughed, and the laugh had a crack in it. "Boy, there's no books on Vhaloria. That's the point of Vhaloria." He drained his glass and left, and I sat there feeling like I'd put my hand into dark water and touched something with teeth.

It was the dredge crew who gave me the good stuff, later, when the ale had loosened them. There'd been men through town, they said. Well-dressed. Asking after a boat out of Kessler Bend, asking after its captain, asking soft the way money asks. One of them had shown a paper around—a warrant, or something wearing a warrant's clothes—and the harbormaster had gotten very helpful very fast.

"They're calling it recovery work," said old Fenwick, who had hands like knotted rope and a memory like a ledger. "Recovery agent. That's the new word for it. Means they take things back for people who lost them." He looked at me over his glass. "Or people who say they lost them. Same thing, half the time."

"What kind of things?"

"The kind you don't ask about, Tam Osei." And he said my name in a way that made my neck prickle, because I hadn't told him my name. Everybody knew it anyway. That's the other thing about river towns: you can't be curious in one without becoming a curiosity yourself.

I should have gone straight back to the boat. I want that written down too. Instead I had one more ale, because I was seventeen, and I walked home along the wharf in the dark, humming, turning it all over—the well-dressed men, the warrant, the captain's face whenever he thought I wasn't watching him watch the chest.

That's when I noticed the footsteps behind me keeping time with mine.

* * *

Here's what I know now that I didn't know then: when a man follows you, he doesn't want to catch you. If he wanted to catch you, he'd have done it already. Following is a message. *We see you. We know where you sleep.* It's cheaper than violence and it works better, most of the time.

Most of the time.

The man behind me peeled off at the fish market, and I told myself I'd imagined the whole thing, and I climbed aboard the *Marrow's Grace* feeling like a fool and a coward in equal measure, which is a bad combination, because it makes you reckless. Gideon was asleep in the cabin—or pretending to be—and the chest sat under the tarp in the hold where it always sat, patient as a debt.

I'll admit I lifted the tarp. Just to look. Just to touch the crest once with my thumb, the way you touch a bruise to check it still hurts.

Wax the color of dried blood. Mountain over crossed keys.

And underneath the wax, so faint I'd missed it every other time, scratched into the lead itself with something sharp: a row of numbers, and beneath them, a single word in a language I didn't know but could sound out, syllable by syllable, like a prayer learned wrong:

*Ost-ro-va.*

"Tam."

Gideon stood in the cabin doorway with the lamp unlit, and in the dark he looked older than the river, older than the mountains, like something the water had been carving for a hundred years. "Get your hand off that."

"Captain—"

"I said get your hand off it." He crossed the deck in four strides and pulled the tarp down himself, and his voice when he spoke again was quiet, which from Gideon Vasquez is worse than shouting. "How many people have you talked to?"

I told him the truth, because lying to that man in the dark felt like lying to the river itself. The Salt Lark. The pilot. Fenwick. The questions. All of it.

For a long moment he said nothing. Then he said, "Pack your bag."

"We're running?"

"We're deciding." But he was already moving, checking the mooring lines, and I saw his eyes go down the wharf to the next boat over—the *Cormorant*, a grain barge, moored empty for the season two hulls down—and I saw him go still again, that same terrible stillness, the way a heron goes still before it strikes or dies.

"Captain? What is it?"

"Smoke," he said. "Do you smell smoke?"

I didn't. Not yet.

* * *

They came at the change of the watch, past midnight, when the wharf lamps burn low and the only men awake are the ones paid to be asleep. I know this because I wasn't asleep either—I was in the fo'c'sle with my bag half-packed and my pride fully wounded, replaying the evening, when I heard the scrape of boots on the *Cormorant*'s deck.

Then the light came.

Not a lamp-light. Lamp-light flickers. This light *roared*, orange and sudden, and the heat of it reached me through the hull before the sound did, and I understood all at once what a burning boat sounds like when you're inside the boat next to it. Like a door slamming in a house fire. Like the river itself drawing breath.

I got out. I don't remember getting out—I remember the fo'c'sle hatch and then I remember the wharf planks under my knees and the *Cormorant* going up like a festival, flame standing straight off her deck in the windless dark, and somewhere in the roar I heard Gideon's voice cutting through it, flat and cold as channel ice:

"Tam. TAM. Roll. Now."

I rolled. Something struck the planks where I'd been kneeling—a shadow with a length of pipe in it, or a pipe with a man attached—and then Gideon hit the shadow the way floodwater hits a piling, no windup, no warning, just arrival. They went down together. The pipe skittered. The man scrambled up and ran, and Gideon let him run, because Gideon never chases what the river will deliver anyway.

Which left us on our knees on the burning wharf with the *Cormorant* dying beside us and half of Harrow's Landing pouring out of its houses with buckets and bad advice.

"Are you hurt?" His hands were on my arms, my shoulders, turning me over like cargo, checking for damage. His palms smelled of pitch and blood. "Look at me. Are you hurt?"

"No. No, I—" My voice cracked, and I hated it. "Captain, they were waiting for me. They followed me from the Lark and they knew which boat was ours, and I led them here, I—"

"You asked questions in a tavern."

"I asked questions in a tavern," I agreed, and then, because the guilt was bigger than my throat, "I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I thought—questions are free, my mother always said—"

"Your mother," Gideon said, hauling me upright, "never pulled a chest out of a river." He looked at the fire, and the fire lit half his face and left the other half in river-dark, and what I saw on the lit half wasn't anger. It was something tired and old and almost gentle. "Come below. Both of us nearly died tonight because you didn't know what you were carrying. So tonight you learn."

* * *

The cabin of the *Marrow's Grace* is six feet by eight and smells of tar and coffee, and I have slept in it two seasons, and until that night I had never once been invited to sit at the little table while the captain unlocked anything.

He brought the chest in and set it between us like a third passenger.

"Vhaloria," I said.

"Vhaloria." He worked the lock with a bent nail, not looking at me. "Twenty-two years ago there was a country by that name upriver of everything, over the Kessler shoulder, in a valley the maps used to carry. I was born there. First seven years of my life, Vhalorian dirt under my nails." The lock gave. "Then the maps changed."

"How does a country stop being on maps?"

"The way a boat stops being on a river." He opened the lid, and the lamplight fell inside, onto the ledger with its columns of names, onto the folded treaty with its ribbons and seals. Onto the inked signature inside the lid, and I watched his jaw tighten around it like a man biting down on a hook. "Somebody burns it. Somebody pays the mapmakers. And everybody who might remember gets very busy remembering something else."

"My father's name is in that book," he said, quieter. "Elias Vasquez. He carried papers for them, near the end. Courier work. Then one day the couriers stopped coming back and my mother stopped waiting up." He closed the lid softly, softer than I'd ever seen him touch anything. "I've spent twenty years on the water because water doesn't keep records. You can't be from anywhere on a river. That's the whole appeal."

"And the men who burned the *Cormorant*—"

"Work for the people who erased it. Or bought what was left of them. Corliss, probably, or Corliss's betters." He rubbed his eyes. "There's a ledger in there full of names, Tam. Living ones, maybe. People who were supposed to get it back and never did. And a treaty that I think is the reason my country isn't a country anymore. The men upstairs want both. The people the names belong to want both. And a boy who works for me for room, board, and eleven coppers a week decided to go asking about it in taverns."

The guilt landed then, all of it at once, like cargo shifting. "I didn't know."

"No. You didn't. That's the only reason you're sitting there instead of floating past the landing come morning." He leaned forward. "So hear me now, because I'll only say it once. From tonight, you don't ask. You don't talk. You don't look at the chest where anyone can see you looking. You pack like we leave tomorrow, because we might. And if a stranger buys you a drink, you drink it slow and you count the exits. Can you do that?"

"Yes."

"Say it like you mean it."

"I can do that." And then, because the fire was still in my nose and the man with the pipe was still in my head and I was seventeen and scared and done pretending otherwise: "Why keep me on? You could put me ashore at dawn and travel lighter. Any sane man would."

Gideon looked at me across the chest, across the dead country between us, and for a second something moved behind his eyes that he'd deny to his grave.

"Because you came back to the boat," he said finally. "Man with a pipe behind you, and you came back here instead of running for the hills. That's the first sensible thing anyone's done on this wharf all week." He stood, and the conversation was clearly over, the way weather is over. "Sleep. We decide at first light whether we burn, sell, or run."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I lay in the fo'c'sle listening to the *Cormorant* settle into herself as she cooled, black ribs above black water, and I thought about a country I'd never seen that lived on in a wax seal and a woman's flea-market plate and the locked jaw of the man who owned my wages.

Toward morning I heard him on deck, talking low—not to me, not to himself. To the river, maybe. Old men do that.

And then I heard the other thing. Hooves on the wharf road, more than one horse, moving slow, the way men move when they're not leaving.

"Gideon," I called through the bulkhead. "Company."

A pause. Then his voice, level as still water:

"Aye. I see them." And then, dry as driftwood, almost a laugh: "Pack light, Tam. Looks like the river's made our decision for us."
