## 10. Leaving the River Behind

The *Maren*—no, that's not her name, and I'll get to why it isn't in a moment—the *Kestrel's Luck* sat low in the water at the Grayhaven dock like an old dog that knows the vet is coming, and I stood on her deck with a bill of sale in my pocket and a price in my head that would have made my younger self laugh out loud.

Three hundred crowns. For a boat I'd sunk two thousand into over eleven years. Tam did the arithmetic for me twice, thinking I'd made an error, and when I hadn't he looked at me the way you look at a man who's started talking to his own hands.

"You're giving her away," he said.

"I'm selling her."

"To Widow Corrick. Who has ninety crowns to her name and three children."

"Then we're negotiating hard," I said, "and she doesn't know it yet."

Here is the thing about riverboat men, the thing nobody who hasn't been one can understand: the boat isn't property. Property is something you own. A boat is something you *are*, or something you've become, the way a hermit crab becomes its shell. Eleven years I'd lived inside that hull. I knew every plank of her by feel, knew which seam wept in cold water and which one lied about it, knew the exact throttle position where her engine stopped complaining and started singing. When I stood at her wheel I didn't pilot her so much as agree with her. She was the last thing my father never touched, which is another way of saying she was the only thing he never ruined, and I had built her into a life precisely because she had nothing to do with him.

And now I was going to hand her to a stranger for less than her anchor chain was worth, because a sealed chest with a dead country's crest on it had turned my name into a target, and because the pass over Mount Kessler closed with the snows, and because somewhere behind me a man named Corliss was spending money like floodwater to buy back paper I hadn't even read all of yet.

You don't outrun that kind of trouble on a riverboat. A riverboat has exactly two directions and everyone knows both of them.

Widow Corrick came down to the dock at midmorning, which told me she'd already heard I was selling, which told me Tam had been talking again, which told me I needed to have a conversation with that boy about the difference between helpful and expensive. She was maybe forty, dressed in mourning gray gone soft at the elbows, with the particular posture of a woman who has learned that the world takes things and has decided to stop being surprised by it. Her husband had run a produce barge upriver until the spring flood took him and the barge together two years back. I remembered the funeral bell. Everyone on the river remembers funeral bells; they're the only bells we get.

"Captain Vasquez." She said it carefully, like a woman counting coins she wasn't sure were real. "They say you're selling the *Luck*."

"They say right."

"For how much?"

I named a figure. It was not three hundred crowns. It was not even close to three hundred crowns. It was a number I'd chosen at four in the morning while staring at the cabin ceiling, a number calibrated to be just high enough that she wouldn't smell charity—because charity from a man like me comes with questions attached, and questions are the one cargo I couldn't afford to be carrying—and just low enough that she could actually pay it if she sold the house on Marsh Lane, which I happened to know she'd been trying to do since summer.

She went very still. Women like her don't gasp. They go still, and you can watch them doing sums behind their eyes, and then they start looking for the trap.

"Why?" she said.

"Because I'm leaving the river."

"Where?"

"Over the mountains."

"Nobody goes over the mountains this time of year."

"People are about to find that out the hard way," I said, and immediately wished I hadn't, because it was too true and too strange, and she narrowed her eyes at me the way Tam does, and I realized with some irritation that I was surrounded by people who'd learned to read me.

"The boat needs work," I said, before she could ask anything worse. "The stern bearing will want attention by midsummer. The boiler's sound but the firebox grate is half-eaten. I'll show you everything. And—" I hesitated, because this was the part that actually cost, though I couldn't have explained why. "I'll stay aboard through the end of the week. Teach you her moods. She's stubborn in a crosswind off the Kessler bluffs and she'll sulk if you let the steam drop below sixty. You have to talk to her."

"Talk to her."

"Out loud. It sounds foolish."

"It doesn't," said Widow Corrick, and something crossed her face that I pretended not to see, because I'd seen enough of it in mirrors. "My husband talked to his barge. I used to tell him the barge couldn't hear him. He said that wasn't the point."

We shook on it there at the rail, her hand small and dry and firm as a cleat, and that was that. Two hundred and ten crowns, payable against the house sale, and the *Kestrel's Luck* passed out of my life in the space of a handshake, eleven years traded away in the time it takes to draw a breath.

I want to tell you I felt nothing. I want to tell you it was easy, that a boat is a boat, that a man who lives light should be able to set things down lightly. But I walked her length that afternoon touching things—her wheel spokes worn smooth as river stones, the brass fitting Tam had polished on a dare, the dent in the rail where I'd misjudged the Grayhaven lock my first season and paid for the repair out of a winter's wages—and every touch was a small argument I was losing. This is yours. This kept you warm. This kept you *hidden*. Because that was the truth under all of it, the truth I'd never said out loud: the river had been my hiding place. A man with no fixed address, no family name anyone connected to anything, no history deeper than the last port—a man like that is invisible, and invisible men don't get found by the past. The *Kestrel's Luck* wasn't just my living. She was my disguise. And I was selling the disguise along with the man who wore it, because the man had finally been recognized anyway, and a disguise that fails is just weight.

Tam found me at dusk sitting in the pilothouse with a mug of coffee I'd forgotten to drink.

"It's a good thing you're doing," he said.

"It's a business transaction."

"It's a good thing you're doing," he repeated, with the patience of the young, who believe that saying a thing twice makes it truer. He leaned against the doorframe. He'd grown in the months since I'd taken him on—all elbows and appetite still, but taller, and with a new habit of standing square-on to things instead of sideways, like a boy deciding to become a man in whatever order the parts arrived. "What happens to us? After the mountains."

"After the mountains," I said, "you can go anywhere you like. You'll have your share. More than your share—I'm cutting you in at crew rates, not deckhand rates."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know what you asked."

"You didn't answer it."

I looked out at the river. It was doing its evening trick, turning copper and then violet and then the color of nothing at all, and somewhere downstream a night boat was coming up with its running lights doubled, and none of it cared what I decided. That's the river's great gift and its great insult: perfect indifference, available free of charge forever.

"I don't know," I said. "That's the honest answer. I've spent eleven years knowing exactly what tomorrow looked like, and I find I don't miss it yet, but ask me again in a month."

Tam nodded slowly, accepting this, or appearing to, which on the river amounts to the same thing.

"And the chest comes with us," he said. Not a question either.

"The chest comes with us."

"Because of your father."

"Because it isn't mine to keep," I said, which was true, and also because of my father, which was also true, and I didn't sort out the proportions for him because I hadn't sorted them out for myself. There's a ledger in that chest with names in it—living names, names of people who crawled out of a drowning country and kept breathing—and somewhere past that pass there's a woman called Maren Ostrova who signed herself *custodian*, and a custodian is someone who holds a thing in trust, and I had come to understand, in the way you understand things at four in the morning, that trust was the whole of it. My father's name was inked inside that lid. Whatever he'd done or failed to do, the ledger was the closest thing to a message he'd ever left me, and messages like that don't get delivered by staying comfortable.

* * *

We bought mules on Thursday.

This deserves its own paragraph, because buying mules taught me more about myself than selling the boat did, and cheaper. There's a horse trader off Cordwainer Street named Fennick who deals in animals the way undertakers deal in clients—respectfully, and always with an eye on the eventual profit. I wanted two mules, sound, sure-footed, mountain-bred. Fennick showed me four. The first was lame in a way he hoped I wouldn't notice until we were a county apart. The second was a liar's dream, calm as a church mouse in the yard, and I watched its eye when a cart rattled past and saw the white rim of a animal that was going to bolt on the first scree slope and take my gear with it. The third was honest but old, and old costs you on a mountain in ways that don't show up in the purchase price.

The fourth was a dun mare-mule with a scarred muzzle and the flat, unimpressed stare of a creature that has been handled by experts and fools and has opinions about both. She bit Fennick while I was looking at her teeth, which endeared her to me instantly. Any animal that bites its salesman is telling you the truth about somebody.

"That one," I said.

"She's got a temper."

"So do I. What's her price?"

He named it. I paid it without haggling, and Fennick's eyebrows climbed toward his hatband, because on the riverfront a man who doesn't haggle is either rich or desperate, and he was shrewd enough to wonder which, and I was tired enough not to care. We took her and a pack mule, a stolid brown gelding with no personality whatsoever, which after the dun was almost restful.

Tam named the dun within the hour. Of course he did. He named her *Vhaloria*, which I let stand because arguing with Tam about names is like arguing with the river about mud—you'll lose, and you'll be muddy afterward—and because there was a certain justice in it. If we were carrying that country over the mountains, we might as well ride it.

Friday morning we loaded. I won't bore you with the inventory except to say that packing for a mountain crossing when you've spent your life packing for a river is an education in subtraction. A riverboat forgives abundance; there's always room for one more crate of something you don't need. A mountain trail punishes it. Every pound you carry is a pound you carry up, and up is the whole job. We took oilskins, wool, rope, the small iron stove, flour and salt pork and dried apples, a canvas shelter-half, medical kit, ammunition, tools. And the chest, wrapped in sacking and lashed under oilcloth, riding in the middle of the dun's load where it would be least conspicuous and best protected, weighing—Tam weighed it, because Tam weighs everything—just under ninety pounds.

Ninety pounds of other people's history. I've carried heavier loads for less reason.

Saturday dawned gray and still, the kind of stillness that on the river means weather gathering somewhere out of sight, saving itself up. I walked the *Kestrel's Luck* one last time at first light, alone, because some farewells you do without witnesses. I checked her lines though she didn't need checking. I stood at her wheel and put my hands on the spokes and felt, absurdly, like a man shaking hands with himself. Then I went ashore and didn't look back, because I've watched enough men look back at boats and I know what it does to their faces.

Widow Corrick was waiting on the dock with her children ranged behind her like a stern little honor guard. The oldest boy, maybe twelve, looked at me with the naked hunger boys that age have for anything with an engine.

"She's yours," I said. "Mind the bearing. Talk to her. And when the spring flood comes, tie off double at Grayhaven—the current sets funny against the north piling and everyone learns it the expensive way."

"Thank you, Captain." She said it simply, and pressed something into my hand—a small cloth bundle, still warm. Bread and hard cheese, wrapped like a gift, because to her this was a gift and not a fire sale, and I found I didn't have the heart or the words to correct her. Some kindnesses you just accept and walk away from quickly, before they finish undoing you.

We went up Cordwainer Street and out past the tanneries, two men, two mules, and a wrapped chest, and the town thinned around us the way towns do—houses giving way to sheds, sheds to fences, fences to the long grass that marks the edge of everywhere. The road bent north toward the gray shoulder of Mount Kessler, visible now most days when the haze lifted, wearing its first suggestion of snow like a man trying on a hat he hasn't decided to buy.

Tam walked beside the dun and talked. He talked about the mountains, about whether mules could swim, about whether there were wolves, about whether I'd ever shot a wolf, about what Vhaloria had been like, about whether Maren Ostrova would be nice. I answered perhaps one question in five and he seemed perfectly satisfied with that ratio, which is one of the hundred reasons I'd grown fond of him and one of the reasons I was dreading the trail, because there is no escaping conversation on a mountain path. On a boat you can send a boy forward. On a trail he's beside you for nine hours a day.

But near midday he went quiet, and I'd learned to respect Tam's silences, because they usually meant he was working up to something real.

"Captain," he said finally. "Do you think we'll come back?"

I considered the question honestly, which is more than I usually manage. Behind us lay the river, eleven years of it, the whole architecture of a life I'd built specifically to avoid being anyone in particular. Ahead lay a pass that would close with the snows, a valley full of ghosts with pulse rates, and a reckoning with a dead man's handwriting. Somewhere between those two points was Sergeant Hale Brannic, whom I hadn't seen since the war and expected to see again sooner than either of us wanted, because Brannic had never in his life left a job unfinished, and I had made myself into his job.

"I don't know," I said. "Ask me again at the top."

And then, because the road bent and the trees opened, I saw the trailhead ahead—the post-and-rail fence, the faded sign, the narrow track climbing into timber—and standing at the gatepost, leaning on it with the easy patience of a man who has been waiting a while and expects to wait longer, was a big shape in a travel-stained coat that I recognized from fifty yards, the way you recognize a voice in a dark room.

Hale Brannic. At the trailhead. Smiling.

"Well," I said to Tam, "looks like we won't have to wonder long."
