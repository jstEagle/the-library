## 12. Up Through the Timberline

I want it on the record that I never agreed to become a mountain man.

I'd agreed to be a deckhand. Decking a hand is what I understood: coiling line, scrubbing decks, learning which end of the boat was which. Somewhere between the river and the treeline, the job description had changed without anyone consulting me, which, come to think of it, was exactly how Captain Vasquez handled everything.

"Keep to the dark ground," Gideon said, not looking back. "Wet dirt shows less than dry. Move when I move. Stop when I stop. And if I drop flat, you drop faster."

"And if you fall off the mountain?"

"Then you're finally captain."

It was the closest thing to a joke I'd heard out of him since the trailhead, and I took it the way a drowning man takes a plank — gratefully, and without examining it too closely.

Behind us, somewhere below the timberline, Sergeant Hale Brannic's soldiers were doing what soldiers do, which is moving in a line and making noise. We'd heard them twice in the first hour: the clink of a canteen against a belt buckle, once, and later a voice calling something muffled and official through the pines. Gideon had frozen both times with a stillness that frightened me more than the soldiers did. It wasn't the stillness of a man hiding. It was the stillness of a man who had done this before, in worse places, and remembered exactly how.

We climbed.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about mountains: they are just rivers running the wrong direction.

I figured this out around midday, watching Gideon read the slope the way he read the Kessler shallows. On the river, he'd squint at a stretch of brown water and know — know, not guess — that a sandbar lay under it, that the channel bent left, that the current would try to kill us in exactly that spot. He'd steer around trouble before I'd even seen the trouble existed.

He did the same thing with the mountain. He'd stop, tilt his head, and study the hillside like it was water.

"See that scree field?" he said, pointing with his chin at a slope of loose gray stone. "Looks like the fast way. It's not. Scree moves. Every step you take, you take twice — once going up, once sliding back. And it sings." He glanced at me. "You know what scree sounds like when a dozen men are crossing it?"

"Loud?"

"Like a river telling on you. We go through the trees. Trees are quiet."

"But the trees are steeper."

"Everything's steeper. Steep is fine. Loud is dead." He said it mildly, like he was correcting my knots, and started climbing.

I followed, and I watched, and I learned. That was the thing about Gideon Vasquez that I'd never understood on the boat and understood completely by the second hour of the mountain: he wasn't a lucky man. He was a *reading* man. The river had taught him a language, and it turned out the mountain spoke a dialect of the same one. Where water pooled, snow would pool. Where the slope was broken and stepped, a man could climb it; where it was smooth and long, it was a slide waiting to happen. He picked our line through the pines the way he'd picked our channel through the shallows — always the way that looked harder and wasn't.

"Water runs downhill," he said, when I asked him how he knew. "Snow melts. Water. Rock wears where water runs. A mountain's just a river with a bad temper. You read it the same way, only slower, and it holds a grudge longer."

"Did you learn that in the war?"

His shoulders did a thing. I'd learned to read his shoulders the way he read rivers.

"Ask me something easier," he said.

* * *

We ate at noon, if you could call it eating. Hard bread, dried meat that fought back, water from a stream Gideon approved of after studying it for a full minute like it might be lying to him.

"Drink small," he said. "Cold water on a hot climb will cramp you. Small sips."

I drank small. I sat on a fallen pine with my back against its trunk and my legs hanging over the drop, and I looked down, and I made a mistake.

The river was down there. Our river. The Kessler, silver and thin as a dropped ribbon, winding through the valley floor like it hadn't a care in the world — which, I realized, it didn't. The river didn't care about warrants. The river didn't care about sealed chests or erased countries or old sergeants with friendly faces and soldiers in the treeline. The river just ran, and everything that had ever happened to me had happened on its banks, and from up here it looked small enough to step over.

"It's strange," I said. "It looks like it belongs to somebody else."

"Everything does, from up high." Gideon was watching the treeline below us, chewing without pleasure. "That's why mountain people are strange. You get far enough above your life, you stop recognizing it."

"Is that what you're doing? Getting above yours?"

He was quiet long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer. He'd gotten good at that lately — the silences. On the boat, he used to fill silence with orders. Up here, he let it sit.

"Below mine, if anything," he said finally. "Come on. We want the saddle before the light goes, or close to it."

"The light goes at what, six?"

"Up here? Four. The mountain eats the evening early." He stood, brushed off his trousers, and gave me a look I'd started to recognize as his version of concern. "How are your legs?"

"Lying to me," I said. "Same as yours, I'd wager."

Something happened to his face then — not a smile, exactly. More like a smile that had gotten lost looking for the door. "Wager accepted. Loser carries the chest."

I looked at the chest. The chest, lead-lined and full of a dead country's names, sat where we'd set it, smug as a tax collector.

"I retract the wager," I said.

"Thought you might."

* * *

The afternoon was when it stopped being an adventure.

I want to be honest about this, because I've heard men talk about mountains the way they talk about women they've only seen across a room — all romance and no morning breath. The afternoon on that slope was not romantic. The afternoon was my thighs filing a formal complaint, my lungs renegotiating their contract, and the air getting thinner with every hundred feet until each breath felt like it had to be requested in writing.

The trees changed first. The big pines thickened, then thinned, then gave up entirely, and we came out into a world of scrub and stone where the wind lived. It hit me the moment we cleared the timber — a long, cold shove that smelled of ice and nothing, like the mountain breathing on the back of my neck.

"Wind's from the northwest," Gideon said, turning his face into it. "Storm weather."

"You can tell that from a wind?"

"I can tell that from a wind *and* the clouds." He pointed up. I looked. The clouds looked like clouds to me — white, gray, up there, doing cloud business. "See how they're building? Stacking themselves? Flat bottoms, tall tops, coming out of the northwest?" He shook his head. "That's not weather. That's a *promise*. And the mountain always keeps its promises."

"How long?"

"Day, maybe two. Maybe less." He said it lightly, but I'd been his deckhand long enough to know that when Gideon Vasquez said *maybe less*, he meant *less*.

We climbed on. The scree fields he'd refused that morning became unavoidable by mid-afternoon, and I learned what he'd meant about taking every step twice. The gray stone slid and clattered under our boots, and every few minutes a pebble would break loose somewhere below us and go ticking down the slope, and both of us would freeze and listen, hearts hammering, until the mountain went quiet again.

Once, it wasn't a pebble.

Gideon's arm shot out and caught me flat against the rock, and we lay there in the shadow of a stone shelf while, far below and to the east, a sound carried up the slope: voices. Boots on scree. The thin, carrying clang of metal that no amount of army discipline ever seems to silence.

"Off our line," Gideon breathed, after a long minute. "They've drifted west. Good."

"How can you tell?"

"Sound carries up in cold air, but it bends. They're below us and east of where they think we are." He lifted his head a careful inch, squinted, and settled back down. "Brannic's good. But he's reading a mountain the way a soldier reads one — ridgelines and sight lines. He's not reading it like water."

"Is that bad for him?"

"It's bad for him." Gideon's mouth did that almost-smile again. "The mountain doesn't care where you *want* to look."

We lay there until the sounds faded, and I lay with my cheek against cold stone and thought about how strange it was — that I was lying on a mountain hiding from soldiers because of a chest we'd pulled out of a river, and that six months ago my biggest worry was whether the *Marianne's* boiler would survive another season. My whole life had been the river, and the river had been the whole world, and now the whole world was a gray slope and a thin wind and a man beside me who'd turned out to have more layers than the mountain did.

"Captain," I whispered, while we waited.

"Mm."

"Why do you know all this? The war stuff. The mountain stuff. You talk about rivers like a pilot and mountains like a — like a *scout*."

He was quiet so long I thought he'd decided not to answer. Then, still watching the treeline below, he said: "My father's people were mountain people. Before the river. Before any of it." A pause. "Vhaloria isn't all lowlands, whatever the maps don't say anymore. There's country up high. There was." He corrected himself the way a man corrects himself when the wound is fresh: *there was.* "I spent summers in it. Before."

"Before what?"

"Before," he said, and that was the end of the conversation, and I knew better than to push. But I turned the word over as we climbed. *Before.* Before the collapse. Before his father vanished into whatever hole history had poured him into. Before the chest, the ledger, the name inked inside the lid that Gideon wouldn't talk about but touched, once, when he thought I was asleep, the way you'd touch a scar to check it's still there.

* * *

We gained the saddle as the light was going, exactly as he'd promised, and I have never been so glad to be wrong about something. I'd figured the saddle was the top. It was not the top. The saddle was a notch between two shoulders of the mountain, a windy col of broken rock and stubborn grass, and beyond it the land rose again into country I didn't have words for yet — higher, whiter, worse.

But from the saddle, looking back, I saw the whole day's work laid out beneath us: the timberline a dark ragged hem, the valley floor beyond it going blue with evening, and the river — our river — a last thin gleam of silver before the dark took it.

"We did that," I said. I was breathing hard and grinning like an idiot. "We climbed all that. In one day. On *legs*."

"We did that," Gideon agreed. He set the chest down — I insisted on taking a turn with it, and he let me for exactly twenty minutes before reclaiming it without discussion — and stood with his hands on his hips, reading the western sky.

The clouds had kept building. They were stacked now like plates on a merchant's shelf, tall and bruised at the bottoms, marching out of the northwest with the patience of something that had never once been late.

"Tam." His voice had changed. I'd learned his voices by then: pilot-voice, captain-voice, the low one he used when he talked about his father. This was a new one. This was the one he used for weather and for trouble, which up here were apparently the same word. "How do you feel about camping cold?"

"I feel like I've never done it and I already hate it."

"Good. Honest. That'll keep you alive up here." He crouched, laid his palm flat on the rock, and left it there a moment, like he was taking the mountain's pulse. When he straightened, he was looking north along the saddle, toward the higher country, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen on the river — not fear, exactly. Recognition. Like a man hearing a language he'd been made to forget.

"There's a place," he said, half to himself. "Above the next rise. Old trail station, maybe. Or a cave. My father—" He stopped. Started again. "There's shelter up there if it hasn't fallen in. Twenty years is a long time for anything to stand on a mountain."

"Twenty years," I said. "Since Vhaloria."

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. The whole day had been an answer: the way he read the slope, the way he knew the wind, the way he'd said *there was* and couldn't leave it alone.

We made camp in the lee of a rock shelf, small fire, small everything, because Gideon said firelight carried on mountains like gossip in a river town. I wrapped myself in the blanket and shook — not from fear, I told myself, though the shaking said otherwise — and watched the last light die on the peaks above us.

"Captain?"

"Mm."

"The soldiers. Brannic. They'll keep coming, won't they. Even in the cold."

Gideon fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "Brannic will. Brannic's got a reason for every step he takes, even the wrong ones. The soldiers with him — they'll follow as long as following's easier than explaining to Corliss why they didn't." He looked up at the stacked clouds, then at me, and for a second something crossed his face that I'd never seen there on the river. It took me a moment to name it, because I'd never seen it aimed at anything before.

It was worry. Not for himself.

"Sleep, Tam," he said. "Tomorrow we go higher. And tomorrow—" he paused, listening to something in the wind that I couldn't hear yet, "—tomorrow the mountain starts collecting."

I slept anyway. What else was there to do? And if I dreamed, I dreamed of water — of the river running silver and easy down below, not caring, not knowing, carrying everything away — and when I woke in the gray before dawn, the first flakes of the year's first snow were landing on my blanket, silent as signatures, and Gideon was already standing, already watching the north, already saying the word I'd spend the rest of that winter learning the true weight of:

"Soon."
