## 14. Snowline

The snow came at dawn on the fourth day above the timberline, and it came like a debt collector—quietly, politely, and all at once.

I woke with Tam's boot in my ribs, which was how the boy expressed urgency. "Captain," he whispered, which was worse than shouting. Tam only called me Captain when he thought something was dying.

I sat up out of the lean-to of pine boughs we'd built against a granite shelf, and the world had gone white. Not the pretty kind of white, either—the kind that erases. The trail we'd been following, the switchbacks below us, the whole patient geography of the mountain: gone. Just a gray sky pressing down on a blank page, and somewhere under that page, three days behind us or maybe one, Hale Brannic and whatever men Corliss's money had bought him.

"Early," Tam said. "Isn't it? Early for this."

"Everything about this mountain is early," I said. "It's an ambitious mountain."

He didn't laugh. Neither did I, really. A man learns to hear his own jokes die and keeps making them anyway; it's how you know you're still yourself.

* * *

We made camp the night before in the shelter of a leaning slab of stone, and by morning the snow was ankle-deep and falling with the patience of something that intended to keep falling until spring if it felt like it. I stood at the mouth of our shelter and did what I'd done my whole life on the river: I read the water. Except up here the water was frozen, and reading it meant reading snow, wind, rock, and the particular silence a mountain makes when it's deciding whether to kill you.

The river taught me everything I know about reading danger, and the first lesson is this: danger doesn't announce itself. It shows you its wake. A sandbar telegraphs itself in the color of the current, the way the surface wrinkles over the shallow. Brannic was a sandbar. He wasn't visible, but his wake was everywhere—in the cut saplings we'd found at the last campsite, still green at the break; in the way the crows had lifted off the valley below us at midmorning, spooked by something two-legged moving through the timber.

"They're close," Tam said. He'd learned to read wakes too, faster than I liked. Boys learn fast when learning fast is the difference between eating and being eaten.

"A day," I said. "Maybe less. They've got mules. We've got feet and your charming company."

"You could be nicer about it."

"I could. But then you'd expect it regularly."

He almost smiled, which was the point. Then he looked down the slope, where the trail vanished into the white, and the smile went wherever smiles go when they're not wanted anymore.

Here's the arithmetic I was doing while the boy watched the slope: the pass over Kessler's shoulder closed with the deep snows. Everyone said so—Ilsa Brandt had said it with the flat certainty of a woman who'd wintered on this mountain longer than most towns had existed. We had maybe four days of walking to the saddle. If the snow kept falling like this, the trail would fill in, and after that it wouldn't matter whether Brannic caught us, because the mountain would catch us first, and the mountain doesn't take prisoners, it takes bodies.

Which meant speed. And speed, on a mountain wearing fresh snow, means choosing which way you want to die: slow, behind, and hunted—or fast, ahead, and stupid.

* * *

By midday the snow thinned to flurries and the cloud tore open just enough to show us the country ahead, and that was when we saw the rockslide.

It had come down sometime in the night or the years before—hard to say—with the casual violence of mountains. A whole face of the slope below the ridge had let go, leaving a scar of raw scree two hundred yards wide, tumbled boulders the size of riverboats fanned across the trail like a hand of terrible cards. The low route, the safe route, the route any sane traveler would pick: buried. You could pick through it, maybe, an hour at a time, testing each stone, but every hour we spent picking through rubble was an hour Brannic spent gaining, and his men would have ropes and numbers and no chest slowing them down.

Or there was the other way.

The ridge. It ran above the slide like a spine, narrow as a gunwale in places, exposed to the wind that was already combing the peaks into long white banners. In dry weather it would have been a hard walk. In fresh snow, with the rock underneath still warm enough from some buried summer to make the footing treacherous—snow over stone over air—it would be a walk where every step got exactly one vote, and a miscount dropped you eight hundred feet onto the rocks below.

Tam looked at the ridge, then at the slide, then at me. He'd gotten good at knowing when I was doing arithmetic I didn't like.

"The ridge," he said. Not a question. That's the trouble with teaching people to read water—they start reading you.

"The ridge," I agreed. "Stay ten feet apart, so we don't load the same stretch of rock twice. Pole from your uphill side." I'd cut him a staff back at the timberline, ash wood, and he'd carried it without complaint, which told me more about how much the boy had changed than anything he could've said. Three weeks ago he'd have complained beautifully. Now he just checked the strap on the chest harness and waited.

Because yes, I was carrying the chest. Forty pounds of lead-lined stubbornness strapped to my back like a second spine. Every sensible cell in my body had voted to leave it in a crevasse weeks ago, and every sensible cell had lost to the same argument: inside that chest were names, and names are the only thing the dead get to keep. You don't rob the dead twice. My father had taught me that, though he'd taught it by example rather than lecture, and his example had been vanishing into a country that stopped existing, and I'd hated him for twenty years for it, and here I was hauling his ghost up a mountain in a box.

Life has a sense of humor. It's just never been pointed at me.

* * *

The ridge took the rest of the day and gave nothing back.

I'll tell you what it was like, since you weren't there and should count that among your blessings. It was like walking the gunwale of a boat in a swell, except the boat was stone, the swell was wind, and falling overboard meant falling onto a fan of broken rock that would sort your bones by size. The snow lay thin on the crest, scoured by wind, and where it lay thick it hid the gaps between stones, and the gaps between stones were the whole game. I went first, because I was heavier and because if the ridge wanted a toll I preferred to pay it myself before invoicing the boy.

Twice the mountain tried to collect early.

The first time was small—a slab the size of a cabin door shifted under my left foot with a sound like a drawer opening, and I did the thing you do, which is nothing, absolutely nothing, weight balanced, breath held, while the stone decided whether it was furniture or avalanche. It settled. I moved on. My heart filed a formal complaint that I ignored.

The second time was not small.

We were maybe halfway along the worst stretch, the narrows where the ridge pinched down to three feet of rock with daylight on both sides, when the wind came around the shoulder of the peak above us and hit us broadside. It hit Tam. He was light—he'd always been light, all elbows and appetite—and the gust picked him like it was owed money. His staff skated off stone. He went down on one knee, sliding, and the snow under him started to go, a soft hiss, the sound the river makes right before it takes something.

I've pulled drowning men out of brown water with one hand. This was the same motion, translated. I dropped flat, hooked his harness strap as he slid past, and the world became two points: my grip and his weight, and the ridge grinding under my ribs, and eight hundred feet of afternoon open below us like a held breath.

"Hold," I said. Which was ridiculous. He wasn't going anywhere; I was the one with the grip. But you talk to drowning men, even when the drowning man is yourself.

Then the chest saved us. I don't know another way to put it. The harness had slipped during the fall and the chest's corner dug into the rock behind me, a forty-pound anchor biting stone, and the drag of it stopped my slide with my boots hanging over the drop and Tam's full weight swinging off my arm like a landed fish.

We got him up. It took longer than it should have, because my arms had turned to rope and my breath was coming in coins, small and expensive, the altitude taxing every one. When he was finally belly-down on solid rock beside me, both of us breathing like bellows with a hole in them, he looked at me with snow in his eyelashes and said:

"You could've let go."

"I could've," I agreed. "But then who'd carry the chest?"

He laughed—the cracked, gasping laugh of a boy who'd just seen the bottom of the sky—and I laughed too, or made a noise adjacent to laughing, and neither of us said the true thing, which was standing right there between us wearing my face: that I hadn't let go because letting go of Tam Osei had stopped being an option somewhere back on the river, and I'd known it the way you know a sandbar under dark water, without looking, without wanting to, without any power to change it.

Survival demands payment. That day it demanded I admit what I'd been carrying besides the chest. I paid it privately and moved on.

* * *

We came off the ridge at dusk, down into a hanging bowl of rock where the wind couldn't reach, and I'll be honest with you: I don't remember much of making camp. There's a kind of tired that edits memory for efficiency. I remember fire—small, screened, Ilsa Brandt's voice in my head saying *a fire they can see is a signal, a fire they can't is a life*—and I remember Tam asleep before his bedroll finished unfolding, and I remember sitting up on watch with the chest between my knees and the ledger's weight inside it, thinking about names.

Maren Ostrova. Custodian. Somewhere beyond the pass, a woman I'd never met was waiting on a box of paper, and twenty-odd men with rifles were climbing her mountain to stop it arriving, and one of those men had once shared my tent through a whole bad winter and taught me a card game I still cheat at.

Hale. I kept trying to fit him into the picture of the men behind us, and he kept not fitting. That was the trap of Hale Brannic: he was good. Genuinely, inconveniently good—a man who'd pulled me out of the Vhalor with a rope and a stream of profanity, who'd written to me for six years after the war until the letters slowed and stopped, who'd taken the recovery agent's badge for reasons I'd bet my boat he'd told himself were decent. Good men make the best hounds. They hunt harder, because they believe.

And they can be turned, if you show them the thing they're hunting isn't what they were told.

That thought had been growing in me since the trailhead, quiet as the snowfall. I turned it over now, watching the firelight move on the lead-lined lid. If it came to it—if the mountain gave me the chance—I might not have to outrun Hale Brannic. I might only have to show him the ledger. Let him read the names. Let him do the arithmetic himself: a treaty worth killing for is a treaty someone's afraid of, and fear that size always means the paper is a lie.

But that was tomorrow's problem, and tomorrow's problem had competition.

Because as I sat there, feeding twigs to the fire and listening to the mountain tick and settle around us, I heard it. Faint, downslope, carried up on the wind that had followed us off the ridge: voices. More than one. And under them, unmistakable to anyone who'd ever worked a deck, the ring of iron on stone—pitons, driven in sequence, unhurried, professional.

They weren't behind us anymore. They'd cut the angle while we crawled the ridge, exactly as I'd have done in their place, and they were coming up the far side of the bowl, and by the sound of it they'd reach the top of their line by morning.

I looked at Tam, asleep with his mouth open and his ash staff hugged like a rifle. I looked at the chest. I looked up, past the rim of the bowl, to where the pass waited—one more day's climb, through snow that was starting again, fat flakes drifting down through the dark like the mountain sending word ahead.

*First snow falls early,* I'd thought at dawn. Now I understood it differently. The snow wasn't early. It was punctual. It was the mountain keeping its own schedule, closing its own account, and the only question left was whether we'd cross the saddle before it shut the book—or whether we'd spend the winter on the wrong side of it, with Brannic's pitons ringing closer every hour.

I kicked snow over the fire and put my hand on the boy's shoulder.

"Tam," I said. "Up. We're burning the night."

He came awake the way he always did now—all at once, no questions—and in the dark, breaking camp by feel, I heard the pitons ring again below us, closer than before, and I understood that by sunrise, one way or another, this mountain was going to settle every account on it at once.
