## 15. Tam Falls

The ridge above the snowline was the kind of ground that made a man argue with himself, and I'd been losing the argument since dawn.

The safe route—the one Ilsa Brandt had traced on her table with a cracked thumbnail—curved east through the timber and down into the long bowl below Kessler Saddle. It would cost us two days. Two days we didn't have, because the weather that had been chewing at the horizon all morning wasn't going to wait for our convenience, and because Brannic's party was somewhere behind us, moving faster than men carrying warrants ought to move.

So I took the ridge.

I told myself it was arithmetic. Faster line, fewer hours exposed, better odds of beating both the storm and the bounty men to the saddle. That's what I told myself. What I actually knew, down in the place where rivermen keep their honest thoughts, was that I was tired of running scared and wanted to be done with this whole cursed errand, and speed felt like the closest thing to control a man could buy on a mountain.

Tam didn't question it. That was the trouble with the boy—he trusted me the way a young dog trusts the hand that feeds it, completely and without any sense whatsoever. He climbed behind me with the chest strapped across his back like it weighed nothing, though I could see by his breathing that it weighed plenty. He'd stopped complaining about it two days ago, which worried me more than complaining ever had.

"Captain," he said, when we paused on a shoulder of gray rock with the wind pulling at us from three directions at once, "you ever notice how everything up here wants to kill you? The cold wants to kill you. The rocks want to kill you. Even the *sun* wants to kill you."

"River's the same," I said. "It just does it slower, so you get comfortable first."

He grinned at that, breath steaming, cheeks raw as butcher's meat. Seventeen years old and grinning on a ridge where the wind could peel a man off the stone like bark off a birch. I turned away before he could see what my face did about it.

We made good time through the morning. Too good. That should have warned me. On the river, when everything runs smooth and the current carries you sweet and the wheel answers your hand like it's reading your mind—that's exactly when you check your depth soundings twice. Smooth water is the river lying to you. Ice is no different.

The trouble came where the ridge narrowed between two shoulders of rock and the wind had scoured it clean, leaving a ribbon of blue ice no wider than a wagon track. I saw it ahead, read it the way I'd read a sandbar—a shine in the wrong light, a smoothness where there should have been texture—and I called back for Tam to move to the rock on the left, kick his heels in, take it slow.

And he did what I asked. He always did what I asked. He stepped left, set his heel—

and the ice under that heel sheared away like a pane of glass knocked out of a window frame.

I heard it before I understood it: not a shout but a scrape, boots on stone going fast, then nothing, then a sound I will hear until I die, which was Tam's voice saying my name once—not screaming, just saying it, *Gideon*, like he was pointing something out to me—and then the silence after.

I was moving before the silence finished. That's the truth of it, whatever else I tell myself about being a careful man. No thought in it at all. I dropped flat, got my boot wedged against a boulder, and looked over the edge into a crevasse shelf—a slot in the mountain where the glacier underneath had pulled apart, roofed over with old snow bridges that had mostly rotted through. Tam had landed on one maybe fifteen feet down, a shelf of packed snow canted against the far wall, and he was on it, alive, on his side, the chest still strapped to him, one arm bent wrong under him.

"Fifteen feet," I shouted down. "You hear me?"

"I hear you." His voice came up thin and strange. "Captain—I can't feel my arm."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fear. It isn't loud. The loud part is afterward, when you're telling the story or lying awake. In the moment itself, fear is very quiet and very precise, like a pilot's hand on the wheel in dead-calm water at midnight. It tells you: rope. It tells you: weight. It tells you: do not look at his arm, look at your anchor.

I had sixty feet of line in my pack—we'd bought it at the trailhead town along with the mules, because Ilsa Brandt had looked at me and said, *You'll want rope, and you'll want to carry it even if you never use it,* and I'd paid for it without arguing, which was unlike me. I anchored it around the boulder, tested the boulder with everything I had, tested it again, and went over the edge.

Going down was easy. Going down is always easy; gravity does the work and pride pays the toll. The shelf held me. Tam was conscious, which was the one piece of good news the mountain was handing out that day. His left arm was broken above the elbow—I could see the shape of it through his coat, wrong the way a bent oar is wrong—and his face had gone the color of the ice.

"The chest," he said. "Gideon, the chest—"

"Forget the chest."

"It's heavy—it's pulling me toward the edge—"

He was right, damn him. The chest had swung around on its strap during the fall and was dragging at him, tipping him toward the lip of the shelf where the snow bridge thinned to nothing and the dark of the crevasse opened up below like a throat. I got my hands on the strap, hauled it off him, and shoved it up the slope of the shelf away from the edge, and then I put my arms around the boy and held him while he shook.

"We're getting out," I said. "You and me. Both of us. Say it back."

"You and me," he said. "Both of us."

His lips were blue. Not river-blue, not cold-morning-blue. Blue like ink. And his breathing had a sound in it I didn't like at all, a wet rattle on the left side that hadn't been there an hour ago.

Getting him up fifteen feet of rotten ice with one working arm took the better part of an hour and cost me skin I'll never grow back. Twice the shelf groaned under us like a ship's hull in a storm, and twice I stopped and listened to the mountain deciding whether it meant it. The second time, a crack ran past my boot like a sentence being written, and I moved faster after that.

When we came over the lip onto solid rock, I lay on my back next to Tam and laughed—actually laughed, short and ugly, the way a man laughs when the alternative is worse. Then I rolled over and looked at his arm and stopped laughing.

* * *

Tam's account of the next few hours is shorter than mine, because most of them he spent drifting in and out of a fever that came on him like weather.

I remember the cold coming down off the peaks early, earlier than it had any right to, the storm front I'd been racing arriving ahead of schedule like a creditor. I remember understanding, with the clarity that only comes to a man who has made a bad decision and watched it bear fruit, that we were not making the saddle tonight. Maybe not tomorrow either. That Tam could not walk on a broken arm with a lung filling up, and that I could not carry him and the chest both through fresh snow, and that the ridge offered nothing in the way of shelter except wind-scoured stone.

I remember finding the hollow.

It wasn't much—a fold in the leeward face where the drift had built up against a slab of granite, leaving a pocket maybe eight feet deep. With the shovel from the mule's panniers—poor beasts were huddled below, tied where the ridge met the timberline, and I prayed they had sense enough to stay put—I cut into the drift the way you'd dig into a riverbank, and in an hour I had a cave of sorts, low and close, walls packed hard enough to hold. I dragged Tam in on the rope like a landed fish, and I got the small spirit stove lit, and I did the things you do for a broken arm—splinted it with a tent stake and strips of shirt, bound it tight—and then there was nothing left to do but sit in the blue light of the stove flame and listen to him breathe.

That rattle in his chest got worse as the dark came down.

"Gideon." His eyes were open, unfocused, fixed on the cave roof. "Gideon, the water's high."

"There's no water, boy. You're on a mountain."

"No—" He licked his lips. Cracked, bleeding lips. "The boat. The *Marguerite*. She's shipping water. Somebody's got to bail. You always say somebody's got to bail."

My father's boat had been called the *Marguerite*. I had told Tam that exactly once, drunk, two years ago, and had assumed he'd forgotten, because I'd built my whole life on people forgetting the things I told them.

"Somebody's got to bail," he said again, and his eyes closed.

I sat there in the stove light with my hands hanging useless between my knees, and I did the thing I am worst at in this world, which is nothing.

* * *

Fever talks. Anybody who has sat a night with a sick man knows that fever talks, and it doesn't talk sense, and it doesn't talk kindly. It goes digging in the rooms of a person's life that they keep locked, and it throws the windows open, and everything comes out.

Tam talked about his mother, who had died when he was nine, in words so plain and small they went through me like splinters. He talked about the day I'd hired him, how he'd lied about his age and been sure I'd seen through it, and how I'd hired him anyway, and he'd walked home that night feeling—for the first time in his life, he said—like a man who belonged to something. He talked about Vhaloria, names from the ledger spilling out of him half-remembered, Maren Ostrova, custodian, custodian, custodian, like a bell ringing.

And near midnight, shivering under every blanket I owned, he said:

"You could've left me down there."

"I couldn't have."

"On the shelf. You could've taken the chest and gone on. Nobody would've known. Nobody would've even blamed you." His eyes found me in the dark, fever-bright. "Why didn't you?"

I thought about giving him the easy answer. The wry one. *Because you owe me a season's wages, boy, and I collect my debts.* Something with a grin in it, something that would let us both off the hook.

But sitting in a hole in a mountain with a dying boy asking you a true question has a way of stripping a man down to his timbers.

"Because," I said, and my voice came out rougher than I intended, "when I went over that edge, I wasn't thinking about the chest. Wasn't thinking about Vhaloria, or the treaty, or Brannic, or any of it. I was thinking there is a boy down that hole and I will go down that hole or I will stop being the man who is me." I fed a stick to the stove, mostly to have something to do with my hands. "I've spent thirty years keeping the world at rope's length, Tam. Boat, river, keep moving, don't stop anywhere too long, don't love anything that can sink. And it worked. It worked fine. Right up until a scrawny dock-rat lied about his age and signed on to my deck, and now—" I stopped. Started again. "Now there's a reason. Do you understand? There's a *reason*. Everything I do, I catch myself doing it for you. Picking the ridge for you. Digging this hole for you. Sitting up all night listening to you breathe, for you. A man can run from anything except being needed, and I let myself get needed, and God help me, I'm not sorry."

Tam was quiet a long moment. The stove ticked. Somewhere above us the wind changed its mind about something.

"That's the nicest thing anybody's ever said to me," he said finally, "and you managed to make it sound like a complaint."

"I'm a riverboat captain. We complain professionally."

He smiled, and then the fever took him under again, and I kept the vigil.

* * *

Toward dawn the rattle in his chest eased—not gone, but eased, the way a storm eases before it decides whether it means to come back. His breathing went shallow and even, and the terrible heat in him banked down to something a body might survive. I slept an hour with my back against his good side, one hand on the rope, because Ilsa Brandt had also said, *carry it even if you never use it,* and I had decided that using it once entitled me to superstition.

I woke to gray light at the cave mouth and Tam watching me.

"How long was I out?"

"Day and a night."

"The chest—"

"Is fine. The mules are fine. You are not fine, but you're going to live, which puts you ahead of my expectations."

He tried to sit up, winced, settled for propping himself on the good arm. His face was gaunt as a drowned man's, but his eyes had cleared, and looking into them I felt something in my chest unclench that had been clenched since the moment I heard his boots go out from under him.

"Brannic," he said. "They'll have found the mules' trail. They'll know we stopped."

"They'll know we stopped somewhere," I agreed. "Not where. This hole's invisible from ten feet away in fresh snow."

"And the pass?"

I crawled to the cave mouth and looked out. The storm had spent itself in the night; the sky above the saddle was torn open in places, showing hard clean blue, and the air had that washed, ringing quality it gets after snow. A window. Maybe a day of it, maybe less, and behind us, downslope, I could see tracks—or thought I could, or imagined I could—dark specks moving in the white of the long bowl below.

A window, and a boy who couldn't walk unaided, and a chest, and twenty miles of the worst ground on the mountain between us and the saddle.

"Captain?" Tam said.

I looked back at him, at that stubborn, fever-hollowed face, and I heard myself say the thing I'd sworn off saying, the arithmetic answer, the ridge answer, the answer of a man who believes in odds.

"We cross today," I said. "Both of us. The chest too. All the way."

And far below us, at the tree line, something glinted—metal catching the new sun, moving upslope.
