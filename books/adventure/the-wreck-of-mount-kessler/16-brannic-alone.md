## 16. Brannic Alone

Twenty-two years in the army taught me three things: how to sleep standing up, how to eat anything that doesn't fight back, and how to read a trail the way a priest reads scripture—looking for what's *not* there rather than what is.

The mountain was telling me plenty by omission.

No woodsmoke on the wind that morning, which meant either Gideon Vasquez had frozen to death overnight or he'd gotten smart about where he built his fires. No birdsong above the timberline, which meant nothing was moving that didn't have to. And no sign of the boy Tam at any of the camps I'd passed since dawn—which worried me more than it should have, because I'd told myself from the start this was a job and jobs don't come with worry attached.

I'd been lying to myself with real dedication for about six days now.

My warrant said: *recovery of stolen property, one sealed chest, contents classified under Crown authority.* My commission said the same thing in nicer handwriting. My boots said something else entirely—they'd been saying it since the trailhead, getting louder with every switchback—and what my boots said was: *Hale Brannic, you are chasing a man who pulled you out of the marshes at Corven's Ford while you were bleeding from places you didn't know could bleed, and you are doing it for money.*

Not even good money. Decent money. The kind of money that buys silence, not freedom.

I climbed.

* * *

You want to know how Gideon and I met? Everybody assumes it was the war proper, some grand set piece. It wasn't. It was a supply depot outside Corven's Ford, in the rain, and he caught me cheating at cards.

That's the truth of it. I had three kings palmed and he reached over, took my wrist, looked at my hand like a doctor looking at a wound, and said, "Sergeant, you cheat like an honest man pretending to be dishonest. It's painful to watch." Then he dealt me into the next hand properly and let me win back what I'd lost, minus a drink, and we'd been friends ever since. That's Gideon. He'll catch you red-handed and then make sure you don't lose everything because of it. There's a generosity in him that he'd deny under torture, and I mean that literally—I've seen him deny kindnesses under considerably less pressure than torture, just to avoid being thanked.

Which is why, when I found him at the ledge, I already knew how it would go. I just hadn't let myself know yet.

* * *

The ledge sat below the saddle like a shelf someone had forgotten to finish building. Wind-scoured rock, a natural overhang, and smoke—the first smoke in two days—threading up thin and gray from behind it.

I went up the way they teach you not to: loud. Boots on scree, hand on the rifle but not raising it. If Gideon wanted me dead I'd have been dead at the trailhead, or at the river town, or any of a dozen places where he'd had the drop on me and chosen, instead, to sit down and drink.

He heard me coming from fifty yards, of course. He always did. When I rounded the overhang he was sitting with his back against the stone, and across his knees—not pointed at me, but present, the way a sentence can be present without being finished—was his river pistol.

"Hale," he said.

"Gideon."

"You look terrible."

"You look worse," I said, and it was mostly true. He'd lost weight since the river. His face had gone lean and brown and hard as old rope, and there was a cut over one eyebrow that hadn't been there at the last town. But his eyes were steady, and steady eyes on Gideon Vasquez meant trouble for somebody.

Then I saw the boy.

Tam Osei was curled against the back wall of the overhang, wrapped in every blanket they owned, and he was burning. You can see fever from ten feet if you've spent enough winters around sick men—it's not the shivering, shivering's just cold, it's the stillness underneath, the way a fevered body goes quiet and wrong like a stove with too much coal in it. His face was flushed dark and his breathing came shallow and fast, and between breaths he made a small sound, half-delirious, that I will hear until I die.

Gideon shifted, just slightly, so that his body was between mine and the boy's. Not aggressive. Automatic. The way a man stands between a lamp and a draft.

"He fell," Gideon said. "Ice shelf, two days back. I got him out. He's been going downhill since."

"Have you—"

"There's nothing down here to have. Willow bark, snowmelt, prayer. In that order." He looked at me then, fully, and what was in his face wasn't defiance. It was something worse. It was *asking*. "Hale. I need a day. Maybe two. He can't be moved in this."

And there it was. The whole job, laid out on a rock shelf with a dying boy between us.

Because here's what I could have done. Here's what the warrant said I should do. Take the chest—he had it right there, wedged behind him like a hen on eggs. Secure the prisoner. Send word down-mountain; Corliss's second team was somewhere below us, and they had a surgeon, supplies, all of it. Trade the boy's life for the treaty and ride home a licensed hero with a bonus and a clean ledger of my own.

All I had to do was say yes.

"Gideon," I said, "give me the pages."

He blinked. "What?"

"The ledger. Not the treaty. The pages with the names." I held out my hand. "You want your day. I want to know what I'm carrying and for whom. Show me, and I'll give you until the weather turns. Hide him deeper if you have to. But I read it first. Alone."

For a long moment he didn't move. The wind came off the saddle and flattened the smoke and made the boy moan, and Gideon put a hand back through the blankets without looking, the way you'd touch a sleeping child, and I understood in that instant that everything I thought I knew about this chase had been wrong from the beginning. This wasn't a man protecting contraband. This was a man guarding the only door out of a burning house.

"You'll read it," Gideon said, "and then what?"

"Then I decide whose side I'm on. Same as you did."

Something moved in his face—not a smile, exactly. Recognition, maybe. He unslung the satchel, took out the ledger, and put it in my hands like a man handing over a live coal.

"Don't read it standing up," he said. "Sit down first. Trust me."

* * *

I sat down at the mouth of the overhang, where the light was best, and I read.

The first twenty pages were what I expected. A census ledger, beautifully kept—names, districts, trades, family lines. Vhalorian script in one column, trade-tongue in the other. A country's worth of people counted the way countries count themselves: proudly, precisely, believing the counting matters.

I'd served in the Vhalorian campaign. I want that understood. I was nineteen and I carried a rifle and I told myself stories the whole time—we were stabilizing a border, we were preventing a greater war, the treaty violations were real, the government was corrupt. Every soldier tells himself something. It's how we sleep.

Page thirty-one is where the names stopped matching the census format. Page thirty-one is where the handwriting changed—tighter, hurried, a different ink—and where each name acquired annotations. Dates. Locations. And beside nearly every entry, in that second hand, a single word.

*Removed.*

I read forty names before I understood what *removed* meant. I read sixty before I made myself say it inside my own head. By eighty I had stopped reading standing-sitting-lying and was simply holding the book the way you hold a wounded bird, very carefully, as though it might feel what you're thinking.

They weren't census records. They were a list of witnesses. Teachers, clerks, ferrymen, archivists—people who had handled documents, signed protests, kept copies, remembered borders. And the dates ran backward and forward through the year of the collapse like a tide chart of drownings. Somebody had used this ledger to hunt them. Somebody had gone down this list, district by district, and *removed* them.

And in the margin of page ninety-four, in the third handwriting—the one I recognized before I could stop myself recognizing it, because I'd seen it on a hundred requisition forms and one letter that Gideon keeps folded in his coat and pretends he doesn't carry—

*Evacuated east, 41 souls. M.O. has the rest. God forgive the instrument.*

His father. Vasquez's father had held this book. Had annotated it. Had smuggled forty-one people east under the nose of the occupation and written *God forgive the instrument* because he knew what the book had been used for before it came to him, and suspected what it would be used for after.

I sat there until my legs went numb.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic I did on that ledge, and I want it on the record that I did it honestly, because whatever else I am, I was a soldier, and soldiers do their sums.

Corliss pays me to recover a sealed chest, contents classified. Fine. Chests get recovered; classification is how empires digest. But Corliss's employers didn't send one team. They sent two, and the second team is equipped for a valley, not a courier—a valley means survivors, and survivors mean the ledger isn't a relic, it's a *threat*. Because a census of the dead, annotated with the method of their dying, in official hands, dated and initialed—that's not history. That's evidence. That's a rope with a government's neck already in the noose, waiting for someone to pull.

And the treaty—the thing I was actually sent for, the thing worth triple season's wages and a burned boat and a dead boy's fever as collateral—is the document that started it. The forgery. I didn't know it was a forgery when I took the commission. I know it now, because Gideon's father's letter was tucked inside it, and because no honest treaty gets carried in a lead-lined box by a man who writes *God forgive the instrument* in its margins.

They didn't send me to recover a document. They sent me to erase the last copy of the lie that killed a nation—and everyone who could prove it was a lie.

I closed the ledger.

Behind me, the boy breathed. Thin. Fast. Still there. Gideon watched me with the pistol across his knees and his whole life in his face, waiting to find out whether twenty years of friendship weighed more than a warrant, and I realized he'd been ready for either answer, and that readiness was the saddest thing I'd seen since Corven's Ford.

"Hale," he said quietly. "Whatever you do—do it fast. He hasn't got many slow ones left in him."

* * *

I stood up. My knees cracked like rifle fire, and neither of us laughed, which tells you what kind of morning it was.

"Second team's two days behind me," I said. "Maybe less. They've got a surgeon named Pell who owes me a favor from the border years, and I've got enough commission scrip left to make the favor expensive." I slung my pack off and dug out the paper packet—fever powder, real stuff, army-issue, the kind that costs more than a deckhand makes in a month. I threw it to Gideon and he caught it without looking away from me, which is a skill, and I've never decided if I admire it.

"That's not free," he said.

"No. It's bought." I picked up the ledger and held it out to him, and my hand was steadier than I deserved. "Here's the price. When the boy can walk, you take him over that saddle, and you give this book to whoever's name is still alive in it. All of them. Every living soul on these pages hears what happened to the rest. That's the debt. That's what the powder costs."

Gideon took the ledger slowly. "And the treaty?"

"I'm not taking the treaty." I turned toward the downslope, where the scree waited and the wind waited and two days' hard walking separated me from men I now intended to mislead, delay, or—if it came to it—stand in front of. "A warrant says the treaty comes back with me. So the treaty stays lost, and Sergeant Hale Brannic reports that the trail above the snowline took it." I looked back once. "You understand what I'm telling you, Gideon?"

"I understand you're about to commit treason for a fever powder and a promise."

"I'm about to do what you did at Corven's Ford," I said. "Catch a man red-handed, and then make sure he doesn't lose everything because of it."

He almost smiled. Almost. Behind him the boy stirred and murmured something that might have been a name, and Gideon's whole body turned toward the sound like a compass finding north, and I left them there—my oldest friend, a dying boy, and a book full of ghosts—while I walked down into the treeline to go lie to the empire.

The snow started an hour later. Early, heavy, wrong for the season.

By nightfall I understood what it meant: the pass would close within days, maybe hours. Whatever window Gideon had to get that boy over the saddle was closing like a door.

And somewhere below me, moving fast and angry because their tracker had found my false trail too easily, Corliss's second team was climbing.
