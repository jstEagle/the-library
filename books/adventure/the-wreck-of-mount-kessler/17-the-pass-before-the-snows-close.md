## 17. The Pass Before the Snows Close

Ilsa Brandt woke us before dawn by throwing a boot at the door.

Not knocking—throwing. A boot, hard-soled, forty years of mountain wear on it, and it hit the planking with a crack that put me upright with a knife in my hand before my eyes had finished opening. Tam came up slower, fever-bright and confused, asking if the roof had fallen in.

"Sky's falling instead," Ilsa said through the door. "Get out and look at it if you don't believe me."

I got out and looked at it.

The clouds that had been sitting on Kessler's shoulder for three days like a creditor who wouldn't leave had lifted. Not gone—stacked up to the west in a black wall that reached higher than the peak itself, boiling slow and patient, the way weather boils when it's decided what it's going to do and is only waiting on the schedule. But between us and that wall was a strip of clean sky, hard and blue and cold as a blade, and in that strip the saddle of the pass showed itself for the first time since we'd arrived at her hut: a pale notch in the ridge, maybe six hours' climb, maybe seven with a sick boy and a chest that weighed what regret weighs.

"One day," Ilsa said, standing beside me with her arms crossed against the cold. "Maybe less. That front's moving faster than it looks. When it comes over the ridge it'll come all at once, and it won't stop for a week. Maybe two. Maybe until spring, if the mountain's feeling spiteful."

"Then we walk today."

"You'll walk today." She looked at me sideways. "I've lived under this pass twenty years, Vasquez. I know what it does to people who argue with it."

"I'm not arguing. I'm agreeing with it while it's still in a mood to be agreed with."

She almost smiled. It was a narrow thing, worn at the edges like everything else she owned, but it was there. "The boy can make it?"

I looked back through the doorway. Tam had gotten himself upright against the wall of the hut, wrapped in every blanket Ilsa owned, and he was watching us with that expression he'd developed since the crevasse—part apology, part stubbornness, all of it saying *don't you dare leave me behind*. His fever had broken in the night. His color was still wrong. His legs would be another question entirely.

"Can you walk?" I asked him.

"I walked yesterday."

"You fell down four times yesterday."

"Three," he said. "The last one was a strategic lean."

Ilsa snorted. I went inside and started packing.

* * *

There's a thing the river teaches you that the mountain never will, and it took me most of the morning's climb to understand that I'd have to unlearn it. On the water, you read the current and you go with it. You let the river carry the weight. Up here, every ounce was mine to carry, and the mountain gave nothing back but altitude.

I carried the chest on my back, strapped tight. I carried Tam's pack on top of it. And for the first hour, when the trail was still trail and not just a suggestion of a line through the scree, I carried Tam too—half-carried, his arm over my shoulders, his boots finding the ground in a way that was mostly his idea and partly gravity's.

"You could leave the chest," he said, somewhere around the second hour, when we stopped to breathe. His breath came out in white flags that the wind tore away. "It's just paper now. The treaty's burned. You said it yourself."

"I said a lot of things in the last two weeks. Most of them were lies I told myself."

"The ledger's not a lie."

"No," I agreed. "The ledger's the only thing that isn't." I shifted the straps and looked up at the notch in the ridge, which had not gotten any closer, which is the mountain's oldest trick. "Maren Ostrova signed that book as custodian. Custodian means somebody's supposed to hand it over. I've been the wrong hands for three hundred miles, Tam. I'd like to stop."

He didn't argue. He was too tired to argue, which I counted as a mercy, and we climbed.

The trail, when it existed, was an old one—Vhalorian, Ilsa had said, cut by mule trains in the years when Vhaloria had been a country instead of a rumor. You could still see it in the cut stones of the switchbacks, in the iron rings set into the rock where ropes had once been tied. A whole nation's commerce had crossed this saddle once, wool and timber and salt, and now the only thing crossing it was a riverboat captain with a dead country's ledger on his back and its last hope wheezing behind him.

There was a sentence I didn't let myself think very often. This was not one of the times I succeeded.

* * *

By midday we were above the timberline, and the world had simplified itself down to rock and sky and wind. The black wall in the west had climbed visibly higher, eating the blue a finger's width at a time, and I found myself doing arithmetic I didn't like. Six hours to the saddle at our pace. The front would arrive in four, maybe five. The arithmetic didn't work. Arithmetic on mountains never does; it just tells you the shape of the trouble in advance.

Tam fell at the third hour. Not a strategic lean—a real fall, knees buckling on a stretch of broken scree, and I caught him by the collar and held him up and felt how little was left in him.

"Talk to me," I said.

"I'm talking."

"Say something true."

He looked at me with those fever-reddened eyes, and for a second I thought he was going to say something that would crack me open, and I braced for it. Instead he said: "I want to see the valley. Maren Ostrova's valley. I want to know it's real. All of it—the ledger, the names, the whole drowned country. I want to stand in the one place where it's still true."

"That's not a reason to keep walking. That's a reason to sit down."

"It's the best reason I've got."

So I crouched, and I put him on my back in front of the chest, which is a configuration I don't recommend to anyone, and I stood up with the boy and the book and the whole weight of two nations' unfinished business, and I climbed.

I want to be honest about what that was like, because honesty is the one luxury I can still afford. It was like drowning, except slower, and with better views. Every hundred steps I stopped. Every stop, I counted to thirty and started again. Somewhere in the fifth hour the wind came around out of the west and went from cold to mean, carrying the first scouts of the front—little stinging grains of snow, dry as salt—and I knew the window was closing faster than Ilsa's boot-throwing certainty had promised.

The saddle was close. I could see the notch, could see the cairns on the far side descending into cloud on the valley side, and between us and it lay the last traverse: a shelf of rock maybe four feet wide, angled outward, with a drop on the downhill side that went down long enough to develop opinions.

I stood at the edge of it with Tam on my back and did the arithmetic again. It still didn't work. It was never going to work. The only question left was whether the last two hours of it happened on this side of the saddle or the other.

"Captain," Tam said, close to my ear. His voice was thin. "If I fall off, don't—"

"Don't finish that sentence."

"—don't put the chest down to reach me. Promise me."

"I don't make promises I intend to break."

"You've broken every promise you ever made yourself. That's why you're here."

The boy had spent two weeks listening to me. I'd have preferred he'd spent them deaf.

I stepped onto the traverse.

* * *

Here is the thing about grace: it never arrives the way you've budgeted for it.

I was a third of the way across the shelf, wind shoving at my left shoulder like a customs man with a grudge, Tam's breath ragged against my neck, the drop yawning away below us with the patience of all geological things—when I saw the figure ahead.

My whole body did the arithmetic of violence before my mind caught up. A man, standing at the narrowest point of the shelf, planted like he owned it. Dark coat, snow dusting the shoulders. And my hand was already moving for the knife at my hip, because there was only one man in the world who would be standing on this shelf in front of me, and the last time I'd seen Hale Brannic he'd been at a ledge below the snowline with a warrant in his pocket and a crack running through his loyalty that I'd watched widen in real time.

"Hold," he called, over the wind. And then, when I didn't stop—because stopping on that shelf was a way of dying with extra steps—he shouted it louder: "*Hold, Gideon. I'm not here for that.*"

He wasn't holding a weapon. He was holding a rope.

He'd driven a piton into the rock at the narrow point—old Vhalorian iron, by the look of the ring he'd run his line through—and he stood braced against it with the rope paid out across the worst of the traverse, a handline stretched taut as a belief. He'd been here long enough to frost over. He'd been here long enough to freeze.

"You're holding the rope," I said, when I'd come close enough not to shout. It came out as an accusation, because I didn't have anything else to shape it into.

"Somebody has to." He looked past me at Tam, at the gray of the boy's face, and something in Brannic's jaw moved. "How is he?"

"Alive. Stubborn. The usual Osei standard."

"Put him on the line. Clip him through. I'll take his weight when he gets to me."

"You're arresting us on the other side, is that the arrangement?"

Brannic looked at me for a long moment. The wind pulled at his coat. Behind him, to the west, the black wall of the front had reached the ridge crest and was beginning to pour over it like ink into water, slow and total.

"I read your pages, Gideon," he said. "Up on the ledge. All of them. You let me read them and I sat there half the night with a lantern reading about a treaty that never happened, signed by men who never met, justifying an army that drowned a country." His voice didn't break. It did something worse—it went flat, the way a man's voice goes when he's already broken and has swept up after himself. "I've spent six years working for the people who did that. I didn't know. I told myself I didn't know, which is what I'll tell the tribunal too, and they won't believe me, and they'll be right not to."

"Hale—"

"Get across the shelf, Gideon." He held the rope out. "I'm not arresting anyone. I'm holding a rope. That's the whole of my jurisdiction today, and it's the first honest work I've done in six years, so don't you dare ruin it with a speech."

So I clipped Tam's harness to the line, and I clipped mine, and we went across.

It took ten minutes. It took a year. The wind found the gap in the ridge and came through it with the full authority of the storm behind it, and the snow stopped being grains and started being weather, and Tam's grip on my shoulders went from holding on to merely being held, and through all of it Brannic stood at the far anchor paying out the line, taking the boy's weight as he'd promised, hauling him the last few feet onto solid rock with hands that must have been past feeling.

I came off the traverse last. I got my boots onto the stone of the saddle proper—Vhalorian stone, cut and fitted by hands twenty years dead—and my knees informed me that they were done, and I informed them that they weren't, and we compromised.

Brannic crouched over Tam, checking him with the brisk economy of a man who'd patched worse in worse places. Then he stood, and we looked at each other across four feet of the highest ground either of us had ever argued on.

"The valley's a mile down, in the cloud," he said. "There's a cairn trail. You can't miss it if you don't overthink it." He pulled something from inside his coat and held it out. My knife—the one he'd taken off me at the ledge, when I was too busy keeping Tam breathing to object. "You'll want that. Corliss's second team came up the south approach two days behind me. They're slower, but they don't stop, and they don't read."

I took the knife. It felt strange in my hand, lighter than I remembered.

"What happens to you?" I asked.

Brannic glanced back the way he'd come—back down the shelf, back toward the warrant and the tribunal and the six years, back toward the storm now swallowing the ridge whole behind us. Snow had begun to land on his shoulders and stay.

"I'm going to stand here," he said, "and hold this rope until that team arrives. And then I'm going to tell them the pass is closed, and show them the weather, and if they don't believe me, I'm going to make them wish they had." He almost smiled. It was Hale Brannic's smile, the one from the war, from the card games and the bad whiskey and the night he'd pulled me out of the flooded bilge of the *Marrow* and called me a lucky idiot. "Go on, Gideon. Get the boy warm. Get the ledger home."

I stood there one second longer than I should have. Then I put Tam's arm over my shoulders, and we went down into the cloud, toward the cairns, toward the valley where a country that didn't exist was still keeping its names.

Behind us, the storm came over the saddle like a door closing.

And somewhere back along that closing door, a man was holding a rope open.
