## 18. Maren Ostrova

I had been expecting the chest for twenty years, which is a long time to expect anything, and I will tell you what twenty years of expecting does to a person: it makes them excellent at bread and terrible at hope.

My name is Maren Ostrova, and for the record—though there is no record anymore, that being rather the point of us—I was the third custodian of the Vhalorian archive. Not the keeper of the crown jewels or the state secrets; those burned or were stolen in the usual way nations die. The archive kept the small things. Parish registers. School rosters. A ferryman's logbook with coffee rings on every page. The proof that we had existed, which after the collapse turned out to be the only treasure worth guarding, because you cannot claim restitution for a people who officially never were.

So when the boy came stumbling down into our valley on the legs of a newborn foal, half-carried by a river captain with frost in his beard and a lead-lined chest strapped to his back like a penance, I did not weep. I want that noted. Everyone who tells this story afterward insisted I wept, because it makes a better scene, but I did not. I set down my bread paddle, looked at the chest, and said, "You're late."

The captain blinked at me. He had the look of a man who had been awake too long at altitude and had stopped trusting his own eyes somewhere around the snowline. "You know what this is," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I know what it is," I said. "I've known since I was nine years old and my mother taught me which names not to say out loud. What I don't know is who you are, why you have it, and how many men are dead between that river and my door. Come inside. All three of those answers can wait until you've had soup, but the soup cannot wait, because if I let it go cold I'll have wasted a morning and I refuse to add that to the day's losses."

That is how Gideon Vasquez entered Vhaloria—or what is left of it, which is forty-one households, two hundred and six souls, one school, one still, and me—and I tell it plainly because everything else about that day has since been embroidered past recognition. By spring they'll say he arrived with trumpets. He arrived with frostbite and a boy who needed lancing blisters, and he put the chest down on my table as carefully as a man sets down something that has been whispering to him for weeks.

* * *

The valley is called Serevan Hollow on no map at all, which suits us. You come over the Kessler saddle and descend through a fold in the mountains that hides you from both directions, and then the trees open and there it is: terraced fields going gold and grey with late autumn, smoke rising straight in the windless cold, and the long low building we call the House of Names, though the children call it the Paper Barn and I have stopped correcting them. Correction is a form of attachment, and attachment, our elders decided long ago, is a luxury we ration.

We are not hiding, exactly. Hiding implies someone is looking. For twenty years nobody has looked, because looking for Vhalorians would require admitting Vhalorians exist, and the nations that signed our dissolution have built twenty years of treaties and borders and solemn commemorations on the foundation of our nonexistence. We are not hidden. We are *unacknowledged*, which is a colder thing, and we have learned to live in it the way fish live in water: without thinking about it constantly, but never once forgetting.

I am fifty-three years old. I inherited the custody from an old man named Pell who inherited it from his aunt, and when I took the ledger's keeping I took an oath that consisted of one sentence: *Somebody must remember all of them.* Two hundred and six of us remember eleven thousand. That is the arithmetic of survival, and it does not balance, and we carry it anyway.

When Vasquez opened the chest on my table—I made him do it himself; I wanted to watch his hands while he did—I saw the ledger for the first time in twenty years, and I will confess now what I denied later: my knees went. Not visibly. But they went.

It is a plain book. That is what always undoes people who imagine archives as grand things. Calfskin gone soft at the corners, a spine repaired twice with waxed thread, pages ruled in a clerk's careful hand. Eleven thousand names, entered over decades, each with a parish, a trade, a date. Births, marriages, deaths, movements. The whole quiet machinery of a nation that never thought of itself as remarkable. My own name is in it, entered in my grandmother's time in her own slanted script: *Maren Ostrova, b. Serevan district, archivist's line.* She wrote "archivist's line" the way other families wrote "soldier's line" or "vintner's line." We were the family that remembered. Somebody must.

"You opened it," I said to Vasquez. Not accusing. Checking.

"Inside lid," he said. "There's a name written there."

"I know whose name."

He looked at me then, really looked, and I saw him decide something—the same decision, I'd wager, that had carried him over a mountain instead of selling the thing to the first man with clean boots and a fat purse. "Then you know my father didn't run," he said. "There was a letter. Folded in with the treaty. It says—"

"It says he tried to buy the fleet passage," I finished. "Twelve ships out of the southern harbors. He put up his own bond against it, and when the harbor commissioners refused to act without ministerial authority, and the ministers had already fled, he forged the authority himself. Yes. Captain Vasquez, I have spent twenty years knowing exactly what your father did, and I have spent those same twenty years unable to tell anyone, because the only person alive who could confirm it was supposed to be dead on a river two thousand miles from here."

* * *

Let me explain the treaty, because everyone wants the treaty explained, and because the explanation is the wound.

When the coalition dissolved Vhaloria—there were four signatories; their names are spoken here only in whispers, and I will not write them even now—they required a justification. Nations always do. No army crosses a border without first commissioning a document to prove the border deserved it. In our case the document was a treaty: a secret military pact, allegedly signed by the Vhalorian crown, allying us with a hostile power and granting that power basing rights along the coast. Aggression planned, sovereignty pre-sold, treachery documented in ink. The discovery of this treaty was announced with great gravity. Sermons were preached. Monuments were commissioned. And on the strength of that single folded page, a country of eleven thousand souls in the districts the ledger remembers was declared a threat to civilization and erased.

Here is what I learned from my predecessor Pell, who learned it from a dying clerk who had held the pen: the treaty was forged. Not by us. By *them*. Commissioned before the war it justified, drafted in the chancellery of the very power that claimed to have discovered it, complete with aged paper, false seals, and a signature copied from a captured Vhalorian naval attaché who was shot within the month so he could never dispute his own hand. The forgery was the casus belli. The destruction was the plan. The treaty was merely the receipt they wrote in advance.

And the letter in your father's hand—which he entrusted to the ledger's courier, a man named Doss, who got as far as the river below Mount Kessler before Corliss's predecessors caught him—was the counter-document. Your father had seen the original forgery in the chancellery files during his bond negotiations. He copied its flaws: the wrong watermark year, a seal impression taken from a stamp retired two years prior, ink chemistry that hadn't existed when the treaty was supposedly signed. His letter laid it out like a merchant auditing a bad invoice. It was, in its dry furious way, the most patriotic document Vhaloria ever produced, and it never reached us. It sat in a chest under a river while we lived twenty years as ghosts.

"Do you understand," I said to Vasquez, and my voice did something I did not authorize it to do, "what that paper is? If it surfaces—if any government, any court, any newspaper publishes it—it doesn't just clear our name. It invalidates every border drawn on our bones. Every land grant. Every indemnity paid to the signatories out of our confiscated treasury. Twenty years of their law, built on a lie, and here is the audit. They cannot permit it. Which is why men have died for it, and why more will, and why—" and here I stopped, because the next part was mine alone to say, and saying it aloud in front of strangers felt like handing them a knife.

Vasquez waited. He was good at waiting; you could see it was a professional habit, the patience of a man who reads water for a living.

"Why," I said finally, "some of us do not want it published either."

* * *

That evening the settlement council met in the Paper Barn, and I want it recorded that I argued badly, because I was arguing against myself.

There were eleven of us around the long table, lamplight and woodsmoke and the smell of the season's last apples in crates along the wall. Old Sorrel, who farms the east terraces and lost two sons in the collapse, wanted the treaty copied and carried out over the mountains in spring, delivered to every chancellery and press that would take it. "Truth is a debt collector," he said, pounding the table hard enough to rattle the lamps. "It comes eventually, and it takes payment with interest. Let them pay."

Young Devi—nineteen, sharp as a fresh blade, born in this valley and never having seen the sea our ledger is full of—wanted it burned immediately. "Every copy is a fuse," she said. "You don't store fuses next to your children."

And between them sat the rest, and the argument went round and round, and I listened to my people divide themselves into the two halves that have divided every survivor community since the beginning of loss: those who would spend anything for vindication, and those who would spend nothing more, having already paid everything.

Vasquez sat against the far wall with Tam asleep against his shoulder—he wouldn't take a bed until the boy was settled—and he said almost nothing, which I have since learned is how he says most things. Once, when Sorrel appealed to him directly—"You carried it. You bled for it. Tell them what it's worth"—he considered for a long moment.

"Worth killing for," he said. "Proven. Twice on my way here, maybe three times, depending on what happened to a sergeant behind me on that pass. I'm a river man. Where I come from, when something's worth killing for, we ask a different question." He looked at the chest. "Not what's it worth. Who pays?"

Nobody answered him, because the answer was obvious and nobody wanted to say it: whoever held the paper last. Truth, I have learned, is not a shield. It is a blade with no handle. Whoever grips it bleeds, and it cuts the gripper before it ever reaches the enemy.

They argued until the candles guttered, and they resolved nothing, because there was nothing to resolve—only a choice that had to be made whole or not at all. Council broke up near midnight, and I stood in the doorway watching breath-clouds rise off two hundred chimneys and thought: twenty years I have kept the names safe, and now the paper has come home, and the paper is worse than the names ever were, because the names only ask to be remembered and the treaty demands to be *used*.

* * *

Later—much later, when the settlement slept and the frost had silvered the windows—I found Vasquez awake in the Paper Barn, sitting where the lamplight could reach, looking at the ledger the way a man looks at a grave he dug himself.

"My father," he said, without preamble. "In your records. Is there anything? Anything at all."

I had prepared for this question for hours and found I still wasn't ready. I took down the second book—not the great ledger but the courier's register, the thin record of what had been sent out into the world and never returned. Doss's entry was there, dated the autumn of the collapse. And beneath it, in Pell's hand, a note added years later:

*Doss reported sighted on the lower Kessler river, alive, chest intact. Then nothing. Assumed lost with cargo.*

"Assumed," I said. "We assumed. It was easier than hoping." I closed the book. "Your father bonded his fleet, forged the authorization, handed the evidence to Doss, and stayed behind to hold the harbor office so the departure would look routine. There was no departure. The coalition sealed the coast that same week. He knew the odds. He sent the truth away anyway, because somebody must remember, and somebody must be able to prove it, and he understood—as I understand tonight, sitting here—that those are different jobs and sometimes they cost different lives."

Vasquez was quiet a long time. When he spoke, his voice was level in the way that costs the most. "He didn't betray them."

"He was the last loyal officer Vhaloria had," I said, "and history will never print his name, because history belongs to the people who won. Unless." And I looked at the ledger, and at the letter, and at the man sitting beside them, and I felt the old fury rise in me again—not the hot kind, which passes, but the cold kind, which furnishes a house you live in forever. "Unless someone makes them print it."

He shook his head slowly. "Ma'am, I burned nothing yet, but I'm telling you plainly: whatever gets decided about that treaty, I've seen what men do to get it. I won't hand anyone a weapon that size. Not for justice. Not for my father's name."

"No," I agreed. "Nor should you." And then, because honesty is a discipline I practice poorly but practice: "But understand what you're asking of us, Captain. If the treaty burns unpublished, we stay ghosts forever. Our valley stays off every map. The children grow up speaking a country that doesn't exist, and when they die, the ledger closes, and eleven thousand names become a private grief instead of a public truth. Burning it is safety. It is also surrender. Do not mistake which one it tastes like."

He met my eyes. "And if it's published?"

"War," I said simply. "Four governments humiliated, twenty years of legitimacy dissolved overnight, borders suddenly negotiable—and every one of them knowing exactly where two hundred and six of us sleep. Truth is a shield or a blade, Captain, and I have spent twenty years telling myself it was a shield because the alternative was admitting I've been keeping a sword in a barn full of children."

The lamp flickered. Outside, somewhere on the terraces, a dog started up at nothing and thought better of it.

"We'll decide tomorrow," I said. "Council, full session, every household represented. That is the Vhalorian way—we vote, even when voting is agony, especially then." I gathered the books back into the chest, and my hands were steadier than they had any right to be. "Get some sleep. You crossed a mountain for this. Whatever happens tomorrow, you've done the hardest part."

He stood, paused at the door, and said the thing I have thought about every day since.

"There's a man behind me on that mountain," he said. "Sergeant Brannic. He let us through the pass. He read enough of the letter to know what it was, and he opened the rope line anyway. If your council decides to burn the treaty, he's the one who'll pay for it—his employers don't forgive witnesses. And if you decide to publish it, he's the one who'll have to choose again, with soldiers on both sides this time." He pulled his coat on. "Either way, ma'am, that man's fate is tied to yours now, and he's a better man than the ones paying him, and I'd hate to see the valley's decision cost him everything."

"He made his choice at the rope line," I said.

"So did you," he said, "twenty years ago, when you took the oath. The question tomorrow isn't whether you keep the names. It's whether you can survive what the names are worth."

He left me alone with the lamp and the chest, and I sat there until the candle drowned in its own wax, doing the arithmetic I have done every night since: two hundred and six souls, eleven thousand names, one forged page, and a soldier freezing somewhere above the snowline who had chosen truth over wages and would soon learn what truth chooses back.

Near dawn, Tam Osei came running up from the watch post with his breath smoking and his face white, and the words he brought made the whole arithmetic moot.

Riders. Below the saddle. More than six. Moving fast.

Corliss had found the valley after all.
