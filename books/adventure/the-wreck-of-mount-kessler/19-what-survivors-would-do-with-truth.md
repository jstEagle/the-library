## 19. What Survivors Would Do With Truth

I have sat through a great many arguments in my life. I was custodian of an archive, after all, and archives are where nations go to argue with themselves in ink. But I had not sat through one like this in twenty years, because for twenty years there had been nothing left to argue about—only to remember, quietly, and plant turnips around.

Now there was a riverboat captain standing in our meeting hall with river mud still on his boots and a burned treaty's ashes somewhere on a mountain behind him, and forty-one Vhalorians who had spent two decades pretending to be nobody were suddenly remembering how to have opinions.

The hall was the old granary, which is what we call every building in this valley that has a roof and no other purpose. Someone had lit the iron stove. Someone else had set out the good cups, which told me everything about how seriously people intended to take the evening. In Vhaloria we had a saying about the good cups: *when they come down from the shelf, someone wants something.* My grandmother said it about suitors. It worked just as well for revolutionaries.

Gideon Vasquez stood near the door with his arms folded, wearing the expression of a man who has delivered a parcel and would very much like his receipt so he can leave. Beside him, the boy Tam sat on a flour barrel with his arm still bound from the mountain, watching everything with the hungry eyes of a sixteen-year-old who has recently learned that the world contains secrets worth having.

"Say it plainly," Davor Kellik was demanding. Davor had been a customs officer before the collapse, which meant he had spent his whole life enforcing rules and had never forgiven himself for it. He was the loudest voice for restitution, and he gestured now with a pipe he never lit, as if the pipe itself were a gavel. "The treaty was a forgery. The forgery justified the invasion. If we publish proof of the forgery, the coalition governments must answer for it. Reparations. Recognition. A seat at whatever table they've built since."

"And who will make them answer?" asked Petra Ilves, dryly, from her chair by the stove. Petra had been a surgeon. She had pulled shrapnel out of children during the burning of the capital and had come away from it with a permanent squint and zero tolerance for optimism. "The governments that signed the forged treaty? The ones that drew new maps over our graves? You'll send them a letter, Davor? With copies enclosed?"

"With copies enclosed," Davor said, "and witnesses, and affidavits, and the sworn testimony of the man who carried the original across Mount Kessler." He pointed the unlit pipe at Gideon. "You saw it. You read it. You can testify."

"I can," Gideon said.

The room leaned toward him the way rooms do. I watched him take the weight of forty-one people's hope without shifting his feet, which I confess impressed me. Most men stagger under less.

"But hear me," he went on, "because I'll only say this once. Paper in the wrong hands killed once already. That treaty didn't die on a mountain because I'm sentimental. It died because the moment it surfaces—anywhere, in any form—it stops being your history and becomes somebody's weapon. The same governments you want to shame will want it suppressed. The men who sent killers up the Kessler trail after me weren't historians. They wanted it back because it was useful, and it stopped being useful the second it burned. You don't get to publish a thing that four people died keeping off the market and pretend it's still yours afterward."

"It was never ours to hide," Davor shot back. "It was ours to use."

"There it is," Petra murmured, to no one. "There's the old country talking."

* * *

Let me tell you what the old country talked like, since I am apparently the only person in this valley old enough to remember it properly and young enough to still be annoyed by it.

Vhaloria ran on documents the way other countries run on armies. We were a small nation wedged between larger appetites, and we survived not by strength but by paper—treaties, charters, ledgers, receipts, a national archive so meticulous that foreign ministers used to joke we could sue God and win on the paperwork. We believed, sincerely, stupidly, magnificently, that truth kept safe was power kept safe. Then the coalition forged a treaty giving them pretext to cross our border, and we learned the other half of the lesson: truth in the wrong hands is a knife, and knives do not care whose hand holds them or what the document originally meant.

I was twenty-six when the archive burned. I carried out what I could—a crate of land grants, a box of family registers, and the ledger that eventually came down to me, the ledger of names, the one the captain's father had helped keep alive in its long exile. I have spent twenty years being custodian of a country that exists only in that crate. I know exactly what documents are worth. I also know exactly what they cost.

So when the argument turned, as I knew it would, to the question of sending copies out over the mountains with the captain—when Davor said, "You could carry them, Vasquez. You got the chest this far"—I stood up, because some debts require you to stand.

"No," I said.

The room looked at me. Maren Ostrova, custodian, signer of names, keeper of the good cups. I am not a woman people expect to interrupt. I have cultivated that surprise carefully.

"Maren—" Davor began.

"You want to send copies out into the world," I said, "carried by a man whom Corliss's people have already tried to kill twice, along a pass that closes with snow, into towns where anyone with coin can buy a bounty man's loyalty. And when those copies surface—and they will surface, Davor, paper always surfaces, that is its nature and its curse—who do you imagine suffers? Not the coalition ministers. They'll burn their own archives first. The schoolteacher in Rennick who taught Vhalorian letters in secret. The ferryman at Sorrel Ford who still says our word for morning. Every name in that ledger becomes a target the day the treaty becomes news. We were erased once by soldiers. Do not volunteer us for erasure again by lawyers."

"That's fear speaking," Davor said.

"Yes," I agreed pleasantly. "Fear speaks very well. It has had twenty years of practice. Fear is why we are all still alive to hold this meeting."

Petra made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a cough; with Petra it is impossible to say. Davor's jaw worked. And Gideon Vasquez looked at me across the granary with something that was not quite gratitude and not quite recognition but was adjacent to both—the look of a man discovering that the quiet woman agrees with him for reasons deeper than agreement.

* * *

We argued until the stove went cold. Of course we did. Forty-one survivors of a dead nation, given one evening and one impossible question: what do the living owe the truth?

Davor wanted restitution and would not be moved. He spoke of justice the way a starving man speaks of bread, and I could not hate him for it, though I could wish he'd stop pointing the pipe at people. Young Sef Aldric—nineteen, born in exile, our first generation raised entirely on stories rather than streets—wanted publication too, but for different reasons, purer and more dangerous ones. "If we stay hidden forever," he said, "then Vhaloria isn't a country. It's a rumor we tell ourselves. Let the world know we existed. Let them choke on it."

"And let them finish the job while choking?" Petra asked.

"Better than suffocating politely," Sef said, and half the room murmured, because he was nineteen and handsome and right in the way that nineteen-year-olds are right, which is to say completely and fatally.

Old Yeva Brandt—Ilsa's aunt, ninety-one years old, deaf in one ear and selective in the other—had the last word before midnight, as she always does, delivered in the flat tone of a woman who has buried everyone she ever argued with. "My mother's name is in that ledger," she said. "So is my daughter's. So is mine. I did not survive the burning of Vhaloria so that its death certificate could be framed and hung in a coalition ministry as evidence of their embarrassment. Keep the names. Burn the weapons. Bury me under a stone that says VHALORIA, and let the snow do the publishing."

Then she went to bed, which ended the session more effectively than any vote.

* * *

Afterward, in the blue hour past midnight, I found the captain outside, standing in the cold looking up at the pass. The storm had sealed it behind us two days ago—you could see the white line of it even by starlight, a scar of cloud snagged on the saddle like wool on wire. No one goes over that pass now until spring. Which meant that whatever we decided tonight, no copy of anything was leaving this valley for months regardless, a fact I suspect Gideon appreciated more than anyone.

"You could have stayed out of it," he said, when I joined him. "Custodian of the archive. Above the fray."

"The archive is a flour barrel and three crates in my cellar," I said. "I've been in the fray for twenty years. I've just been quiet about it."

He almost smiled. It was the first time I'd seen his face do anything but endure. "Your people want me to carry copies out in spring."

"My people want a great many things. It's what happens when you give people hope—they immediately start making demands with it. Terribly inefficient system. I recommend despair; much lower maintenance."

"What happens in spring?"

"In spring," I said, "we decide again. That is what survivors do, Captain. We don't resolve things. We defer them, season by season, until either the problem dies or we do. It's undignified, but it keeps the turnips planted." I studied him. He had the look of a man carrying something heavier than a chest, and I recognized it, because I'd worn it myself for two decades. "You burned the treaty at the summit. Why? Truly. Not the speech you gave Davor. The true reason."

He was quiet long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "My father's name was inside that lid. His signature. Twenty years I thought he abandoned me, or worse—that he sold out whatever cause took him. Turns out he was trying to save a country with a pen instead of a gun, and it killed him, and the proof of it was sitting in a box at the bottom of my river the whole time." He shook his head slowly. "That paper was the last thing holding him hostage. I wasn't going to let it hold anyone else."

I nodded. There was nothing useful to add, and I am old enough to know when silence is the correct document.

"They'll ask you again in spring," I warned him. "Sef Aldric will ask you. Davor will ask you. And they'll be persuasive, because grief makes excellent rhetoric."

"I know," Gideon said. "I've met grief. We're old friends. It doesn't get a vote."

* * *

But here is the thing I did not say to him, standing in the cold under the sealed pass, and it is the thing I have not said to anyone, and I find now, writing this in the ledger's margin where no one will look for it, that I must say it somewhere:

They are going to ask him anyway. Davor and Sef have already begun drafting the petition—Sef showed me the pages this morning, written in that beautiful careful Vhalorian script they teach in secret, asking for restitution, recognition, return of seized lands, the whole impossible shopping list of a resurrected nation. And they mean to present it to the valley council at the thaw, with or without the captain, with or without my blessing, and they mean to send it out with the first meltwater.

And I looked at those pages, and I felt the old sickness rise in me, the archive-burning sickness, the knowledge of exactly what a beautiful document in hopeful hands can cost—and I also felt something I have not permitted myself in twenty years, which was the temptation to let them try.

Because Yeva is ninety-one. Because Petra's hands shake now. Because I am fifty-three and the crates in my cellar are heavy and someday soon there will be no one left who remembers the smell of the archive, and a rumor we tell ourselves is not a country, whatever I said to the contrary in front of the stove.

Truth is a shield and truth is a blade, and I have spent my whole life deciding which, and I no longer know.

The snows are sealed over the pass. Spring is five months away. And the boy Tam tells me the captain means to walk back down the mountain alone when it opens—which means that when the valley votes, the one man who knows what that paper costs will already be gone.

I may have to change his mind about leaving. Custodians, after all, are permitted to acquire new holdings.
