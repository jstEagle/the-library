## 20. The Burning at the Summit

The dogs started barking an hour before dawn, and I knew before my boots hit the frozen ground that it wasn't wolves.

Wolves announce themselves. Wolves want you to know they're coming; it's half their strategy. These were the other kind of sounds—the clipped, disciplined kind, the kind men train into animals so the animals can do quietly what the men would rather not be seen doing. Three sharp bursts from the eastern picket, then silence, then Tam's voice cracking across the valley like a whip: "Gideon! East meadow! Boats on the water—no, sledges—"

I was already moving.

The valley beyond the pass is a thing I still don't have words for, and I've had twenty years to practice. A bowl of green tucked under the glacier's chin, terraced fields going gold with late barley, smoke rising from chimneys that don't appear on any map because there is no map. Two hundred souls, maybe more, living in a country that officially does not exist, farming land that officially belongs to nobody, speaking a language that officially died two decades ago. Maren Ostrova calls it Vhaloria. She says it without irony, without apology, the way you'd say your own mother's name. The first time I heard her say it, something in my chest that had been rusted shut since I was nine years old creaked open about a quarter inch.

Now men with rifles were coming up the glacier tongue to put it out again.

"They came over the saddle," Maren said, meeting me at the stockade gate with a lantern in one hand and a pistol in the other, which told me everything about how the last twenty years had gone for her. "Six of them, maybe eight. They took Brandt's cabin."

Ilsa Brandt. Eighty years old if she was a day, and the only reason we'd found the valley at all. My stomach did something ugly.

"Alive?"

"For now. They'll want her to talk. Old archivists know where things are hidden." Maren's face in the lantern light was carved out of something harder than wood. "They think she knows where the treaty is."

And here is the joke, the black, river-bottom joke of it all: the treaty was fifty feet behind her, wrapped in oilcloth inside a lead-lined chest, sitting in the meeting house like a loaded gun on a kitchen table. We'd carried it over the mountain through snow and blood and Brannic's unexpected grace, and now Corliss's second team had walked right up to it while we slept.

"Get it out of the meeting house," I said. "Now."

"It stays with the people, Vasquez. That was the agreement."

"The agreement was made before there were guns on the glacier." I grabbed her shoulder—not gently; I've never been gentle when the water's rising—and pointed east, where the first grey light was showing me what I'd feared: figures moving along the moraine in loose skirmish order, professional, unhurried. Men who knew exactly what they were looking for and exactly what they'd been paid to do about anyone standing between them and it. "That's not a raiding party. That's a retrieval team. They didn't come for the ledger. Ledgers are just paper to Corliss. They came for the treaty, because the treaty is the only document in the world that can either resurrect Vhaloria or bury it so deep no shovel ever finds it again."

Maren looked at me for a long three seconds. Then she turned and shouted something in Vhalorian, and the valley woke up the way rivers wake before a flood—all at once, everywhere, no single point you could point to.

* * *

What followed was not a battle. I want to be honest about that, because I've been in battles, and battles have lines and drums and some pretense of shape. This was two hundred farmers and fishermen who had spent twenty years practicing being invisible discovering that invisibility had failed, moving their children into the limestone caves above the terraces while eight professionals advanced on their homes. There was shooting—the settlement had old rifles, hunting pieces, and the men who held them knew every rock and fold of that valley the way I know a sandbar—but it was scattered, defensive, buying-time shooting. Nobody was trying to win. Everybody was trying to make the other side pay too much to stay.

Tam found me at the meeting house, breathless, cheeks bright with cold and fear in equal measure.

"They're at Brandt's cabin. Gideon, they dragged her out onto the porch, and there's a man—" He stopped, swallowed. "There's a man asking her questions and she's just laughing at him."

"That sounds like Ilsa." I threw back the oilcloth. The chest sat there, patient as a grave. "Help me with this."

"Gideon, the people—Maren said—"

"I heard what Maren said." I got my hands under the chest's lip. Lead-lined, river-heavy, the same weight it had been the day I pulled it out of the shallows below Mount Kessler, except now it weighed about four hundred years of dead country instead of forty pounds of metal. "Tam, listen to me, because I'm only going to say this once and then we're both going to be very busy. Everything those men want is in this box. Not the names—the names are safe, the names are just grief, and grief never made anybody rich. But this—" I lifted out the treaty, the folded forgery, the beautiful lie with signatures on it that had justified burning a nation off the map, "—this is worth more than the valley. This is worth more than everyone in it. And as long as it exists, there will always be another Corliss, and another after him, climbing over that pass."

Tam went pale in a way that had nothing to do with the cold. "You're going to burn it."

"I'm going to burn it somewhere it can't be argued with."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about carrying a thing to its funeral: the walk is longer than you planned.

The signal fire stood on the shoulder below the pass—a stone platform the Vhalorians kept stacked and dry, a beacon meant, I gathered, to warn the valley if anything ever came down the glacier. It hadn't been lit in living memory. It was maybe nine hundred vertical feet above the settlement, up a switchback trail cut into the scree, and the storm front that had given us our one-day window was already chewing at the western ridgeline, throwing out gusts like a card cheat testing his luck.

I ran anyway. Forty-eight years old, river-soft in the legs, running uphill with a forged treaty inside my coat and the sound of rifle fire coming up from the valley behind me. Somewhere around the fourth switchback my lungs filed a formal complaint and I ignored it, because below me I could see two of Corliss's men breaking away from the main group and starting up the trail after me, and because the chest—I'd left the chest with Maren, but the treaty rode against my ribs like a second heartbeat—because the treaty could not be allowed to fall into hands that were faster than mine.

Halfway up, the wind found me.

It came off the glacier sideways, the kind of wind that has opinions, and it put me down on the scree hard enough to ring my head like a bell. The treaty slid halfway out of my coat. I grabbed it, rolled, came up on one knee, and looked down the trail into the face of a young man with a service rifle and the particular empty expression of somebody doing a job he hasn't thought too hard about.

"Don't," he said.

"Son," I said, still on one knee, still holding the folded paper against my chest like a man protecting his heart from a knife, "do you know what that document is?"

He didn't answer. His eyes flicked past me, up the trail, checking his partner's position, and in that flicker I saw everything I needed: he was maybe twenty-two, he'd been told this was a recovery operation, and nobody had told him what he was recovering or why eight armed men had marched over a glacier to take a piece of paper from farmers.

"It's the reason your employers' bosses burned a country," I said. "It's a forgery. Every signature on it is fake. It was manufactured to justify a war, and the day it surfaces anywhere—in a court, in a newspaper, in a chancellor's desk drawer—it starts the same war again, except this time everybody knows it was a lie the first time, and lies that get caught kill more people than the original crime ever did." I stood up slowly, hands visible, the treaty pressed flat against me. "You can shoot me. I'd probably deserve it for something. But if you take that paper down the mountain, you're not finishing a job. You're lighting a fuse, and you'll be standing a lot closer to it than I will."

His rifle wavered. Behind him, farther down, his partner shouted something I couldn't hear over the wind.

Then the signal fire saved us both, though neither of us knew it yet—because Maren Ostrova, who understood documents better than any living soul, had reached the same conclusion I had, and she'd sent six of her people up the far side of the shoulder with torches, and the beacon caught with a roar like the whole valley exhaling.

Flame goes up thirty feet when you feed it pitch pine in a high wind. You can see it from the glacier. You can see it from the lowlands, if the passes are open. It says: *here*. It says: *we are here, and we are not hiding anymore*, or it says *come*, depending on who built it and why. The young man turned to look at it, and in the firelight his face did something complicated—young men's faces do that when the story they've been telling themselves runs out of pages—and I went past him up the trail at the best speed my legs had left, which wasn't much, but it was enough.

* * *

The stone platform was hot on one side and freezing on the other, and the wind wanted to put the fire out almost as badly as I wanted to feed it. I got the treaty out of my coat and stood there holding it over the flames, and I'll confess something, since honesty is the only inheritance my father left me that turned out to be worth anything: I hesitated.

Not because I doubted. Because it was the last piece of him.

Inside the lid of that chest, in ink gone brown as old blood, was Elias Vasquez, and the letter folded with the treaty was in his hand, and it said what it said—that he hadn't sold Vhaloria, that he'd tried to buy it another day, that he'd died—or worse than died, vanished—carrying the proof of the lie that destroyed it. Twenty years I'd believed my father was a traitor or a coward or a fool, and one letter had rewritten all of it, and now I was about to burn the only physical thing left in the world that connected me to him besides a name and a bad temper.

Below me, the valley burned in small ways—roof fires, muzzle flashes, the beacon roaring. Above me, the storm front came on like a debt collector. And somewhere on the trail, boots on scree, more than one pair.

"You'd understand," I said to the paper, to him, to nobody. "That's the hell of it. You'd be the first one to tell me to burn it."

Because that was the truth of Elias Vasquez, newly discovered: he hadn't died clutching the treaty. He'd been carrying it to someone who could use it to save people, and when carrying it stopped being possible, the plan had always been the same one I was standing on a mountaintop holding. Some things aren't evidence. Some things are weapons wearing evidence's clothes, and the only safe place for a weapon like that is nowhere.

I fed it into the fire.

It didn't go dramatically. Paper never does. It curled, browned, lifted a corner like a hand waving off an offer of help, and then the flame took the signatures first—of course it did, they were the freshest ink—and the body of the lie went up in a column of sparks that the wind tore apart and scattered over the glacier, over the pass, over the country that had died for it. I watched until there was nothing in my hands but heat.

Then I turned around, and the young man with the rifle was ten feet away, and his partner was beside him, and both of them were staring past me at the fire with the expressions of men watching a fortune combust.

"It's done," I said. "You can shoot the messenger if it helps. But the message just went up with the rest of it, and Corliss doesn't pay for ashes."

For a long moment nobody moved except the fire. Then the young man lowered his rifle—not all the way, but enough—and said, in the flat voice of a man deciding to be deaf later, "We didn't see which way you went."

They turned back down the trail. I stood there a while longer, letting the fire warm the side of me the wind couldn't reach, and I won't pretend I didn't say goodbye. A man should get to say goodbye.

* * *

By the time I got down to the valley, it was over—not won, nothing that clean, but *over*: Corliss's team had broken contact when the beacon went up, dragging their prisoners with them, including, God help us, Ilsa Brandt, who by all accounts had laughed at them the entire way off her own porch. Maren found me at the stockade gate, soot-faced, and read the answer off me before I said a word.

"It's gone?"

"Burned at the beacon. Ash on the glacier."

She closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were wet and furious and relieved, all three at once, which is a combination I hope never to see on a face I love again, though I suspect I will. "Twenty years," she said. "Twenty years we kept it, in case. In case justice needed it. In case restitution—" She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her sternum like she was keeping something in. "You did the right thing."

"I did the only thing. Those aren't always the same, but today they were."

"The ledger stays."

"The ledger stays." I put my hand on her shoulder, the way I'd grabbed it that morning, gentler now. "Names aren't weapons, Maren. Names are just the truth wearing its work clothes. Let them keep it."

She nodded, and then her face changed, sharpened, and she pointed west, toward the pass, toward the saddle where the storm front was now visibly devouring the ridgeline, swallowing the trail foot by foot like the river swallows a sandbar.

"There's a man coming down from the beacon," she said. "Alone. No rifle. He's been walking slow, like a man who wants to be sure of his welcome."

I looked. Grey figure against grey rock, deliberate, familiar even at that distance in the set of the shoulders.

Brannic. Coming down alone, unarmed, out of the closing snow, and behind him the pass was sealing itself like a book shutting, and whatever he'd decided up there on the ridge—whatever he'd read, whatever he'd chosen—he was bringing it down the mountain to me with the weather at his back and no way left for either of us to run.

"Well," I said, and handed Maren my coat, because the wind had teeth and the conversation ahead was going to be long. "Let's go find out which side of himself he brought."
