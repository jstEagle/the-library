## 21. Reckoning Below the Snowline

I have arrested men for less than what I did that morning, and I have let worse men walk because the paperwork suited them. That is the honest arithmetic of a recovery agent's career: you learn early that justice is a ledger too, and somebody upstream is always cooking the books.

The morning Corliss's second team came down into the valley, I was standing at the treeline with a warrant in my coat and a choice in my chest, and only one of them was going to survive the day.

* * *

Let me back up, because a man ought to account for himself properly before he burns his own house down.

I had been hunting Gideon Vasquez for eleven days by then. Licensed recovery agent, badge on a chain around my neck, papers signed by the Ministry of Reclamation—which is a fine, respectable name for an office whose whole business is making sure dead countries stay dead. Twenty years ago Vhaloria drowned—flooded, burned, struck from the maps—and twenty years ago the men who arranged it signed their names to a treaty justifying every drop of it. The treaty was forged. I didn't know that yet when I took the contract. What I knew was: sealed chest, river salvage, reward enough to retire on.

What I also knew, though I'd have denied it under oath, was that the man carrying that chest had once dragged me out of a canal at Sarrow Ford with two bullets in me and never once mentioned it afterward, not even when it would have gotten him promoted.

That's the trouble with old friends. They hold paper on you too.

I caught up with him twice. Once at the trailhead, where I offered him the clean deal—treaty to me, ledger his, walk away—and watched him read the soldiers I'd hidden in the pines like he used to read sandbars, all instinct and no chart. He ran. I let him get a hundred yards before I called the pursuit off the high route, telling myself the weather would do my work for me.

Then again on the ledge below Kessler Saddle, where I found him crouched over a feverish boy with snow to his waist and nothing left in him but stubbornness. And there—God help me—there he showed me pages. Not the treaty. The ledger. Names. Hundreds of names, written in a careful clerk's hand, and beside some of them, in fresher ink, little annotations: *alive*, *alive*, *dead*, *alive — north valley*. A schoolteacher. A ferryman. A woman signing as custodian.

And tucked among the treaty folds, a letter in another hand entirely. Vasquez's father's hand. I knew it from the war—every man in our company knew Captain Vasquez's hand, because he wrote the letters home for the ones who couldn't write, and he never charged them for it.

The letter said the treaty was false. Said the border incident that justified the invasion had been manufactured, that the signatures were bought, that the captain had evidence and meant to carry it out through the mountains. It was dated nine days before Vhaloria fell. Nine days. The man had been trying to save a country with a satchel of paper and a fast horse, and the country drowned anyway, and then the maps forgot it, and then the Ministry paid men like me to make sure nobody ever went digging.

I sat on that ledge reading by lamplight while Vasquez watched me with eyes like a man watching floodwater come up past his doorstep, and I understood three things at once.

One: my employers hadn't hired me to recover property. They'd hired me to bury evidence.

Two: if the treaty surfaced, it wouldn't resurrect Vhaloria—it would ignite a war, because half the signatories' heirs still held seats and borders, and nations do not apologize; they double down.

Three: I was going to lose either my career or myself, and I had maybe a day to decide which.

* * *

Corliss's second team came down out of the pass at first light, six men with mountain rifles and the particular confidence of people who believe they are the law because someone important told them so.

I met them at the mouth of the valley trail, alone, badge out, warrant in hand—the warrant I'd been carrying since the trailhead, the one authorizing seizure of "reclaimed state documents." Corliss wasn't with them. Corliss, I learned later, had come as far as the saddle and stopped, the way rats stop at the edge of a pantry. But his lieutenant, a narrow man named Fess with a voice like a file drawn across iron, knew exactly who I was.

"Agent Brannic." He said it like a man confirming a delivery. "Ministry's compliments. You're relieved. Contract's been reassigned."

"Reassigned." I looked at his six men, at the rope and pickets they were uncoiling—not climbing gear, restraint gear. "Those are arrest irons, Fess. Warrant says documents. Who are you arresting?"

He smiled the way a ledger balances. "Witnesses."

There was one settlement in that valley. Maybe sixty souls, counting children, counting an old woman named Brandt who'd once kept the Vhalorian archive and now kept goats. Witnesses. Sixty witnesses, and a pass about to close behind them until spring.

I want to be able to say I decided in that moment. The truth is less flattering. The truth is I'd decided on the ledge, at two in the morning, holding a dead man's letter in a living man's firelight, and everything since had just been me walking toward it slowly enough to pretend I still had options.

"Fess," I said, "how long have you worked for Corliss?"

"Three years."

"And in three years, has he ever once told you the whole of what a job was for?"

The smile flickered. Good. Doubt is a door; you only need it open a crack.

"The contract," I said, and pulled the letter from my coat—not the original; I'd copied it out in my own hand during the night, because I'm a bureaucrat down to the marrow and bureaucrats copy things—"is not a recovery contract. Read this. Then tell me whether you want your name in the same ledger as the men who forged that treaty."

He didn't take it. His men shifted. Somewhere above us, wind moved through the pass with a sound like a long inhale, and I thought: *snow's coming early. Everything after today happens fast.*

"You're relieved of duty, Brannic," Fess said, but quieter now, and quieter is how you know you've got a man.

"I am," I agreed. "As of this sentence." And I dropped my badge in the snow between us.

Twenty-two years I'd worn that badge. It made a small, unsatisfying sound going down—a tin click, swallowed instantly by white. All that weight, and no sound worth hearing. I'd have laughed if there'd been time.

Then I did the thing I'd rehearsed in the dark with the boy's fever-warm breath rasping behind me and the storm building over the saddle. I stood in front of six armed men on behalf of sixty hidden ones, and I testified.

Not grandly. There was no crowd, no tribunal, no dramatic raising of the right hand. Just a tired man in a bad coat reading aloud, in a cold wind, the words of a captain who'd died trying to stop a massacre: the forged incident, the purchased signatures, the names of the men who'd done the purchasing—names Fess would recognize, because two of them still had ministries named after their families.

I read until my voice went rough. I read the part where Captain Vasquez wrote, *If this reaches no one, let it at least be true somewhere that we were lied about.* And when I finished, the wind filled the silence, and Fess stood there with his jaw working like a man chewing on a bone he couldn't crack or swallow.

"Vasquez," one of his men said finally—young, red-faced, the kind who signs on for the uniform and finds out later the uniform has opinions. "The riverboat man? My uncle crewed the *Larkspur*. Said Vasquez pulled four men out of the water at Halden Bend when the boiler went. Never took a coin for it."

"That's the one," I said.

Fess looked at his men. Six men, and I could see the arithmetic running behind his eyes—reward against risk, obedience against the specific, corrosive discomfort of being asked to iron sixty children in a hidden valley on behalf of a lie. Men like these aren't evil. They're load-bearing. They hold up whatever roof they're standing under, and the only question anyone ever needs to ask them is: *whose roof?*

"We're turning back," Fess said at last, and it cost him visibly, like passing a kidney stone. "Storm's coming anyway. Nobody wants to die on a mountain for paperwork."

"For a lie," I corrected. "Say it properly, if you're going to say it."

He gave me a look I'll carry to whatever grave I get, half hatred and half something that might, in a better world, have become respect. Then they turned around, and I stood alone in the falling snow and listened to six men walk away from a fortune, and understood that I had just spent my career buying perhaps a season's grace.

Because Corliss was still up there. And Corliss did not turn back for weather.

* * *

He came down an hour later with two men I'd never seen—quiet professionals, the kind the Ministry denies employing—and he came not along the trail but straight down the scree, which told me he'd had eyes on the valley longer than any of us knew.

"Gideon," he said—I mean Vasquez; by then I'd found him again, or he'd found me, standing together at the valley mouth like a pair of old fence posts. Corliss nodded to me without surprise. "Sergeant. Or is it *mister* now? I heard something hit the snow earlier. Sounded expensive."

"Retirement," I said.

"Mm." He looked past us both, up the valley, at smoke rising thin and domestic from chimney fires he could not see but could certainly count. "Here's the shape of it, gentlemen, since we're all being honest in our various ways. The treaty comes back with me, or copies of it go to certain parties who will make this valley a matter of public record within the year. You've read it, Sergeant. You know what publication does. Borders move. Armies mobilize. And armies are not precise instruments—they are weather. This valley drowns the way Vhaloria drowned. I'm offering the gentler mathematics."

He was right. That was the obscene thing—he was *right*, in the narrow way that clever men are always right five minutes before they're catastrophically wrong. Publish the forgery and demand restitution, and you get tribunals, and you get reparations, and you get divisions marching through passes exactly like this one, and the survivors of one drowning get to watch the current rise again with their own testimony as the rainmaker.

Vasquez must have already settled it in his mind, because he wasn't arguing. He was looking up the slope toward the signal station on the shoulder below the pass—an old survey beacon, stacked wood, lit each autumn by trappers—and his face had gone quiet the way rivers go quiet over deep water.

"There's a third mathematics," Vasquez said.

Corliss raised an eyebrow. "Enlighten me."

"A country isn't its treaties. It's its names." He tapped his coat, where the ledger rode against his ribs. "The names go to the people they belong to. The weapon goes nowhere. No proof, no restitution—but no war either. Your employers keep their shame, and these people keep their lives. Everybody keeps exactly what they can carry."

"And the truth?"

"The truth," Vasquez said, "was never yours to sell."

What happened next happened quickly, and I will render it honestly even though honesty costs me here: I put myself between Corliss's professionals and Vasquez, and I did it with a sidearm I had no legal right to carry anymore, and for about forty seconds on a snowy trailhead three old liars stood very still and renegotiated the entire meaning of the word *duty*.

It ended without a shot. It ended because Corliss, for all his polish, was a servant, and servants understand ledgers better than they understand courage. Two agents dead or captured buys him nothing if the document itself is ash; a live agent's report, however damning, is just one more piece of paper the Ministry can sit on. He ran the numbers. I watched him run them—that flicker behind the eyes, the abacus clicking—and then he smiled his ledger-balancing smile and said, "You understand this changes nothing upstream. The men who signed that forgery are still alive, still seated, still hungry."

"They're old men," I said. "Hunger doesn't last forever. Paper does. That's why you're all so frightened of it."

He withdrew. Up the scree, the way he'd come, unhurried, empty-handed, leaving bootprints the snow was already filling in. I have wondered since whether he reported us as failed or as contained. I suspect *contained*. Bureaucracies prefer contained. It requires no follow-up questions.

* * *

The burning happened an hour later, up at the beacon, and I won't describe it here because it belongs to Vasquez's chapter, not mine. Suffice to say I stood watch at the tree line while a twenty-year-old lie went up in orange light against the coming storm, and I felt—there's no dignified word for it—*relief*. Not triumph. Relief, like setting down a pack you'd forgotten you were wearing.

The ledger stayed in the valley. Maren Ostrova received it with hands that shook and a face that didn't, and she thanked neither of us, which was correct. Custodians don't thank couriers. They log them.

* * *

Now the accounting, since I promised one.

I am writing this—I don't know why I'm writing this, except that a man who testifies ought to leave a record, and paper is the only witness that never recants—in the settlement's common room, on the far side of a pass that closed behind us yesterday afternoon with a sound like a slammed door. The storm came down the mountain two days early and buried the trail under twelve feet of new white. Nothing goes in or out until thaw. Corliss is on the wrong side of it, or the right side, depending on your sympathies, and whatever he reports will reach the Ministry sometime in late spring, and sometime after that a man will come looking for the sergeant who turned.

They'll find me here, or they won't. If they find me, I'll say what I said to Fess in the snow, at full volume, with documentation. It's the only skill I ever actually had—reading the record and telling the truth about it—and it turns out the skill transfers. Careers end. Testimony doesn't, not really. It sleeps in drawers and waits.

Costs, itemized: badge, pension, rank, reputation, the good graces of every ministry I ever drank with. Gains, itemized: sixty-odd lives, one ledger delivered, one lie reduced to smoke, and the company of a riverboat man who owes me nothing and keeps acting like he owes me everything, and a deckhand boy who looks at me now like I'm a story he hasn't finished deciding whether to believe.

Justice partial but real, as my old instructor used to say about every case he ever closed. Partial because the men who forged the treaty sleep warm tonight in a capital I'll likely never see again. Real because a woman named Ostrova opened a ledger this evening and read her people's names aloud, one by one, in a warm room, while snow sealed the doors of the world behind them—and every soul in that room answered *here*.

You cannot prosecute a nation into existing. But you can stand in a valley with the last of it and hear sixty voices answer *here*, and decide that this, too, is a verdict.

Tomorrow Vasquez means to start down before the melt, taking the boy, heading for the river. He asked me tonight, in the doorway, snow-light behind him, whether I was coming.

I told him I'd stay the winter. Somebody ought to be here in spring when the first official fool sticks his head over that pass—somebody with a badge-shaped hole in his coat and a great deal of free time and, as of this week, absolutely nothing left to lose.

He grinned at me the way he used to grin across a card table when he was holding trash and bluffing like a king, and he said, "Try not to arrest anybody, Sergeant."

"No promises," I said.

Outside, the snow kept falling on the pass, soft and total and final, and for the first time in twenty-two years, every road I owed anything to was closed—all but one, and it pointed downriver, and it would keep.
