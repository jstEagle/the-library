## 22. Downriver Again

The river didn't care that I'd changed. That was the first thing I noticed, standing on the landing at Harrow Bend with a carpetbag in one hand and nothing else to my name worth mentioning. The water ran the same brown-green it always had, carrying the same silt and the same secrets south, indifferent as a tax collector. Twenty years of running this river and I'd half believed it would notice my absence—would hold my boat's wake a little longer, would keep a channel open where the *Maren's Luck* used to draw her four feet of draft.

It hadn't. The river had already forgotten me.

I found that strangely comforting. A thing that forgets you can't hold anything over you.

Tam came down the gangplank ahead of me, carrying both our bags because he was twenty-two and still thought strength was a currency he could spend freely before inflation caught up with him. He'd filled out over the winter in the valley—mountain food and Maren Ostrova's stubborn hospitality will do that—and he walked like a man who'd seen the top of the world and expected the rest of it to make an effort.

"Captain," he said, setting the bags down on the planks. "That's her."

*Her* was the *Cormorant*, a stern-wheeler maybe fifteen years younger than my old boat, tied up taking on freight. Her master stood at the rail—a broad woman with sun-cracked hands and the permanent squint of someone who'd spent thirty years reading water instead of ledgers. Captain Odile Ferran, according to the bill of lading Tam had charmed out of her mate an hour earlier.

"You haggled yet?" I asked.

"I told her we'd work passage."

"You told her *we'd work passage.*"

"They need deckhands for the low-water run. I saw them loading light." He grinned at me, and it was his father's grin, or what I imagined his father's grin had been—I'd never known the man, but Tam carried it like an heirloom. "You said heading nowhere in particular. Nowhere in particular doesn't come cheap."

"It comes free if you own your own boat," I said, and then stopped, because that sentence had a hole in it where the *Maren's Luck* used to be, and Tam had the grace not to look at the hole. The widow Anselm was running her upriver by now, hauling seed grain and secondhand furniture, probably renaming her something sensible. *Provident*. *Charity*. Widows name boats like they name everything else—after the virtues they're still holding onto.

Captain Ferran came down to the landing herself, which told me she did her own arithmetic. She looked at Tam, then at me, then at my hands, which is how river people interview each other. Hands don't lie about rope work.

"Heard you used to run the Kessler stretch," she said.

"Used to."

"Low water's bad below the Narrows. Sandbars move every flood."

"They do," I agreed.

She waited. I let her wait, because there's no faster way to lose a negotiation than to seem eager, and because some part of me wanted to hear myself say it plainly, once, without a buyer or a bounty man or a burning treaty attached to it.

"I can read your bars for you," I said. "He can haul your freight. Passage south, wages when you have them, and we get off wherever we feel like getting off."

"Wherever you feel like," she repeated, flat.

"Nowhere in particular," Tam said helpfully.

Ferran looked at him the way you look at a dog that's brought you a dead bird—some admiration, mostly resignation. "Deck and pilothouse. You eat what we eat. First lie-down is under the cargo if we take water."

"Done," I said, and shook her hand, and that was that. No chest. No ledger. No name inked inside any lid but my own sea chest, and even that only mine now.

We went aboard.

* * *

The first week south was the easiest week I'd spent on the water in twenty years, which should tell you something about the other nineteen.

There's a particular peace in piloting a boat you don't own. Every creak of the hull belongs to somebody else. Every sandbar you dodge is a favor you're doing a stranger, not a debt you're paying yourself. I stood Ferran's watches, called her bars—*left side shoaling, give her the right, there's a snag under that willow line you'll want to honor*—and slept at night like a man who owed nobody an explanation.

Tam learned the *Cormorant*'s lines and made friends with her engineer, a laconic Kesseler named Dov who'd lost two fingers to a flywheel and talked about it the way other men talk about weather. I'd catch fragments of their conversations through the engine room hatch—Dov explaining valve timing, Tam asking questions in that hungry way of his—and I'd find myself smiling into the river wind like a fool.

One evening off the old Vhalorian trade reach, Tam climbed to the pilothouse uninvited, which he'd earned the right to do somewhere around the third set of rapids, and leaned on the rail beside me.

"You're different," he said.

"Older. It happens."

"That's not it." He watched the water slide past below us, brown and patient. "Last spring, you steered like the river was chasing you. Like every bend was a door closing behind you. Now you steer like—" He hunted for it. "Like you're going somewhere on purpose. Even though we're not going anywhere."

I thought about that. The wheel hummed under my palms. Somewhere below us, under forty feet of moving water, lay the gravel bar where I'd pulled a sealed chest out of the shallows and let a country I'd spent twenty years forgetting back into my life. The river had given it up; the mountains had taken it back. Fair trade, more or less.

"When I was young," I said, "my father told me the river forgives everything except stopping. Said a man who ties up too long starts to rot from the keel up. I believed him so hard I never tested it. Never stayed anywhere longer than a season. Never owned a chair I couldn't carry."

"And now?"

"Now I think he was describing himself, not the river." My father, who'd signed his name inside a lead-lined lid and gone east over the pass to save a nation and vanished doing it. Who I'd spent my whole life reading as a man who ran away, until a letter tucked in a forged treaty taught me he'd been running *toward* something the whole time. "Some men tie up because they're afraid. Some men stay moving because they are. Took me a burned treaty and a frozen mountain to tell the difference."

Tam nodded slowly, the way he did when he understood about six parts in ten and trusted the rest to arrive later. Then: "Harrow Bend's coming up. The one with the good pies."

"The one with the good pies," I confirmed.

"We could stop."

"We could."

"For a while. Not just pies."

I looked at him. He was watching the water, all studied casualness, ears slightly pink. Twenty-two years old and already learning to negotiate like a riverboat man—which is to say, pretending the thing you want most costs nothing at all.

"There's a chandlery there," I said. "And a ferryman who owes me a favor. And the pie shop has rooms above it."

"So we'd stop."

"For a while," I said. "Not forever. But for a while."

He grinned and went down to tell Dov, loudly, as though it were settled business, and I turned the wheel a point to starboard and felt something in my chest unknot that I hadn't known was knotted. Stopping. On purpose. Because I chose to, not because the snows closed the pass or the law closed the town.

My father would've been appalled. That was the best recommendation it had.

* * *

Harrow Bend smelled the way it always smelled in late spring—cut grass, tar, frying onions, and the green rot of the flooded timber downstream. We tied up at the public landing at midafternoon, and Captain Ferran gave us leave until her freight cleared customs, which meant three days, which meant, knowing Tam, two and a half.

I did the things a man does in a river town. I bought a new oilskin, because the old one had been through a war, a winter, and a rockslide, and had earned its retirement. I drank one beer at the Landing House with Dov and listened to him complain about Ferran's boiler in the affectionate tone engineers reserve for boilers they intend to fix. I visited the ferryman, Oskar Brandt—Ilsa's cousin, as it turned out, which is the kind of coincidence rivers specialize in—and paid off a debt from another life with a bottle of plum brandy and news of his cousin, alive and well and still keeping her archive in the high valley, still refusing to say more than three sentences at a time.

Oskar took the brandy, took the news, and asked the question everyone in that country eventually asks anyone coming down from Kessler: "Is it true? What they say's up there?"

"It's true," I said. "They're up there. They're planting barley."

He stared at me a long moment, and I watched him decide—not to believe, exactly, but to *carry* it, the way river people carry things they can't yet put down. "Barley," he said finally.

"Good barley, from what I'm told. I'm no judge."

He laughed then, short and startled, and clapped my shoulder hard enough to mean something, and I walked back toward the pie shop feeling lighter than a man with my history had any right to feel.

That was when I saw the notice.

It was nailed to the customs board outside the Landing House, among the freight tariffs and the reward posters for horse thieves, and I might have walked past it entirely except that it was printed on good paper, and good paper means money, and money means somebody wants something badly.

**RECOVERY NOTICE**, it read. *Articles of historical significance, lost or misappropriated in transit, Kessler district. Substantial reward. Inquire Corliss & Associates, Meridian House, Southbank.*

No description of the articles. No crest. Just that soft, expensive word—*misappropriated*—like the treaty had wandered off on its own, like it hadn't been fed into a signal fire at ten thousand feet while its owners' descendants watched it curl and go to ash.

Corliss & Associates. So the man himself had survived the winter, and Brannic's testimony had cost him the contract but not the firm. I stood there reading it twice, three times, waiting for the old cold to climb my spine—the cold that used to arrive whenever the past got too close, the cold that had kept me moving for twenty years.

It didn't come. What came instead was something almost like amusement.

*Misappropriated.* Let them post their notices till the paper rots. There was no treaty. There was no proof, no restitution claim, no weapon—only a ledger of names living quietly in a valley full of barley, and a handful of people scattered along this river who knew why, and would carry that knowledge the way you carry a coal in cupped hands: carefully, and not for long, and passing it on before it burned you.

A boy's voice at my elbow: "You want me to tear it down?"

Tam had appeared the way he did, silent as a cat in deck shoes, and he was looking at the notice with an expression I recognized from mirrors—old anger, banked but lit.

"No," I said.

"No?"

"Notices burn. Paper remembers being torn—it leaves the nails behind, and the nails ask questions." I tapped the board once. "Besides. It's honest work for whoever posted it, chasing a ghost. Better than most."

"You're not worried."

It wasn't quite a question, but I answered it anyway. "Ask me that a year ago, I'd have been on the next boat south before sundown." I looked at the notice one last time—at the fine paper, the careful type, the whole apparatus of men who believed that everything lost could be recovered if you paid enough—and I found that I pitied them, a little, the way you pity a man fishing a river that's already given up its fish. "Now? They're a year too late and a mountain too low. Come on. The pies close at seven, and I'm told the cherry ones go first."

* * *

We stayed nine days.

I know because Tam counted, and because Oskar's wife cornered me on day six and asked, with the directness of a woman who runs a pie shop above her own premises, whether we intended to winter there, because if so she had a room opening up in October and she wasn't offering it twice.

Nine days. Longer than I'd stayed anywhere since I was a boy in a house with my father's maps on the walls. And here's the thing I keep turning over, the thing I suspect I'll turn over for the rest of whatever years the river grants me: it didn't hurt. Stopping didn't cost. The sky didn't fall, the past didn't rise up out of the water, the man from Meridian House didn't appear on the landing with his soft voice and his triple wages. The world, it turns out, mostly leaves alone a man who isn't running from it.

On the fourth day I bought a chair.

It sounds like nothing. It was nothing—a plain pine chair from the carpenter by the millrace, three coins, done. But I sat in it that evening on the pie shop's little balcony with my feet on the rail, watching the evening traffic work the channel—Ferran's *Cormorant* already two days south, a log raft wallowing down from the pineries, a customs launch making its rounds with the bored diligence of all officialdom—and I understood that I had never in my adult life owned a piece of furniture I couldn't carry, and that this was the whole of my father's lesson, arrived twenty years late and upside down.

He'd been wrong about the river. Or rather, he'd been right about the river and wrong about himself, and had handed me his fear like a pocket watch, and I'd wound it faithfully every morning of my life until a mountain broke the mainspring.

The river forgives everything. Including stopping. Especially stopping. It's the only institution I know with no memory and infinite patience, and I had spent my prime treating it like a creditor.

"You're grinning at the chair," Tam said, dropping into the doorway with two plates of cherry pie.

"I'm grinning at the chair."

"One chair. What happens when you own two?"

"Then I suppose I own a household," I said, "God help me," and took my plate, and we ate pie on the balcony while the bats came out over the water and the landing lamps bloomed one by one down the bank, and neither of us said anything for a long time, because there was nothing that needed saying, which after everything—the fire, the ice, the crevasse, the saddle, the burning—struck me as the rarest luxury afloat or ashore.

* * *

We signed back aboard the *Cormorant* on the tenth morning, because Ferran was loading for the delta run and the delta in June is a thing worth seeing once, and because a man who owns a chair needs somewhere to hang his hat between visits to it. Oskar's wife pressed a wrapped pie on Tam and told him, in front of witnesses, that the October room offer stood, and that he should bring the captain, meaning me, and that she'd want the story of the mountain in exchange, the true one, and Tam promised it before I could stop him.

So we'll be back. That's the shape of it now. Not fleeing to, not fleeing from—coming back. To a chair, a room, a woman who makes better cherry pie than the river deserves. I don't know what I'll do through a whole winter in one town. I've never tried. But I've read water my whole life, and I know a channel that's opening when I see one, and I know better now than to fight the current just because standing still frightens me.

The morning we cast off, I stood at the *Cormorant*'s stern rail and watched Harrow Bend shrink astern—the pie shop, the customs board with its expensive useless notice, the pine chair visible through the balcony window if you knew where to look, and I knew exactly where to look. Tam was forward, coiling lines with Dov, laughing at something. The river ran brown and green and endless, the same river that had given up a sealed chest to a tired captain on a gravel bar below a white mountain, the same river that would run the same way long after every name in the ledger and every man who hunted them had gone to silt.

It hadn't changed. Not one bend of it.

But the man at the wheel had, and for the first time in twenty years, Gideon Vasquez was steering nowhere in particular, on purpose, and the water ahead looked—for the first time I could remember—less like a debt than like a road.

Somewhere south, past the Narrows, past the delta, the season was turning. And I found I was in no hurry at all.
