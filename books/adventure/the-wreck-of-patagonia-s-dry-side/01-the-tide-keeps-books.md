## 1. The Tide Keeps Books

The wind came off the Atlantic the way it always did in Puerto Deseado — sideways, salted, and without apology. It rattled the loose pane in the window of Voss's rented room above the closed chipería, and it carried with it the smell of kelp and diesel and old fish, the smell of a town that had stopped pretending to be going anywhere.

Edmund Voss sat at the table by that window with a ledger of her own open before her, because even disgraced guides keep books, and hers told a simple story: eleven months of red ink, a debt to the fuel dock, and enough money left for three more weeks of sardines and bread before the landlord's patience ran out along with everything else.

She was thirty-four years old and her name was worth nothing on this coast. That was the arithmetic no ledger could hold. Ten years she'd built a reputation in the cordillera — glacier crossings, winter traverses, the kind of routes other guides pointed at and said *she did that first*. Then the Ferreyra expedition went wrong above Lago San Martín, two clients dead in a serac fall that every inquiry since had decided was her fault, though the ice had its own opinion and the ice wasn't talking. The association struck her from the rolls. The outfitters stopped answering her calls. Her father, when he was alive, used to say that on the dry side of Patagonia a man's name was the only rope he could trust, and hers had been cut clean through.

So now she guided tourists to see penguins. Rockhopper colonies out at Isla Pingüino, gentoos up the Ría Deseado, forty dollars a head if the weather cooperated, which in September it rarely did. She knew the estuary better than anyone living — every channel that shifted with the storms, every reef that showed its teeth at low water — and she spent her days pointing at birds for honeymooners who asked if the penguins were real.

The knock came at seven in the evening, which was itself an event. Nobody knocked on Voss's door after dark except creditors, and the creditors had learned there was nothing to take.

The woman on the landing was seventy if she was a day, dressed in a wool coat that cost more than Voss's truck, with a driver waiting below in a black 4x4 that had no business on these streets. Silver hair pinned back with the severity of a woman who had never once been talked into anything. She looked at Voss the way one looks at a horse being considered for purchase.

"Señora Voss." Not a question. "I am Inés Aldao."

Voss knew the name the way everyone on the coast knew it. The Aldao estancia lay sixty miles south, two hundred thousand hectares of sheep and scrub between the road and the sea, a family so old their name was on charts older than the nation. Old money, Patagonia-style: land taken before there were laws to take it from, held ever since by a patience that outlasted governments.

"I know who you are," Voss said. "I don't know why you're here."

"May I come in? The wind is making an argument and I am too old to win it."

Voss stepped aside. The room was small and honest about it: bed, stove, table, chart of the ría thumbtacked to the wall, a coil of rope in the corner that she still touched some mornings like a rosary. Inés Aldao took in all of it without comment, which was worse than commenting, and sat where Voss indicated, spine straight as a mast.

"You were recommended to me," the old woman said. "Not for your reputation, which I understand is complicated. For your knowledge. There is a stretch of coast on my land where the tide does something unusual. Once or twice a year — at the extreme ebbs, the spring lows around the new moon — the water withdraws far enough to expose a cave in the cliffs below the old point. My late husband saw it once, as a boy. His father wrote about it." She paused, and something moved behind her eyes that might have been grief or might have been arithmetic. "There is reason to believe something is inside it. Something from a ship. I want it recovered properly, before the sea takes it back. I am told you are the only guide within three hundred miles who reads tides the way my husband read horses."

"How much?" Voss said, because she had learned that the rich respected directness and she couldn't afford not to be respected.

Inés named a figure. Voss kept her face still through long practice, but inside her chest something turned over like an engine catching. It was four years of penguin tours. It was the fuel dock paid off with interest. It was a winter without counting sardines.

"For how long?"

"A month. Preparation, then the expedition itself. You would lead a small team — my grandson, an archaeologist we've engaged, and a man who handles logistics. Room and board at the estancia. Full authority over matters of safety and tide. That last condition is mine to offer and yours to insist upon; I'm told you're the type."

"I am the type," Voss said. "What's inside the cave?"

"That," said Inés Aldao, "is what we are paying you to find out."

It was a reasonable answer and Voss didn't believe a word of it. Rich families didn't fund expeditions into mysteries; they funded expeditions into confirmations. Somewhere behind those grey eyes was a thing she already suspected or already knew, and the money was not for discovery but for control of whatever came up out of the dark. Voss had grown up poor among the wealthy-adjacent, guiding for estancia owners who treated the coast as a private wing of their houses. She knew how they breathed.

And none of it mattered, because the number was the number, and pride was a luxury she'd sold somewhere around the second month of eating sardines.

"I'll need to see the journals," she said. "Whatever the grandfather wrote about the cave. Tides, approaches, dates of observation. And I'll want a day surveying the coastline before anyone talks about descent. If the window is what you say — a few hours at most, once or twice in a season — then preparation is everything. People die in caves because someone guessed about the water."

"My husband's father guessed about nothing," Inés said, and for the first time something cold and precise entered her voice, like a blade being set down on a table. "He recorded. Dates, heights, times. Sixty years of observations in his hand. You will have access under supervision."

*Under supervision.* Of course. Voss nodded slowly and looked past the old woman at the darkening window, at the wind worrying the loose pane, at the black water of the harbour just visible beyond the rooftops where the tide was doing what it always did, coming in or going out, keeping its accounts.

One thing she had learned in ten years of mountains and one bad year of ruin: the sea keeps books too. Every wreck, every drowned man, every cargo that slipped beneath the surface — the water writes it all down in its own ledger and waits, patient as any creditor, for the account to come due. She had a sudden, unaccountable image of her grandfather — dead before she was born, a deckhand out of Comodoro whose ship never made port in '12 — and shook it off. Coincidence. Half the coast had someone the sea had taken.

"When do I start?" she said.

"Thursday. A car will collect you at dawn." Inés Aldao rose, buttoned her coat with two economical movements, and paused at the door. For a moment the severity cracked, just slightly, the way ice cracks before it goes. "Señora Voss. Whatever you find down there — my family has waited a long time for it. I would ask you to remember whose interests you were engaged to serve."

"I was engaged," Voss said, "to keep everyone breathing. That's the only interest I serve."

The old woman studied her for a long moment, the way one studies weather coming in over open water. Then she inclined her head, almost a bow, and went down the stairs, and the black truck pulled away into the wind.

Voss stood at the window a while after, watching the harbour lights smear in the wet glass. Below, the tide was turning, beginning its long pull out toward the open Atlantic, and she thought about the figure Inés had named, and about the word *supervision*, and about a grandfather whose grave had never held anything but a date.

She got out her own ledger and wrote, in careful figures, the contract amount against eleven months of red ink. The numbers balanced for the first time in a year. It should have felt like relief.

It felt like signing on to a crew whose ship she hadn't seen yet.

Outside, the wind picked up, and far down the coast, under sixty miles of darkening water, something in a cliff waited for the moon to pull the sea away from its door.

* * *

Thursday dawned hard and clear, the kind of light that makes the whole dry side look newly minted. The car came at first grey, and Voss rode south with her kit in the back — ropes, harness, tide tables copied out in her own hand, a crowbar she'd bought with the last of her cash and told herself was a tool and not an omen.

Sixty miles of gravel and guanaco country unrolled past the windows. The estancia appeared the way the Aldao fortune must have been built: gradually, then all at once. Windbreaks of cypress, a chapel, shearing sheds, and finally the big house itself, low and long and white against the brown hills, facing the sea like a man facing an old adversary across a table.

On the steps, waiting, stood three figures: a young man with his grandmother's bones and none of her patience, fidgeting like the ground was electrified; a woman in field clothes with sharp eyes and a canvas satchel, who looked at Voss with the frank assessment of a professional sizing up another professional; and a heavyset man with a broken-nosed face who smiled without engaging anything above the mouth.

"Tomás Aldao," said the young man, shaking hands too quickly. "This is Camila Reyes, archaeology, Universidad de Buenos Aires. And Bruno Sartori — he handles things."

"Logistics," said the heavyset man pleasantly.

Voss looked at the three of them, and then past them at the house, and then south along the coast toward the invisible point where the sea kept its secret door. The wind came up off the water, steady and salted, carrying the smell of kelp and distance.

Somewhere down there, at the bottom of an ebb that came once a season, was a chest, or a wreck, or a grave — and a family that had waited a hundred years to decide what the world would be allowed to know about it.

"Well," Voss said, shouldering her pack. "Show me your tides."
