## 2. A Family of Anchors

The estancia appeared out of the steppe the way a ship appears out of fog — first the windbreak of poplars, absurd and vertical against a horizon that had never learned to hold anything taller than grass, then the red tile roofs, then the long white façade with its iron balconies, all of it facing east toward the Atlantic as if the house itself were waiting for something to come ashore.

Edmund Voss rode in from Puerto Deseado in the back of a hired Land Cruiser, her duffel on the seat beside her, her climbing rack coiled at her feet like a sleeping animal. The driver, a silent man with a crucifix swinging from the rearview mirror, had not spoken in ninety minutes. That was normal for Patagonia. What was not normal was the money that had been wired into her account before she'd even signed anything — half up front, which no one did, not for a guide with her reputation, unless they wanted her badly or wanted her bound.

She watched the fences tick past, wire and weathered posts marching in straight lines across country that resented every straight line ever drawn on it. Sheep, thin as opinions, scattered when the truck passed. Somewhere out there, forty miles south along the coast, a cave sat under the sea like a held breath, waiting for the tide to drop low enough to show what it kept.

The journals had been described to her in Inés Aldao's letter: *my grandfather's account of the wreck of the Santa Eulalia, 1912, and his belief that survivors sheltered in a cavern below Punta Barrosa.* Survivors. Gold. A cave that opened perhaps four days a year, and only when the moon pulled hardest. Voss had read the letter eleven times on the bus down from Comodoro Rivadavia, looking for the lie in it, and had found nothing except the fact that rich families did not spend this kind of money on sentiment.

The truck crunched to a stop on white shell gravel. Doors opened somewhere ahead, and the wind came through them carrying woodsmoke and eucalyptus and, beneath it, the salt smell of the sea she couldn't yet see.

* * *

Inés Aldao received her in a room built to make visitors feel small in a way that could be mistaken for awe. It was two stories tall, paneled in dark coihue wood, hung with paintings of ships — ships in storms, ships becalmed, ships burning on a horizon — and at its center stood a table of black granite on which lay a single object under glass: a ship's chronometer, stopped at some hour Voss couldn't read from the door.

"Señora Voss." Inés rose from a wingback chair by the fire. She was small, straight-backed, seventy if she was a day, dressed in black wool as though the whole coast were already in mourning. Her handshake was dry and brief and strong. "You came."

"You paid," Voss said. "That's usually how it works."

Something flickered at the corner of the old woman's mouth — approval, maybe, or the memory of it. "My grandson will show you the journals after dinner. Tonight you eat, you sleep, you look at my coast. Tomorrow we talk terms properly." She turned back to the fire, dismissing her, and then, without turning around: "You have your father's name."

Voss stood very still. "I don't know what my father has to do with this."

"Nothing," Inés said pleasantly. "Nothing at all. Dinner is at eight."

* * *

Dinner was lamb roasted over coals, potatoes in fat, wine so dark it looked like it had been decanted from the night itself. There were five places set. Voss counted them twice.

Tomás Aldao arrived late, apologizing before he'd fully entered the room, a man of perhaps thirty-five assembled from expensive parts that didn't quite fit together — good jacket, bad posture, hands that went to his wineglass the way other men's hands went to cigarettes. He shook her hand too long and looked at everything except her face.

"The guide," he said. "Excellent. Excellent. Camila said you were coming today, and here you are." He laughed at nothing. "It's extraordinary country, isn't it? People think it's empty. It's not empty. It's just — patient."

"It's honest," Voss said. "Country like this tells you exactly what it'll do to you."

Tomás laughed again, higher this time, and drank.

Camila Reyes was already seated, and Voss marked her immediately as the only person at the table who hadn't been born into this house or bought by it. Mid-forties, sun-cured, forearms scarred the way divers' forearms get scarred — rope, rock, rust. She ate with an efficiency that suggested field rations were her native cuisine, and she looked at Voss with the frank professional assessment of one specialist reading another.

"Marine archaeologist," she said, offering her hand across the table. "Camila Reyes. Universidad Nacional de la Patagonia. You're the climber."

"I'm the guide."

"Climber and guide. I read about Cochrane." She let that sit. The Cochrane route, the storm, the client who died, the inquiry that had stripped Voss's certification and put her name on every outfitter's blacklist between Ushuaia and Santiago. "The report said the anchor point failed."

"The report said what the insurance company needed it to say."

Camila nodded slowly, and there was something in the nod that wasn't judgment. "Then you know what it costs when the official story and the true story don't match."

Across the table, Tomás's glass stopped halfway to his mouth.

The fifth chair stayed empty until the coffee, when the door opened without a knock and a man came in who moved like a man who had spent his life being the last thing other people saw. He was broad, gray at the temples, wearing a canvas jacket that had been expensive once and practical ever since. He didn't introduce himself. He poured his own coffee, took the empty chair, and looked at Voss with pale eyes that weighed her the way a butcher weighs meat he intends to keep.

"Bruno Sartori," Inés said from the head of the table, though Voss suspected the man had never needed introducing in his life. "He manages the estancia. He'll manage the expedition's logistics. Anything you need — boats, fuel, permits, discretion — Bruno provides."

"Discretion," Voss repeated.

"Everything on this coast requires discretion," Sartori said. His voice was mild, almost kind. "The weather. The tides. The family." He smiled, and it reached his eyes without warming them. "You'll find me reasonable, Señora Voss. I'm reasonable with everyone who gives me nothing to be unreasonable about."

Under the table, Voss loosened the knife hand she hadn't realized she'd tensed.

* * *

After dinner, Tomás led her down a corridor lined with photographs — generations of Aldaos standing in front of ships, in front of sheep, in front of this house, each generation more formally dressed and less certainly alive behind the eyes — to a room at the east end with a single heavy door and, Voss noted, a new lock where an old one should have been.

Tomás unlocked it with a key on a chain around his neck, tucked inside his shirt, and Voss understood that the boy slept with the key against his skin, and that this told her more about the family than any journal would.

The room inside was a library, or the skeleton of one. Empty shelves mostly, dust shadows where books had been removed, and in the center, on a table under a lamp, three leather volumes and a tin document box, arranged with the care of relics.

"My great-grandfather's," Tomás said. His voice changed here — steadied, oddly, as if the dead man's handwriting gave him a spine his own life hadn't. "Ernesto Aldao. He kept these from 1909 until his death. The 1912 volume is—" He stopped, swallowed. "You should read it yourself."

Voss put on the cotton gloves Camila handed her without either of them discussing it, and opened the middle volume.

The handwriting was small, precise, unhurried — a man writing because writing was how he thought. Weather entries. Stock counts. And then, dated the third of August, 1912:

*Wreck reported on the Barrosa reefs during the gale. The Santa Eulalia, out of Hamburg, broke apart within sight of the house. We rode out at dawn.*

Voss turned pages. The prose tightened as it went, sentences shortening like a man walking faster.

*Found eleven alive among the rocks. Took them to the cave at Punta Barrosa — the old smugglers' entrance — to shelter from the wind while we fetched carts. The sea was making up from the southeast.*

*Could not return. The swell held four days. When we got back in, the cave was empty of the living.*

There was a gap of several pages, and when the handwriting resumed it was smaller, harder, pressed deep into the paper as though the pen might break through and escape.

*What was in the cave was not ours to take. But what was in the strongbox was owed to no one living. I have made arrangements.*

And then, three days later, a single line, almost casual, which was the most terrible thing in the book:

*The gold is safe. The men are at peace. God sees the difference, even if Deseado does not.*

Voss closed the volume. The lamp hummed. Outside, the wind had come around and was working at the east windows with long patient fingers, the way water works at stone.

"'At peace,'" she said.

"That's how he wrote it." Tomás was staring at the table, not at her. "Eleven men. Eleven survivors, and my family has been — we've been—"

"Wealthy," Camila said quietly, from the doorway. Voss hadn't heard her come back. "For three generations. On money that washed in with the tide."

Tomás flinched as if struck. "We are trying to *find* them. To recover them. To give them burial, names, dignity — that's why you're both here. That's the entire purpose of this expedition." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and frightened and Voss believed every word he said, which was somehow worse than disbelieving him. "Isn't it?"

Neither woman answered fast enough, and the silence answered for them.

Sartori appeared behind Camila in the corridor, filling the doorway the way weather fills a window. "Señora Voss," he said. "Your room is ready. You'll want to sleep. Low tide comes Thursday, and the sea doesn't wait for anyone — not for Aldaos, not for archaeologists." His gaze rested on her a moment longer than courtesy required. "Not for guides, either. Whatever else they might be."

Voss walked back down the corridor of dead Aldaos with the ghost of her grandfather's name sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone. She had never met Ernesto Aldao. She had never known the name of the cave, or the reef, or the year. But her grandfather had gone to sea out of Puerto Deseado in 1912, and had never come home, and the family story — the one her father had died still asking questions about — was that the sea had simply taken him.

Now she knew the sea had help.

In her room, she unpacked her rack, checked every carabiner twice, and lay listening to the wind count the hours down to Thursday. Somewhere in the house, a lock turned. Somewhere closer, footsteps paused outside her door, considered, and moved on.

Thursday, the tide would fall. And whatever waited in that cave, it had been waiting a hundred years for someone to come down who wasn't an Aldao.
