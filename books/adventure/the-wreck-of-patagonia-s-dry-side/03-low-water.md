## 3. Low Water

The tide began to leave at four in the morning, and by seven the sea had pulled back far enough to show them the mouth of the cave.

Voss stood on the black basalt shelf with her binoculars and watched the water drain away like something being poured out of a jug. It went in long, muscular surges, each wave retreating a little farther than the last, until a channel opened between the outer rocks and the headland — a channel that would not exist again for another fortnight. Beyond it, half-hidden in spray and shadow, was the opening: a tall arch of darker stone, maybe three meters high, its edges worn smooth as the inside of a throat.

She had seen it once before, eleven years ago, from a fishing boat in a storm. The old men of Puerto Deseado called it *la boca del ahogado* — the drowned man's mouth — and they did not fish within a mile of it. She had thought then that it was superstition dressed up as seamanship. Now she wasn't so sure.

"Is that it?" Tomás Aldao stood beside her, too close, his breath quick and shallow. He had not slept well; she'd heard him pacing the estancia's stone corridors past two in the morning. "That's what my great-grandfather wrote about?"

"That's it," Voss said. "And we have maybe five hours before it closes."

Inés had come down to the cliff top to see them off, wrapped in wool despite the wind, standing apart from the others like a figure on a monument. She did not wish them luck. She looked at Voss for a long moment and said only: "Bring back what belongs to this family." Then she turned and walked back toward the Land Rovers, and Bruno Sartori held the door for her without meeting anyone's eyes.

*What belongs to this family.* Voss turned the phrase over as she led them down the goat track to the waterline. It was an odd thing for a matriarch to say about a cave that had never, in any record she could find, belonged to anyone.

* * *

The crossing was easier than it looked from above. The channel was knee-deep at its center, running cold over rounded stones, and Voss took them across in single file with their packs held high — Camila Reyes first, because the archaeologist moved like someone who had spent her life on wet rock, sure-footed and silent; then Tomás, who slipped twice and swore in a whisper both times; then Bruno, carrying more rope than any sane expedition needed; then Voss herself, last, watching the sea behind them the way you watch a dog you don't trust.

Inside, the cave opened up beyond all promise of its entrance.

Her headlamp found a vaulted ceiling hung with pale curtains of calcite, forty feet overhead, dripping in slow metronome. The floor sloped gently upward away from the water, floored with fine grey sand that held no footprints — or none that the tide hadn't erased. The air smelled of salt and iodine and something older underneath, something mineral and dead, like the inside of a sealed room.

"God," Camila breathed. Her lamp swept the walls. "Look at the stratification. This place has been dry at low tide for centuries. Centuries, Edmund. There could be intact deposits down here that—"

"Save it for the report," Bruno said mildly. He had taken up a position near the entrance, facing outward, and he did not move from it.

They followed the slope up. Fifty meters in, the sand gave way to shingle, and the shingle gave way to the longboat.

It lay on its side, keel toward them, half-buried where it had been dragged — dragged, Voss realized, not washed; there were skid-marks fossilized in the hardened sand beneath the hull, parallel grooves running back toward the waterline. The wood had gone silver-black with age, but the construction was unmistakable: clinker-built, oar-ports along the gunwale, a boat built to carry twelve men off a sinking ship and through surf that should have killed them all.

"The *Santa Elena*," Tomás whispered. He had gone very still. "1912. My great-grandfather's ship. He wrote that the longboat made shore here."

"He wrote that the longboat made shore," Voss said quietly, "and that's all he wrote about it?"

Tomás didn't answer. His lamp was fixed on the boat as if it might move.

Camila was already circling it, photographing, her movements professional and fast. "Dragged uphill. By survivors. They came ashore alive, Edmund. They got the boat above the tideline." She crouched at the stern post and went quiet. When she spoke again her voice had changed. "There's a marking here. A date. And initials."

Voss leaned in. Scratched into the blackened wood in a careful hand: *E.V. — 14 Mayo 1912.*

Her grandfather's initials. Her grandfather's date. She had grown up on that date the way other children grew up on birthdays — the day Eduardo Voss's ship went down off the Patagonian coast, the day the family's story became a wound passed down like an heirloom. Eleven men in a longboat. No record of what happened after.

"Edmund?" Camila said softly.

"I'm fine." She wasn't, quite. Something cold had begun moving in her chest, patient as the tide.

Beyond the longboat, the cave narrowed and then opened again into a second chamber, and there the lamps found what none of them had prepared for.

Bones.

Not scattered. Arranged.

They lay in rows on a ledge of clean stone above the high-water line — skulls at one end, long bones laid parallel beside them, ribs stacked with a precision that could only be deliberate. Twelve positions. Eleven occupied. Someone had carried these men here, or their remains, and laid them out with care, with order, with something close to tenderness. Small stones marked each skull like pillows. At the head of the row, a flat slab of slate bore a single line scratched deep:

*AQUÍ ESPERAN.*

Here they wait.

Camila made a sound low in her throat and put her hand over her mouth. Even Bruno had come forward now, drawn out of his sentry post, and stood looking at the bones with an expression Voss couldn't read.

"They were buried," Tomás said. His voice had gone thin. "Someone buried them properly. That's — that's good, isn't it? That means someone cared for them."

"Someone cared for them," Voss agreed, "or someone wanted them accounted for." She knelt by the slate. The scratching was fresh-cut when it was made, but the hand was steady, unhurried. You didn't carve like that in panic. You carved like that when you had time — when whatever was going to happen to these men had already happened, and there was nothing left but bookkeeping.

Bookkeeping. The word arrived in her mind uninvited and stayed.

It was Camila who found the chest. It sat in an alcove behind the bone ledge, half-hidden by fallen stone: an iron strongbox, riveted, its lock green with a century of salt corrosion. Stenciled on the lid, still legible under the lamplight: *S.A. ALDAO Y CIA. — PUERTO DESEADO.*

Tomás made a noise like a man being struck.

"That's ours," he said. "That's company property. That's—" He stopped himself, but too late, and Voss watched the color drain out of his face as he heard what he'd just claimed: ownership of a strongbox sitting behind the arranged bones of eleven drowned men.

"It's evidence," Camila said quietly.

"It's *ours.*"

"Tomás." Voss kept her voice level. "We document it. We don't open it here — the lock needs tools we don't have, and we're not breaking into anything on your great-grandfather's behalf without your grandmother's say-so. We mark the location, we come back with proper equipment on the next spring tide, and we do this correctly. Agreed?"

He stared at her. For a moment she thought he might argue, might demand they force it here and now, and she braced for the ugliness of it. But what crossed his face instead was fear — naked, animal fear, of the box, of the bones, of the whole dark chamber — and he nodded quickly, twice, like a man agreeing to anything to be allowed to leave.

Which was when Voss looked at her watch, and felt the dread arrive fully formed.

* * *

The tide had turned ninety minutes ago.

She knew it the way she knew weather in her teeth, a pressure change behind the eyes, and when she got back to the entrance chamber and looked out through the arch, the channel they'd waded across was already a river. Grey-green water ran fast between the rocks, flecked white, and beyond it the swell was marching back in long disciplined lines, each one closer than the last.

"We're leaving. Now."

Bruno was already coiling rope. "I checked it twenty minutes ago," he said. "It was slow."

"Tides lie." She was moving as she talked, counting heads, checking packs. "The ebb runs out slow and comes back fast — everyone knows that and everyone forgets it. Camila, front. Tomás, behind her. Bruno, you take the rear with me. Nobody stops. If you go down, you get up. Move."

The channel had been knee-deep going out. Coming back it was thigh-deep and accelerating, the current grabbing at their legs with hands of cold iron. Voss clipped a line between them — Bruno's idea, thrown without discussion, and she was grateful — and they went in together, leaning against the flow, the sea slamming cold against their chests. Halfway across a swell broke over the outer bar and hit them waist-high, and Tomás went down with a strangled shout, and the line snapped taut, and Voss hauled him up by main strength while Bruno anchored them both against the drag.

"On your feet," she shouted into his ear. "The sea doesn't care about your name. Walk."

Somehow he walked. They all walked, staggering, gasping, and came out the far side onto the basalt shelf soaked to the collarbones, and behind them the channel was gone — erased, a boiling white seam where dry stone had been. In twenty minutes the arch itself would be underwater. In thirty, the cave would be sealed, holding its bones and its box and its carved slate in the dark, waiting for the next extreme ebb to breathe again.

Tomás sat down hard on the rock and vomited seawater. Camila crouched beside him, saying nothing, looking back at the drowning cave with an expression Voss recognized and did not yet understand.

Then she understood it.

Because Camila Reyes, kneeling in the spray, drenched and shaking, was smiling. Not relief. Not hysteria. Something colder and more certain than either — the smile of a woman who had seen exactly what she had come to see, and who had, in the last hour, photographed a great deal more than stratification.

Voss looked at her. Camila looked back, and let the smile fade deliberately, and said nothing at all.

Above them, on the cliff top, Inés Aldao stood watching the sea reclaim the cave, and even at this distance Voss could see that the old woman was not watching the water.

She was watching them.
