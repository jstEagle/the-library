## 5. The Price of Silence

The offer came at dusk, when the light on the estancia's whitewashed walls turned the color of old bone.

Voss had spent the hour after supper on the veranda, mending a frayed rope coil by feel more than sight, watching the Atlantic go from pewter to ink beyond the fence line. The wind had dropped for once — that rare, held-breath stillness the coast offered maybe twice a season — and in it she could hear everything: the horses shifting in their paddock, the far-off complaint of oystercatchers, and now the deliberate click of Inés Aldao's cane on the boards behind her.

She didn't turn around. You learned that much working these coasts. Let people arrive at you.

"Señora Underhill." The old woman lowered herself into the chair opposite with the care of someone who had stopped pretending her body was an ally years ago. "You mend rope like a sailor."

"My father taught me. He said a guide who can't fix her own gear deserves whatever happens to her."

"A hard philosophy."

"A cheap one. It costs nothing to believe it until the day it saves your life."

Inés smiled — a small, dry movement, like paper folding. She wore black as always, high-collared even indoors, silver at her throat and wrists that caught the last light and gave none of it back. On her lap sat a leather folio, and Voss understood before it was opened what kind of evening this was going to be. She had been summoned to enough tables in enough estancias to know the difference between a conversation and a transaction. This was the second.

"You were down there," Inés said. "In the cave. Before Tomás panicked and you pulled my family out like drowning kittens."

"The tide did most of the work. I just read it."

"Humility. From a mountain guide." The old woman's eyes were pale gray, almost colorless, and they did not blink often. "Camila Reyes says there is a chest. Iron, sealed, colonial hinges. She says it may contain documents relevant to the *Santa Elena*, lost with all hands in June of 1912."

"She says a lot of things. She's an archaeologist. It's her job to say things."

"And what do you say?"

Voss set down the rope coil. Her hands wanted something to do and she denied them. "I say the chest stays shut until we have proper conditions, proper equipment, and a conservator who knows what salt air does to ninety-year-old paper. I say we come back next spring tide with a plan instead of a panic. That's what I'd tell any client."

"That is not what I asked."

"No," Voss agreed quietly. "It isn't."

The wind returned then, or seemed to — a breath off the water carrying salt and kelp-rot, stirring the jasmine along the veranda rail. Somewhere past the paddocks a dog began barking and thought better of it.

Inés opened the folio. Inside was a single sheet, typed, with a figure at the bottom that made Voss's chest tighten in a way she refused to show on her face. It was more money than she had earned in the three years since the Aconcagua inquest. More than she'd earned in the five before that, honestly, if she counted what the blacklist had cost her — the contracts that went quiet when she called, the agencies whose phones developed sudden faults, the colleagues who met her eyes a half-second too long before looking away.

"It would be a simple report," Inés said. "A professional assessment. You are the expedition's safety officer; no one will question your judgment. The cave is structurally unsound. The tidal window is too narrow for safe recovery. The chest, in your expert opinion, is likely corroded through, its contents destroyed by seawater. There is nothing of value. There never was." She slid the page across the boards between them. "Sign it, and the number becomes yours. Sign it, and there will be other numbers after it. My family does not forget its friends, Señora Underhill. Ask anyone in this province."

*Ask anyone in this province.* Voss looked at the figure and thought about the phrase, and about how many people in this province had been asked, over the years, exactly what.

"And if I don't sign it?"

"Then we part ways at the end of the contract, with mutual respect, and the assessment is written by someone else." Inés folded her hands atop the cane. "I am offering you a choice, which is more than life offers most people. I am not threatening you. I am seventy-four years old; threats are for people with time."

But her voice had gone flat as the horizon, and Voss heard underneath it the thing the voice was built to cover: that the choice was real only in the way a door is real when one side of it is already burning.

She didn't touch the paper. "Why?" she asked. "Why does it matter so much whether an iron box has paper in it? If it's nothing — if it's just some salvage manifest, some shipping ledger — why pay this?"

For the first time, Inés Aldao blinked slowly, like a lizard in cold sun. "Because my grandfather wrote those journals you've all been reading under supervision," she said. "And because a woman who reads journals for a living learns what men leave out of them. Good night, Señora Underhill. Consider it. Not for long — the spring tide won't wait, and neither shall I."

The cane clicked away across the boards, into the dark of the house, and Voss sat alone with the page and the sound of the sea coming back in.

* * *

Bruno Sartori found her an hour later at the equipment shed, where she'd gone ostensibly to inventory the climbing rack and actually because the shed had one small window facing east and no line of sight to the main house.

He didn't knock. He never knocked. He came in smelling of cigarette smoke and engine grease, closed the door behind him with exaggerated gentleness, and leaned against it — not blocking it, Voss noted, but standing near it, the way he always stood near exits. A man who had made a profession of knowing where doors were.

"You saw the old lady," he said.

"I saw the old lady."

"And?"

"And nothing. I haven't decided."

Bruno grunted. He took out a cigarette, looked at the shed's dry timber walls, and put it away unlit — a small courtesy that somehow unsettled her more than the offer had. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a face like a shovel and small careful hands, and in two weeks on the estancia Voss had catalogued him the way she catalogued weather: capable, observant, loyal to whoever was paying this month, and dangerous mostly in the way a riptide is dangerous — not malicious, just indifferent to what it takes.

"I'm going to tell you something," he said, "and then I'm going to leave, and tomorrow neither of us remembers it happened."

"Bruno—"

"There was a guide," he said. "Before you. Nineteen eighty-nine, ninety. Name of Ferrer. Worked out of Río Gallegos. The Aldaos hired him for a survey — different coast, same business. He wrote his report honest. Said there were things down there worth studying. Bones, artifacts, papers." Bruno's small hands laced together, unlaced. "Six months later he had a new contract in Mendoza. Big money, prestige route. The mountain took him. Avalanche, they said. Experienced man, stable snow, no warning. They said a lot of things."

The shed was very quiet. Outside, the wind worried the tin roof.

"People die in mountains," Voss said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her.

"They do. And sometimes they die in rivers, and sometimes their trucks go off the road to Comodoro, and sometimes their boats are found swamped with the drain plug out." Bruno shrugged, a slow rolling motion like the sea deciding something. "I'm not saying anyone pushed Ferrer. I'm saying the Aldaos have employed fixers for sixty years, and fixers don't get paid to write letters. And I'm saying that when the old lady told you she wasn't threatening you, she was telling you the truth. She doesn't have to. That's what people like me are for."

"Why are you telling me this?"

He looked at her then, really looked, and for a moment the shovel-face was just a tired man's face, sixty-some years deep in other people's errands. "Because you pulled Tomás out of that cave without hesitating, and he'd have let Camila drown to save himself. Because you check everyone's harness every morning including mine, especially mine, like you actually give a damn whether we live." He straightened off the door. "And because I've been paid to make problems disappear for twenty-two years, and I know what a problem looks like, and you're starting to look like one. So take the money, Underhill. Take the money, write the report, go be poor somewhere warm. It's a better trade than the alternative."

He opened the door. Cold air and starlight came in.

"One more question," Voss said. "Ferrer's survey. What coast? What site?"

Bruno paused in the doorway, half in shadow.

"Punta Guanaco," he said. "Same headland. Same cave."

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat on her bunk in the guides' quarters with her father's old notebook open on her knees — the one she carried on every job, waterproof paper, grease pencil, the gospel of a man who'd taught her that the sea keeps books and settles accounts.

Her father had worked this coast too. That was the thing she kept circling back to, the splinter under the skin. Rodrigo Underhill, guide and salvor, out of Puerto Deseado, who in 1984 had started asking questions about a wreck called the *Santa Elena* and about the Aldao family's shipping insurance claims, and who by 1986 was ruined — license revoked on a technicality, boat seized for unpaid harbor fees he swore he'd paid, name dragged through the provincial papers until no one would hire him. He drank himself dead within the decade. Voss had grown up on the story the way other children grew up on fairy tales: *they destroyed him, mija, because he knew too much.* She'd always assumed grief had inflated it. Fathers do that. Sons of ruin exaggerate.

But her grandfather — her mother's father — Mateo Arrieta, stoker, third class, age thirty-one, lost with all hands off Punta Guanaco, June 1912. The *Santa Elena*. She had known the name since childhood. She had assumed it was coincidence that the Aldao journals pointed to the same reef, the same cave, the same drowned night. Patagonia was empty; coincidences pooled here like rainwater.

They weren't coincidences. They had never been coincidences.

She thought about the bones she'd seen in the torchlight, arranged with care against the cave wall — laid out, not scattered, someone had *tended* them — and the longboat stove-in above the tideline, and the journal entries she'd been allowed to read under Tomás's watchful eye, all weather and bearings and nothing about survivors, nothing about the eleven men the wreck reports said reached shore alive. Eleven men reached the beach in 1912. The official record ended there. The sea keeps books.

So did the Aldaos.

The expedition had never been about discovery. It was containment — a controlled descent, supervised readings, a family member on every trip, and now a bought report from a disgraced guide whose word no one would ever trust anyway. *Who would believe her?* Inés hadn't needed to say it. Voss had been hired precisely because she was unbelievable, and because she was broke, and because — the cold clarity arrived all at once, complete, like a tide turning — because if her own grandfather's name was in that chest, she was the perfect person to bury it. Who else could be trusted to keep the secret that would break her?

The lamp guttered. She closed her father's notebook and pressed her palm flat against its cover, the way you'd press a hand on a grave.

Outside, far down the coast, the spring tide was beginning to fill the cave again, sealing the chest under forty feet of black water. Three days left in the window. Then the tides failed until autumn, and the estancia gates, she suspected, would close with them.

In the dark, Voss began, very quietly and very carefully, to plan.
