## 6. Bruno's Ledger

Bruno Sartori had kept the ledger in the same place his father had kept his: inside a false bottom of a leather writing case that had crossed the Atlantic twice and been re-stitched three times. He had never wanted it. It had come to him the way a debt comes to a son — folded into an inheritance he hadn't asked for, wrapped in oilcloth, smelling of lamp oil and old paper.

He sat in the tack room behind the estancia's main house, past midnight, with a hurricane lamp turned low and the case open on his knees. Outside, the wind worked at the corrugated roof in long, patient pulls, like something trying to get in. Inside, the horses shifted in their stalls and said nothing.

Three generations of Sartoris had served the Aldaos. His grandfather had been a gaucho who could read, which in 1920s Santa Cruz made him dangerous, and so they'd made him useful instead. His father had carried the ledger through the years when "fixing" meant telephone calls to men in Comodoro Rivadavia who knew judges by their first names. And Bruno — Bruno had learned early that loyalty was a currency, and that he was rich in it, and that being rich in a currency nobody else could spend was its own kind of poverty.

He opened the false bottom now because he had run out of reasons not to.

The pages were ruled in faded blue ink, one entry per line, dates marching back through decades like footprints going the wrong way. His grandfather's hand was cramped and careful. His father's was looser, faster, written in bars and hotel rooms. His own entries began in 1998, in ballpoint, and Bruno read them the way a man reads the autopsy of someone he loved without ever admitting he loved them.

*1974 — Estancia La Angostura. Peón Riquelme asked about the cave. Paid off. Left the district.*

*1981 — Voss, E. Guide, Puerto Deseado. Asking questions re: 1912 papers. Handled.*

*Handled.* His father had underlined it twice, as if emphasis were the same thing as conscience. Bruno knew what *handled* meant. It meant a season of work drying up. It meant credit refused in every shop between Deseado and Jaramillo. It meant a man with a wife and a daughter watching his name turn to poison on the coast where his family had lived for sixty years, until there was nothing left but a boat sold cheap and a silence that outlived him.

Edmund Voss. The guide. The woman with the gray eyes and the way of looking at you like she was measuring how far you'd fall.

He had known her father's name before he'd ever met her. That was the part that sat in him like a swallowed hook. When Inés Aldao had told him to hire the best guide available, blacklisted or not — *especially* if blacklisted, Tomás, because a desperate guide asks fewer questions — Bruno had gone down to Deseado and found her mending nets for pocket money, and he had felt something turn over in his chest that he had spent thirty years convincing himself wasn't there.

He had told himself it was business. Everything was business. That was the whole architecture of his life: a man paid to make problems disappear, telling himself that problems were weather, that weather was nobody's fault.

The lamp guttered. Bruno turned the wick and looked at the newest entries, his own, and hated their neatness.

*2011 — Reyes, C. Archaeologist. Contracted. Watch her camera equipment.*

He had watched Camila Reyes' camera equipment. He had watched it again after the second descent, when she came up from the cave with salt in her hair and a look on her face that he recognized from photographs of his own grandfather — the look of a man who has found something in the ground that changes what the ground means.

And tonight, an hour ago, she had shown him why.

She had come to his door past eleven, no lantern, rain jacket over her clothes, and held up her camera screen without a word. Photographs. Dozens of them, taken in the iron chest's interior during the forty minutes they'd had before the tide turned: survivor lists in a clerk's copperplate hand, columns of names, and beside each name a notation in the patriarch Ernesto Aldao's own writing — *expended*, *expended*, *expended* — as though the castaways were fuel burned to keep the family furnace lit. Ledgers of salvage claimed. Ledgers of insurance collected on cargo that had never reached port because the longboats sent to meet the wreck had orders Bruno could read plainly even in the photograph: *take what floats, leave what breathes.*

And among the names, in the column marked *left on the point, October 1912*: **VOSS, EDMUND** — no relation to the guide, except every relation there was. Her grandfather. The man whose ruin she'd inherited the way Bruno had inherited a ledger.

"I need to get these to town," Camila had said. "Tomorrow. Before Inés seals this place."

"The radio's dead," Bruno had told her. "Has been since Tuesday. I checked."

"Then we drive."

"The trucks are locked and Tomás has the keys and two of the peones are on Inés's payroll, which is my payroll, which is the problem." He had looked at the screen a long time. "Give me until morning."

She had given him a look — not trusting, but deciding to act as if she trusted him, which was worse — and gone back through the dark toward the guest wing.

Now Bruno closed the ledger and did not put it away.

* * *

Tomás found him at dawn in the stable yard, feeding the horses, which was where Bruno went when he needed to think somewhere with honest smells. The young man came across the gravel too fast, coat unbuttoned, and Bruno watched him come and thought: *there it is. Third generation. They always come to the fixer last, after God and before the police.*

"You've seen her," Tomás said. "The archaeologist. She's been asking about the trucks."

"She's been asking me about the trucks. There's a difference."

"My grandmother wants the expedition ended. Today. She wants the chest contents catalogued privately and the cave — " he faltered, and Bruno saw the boy's whole inheritance pass across his face like weather — "she wants the entrance sealed. Permanently. Stone and concrete. There's a crew coming from Gobernador."

"Sealed," Bruno repeated. "With everything in it."

"With *nothing* in it. That's the point." Tomás's voice climbed, cracked, dropped. "You don't understand what this does to us. To the name. A hundred years of — everything — and some diver finds a bone with a ledger next to it and it's over. The banks call the loans. You've seen it happen."

"I've made it happen," Bruno said quietly.

Tomás stopped. For a moment the wind filled the space between them, and Bruno saw the young man do the arithmetic he'd been avoiding: that the fixer standing in front of him was not furniture.

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means I know what sealing costs. Not money. The other cost." Bruno set down the feed bucket. "Your great-grandfather wrote *expended* beside the names of forty-one people who survived a wreck and trusted the boats that came for them. Your grandmother has spent eighty years making sure nobody read it. And now you want to pour concrete over the only proof, and pay me to stand there while it dries."

"It's not your name on it."

"No," Bruno agreed. "It's worse than that. It's my hand on it. Three generations of hands." He wiped his palms on his thighs, slowly, as if the dirt went deep. "I can't take this job, Tomás."

Something moved behind the young man's eyes — fear first, then the calculation fear becomes in families like this. "My grandmother will ruin you. She'll ruin you the way she ruined — " He caught himself.

"Voss's father," Bruno finished. "Yes. I know. I have the entry right here." He tapped his breast pocket, where the ledger rode against his ribs like a second heart. "I've had it all along. I just never read mine."

* * *

Camila found him at noon, at the windbreak above the sea cliffs, where he'd gone to watch the water and think about tides. She stood beside him without speaking for a long moment, arms crossed against the wind, and then said, "You didn't sleep."

"Neither did you."

"I slept fine. I know exactly what I'm doing." She turned to face him. "You're the one doing arithmetic."

Bruno almost smiled. "You photographed more than the lists."

"I photographed everything. Every page. Twice." Her jaw was set the way stone is set — by pressure, over time. "Forty-one names, Sartori. Forty-one people who waved at those boats. One of them was her grandfather. She doesn't know yet — I haven't told her, I wanted — " She stopped, breathed. "I wanted to know whose side you were on first."

"And now?"

"Now I'm asking."

Below them the Atlantic ran gray-green to the horizon, indifferent, keeping its books. Bruno took the ledger from his coat. He had decided sometime around four in the morning, staring at the ceiling, that he would not hand it over gently, because gentle would be a lie; he had carried it thirty years and handing it over should hurt.

"My grandfather started this book in 1913," he said. "Six months after the wreck. He was the one who rowed the second boat. He wrote down what the orders were, and who gave them, and what was paid to whom to keep it quiet. My father maintained it. I maintained it." He held it out. "Every fixer the Aldaos ever owned is in here. Including the ones they ruined. Including Edmund Voss's father, in 1981, in my father's handwriting, underlined twice."

Camila took it with both hands, the way you take something that might still be alive.

"Why give me this?" she asked. "What's your price?"

That was the question, and he let himself sit inside it for a moment, honestly, because he owed the question honesty if he owed anything at all. Thirty years of prices. He knew the market rate for everything on this coast.

"There isn't one," he said, and heard how strange the words sounded in his own mouth. "There's a price I won't take. Turns out it was this. Concrete over bones. Her grandfather's name poured shut like a drain." He looked out at the water. "A man should find the floor of himself eventually. This is mine."

"The tide window closes Friday," Camila said. "After that, spring lows are done until next season. If we're going back for the originals — the real ledgers, not photographs — it's Friday's ebb, and it's a night ebb, and it's forty miles of salt flat from here with the trucks locked."

"I can get a truck," Bruno said. "There's one the peones use for fence posts. Old, slow, and not on anyone's list."

"And Tomás?"

"Tomás will tell his grandmother what I said this morning. Which means by tonight, Inés Aldao knows her fixer has changed sides." He shrugged into the wind. "Which means we move tonight, not Friday, and we pray the ebb comes early. Because after tonight, this estancia is going to close around us like a fist."

Camila tucked the ledger inside her jacket, against her body, and for the first time since the cave she looked afraid — properly afraid, the useful kind.

"Then go start the truck," she said. "And someone has to tell Edmund what's in here. About her father. About her grandfather."

Bruno nodded once, already turning toward the sheds, already feeling the strange lightness of a man who has finally set down something heavy and discovered the weight was holding him to the ground.

Behind them, far up the coast, the first truck from Gobernador was already on the road — carrying stone, and cement, and men who poured things shut for a living.
