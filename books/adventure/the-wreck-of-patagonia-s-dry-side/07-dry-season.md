## 7. Dry Season

The tides began to fail the way a sick man's pulse does — not all at once, but in a slackening you could feel if you put your fingers on it.

Voss had spent twenty years reading tide tables the way other people read scripture, and she knew what the almanac was telling her now. The spring tides that had opened the cave in March were draining out of the calendar like water through shingle. Each successive low came shallower than the last. The moon was waning toward neap, and after neap there would be nothing but ordinary lows, and after those, weeks of water too high to even see the cave mouth, let alone walk into it.

The iron chest sat behind a curtain of stone forty meters inside a rock throat that drowned twice a day.

And Inés Aldao had announced at breakfast that the expedition was over.

* * *

She made the announcement the way she did everything — seated, unhurried, with her hands folded on the tablecloth as though the whole coast of Patagonia were an estate matter to be settled between courses. The comedor of the estancia smelled of woodsmoke and fresh bread, and the long windows showed the sea lying flat and silver under a sky scrubbed clean by wind, innocent as a church ceiling.

"The season is against us," Inés said. "The tides are failing, Dr. Reyes has her photographs, and my grandson's health—" she glanced at Tomás, who sat gray-faced over his coffee and did not object, "—cannot be risked for another descent. We will close the work here. You'll all be paid in full, of course. Generously."

"Of course," Voss said.

Camila's fork paused above her plate for half a second — half a second, no more — before continuing. Bruno Sartori ate steadily, his eyes on his food, his face arranged into the blankness of a man who has heard orders before and expects to hear worse ones later.

"You seem disappointed, Ms. Voss," Inés said pleasantly.

"I'm a guide. The season ends when it ends." Voss buttered her bread with steady hands. "I'd have liked one more look at the chest. For the record."

"The record," Inés repeated, tasting the word. "The sea keeps its own records, I think. Better ones than we do." She smiled, and it was almost warm. "You've done excellent work. My grandfather would have approved of you. He valued competence above all things."

*He would,* Voss thought. *He valued competent people the way a butcher values a sharp knife.*

That afternoon the estancia began to seal itself, and once you noticed it, you could not stop noticing. The gate across the entrance track, which had stood open since her arrival, was closed and chained. The peón who drove the supply truck to town twice a week made no move to go; the truck sat in its shed with dust gathering on the windshield. When Voss asked casually about mail, the housekeeper told her the radio room was closed for repairs. When she tried the radio herself that evening, on the pretext of confirming a weather report, the set was dead — not broken, but unplugged, and the plug socket behind the cabinet had been unscrewed from the wall.

Forty miles of salt flat and basalt between the estancia and Puerto Deseado. One road. One gate. And a family that had, by its own ledgers, left two hundred people to die on this coast and written the word *expended* beside their names in a fair, careful hand.

Voss stood in the darkening radio room with the dead set cold under her fingers and felt something settle in her chest that was not fear. Fear had been her companion for years, since the accident on Monte Olivares, since the blacklisting, since the slow bankruptcy of her reputation. This was different. This was clarity — cold and level, like the light before a gale.

They had hired her to bury her own history. Her grandfather's name was in that chest, third column from the top: *E. Voss, carpenter's mate, expended.* And now they meant to let the tide close over it forever, pay her off, and drive her home through a gate they controlled.

No.

* * *

She found Camila on the terrace an hour after full dark, wrapped in a blanket against the wind, staring at the black line where the sea ended. Voss sat down beside her without asking. For a while neither spoke. The wind worried the wire grass below the wall and somewhere inland a dog barked twice and gave up.

"They've cut the radio," Voss said quietly.

"I know. I tried the satellite phone in my kit this morning. The SIM won't register. Someone swapped it." Camila didn't turn her head. "Bruno, probably. Or he let someone else do it."

"And you're sitting out here drinking tea."

"What would you have me do? Storm the gate?" She laughed, but there was no humor in it, only exhaustion stretched thin. "Edmund. They photographed well, my copies. But copies aren't provenance. Any court in this country will say I forged them. A disgraced archaeologist and a disgraced guide — do you know how easy we are to dismiss?" She pulled the blanket tighter. "The originals are in that chest. Paper from 1912, ink from 1912, in the Aldao patriarch's own hand. That can't be forged. That can't be dismissed."

"The next extreme low is in nine days," Voss said. "After that, nothing until the equinox. Six weeks."

"Inés leaves for Buenos Aires in five. Tomás told me at lunch — he talks when he's frightened, he always talks. She's taking the journals back with her. Everything goes into the family archive in Recoleta, where lawyers sit on it for a century." Camila finally turned, and in the dark her eyes were wet and furious. "Nine days. It might as well be nine years. There's a gate, a radio that doesn't work, a truck that doesn't run, and forty miles of nothing, and they know we know."

"Do they?"

Camila blinked. "What?"

"They know *you* know. Maybe. They suspect. But suspicion isn't certainty, and Inés Aldao doesn't act on suspicion — she acts on ledgers. It's the family religion." Voss looked out at the dark sea. "So we give them nothing to read. We eat breakfast. We pack our kits. We thank our hostess. And in nine days, when the tide drops, we're already gone."

"That gate—"

"There's more than one way off an estancia. There always is. Ask any shepherd." She stood, brushing the damp from her trousers. "Get your photographs off that camera and onto every card you own, and hide the cards separately. If we don't come back, the copies still exist. That's not vanity. That's tactics."

Camila caught her wrist. "Edmund. If they catch us on the flats, there's no cover out there. None. You've seen it."

"I grew up on it," Voss said. "My father ran sheep on country like this until the Aldaos' bank called his loans. I know what forty miles of nothing looks like at night." She freed her wrist gently. "It looks like a road, if you're the only one on it."

* * *

Bruno Sartori found her in the tack room two nights later, where she had no legitimate reason to be, checking the condition of saddle leather she had no intention of using.

He closed the door behind him. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a face like a dropped brick and hands that had clearly done work no estancia required of a fixer. He leaned against the door and looked at her for a long moment.

"The gate chain," he said. "Third link from the bottom. It's old. It's been cut before and re-welded badly. A pair of bolt cutters would open it quiet, if a person had bolt cutters."

Voss kept folding the saddle blanket she was pretending to inspect. "Would a person find bolt cutters on this estancia?"

"Toolshed, second shelf, behind the post-hole digger. Combination's the patriarch's birthday — they use it for everything, they've used it for sixty years." He said it flatly, like a man reciting inventory. Then, after a silence: "The peón, Herrera. He drives to Deseado Thursdays for supplies. Tomorrow is Thursday. His route passes within six kilometers of the cave track. He hates the Aldaos. His grandmother was a Salas, from the fishing families. He knows what name is in that chest."

Voss looked up at him. "Why are you telling me this?"

Bruno's jaw worked. Outside, the wind pushed at the tack room door and gave up, pushed and gave up, like a patient animal.

"My father worked for the old man," he said finally. "My grandfather before him. Three generations of Sartoris making Aldao problems disappear. I used to think it was service. Then I read enough of those journals to understand what kind of problems the family has, and how far back it goes, and whose names keep appearing in the margins." He straightened off the door. "Your father. 1984. He wrote to a newspaper in Comodoro about land titles. Six months later he was ruined, drunk, dead inside ten years. I was nineteen. I drove the man who did it to the bus station afterward and shook his hand."

The blanket had stopped moving in Voss's hands. She hadn't known the details — only the shape of the ruin, the shape her mother's bitterness had worn all her childhood. Hearing it laid out in Bruno's gravel voice, dated and sourced like an entry in a ledger, made it real in a way grief alone never had.

"So this is guilt," she said.

"This is arithmetic," Bruno said. "I've been paid by that family my whole life. Every peso of it sits in an account in Bahía Blanca, and every peso of it is dirty, and none of it buys back one night's sleep since I read the word *expended* in a dead man's handwriting." He opened the door. "I'm not coming with you tomorrow night. If I'm gone, they'll know within the hour, and they'll know which direction. I stay, I keep Inés looking at nothing. But the chain will be cut, the tools will be where I said, and Herrera's truck will idle at the north fence line at two in the morning whether anyone flags him down or not."

"Bruno." He stopped in the doorway, his back to her. "If this goes wrong—"

"It went wrong in 1912," he said. "Everything since is just interest." And he was gone down the corridor, his boots fading into the sound of the wind.

* * *

The last three days, Voss performed. She performed so well she nearly disgusted herself.

She helped the housekeeper inventory the expedition stores. She walked the cliffs with Tomás, who had begun drinking before noon and talked compulsively about his father, about Buenos Aires, about how none of this was what he'd wanted, and she nodded in the right places and learned, incidentally, that Inés planned to fly out of the strip near Punta Guzmán on Tuesday morning. She thanked Inés at dinner with grave sincerity and watched the old woman's eyes flicker over her face, searching for the flaw in the performance, finding nothing — or finding nothing she trusted enough to act on.

In her room each night, Voss drew the coast from memory on the back of a tide table: the estancia, the fence lines, the salt flats, the basalt reef, the cave track, the distances, the water sources, the places the crust would hold a vehicle's weight and the places it would swallow one to the axles. She memorized it, then burned the paper in the stove and stirred the ash.

Nine days became six. Six became four. The low tides crept up the chart like a man drowning in reverse, each one shallower, the window narrowing from ninety minutes to seventy to sixty-five.

On the fourth evening, packing her kit in full view of anyone who cared to look — a guide going home, nothing more — Voss checked the almanac one final time.

Friday. Spring low at 03:40. The last usable tide of the season. Water off the cave mouth for perhaps eighty minutes, if the barometer held, if the wind stayed west, if the sea cooperated with the mathematics men had written for it.

At midnight she lay dressed in the dark, listening to the estancia breathe — the wind in the poplars, a door somewhere, Tomás's uneven footsteps crossing the courtyard below her window, late and restless.

Two o'clock. The north fence line. Herrera's idling engine. Forty miles of nothing under a waning moon.

Voss picked up her pack, and went out into the dark.
