## 8. The Long Ebb

The salt flats began where the last fence post leaned drunk against the horizon, and after that there was nothing to steer by but the stars and the memory of a map Voss had burned that morning.

Forty miles of it. Crusted ground that rang like crockery under their boots in the cold hours, then went soft as old bread when the night warmed toward dawn. The wind came off the Atlantic in long flat shoves, carrying the smell of iodine and kelp, and it never stopped, never varied, so that by the third hour Voss had stopped hearing it as sound and started feeling it as weight — a hand pressed between her shoulder blades, pushing her south toward the water.

She walked point. She had insisted on it, and neither Camila nor Bruno had argued. Behind her, Camila's breathing kept a steady runner's rhythm even at a walk, disciplined, economical; behind her, Bruno sounded like a man twice his size moving half as quietly, which was exactly what he was. They carried what they could not afford to lose: water, rope, the crowbar Bruno had stolen from the estancia's own toolshed with a fixer's unembarrassed competence, and wrapped in oilcloth against Voss's spine, the photographs Camila had taken of the ledgers — copies, shadows of shadows, worth nothing to anyone who wanted the truth rather than the rumor of it.

The originals were still in the iron chest, forty miles ahead, under a lid of returning sea.

"You're smiling," Camila said, coming up beside her on a stretch of ground hard enough to hold their weight.

"I'm doing arithmetic."

"Arithmetic looks like that on you?"

"On me it does." Voss nodded at the eastern dark, where the stars over the ocean had begun to smolder with the first suggestion of light. "Spring low tomorrow is at 6:40. The almanac gives us maybe three hours of workable water inside the cave mouth before the flood turns. We need ninety minutes in, forty minutes for the chest, and we want to be standing on high ground when the sea comes back for its furniture."

"And if the almanac is wrong?"

"Then we learn to breathe seawater." She said it lightly, and watched Camila decide not to be frightened by it. That was part of the job too, the part no one paid guides for: deciding how afraid everyone else was allowed to be.

They did not speak again for an hour. There was nothing to say that the wind wouldn't take anyway, and words cost breath, and breath was fuel. Voss rationed everything now — water, breath, attention. She had spent two days watching the estancia seal itself like a wound closing: the radio shed locked, the truck's distributor cap missing from her Land Rover, Inés Aldao's polite note informing them the expedition was concluded early, with gratitude, and would they care to wait for the family pilot? Wait. As if waiting had ever saved anyone on this coast.

It was Bruno who had come to her room at midnight with a map drawn from memory and one sentence: *I have driven this coast for thirty years and I am tired of knowing what happens at the end of the roads.* It was Camila who had already packed. It was Voss who had looked at the two of them — the archaeologist with her evidence and her fury, the fixer with his thirty years of sins — and understood that she was no longer leading an expedition. She was leading a getaway. The difference, she thought, was mostly in the paperwork, and they were going to steal the paperwork.

* * *

Dawn caught them in the drowned forest — a mile of petrified trees standing knee-deep in the tidal marsh, black trunks polished by four hundred years of salt wind, leaning all one way like a congregation frozen mid-flee. Voss called the halt there because the ground gave them cover and because Camila's ankle had begun to turn on the crust, and because a guide who drives people past their limits to make a schedule is just a slower kind of killer.

They ate cold. They drank measured. Bruno climbed one of the dead trees with a surprising grace and came down frowning.

"Vehicle," he said. "South-southwest, maybe six miles. Headlights off. Sitting."

"Waiting for us to come to him or sweeping?"

"Sitting. Which is worse. Sweeping means he doesn't know where we are." Bruno spat into the brine pool at his feet. "Sitting means he does."

Voss took the glasses and found it: a pale rectangle of dust hanging in the distance where a vehicle had parked on the only track that crossed the flats, patient as a heron. Tomás. Or Tomás's men. It hardly mattered which; the money was the same either way, and so was the intention. She thought of the boy's face in the cave on that first descent, white as the bones around him, and felt something that was not quite pity and not quite contempt. He had been raised in a house built on a graveyard and told it was a garden. Some men never survive learning better. Some men hire other men instead.

"We go wide," she said. "East, along the low shelf where the mud holds. It'll cost us two hours."

"Two hours we don't have," Camila said.

"Then we'll spend them anyway. Dead women keep no schedule." Voss folded the glasses away. "Bruno, when we hit the coast road cut, I want you on the rear glass. Camila — your ankle taped, your pack on my shoulder, and don't argue, arguing costs time too."

The archaeologist looked at her for a long moment, then handed over the pack without a word, and Voss understood that something had settled between them in that exchange — not trust exactly, but the thing trust is made from: a debt acknowledged on both sides.

* * *

They reached the headland above the cave at 4:10 in the afternoon, with the sea already falling away from the land like a held breath being released.

Voss had seen tides all her life. She had been raised on them, nearly killed by them twice, licensed once upon a time to judge them for other people's children. But the extreme lows of a dry-season spring were a different animal, and watching this one come off the rocks below her was like watching the ocean perform an act of confession. The water drew back and back, uncovering shelves of black stone furred with weed, pools steaming faintly in the cold air, and at last, far out, the cave mouth itself — a ragged arch in the cliff base, dripping, exhaling, its floor strewn with the debris of every storm since the world began.

Below it, the flats lay open and gleaming, a mile of wet sand and stone ribbed like a plucked muscle. A highway, for the next three hours. After that, a grave.

"Almanac says the turn is 6:40," Bruno said, checking his watch against the sun. "But look at the swell line. Look at it."

Voss looked. Far out, beyond the exposed reef, the swell was building from the southeast — long, glassy, organized lines of it, marching in with a discipline the morning's choppy sea hadn't shown. Wind shift. Somewhere out past the horizon a low was spinning up, and the barometer falling meant the water would come home heavier than any table predicted, pushed by wind and pressure like a debt collector.

"It'll be early," she said. "Maybe thirty minutes. Maybe more. We move now, we work fast, and nobody falls in love with anything down there."

They went down the goat path single file, Voss first, testing each hold, the packs riding high, the crowbar clinking against Bruno's hip with every step. At the cave mouth the air changed — colder, mineral, tasting of deep stone and old iron — and the light died to a gray seep from the arch behind them. Voss got her lamp going, then Camila's, then Bruno's, and the three beams swung into the dark like searching hands.

The longboat was still there, of course. Bones still arranged with that terrible care, shoulder to shoulder, the living formation of people who had lain down together. And beyond them, against the inner wall, the iron chest, furred with rust and salt-bloom, exactly where the turning tide had forced them to leave it eleven days ago.

Camila made a sound low in her throat, half prayer, half apology, and knelt among the bones without touching them, murmuring names from her lists — *expended*, expended, expended — reading the dead their own names back at last.

Voss set Bruno on the chest with the crowbar and turned to the entrance, to the sea, to the arithmetic. The light through the arch had gone amber. The nearest swell line was closer than it should have been.

"Bruno. Faster."

"I'm going as fast as iron allows—"

"Faster than that." She was back at the chest now, taking her turn on the bar, feeling the hasp groan, giving it her whole weight, her shoulders burning. "The water's coming early. I can feel it in the floor — the seep's rising." It was true; a thin sheet of water had begun to slide across the cave floor toward the inner dark, silent, unhurried, the way the sea always arrived, as though it had all the time in the world and knew it.

The hasp tore free with a shriek that echoed off the vault overhead. Inside, wrapped in tarred canvas gone stiff as bark, the ledgers lay in their nest of rot — three of them, swollen with salt, heavy as bricks, heavy as verdicts. Camila lifted them out with both hands, the way you lift a child, and got them into the oilcloth, into the waterproof bag, into her pack, and cinched it down.

And that was when Voss, standing in the archway with the last of the daylight on her face, saw the vehicle.

It was a quarter mile out on the flats, engine off, lights off, but the low sun caught the windshield and threw it back at her like a signal mirror. Three figures beside it. One of them tall and narrow-shouldered, standing very still, looking at the cave mouth the way a man looks at the door of a house he grew up in.

Behind her, the first swell broke against the outer reef, and the sound rolled into the cave like a drumbeat, and the water on the floor slid another inch inland.

"They're coming down," Voss said. She heard her own voice go flat and calm, the guide's voice, the voice that had talked panicked clients off wet rock in three countries before anyone took her name away. "Bruno, get her up the north passage to the blowhole — start now, don't wait for me. Camila — whatever happens, that bag goes to Buenos Aires if it goes over my body."

"And you?" Camila said.

Voss stepped out onto the shining flats, alone, into the path of the three figures, and checked the water at her back.

"I'm going to hold the door," she said.
