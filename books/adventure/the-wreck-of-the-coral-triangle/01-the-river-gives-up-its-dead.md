## 1. The River Gives Up Its Dead

The river had been misbehaving all morning, and Ines Dravenmoor trusted it the way one trusts a dog that has recently learned to open doors.

"Current's wrong," they said, for the third time since dawn.

"It's always wrong," said Breck Halloway, who owned the barge and half its cargo and none of its sense. He was a broad man built like a doorstop, with a laugh that arrived a full second after his jokes, as though it had to travel up from somewhere deep and damp. "That's what makes it a river and not a road."

"That's what makes it a river," Ines agreed, "and not something you argue with."

They were three days out of Corvane with a hold full of things nobody would admit to owning — salted eel, uncut garnets, forty crates of a cough syrup that was mostly brandy — and Ines was being paid, badly but reliably, to get all of it past the customs narrows at Vell's Crossing without anyone asking cheerful questions. It was not guiding work. It was not even honest smuggling, which at least had traditions. It was the kind of work a blacklisted mountain guide fell into when the debts got tall enough to have their own weather.

Ines stood at the bow rail and watched the water do the thing it had been doing since first light: running backward.

Not backward, exactly. That was the word Breck kept using, and it was wrong in a way that made Ines's teeth itch. The current wasn't reversing. It was *hesitating* — gathering itself in slow coils along the southern bank, where the channel bent around a drowned orchard, then releasing all at once, like a held breath let go too fast. Ines had spent eleven years reading snowpack and four years learning, painfully, why people no longer hired them to read anything. Rivers were just mountains lying down. They had the same moods. They kept the same secrets under their surfaces.

And this one was keeping something very specifically today.

"There," Ines said. "Off the port side. See the foam?"

Breck squinted. "I see foam."

"The foam's moving against the current. Something down there is pushing water sideways." Ines drummed fingers on the rail — left hand, two knuckles missing from an ice axe incident they did not discuss at parties, or anywhere. "Something big enough to change how the river breathes."

"You want me to stop the barge," Breck said slowly, "in the middle of a run, because of *foam*."

"I want you to slow down near the bend because I'm going to go look."

"Ines." Breck lowered his voice with the delicacy of a man handling someone else's grief. "We are carrying contraband through a customs gap. Every minute we drift is a minute some Vell's Crossing boy with a whistle gets ambitious. Whatever's down there, the river can keep it."

The river, as if offended, chose that moment to give it up anyway.

It happened without drama, which was somehow worse. One moment the water off the port bow lay flat and brown-green and dull as old pewter; the next, a section of surface simply rose — a slow, groaning displacement, like something enormous turning over in bed — and a shape broke through, streaming weed and silt, and settled against the bank with a sound Ines felt through the deck boards more than heard. A deep wooden knock. A knock on a door.

It was a chest. A bronze-bound chest the size of a coffin, furred with forty years of river growth, riding half-submerged in the shallows of the drowned orchard as if it had been waiting there for a scheduled appointment and had grown impatient.

Nobody spoke for a long moment. Even the gulls went quiet, which gulls never do, and which Ines would remember later as the first wrong note.

"Well," said Breck at last, with the enthusiasm of a man watching a lottery ticket blow toward a drain. "That's not ours."

"No."

"We're not stopping."

"No," Ines said again, and was already swinging over the rail.

* * *

The water came up to Ines's ribs and was cold in the way of deep rivers — a cold that didn't care about seasons or skin, a cold with institutional memory. Ines waded the last few meters to the chest, boots finding submerged fence posts from the old orchard, and put both hands on the bronze binding.

It was heavier than a coffin. It was heavier than most sins.

The growth came away in sheets when Ines scraped at it — pale weed, then a crust of river lime, then, underneath, metal so clean it could only mean the chest had been sealed. Sealed things stayed clean. That was the whole point of sealing things.

And there, across the lid, stamped deep enough to survive four decades underwater: a seal. A triangle of interlocking waves around a single star, and beneath it, worn but legible, a word in the old Vhalorian script that Ines knew only from the margins of banned atlases and the mutterings of drunk exiles in riverport bars.

Vhaloria.

A country that had been on maps when Ines was a child and was not on them now. Not conquered, not renamed — *erased*. Sunk, if the stories were true, in a single catastrophic season: treasury, archives, records, debts, everything a nation keeps in writing, gone into the water in one deliberate night. The cartographers hadn't even gotten a war out of it. There'd been nothing to draw borders around. Just a valley, a lake that hadn't existed before, and a silence that everyone agreed, without ever saying so, had been purchased by somebody.

People who talked about Vhaloria in riverports talked about it the way people talk about a relative who died in disgrace — quickly, quietly, and with frequent glances at the door.

"Ines." Breck's voice from the barge, pitched low and urgent. "Ines, there's a boat coming up the far channel. Cargo skiff, no lights, moving like it means it."

"How far?"

"Ten minutes. Maybe less."

Ines looked down at the seal under their hands. The star at the center caught the light — not the flat gleam of ordinary bronze but something brighter, lacquered, deliberate. Stamped into the rim, smaller, was a line of numerals. A date. Forty-one years ago. Three days before the nation stopped existing.

Everything in Ines's professional life said *leave it*. Eleven years of mountain work boiled down to one rule drilled deeper than any other: the mountain gives you exactly one warning, and curiosity about the second one kills you. Rivers, presumably, operated on similar terms. Here was a sealed box from a drowned country, surfacing on cue in front of a smuggler's barge, while another boat closed fast from downstream. Every element of that sentence was a warning label.

But Ines's hands were already hooking under the chest's iron handles, and Ines's shoulders were already taking the weight, and somewhere behind the sternum — in the small cold room where Ines kept the debt ledger of their own life, the numbers owed to lenders and the larger number owed to a client abandoned on a white mountain six years ago — something shifted its weight and settled, like ballast finding its level.

"Bring it aboard," Breck called down, resigned. He had the tone of a man who had already lost the argument and was negotiating only for dignity. "But I want it understood that whatever comes of this, my share of the blame is the *smaller* share."

"Noted," Ines said, and heaved.

* * *

Getting the chest onto the barge took both of them, a cargo net, and language that would have gotten them fined in any civilized port. It landed on the deck with a boom that rattled the eel barrels, and sat there, dripping, enormous, patient.

Breck circled it twice, arms folded. "Open it."

"I thought we weren't stopping."

"We stopped. We're committed now. Open it."

Ines knelt. The lock was a mechanism, not a padlock — a bronze dial set flush into the hasp, ringed with characters in the old script. No keyhole. It wanted to be *read*, not forced. Ines turned it once, experimentally, and felt the mechanism engage with a click that sounded, absurdly, like a throat clearing.

Then Ines stopped, hands flat on the lid, and looked at the date stamped on the rim again. Three days before the end. Which meant whoever had sealed this chest had sealed it knowing what was coming. Had packed it, locked it, weighted it or hidden it or entrusted it to the water — three days before the night the lake swallowed the valley.

This wasn't wreckage. Wreckage drifted loose and arrived broken. This had been *sent*. Delivered. Addressed.

Forty years in transit.

"Ines?" Breck said. "You've got a look. I don't love the look."

"What look?"

"The look of a person about to make my life complicated."

Ines almost laughed. Almost. What came out instead was quieter. "My mother used to say the river remembers what the map forgets."

Breck blinked. "Your mother said that? Your mother was a—"

"A hydrologist. For the delta reclamation board, before the funding died." Ines turned the dial another notch. Another click. Somewhere inside the chest, chains — actual chains, Ines could hear them — began to pay themselves out. "She said it about flood plains. She said a lot of things. Most of them were about drainage."

"And the rest?"

Ines didn't answer, because the rest — the rest was a postcard that had arrived eleven years ago, forwarded through three addresses, unsigned, with a single line in handwriting Ines would know blindfolded: *If the water ever offers you something, take it. It will only offer once.* And then, eight months later, the notice from the delta authority confirming what the postcard had implied without saying: dead, they said, in a survey accident on the lower reaches. Lost to the river. The river keeps what it keeps.

Ines had spent eleven years being angry at mountains and four years being angry at ledgers, and had never once found room to be angry at the river, which now seemed like a remarkable piece of administrative oversight.

The last notch turned. The hasps sprang. The lid rose an inch on its own — counterweighted, designed to open gracefully even after decades — and a smell rolled out that Ines felt in the back of the teeth: wax, oilcloth, cold metal, and under it something older, something like a church that had been closed for repairs and never reopened.

Inside, packed in oiled wool: ingots? No. Ines's first guess dissolved on contact with reality. Not treasure. Paper. Dozens of folios wrapped in waxed cloth, bound in bundles, each bundle tagged with a strip of lead stamped in that same interlocking-wave seal. And on top of the pile, alone, unwrapped, laid there deliberately to be found first:

A ledger. Small, dark, its cover cracked with age, its spine reinforced with brass corners. No title. Just the seal, embossed blind, and beneath it a single word in the old script that Ines couldn't read but that needed no translation, because some words carry their meaning in their weight:

*Accounts.*

"Debts," Breck breathed, peering over Ines's shoulder with the shamelessness of a business partner. "It's a book of debts. We hauled a drowned country's *accounts receivable* out of a river." He laughed his delayed laugh, alone, because Ines hadn't joined. "There'll be collectors. There's always collectors. Ines, there are men whose entire profession is chasing paper like this, and they have boats, and guns, and — Ines. Why are you looking at me like that?"

"Downstream boat," Ines said. "Still coming?"

"Gone. Turned off at the orchard fork." Breck frowned. "Which is stranger, honestly. Who turns *away*?"

Someone who had seen what they needed to see. Someone who hadn't come for the chest at all — only to confirm it had surfaced. Someone who would be telling somebody else, right now, at speed.

Ines looked down at the ledger. The waxed cloth of the top bundle had worked loose in the river's long handling, and one corner of the first page showed through — dense columns of names and figures in a fine, upright hand, and beside each name, in a second column, a notation Ines's eye snagged on like a boot heel in scree.

Not amounts. Dates. Each name carried a date, and every date was within three days of the end.

A list of who owed what — or perhaps of who was owed — written in the final hours of a country that was about to be erased from every map and memory its neighbors could manage. Somebody had spent the nation's last seventy-two hours doing bookkeeping. Somebody had believed the accounts would matter afterward. Somebody had sealed them, addressed them to the future, and given them to the water.

*If the water ever offers you something, take it.*

Ines closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping person, and became aware that Breck was talking.

"—saying we throw it back. Or sell it whole, sight unseen, to the first broker in Vell's Crossing who doesn't ask questions. There's always brokers. Ondar's people run that stretch, they'd pay—"

"Ondar," Ines repeated.

"Sef Ondar. Cartel factor. Buys anything with a history and burns anything with a future." Breck shrugged. "Point is, options. We have options, and every one of them ends with us richer and uninvolved."

Ines stood. Below them, the river ran on, smooth and innocent again, its hesitation finished, its delivery complete. Behind them, ten miles back downriver, the customs narrows waited with their cheerful whistles. Ahead, the channel forked, and one branch of it — the one the downstream boat had taken — wound upriver, toward the high country, toward the forbidden valleys, toward the source of everything.

Ines thought about debts. About the ones with numbers attached, which could be paid down crate by crate of smuggled cough syrup, year by gray year. And about the other kind — the kind with a name attached, a client's name, a face left white on a white slope — which no crate would ever touch.

And underneath both, older than either, a third entry. Unsigned. Undated. A postcard line in a dead woman's hand, sitting in the cold room of Ines's chest like a promissory note: *take it; it will only offer once.*

"Breck," Ines said. "How much do you actually know about Vhaloria?"

"Enough to know everyone connected to it is either dead, exiled, or rich in ways that frighten governments." Breck's eyes narrowed. "Why?"

"Because I think we just became connected to it." Ines picked up the ledger and felt its weight settle into the hands like something that had been measured for them. "And I think the river doesn't offer twice."

Far upstream, faint as a memory, a bell began to ring — though there was, as far as either of them knew, no bell, no tower, and nothing on any chart for fifty kilometers in that direction but water and the drowned places water keeps.

Breck stared north. "Tell me you heard that."

"I heard it," Ines said, and turned the barge's nose upriver.
