## 2. A Ledger of the Drowned

The safehouse above the Salt & Anchor smelled of tallow, river mud, and the particular despair of a man who had once been somebody. Ines knew the type. Ines *was* the type.

His name was Corvo Pell, and he had been a customs officer in Vhaloria's last government, which meant he had been an employee of a country that no longer existed, which meant he had spent forty years being unemployed in the most technically correct way possible. He kept his old brass customs stamp on the mantelpiece like a saint's relic and polished it every morning, and he greeted Ines at the door with the weary dignity of a man who had been asked to hide things before and had never once been thanked for it.

"You brought it up three flights of stairs," he said, looking at the chest slung across Ines's back with rope.

"It didn't give me a choice."

"Nothing worth hiding ever does." Corvo stood aside. "Table's in the back. Don't scratch it. My wife made that table."

Ines set the chest down on the table of the late Mrs. Pell as gently as if it were the widow herself. It was bronze, green-scaled with age where the river hadn't scoured it clean, and across its lid ran the seal: a triangle inverted into water, three stars drowning inside it. The mark of Vhaloria. Ines had seen it twice before in their life — once on a coin their mother kept in a tin she never explained, and once on the stern of a boat that shouldn't have existed, forty years after the country that built boats had stopped existing.

Corvo lit two lamps and then, after consideration, a third. "You want to tell me where you got it?"

"The river gave it to me."

"The river gives up bodies and old boots. It doesn't give up sealed state property."

"It did tonight." Ines worked the wet knots loose. Their hands were still shaking — not from cold, though the cold helped excuse them. "We were running cargo past the Silt Steps, the shallows below the old weir. The whole channel shifted after the spring floods. There was a snag — a whole drowned structure, maybe. The current peeled something off it and handed it to us like a bill."

"And you took it."

"I'm in debt, Corvo."

"I know what you're in." Corvo said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "Everyone on this stretch of river knows what you're in. That's why you're running smuggled lamp oil instead of guiding climbers over passes, isn't it? Blacklisted twice over. The great Ines Dravenmoor, reduced to hauling kerosene for men who can't read a map."

"Flattery will get you everywhere."

Corvo almost smiled. Then the last knot came free, and neither of them smiled at all.

The lid rose with a groan of bronze hinges that had waited four decades to complain. Inside, packed in wool gone grey and stiff as old snow, lay papers. Hundreds of them. Bundles tied with waxed cord, ledgers bound in blue leather gone black at the corners, folios stamped with the inverted triangle. And on top, alone, wrapped separately in oilcloth — as if whoever packed the chest had wanted it found first — a single ledger, slim, its cover stamped with a number rather than a name.

*Debts Outstanding,* said the embossed title. *Register of the National Obligation.*

Corvo sat down slowly, the way old men sit down when their knees and their convictions fail at the same time. "That's a treasury register," he said. "That's — do you understand what that is? When they sank her, when the cartel flooded the valley and the maps were redrawn, everything Vhaloria owed and everything owed *to* Vhaloria went down with her. Every debt, every bond, every promise. They erased the country so they could erase the arithmetic. And here—" His voice cracked and he covered it with a cough. "Here's the arithmetic."

Ines lifted the ledger out. It was lighter than it should have been, the way all terrible things are.

* * *

They worked through the night by lamplight, and by the third hour Ines had stopped seeing names and started seeing people.

That was the trick of it — the horror of it. A debt ledger should be dry. Columns, sums, dates. But Vhaloria had kept its books like a family keeps its letters. Each entry carried more than a figure. *Marta Ilves, weaver, owes the state 40 crowns for loom credit — noted: repaid in cloth during the hard winter, balance forgiven, see marginal note.* And there would be a marginal note, in some clerk's careful hand: *Her cloth kept the garrison warm. The nation remembers.* Or: *Joren Bacht, ferryman, owed 12 crowns in flood relief — paid in full by carrying children across the Spillwater, no charge. The nation does not forget.*

"They wrote kindness into the accounting," Corvo murmured. He had removed his spectacles twice to wipe his eyes and pretended both times that it was the lamp smoke. "Forty years I've told people my country died of greed. Greed sank her, yes. But look — look how she lived."

Page after page. A schoolteacher's pension. A doctor's unpaid fee, marked *waived — delivered a child in a storm, the child is the nation's now.* A boy of nine who owed three crowns for a lost library book, with a note in a different, warmer hand: *Let him keep the book. Some debts are good to carry.*

Ines read that one twice and thought of nothing at all, which was a kind of mercy.

Then Corvo turned a page and went very still, and the mercy ended.

"Ines," he said. "Look at the hand."

The final entry. The last line of a dead nation's living book. Not printed in a clerk's round script but written in a quick, slanted, left-leaning cursive that Ines knew better than their own heartbeat, because Ines had seen it on lunch notes tucked into satchels, on recipe cards, on the single letter that had arrived after the funeral from a woman claiming to be a friend and signing herself only *E.*

The entry read:

*Final reconciliation. All obligations herein are transferred to the bearer of this register, who will know why. The debt is not owed to the dead. It is owed forward. — E. Dravenmoor*

Below it, a sum. Not in crowns. In a word: *Everything.*

The lamps hissed. Somewhere down on the street a drunk was singing a song about a girl from Kiln Reach, badly, with great conviction.

"That's your mother's name," Corvo said quietly.

"Yes."

"E. Dravenmoor. Your mother was—"

"I don't know what my mother was." Ines heard their own voice come out flat and cold, a guide's voice, the voice used above the treeline when panic is a luxury. "She sold maps. She repaired clocks. She made a stew on Sundays that could raise the dead, and she never once, not one time in my entire childhood, mentioned that she came from a country that doesn't exist. People don't hide a homeland, Corvo. They hide crimes."

"Or they hide from them."

"Same thing, usually." Ines turned the ledger over, looking for forgery the way a locksmith looks for picks — out of professional habit. The paper was right. Old rag paper, water-marked, consistent with the rest of the register. The ink had bloomed the same way the other entries had. If it was forged, it had been forged forty years ago, by someone with access to a treasury's stationery and a very long patience.

That was the problem. That was the whole problem, sitting in Ines's chest like swallowed river water.

Because there were exactly two explanations, and Ines hated them equally.

Either the entry was genuine — in which case their mother, quiet map-mending Sunday-stew mother, had been inside Vhaloria's government at the end, close enough to write the last line of its national soul, and had chosen to say nothing for the rest of her life while her child grew up thinking she was nobody from nowhere.

Or the entry was false — planted, seeded into a sunken chest like a hook into a fish's mouth, waiting forty years or forty days for someone foolish enough to pull it from the river. Someone with debts. Someone with a name that matched. Someone whose grief could be used as a handle.

And the truly unbearable part — the part Ines kept circling like a hawk over a carcass — was that both explanations felt true. The handwriting was right. The pressure of the pen, the way the *r*s kicked, the little impatient slash through the *t* — Ines would swear it on any mountain you liked. But grief swears anything. Grief is the worst witness in the world; it identifies every stranger at the funeral as family.

"Say it's real," Corvo said. He had put his spectacles back on, and behind them his eyes were wet and fierce. "Say your mother wrote that. *Transferred to the bearer, who will know why.* Do you know why?"

"No."

"Do you want to?"

Ines looked at the page. At the word *Everything* sitting under their mother's hand like a stone at the bottom of a clear pool — visible, reachable, and deep enough to drown in.

"Yes," Ines said. "That's what frightens me."

* * *

Dawn came up grey over the riverport, and with it came the smell of frying fish from the tavern below, which was the only optimistic thing in the building. Corvo brewed coffee strong enough to stand a spoon in and refused, with great ceremony, to discuss the ledger any further until Ines had eaten. "Decisions made hungry are decisions made stupid," he said. "I watched a parliament do it for twenty years."

So they ate fried fish and bread, and Corvo told two stories about the old customs house, one of which was even true, and for about eleven minutes Ines almost felt like a person instead of a debt with legs.

Then the knock came.

Three raps. Even, unhurried, polite. The knock of someone who has never in his life needed to break a door because doors open themselves when they see him coming.

Corvo's face changed. He looked at Ines, and Ines looked at the ledger, and the same thought passed between them without either saying it aloud: *nobody knows we have it except the crew, and the crew was paid to forget, and men paid to forget sometimes remember for money.*

"Through the back," Corvo whispered. "Stairs to the roof, roof to the chandler's, chandler's to the water. Go."

"Not without you."

"I'm a customs officer of a dissolved nation, child. I have been obsolete for forty years. Obsolete men are safe — nobody robs a museum." He was already moving, sliding the ledger into a canvas satchel and pressing it into Ines's hands. His grip was surprisingly strong. "But you — you're the bearer. *Who will know why*, remember? Whoever's knocking, they're not knocking for me."

Another knock. Same rhythm. Then a voice, smooth as oiled rope, carrying easily through the door:

"Mister Pell. Good morning. I'm looking for a guide — I understand one stayed the night. Tell them Sef Ondar has no interest in trouble. Only in purchases."

Corvo mouthed the name like it tasted of copper. Ines had never heard it before, and understood immediately, the way animals understand weather, that it was going to matter.

"Back stairs," Corvo breathed. "Now. And Ines — whatever he offers, remember what your mother wrote. *Owed forward.* Things owed forward can't be sold. They can only be carried."

From beyond the door, pleasantly, patiently, the voice began again — and this time it used Ines's name.

Down in the street, the drunk had stopped singing. That was the detail Ines would remember afterward, in the bad hours: not the voice at the door, but the silence outside the window, where a man singing about a girl from Kiln Reach had simply — stopped.

Someone had paid him to stop. Which meant someone had bought the whole street.

Which meant the chest had never been lost at all.

It had been *delivered*.
