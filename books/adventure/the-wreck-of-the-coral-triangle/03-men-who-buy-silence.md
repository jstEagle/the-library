## 3. Men Who Buy Silence

The safehouse smelled of mildew and someone else's cooking, which Ines had decided was the safest possible combination of smells. Nobody raids a building that smells like old cabbage. They'd been there two days — Ines, the chest, and a riverport landlord named Brannic who asked no questions because he charged extra for not asking them.

Ines spent those two days doing what Ines did best when frightened: organizing things that did not need organizing. The rope was coiled three times. The boots were polished. The chest sat in the corner under a tarp like a sleeping dog they were all pretending wasn't there.

"You're going to wear a groove in my floor," Brannic said from his chair by the window, where he sat shelling beans with the patience of a man who had outlived three wives and every war he'd ever heard of.

"I'm pacing."

"You're pacing *loudly*. There's a difference. Pacing is quiet and sad. What you're doing is marching."

Ines stopped mid-stride, one boot raised, feeling deeply foolish. "I'm thinking."

"Thinking's allowed. Thinking's free." Brannic flicked a bean into the bowl. "But you've looked at that window forty times since noon, and the window hasn't changed, so either you think the river's going to do something new, or you're waiting for company."

The river was doing something new, actually. It was low. Lower than it should have been for the season, and Ines had spent enough years reading water the way other people read faces to know that a low river meant exposed things. Sandbars. Wrecks. The bones of whatever the water had swallowed and decided, on reflection, to give back.

It had already given back one thing. That was rather the problem.

"I want to know who else saw it come up," Ines said.

"Everyone saw it come up. A bronze box floats past a fishing village wearing a crown stamped on its lid, everyone sees it." Brannic shrugged. "Most of them decided it was bad luck and went back to their nets. One of them didn't."

"One of them didn't," Ines repeated, and the words sat in the room like a third guest.

Because that was the arithmetic Ines couldn't stop running. The seal on the chest belonged to Vhaloria, and Vhaloria had been erased from maps forty years ago — drowned, bought, buried, depending on which drunk told the story. Nations didn't drown by accident. And whoever had done the drowning might reasonably object to their paperwork floating downstream into the hands of a disgraced mountain guide with debts in four territories and a talent for being exactly where trouble landed.

The knock came at dusk.

Three knocks. Evenly spaced. The kind of knocking that came from a man who had never once in his life needed anything urgently, because everything urgent eventually came to him.

Brannic set down his beans. "Company," he said, without surprise.

Ines crossed to the wall and took down the climbing axe — an affectation in a river town, but habits outlive their usefulness, and the axe had outlived several mountains. "Through the back?"

"Back door sticks. Front door's fine. He's alone." Brannic tilted his head, listening. "I can hear it. One man walks differently than five men pretending to be one."

That, Ines would learn later, was the most valuable sentence anyone spoke that night.

* * *

Sef Ondar was a small man dressed expensively, which was somehow worse than a large man dressed expensively. Large men used money as armor. Small men used it as a blade, kept sharp and close. He wore a grey coat that probably cost more than Brannic's house, and he stood in the doorway holding a bottle of wine in both hands like an offering at a shrine.

"Mistress Dravenmoor." His voice was soft and precise, each word placed like a stone in a garden path. "Or is it Mister? Forgive me — the records are inconsistent, and I've found inconsistency usually means the truth is more interesting than either option."

"Ines is fine."

"Ines, then." He smiled. It was a good smile, well-maintained, like the coat. "May I come in? I brought wine. It's a peace offering, though I'll admit up front there's nothing to make peace about yet. Consider it an advance."

Brannic hadn't moved from his chair. He looked at Ondar the way a man looks at weather coming in off the water. "You know him?" Ines asked quietly.

"I know the coat," Brannic said. "Coats like that don't walk up my stairs by accident."

Ondar stepped inside, glanced around the safehouse with the frank appraisal of a man pricing a property, and set the wine on the table. "Vhalorian red, actually. From before. There are perhaps six hundred bottles left in the world, and I own four hundred of them." He said this the way other men mentioned their children. "Drink it or don't. But it seemed fitting."

"Fitting for what?"

"For the conversation." He pulled out a chair uninvited and sat, folding his small hands on the table. Outside, the light was going blue over the river, and somewhere a fisherman was swearing at his nets in a way that suggested the evening catch had opinions. "You opened the chest."

It wasn't a question, so Ines didn't answer it. Ondar nodded as if the silence were itself an answer, and perhaps it was.

"The ledger inside lists one hundred and fourteen names," he continued. "Debts owed to the state of Vhaloria at the moment of its dissolution. Some of those debts are trivial. Some are considerable. All of them are now, technically, collectible by whoever holds the ledger — because a debt needs a creditor, Mistress Dravenmoor, and creditors can be purchased." He let that sit. "I am offering to be the purchaser."

"You want to buy the chest."

"I want to buy the *debt*, and the chest happens to be where it lives. Yes." He produced no papers, which was itself a kind of statement. Men who wanted things on record brought papers. Men who wanted things forgotten brought wine. "Name a figure. Be ambitious. I respect ambition in sellers; it saves negotiation time."

Ines leaned against the wall, arms crossed, and made themselves look at the wine instead of the man. Vhalorian red. Six hundred bottles left in the world. Somewhere in that number was a vineyard underwater, a country underwater, a hundred and fourteen names on paper that had spent forty years in the dark and one week in Ines's keeping.

One of those names was written in a hand Ines knew better than their own. Had known better than their own, once. A hand that slanted left and pressed too hard and always, always dotted its letters like it was stabbing them.

"What happens to the names," Ines said, "after you buy them?"

Ondar's smile adjusted by a degree. It was remarkable, watching it — like watching a helmsman correct course by inches. "They become mine. Which means they become quiet. Which means—" he spread his hands, generous, reasonable, "—they stop being dangerous. To anyone. Including you."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer that matters." For the first time, something moved behind the pleasant face — not anger, but a kind of tired patience, the look of a man who had given this speech many times and could not understand why it kept failing. "Mistress Dravenmoor. Ines. You are blacklisted in two countries. You owe money to people considerably less polite than I am. You are sitting in a rented room above a bean-sheller, guarding a box that three separate organizations will kill for, and your plan appears to be *sitting here*. I am offering you enough money to disappear forever, comfortably, with a new name and a coastline. This is not a threat. This is the kindest offer you will receive, and I would like you to notice that I noticed that."

The room was very quiet. Brannic had stopped shelling beans entirely, which Ines suspected was the local equivalent of drawing a weapon.

And Ines thought about the name in the ledger. Thought about saying yes, taking the money, letting the whole drowned country sink a second time — this time on purpose, this time paid for. It would be so easy. It would be *warm*. There was a version of Ines Dravenmoor who took that deal, and Ines had met that version before, on a mountain, years ago, and had spent every day since trying not to become them again.

"No," Ines said.

Ondar waited, as if the word were incomplete.

"No," Ines said again, and found, to their surprise, that the second one was easier. "The ledger isn't for sale. Not to you. Maybe not to anyone. But definitely not to you."

The smile went away. It didn't collapse; it was simply withdrawn, folded and put away like the good coat it was. "May I ask why?"

"Because you called the names dangerous." Ines pushed off the wall. "Dead men aren't dangerous, Master Ondar. Debts to dead men aren't dangerous. You're not afraid of the ledger. You're afraid of what it *proves*."

For a long moment Sef Ondar looked at Ines with genuine, almost scholarly interest — the interest of a man encountering an unexpected variable. Then he stood, straightened his expensive coat, and picked up the wine.

"Keep it," he said. "You'll want a drink tonight." At the door he paused, half-turned, silhouetted against the last blue light. "For what it's worth, I hoped you'd say yes. It's rare, in my line of work, to meet someone who surprises me. It's also expensive." He tapped the doorframe twice, gently, like knocking on wood for luck. "Good evening, Ines Dravenmoor."

Then he was gone, down the stairs, unhurried, one set of footsteps.

Brannic exhaled through his nose. "Well," he said. "That was the politest death sentence I've ever heard served."

"You think—"

"I think coats like that don't take no for an answer, they take no for a *schedule*." Brannic was already up, moving faster than a man his age had any right to move, snuffing lamps. "Back door doesn't stick, by the way. I lied. It sticks for strangers. For friends it opens if you lift and push. Take the chest. Leave the wine — no, actually, take the wine, you've earned it, and I don't drink anything I can't afford to replace."

"How long do we have?"

Brannic paused at the window. Down on the dark street, lanterns were moving. Not walking-lanterns, which bobbed and wandered. These moved in lines. In formation.

"Less than that," he said.

* * *

They nearly made it.

The back alley behind the safehouse ran toward the water stairs, and Ines had the chest strapped to their back — heavier than it had any right to be, bronze and paper and forty years of somebody's careful accounting digging into their shoulders — while Brannic led the way with a shuttered lamp and the unbothered gait of a man going out to check his crab pots.

The fire started behind them while they were still in the alley.

No shout, no warning. Just a sound like a great indrawn breath, and then orange light climbing the walls ahead of them, and the smell — oil, not lamp oil, something thicker, industrial, meant for burning buildings rather than lighting them. They had gone in the front door while Ines was still deciding whether to run. Professionals. Ondar hadn't even waited to reach his boat.

"He burned it," Ines said stupidly, turning. "He burned the *room*. There was nothing in the room—"

"There was you in the room, this morning." Brannic didn't break stride. "Men like that burn evidence. You declined to become evidence, so he's tidying up. Keep moving. Water stairs. There's a skiff."

The heat came down the alley after them like a living thing, and with it came footsteps — two men, maybe three, cutting off the far end. Ines saw them in the firelight: river jackets, knives, the flat calm of hired work. Not soldiers. Worse. Soldiers believed in something. These men believed in wages.

"Ines," Brannic said, very calmly, "the skiff is that way, and these gentlemen are this way, and I am an old man with a lamp, so I suggest you choose quickly."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You're not *staying* with me either, boy, because I'm about to do something extremely embarrassing for a man of my dignity, and I'd rather you didn't watch." And Brannic — seventy if he was a day, bean-sheller, philosopher of windows — hurled his shuttered lamp at the lead man's face and screamed like a fishwife, which was apparently a battle cry in riverport culture, and it worked about as well as anything has ever worked, because the man flinched, and flinching men stumble, and stumbling men go down hard on wet cobblestones.

The second man didn't flinch. He came for Ines with the knife held low and professional, and Ines did the last thing anyone expected, including Ines: they charged *into* him, chest-first, using the weight of the bronze box on their back as a ram. It was not technique. It was not skill. It was a mountain guide's oldest instinct — when the slope moves, move with it or be buried — and it drove the man backward into the alley wall hard enough to crack something, in the wall or the man, Ines honestly couldn't tell and didn't stop to check.

But the knife caught them anyway. A short, hot line across the forearm, shallow, insulting more than wounding, and then the man was sliding down the wall and Ines was standing over him with blood on their sleeve and the fire roaring at their back, and Ines understood, with a strange floating clarity, that they had just hurt someone. Badly. On purpose. First blood in nine years, and it didn't feel like the stories said. It felt like a bill arriving.

"Skiff," Brannic gasped, hauling at Ines's strap. "*Now.*"

They went down the water stairs two at a time, firelight dancing on the black river, and shoved off into the current just as the roof of the safehouse came down in a column of sparks. Behind them, on the stairs, figures gathered and did not follow — no boat, Ines realized. Ondar's people worked from shore tonight. Which meant the river was still open.

Which meant the river was the only thing open.

Ines rowed until the fire was a smudge and the shouting faded, then let the current take the skiff, arm bleeding sluggishly onto the boards, the chest between their feet like a passenger. Brannic sat opposite, breathing hard, dignity thoroughly rumpled.

"Well," the old man said finally. "That's the last time anyone rents from me. Word gets around."

"I'm sorry. Your home—"

"My home was a room above a room. My home is a boat, currently, and you're rowing it badly." He squinted upstream, where the river ran dark toward the hills and the high country and places maps got vague about. "You can't stay on the water, you know. He'll buy boats. He buys everything. You need to go where money doesn't reach."

"Where's that?"

"Upstream." Brannic said it the way other men said *into the volcano*. "Past the ferry lines, past the toll bars, up to where the river starts forgetting it's a river. There's a woman runs cargo up there. Odile Vasse. Ferrymaster. Mean as a snapped cable and honest as gravity, which in my experience is the best combination available." He looked at Ines, at the chest, at the blood. "She won't like you."

"Nobody likes me."

"Good. You'll get along." Brannic settled back, wincing as his back complained. "There's a landing two miles down, on the east bank. She takes on cargo at dawn. Tell her Brannic sent you and that you pay in advance and apologize for the smell."

"What smell?"

"She'll know." He closed his eyes. "Wake me when we're there. And Ines — the man you put down tonight. He'll live or he won't, but either way, carry it light. Heavy guilt sinks boats."

Ines rowed on through the dark, arm throbbing, the first blood drying stiff on their sleeve, and ahead the river ran north into the hills where every rumor said the water began — and where, if half the stories were true, a drowned nation's secrets were still waiting, patient as debt, for someone foolish enough to come collect.

Dawn was two hours off. Odile Vasse was one landing away.

And somewhere behind them, Sef Ondar was already buying boats.
