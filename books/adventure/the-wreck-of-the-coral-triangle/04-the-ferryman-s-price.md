## 4. The Ferryman's Price

Odile Vasse had been running the Marrow for thirty-one years, and in all that time she had learned exactly three things worth teaching: never trust a passenger who apologizes before boarding, never trust one who doesn't apologize at all, and never, under any circumstances, let a man named Sef Ondar stand on her deck.

The first two rules she'd learned from her father. The third she'd learned the hard way, and it was the third rule that made her squint through the drizzle at the woman standing on the Kiln Reach landing stage with a river-locked chest at her feet and soot on her collar.

"That's Dravenmoor," said her deckhand, Pieter, a boy of nineteen who had the observational instincts of a fencepost. "The mountain guide. The blacklisted one."

"I have eyes, Pieter."

"She's got a chest."

"I noticed."

"It's got a seal on it. A funny seal."

Odile lowered her spyglass with the patience of a saint, which is to say she lowered it after gripping it hard enough to leave dents. "A funny seal," she repeated. She raised the glass again.

The woman on the dock was tall in the way of people who had spent their lives being looked up at by mountains — shoulders back, weight even on both feet, hands loose and ready. She wasn't looking around for help. She wasn't looking around for trouble either, which was more telling. She was looking upriver, toward the Gorge of Names, with the expression of someone doing arithmetic they didn't like.

And the chest — Odile's breath fogged the spyglass lens and she wiped it on her sleeve without taking her eyes off it. Bronze, river-stained green at the corners, and stamped on its lid with a device she hadn't seen since she was a girl sitting on her grandmother's knee: a triangle of coral inside a broken circle, worn but unmistakable.

Vhaloria.

"Well," Odile said to nobody in particular. "That's going to be expensive."

* * *

She brought the ferry in slow, because you always came in slow when you wanted leverage, and because the Marrow handled like a drunk ox with opinions. The barge was forty years old, flat-bottomed, ugly as sin, and could carry eleven tons of cargo or sixty passengers or, in emergencies, one disgraced guide and a chest that smelled like the bottom of a drowned country.

"Passage?" Odile called from the wheelhouse, not bothering to hide the once-over she gave the stranger. "I don't run charity. Cash up front, no questions asked, questions cost extra."

"How much extra?"

"Depends how good the questions are."

Something flickered across the woman's face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of where a smile used to live before life evicted it. "Ines Dravenmoor."

"I know who you are. Everyone on this river knows who you are. You're the one who left a client on a mountain." Odile said it flatly, watching. It was an old trick: throw the worst thing you know about a person at them and see what they do with it. Guilty people flinch. Liars argue. Honest ones —

Honest ones go very still, the way this one did, and then say, quietly: "Yes. I am."

Well. That was new.

"Where to?" Odile asked.

"Upriver."

"The whole river ends upriver. Be specific."

Dravenmoor set one boot on the gangplank, testing it, and Odile noted that she put her weight down the way a careful person does — heel first, ready to withdraw. "The source valley."

Pieter dropped a coil of rope. It hit the deck with a slap that echoed off the water like a gunshot.

Odile didn't drop anything, because she was thirty-one years past dropping things, but something in her chest went tight and cold all the same. "The source valley," she said, "is a story grandmothers tell children to keep them away from the gorge. There's nothing there but waterfall and bad air and the bones of fools."

"Then it'll be a short trip," Dravenmoor said. "Name your price."

And there it was — the moment every ferryman lives for and dreads, the moment when the cargo names its own value. Odile leaned on the rail and looked at the chest again. The coral-and-broken-circle seal. Her grandmother's voice rose up out of thirty years of silence: *We were Vhalorian once, little fish, before the maps forgot us.*

Her grandmother had also been a liar, a smuggler, and the finest card player between Kiln Reach and the sea, so Odile took everything she'd ever said with a grain of salt the size of a fist. But the seal was real. And whatever was in that chest had already burned one safehouse behind this woman — Odile had heard the smoke on the morning wind two days ago, and heard other things too, things with engines, moving upriver against the current where nothing moved upriver except trouble.

"Two hundred crowns," Odile said, "and ten percent of whatever's in the box."

Dravenmoor's eyebrows went up. "You haven't asked what's in the box."

"Don't need to. If it's worth nothing, ten percent costs me nothing. If it's worth everything, I want my cut. That's not greed, that's arithmetic." She held out a hand, palm up. "Cash first."

"I have eighty crowns."

"Then you have eighty crowns' worth of passage, which gets you to the bend below the gorge, and you can swim the rest."

They looked at each other across the gap of the gangplank, and Odile watched the woman do sums of her own — not money sums, other kinds. Trust sums. Risk sums. The kind where you add up what you know about a person and subtract what you can afford to lose.

"Sixty now," Dravenmoor said. "Forty at the bend. Ten percent stands."

"Seventy now."

"Sixty-five, and I take a watch shift so your boy sleeps."

Pieter, who had been eavesdropping with all the subtlety of a church bell, straightened up and mouthed *yes please* behind her back.

"Done," Odile said, before she could think better of it, and hated herself a little for how fast she'd said it. "Sixty-five, forty at the bend, ten percent, and you pull your weight. Board. Touch nothing. And if Ondar's people come asking after you, I never saw you, you were never here, and I have never in my life met anyone interesting."

"You know Ondar?"

"Everyone knows Ondar. That's the problem with Ondar." Odile turned to the wheel. "Cast off, Pieter. And stop grinning, it makes your face look broken."

* * *

The Marrow beat her way upriver through the afternoon, past reed banks and rotting pilings and the little shrines fishermen built to saints who had probably never existed. Dravenmoor worked, Odile would give her that. The woman hauled line like she'd been born to it, coiled rope in proper river fashion within an hour of being shown, and asked no questions about anything except the channel markers and the depth of the gorge shallows.

Too few questions. Odile had ferried smugglers, fugitives, tax collectors, one opera singer fleeing three husbands, and a bishop carrying a crate he claimed contained vestments and most certainly did not. Every one of them asked questions. Silence meant a person was rehearsing their answers instead.

At dusk she sent Pieter below and poured two cups of chicory coffee, thick enough to stand a spoon in, and carried one out to the bow where Dravenmoor sat with her back against the chest, keeping watch over it like a dog over a bone.

"Drink," Odile said. "It's terrible, but it's hot."

Dravenmoor took the cup. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Thanking implies a relationship." Odile settled onto a bollard, joints announcing their complaints one by one. "Tell me about the seal."

"No."

"Hm. You bargain harder than my grandmother, and she once talked a customs man into paying *her* duty." Odile sipped. "Fine. Then tell me this: why does a mountain guide, blacklisted in two countries, with eighty crowns to her name and a burned safehouse behind her, want to go to a place that isn't on any map?"

The rain had stopped. The river ran dark and patient under the hull, and somewhere upstream a night bird cried out twice and thought better of it.

"My mother," Dravenmoor said at last. "There's a debt ledger in this chest. Names of people who owed Vhaloria. The last entry is in her handwriting."

Odile kept her face still, which took effort, because her heart had just done something complicated involving her grandmother and a broken circle. "Your mother was Vhalorian."

"I don't know. I thought I knew everything about her. Turns out I knew a woman who made excellent bread and lied about where she came from." Dravenmoor turned the cup in her hands. "She died four years ago. Never said a word about any of this. Now her name's in a dead nation's books and men with gunboats are willing to kill for the paper it's written on, and I'd like to know why before they catch up to me."

"And if the answer is worse than the question?"

"Then at least I'll stop wondering."

Odile nodded slowly. It was, she had to admit, the same reason she'd spent three years and most of her savings hunting down the truth about her father's last run — the one he never came back from, the one everyone told her to let lie. Some debts aren't owed in money. They're owed in answers, and the interest compounds daily.

"Ten percent," Odile said. "Of whatever the source holds. That was our price."

"That was our price."

"Good. Because I'm raising it to twenty."

Dravenmoor stared at her. "You just — we agreed—"

"We agreed before I knew you were Vhalorian blood. That changes the market." Odile held up a hand before the woman could load her indignation. "Hear me out, because I'm only saying this once, and I've never said it to anyone. My grandmother came off those mountains when the maps changed. She had a ring with that seal on it and she threw it in the sea rather than wear a dead country's mark, and she cried doing it, and she never told me why, and she died with the answer in her teeth. So yes. Twenty percent. Not for the treasure, if there is treasure. For the answer. Half yours, half mine, and we split the grief down the middle too, because grief's the part nobody ever budgets for."

The silence stretched long enough that Pieter, below decks, presumably fell asleep, woke up, and fell asleep again.

"Fifteen," Dravenmoor said finally. "And you get the answer about your grandmother whether there's treasure or not. That part's free. That part I promise."

Odile looked at her for a long moment. Rain-wet hair, soot still in the creases of her collar, hands wrapped around a terrible cup of coffee like it was the last warm thing in the world. A person who admitted the worst thing about herself without flinching. A person who bargained honestly and offered the priceless part for free.

Those were the dangerous ones. Those were the ones you ended up liking.

"Fifteen," Odile said, and spat in her palm, and held it out. "Once, Dravenmoor. We trust each other exactly once. After that, everything's earned daily."

Dravenmoor spat in her own palm — clumsy at it, clearly out of practice — and shook. Her grip was strong and dry and steady. "Exactly once."

"Exactly once," Odile agreed, and then, because the moment had gotten entirely too solemn for her taste: "Now get below and sleep. You take the middle watch. And if you snore, the deal's off."

"I don't snore."

"They all say that."

* * *

Odile took the wheel alone through the small hours, as she always did, because sleep and she had reached an understanding years ago that mostly involved avoiding each other. The Marrow pushed upstream through the dark, lamps hooded, past the last lit windows of the last honest town.

It was near dawn when Pieter scrambled up to the wheelhouse, white-faced, whispering like the dark might overhear.

"Skipper. Lights. Behind us. Three of them, low and fast, running without lanterns."

Odile raised the spyglass. Against the last of the stars, three shapes moved against the current — sleek, low, wrong. Gunboats. Nobody ran unlit at night on the Marrow unless they were hunting, and nobody hunted this far upriver unless something worth killing was aboard her boat.

Her boat. Her one thing. Thirty-one years of it.

Behind her, she heard the hatch open, and soft footsteps, and Ines Dravenmoor came to stand beside her at the window, following her gaze to the lights that weren't lights yet but soon would be.

"Ondar?" Dravenmoor asked quietly.

"Who else runs blind in the dark?" Odile spun the wheel, jaw tight, and felt the old familiar cold settle into her stomach — the cold of a gamble beginning. "Congratulations, Dravenmoor. Your trust bought you exactly one day. Let's see if we can make it two."
