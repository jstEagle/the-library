## 6. The Remnant at Kiln Reach

The river narrowed at Kiln Reach the way a throat narrows before it says something important.

Ines saw the town before Odile did — or rather, smelled it. Woodsmoke and wet clay, kilns running hot somewhere behind the terraced banks, and under all of it a faint mineral tang that Ines had learned to distrust: old bronze, old water, old things that had been underwater longer than they should have been.

"Kiln Reach," Odile said, spitting over the rail with the ceremony of a woman performing a sacrament. "Half the boats that stop here come out lighter by one passenger."

"And the other half?"

"Heavier by one debt." She worked the wheel, easing the ferry toward the landing where pilings rose from the shallows like the fingers of drowned men. "You want my advice?"

"No."

"Good. You wouldn't take it anyway." She squinted at the town. "But I'll give it free, since you're paying for the passage. Don't tell anyone what's in your bag. Not the color of it, not the weight of it, not the sound it makes when you set it down. People here can hear a secret through a wall."

"What people?"

Odile's jaw tightened in a way that answered the question before her mouth did. "Vhalorians," she said. "Or whatever they call themselves now that there's no Vhaloria to be from."

* * *

Kiln Reach was built on both banks and neither, its houses stacked up the slopes on stilts and switchback stairs, connected by rope bridges that swayed when anyone so much as thought about crossing them. The kilns themselves were cut into the hillside, brick throats breathing orange heat into the evening air, and everywhere — everywhere — there were marks.

Ines had been trying not to look at them since the chest came out of the river. The seal of Vhaloria: a circle broken by a wave, the way a ring is broken by a finger slipping off a drowning hand. It was stamped on the chest. It was carved above doorways. It was pressed into the clay tiles being hauled up the slope in baskets, fired in the kilns, sold to boat traffic as souvenirs of a country that existed nowhere but in clay and memory.

"They make their own country," Ines said quietly. "Out of roof tiles."

"People do worse," Odile said. "People do much worse. I've ferried most of them."

At the landing, a woman waited who had clearly been waiting for some time. She stood with the stillness of someone who had decided long ago that fidgeting was for other people: gray braid coiled at her neck like a rope with opinions, spectacles fogged from the kiln heat, hands folded over a ledger of her own — smaller than Ines's, older, its corners soft as cloth.

"You're the ferry from downriver," the woman said. Not a question. "And you're the guide who pulled something out of the water at Marlow Bend."

Ines kept her face doing nothing at all. It was a skill, and she'd paid dearly for it. "Word travels fast."

"Water travels faster." The woman extended a hand. "Tam Reyle. Archivist. What's left of us has an archive, and I am what's left of the archivists." Her grip was dry and precise, like a handshake from a filing cabinet. "You have something that belongs to the nation of Vhaloria."

"There is no nation of Vhaloria."

"Yes," Tam said, with the mild patience of a person correcting a child's arithmetic. "That is our national tragedy, not our national status. May I see it?"

"No."

Tam nodded as if this were also correct. "Then may I buy you dinner? Refusals go better fed."

* * *

The meal was served in a room above the largest kiln, where the floor was warm through the boards and the whole building hummed like a sleeping animal. There were perhaps thirty people eating — exiles, Ines supposed, though exile implied you'd left somewhere, and these people had stayed while their country left them. Old men arguing about canal tolls that hadn't been collected in forty years. A girl practicing script on a slate, copying letterforms from a language Ines had only ever seen stamped on bronze.

It was, Ines realized with a discomfort that arrived like a stone dropped into still water, the warmest room she had been inside in eleven years.

"The chest," Tam said, setting down her cup. "Tell me about finding it."

"A river gave it up. Rivers do that."

"This river doesn't. This river keeps." Tam leaned forward. "The Coral Triangle went down with everything Vhaloria had — treasury, records, the state itself, sunk in a single night and called an accident. Everything we are is at the bottom of it. And forty years later, one piece surfaces, and it surfaces *into your hands.*" She let that sit. "Do you believe in coincidence, Guide Dravenmoor?"

"I believe in currents."

"So do we. We've studied them for four hundred years. Currents are how nations drown." Tam removed her spectacles, polished them on her sleeve, and put them back on, and somehow this small ritual made her next words land harder. "There were two hundred and six names on the last page of that ledger. I know because I wrote the copy that went aboard. If even one of those names is legible, then one family in this town gets to stop mourning a rumor and start mourning a fact. That is worth more to us than the gold everyone assumes you're carrying."

Ines looked at the slate with its careful borrowed letters. At the old men arguing about tolls. At the warmth of the floor under her boots, which felt like standing on something alive.

"You're very good," she said. "Has anyone ever handed it over?"

"Once. A man in Corvess. He cried the whole time and asked us to tell him he'd done the right thing." Tam smiled without humor. "We told him yes. We've regretted the honesty ever since; he tells the story at every festival, and now everyone expects to cry."

* * *

Later — much later, after the plates were cleared and the kilns banked low — Tam walked Ines out onto the highest rope bridge, where the town spread below like embers raked across dark water, and made the offer Ines had known was coming since the handshake.

"Stay," Tam said. "Not forever. A season. Teach the young ones the mountains — we have river people and kiln people and not one soul who knows which way is up. And when you're ready, give us the chest. In exchange: a home. Protection. When Ondar's people come asking — and they will come, Guide, brokers like Sef Ondar don't lose a scent — you will be among three hundred people whose entire history is refusing to hand things over."

The bridge swayed. Below, the river ran black and patient.

It would be so easy. That was the thing Ines noticed first, the way you notice a stair missing in the dark — the absence where resistance should be. A bed that wasn't a safehouse. A name that wasn't a blacklist entry. Rooms full of people who would say *Dravenmoor* and mean a neighbor instead of a headline. Eleven years of doors closing, and here was a whole town holding one open, warm air spilling out of it, smelling of bread and clay smoke.

Her mother had taught her that mountains didn't kill people. *Decisions* killed people, usually made at altitude, usually tired, usually wanting the summit too much. And every disaster Ines had ever witnessed started with someone looking at an impossible thing and thinking: *just this once, the easy way.*

"You want the ledger," Ines said slowly, "because it's yours."

"I want it because it's ours," Tam corrected gently. "Yours is a different question. And I think you already know the answer, which is why you haven't opened it past the first page."

Ines said nothing. The night said plenty.

"Here is what I can offer that Ondar cannot," Tam went on. "Passage upstream. Past the checkpoints, past the tolls, past the reach of men who buy silence. Our boats are clay-bellied and slow and nobody searches a funeral barge. Take it as far as you need — the source valley, if that's truly where you're going, and don't bother denying it, the bell-hummers downstream talk faster than the current. All we ask is the chest. One thing, Guide. One country's whole memory, weighed against your freedom of movement."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you leave tomorrow on Odile's ferry, alone, with everything you have and no one to help you keep it." Tam folded her hands again. "I'm not threatening you. I'm describing Tuesday."

The bridge swayed. Somewhere below, the girl with the slate was being called in to supper, and her voice — bright, unbothered, belonging utterly to this place — carried up through the dark like something Ines had lost years ago and never reported stolen.

A home. Offered at exactly the wrong price. Or maybe — and this was the thought that frightened her more — offered at exactly the right one, and that was why her hands wanted to shake.

"I need until morning," Ines said.

"Of course." Tam turned back toward the town, then paused. "For what it's worth, Guide Dravenmoor — whoever packed that chest loved what was in it. Love shows in packing. Even after forty years underwater." She descended the swaying planks without another word.

Ines stood alone on the bridge a long time, the chest heavy in the room behind her, heavier in the thought of staying, heaviest of all in the thought of the source valley waiting upstream like a held breath.

She was still deciding when she heard the engines.

Not Odile's ferry. Not the kiln barges. Something bigger, running dark, coming upriver fast against all sense and all current — and beneath its engine note, faint but unmistakable, the sound of men who had found the scent again.

Down in the town, the first lamp went out. Then the next. Then all of them, all at once, as if Kiln Reach had practiced exactly this.
