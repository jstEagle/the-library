## 7. Ondar's Long Arm

Odile Vasse had a rule about dawn: nothing good ever happened at it. Dawn was for smugglers, tax collectors, and people who wanted something from you before you'd had coffee. So when she woke on the *Marrow's Grace* to the sound of engines — three of them, heavy, running without lights — she was already reaching for her boots before her eyes were fully open.

The river mist lay thick over Kiln Reach, the kind of fog that turned every silhouette into a rumor. Through it came the gunboats: low, black-hulled things with the graceful ugliness of machines built by people who had never once been asked to make something beautiful.

"Up," she said, shaking Ines's shoulder. "Company."

Ines came awake the way mountain people do — all at once, no groggy middle ground, one hand already finding their boots. "How many?"

"Three. Coming up from the downstream bend." Odile was already at the wheelhouse window, squinting into the grey. "They've been sitting out there since midnight. I heard them drop anchor and I told myself it was fishermen. I'm an idiot."

"You're an optimist," Ines said. "It's different."

"It's worse. Optimists die surprised."

Below deck, Tam Reyle was already moving, arms full of the chest's contents spread across his bunk like a man playing solitaire with a nation's debts. He'd hardly slept — none of them had, not really, not after two days in Kiln Reach among the exiles who touched the bronze seal like it was a relic and looked at Ines like it was a miracle. Tam had spent those days copying pages by lamplight, his neat archivist's hand reproducing entries with the patience of a monk.

"They're here for the ledger," he said. His voice was steady, but his hands weren't. "Or for us. Possibly both. The order of operations will tell us how much they know."

"And if they know everything?" Odile asked.

Tam looked up at her, and behind the fear in his face there was that dry Vhalorian humor she was starting to recognize, the kind that had survived forty years of being a people without a country. "Then we're about to have a very educational morning."

* * *

Kiln Reach woke slowly, the way river towns do — first the gulls arguing over the fish racks, then the smell of woodsmoke, then old Marta Kell opening her bakery because some things in this world remained sacred regardless of circumstance. Odile had moored at the town's upstream dock specifically so that any trouble would come from behind her, which was exactly where it now came from, which proved that geography was no substitute for luck.

The first gunboat slid alongside the public pier just as the mist began to lift, and men came off it in a line — six of them, carbines slung but hands resting near, wearing the flat expressions of professionals doing a job they'd done enough times to stop finding interesting. Behind them, on the boat's stern, stood a woman in a grey coat with a clipboard, and Odile felt her stomach drop, because cartels didn't send clipboard women for simple raids. Clipboard women meant inventory. Inventory meant they intended to take everything, catalog it, and leave.

"Ines," she said quietly. "Back door of your situation is closing. What's the play?"

Ines stood at the rail, and Odile watched them do the thing she'd seen them do once before, back at the safehouse fire — go very still, very quiet, the stillness of someone weighing lives on a scale only they could see. She hated that look. She hated it because she recognized it, because she wore it herself every time she chose cargo over passengers, schedule over safety, the hundred small betrayals that kept a ferry captain solvent on a lawless river.

"The exiles can't fight that," Ines said. "Six guns against a town of bakers and net-menders."

"No. They can't."

"So we give them what they want before they start taking it out of hides." Ines turned, and their jaw was set in a way Odile distrusted deeply. "I'll go down. Tell them the chest is mine, I'll hand it over, nobody else gets touched—"

"—and they take the ledger, take you as a bonus, and burn the town anyway for witnesses," Odile finished. "I've dealt with Ondar's people. They don't do clean exits. Clean exits are bad for business — makes people think refusal is survivable."

"Then what?"

Odile looked at her boat. Twenty-two years she'd owned the *Marrow's Grace*, and in that time she'd run grain past toll stations, medicine past blockades, and one memorable season, an entire circus past a customs house that had been bribed by a rival circus. The boat had taken bullets, taken fire, taken a bear. She loved it the way you love a thing that has never once lied to you.

"I run the blockade," she said. "You get Tam and whatever pages matter most. We go upstream through the shallows past the kilns, where their draft won't let them follow until the channel widens. It'll cost us the hull paint and possibly my dignity."

"That's it? That's the plan?"

"You want a better plan or a faster one? Because I can only build one before those men reach the top of the pier."

Down on the dock, the clipboard woman had stopped in front of Marta Kell's bakery, said something, and been handed a roll. She ate it while her men fanned out. Even cartel functionaries, apparently, could not resist Marta's morning batch. Odile filed this away as either useful intelligence or a sign she'd been on the river too long.

* * *

It went wrong, of course. Plans on rivers always went wrong; the river itself saw to it, out of professional jealousy.

They nearly made it. Tam had the ledger wrapped in oilcloth and strapped inside his coat, Ines had the loose pages that mattered — including the one bearing their mother's name, which Odile had watched them handle these past days with the tenderness of someone holding a live coal — and the *Grace* was backing off the dock when the second gunboat came around the upstream point, early, engine muffled, having cut through the reed channels on the far side like it knew them. Which meant someone local had sold the route. Which meant Kiln Reach had a leak, and Odile added that grievance to a list she kept for slow evenings.

"Hard to starboard!" Ines shouted, already hauling the bowline in.

The gunboat's wake caught them broadside and slammed the *Grace* against the pilings hard enough to knock Odile's teeth together. Men poured onto the dock above them. Somewhere a woman screamed — not Marta, thank God — and then the morning stopped being a maneuver and became a riot.

What Odile remembered afterward, in the fragments the mind keeps when it's deciding what you can afford to carry: Tam pushing the ledger into her arms and going back for the master copy he'd left below. Ines lunging after him. A rifle butt catching Ines across the shoulder, spinning them into the rail. Tam standing on the dock with his hands raised, saying, in a clear carrying voice that the whole town would remember, "The archive is not yours to hold," and the clipboard woman saying, mildly, "Everything is ours to hold. That's rather the point of us." And Tam being walked down the pier between two gunmen with his dignity intact and his freedom gone, pausing only to call out — to Ines, to Odile, to the river generally:

"The page! Whatever happens, the page must reach the source!"

Then the third gunboat put a burst across the *Grace*'s bow, close enough that splinters stung Odile's cheek, and the argument moved from persuasion to arithmetic.

She ran the blockade.

Later, people would ask her about it, and she would say it was nothing, a morning's work, the sort of thing any competent captain could do. This was a lie. It took eleven minutes that aged her a year apiece: threading the *Grace* between the two gunboats while they were still tangled around the dock, riding the wake chaos, engine screaming at a pitch she'd hear in her dreams for weeks, the shallows past the old kilns scraping bottom with a sound like the river clearing its throat, Ines beside her at the rail bleeding from a split brow and shouting bearings from memory of a chart they'd studied for exactly one evening. Twice she thought they were done. Once she was certain. And then the channel widened, the water deepened, and the gunboats fell behind, held back by their own tonnage, their engines idling in frustration like dogs at the end of a chain.

Odile eased off the throttle. Her hands were shaking so badly she had to sit on them.

"Well," she said, when she trusted her voice. "That went well."

Ines slid down the wheelhouse wall to the deck, pressed the heel of their hand against their bleeding forehead, and started laughing — the ragged, unlovely laugh of someone whose body needed to discharge something before it curdled. "We lost Tam. We lost the master ledger. We lost your hull paint."

"We lost the paint years ago, don't be dramatic." But the joke died in her mouth, because the count was wrong, and they both knew it. She unwrapped the oilcloth bundle with fingers that wouldn't quite obey. Pages. Some of them. The ones Ines had carried, the ones Tam had thrust at her in the last seconds — but not all. Not the master. And when Ines went through them with trembling hands, page by page, entry by entry, the silence that fell told Odile the answer before Ines said it.

"It's gone," Ines said. "My mother's page. The one in her hand. It was in the master bundle — Tam had it with the copies —" Their voice did something complicated and then went flat, which was worse. "Ondar has it now. Or whoever that woman reports to does."

Odile said nothing. There was nothing clever to say, and she was, despite appearances, capable of recognizing such moments.

They sat in the wheelhouse while the *Grace* drifted with the current, upstream-bound and pilotless, and the mist burned off, and Kiln Reach shrank behind them into a smudge of smoke and gulls. Somewhere back there, Tam Reyle was in a cage or a hold, and a town that had fed them and sheltered them was counting its costs, and a page of paper with a dead woman's handwriting on it was being logged into an inventory by a woman with a clipboard.

"He told me to leave him," Ines said finally. "At the rail. He pushed the ledger at me and stepped back. On purpose."

"Yes."

"He knew they'd take him if he stayed aboard. He made himself the thing they'd catch instead of the thing they wanted."

"Yes," Odile said again, gentler.

"Why?" And there it was under the question, the thing actually being asked: *why does everyone keep paying for my refusal?* The safehouse fire. The ferrymen at the toll station who'd taken a beating covering their escape. Now Tam, and Kiln Reach, and a stolen page. Ines sat with their head in their hands like a person trying to hold water.

Odile considered several answers. *Because they believe in the archive.* *Because they're Vhalorian and stubbornness is hereditary.* *Because people are like that, and it isn't your fault.* All true, probably. None sufficient.

"Because you're not the one choosing," she said instead. "That's the part you keep missing. Ondar chooses to burn towns. Tam chooses to stand on docks. You choose whether to hand over a book. Everyone else owns their own piece of it." She shrugged. "Guilt's a heavy cargo, and it doesn't pay freight. I know. I've hauled it twenty years."

"What are you guilty of, then?" Ines asked, and there was no challenge in it, only exhaustion, and maybe the beginning of curiosity — the look of someone realizing the person steering might be a person.

Odile watched the river for a while. The current here ran smooth and deep, hiding its rocks, which she'd always found honest of it.

"Ask me tonight," she said. "After dark, on the water, everyone tells the truth eventually. River rule." She stood, rolled her shoulders, and put her hands on the wheel. "Right now we have a more pressing problem, which is that I'm the only navigator aboard, my charts are thirty years out of date, and the source valley is upstream through country where the maps stop pretending to know things."

Ines looked up at her, blood drying on their brow, grief and guilt and something harder underneath it all — something that had survived the morning and was, terrifyingly, getting stronger.

"Then teach me the river," they said.

And Odile, who trusted no one, who had agreed to trust this one exactly once and was beginning to suspect the contract had fine print, found herself nodding.

"First lesson," she said, turning the *Marrow's Grace* upstream into the current. "The river always wins. Our job is just to lose slowly enough to get where we're going."

Behind them, faint on the wind, a bell began to ring in Kiln Reach — the old chapel bell, or perhaps another one, calling the town together to count what the dawn had cost. Odile didn't look back. Looking back cost speed, and speed was the only prayer she had left.

But ahead, somewhere past the last honest miles of the map, the source waited. And now it had Tam too.
