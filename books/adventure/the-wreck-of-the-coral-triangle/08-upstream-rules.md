## 8. Upstream Rules

The river above Kiln Reach was a different animal entirely.

Odile Vasse had been running the Sere for thirty-one years, and she could tell you that the lower stretches — all the way from the salt mouth to the Gorge of Names — were a river that *pretended*. It showed you its channels like a shopkeeper showing off his best goods: wide, deep, honest water you could run blindfolded on a moonless night if your engine was strong and your nerve stronger.

Upstream of the Reach, the pretense ended. The Sere up here was a liar.

"See that?" she said, pointing her chin at what looked like a perfectly respectable channel threading between two gravel bars. "That's the Widow's Smile. Looks deep enough to float a cathedral. There's a sandbar under it shaped like a hook, and it will take your keel and keep it." She swung the wheel two points to starboard, and the *Maren's Grace* slid into water that looked worse and ran better. "This one looks like a cattle track. It's nine meters at low water. The river lies to you every day, and it never lies the same way twice."

Ines Dravenmoor stood at her shoulder in the wheelhouse dark, hands loose at their sides, watching the water the way Odile had seen soldiers watch treeline. "How do you know which lie is which?"

"You don't know. You *remember*." She tapped her temple. "Thirty-one years of being lied to. Every channel on this stretch has a name, and most of them are named after somebody who got it wrong."

"That's cheerful."

"It's efficient. Grief makes good navigation marks." She glanced sideways. Ines had a face built for weather — the kind of face that had been rained on so long it had stopped expecting sun — but there was something underneath it tonight, some tired animal thing. They'd run the blockade at dawn, lost Tam Reyle to Ondar's gunboats, watched a page of a dead nation's ledger go out the window of history in a cartel man's pocket, and now it was night and nobody had slept and the wheelhouse smelled of diesel and cold tea. Odile figured she owed herself a little honesty before the river took its next bite.

She killed the running lights at the bend past Heron Shoal — upstream rules: no lights where the current braided, because light drew eyes and eyes drew boats and boats drew bullets — and steered by memory alone, which was to say by love, which was to say by thirty-one years of small corrections made without thinking, the way a mother finds her child's hand in the dark.

"Sit down," she said. "You're making my shoulder nervous."

There was one stool in the wheelhouse, bolted beside the chart table, and Ines took it. In the dark they were mostly voices and shapes, two silhouettes against the faint gray glow of the compass card.

* * *

"Tell me about the mountain," Odile said, when the shoal water had smoothed out under them and the engine had settled into its long patient drone.

"I thought we agreed not to talk about that."

"We agreed not to talk about it while Tam was bleeding in my galley. Tam is asleep. The river is empty. And I just scuttled half my savings outrunning men who want your chest, so I think I've earned the story." She kept her eyes forward. "You don't have to tell me the version you tell strangers. Tell me the true one."

For a long time there was only the engine and the water talking to itself along the hull. Then Ines said, quietly, "Which do you think is the true one? The one where I abandoned a client, or the one where I didn't?"

"The one you haven't told anybody," Odile said. "Those are usually the true ones."

Another silence. Odile let it be. Upstream rules: you don't pull on a rope to see if it holds; you wait and see what it carries.

"There was a storm," Ines said finally. "Late season. I told the client group it was a go/no-go call and I called it no-go. Five of six turned back with me. The sixth — Halloran — he'd paid more than the others, he'd waited two years for the permit, and he said he'd go up without a guide if I wouldn't take him." A breath. "So I went. Halfway. Turned at the col when the wind hit ninety kilometers an hour, and I told him we were turning, and he said he was going on, and I—" The voice caught on something and climbed over it. "I let him. That's the truth nobody prints. I didn't abandon him on the mountain. I abandoned him in the argument. I decided that a man who wouldn't listen wasn't my responsibility anymore, and I walked down, and the storm took him, and the inquiry asked me why I hadn't physically restrained him, and I had no answer that wasn't shameful, so I gave them the answer that was a lie, and the lie got me blacklisted anyway because lies are heavier than the truth, they sink faster."

Odile steered through the dark and thought about that. "What would the truth have gotten you?"

"A trial instead of a blacklist. Maybe prison. Definitely the same dead man."

"And the debt you're paying down — the smuggling runs, the guide work for people who don't ask questions — that's the fine for the lie?"

"That's the interest," Ines said. "The principal I'm still working out how to pay."

Odile nodded, though Ines couldn't see it. "My husband used to say guilt is a currency. You can spend it or you can hoard it, but either way it's yours."

"Is that gentle humor, or do you actually have a dead husband?"

"Dead wife," Odile said. "And it's both. Maren. The boat's hers — well, was ours. She ran this stretch with me for nineteen years. Knew the Widow's Smile better than I did. Used to stand exactly where you're standing and complain that I drove like a drunk." She felt her own throat do the catching-and-climbing thing. "She died four years ago. Fever, nothing romantic, nothing the river had anything to do with. And I kept the boat, and I kept running the river, and everyone in the ports says, ah, Odile, such devotion, such loyalty, and the truth is I don't know if it's devotion or if I just don't know how to stop steering toward the places she isn't."

The compass card glowed. Somewhere ahead an owl worked the far bank.

"So that's mine," Odile said. "Duty to the dead. You carry yours in a ledger. I carry mine in a hull. Same weight, different packaging."

"It's not the same," Ines said. "Your duty is to a memory of someone who loved you back. Mine is to a decision I made wrong."

"Mm." Odile adjusted the wheel a hair, feeling the current lean. "And the woman in the ledger — your mother. Is that duty too? Or is that the other thing?"

"What other thing?"

"Hope." She said it plainly, the way you'd name a sandbar. "You opened that chest and found her handwriting and you haven't put it down since. I've watched you. You sleep holding it like a child holds a lamp. That's not guilt, Ines. Guilt you'd have burned by now, or thrown overboard. That's a person hoping the dead left them instructions."

Ines was quiet long enough that Odile began to regret saying it. Then: "And if they did? If there's something at the source with her name on it — what am I supposed to do, walk into a drowned country and ask my dead mother for permission?"

"People have asked rivers for less," Odile said. "The river usually answers. Just not in words you'll like."

* * *

They came to the Cut-Offs an hour before dawn, and Odile woke Ines — who had fallen asleep upright on the stool, chin on chest, in a posture that would have gotten them laughed out of any guide school in two countries — to show them the trick of it.

"Here," she said, cutting the engine to idle. "This is the part I promised to teach you, so pay attention, because if I go over the side tomorrow you'll need it."

"Don't say things like that."

"Somebody has to. Listen." She pointed into the blackness ahead, where three channels split around a wooded island no bigger than a chapel. "Left channel looks widest. It is widest. It also ends in a logjam two kilometers up that has been there since before Maren died, and the cartel knows it, because it's the obvious route and lazy men take obvious routes. Right channel is shallow but fast, and it dumps you into open water where a gunboat can see you from the Reach. The middle one—" she grinned, and was glad of the dark so Ines couldn't see how much she enjoyed this, "—the middle one doesn't exist."

"It's right there."

"It's on no chart. It floods only in the last week of the thaw month, when the snowmelt pushes the river over its own banks, and it runs behind the island through old timber-cutting sluices dug eighty years ago and forgotten by everyone except me and a dead woman who thought they were beautiful. Nine days of the year it exists. This is day six." She eased the throttle forward. "Welcome to the middle channel. Mind the stumps."

The *Grace* slipped into water narrow enough that the branches on both sides brushed the rail, running smooth and brown and secret between walls of alder, and Odile felt the old joy come up in her like groundwater — the joy of knowing something the map didn't, of being trusted by a river that lied to everybody else. She talked the whole way through, naming the bends, calling the depths from memory, and Ines repeated everything back in that flat guide's cadence, filing it away, and somewhere in the middle of it Odile realized she was doing the thing Maren used to accuse her of: teaching someone the river as if the river were a language, as if learning it meant staying.

Careful, she told herself. You've known this one four days.

But four days was how long she'd known Maren, too, before the fever year, before the nineteen. Time ran strange on the Sere. Trust didn't count in days; it counted in storms shared.

At the far end of the sluice the channel widened, the banks fell away, and the sky ahead had gone the color of pewter. Dawn coming. Odile cut the engine to a whisper and let the current carry them, and in the quiet Ines said, "Why are you doing this? Really. Not the share of the source — I mean the blockade, the scuttling charges, teaching a stranger your secret channel. What's in it for you?"

Odile considered lying, decided against it — upstream rules, she was starting to call them, the rules of water that shows itself honestly only in the dark.

"When they took Tam," she said, "and burned the Reach, I understood something. Ondar's people don't want the ledger. Ledgers are paper. They want the *river*. They want the Sere to be theirs the way it was almost theirs forty years ago, when they sank a whole country in it. My boat, my channels, my widow's smile — all of it, owned by men who've never once been lied to by moving water." She flexed her hands on the wheel. "I've spent my life learning this river's secrets. I'll be damned if I hand them to a man who thinks secrets are inventory."

"So it's duty again," Ines said. "The boat."

"The boat," Odile agreed. "Always the boat."

"And if it comes down to it — if there's a choice between the boat and—"

"Don't finish that question on my deck."

Ines didn't. But the shape of it hung in the wheelhouse between them, gray as the coming dawn, and neither of them pretended otherwise: Odile's duty was the boat, and Ines's was the dead, and both of those duties were headed upstream to the same drowned valley, and rivers, Odile knew, had a way of making tributaries argue about whose water reached the sea.

She was still turning that over — the quiet argument beginning, love and duty circling each other like two currents meeting — when the sound started.

Low. Under the engine noise. Under the water noise. A hum, rising through the hull and up through the soles of her boots and into her back teeth, a sound like a bell being struck very far away and very slowly, once, and then again.

Ines was already on their feet. "Do you hear that?"

"I hear it." Odile's mouth had gone dry. She knew that sound. Every child raised within a hundred miles of the Sere knew that sound, from the stories the exiles told their children at night: the bell of Vhaloria, drowned with its country, that rang when you came near the places the water kept.

It was ringing now. Ahead of them, upstream, in the gray light, faint and patient and getting louder.

"Well," Odile said, and put her hand on the throttle. "Either we've found the source valley early — or something down there knows we're coming."
