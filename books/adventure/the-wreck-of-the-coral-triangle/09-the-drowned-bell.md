## 9. The Drowned Bell

The river changed its mind about us somewhere past the second bend above Kiln Reach.

I'd been on water my whole life in the way mountain people are on water — respectfully, at a distance, the way you're respectful of a dog that's bitten someone. But Odile Vasse treated the Sere like an old argument she intended to win. She stood at her wheel with one boot hooked on a rail cleat, chewing the same unlit pipe she'd been chewing since we'd fled the burning safehouse, and she read the current the way I read snowfields: by what it refused to say.

"Slack water ahead," she said, without looking at me. "Means something underneath is drinking it."

"Something like what?"

"Something like whatever we came up here to find." She spat over the rail, purely out of habit; there was nothing left in the pipe to spit around. "Or a rock. Rivers keep their secrets badly and their rocks worse."

We had been three days upstream from Kiln Reach, running the channels Odile called *the veins* — backwaters and drowned side-sloughs that no chart marked because no chartmaker had ever come back to correct his errors. Tam Reyle sat amidships wrapped in two blankets and one of Odile's spare coats, still gray around the mouth from the beating Ondar's men had given him, but his eyes had recovered first. Archivists, I was learning, kept their eyes in better condition than the rest of them.

He had told us about the forgery on the second night — how the ledger in my chest wasn't Vhaloria's debt book at all but a laundering instrument, a fiction written by the cartel that had sunk the nation, so that the theft of a treasury could be dressed as the collection of legitimate debts. He'd said it gently, the way you tell someone their inheritance is counterfeit.

Except the last page hadn't felt counterfeit. The last page had been in my mother's handwriting — the small, stubborn script she'd used for grocery lists and apology letters, letters that slanted left when she was tired. Forgers could copy a hand. Forgers could not copy the way she crossed her t's like she was settling a score.

So I carried both truths upriver: the ledger was a lie, and my mother had touched it anyway. I turned them over and over like stones in a pocket, waiting for one of them to warm.

The slack water arrived as promised. Odile killed the engine and let the ferry drift, and the silence that came down over us was of a different quality than river silence — denser, listening. Ahead, the valley narrowed into a gorge whose walls went up and up into mist, and the water beneath us went from brown to green to a green so deep it looked deliberate.

"There," Tam said softly.

I didn't see anything. Then I did: a shape below the surface, maybe four fathoms down, pale against the dark — not a rock. Rocks don't have curves like that. Rocks don't have *handles*.

"A bell," Odile said. Her pipe stopped moving entirely, which I would learn was her version of falling to her knees. "That's a channel bell. My grandmother ran this stretch before the maps burned. She used to sing about it — thought it was a song, all nonsense syllables. *Dinna veyle, dinna vey.*" She looked at me. "It wasn't nonsense. It was Vhalorian."

"How do you raise a bell off a riverbed with no crane and no crew?" I asked.

"With a guide," Odile said, "who owes me passage, and an archivist who owes me a share, and a grapple hook I've been meaning to use on something worth the effort."

* * *

Raising the bell took the better part of the afternoon and all of my dignity.

The rig was simple enough: grapple, chain, the ferry's single mast swung out as a derrick, and a block-and-tackle older than any of us. The problem was the cold. This far upriver the water came straight off the glaciers, and it held its temperature like a grudge. I went in twice. The first time was to set the hook, and I came up shaking so hard Odile laughed until she had to sit down on her own wheelbox.

"The famous Dravenmoor," she wheezed, wrapping me in a blanket. "Blacklisted in two countries. Defeated by a bath."

"The water in the Alps," I said through chattering teeth, "was never this personal."

The second dive went better because Tam talked me through it. He stood at the rail and described the bell as he imagined it — cast bronze, Vhalorian foundry marks, probably consecrated in some rite neither of us had words for — and somehow his voice made the dark water feel less like drowning and more like visiting. I got the hook seated in the crown yoke on the third try, and when I kicked for the surface the shape rose with me, shedding silt in slow gray banners, and for one moment underwater I hung beside it face to face.

It was beautiful the way shipwrecks are beautiful: whole, and wrong, and where it should never have been. The bronze was furred with forty years of river growth, but under the fur I could feel lettering, bands of it, ringing the throat of the bell. And there was a mark near the crown — a circle broken by a vertical line. The seal from my chest. The seal of a country that existed now only in metal and memory and, apparently, in the handwriting of dead mothers.

Odile's tackle groaned. The bell broke the surface at dusk, streaming, swinging slow under the derrick, and nobody spoke while they swung it inboard and settled it onto bedded planks in the well deck. It was taller than Tam. When the water finally drained out of its mouth, the last of it fell with a sound like a word being finished.

Then Odile struck it. Once, lightly, with the flat of her knife.

The sound was wrong. That's the only way I can put it. A bell says *dong* and lets go. This one said something and then *kept saying it* — a hum, low, below hearing and then at the edge of it, a tone you felt in your molars and the bones of your wrists. It didn't fade the way sound fades. It faded the way a tide goes out, reluctantly, checking behind itself.

And then, just before it stopped, it changed pitch. Rose slightly. Leaned, if a sound can lean, toward the head of the gorge. Toward upstream.

Toward the source.

"It's a compass," I said.

"It's a relic," Tam said, and his voice had gone careful, the way it did when he was handling facts too hot to grip barehanded. "The bells were tuned to the archive resonance. Every account agrees — the old pilots navigated the last miles by ear. When the hum sharpens, you're close." He looked up at the bell, at the seal, and then at me, and there was something in his face I didn't like, which was hope. "Ines. Do you understand what this means? Your mother may have chosen this bell's route. She may have run this same water."

I understood something else, which I didn't say. I understood that the hum had gone through my chest and found something there that answered it — the ledger, stowed below, answering its country's bell like two instruments tuning to each other. Inheritance or trap. The distinction had been getting thinner every day, and now it was gone entirely.

* * *

We moored that night in a side-slough Odile trusted, willows closing over us like a curtain drawn by someone considerate. She cooked river fish with a brutality of salt that Tam declared, after one bite, to be "a crime against both nations involved." Odile looked pleased.

It was the first evening since the burning safehouse that felt almost like peace, which is why I should have known better.

I was on watch. The bell sat on its planks ten feet away, and I confess I'd been touching it — running my thumb along the lettering band, trying to catch a character here and there through the growth. Tam had identified maybe a dozen glyphs. Most were names. Bells in Vhaloria, he said, weren't cast with saints or kings. They were cast with the names of the people who paid for them, so that every time the bell rang, the whole town rang with it.

My mother's name wasn't on it. I checked twice. I'm not sure whether I was relieved or wounded, and I've decided never to find out.

The hum started at moonrise.

Not from us striking it — nothing had touched it. It simply began, low and patient, the way a kettle begins before anyone admits the water's hot. And this time the pitch leaned harder upstream, insistent, and the hair on my arms stood up, because it sounded less like a compass now and more like a summons. Like something at the head of the valley had noticed the bell was moving again and was calling it home.

"Wake her," I said to Tam.

"I heard it," Odile said from the wheelhouse, already awake, already pulling boots on. Nobody sleeps through that sound. You can't. It isn't loud. It's just *certain*, and certainty travels.

She came out and stood beside me, and we listened together, shoulder to shoulder, our breath fogging in the cold. The hum sharpened another degree. Somewhere in the dark upstream, an echo of it came back — fainter, higher, like a voice answering across a lake.

"That's the archive," Tam whispered. "That's the reservoir singing back. Forty years, and it's still—"

"Still what?" Odile asked.

"Still waiting to be found," he said. "Or still waiting to be finished."

I looked at the bell, and the bell, in its way, looked at me. And the warmth in my chest — wonder, awe, whatever you want to call the feeling of standing next to a miracle — curdled exactly on schedule, because I finally asked myself the question I'd been avoiding since the safehouse:

If the bell calls to the source, and the source answers the bell —

Who else could hear it?

Ondar's men had gunboats. Ondar's men had hydrophones, sonar rigs, salvage crews, and a broker's patience. A sound that traveled through forty years of water and stone to reach my molars did not need my permission to travel anywhere else. We hadn't raised a relic tonight. We'd lit a lamp in a window and hung it out over a dark country full of people who wanted what was behind the glass.

"Odile," I said quietly. "How fast can you run these veins in the dark?"

Her eyes went to the bell, then upstream, then to me, and I watched her do the same arithmetic I'd just done. Her jaw set in that way I was beginning to read fluently — the way it set when she was about to choose something expensive.

"Faster than is wise," she said. "Which is the only speed that matters tonight."

She moved for the wheelhouse. Tam began lashing the bell down, hands quick despite the bruises, murmuring to it in Vhalorian like a man soothing a horse before a storm. I stayed on deck one moment longer.

The hum leaned upstream. Far off, through the gorge, the answer came again — closer now, or louder, or both.

And beneath it, so faint I might have invented it, from downstream — from the direction of everything we'd fled — came a second sound. Not a hum. An engine. More than one.

The past wasn't just calling us in.

It was calling everyone.
