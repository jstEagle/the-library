## 10. Tam's Testimony

Pain, Tam Reyle had learned in his sixty-one years, was a language with many dialects. There was the sharp dialect of the knife, the dull dialect of the bruise, and there was the particular dialect spoken by a man who had been dragged out of a burning river town by strangers and laid on the deck of a ferry that smelled of tar and pickled herring.

He was fluent in all of them. He had been an archivist for the Vhalorian remnant for thirty years, and archivists, he had long ago concluded, were simply people who collected pain and filed it under *later*.

*Later* was now.

Tam opened his eyes to a low ceiling of weathered planks, a lantern swinging on its hook, and a woman with river-grey hair sitting beside him, holding a cup of something that steamed like it had opinions.

"You're awake," she said. "Good. You've been talking in your sleep. Mostly numbers."

"Numbers are what I dream in," Tam croaked. "Where am I?"

"My boat. The *Marenne*. Odile Vasse." She pressed the cup into his hands. "Drink. It's willow bark and honey. The honey is mostly theoretical."

He drank. It tasted like punishment sweetened by hope. Around him, the wheelhouse — no, not the wheelhouse; a converted cargo hold, crates lashed down, a canvas stretched between two hooks as a bunk curtain. Through the gap in the canvas he could see the river sliding past, dark and patient, and at the far end of the hold, sitting on an upturned crate with their elbows on their knees, the guide. Dravenmoor. The one whose name had been in the ledger.

The one whose mother's name had been in the ledger.

Tam's chest tightened, and not only from the cracked ribs. He had seen the page. He had spent two days in Ondar's custody watching men who smelled of engine oil and cheap tobacco photograph it, copy it, argue over it. And in those two days, in the intervals between the questions and the worse-than-questions, he had done what archivists do. He had read. He had compared. He had noticed.

And what he had noticed was going to break this quiet woman's heart, or mend it, or both, and Tam Reyle had never been good at delivering either kind of news.

"How bad?" Dravenmoor asked. Their voice was flat, careful, the voice of someone who had learned to ask about injuries because it was safer than asking about anything else.

"Two ribs cracked, probably three," Tam said. "A concussion I'll deny having. They took my glasses, which is a crime against scholarship, but I can see you well enough. You have your mother's chin."

Dravenmoor went very still. "You knew her?"

"I knew *of* her," Tam said gently. "Everyone in the remnant knew of her. Maren Dravenmoor. Assistant conservator, then chief conservator, then—" He stopped himself. "Later. First I need to tell you what they did to me, because it isn't really about me at all."

Odile snorted from her post by the little iron stove. "Archivists. You'd schedule your own funeral and insist on reading the minutes aloud."

"I would insist on *accurate* minutes," Tam said with dignity, "which is more than can be said for anyone else in this story."

* * *

He told it slowly, the way he told everything, because speed was how lies traveled and truth preferred to walk.

"They took me at dawn," he said. "I heard the gunboats before I saw them — Ondar's engines have a cough, a dry rattle at idle, like a man clearing his throat before lying. I got the master index out through the kiln chute, into the clay pit, but they had the ledger page already. Dravenmoor's page. They'd photographed it during the night, I think, from the informant inside Kiln Reach. I never learned who. I suspect I sat across a table from them at festival dinners for years."

"The informant," Odile said. "You're sure there was one?"

"Certain. They knew which floor my office was on. They knew I kept the second key in my left boot. Only four people alive knew that." He held up fingers. "Me. Our elder, Sefa. My apprentice, who is twelve and cannot keep a secret longer than a fish keeps water. And the man who made the boots."

"A bootmaker betrayed us," Odile said. "There's a ballad in that nobody will want to sing."

"They didn't beat me much, at first," Tam went on. "That's the thing I want you both to understand. They didn't need to. Ondar wanted me *cooperative*, not broken. A broken archivist sulks. A cooperative one files things correctly." He smiled, thin and bitter. "So they gave me work. That was the torture, if you want the truth of it. They gave me the thing I love most and asked me to do it badly."

Dravenmoor leaned forward. "What work?"

"Authentication." Tam let the word sit in the swaying lantern light. "They had documents. Boxes of them. Debt instruments, treasury notes, transfer records — forty years old, supposedly. Paper from the last days of Vhaloria, when the state was drowning and its creditors were circling. Ondar's employers have spent four decades trying to make those debts legally real, so they can claim salvage rights, mineral rights, archive rights — the whole drowned inheritance. But paper needs provenance. Paper needs an expert to say: yes, this is genuine. Yes, this debt was truly owed."

"And they wanted you to say it," Odile said.

"I said it," Tam admitted. His hands, wrapped around the cooling cup, had begun to shake, and he watched them shake with a scholar's detached interest, as though they belonged to a specimen. "I authenticated every document they put in front of me. Signatures, seals, watermarks, the lot. I told them the ink oxidation was consistent with the period. I told them the rag content of the paper matched Vhalorian mill stock. I was very thorough. I was very convincing." He looked up. "Because every single word was true."

Silence, except for the river and the tick of the stove.

"That's the part I need you to hear," Tam said, and now his voice dropped, the way it did in the archive when he approached the locked cases. "The documents are genuine. The *paper* is genuine. Forty-year-old Vhalorian bond paper, real seals, real signatures. Ondar's employers didn't forge the documents. They forged the *ledger*."

Dravenmoor's breath caught. "Explain."

"The debt ledger in your bronze chest — the one listing what Vhaloria owed, entry by entry, name by name. It's written on genuine period paper in genuine period ink. But it wasn't written in the last days of Vhaloria. It was assembled afterward. Recently. By people who had access to authentic blank pages, authentic ink formulas, and a genuine sample of the original secretary's hand." He paused. "They had something better than a forger, Dravenmoor. They had a template. The original ledger existed — the true one — and they copied its format, its abbreviations, even its errors, and then they added entries. Entries that transferred real obligations onto new names. Names of people who could be found. Who could be *collected from*."

"And my mother's entry," Dravenmoor said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"May be one of the additions," Tam said. "Or may not. That's the cruelty of it. I examined that page for eleven hours while they slept, by memory, because they'd taken my glasses and I know that ledger's format the way you know your own heartbeat. The hand is right. The ink is right. The entry sits exactly where the original secretary would have placed it, in sequence, with the correct running total carried forward." He shook his head slowly. "It's perfect. Which is precisely why I don't believe it."

Odile frowned. "Come again? Perfect means fake?"

"Perfect means *made by someone who cared whether it fooled an expert*," Tam said. "The original ledger has errors. Small ones. The secretary transposed figures twice in the final month — grief does that, she was writing while the country was going under, and her totals drift by a few units in three places. Any honest copy reproduces the errors, because a copyist copies what they see. But the forged entries don't just avoid the errors — they *correct* them. The running totals in the forged section are arithmetically cleaner than the originals. Someone reconciled the books." He laughed, short and awful. "Nobody reconciles the books while their nation drowns. Somebody reconciled them later, at a desk, with time and coffee and a mandate."

"So the forgery proves itself," Dravenmoor said slowly, "by being too good."

"By being too *tidy*. Grief is messy. Theft is tidy." Tam set the cup down. "But here is where I must be honest with you, because honesty is the only asset I have left and I intend to spend it carefully. The perfection tells me the page *could* be forged. It doesn't tell me it *was*. There is one other possibility, and I've turned it over all night like a stone looking for what's underneath."

"Say it," Dravenmoor said.

"That your mother wrote that entry herself. Truly. In her own hand, in the true ledger, before the end. And that Ondar's people found the true ledger, copied it faithfully — errors, corrections, everything — and used it as their template. In which case the page in your chest is a forgery of a truth, and the only way to know which is to compare it against the original." He met Dravenmoor's eyes. "And the original is in the source valley. Under the lake. In the archive your mother helped drown."

* * *

The lantern swung. Somewhere above them, water slapped the hull in a rhythm like slow applause.

Dravenmoor stood, walked to the gap in the canvas, and stood looking at the river for a long time. Tam watched the set of those shoulders — a guide's shoulders, built for carrying loads — and thought: *there goes a person deciding how much weight to pick up.*

"You're telling me," Dravenmoor said finally, without turning, "that the one thing I've been carrying since the river gave me that chest might be counterfeit. That my mother's handwriting — the last thing I have of hers — might be a stranger's imitation of it. And that the only proof either way is at the bottom of a lake guarded by the same cartel that's chasing us."

"That is a fair summary," Tam said. "Though I'd add that the imitation, if it is one, was made from love of accuracy. Whoever copied her hand studied it closely. Spent hours. Perhaps weeks." He hesitated, then said the harder thing. "Whoever forged that page knew her writing intimately. That narrows the list of suspects considerably. And most of the names on that list are dead, or were in the valley at the end, or—"

"Were *in the valley*," Dravenmoor repeated. They turned. Their face in the lantern light was unreadable, which Tam recognized as its own kind of eloquence. "You're saying the forger might have been there. At the sinking. One of the people who chose to drown it."

"I'm saying the evidence points toward the archive itself," Tam said. "Which is either the worst news you've ever received, or the best, depending on what you find when we get there."

"*When*," Odile said from the stove, and there was something in her voice — not quite surprise, more like a bet settling. "Listen to him. *When.* An hour ago you were a passenger with cracked ribs, Reyle."

"I was a hostage with cracked ribs," Tam corrected. "Passengers get to disembark. Now — may I ask a question of my own? I've been answering yours since I woke."

Dravenmoor nodded.

"The page they stole from me at Kiln Reach. Your mother's page. Why?" Tam leaned forward, wincing as his ribs objected. "Think about it. Ondar has the template. Ondar's employers have the boxes of genuine paper. If the forged ledger exists in copies, they hardly need one more. Unless—" he raised a finger, the pedagogue's gesture that thirty years of students had come to dread, "—unless that page is the one thing they *don't* control. Unless your mother's entry is the loose thread. The one entry they couldn't verify, or couldn't reproduce, or couldn't afford to let anyone examine too closely."

"Because it's real," Dravenmoor said.

"Because it's real," Tam agreed. "Or because it's the flaw in the forgery. Either way, they wanted it back badly enough to burn a town for it. People don't burn towns for paper unless the paper is dangerous." He smiled ruefully. "Believe me. I have devoted my life to dangerous paper, and I have never once burned anything down over it. Though there was an incident with a candle in '81 that the elder still brings up at festivals."

Despite everything, Odile barked a laugh. Even Dravenmoor's mouth twitched, briefly, like a fish testing the surface.

"There," Tam said, satisfied. "That's the first thing any of you have believed without needing provenance. Hold onto that. You'll need the practice."

* * *

Later — after Odile had fed them all a stew of fish, onions, and optimism, and Tam had been reinstalled in his bunk with his ribs bound in river linen — Dravenmoor came and sat on the crate beside him in the dark.

The boat ran on. Odile's silhouette moved behind the wheelhouse glass, steady as a metronome.

"My mother left when I was nine," Dravenmoor said, to the darkness rather than to him. "No note. No explanation. Just gone, and everyone said she'd run off, and I believed it, because believing it was simpler than the alternative. Then a river hands me a chest with her name in it, and now you tell me she might have been an archivist who sank a nation. Or a fiction written by thieves. I don't know which version of her to grieve."

"Then grieve neither yet," Tam said. "Grieve the uncertainty. It's the only version you can be sure of." He shifted, gasped, continued. "When we reach the gorge — the canyon with the names carved in it, two days upstream — look for her there. The exiles chisel the names of the lost as they confirm them. If Maren Dravenmoor is on that wall, the wall will tell you when her name was cut. Fresh stone or old. The rock keeps better records than paper does. Rock doesn't lie; it only erodes, and erosion takes decades."

"And if her name is fresh?"

Tam was quiet a moment. The lantern had been turned low, and in its ember-light his face looked like something excavated.

"Then she died recently," he said, "and recently enough that someone who loved her — or owed her — put her name there. And either answer means the same thing, Dravenmoor: she was alive far longer than you were told, and close enough to the end of this story to leave you a message. Forged or true, that page in your chest is a letter. We're going upstream to find out what it says."

Dravenmoor was silent for a long moment. Then: "You sound like you believe we'll actually get there."

"I'm an archivist," Tam said, settling back against the hull with a wince and a small, stubborn smile. "My entire profession consists of believing that things survive when they have no earthly reason to. Countries sink. Archives flood. Cartels burn towns." He closed his eyes. "And still the paper gets through. Usually carried by someone with cracked ribs and no glasses, on a boat that smells of herring. The record always finds its reader. It's the one law of history I've never seen broken."

Above them, faint through the deck planks, the river murmured on toward the gorge — toward the wall of names, and whatever fresh-cut stone was waiting there with Dravenmoor's family in it.
