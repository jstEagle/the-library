## 11. The Gorge of Names

The river narrowed the way a throat narrows before a scream.

For two days Odile had threaded them through channels she named like old friends — the Widow's Elbow, the Sighing Narrows, a stretch she called simply "the Bad Manners" because, she said, the current there shoved you around without apologizing. The bell rode lashed to the skiff's mast, wrapped in sacking, and every few hours it hummed through the canvas like a sleeping animal dreaming of somewhere else.

"Quieter now," Tam said on the morning of the third day, one hand resting on the wrappings. "That means we're close. It only sings when it remembers where it's going."

"Everything sings when it remembers where it's going," Odile said from the tiller. "Boats do it too. You get used to ignoring it or you go mad."

Ines said nothing. They had been saying very little for three days, which Odile had noticed and Tam had noticed and Ines knew they both had noticed, and which made the silence heavier rather than lighter. There is a particular loneliness in being watched kindly by people who are waiting for you to break.

The canyon announced itself first by sound. The water slowed and deepened, going green-black, and the echoes changed — instead of bouncing off banks they climbed, rising up and up, and when Ines looked up to find out what they were climbing toward, they forgot, briefly, how to breathe.

The walls went up forever. Grey stone streaked with white mineral tears, so tall that the sky became a ribbon, so tall that the morning sun would touch the top of the eastern wall hours before it ever reached the water. And carved into those walls — not painted, not inlaid, *carved*, cut deep enough to catch shadow and hold it — were names.

Thousands of names. Tens of thousands. They began at the waterline and marched upward in careful columns, in scripts Ines half-recognized from the ledger's older pages, in alphabets that must have died with the country that invented them. Some names were small as a thumb. Some were taller than a man. The whole gorge was a wall of grief built by people who had refused to let the water have the last word.

"Oh," Tam said softly. It was not a word so much as a wound opening.

"The Gorge of Names," Odile said. Her voice had lost its usual dryness; it sounded scoured. "My grandmother talked about it. I always thought she was exaggerating." She brought the skiff alongside a shelf of rock that jutted out like a lower lip, and killed the engine, and for a while none of them moved, because moving felt like interrupting a funeral.

It was Tam who stepped off first, onto the shelf, boots crunching on gravel worn smooth by forty years of pilgrims. "They come here," Tam said. "The remnant. Every family that could make the journey. They add names." A hand rose, trembling slightly, toward the wall. "When someone dies in exile, they don't bury them under ground. They bring the name home and cut it here. So the dead can be Vhalorian even if the living never can."

"How many?" Ines asked. Their voice came out rough.

"No one counts anymore. Counting stopped being possible around year nine."

* * *

They walked the shelf slowly. The bell, left on the boat, hummed low and constant now, a note you stopped hearing and then suddenly heard again, like your own heartbeat.

Odile drifted along behind, hands in her pockets, reading names the way other people read gravestones — with her lips moving. Once she stopped and touched a column of small letters and didn't explain, and Ines didn't ask, because some things you carry alone or not at all.

Tam, though, walked with purpose. Tam had a destination. Ines watched them scanning the wall with an urgency that finally resolved itself into something worse than grief.

"It should be here," Tam said. "Near the waterline. The early ones, the ones lost in the sinking itself — they're all down low, where the families could reach without ropes." They moved faster. "Reyle. My family name. My father cut it himself, the year after. He carried the chisels forty miles upriver on his back because he wouldn't trust them to a cart." Tam laughed, a broken little sound. "He said a cart might drop them. As if his back couldn't."

And then Tam stopped.

Ines saw it before Tam spoke — saw the stillness, the particular quality of a person looking at something they cannot yet believe. Then Tam raised one hand and laid it flat against the stone, against a name cut into the wall at chest height, in clean modern lettering among all the ancient scripts:

**MIRA DRAVENMOOR**

The world went very quiet. The river kept moving. The bell kept humming. Ines stood in the middle of all of it, unable to do either.

"That's—" Tam turned. "Ines. That's your mother's name."

"I can read," Ines said, and hated how flat their voice came out, how it sounded like a man holding a door shut against a flood.

Because it wasn't just the name. It was the *stone*. Every other carving on this wall wore forty years of weather — edges softened, shadows gentled, lichen creeping into the deepest cuts like green fingers. This name was sharp. This name was pale, raw stone showing through like new skin. The chisel marks were crisp as yesterday.

Someone had cut Mira Dravenmoor's name into the Gorge of Names recently. Within the last few years. Perhaps within the last few months.

"She died eleven years ago," Ines said. Each word placed carefully, like footing on rotten ice. "I buried her. I dug part of the grave myself because the ground was frozen and the man with the machine wanted double."

"Then who cut this?"

"I don't know." But even as they said it, Ines's eyes were doing what eyes do — measuring, comparing, falling back on the old guide's habit of reading terrain for truth. The height of the cut. The angle of the strokes. The way the letters leaned slightly left, the way the crossbar of the R hooked deeper than any of the surrounding carvings.

Ines knew that hand. Not the handwriting — the *chiselwork*. Their mother had been a stonemason before she'd been anything else, before the drowning, before the ledger, before the silence that had filled the last twenty years of her life like water filling a hull. Ines had grown up watching those hands. Had learned to hold a chisel watching those hands. Had been told, more than once, *you carve like you climb, Ines — too fast, trust the stone.*

This was slow work. Patient work. Work done by someone who trusted the stone completely.

"This isn't fresh-fresh," Ines heard themselves say, from a great distance. "Look at the tool marks. There's weathering in the cuts — a season, maybe two. Someone came here last autumn and cut my dead mother's name into a wall of the drowned, and then left."

"Why?" Odile had come back down the shelf. She was looking at the name too, and her face held none of its usual irony. "Names go up here when someone dies, Tam said. Your mother died eleven years ago. Why wait?"

"Unless," Tam said quietly, "she didn't die eleven years ago."

The sentence hung there. Ines let it hang, because grabbing at it felt like grabbing at a rope over a drop — you might catch it, or it might take your arm off.

"My mother was a quiet woman," Ines said at last. "Quiet the way deep water is quiet. She kept a house in Corvane for thirty years. She grew beans. She mended nets for the neighbors and never once, not once, mentioned Vhaloria. When I found the seal on that chest, I thought — I thought maybe she'd fled the sinking as a child. A refugee. That the debt ledger was some old obligation her family never escaped." Ines's jaw tightened. "I built a whole story out of almost nothing, because the story was kinder than no story."

"And now?" Odile asked.

"Now I'm standing in front of proof that somebody loved her enough to lie about when she died." Ines reached out and put their fingers into the chisel cuts. The stone was cold. Stone is always cold; that's the first thing it teaches you. "Or proof that she was never who I buried."

* * *

They made camp that night on the wide shelf, because the channel above the gorge ran shallow in the dark and Odile refused to risk the hull. Tam built a small fire in a ring of stones that generations of mourners had blackened before them, and the flames threw their light up the wall, and the names caught it, thousands of them, flickering like a city seen from far away at night.

Ines sat apart, the way they always did, and found that Odile had sat down next to them anyway, the way she always did.

"You've got the look," Odile said.

"What look?"

"The look of a person doing arithmetic with their whole life." She passed him a tin cup of tea that tasted mostly of pine needles. "Go on, then. Say it out loud. It's smaller once it's outside."

Ines turned the cup in their hands. Above them, the firelight climbed the wall and died.

"When I was nine," they said, "my mother took me up a mountain. Not a working trip — she wasn't a guide, she was a mason, she had no business above the treeline. But she wanted to show me something, and she wouldn't say what. We climbed two days. On the second night she woke me before dawn and made me sit on a ridge and watch the sun come up over a valley full of cloud, and she said—" Ines stopped. The memory arrived whole, unbidden, in her voice, in her accent, which had never quite matched the town they lived in. *One day you'll understand why some things have to stay under.* "She said some things have to stay under. And I thought she meant the valley. I thought it was a poem. Mothers say things like that."

"And you think now—"

"I think she was looking at water," Ines said. "I think she was looking at a lake, or thinking about one. I think my mother spent thirty years growing beans and mending nets and keeping a secret the size of a country, and I lived in that house, Odile. I ate at her table. And I never once saw it." Their voice cracked on the last word, and they were glad the fire was low and the dark was thick. "What kind of child doesn't see that? What kind of guide reads every slope and every sky and misses the avalanche in their own kitchen?"

"The kind who was loved," Odile said simply. "People hide things from the ones they love best. Strangers get the truth because strangers can't be hurt by it." She drank her terrible tea. "My grandmother hid a whole language from me. Forty years I thought she muttered to herself. Turns out she was praying in Vhalorian. I found out at her funeral, from a woman I'd never met, who kissed my hands and called me by a name I didn't know was mine." She shrugged. "Families are just secrets that learned to share a roof."

Ines huffed — half a laugh, half something else. "You're very wise for a smuggler."

"I'm very drunk on pine needles for a smuggler. Different thing entirely."

Across the fire, Tam lay wrapped in a blanket, awake, staring up at the wall. Ines watched them for a while.

"Tam," they called softly. "Who else comes here? Recently. Who could have cut that name?"

Tam sat up slowly. "Pilgrims come in autumn, after the rains, when the shelf is dry. Small groups. The remnant keeps a list — a caretaker walks the wall each spring and records new carvings." A pause, and Ines heard the thought arrive in Tam the same moment it arrived in themselves. "A new carving would be recorded. A name added eleven years late, by an outsider, by a foreigner—" Tam's voice dropped. "The archivists would have flagged it. They would have *asked questions*. Whoever cut your mother's name either answered those questions—" 

"—or was someone the archivists already knew," Ines finished.

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere high above, wind moved through the canyon and the whole wall of names seemed to sigh, ten thousand voices exhaling at once.

"There's one place ahead of us," Tam said, barely audible. "The source. The archive. If anyone survived the sinking who knew your mother — if anyone could tell us who she really was, and why her name went up on this wall four decades late — they're there. Under the lake."

"Then we keep going," Ines said.

It came out steadier than they felt. Odile glanced at them, and Ines met her eyes, and something passed between them that neither of them would have put into words — the thing that had been passing between them for days now, quiet as the bell's humming, patient as the current.

"You understand," Odile said, "that every mile upstream, this stops being a salvage job and starts being a pilgrimage. You're not chasing a chest anymore. You're chasing your mother."

"I know."

"And when you find her — the real her, the one under thirty years of beans and nets — she might not be someone you like."

Ines looked up at the wall, at the raw pale letters catching the last of the firelight. *Mira Dravenmoor*, cut by a hand Ines had known since childhood, by someone who had climbed forty miles of gorge to tell the world — or whoever came asking — that she belonged here. Among the drowned. Among the chosen dead of a nation that no longer existed.

Which meant she hadn't fled Vhaloria.

Which meant she had been *there*. At the end. On purpose.

"I'll take that chance," Ines said.

* * *

They slept in shifts, as they had since Kiln Reach. Ines took the middle watch, sitting with their back against the skiff's hull, the bell's warmth humming faintly through the wood against their spine like a second heartbeat.

It was near the end of their watch when they heard the engine.

Far downstream — miles, maybe, but the gorge carried sound the way a cupped hand carries a whisper — a motor turned over, throttled, and settled into a low, patient idle. Then a second one joined it. Then a third.

Ines rose silently and climbed the shelf to the gorge mouth, and looked back the way they had come.

Downriver, small against the stars, three sets of running lights moved up the channel. Steady. Unhurried. Boats that knew exactly where they were going, because the bell had been singing to them too.

Ines stood in the dark between the sleeping camp and the coming lights, and did the arithmetic one more time. Two hours, maybe three, before the lights reached the shelf. One shallow channel ahead. One bell that couldn't stop singing.

They went back down and put a hand on Odile's shoulder.

"Wake up," Ines whispered. "We have company."
