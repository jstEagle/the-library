## 12. Odile's Anchor

The *Marrow's Grace* had a voice, and Odile Vasse knew every word of it.

Forty-one years on the river, and she could tell you what the hull was saying just by the pitch of the water against her planks. There was the easy murmur of slack current, the gossip-rattle when debris scraped the keel, the low groan she made in cold water that Odile had long ago decided was complaint rather than damage. Tonight, running dark above the Gorge of Names with her lamps shuttered and her engine at half-sigh, the old ferry was whispering one thing over and over:

*They're behind us.*

Odile felt it before she heard it — a change in the wake, a wrongness in the way the river folded back on itself. She stood at the wheel with her hands light on the spokes and listened the way other people prayed.

Behind her, in the wheelhouse's one chair, Ines Dravenmoor was asleep sitting up, which was a skill Odile suspected came from years of sleeping badly in places where waking up mattered. The ledger sat on their knees like a cat that had chosen them and would not be argued with. Even asleep, Ines kept one hand flat on it.

Odile looked at them for longer than was strictly professional.

This was the trouble with picking up strays. You fed them once, you let them steer through the narrows once, you traded confessions in the dark once — and then some traitorous organ in your chest started doing arithmetic it had no business doing. *One boat. One life. One person who looks at the river the way I do.* The sums were simple. The answers were not.

The bell they'd raised two days ago lay wrapped in canvas under the cargo netting amidships, and even muffled, Odile swore she could feel it humming through her boot soles — a sound below sound, like a hymn sung by a drowned congregation. It hummed louder as they went upstream. Toward the source. Toward whatever Ondar wanted badly enough to burn a safehouse and take a hostage for.

And tonight, toward them.

*There.* Not a sound this time but a light — a single red point far downstream, winking twice, pausing, winking twice again. Signal lanterns. Odile knew the grammar of that code from her smuggling days: *vessel ahead, prepare to board.*

"Dravenmoor." She said it quietly, then less quietly, then put her boot against the chair leg and rocked it until Ines came up out of sleep like a diver surfacing — fast, silent, already reaching for something sharp.

"How long?"

"Ten minutes, maybe twelve. Two boats, I think. Maybe three." Odile nodded at the red wink astern. "That's Ondar's manners. He knocks."

Ines was fully awake now, doing the math Odile had already done. "We can't outrun them. Not loaded, not at night, not with the gorge bends coming."

"No," Odile agreed. "We can't."

"We could run her aground. Go into the trees—"

"And leave the *Grace* to them? They'd strip her to the rivets by morning and burn what wouldn't come loose." Odile heard her own voice go flat and hard, the voice she used on passengers who tried to bring livestock into the cabin. "No."

"Then we fight." Ines was already moving toward the locker where the flare gun lived. "Tam's still weak but he can hold a lamp, we've got the flare pistol, the gaff hook—"

"Dravenmoor." Odile caught their sleeve. "Come look at my boat."

* * *

She took them down into the hold, past the coiled rope and the salt barrels and the bell humming its drowned lullaby, to the aft bilge where the *Marrow's Grace* kept her oldest secret.

"This is Marrow," Odile said, resting her palm on a steel plate bolted over the keel seam. "My husband. Forty-one years married to a ferry, and people still ask why I never remarried."

Ines blinked at the plate. "You named a valve?"

"I named an *arrangement*. Marrow's a sea-cock I had installed the year I bought her, back when the cartel was squeezing every independent boat off this river and I wanted to know — in my bones, in my *keel* — that there was one thing on this vessel nobody could ever take from me without my permission." She tapped the wheel mounted on the plate. "Turn this, and the *Grace* opens her belly to the river. Ten minutes, maybe less, and she's on the bottom in six fathoms of cold black water."

Silence. Somewhere above them the red light winked, closer now.

"You built your own boat a self-destruct button," Ines said slowly.

"I built her a *dignity*. There's a difference, and it matters." Odile found she couldn't look at the plate anymore. "Every man who ever tried to board me uninvited, I told them about Marrow. Just telling them was usually enough. Nobody wants to rob a woman who's willing to sink her own ship with herself aboard." A dry laugh escaped her. "It's been forty-one years of insurance I never had to use. And now—"

"Now you're thinking about using it."

"I'm thinking about what's aboard worth more than the boat." Odile finally turned and looked at Ines straight on, which cost her more than any boarding party ever had. "A ledger full of names. An archivist who took a bullet for it. And one stubborn, blacklisted, beautiful disaster of a guide who thinks debt is something you pay with your whole life."

"Don't—" Ines started.

"Don't what? Don't say true things?" Odile held up a hand. "Save it. If we live through tonight, you can argue with me about adjectives. Right now I need to know if you'll help me work the pumps, because scuttling her here means swimming for shore, and the shore on this stretch is a mile of gravel and cold."

Ines stared at her. "You're really going to do it. Sink forty-one years."

"I'm going to do what the boat would want." Odile patted the hull, and the hull groaned, and she chose to believe it was agreement. "She was born to carry things past men who'd stop her. That's all she's ever done. Sinking to finish the job isn't losing. It's the last delivery."

Above them, faint but unmistakable, came the sound Odile had been dreading: an engine dropping to idle, close. Very close. Then another.

"They're alongside," Ines said.

* * *

What followed happened in pieces, and afterward Odile would remember it in pieces too, the way rivers remember floods — not as a story but as a series of violent gifts.

First: the grapnel, biting into the rail with a sound like a tooth breaking.

Second: Tam Reyle, propped in the cargo netting with the flare pistol in both hands because his legs wouldn't hold him, saying with the mild courtesy of a lifelong archivist, "I should warn you gentlemen that I have catalogued four hundred and eleven varieties of unpleasant death, and I am prepared to improvise a four hundred and twelfth."

Third: boots on deck. Four men, five, lanterns swinging, and the big one in front — Ondar's bosun, a slab of a man called Ferro whose face Odile remembered from a bad year a decade ago — grinning around a knife held in his teeth like a rose.

"Well," Ferro said around the blade. "The widow Vasse. Ondar says hello. He says you can keep your life and your guide if the chest stays."

"The chest stays with us," Ines said, stepping forward, and Odile saw them plant themselves between Ferro and the netting where Tam lay, and thought, *oh, you idiot, you brave magnificent idiot,* and reached for the gaff hook.

Fourth: everything at once.

Ferro lunged; Ines met him with the boathook and the two of them went across the deck in a tangle. A second man grabbed Odile's arm and she broke his grip the way you break a chicken's — quick, downward, unloving — and drove her elbow into his throat. Tam fired the flare pistol wildly and set the cargo netting smoldering, which lit the whole deck in hellish pink and did more damage to the boarders' nerve than to anything else. Someone shouted. Someone else slipped in fish brine. The bell, jostled, hummed a note that made every man on deck flinch like they'd heard their own name called from underwater.

And Odile, pinned against the rail with a knife at her collarbone and Ferro's breath in her face, heard him say, sweetly, "Last chance, widow. Where's the chest?"

"It's aboard," she said. "Everything I own is aboard. Everything I *am* is aboard."

"Then hand it up."

"Ferro," Odile said, and her voice came out calm as a tide table, "do you know what's directly beneath your feet?"

He frowned. Beneath them, muffled by planking and forty-one years of paint, the bell hummed on.

"Six fathoms of river," Odile told him, "and a valve I'm turning right now."

His eyes dropped to her hand — to the small brass wheel she'd gripped through the rail stanchion, down at the end of the rod she'd had installed the same week she'd buried her husband, the rod that ran down through the deck into the dark of the hold, into the heart of the *Marrow's Grace*.

He understood one heartbeat too late.

Odile turned the wheel.

* * *

The sound the *Marrow's Grace* made when she opened was not dramatic. That was the thing Odile would carry longest. No explosion, no scream. Just a deep, unhurried gulp, like an old woman taking the last sip of tea, and then the deck tilting, and the river coming up through the seams everywhere at once, patient as arithmetic.

The boarders lasted less time than the boat. Ferro went over the rail with a shriek that turned into splashing and then into distance. His men followed, some diving, some falling, all of them suddenly experts in the geography of the nearest gunboat. Odile stood at the rail — her rail, tilting now, the lanterns guttering in the wash — and watched them go, and felt nothing she'd expected to feel. No triumph. No grief, even. Just a strange, enormous quiet, like the moment after a bell stops ringing when the air is still shaped like the sound.

"Ines!" she shouted. "Tam! Off, both of you, she's going!"

Tam went first, over the side and into the water with a splash and a stream of archival profanity. Ines didn't go. Ines came *toward* her, fighting the tilt, and grabbed her arm with river-cold fingers.

"The skiff," Odile said. "Davit line. Go."

"Not without—"

"Dravenmoor." Odile put her free hand over theirs, on her own arm, and looked at them in the dying pink light of the flare, and said the truest thing she'd said in forty-one years: "I have spent my whole life keeping this boat afloat. Let me spend one minute deciding where she goes. Now get in the skiff, because I am not losing you the same night I lose her. I refuse. I forbid it. I will haunt you."

Something moved through Ines's face — grief and fury and something warmer underneath, fighting for room — and then they went, dropping into the skiff and holding it against the ferry's side while the water climbed the deck.

Odile took her time. She was owed that.

She walked the tilting deck once, touching things. The wheelhouse door. The stove where she'd burned ten thousand suppers and gotten most of them eaten anyway. The spot by the mast where a younger Odile had carved a bad likeness of her husband's face, and then, widowed, had carved beside it a worse likeness of herself, so neither of them would be lonely. The bell, still wrapped, still humming — she cut it loose and heaved it into the skiff, because some things you deliver or you die trying.

Then she stood at the stern rail, where the name was painted, and read it one last time in the moonlight. *Marrow's Grace. Kiln Reach registry.*

"Take her down easy, love," she told the boat. "You always did hate rough landings."

The river took the rest. It rose up the deck in a black silk rush, swallowed the wheelhouse windows, the stove, the carvings, the name, and then the *Marrow's Grace* slid under with barely a ripple — a held breath released after forty-one years — and the river closed over her like a book.

Odile sat in the skiff, soaked to the ribs, holding an oar she didn't remember picking up, watching the empty water where her entire life used to be.

Beside her, Ines was very carefully not touching her, which was somehow worse than pity. Tam, gray-lipped and shivering in the bow, whispered something in old Vhalorian that Odile didn't know but understood anyway. A rite for the drowned. Except the drowned thing was a boat, and the mourner was the one who'd sunk her, and the rite fit anyway.

"She was a good ship," Tam offered at last.

"She was a *stubborn* ship," Odile corrected, and her voice cracked clean down the middle on the second word, and she let it. "Forty-one years. Do you know what I had in that wheelhouse? My mother's compass. My wedding ring's been in the chart drawer since '09 because the river swells your fingers. Every logbook since I was nineteen years old. I just fed my whole autobiography to a catfish."

Ines made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been the opposite, and then, finally, put a hand on her shoulder. Odile allowed it. Then she leaned into it, briefly, thoroughly, the way a dock leans into its pilings.

"They'll dredge her," she said after a while. Talking helped. Talking kept the cold out. "Ondar's people will drag that hole within the week looking for the chest. Which is why—" she straightened, and the pilot's spine came back into her, the one thing the river hadn't taken, "—why the chest is not staying in this skiff. We beach at the head of the gorge, we wrap the ledger and the bell in oilcloth, and we walk. Three days on foot to the source valley, if the maps Tam keeps reciting are honest."

"Three days," Tam said faintly. "On foot. With my ribs."

"You catalogued four hundred and twelve deaths, archivist. Starvation's only number thirteen. You'll manage."

Ines was looking at her across the skiff, and in the moonlight their expression was one Odile had seen on passengers crossing a flood rapid: terror and trust in equal parts, waiting to see which one the pilot chose.

"Odile," they said quietly. "Your boat. Your whole life. For a ledger and a dead woman's name. Was it worth—"

"Ask me at the source," Odile said, and dug the oars in, and pointed the skiff's nose at the black wall of the gorge where the drowned names watched them pass. "Until then, row."

Behind them, far downstream, a gunboat's searchlight swept the empty water, back and forth, back and forth, finding nothing but the river's smooth indifferent face — and above the gorge rim, unseen, the path climbed north toward the ice, three days long, with rations for two and mouths for three.
