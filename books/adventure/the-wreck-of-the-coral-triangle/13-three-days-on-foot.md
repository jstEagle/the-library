## 13. Three Days on Foot

The first thing the mountains took was Odile's good mood.

She'd surrendered it somewhere above the tree line, around the hour when the ferryman's shoulders — shoulders built for a wheelhouse and a winch, not for a pack strap cutting into them at a forty-degree angle — began to complain in a language only she could hear. She answered it in kind, under her breath, in a dialect of curses so old and regional that Ines suspected half of them had been retired by the church.

"You're doing well," Ines offered, somewhere behind her.

"I'm doing *arithmetic*," Odile said, without turning. "That's different."

Tam Reyle said nothing at all. The archivist climbed like a man reading: steadily, head slightly bowed, as though the switchbacks were sentences he intended to finish before dark. He had been shot nine days ago at Kiln Reach, and the bandage under his shirt had gone from white to a color Ines didn't want to name, but Tam walked anyway, one hand pressed to his side, the other holding his hat against the wind with the stubborn courtesy of a man who believed that losing one's hat to weather was how nations lost their dignity.

They were three days from the source valley, if the map Tam carried was honest. They had rations for two.

Ines had done that arithmetic too, back at the scuttled ferry, standing in the shallows among the bubbles of Odile's livelihood while the cold worked its way into their boots. Two packs pulled from the wreck. One tin of smoked fish, split into three portions because you couldn't look two people in the eye and eat alone. A bag of hardtack. A brick of tea. A jar of honey, which Odile had refused to surrender even to arithmetic itself, cradling it like an infant.

"Three people," Ines had said. "Two days of food."

"Then we walk fast," Odile had said, "and we don't talk about it," which was, Ines would learn over the next thirty-six hours, the single most discussed topic available to them.

* * *

The first day, they talked about everything else instead.

Odile told the story of how she'd come to own a ferry at nineteen, which involved a dead uncle, a forged bill of sale, and a card game she still insisted she'd won fairly. Tam explained, unprompted and at length, why Vhalorian river towns built their chimneys crooked — "so the smoke would remember it had once been free to go where it liked" — and then apologized for the length of the explanation, and then, when neither of them objected, gave a second, longer explanation about chimney tax fraud.

Ines listened. That was mostly what Ines did now: listen, climb, count steps. It was a guide's habit, counting steps, and it had survived the blacklist the way a scar survives a change of address. Four hundred steps between rests. On the fourth hundredth step, Ines would call the halt, and they would sit on whatever stone offered itself, and divide nothing, because dividing happened only at morning and evening, and the hours between were held together with tea made weak enough to see through.

It was during one of these halts, watching Odile measure out the leaves with a spoon and a long face, that Ines caught themselves thinking of their mother's hands doing exactly the same thing — stretching a pinch of tea across a week, back in the years when money was a rumor and hospitality wasn't. The thought arrived without permission, the way thoughts of the dead do, and sat down heavily, and stayed.

"You knew her," Ines said. It came out blunter than intended. Most things did, at altitude.

Tam looked up from his map. "I knew of her. Everyone in the remnant knew of her. She was—" He stopped himself, the way careful men stop themselves near edges. "She was the last archivist. What she did at the source—"

"Save it." Ines hadn't meant to say that either. But there it was. "Not yet. I can't carry it and this grade at the same time."

Tam nodded slowly, and folded the map away, and Ines understood that the old man had just agreed to hold something heavy for them until their hands were free. There was a word for that in every mountain culture Ines had ever guided through. Usually it was a swear word.

Odile handed around the tea. Three cups, one after another, each weaker than the last, and nobody commented on it, and everybody noticed.

* * *

The second day, the mountain stopped being polite.

It started with the wind, which came off the glacier fields above them with the specific intention of making them regret their life choices individually. Then the trail narrowed to a shelf of rock barely wide enough for a determined goat, and dropped away on the left into a gorge so deep that the river they'd followed for weeks became a sound rather than a sight — a thin silver whisper, like someone far below practicing an apology.

Ines went first. That was non-negotiable; some habits are load-bearing. They moved along the shelf with the old certainty coming back into their body, the body remembering what the blacklist had tried to make it forget: weight on the uphill foot, three points of contact, eyes on the next placement and never on the drop. Behind them, Odile pressed herself to the wall and swore her way across in that antique dialect, and Tam crossed last with his hat in his teeth, humming — actually humming — some slow Vhalorian tune that Ines suspected was a funeral song, because Tam hummed funeral songs the way other men whistled at work.

At the far side, where the shelf widened into a shoulder of scree, Odile sat down harder than necessary and announced: "Lunch."

"It's mid-morning," Tam said.

"It's lunch *somewhere*," Odile said, already untying the ration bag. "Somewhere with better time zones."

They divided the day's portion on a flat stone, with the ceremony of people who have begun to treat food as liturgy. Half a tin of fish each, gone in four bites. Hardtack, which Odile soaked in melted snow to make it surrender. Honey — one spoonful each, measured with the gravity of a sacrament, except that Odile gave herself visibly less and claimed the spoon had been uneven since Kiln Reach.

"You're shorting yourself," Ines said.

"I'm *budgeting*."

"You're lying."

"I'm a ferry operator, Dravenmoor. Lying is a core competency." But she took the extra half-spoonful Ines pushed toward her, and ate it looking out at the gorge, and said nothing more about it, which was how Odile Vasse said thank you.

It was Tam who raised it, that evening, in the lee of a boulder with the fire no bigger than a cupped hand — small fires only, Ines had insisted, small fires and cold camps, because Ondar's people had gunboats and gunboats had eyes, and even here, four thousand feet above any water that could float them, Ines trusted the cartel the way you trust weather: completely, and at a distance.

"We should discuss the arithmetic," Tam said.

"No," said Odile.

"—because I've done it," Tam continued, with the mild immovability of a man who had spent forty years cataloguing other people's debts and had developed strong opinions about unpaid ones. "Two days of food, three mouths, three days of walking. Tomorrow we arrive hungry. The day after, we arrive *dangerous*. Hunger does that. It doesn't ask permission. And I am the least useful of us — I'm wounded, I'm slow, and my primary skill is knowing where things are written down, which will matter enormously once we reach the archive and not at all before it."

"No," Odile said again, flatly, and Ines heard what was underneath the word, which was: *not again.* Odile had lost a boat, a living, and a past in one night of black water. She was not going to sit through a polite seminar on who should be left behind.

"That's not what I'm proposing," Tam said gently. "I'm proposing we decide *together*. Openly. Tonight, while everyone can still think clearly. Because tomorrow night we won't be able to, and the night after that, hunger will decide for us, and hunger is a poor parliament."

He said it in the tone of a man quoting scripture, or a ledger. Ines looked at him — at the bloodstained bandage, the patient hands, the hat set carefully beside him like a sleeping animal — and felt the full weight of what the old man was offering: not his share of the food, but his share of the *decision*. As if that were the greater gift. As if, for a man whose nation had been erased by decisions made in closed rooms, an open room was the whole point.

"The Vhalorians voted," Ines said quietly. "Didn't they. Before the end. Your mother told you that."

"My mother told me the archive kept minutes," Tam said. "Even for the last council. Even for the sinking. Eleven votes recorded, in order, with reasons attached." He smiled, thin and tired. "We were a nation of bookkeepers, Dravenmoor. We believed that if you wrote down why you chose a thing, the choosing might stay honest."

"And did it?"

"No," Tam said. "But we knew exactly how we'd failed. That's worth something. Most peoples don't get to know."

The fire ticked. Somewhere above them, ice calved off the glacier with a sound like a door closing in an empty house.

"So," Odile said at last, into the silence, in the voice of a woman conceding a point to an armed opponent. "A vote. On whether we turn back."

"On whether we go on," Tam corrected. "Same vote. Different ledger."

* * *

They slept badly, which was appropriate, and woke before light, which was worse.

The third day's ration was the smallest, and they ate it in the blue-grey hour before dawn, standing up, because sitting down invited conversation and conversation invited the vote, and none of them wanted the vote to happen on an empty stomach. Which was, Ines reflected, exactly backwards — but human beings had been postponing difficult parliaments since before the invention of bread, and there was no reason to break tradition on a mountainside.

By noon the trees were a memory. By early afternoon the world was reduced to its essentials: grey rock, white sky, the wind, and the sound of three sets of boots finding purchase on ground that had opinions about them. Ines counted steps. Four hundred, rest. Four hundred, rest. On the sixth cycle, Tam stumbled, caught himself on Ines's arm, and laughed — a small, startled laugh, embarrassed.

"Funny," he said. "I catalogued eleven thousand famine records once. Grain ledgers, ration rolls, the whole miserable genre. I thought I understood hunger the way a man understands a flood from a book." He straightened, breathing carefully around his wound. "The books leave out the part where it gets *chatty*. Where your own stomach starts negotiating with you. Mine has offered me my boot leather twice and my hat once."

"Don't take the deal," Odile said. "The hat's load-bearing."

They crested a ridge as the light went long and gold, and there it was — the valley, or the throat of it: a notch in the wall of the world, blue-shadowed, with the glacier field spilling down toward it like a frozen tide. Beyond the notch, invisible still, lay the lake, and beneath the lake, the drowned city, and beneath the city, everything Ines's mother had chosen to sink.

One more day. Maybe less, if the notch went easy.

They made camp in a hollow out of the wind, and this time there was no pretense of supper. There was hot water with honey scraped from the very bottom of the jar — Odile presiding over the last of it with the expression of a queen distributing the crown jewels — and then the three of them sat in a triangle around the tiny fire, and nobody spoke, because the moment had arrived and everyone knew it, and pretending otherwise would have insulted it.

"All right," Ines said finally. "The parliament of the hollow. Motion on the floor: we go on. One day's march, no food, cold camp at the top. Against: we turn back, two days down to the river, and we probably live."

"'Probably,'" Odile repeated. "Inspiring."

"I'm a guide. I deal in probabilities, not promises. Promises are for people who've never watched a mountain change its mind." Ines looked at the fire. "You want my reason? Here it is, for the record. My mother is down there. Or what she did is. Either way, I've spent my whole life carrying a debt I never signed for, and I'm done paying interest on a loan I can't read. I vote to go on. Not because it's smart. Because I'd rather die literate."

Silence. The wind leaned on the hollow and was refused.

"Tam?" Ines said. "You don't have to—"

"I vote to go on," Tam said, simply. "For the record, and with reasons attached. The archive is the last true thing my country owns. Everything else — the remnant, the exiles, me — we're copies. Copies degrade. If the original survives one more generation, we get to be a nation again instead of a memory of one. Also," he added, with the ghost of his dry humor, "my stomach has withdrawn its offer on the hat. It says the hat and it have reached an understanding."

"Odile," Ines said.

The ferrywoman was quiet for a long time. She turned her cup in her hands, once, twice — the same motion, Ines realized, that she used at the wheel, holding a course.

"You know what my duty is?" she said at last. "Everyone keeps saying duty like it's a grand thing. Duty's small. Duty is: the boat floats, the passengers land, the crew eats. That's it. That's the whole religion." She looked up. "My boat's at the bottom of a lake. So." A breath. "I vote to go on. Reasons attached: because if I turn back now, I'll spend the rest of my life being a woman who almost saw a miracle and chose dinner instead. And because—" she paused, and Ines watched her decide whether to say the rest, and say it, "—because somebody down there taught my mother to swim, forty years ago, in a river town that doesn't exist anymore, and never sent a bill. I checked. I asked around for years. No debt was ever recorded." Her jaw tightened. "Somebody in that drowned city forgave my family something, Dravenmoor, and I have been carrying it around like loose change ever since. Time to pay it forward."

The fire settled. Above the notch, the first stars were coming out over the glacier, sharp and indifferent and beautiful.

"Motion carries," Ines said. "Three votes, reasons recorded. Somebody find me something to write on."

Tam produced, from an inner pocket, a stub of pencil and the corner of a map he was evidently willing to sacrifice, and Ines wrote it down the way Tam had taught without teaching: the date, the place, the names, the votes, the reasons. Small letters, steady hand. When they finished, Tam read it over and nodded once, formally, like a man closing a ledger entry.

"There," he said. "Now however it ends, it ends honestly. That's more than most nations manage."

Ines folded the scrap away inside their coat, over the heart, where the important papers go.

And that was when Odile, who had been staring past the fire toward the notch with the fixed attention of a woman who trusted her eyes more than any parliament, put out one hand and said, quietly:

"Down. All of you. Down."

Because up on the skyline of the notch — tiny, black, unmistakable against the last of the light — figures were moving. Three of them. Five. Roped together, moving with the unhurried competence of professionals, descending toward the valley they had all just voted to reach.

"Well," said Tam, into the silence. "It appears we are not the only parliament interested in the source."

Odile began, softly, to swear — in that old dialect, at length, with feeling.

Ines counted the figures on the skyline twice, and found seven, and reached for the pencil.
