## 14. The Forbidden Source

The last morning of the climb, Tam woke them by singing.

It was not good singing. It was a thin, wavering thing in old Vhalorian, a walking song about bread and bridges, and it scraped against the dawn like a spoon against a pot. Ines lay in the frost-stiffened tent with their eyes open and let it wash over them, because three days ago Tam had taken a bullet through the shoulder for a ledger, and a man who did that had earned every bad note he wanted to sing.

"He does this," Odile said from beside them, her voice gravelled with sleep. "Every morning since Kiln Reach. I keep waiting for him to run out of verses."

"There are forty of them," Tam called cheerfully through the canvas. "My grandmother made me learn all forty. She said if Vhaloria ever came back, someone would need to know how to start the day."

"And if it doesn't come back?"

"Then someone still needs to know how to start the day."

Ines crawled out into the blue cold. The valley opened below them.

They had been climbing toward it blind for two days — through scree and stunted pine, along goat tracks that Odile swore she remembered from a smuggler's map she'd once sold her honesty to buy — but nothing had prepared them for the shape of it. The mountains fell away on three sides like the petals of some enormous stone flower, and at the center sat the lake.

Not a mountain lake. Not the tarns Ines had guided clients past for twenty years, gray-green and shallow-hearted. This water was the color of a blade held up to winter light — pale, glassy, impossibly deep — and it lay so still that the peaks stood in it upside down, perfect, as though the world had been folded in half and pressed flat.

"The source," Tam said quietly, all his morning music gone out of him. "The headwaters of the whole river. Everything we've floated, everything we've fled down — it all starts here."

"It's beautiful," Odile said, and then, being Odile: "I hate that it's beautiful. Nothing that's cost this much blood gets to be beautiful. It's cheating."

But she was smiling when she said it, and Ines found they were too, and for a moment none of the three of them moved, because moving would have meant admitting the moment was going to end.

* * *

They came down to the shore by noon, picking their way over terraces of old masonry that surfaced from the earth like the backs of sleeping animals. Walls. Steps. The corner of an archway, its keystone carved with the drowned seal — the wave and the closed book — worn smooth by four decades of weather.

"This was a town," Ines said, crouching to touch the stone. "Before the flooding."

"Vessamar," Tam said. "The archive city. My grandmother was born here." He said it the way other people named saints. "When the cartel came for the treasury, the archivists didn't run. They opened the sluices. They drowned their own city rather than let the collection be carried off. Every document, every record, every name — sealed under the water where no one could steal it, because no one could reach it."

"And your grandmother walked out," Odile said. It wasn't quite a question.

"She walked out." Tam looked at the water. "She never once said whether she thought it was worth it."

The lake lay before them, holding its silence. Ines had spent their life reading water — reading current, reading ice, reading the small treacheries of snowfields — and this water read like nothing they had ever met. It wasn't hiding anything. That was what unsettled them. Mountain lakes were always hiding something: rocks, corpses, the bodies of bad decisions. This one simply waited, patient as a library after closing, full of everything and giving up nothing.

"So it's true," Ines said. "All of it. The rumors we used to trade at the riverports. The drowned nation. I always thought it was a story guides told drunk clients."

"Oh, it's a story guides tell drunk clients," Odile agreed. "And here we are, three idiots who walked two hundred kilometers on half rations to check the footnotes."

Tam laughed, then winced, then laughed again anyway, one hand pressed to his bandaged shoulder.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a ruined boathouse whose roof still stood, half its tiles intact, as though the building had simply lain down for a nap and expected to be woken. Odile got a fire going with the efficiency of a woman who had cooked ten thousand meals on ten thousand hostile riverbanks. Tam laid out the last of the food with the grim arithmetic of a man who had done the same sums three times and gotten worse answers each time.

"One more day of eating," he reported. "Half a day, if we're honest about portions, which historically we have not been."

"We're honest today," Odile said.

"Then half a day."

Ines turned their share of dried fish over in their fingers and didn't eat it. They were looking at the lake again. There was something under the surface near the shore — a regularity in the shadows, straight lines where nature drew none. Streets. A grid of streets, visible through thirty fathoms of impossible clarity, running away from them into the blue like a hand letting go.

"I can see the roofs," they said.

Odile came to stand beside them. For a while neither spoke.

"My father fished this river his whole life," she said finally. "He used to say the water above Kiln Reach tasted different. Sweeter, he said, like it had been filtered through paper. I thought it was an old man's nonsense." She shook her head slowly. "He was tasting a library, Ines. Forty years of my father drinking tea made out of somebody's entire history, and he never knew."

"That's either very funny or very sad."

"Yes," Odile said.

Down the shore, Tam had begun unpacking the things he'd carried through gunfire and gorge and starvation rations: wax tablets, a lead stylus, oilcloth wraps, the small careful kit of a man whose profession was remembering. He caught Ines watching and shrugged with his good shoulder.

"If we can get down there," he said, "even briefly — even to one room — I can copy enough to matter. The master catalog was kept in the low vaults, closest to the sluice gates. Cold water preserves everything. Paper, ink, vellum. It's all down there waiting, Ines. Forty years of waiting, and it's perfectly fine. Better than fine. It's *patient*." His voice cracked on the word and he pretended it hadn't. "Everything Vhaloria was, sitting in the dark, keeping itself."

"And getting it will kill us," Odile said flatly. "Cold like that stops a heart in minutes. We have no dive gear, no line, no boat. We have half a fish apiece and one working shoulder between us."

"I'm aware of the logistics."

"You're aware of the logistics the way a moth is aware of windows."

Tam smiled sadly. "Yes," he said, which seemed to be the family word for agreement.

* * *

Evening came down the valley like poured ink, and the temperature with it. They huddled at the fire, and Ines found themselves talking before they'd decided to — which, with Odile, was becoming a habit, and a dangerous one.

"When I was blacklisted," they said, staring into the flames, "the worst part wasn't the tribunals or the newspapers. It was that everyone assumed I'd feel guilty, and I did, but not about the thing they thought. They thought I'd abandoned a client on the mountain. What actually haunts me is smaller. Stupider. That night in the tent before the storm, I remember thinking: *if this goes wrong, my career goes wrong.* Not *if this goes wrong, a man might die.* My career. I did the arithmetic wrong, and a man died of it, and I've been paying installments ever since." They turned the fish over without eating it. "Standing here, looking at that water — I keep doing the same arithmetic. What's it worth? What's it cost? And I can't make the numbers come out."

"Because there aren't any numbers," Odile said. "That's the trick of places like this. They don't fit in columns." She fed a stick to the fire. "You want to know what I think? I think your mother stood somewhere near this exact spot and did the same sum, and couldn't make it come out either, and so she chose with her gut instead of her ledger. And look —" she gestured at the dark water, the drowned streets, the whole patient weight of it — "look what her gut bought. Forty years, and nobody has stolen a single page."

"Nobody but us is trying."

"Nobody but us is trying," Odile agreed, "and honestly, I'm starting to wonder if we're the villains of this particular story. Three starving strangers creeping up on a nation's grave with theft in mind."

"It's not theft if you're inheriting," Tam said mildly, from across the fire.

"Says the man who wants to burgle a lake."

"Says the archivist," Tam corrected, with dignity, "who wants to *liberate* a lake."

Ines laughed — really laughed, the first time in longer than they could calculate — and the sound went up off the water and came back changed, flattened, strange. All three of them noticed. The fire crackled. Somewhere high on the valley wall, loose stone ticked downward in the cold.

"The bell," Ines said suddenly.

"What about it?"

They'd raised it a week ago, miles downstream — the bronze bell from the drowned church of some sunken village, humming faintly ever since, growing louder as they climbed. It hung now from Odile's salvaged pack frame, wrapped in oilcloth, wrapped again in wool, muffled within an inch of its life. Ines had almost stopped hearing it, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat.

But tonight, unwrapped by accident when Tam shifted the pack, it was ringing. Softly. Steadily. A low bronze tone that rose and fell like breathing.

"It's never sung like that," Ines said. They crossed to the pack and put their palm flat against the metal. The vibration ran up their arm, gentle and insistent, like a hand tugging a sleeve. "It's louder here. Much louder. As if—" They stopped.

"As if it knows it's home," Tam whispered.

They stood around the bell in the firelight, three exhausted people and one ringing piece of bronze, and Ines felt the awe of the afternoon turn over and show its underside. Because the bell was a relic, yes. A guide. But Tam had told them the truth about it weeks ago, back when truth was cheaper: a bell that sang near the source could be heard by anyone else who knew how to listen. It was a compass needle. And compass needles pointed for everybody.

"Ondar's people had divers on the archive before we ever left the gorge," Ines said quietly. "That's what the bell's been telling us this whole time, isn't it? Not *come home.* Come home *quickly.*"

No one answered. The bell hummed against Ines's palm, patient and urgent at once, and far out on the black water — so far it might have been imagination, might have been a star falling the wrong direction — a light blinked twice, and went out.

* * *

Ines did not sleep. Around midnight they walked down to the shore alone, boots crunching on forty-year-old cobblestones, and stood where the drowned streets began.

The moon was up. Under it, the lake gave up its secrets grudgingly, in silver fragments: a streetlamp's arm, a bell tower's tip leaning like a drunk against nothing, the long straight shadow of a canal. An entire city, lying down in the cold with its eyes open. Keeping itself. Waiting for someone to decide whether memory was a burden or an inheritance — and Ines understood, standing there with numb toes and a full heart, that the decision had already been made once, by a woman with their same stubborn jaw, on a night exactly like this one.

"All right, Mother," they said aloud, to the water, to the dark, to whoever was listening. "I'm here. Show me what you saw."

The lake said nothing, in the way of libraries.

But behind them, up the slope, the bell rang out once — clear, unmuffled, unmistakable — and Ines spun, heart hammering, to see Tam standing by the fallen pack with his hand over his mouth and his eyes wide.

"I didn't touch it," he said. "Ines. I swear it. I didn't touch it."

Out on the water, the distant light blinked again.

Twice.

And this time, it did not go out.
