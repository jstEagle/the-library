## 15. Ondar Arrives First

Sef Ondar had been in the valley for three days before Ines Dravenmoor ever saw it, and he considered this his finest professional achievement.

Not the diving. Not the negotiation he was about to attempt. The *waiting*. Anyone with money and guns could arrive first. Arriving first and then having the discipline to sit quietly in a tent drinking bad coffee while your divers worked — that took temperament. That took a man who understood that most disasters were simply impatience wearing a uniform.

"Mr. Ondar." His dive supervisor, a broad woman named Kess with forearms like dock rope, ducked into the command tent. "Third line's down. They've reached the colonnade."

"The archive proper?"

"Edge of it. Visibility's better than we hoped. Cold as a tax audit, but clear."

Ondar smiled at that. He liked Kess. She had no imagination whatsoever, which made her the only person on his payroll he never had to double-check. "And our friends on the mountain?"

Kess shrugged. "Spotted them at dawn, two ridges down. Three of them. Moving slow. Your guess is they reach the lake by tomorrow midday."

"Then we have tonight," Ondar said, "to be generous."

He stepped out of the tent into the evening, and — because he always did this, because it was a habit older than his career — he looked at the valley and let himself feel it properly.

The source valley of the Vhalor was not beautiful in the way postcards meant. It was beautiful the way a held breath is beautiful. Glacial walls rose on three sides, blue-grey and streaked with old avalanche scars, and between them lay the lake: a sheet of water so still and so palely green that it seemed less like water than like glass someone had forgotten to finish installing. Somewhere under that glass lay a city. Streets, libraries, vaults, forty years of silence. A nation that had gone under all at once, on purpose, and taken its memory with it rather than let certain hands carry it up.

Ondar's employers wanted those hands. He was paid extremely well to be one of them.

He did not think of himself as a thief. Thieves took things; Ondar arranged transactions. The distinction mattered to him the way religion matters to other men. When Vhaloria sank, someone had signed the orders, someone had moved the money, and somewhere in the chain a document existed that said, in effect, *this was agreed*. Ondar's entire profession consisted of finding such documents and making sure they continued to say what his employers needed them to say. History, he had long ago concluded, was just bookkeeping that had learned to hold grudges.

Which brought him, inevitably, to Ines Dravenmoor.

He poured himself the last of the coffee and sat on an equipment crate with the file across his knees. Not the forged ledger — he'd burned through his interest in that particular piece of fiction weeks ago. The other file. The true one. Recovered from a bank vault in a country that no longer admitted it had ever held Vhalorian assets, purchased at a price that would have made a cardinal weep, and carried since then in a waterproof case against Ondar's chest like a second heart.

*Dravenmoor, Mirela. Archivist, Second Rank.*

He had read it eleven times. He knew what was in it. He also knew — and this was the part he found genuinely, professionally fascinating — that Ines Dravenmoor did not know. The child had spent a lifetime carrying a debt that was, in the strictest sense, a forgery. A name entered in a false ledger by men who needed the drowned treasury to look like a drowned *loan*. And now the child had hauled that forgery four hundred miles upriver, pursued by guns, dragging along a ferrymaster and a half-drowned archivist, all to pay back a debt that had never existed.

It was, Ondar thought, almost beautiful. People built their whole lives around paperwork. He'd seen it ruin marriages, start wars, put perfectly decent men on the bottom of rivers. Paperwork was the most honest lie ever invented.

The light went off the glacier walls. Somewhere below, a diver's bubble-line broke the surface of the lake in small silver rings, and the stillness closed over them again as if nothing had ever come up for air.

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow he would make his offer.

He was very good at offers.

* * *

They came down out of the pass at midday, exactly as Kess had predicted, and Ondar watched them through field glasses with something close to affection.

There was Dravenmoor in front — of course in front, mountain people always walked point even when walking point would kill them — moving with that careful, economical stride of someone who had spent years being paid to keep other people alive on bad ground. Behind them, a tall woman with a boatman's roll to her gait and the expression of someone cataloguing exits. And limping between them, wrapped in half a sleeping bag despite the summer air, a thin man who could only be Tam Reyle, late of Kiln Reach, shot in the shoulder and alive anyway, which Ondar noted with genuine respect. Men who survived being shot by his people tended to be either very lucky or very useful, and he was prepared to work with either.

"They've seen us," Kess said beside him. "The guide stopped. Watching."

"Of course they've seen us. I didn't buy three gunboats and burn a safehouse to sneak up on anyone. Sneaking is for thieves." He handed her the glasses. "Get the table set up outside the big tent. The good coffee. Two chairs facing each other, none of that standing-over-the-map nonsense. And nobody touches a weapon unless Dravenmoor starts it. Am I understood?"

"You're paying us to understand you, Mr. Ondar."

"I'm paying you to *pretend* to understand me," Ondar said pleasantly. "The understanding comes later, if you're lucky."

He watched the three figures confer on the ridge. Even at this distance he could read the argument: the ferrywoman gesturing at the camp, at the divers' lines, at the obvious arithmetic of guns; Reyle shaking his head, pointing at the lake with the urgency of a man who could see his whole nation under the water; and Dravenmoor standing still through all of it, still as the lake itself, doing the thing Ondar had counted on from the first day he'd read the name.

Thinking about their mother.

"There," Ondar murmured. "That's it. That's the door."

* * *

Ines Dravenmoor came down alone.

Ondar had expected this too, and he had the table set where he wanted it: on the flat shelf of rock above the shore, close enough to the water that the divers' bubbles were visible, far enough from the tents that no one could claim ambush. One chair for each of them. Coffee. No guards in sight — though he had eight within a whistle, and both of them knew it, and pretending otherwise was part of the courtesy.

"Ines Dravenmoor." He stood and offered his hand. "Sef Ondar. Thank you for coming. Sit, please. The coffee's terrible but it's hot, and at this altitude terrible and hot are the only two categories that matter."

Ines did not take the hand. They looked at it, then at him, with the flat grey patience of a cliff face, and sat down without waiting to be invited twice. Up close they were younger than their reputation and more tired than their years, windburned, one hand resting near the knife at their belt in a way that wasn't a threat so much as a habit of arithmetic.

"You burned Kiln Reach's dock," Ines said. "You took Tam. You put a bullet in him."

"My people took Tam. The bullet was an accident of enthusiasm, and I've already docked the shooter's pay, which in my organization is the sincerest form of apology." Ondar sat, unhurried, and poured two cups. "I won't insult you by denying the rest. Yes. All of it. You want to know why, and you've walked three days on starvation rations to ask me in person, so let's skip the part where we pretend this is a social call."

"Talk, then."

"I'm going to offer you a trade." He set the waterproof case on the table between them, square and unremarkable, and rested one finger on it. "But first I'm going to tell you a story, because you strike me as someone who doesn't sign anything they haven't read to the end."

Ines said nothing. Ondar took it as encouragement.

"Forty years ago, Vhaloria was drowning financially before it ever drowned literally. Bad loans, worse neighbors, a treasury full of paper worth less every season. My employers — and I'll spare you the names, not because you don't deserve them but because the names would bore you — saw an opportunity. They offered to buy the national debt. All of it. Every obligation, every name, everything owed to the state or by it." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "Vhaloria's government refused. But governments are committees, and committees have members, and some of those members thought drowning was preferable to selling and some thought selling was preferable to drowning, and the argument got loud enough that my employers decided to settle it themselves."

"And the sinking."

"And the sinking," Ondar agreed. "Which required paperwork. It always does. You can't move a treasury underwater without documents saying the treasury was *moved*, legitimately, in settlement of debts. So a ledger was created. Debts owed to Vhaloria by its own citizens — carefully chosen names, scattered, plausible. If the state died owing nothing and being owed nothing, then nothing had been stolen. Do you see? The theft needed victims on paper. The ledger gave it victims."

Ines's face did not change. Their voice did. "My mother's name is in that ledger."

"Yes."

"You're telling me it was put there."

"I'm telling you," Ondar said gently, "that the ledger is a forgery, and your mother's entry is part of the forgery, and whoever wrote it chose her name deliberately — because she was there, and because using her made the fiction harder to question. Grief is excellent collateral. Forged debts are safest when they're owed by people who can't dispute them."

For a long moment the only sound was the lake, lapping at the shelf of rock, patient as everything else in this valley. Then Ines said, "Tam told us. On the river. He told us the ledger might be forged."

"Did he." Something flickered behind Ondar's pleasant expression — surprise, quickly filed away. "Then you already know. And yet you kept carrying it. Interesting."

"It's the only thing of hers I've ever had."

"*No,*" Ondar said, and here he leaned forward, and let the salesman fall away from his voice entirely, because this next part deserved honesty. "It isn't. That's the whole point, Dravenmoor. That's why I'm sitting here instead of just taking the chest off your corpse." He tapped the case once. "This is her real file. Personnel record, Second Rank, Chief Archivist of the Vhalorian State Archive. Her actual handwriting. Her actual decisions. What she actually did in the last seventy-two hours of the country — and I promise you it is not what the ledger says, and it is not, I suspect, what you believe. You've spent your life inheriting a debt. This is the inheritance. The difference between the two is the difference between a cage and a key."

Ines stared at the case. Ondar watched them not touch it, and admired the discipline enormously.

"What do you want for it?"

"Straight to terms. Good. I hate haggling; it wastes everyone's dignity." He folded his hands. "Here is my price, and it has exactly one clause. The chest, the forged ledger, and everything in them come to me. In exchange: this file, unopened by me, sealed, yours forever. Safe passage downriver for you, the ferrywoman, and Reyle — my boats will escort you past every checkpoint as far as the sea, and my word on that is worth more than my reputation, which is saying something, because my reputation is immaculate. And one more thing."

"Which is?"

"No one dies today. Not Reyle, not the woman, not you. I've had enough bodies on this river. Bodies attract auditors." He smiled briefly. "Even mine."

"And the archive." Ines's eyes went past him, to the lake, to the divers' lines rising silver out of the green. "Your team's already in it. What happens to what they bring up?"

Ondar considered lying, rejected it — lies were for people who hadn't done the arithmetic — and told the truth.

"They bring it up. It goes to my employers. It disappears into a vault, and forty years from now some historian will write a footnote about gaps in the record, and no one will ever know what was lost, because that's how erasure works: it edits the memory of the edit." He spread his hands. "I'm not offering to save Vhaloria, Dravenmoor. Vhaloria has been dead for forty years and dead things stay sold. I'm offering to save *you* — and to give you the truth about your mother, which is the only piece of her anyone can actually hand you. The rest is water."

The wind came down off the glacier and moved the surface of the lake in long slow wrinkles, and beneath them, faintly, the bell they'd raised two days ago — Ondar's hydrophones had tracked its humming for a week, a low bronze note like a throat clearing underwater — seemed to answer it. Ines heard it too. He saw it land on them like weather.

"You killed people to get here," Ines said at last. "Kiln Reach. The safehouse. The ferryman you sank in the narrows — Odile told me about him. Her friend."

"I did," Ondar said. "And I won't pretend those deaths weigh on me the way they'd weigh on you, because we'd both know I was performing. But I'll say this much, and mean it: I don't enjoy them, I don't collect them, and I am offering you the only deal on this river that ends with everyone breathing. There are men downstream of me who wouldn't offer anything. They'd offer a grave." He pushed the case an inch closer. "Take the file. Take the escort. Let the dead keep their secrets — they've kept them this long, and they seem comfortable."

Ines looked at the case for a long time.

Then they reached out — not for it, but for their cup of coffee, untouched this whole while, gone cold — and drank it anyway, all of it, grimly, like a person finishing a chore. And Ondar, who had negotiated with generals and ministers and once, memorably, a pirate archbishop, recognized the gesture for what it was: a mountain guide buying a moment to think, the way you'd rest on a ledge before deciding whether the next pitch went.

"One condition of mine," Ines said.

"Name it."

"I read it here. At this table. Before I agree to anything. If I'm trading away a country's grave, I find out first what my mother actually did." Their eyes came up and fixed on him, grey and level and absolutely immovable, the eyes of someone who had once stood on a mountain and made a decision that had followed them ever since. "You open the case. You hand me the file. And you sit there and watch me read every page while your divers rob the dead underneath us. That's the price of my yes. Take it or leave it."

Ondar sat back.

Every instinct he had — thirty years of them, the whole careful architecture of his professional soul — said *never let them read it first, never give the merchandise its power back.* And underneath that instinct, quieter, older, was the other thing, the thing he rarely listened to and was listening to now: the fact that he had carried this file against his chest for five hundred miles, and read it eleven times, and every single time had ended up sitting alone in the dark thinking about a woman who had flooded her own country's memory rather than let men like him carry it up — and wondering, in a way that had begun to feel less like professionalism and more like a symptom, what exactly that made him.

"Done," Sef Ondar said, and opened the case.

Inside, dry after forty years, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a folder with a broken wax seal and a name on it in faded ink: *M. DRAVENMOOR.*

Ines drew it out with both hands, the way you lift something that might still be alive.

Under the lake, the bell hummed. Above it, the first page turned.
