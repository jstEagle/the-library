## 17. Standoff in the Cold

Tam Reyle had spent forty-one years of exile believing that the worst thing that could happen to a person was being forgotten. He had built his whole life around the opposite of forgetting: memorizing names, copying names, whispering names into the dark of river towns where Vhalorian children grew up thinking their homeland was a story their grandparents invented to make the porridge seem more interesting.

Standing at the edge of the glacial lake with a rifle he did not know how to use and a master ledger wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, Tam revised his theory. The worst thing that could happen to a person, he decided, was being right about everything and still being ignored.

"They're diving," he said. "I told you they were diving. I said it at dawn. I said it with diagrams."

"We heard you the first time," Odile Vasse said, not looking up from the telescope she'd braced against a boulder. "You also said the lake was bottomless, and then you said it was forty meters deep, and then you said both things were true in different places, which is not how lakes work, Tam."

"It's how *this* lake works. It's how Vhaloria worked. Nothing about us was ever simple." He clutched the ledger a little tighter. The oilcloth was slick with his sweat. Below them, the water lay flat and pale as a coin, and rising from its center came the slow, silver strings of bubbles that marked where Ondar's divers had gone down through the breach in the ice — the same breach Ines Dravenmoor had widened with a pry bar three days ago, swearing in two languages and part of a third.

Ines was down there now. That was the part Tam could not stop chewing on. Ines had gone into the water after the divers, armed with a diving knife and a temper, and Odile had let them go, and Tam had not argued because arguing with Dravenmoors was apparently a family tradition that outlived the family.

"You should be down there too," Odile said, reading him the way she read water.

"I'm the archivist. The archive is the thing. You don't send the thing to defend the thing."

"That's not an argument, that's a riddle."

"I'm an archivist. Riddles are most of the job." He tried a smile. It came out crooked, which he supposed was honest. "Besides, someone has to stay up here and hold the ledger, and if you take it from me I'll only follow you and complain."

Odile lowered the telescope. For a moment her face did something Tam had not seen it do before — softened, around the eyes, like ice going to slush. "Hold it like it's a person," she said. "Not a thing."

"It *is* a person," Tam said. "It's forty thousand people. It's every name my grandmother couldn't say out loud without sitting down afterward."

The bubbles kept rising. Thin, patient, silver. Somewhere under that pale coin of water lay a drowned city — streets, stairways, a great vaulted hall of shelves and sealed cases, the entire memory of a nation preserved in cold water the way a fruit is preserved in syrup. Tam had dreamed of it since he was nine years old. In the dream, he always arrived dry, and the doors always opened, and someone was always waiting inside to say *welcome home*.

In the dream, nobody had a rifle.

* * *

The rifle belonged to Odile, and she had taught him to use it that morning with the tenderness of a woman teaching a child to hold a hot pan. "It kicks," she'd said. "Everything true kicks. Aim low, because you'll flinch high, and if you can't bring yourself to fire at a person, fire at the ice, and run."

"I'll fire at a person," Tam had said, with more conviction than he felt.

"You're a man who alphabetizes his grievances," Odile said. "I've seen your notebook."

"My grievances are *cross-referenced*, thank you."

But now, watching the bubbles, he found his hands were steady. That surprised him. He had expected terror. What he felt instead was something older and colder and much more useful: the particular calm of a man who has already decided what he will not give up. He had carried the master ledger out of Kiln Reach under his coat while the town burned. He had carried it up a mountain on half rations while Ines voted them forward and Odile pretended to agree for Ines's sake. He had slept with it against his ribs like a second heartbeat. It was not national property. It was not a treasure. It was a list of every debt the cartel had forged to launder what they stole — and, in the back pages, in the old hand, the true ledger, the one that proved the forgery. Names on one side. Numbers on the other. And between them, the whole machinery of a murder dressed up as bookkeeping.

You could drown a country, Tam thought. You could not drown an entry.

The lake spoke first.

It was not a loud sound. That was the terrible part. It was a *click* — deep, muffled, almost polite — the sound of a seal gate giving up a bolt after forty years of cold. Then a longer sound beneath it, a groan that Tam felt in his back teeth, and the surface of the lake shivered, and the bubbles changed. They stopped being thin strings and became a column.

"They're in the vault," Tam whispered.

Odile already had the telescope up. "I see the gate marker. It's — " She stopped. Tam had learned, these past days, to fear the pauses of capable people more than the exclamations of incompetent ones.

"It's what?"

"It's open. They've got the gate open." She lowered the glass. Her voice had gone flat, which Tam had also learned to fear. "That's not a breach. That's an invitation. They mean to bring things *out*. Crates. Cases. They mean to empty it."

"They can't. The cases are sealed, the paper — "

"Paper survives in cold water, Tam. You told me that yourself. You told me that was the whole *point*."

He had. He had stood on the shore two days ago, blue-lipped and radiant, and explained that the cold had kept the archive alive, that Vhaloria's memory lay in the dark reading itself over and over, waiting. He had been so happy. He remembered being so happy.

The column of bubbles thickened. And then, faintly — so faintly Tam thought at first it was his own blood in his ears — came the bell.

The drowned bell. They had raised it a week ago from the shallows downstream, and it had hummed when the source drew near, a low bronze note that lived in the chest more than the ear, and Odile had said *it's a compass* and Ines had said *it's a dinner bell* and they had both been right. Now it rang under the water, once, twice, a slow bronze tolling that came up through the rock and the ice and the soles of Tam's boots.

"They're ringing it," Tam said. "Why would they — "

"Signal," Odile said. "Calling the boats. Ondar's second team. The ones on the far shore." She was already moving, slinging the telescope, kicking snow over their small fire. "They found something worth announcing. Which means we have minutes. Maybe fewer."

"Where's Ines?"

"Under. With the divers." Odile looked at him, and Tam watched her do the arithmetic she hated — the arithmetic of who comes back. "Ines went down to stop them opening the inner vault. The gate's open, Tam."

"Then Ines failed."

"Then Ines is *buying*," Odile said. "There's a difference, and it's measured in minutes, and we're spending them."

* * *

They came down to the shore at a run, which on ice at altitude is a generous word for what they did — a controlled fall in a forward direction, repeated. The master ledger rode against Tam's chest in its oilcloth, and every jarring step he put one hand flat against it, the way you steady a child on a horse.

The breach in the ice was a black mouth six feet across, ringed with shattered white. A rope ran down into it, tied off to an ice screw. The rope was moving. Someone was coming up.

Tam skidded to a stop at the edge, and Odile caught his collar before he could do the stupid thing, which was lean over the hole, which was what every part of him wanted to do.

The rope jerked. A head broke the surface — a diver's hood, a mask, and behind the mask a face Tam knew from the gunboats at Kiln Reach. One of Ondar's men. The diver got an arm over the ice, saw them, and froze, and for one long second the three of them regarded each other with the perfect courtesy of people deciding.

Odile did not decide. Odile fired at the ice beside the man's hand, and the ice starred white, and the diver let go with a yelp and dropped back into the dark, and the rope whipped, and the ice screw groaned.

"You said you'd fire at a person," Odile said.

"I was about to!"

"You alphabetized first."

"There's a *system* — " But the argument died, because the lake made its next sound. Not a click this time. A *tear*. A long, low, structural sound, the sound a house makes in a dream when you know, even asleep, that the floor is going. The ice under their feet shivered from shore to shore. Far out, near the center, a seam opened — a dark line, thin as a hair, running thirty meters, forty, and through it came not bubbles but *water moving*, a slow exhale, the lake beginning to find a way out.

"The breach," Tam breathed. "They didn't just open the gate. They broke the old drain. The under-channel — my grandmother told me about the under-channel, the founders cut it to flood the valley in the first place, it was supposed to be sealed — "

"Tam."

"The lake is draining *into the archive*, Odile. The whole lake. It's going to take the city down into the deep channels and it's never coming back, and Ines is *under it*."

He was shouting. He heard himself shouting and could not stop, because grief and fury had gotten tangled somewhere around his sternum and were pulling in opposite directions like dogs on one lead. Forty years he had waited to see the archive. Forty years he had told children about it in rented rooms over river taverns. And now it was going to die twice — once when the cartel sank it, once now, forever, at the bottom of a channel no diver would ever reach — and the man carrying its memory was standing on cracking ice doing *nothing*.

The rope moved again.

This time the head that broke the surface wore no hood. Dark hair plastered flat, a mask pushed up, and a face Tam knew better now than he had ever expected to — gray-lipped, furious, alive.

"Ines!" Tam was on his knees at the hole before he had decided to be, hauling on the rope with Odile, and together they dragged Ines Dravenmoor out of the black water and onto the ice like landing a very large, very cold fish.

Ines rolled onto their back and coughed half the lake up and then, because they were who they were, said: "The inner vault's sealed. I wedged it. It'll hold."

"The lake's draining," Tam said. "Ines, the lake is — "

"I heard it. I *felt* it. It's coming down the under-channel." Ines sat up, water streaming off them, and looked at the seam in the ice, and Tam watched the guide take over from the person — the flat, quick, measuring look, the look that had gotten them up mountains and gotten them blacklisted and gotten them here. "How long?"

"I don't know. Hours? Less."

"Then Ondar has hours to empty a drowned city, and we have hours to stop him, and both of us are standing on the lid of the pot." Ines got to their feet, swaying. Odile put a shoulder under their arm without being asked. "Tam. The ledger. Tell me you still have it."

Tam drew the oilcloth bundle out of his coat. It was warm from his body. It was, he realized, the warmest thing on the entire lake.

"Here," he said. "It's here. It never left."

"Good," Ines said. "Because if the archive goes, this is the last copy of the truth in the world, and Ondar knows it. He doesn't need the vault if he takes this. One ledger. One knife's reach."

As if the words had been a summons, the bell tolled again under the ice — three notes now, quick, urgent — and across the lake, on the far shore, Tam saw lanterns moving. A line of them, descending toward the water. Men. Rope. A boat being dragged across the ice on runners.

Odile counted under her breath. "Eight. Maybe nine. And we have one rifle, one knife, and an archivist."

"Two knives," Ines said. "And an archivist who's been waiting forty years." Ines looked at Tam, and there was something in their face Tam had not seen before — not the guilt he carried like ballast, not the guide's flat arithmetic. Something almost like an apology, offered in advance. "Tam. If it comes to choosing between the ledger and — "

"Don't," Tam said. "Don't you dare finish that sentence. Nobody is choosing anything. I've spent my whole life being the man who *kept* things while other people decided. Not today. Today I decide."

The ice sang beneath them, a long cold note, and the seam crept another meter toward shore, patient as bookkeeping.

Across the lake, the lanterns reached the water's edge, and the first of Ondar's men stepped onto the ice, and began to run.
