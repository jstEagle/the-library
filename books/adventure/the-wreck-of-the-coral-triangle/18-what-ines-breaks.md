## 18. What Ines Breaks

The water was rising and Tam was bleeding and somewhere below them Ondar's divers were prying open a door that had been shut for forty years, and all of it — every piece of it — came down to two iron wheels set into the wall of the seal gate chamber.

Ines stood between them with a lantern in one hand and nothing in the other, and thought: *So this is what she saw.*

The chamber was older than everything else in the drowned city. The archive above it — the flooded streets, the library towers standing patient under the glacial lake like a congregation waiting for a hymn — had been built around this room the way a heart is built around its valves. Two gates. One to hold the lake out of the deep vaults, one to hold the vaults' own water back from the dry galleries where the archivists had once worked by lamplight. Between them, a maintenance gallery barely wide enough for two people shoulder to shoulder, which was exactly how many people were in it now.

Odile had Tam's arm over her shoulders. The shot had gone through the meat of his forearm, high, ugly but not mortal, and he had made a joke about it on the way down that nobody had laughed at, which he'd taken as a professional criticism. Now he leaned against the wall, gray-faced, watching Ines look at the wheels.

"You're doing the arithmetic," Tam said.

"I'm always doing arithmetic."

"Then let me save you some." He fumbled inside his coat with his good hand and brought out the master ledger — or what was left of it. Water had found it during the run from the vault stairs; half the pages had fused into a brick of pulp. "It's done, Ines. Whatever they pull up from those galleries, whatever Ondar thinks he's buying — the record itself is finished. All that's left down there is paper and patience."

"Paper and patience is the whole country," Ines said. "That's what you told me in Kiln Reach. You said Vhaloria wasn't the land, it was the keeping."

Tam closed his eyes. "I did say that. I was hoping you weren't listening."

Above them, muffled through forty feet of stone and water, came a sound like a giant tapping on a coffin lid. The divers' tools. They'd been at it for an hour, working the vault door's ancient lock from the outside, and the intervals between their pauses were getting shorter. Ondar hadn't sent them down to browse. He'd sent them down because Ines had read the file and come up the stairs with a face that told him everything he needed to know about what the file contained, and because Ondar Sef was a man who understood, better than anyone Ines had ever met, that information not seized was information lost.

"He'll get it open," Odile said. It wasn't a question. She'd heard the tapping too. "Another hour. Maybe less if he's got the right charges, and he does, because he's got money, and money is just patience with better tools."

"Then we have an hour," Ines said.

"To do what?" Odile turned, and even now, even bleeding-adjacent and soaked to the ribs, she managed to look at Ines like Ines was being deliberately slow, which was one of the things — Ines admitted it to themselves in the privacy of their own skull — that they had come to love about her. "To leave? The spillway's behind us, the skiff's gone, the lake drains through that breach for another day before it's low enough to walk out. We *have* nowhere to be. We could sit here and eat the last of the rations and let him have it."

"And then he has it."

"Ines—"

"He has the whole country, Odile." Ines heard their own voice go flat and guide-calm, the voice they used above the icefall when panic would kill everyone faster than the mountain would. "Not the treasure. There isn't any treasure, there never was, that was the lie that got everyone drowned. He gets the memory. Every name. Every record of who they were and what they chose and why they chose it — including her. Including my mother, and what she did, and why. He gets to decide what the world remembers about Vhaloria, and he will decide it was a treasury worth stealing instead of a people worth erasing, and he'll be right, because he'll be the only one holding the proof otherwise."

Tam made a small sound. When Ines looked at him, the archivist was smiling, faintly, painfully, the way men smile at funerals when the eulogy gets something right.

"That's the speech," Tam said. "That's the speech she gave. Word for word almost, the last night. My mother told me about it my whole childhood — *the archivist stood up in the council hall and said, they can take the gold, gold is just weight, but they cannot take the keeping unless we hand it to them.*" He swallowed. "She didn't say what she intended to do about it. Not until the water came."

Ines looked at the wheels again.

They were beautiful, in the way old working things are beautiful. Iron gone soft with cold, hand-holds worn to a shine by generations of archivists running drills — because of course there had been drills, of course the keepers of a nation's memory had rehearsed its drowning the way coastal towns rehearse floods. The mechanism was counterweighted. One strong person, both hands, a quarter turn to unseat the gates, and then the lake above and the vaults below would meet in the middle, and everything between them would be water, forever.

One wheel opened the upper gate. Lake into vaults.

One wheel opened the lower. Vaults into the drainage galleries, into the gorge, into the river, gone.

Both together, and the archive drowned entire — not flooded and preserved in cold stillness the way it had been for forty years, a museum under glass, retrievable by anyone patient enough — but *scoured*. Torn loose, pulped, scattered down a mountain. Nothing left to dive for. Nothing left to sell.

Nothing left to remember anyone by.

*You're doing the arithmetic,* Tam had said, and here it was, laid out plain: on one side, a chance — thin, maybe no chance at all — that the archive survived Ondar, that someday the exiles raised it the way they'd raised the bell, that names came back up into the light. On the other side, a certainty: that Ondar never sold so much as a page of it.

And underneath the arithmetic, quiet as a stone in a pack, the other thing. The thing Ines had been carrying since the file. Since reading, alone in the lamplight of the survey hut, the report in their mother's steady hand describing, in the language of procedure, how she had opened these same gates while her colleagues evacuated, and stayed to watch the water come, because someone should.

*She chose this,* Ines thought. *She stood where I'm standing and she chose this, and the choice didn't make her a monster, it made her the last archivist of Vhaloria. And it made me —*

Made them what? An orphan raised on a story with a hole in it. A guide who'd learned to walk away from people on mountains. A debtor. A runner. Someone who had spent thirty years believing the worst thing they ever did was happen on a glacier on a bad day, only to learn that the family inheritance wasn't debt at all. It was a decision. Waiting, patient as paper, to be repeated.

"I need you both to know what I'm thinking," Ines said, "so you can stop me if I'm wrong."

"We won't stop you," Odile said.

"You don't know what I'm—"

"Yes, we do." She shifted Tam's weight against the wall and came to stand beside Ines, close enough that their shoulders touched, and looked up at the ceiling as though she could see through it to the lake, to the drowned streets, to the bell they'd raised together humming its one low note somewhere over the library square. "I've known since the gorge. Since you found her name chiseled fresh among the dead and didn't say a word for a full day, and then at the fire you asked me — do you remember what you asked me?"

Ines remembered. *If your boat was sinking and the river offered to take the cargo instead of you, would you let it?* And Odile had said: *Depends whose cargo.* And Ines had said: *Everyone's.* And Odile had been quiet a long time, and then she'd said, *Then you already know what kind of person you are, and you're just waiting for permission.*

"I'm asking anyway," Ines said. "Because this isn't a boat. This is a country. This is everybody's grandmother's letters and everybody's birthday records and the names on the gorge wall times ten thousand, and I'm going to put all of it under the water on purpose, and I want—" Their voice cracked, and they hated it, and kept going. "I want somebody to tell me it's allowed. That grief gets a vote."

Tam pushed himself off the wall. He crossed the gallery slowly, favoring the arm, and put his good hand flat on the lower wheel, the one that would send the vaults' contents down into the dark of the drainage system, out of any human reach, forever.

"It's allowed," he said. "I'm the archivist. That's literally my office. And I'm telling you what my mother's mother told her, which is the oldest rule we have: *the keeping serves the people, never the reverse.*" His eyes were wet and furious and calm, all three at once. "A memory that can be bought by the man who killed us isn't a memory. It's a hostage. And you don't negotiate with hostage-takers, Ines. You've met my negotiation style."

Despite everything — the blood, the water, the tapping overhead — Ines laughed. It came out broken, but it came out.

"The ledger," Ines said. "The forged one. Ondar's copy went down with his divers' surface tender?"

"Sank at anchor. I watched it." Odile's smile had no warmth in it at all. "Very careless anchorage. Almost like someone cut the line."

"So the forgery dies with the archive. Every false debt, every laundered name — including hers, including mine." Ines took a breath. "Free and clear."

"Don't say free and clear yet," Odile said. "Say it after the wheels."

* * *

They did it together, because some doors should not be opened alone.

Ines took the upper wheel — the lake — because it was theirs by right of inheritance, and Tam put his good hand beside theirs on the iron, and Odile stood at the lower wheel with her jaw set like a woman warping a ferry against a flood tide, which was more or less what she was doing.

"On three," Ines said. "And Tam — thank you. For teaching me the difference between a burden and an inheritance."

"What is it?" Tam asked. "In case anyone asks me later and I have to sound wise."

"A burden is something you carry for someone else," Ines said. "An inheritance is something you carry for yourself. Same weight. Different direction."

They turned.

The upper wheel fought for the first quarter-turn, iron groaning through forty years of disuse, and then the counterweights caught and it spun loose and somewhere far above them a door the size of a cathedral facade swung inward, and the lake began to come home.

They felt it before they heard it — a change in pressure, a shiver in the stone, the particular hush that mountains make when they are about to move enormous amounts of water. Then the sound arrived, vast and layered, a roar with a choir in it, and cold air rushed through the gallery carrying the smell of the deep lake, mineral and ancient and clean.

"Now the lower!" Tam shouted. "Before the surge finds the junction!"

Odile threw her whole body against the wheel. Ines let go of the upper — it was spinning freely now, feeding itself — and added their weight to hers, four hands on the iron, and the second gate unseated with a boom they felt in their teeth, and beneath their feet the vaults opened their throats and gave everything back to the water.

The meeting, when it came, was not violent. That was the terrible, holy thing about it. The lake came down and the archive went out and they folded into each other somewhere below the gallery floor with a sound like a held breath finally released, and then the roar settled into a single sustained note — the note of moving water filling every channel of a drowned nation at once — and Ines stood in it with their hands still on the wheel and felt the vibration come up through their arms and into their chest and knew, with a certainty that would never leave them, that somewhere in that water were ten thousand names becoming silt, and one of them was theirs by blood, and they had done it, they had actually done it, they had chosen the water twice in one family.

*Come and see,* their mother had written in the file, in the last line, the line Ines had read eleven times. *Come and see what I kept safe by letting it go.*

*I saw,* Ines said to her, silently, into the roar. *I hope it was enough. I hope I did it right. I hope—*

The gallery shuddered.

Not the settling shudder of water finding its level. A structural shudder, hard, percussive, followed by a second one, and dust sifted down through the lantern light, and Odile grabbed Ines's collar and pointed up without saying anything, because she didn't have to.

The tapping from above had stopped. What replaced it was worse: a rhythmic, deliberate thunder. Charges. Ondar's divers weren't picking the vault door anymore — they were blowing the surrounding rock, opening their own entrance, except the geometry they'd planned around no longer existed, because the geometry was currently underwater and rearranging itself.

"The gallery roof," Tam shouted. "If they bring down the crown of the vault—"

He didn't finish, because the third charge went off closer, much closer, and the maintenance gallery's far end — the direction they'd come from, the route back toward the spillway — collapsed in a single grinding exhalation of stone, sealing itself like a wound closing, and the lantern guttered in the pressure wave and steadied, and the three of them stood in the ringing dark looking at a wall of rubble where the exit used to be.

"Well," Odile said.

"There's the spillway shaft," Ines said. Their guide's mind was already moving, drawing the map of the complex the way Tam had taught it to them on the climb, the surveyor's old diagrams overlaid with everything they'd seen. "The emergency drain. It runs from the gallery floor down past the seal chamber and out to the gorge face. It's a crawl for most of it. It's how the original builders maintained the gates."

"How do you know it's open?"

"Because the water's going somewhere," Ines said, kneeling, putting a palm flat to the stone floor. Cold air moved against their skin, faint but insistent, breathing up through a seam near the wall. "There. Feel it. The drain's pulling."

They pried up the flagstone together — Odile's knife, Ines's shoulders, Tam directing with the serene authority of a man who had memorized the building plans as a child the way other children memorized songs — and beneath it a shaft dropped away into darkness, round, slick, wide enough for a body, with iron rungs set into the curve, descending farther than the lantern could reach.

Somewhere above, muffled now by stone and water, a voice carried down through the new breach Ondar's charges had torn. Ines couldn't make out the words. They didn't need to. They knew the voice. Reasonable. Patient. The voice of a man making an offer.

"He'll follow," Tam said quietly. "Through the breach, down the flooded galleries, into the drain. He's not leaving without something, and the something is us."

"Then we'd better be gone before he arrives," Odile said, and swung herself onto the top rung, and paused, and looked back at Ines with an expression that in any other week of their life Ines would have needed a month to decode and now read instantly: *you did the right thing, and it cost what it cost, and I'm going to be next to you for all of it.*

Ines took one last look around the seal chamber — the wheels settling silent, the lantern light trembling on wet stone, the whole buried weight of a nation shifting overhead as the lake completed its second drowning — and felt the strangest thing settle over them, which was not grief, or not only grief. It was the feeling from the summit of a hard climb, when the work is done and the weather holds and there is nothing left to do but go down.

Their mother had stood here. Their mother had chosen this. And now the choice belonged to Ines too — not as a debt, not anymore, the ledger was pulp and the forgery was sinking — but as an inheritance, carried forward, in the only direction that mattered.

Ines swung onto the ladder and started down into the dark, Odile above them, Tam below, the water roaring softly at their backs like a country saying goodbye.

Halfway down the first flight of rungs, the lantern light caught something scratched into the shaft wall at eye level — old, rust-flecked, nearly erased by time. Letters. Vhalorian script, which Ines could read now, thanks to three weeks of Tam's patient tutoring on the river.

Two words. A hand they recognized from the file, from the ledger, from a lifetime of not knowing they knew it.

*Keep going.*

Ines laughed — a real laugh this time, startled out of them, echoing down the shaft — and kept going.

Behind and above, faint through the stone, came the sound they'd been dreading: boots on rubble, and then the scrape of a body entering the drain shaft after them, and Ondar's voice, echoing down the dark, perfectly calm, perfectly reasonable:

"Dravenmoor. We should talk about what you owe me."
