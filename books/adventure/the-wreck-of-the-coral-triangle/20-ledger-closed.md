## 20. Ledger Closed

The lake had gone quiet in the way that only very large things can be quiet.

Ines stood on the shelf of black stone above the waterline, where the glacial melt ran clear as glass over the drowned archive, and held the ledger in both hands. It was lighter than it should have been. That was the first thing. Forty years of debt, a nation's worth of names, and the thing weighed less than a loaf of bread. Tam sat nearby on a flat rock with his arm bound and his face gray, watching Ines the way a man watches weather he can't decide whether to trust.

"You don't have to do this here," Tam said. "You could take it downriver. Show people. Let them see what Ondar's employers built."

"And then what?" Ines asked. "They frame it?"

Tam didn't laugh. His ribs were probably in no condition for laughing. But something moved at the corner of his mouth anyway — the exiles' particular talent, Ines had noticed, for finding humor in places where humor had no business surviving.

"It's a fair point," Tam admitted. "We're not famous for our museums."

Below them, through forty feet of impossibly clear water, the archive city lay in its cold sleep: rooftops like sleeping animals, streets laid out in the patient geometry of people who had expected to come back. Somewhere down there was the vault Tam had bled for. Somewhere down there was a file with Ines's mother's name on it, and Ines had read it, alone, three days ago, and had not yet found a way to say out loud what it said.

*She chose this,* the file said, in the language of officialdom that makes even heroism sound like paperwork. *Archivist Maren Dravenmoor initiated the seal-gate protocol at 04:12. All records preserved by submersion. No treasure recovered.*

No treasure recovered. As if the entire memory of a nation were an entry in a shipping manifest.

Odile came up the path from the skiff carrying the small iron pot she used for heating coffee over a spirit flame, because Odile Vasse's answer to grief, exhaustion, and apocalypse alike had always been to make coffee. She set it down on the stones, looked at the ledger in Ines's hands, and did not ask if Ines was sure. She had stopped asking that somewhere around the gorge, when Ines had voted to keep climbing on rations meant for two, and Odile had learned that asking Ines Dravenmoor if they were sure was like asking a river if it intended to run downhill.

"Wind's coming up the valley," Odile said instead. "If you're going to burn paper, burn it now, before it changes its mind."

"Practical," Ines said.

"I'm a practical woman. It's my only virtue and I've grown fond of it."

Ines knelt at the water's edge. The pages were damp from the lake spray, which was fitting, Ines thought. The forged ledger had been born in water — Vhaloria drowned so that its debts could be written, debts invented after the fact to launder a theft, names added to a book that had never owed anyone anything. And now it would end in water too, or fire, or some argument between the two.

The first page was the index. Columns of names in a clerk's careful hand, each one followed by a sum, each sum followed by the word *outstanding*. Outstanding. As if these people had simply failed to pay. As if a drowned nation had been delinquent.

Ines read a few of the names. Not all — there were hundreds, and reading all of them would take until dark, and besides, Ines suspected the dead did not need to be read aloud so much as released. But Ines read a few. A cooper named Hallas Verren, who owed four hundred crowns. A schoolteacher named Ilsa Merrow, who owed eleven. Eleven crowns. Someone had sat at a desk in a cartel counting-house and decided that a schoolteacher's ghost owed eleven crowns, and had felt nothing at all while writing it.

"That's the one that gets you," Tam said quietly, following Ines's eyes. "Not the big sums. The small ones. The big ones are greed. The small ones are habit."

Ines struck the spirit flame and touched it to the corner of the index page.

Paper burns strangely when it's damp. It doesn't catch so much as surrender — a slow, hissing, reluctant giving-up, the flame walking the margins like a cautious animal testing ice. Ines held the page until the heat crept toward the fingers, then set it on the stones and watched the names go. Hallas Verren went first, curling into black lace. Then Ilsa Merrow, her eleven crowns evaporating into the thin mountain air, owed to no one now, owed by no one, a debt unmade.

It should have felt like more, Ines thought. It should have been thunderous. Instead it felt like setting down a pack after a long carry — the sudden lightness almost painful, the shoulders rising half an inch on their own, the body refusing at first to believe the weight was really gone.

* * *

Odile poured the coffee without being asked, which was how Odile said most things.

They drank it sitting in a row on the black stones like crows on a fence, three ruined people watching a fire eat a lie. Below them the archive gleamed. Above them the glacier creaked its slow ancient sentence. Somewhere downriver, Ondar's body was still lodged in the spillway wreckage, or maybe the river had already taken him — rivers are efficient about the dead, Ines had learned, they process grief faster than people do.

"He offered me her file," Ines said. It was the first time Ines had said it aloud. "For safe passage out. Before the standoff. He had it in a waterproof case, like it was merchandise."

"What did you say?" Odile asked, though Ines suspected she already knew. You didn't collapse a nation's memory on top of a man and then negotiate with his ghost.

"I said I'd already read it." Ines turned the mug in their hands. "He thought the file was the prize. He thought truth was something you could hold hostage." A breath. "But it isn't currency, is it. That's the whole trick of it. He spent his whole life treating truth like coin — buying it, hoarding it, spending it where it bought him leverage. And it never once occurred to him that some truths aren't for sale because they were never property to begin with."

"Your mother knew that," Tam said.

"My mother knew that." Ines laughed, and it came out wrong, wet at the edges. "Forty years ago. She stood in a control room under this valley and pulled the gates shut on everything she loved, because the alternative was letting men like Ondar turn it into inventory. And then she walked out of history, changed her name, had a child, and never told me a word of it. She let me grow up thinking we were nobody from nowhere. She let me think the silence was shame."

"Wasn't it?" Odile asked gently.

"No." Ines looked at the last page of the ledger, still dry in their hands. The final entry. *M. Dravenmoor — outstanding.* Her handwriting, or a forgery of her handwriting, good enough to break a heart at ten paces. "It was armor. She sank the ledger's true twin along with everything else, and this one — this forgery — is the only version of her debt that ever existed. She never owed them anything. They wrote her name in a book to own her, and she beat them by becoming someone the book couldn't find."

Ines touched the flame to the last page.

Maren Dravenmoor burned slowly, the way mothers do, reluctant to leave anything unfinished. Ines held the burning page out over the lake and let the wind take the ash, and the ash drifted out across the clear water and settled, faintly, over the rooftops of the drowned city — a gray snowfall on a nation's grave, or perhaps, Ines thought, a letter finally delivered.

"There," Ines said, though there wasn't a there to point to. "Ledger closed."

Nobody spoke for a while. The fire ate itself down to a red eye and closed. The coffee steamed. Far below, a fish — or something shaped like a fish, or possibly just the light playing tricks on grieving eyes — moved along a drowned street as if walking home.

* * *

They broke camp in the gray gold of late afternoon, because Odile did not believe in camping anywhere longer than necessary and Tam's wound needed a real doctor, or at least a fake doctor with clean hands. Loading the skiff took an hour. There wasn't much to load. Three people, one dented coffee pot, a bell that hummed when the source drew near and would presumably hum forever now, having arrived, like a dog that wouldn't stop singing once it got what it wanted.

Ines carried the ledger's bronze chest down to the waterline last. Empty now. Just a box with a seal on it, heavy as sin and twice as useless.

"Throw it in?" Odile asked.

Ines considered it. The lake was right there, deep and patient, ready to keep another secret for another forty years. It would be easy. It would be tidy. It would be exactly the kind of ending a novelist would write and a guide would distrust.

"No," Ines said. "I'm done throwing things in water for other people's reasons." They stowed the empty chest in the bow, where it could sit in the daylight like any other piece of salvage. "Let it be junk. Junk floats. Junk gets sold. Junk doesn't drown anybody."

Tam managed a real laugh this time, and paid for it with a wince, and pronounced it worth it.

Odile took the tiller. The skiff slid off the black stones and out onto the lake, and the source valley began, gently, without ceremony, to close behind them — the walls drawing together, the glacier receding, the whole impossible place folding itself back into rumor. Ines stood in the stern and watched it go and did not feel free, exactly. Freedom, Ines was beginning to suspect, was not a feeling but a practice, something you did daily, like stretching, like honesty.

But something had gone out of the water ahead of them. Or into it. The ash of four hundred names, settling over the rooftops of the city their mother had saved by drowning it. The debt was never owed to the dead, Ines thought. The dead were paid in full the day they died. The debt was to the truth — and the truth, unlike treasure, survived submersion.

"Deckhand," Odile called forward, in the tone she used for orders disguised as questions. "You planning to stand there communing with geography all the way to Kiln Reach, or are you going to earn your keep?"

"Communing first," Ines called back. "Earning second. I'm told I'm expensive."

"You're told correctly."

And that was when Ines saw it — far down the lake, where the valley opened toward the river, a shape on the water that was not water. A boat. Small, moving fast, running without lights in the last of the day, coming up the channel toward the source that everyone alive believed was unreachable except to the three of them and one dead man.

Tam saw it a heartbeat later and went very still. "That's a remnant boat," he said. "That's our hull paint. That's—"

The boat turned slightly, catching the sun, and Ines saw the figure at its tiller raise one arm — not in threat, but in the unmistakable gesture of someone signaling a greeting, or a warning, or both.

Odile's hand found Ines's shoulder. "Well," she said. "Company."
