## 22. The Guide Who Walked Away

The river ended the way most things end — not with a crash but with a spreading, a letting go. Ines stood on the last jetty of Port Meridian and watched the brown water fan out into the gray-green sea, losing its argument about direction, becoming simply *gone*.

Six weeks since the source. Six weeks since the lake closed over Vhaloria's memory like a hand closing over a candle flame, and the three of them had come down out of the mountains with frostbitten fingers and nothing to show for it but each other and a story nobody would believe.

Ines had three letters in an inside pocket, and had been carrying them for six weeks the way you carry a stone you've decided not to put down.

"Staring at the water won't make it write itself," Odile said, coming up the jetty with two cups of something hot and terrible. The ferryman's coffee had survived everything the river had done to them — scuttled boat, blockade, three days on half rations — with its flavor intact, which was to say, punitive.

"I'm not staring at the water."

"You're staring at the water."

"I'm *contemplating the sea*," Ines said, accepting the cup. "It's a different activity. More dignified."

Odile leaned on the rail beside them, a compact woman with salt in her gray braid and a way of standing that suggested she'd never once been knocked off her feet by anything, including the news that her livelihood was forty feet down in the Gorge of Names. She'd salvaged the skiff, though. Of course she had. Odile Vasse did not lose things; she relocated them to the riverbed for safekeeping.

"Tam's letters went out on the morning packet," she said. "Twelve of them. One to every Vhalorian name she could find in the coastal registries." Odile sipped her coffee without flinching. "She says the exiles are starting a new ledger. Debts of the living. She wants to know if we'll sign the first page."

"What does the first page say?"

"'We were never erased. We were only waiting.'"

Ines looked at the sea for a while. "She's going to be insufferable now that she can walk again."

"She was insufferable before. The cane just slows her down enough for us to enjoy it."

* * *

The post office at Port Meridian was a green shack at the end of Fishgut Lane, run by a woman named Bree who had one arm, a parrot with a vocabulary that would have gotten them both exiled from a third country, and the serene authority of someone who had seen every kind of letter a human being could write and had stopped being impressed around her fortieth year.

Ines laid the three envelopes on the counter.

Bree looked at them. Looked at Ines. Looked at the addresses — the Mountain Guides' Guild of Corvane, the Alpine Authority of Sereval, and the Guild's disciplinary board, which had its own building and its own wax seal and had once sent Ines a letter with the word *BLACKLISTED* embossed in gold, as if it were an award.

"These going to be a problem?" Bree asked.

"They're confessions."

"Mm." Bree weighed the topmost envelope in her hand. "I get a lot of confessions. Most people write them and then come back the next day and buy them back." She set it down. "You going to be one of those?"

Ines thought about the mountain. Six years of thinking about it, and it had never once gotten smaller. A client named Aldous Wren, left above the storm line because Ines had made the call — the wrong call, the fast call, the call that kept the rest of the rope team alive and let one man's light go out alone in the white. Ines had told the guild a version of it. The version where the weather did it. The version where there was no choice.

There had been a choice. There was always a choice; that was the whole terrible point of being a guide. You were paid to choose, and the mountain charged interest.

"No," Ines said. "I'm not one of those."

Bree nodded, unimpressed in the way of the truly wise, and stamped the letters. The parrot screamed something in what might have been Vhalorian — Tam had been teaching it, gods help everyone — and Ines paid the postage with the last of the cartel money Ondar's second team had left in their abandoned dive camp, because there was a certain poetry in it that Ines intended to enjoy.

Outside, the morning was doing its best. Gulls wheeled over the fish market. Somewhere a bell buoy clanked — an ordinary bell, toneless, that hummed for no one.

"You know what happens now," Odile said. She'd followed, or more accurately she'd never left; she had a talent for being exactly where she'd decided to be. "Corvane will want you to stand trial. Sereval will want you to stand trial. The trials will take two years and end in the same blacklist, because confessions don't buy forgiveness, they just buy accuracy."

"I know."

"And you'll go anyway."

"I'll go anyway."

Odile looked out at the water. "Good," she said. "I'd have been disappointed otherwise. Can't have a deckhand with an unpaid moral debt; it's bad for the boat."

Ines turned. "Deckhand?"

"You heard me."

"I heard a word. I'd like to hear the terms."

Odile counted on her fingers, which were scarred from forty years of rope. "One: you haul when I say haul. Two: you don't steer. Three: you cook, because I cook like a man defending a position, not a woman feeding people." She paused. "Four: when your trials are done — both of them, however long it takes — the skiff comes back for you. Wherever you are. Whatever the weather."

"And if I'm blacklisted from a third country by then?"

"Then I'll have a deckhand with range," Odile said, and drank her coffee.

Ines had spent six years believing that the debt — the real one, the one under the money — could never be paid, only carried, like a stone in the chest, like a name on a ledger. Then they had burned the forged pages at the source and watched the ash go up the thermals, every freed name rising off the paper, including the mother's, including Ines's own, and had understood at last that the debt was never owed to the dead. The dead didn't want payment. They wanted the truth told and then left alone.

The letters were the truth told. The trials would be the leaving alone.

"Odile Vasse," Ines said formally, "I accept employment aboard your vessel at the disgraceful wage you're about to name."

"Room and board and a share of whatever the river gives up," Odile said. "Which, based on our history, will either be nothing or a national crisis."

"I'd take those odds."

* * *

They spent the afternoon fitting out the skiff, which Odile had named — with the grim satisfaction of a woman resurrecting her own funeral — the *Second Vasse*. Ines coiled line. Odile cursed at the tiller fitting. Tam arrived on her cane in the late light to supervise, which is to say to sit on an upturned crate and issue opinions.

"You're really going to stand trial," Tam said. It wasn't a question. Tam had spent a nation's last years learning the difference between questions and verdicts.

"Both countries."

"They'll take your certification permanently."

"They took it permanently six years ago. This just makes it honest."

Tam was quiet a moment. She was better at quiet than she used to be; the bullet had taught her some things the archive never could. "You know what I keep thinking about," she said. "The gorge. Your mother's name, chiseled fresh into the wall. She stood there, Ines. She stood in that canyon and she let them carve her in with the drowned, knowing what she was about to do, knowing the water was coming for her too." Tam's hand rested on her cane. "And you stood in the same place and did the same thing. Not because of the ledger. Because it was right."

"The ledger was forged. It wasn't right or wrong. It was paper."

"Everything's paper," Tam said. "The point is what you do with it."

The parrot, riding Tam's shoulder, chose this moment to announce, in a perfect imitation of Odile's gravel voice: *"You don't steer. You don't steer."*

"See?" Odile said. "Even the bird has absorbed the terms."

* * *

Ines mailed the letters at dusk, when the packet boat came through and Bree's window glowed yellow against the blue coming off the sea. It took four seconds. The clerk stamped them, the parrot said something unspeakable, and six years of carrying ended in a canvas mailbag.

Ines stood outside the green shack afterward and felt — nothing dramatic. No lightning. Just a lightness, suspicious at first, like a rope gone slack, and then, gradually, real. The mountain was still there. It would always be there. But it was no longer *owed* anything, and neither was Ines.

Odile was waiting at the jetty with the last of the coffee, which had somehow gotten worse with age, like a grudge.

"Well?" she said.

"Mailed."

"Feel anything?"

"Lighter. And slightly nauseous. Though that might be the coffee."

"It's the coffee." Odile handed over the cup anyway, because love is mostly handing people things that are bad for them. They stood together while the packet boat's lights crawled out toward the open water, carrying three envelopes toward two trials and a truth.

"The way I see it," Odile said, "we've got the whole coast and no schedule. There's a lighthouse north of here that pays salvage for anything the sea gives up. There's a festival at Colder Bay where they race punts and cheat shamelessly. And there's a stretch of river above Kiln Reach where Tam swears the old Vhalorian bells still hum, if you know how to listen — not the drowned ones. New ones. Ones people are casting."

"Bells for a country that doesn't exist."

"Bells for a country that's deciding to," Odile said. "First one's being raised next month. They asked if the *Second Vasse* would come. Something about needing a crew with experience losing things to the water and getting them back."

Ines looked down the jetty at the skiff — small, patched, unsinkable in the way of all things Odile had decided upon — and at Tam's silhouette in the cabin doorway, and at the dark line of the river running out to meet the sea, going on ahead of them like a road with no map.

"Deckhand Dravenmoor," Ines said, testing it. "Reporting for duty."

"Steer nothing," Odile warned.

"Steer nothing."

"And Dravenmoor?" Odile's voice went softer than the water. "Whatever they say at those trials — whatever they take — you come back to this boat. That's not a term of employment. That's a fact of the river."

Ines drank the terrible coffee and watched the lights go out and found, to their considerable surprise, that they had no argument left in them at all.

"Fact of the river," Ines agreed.

The bell buoy clanked. The tide turned. And in the morning, with no debts and no map, the *Second Vasse* pointed her patched bow up the coast, toward the sound of new bells.
