## 1. The Man Who Pays in Small Notes

The ferry into Lowmark ran with its engine cut, drifting on the current the last half-mile because the pilot was too poor for fuel and too proud to admit it. Vesper Ravensworth stood at the rail with his collar up and his papers in his breast pocket and watched the town assemble itself out of the rain: a brown river, a brown sky, and between them a shelf of rooftops stacked up the hillside like crates that had been unloaded by someone in a hurry.

He counted the chimneys smoking. Fourteen. A town that size would have a harbor office, a constable, a rail agent, and a tavern where all three drank. It would not have a telegraph line worth trusting, or a photograph of him, or anyone who had seen his face before Tuesday. That was the arithmetic he ran on every new place, and in nine years the arithmetic had never once been wrong.

The ferry grounded on the shingle with a shudder. He stepped off into water two inches deep, which told him everything about the season. The river was already up.

"Name?" said the harbormaster, a woman with a ledger and a cold in her sinuses.

"Corben Ash," he said, and let her hear the flat vowels of the eastern coal towns, the accent he'd bought in a dockside tavern in Marrowgate for the price of a bottle and an hour's practice. "Fitter. Looking for work."

She wrote it down. *Corben Ash.* The name was three weeks old. He had been Tomas Reeve before that, and a man with no name at all for a month in the middle, riding gravel trains and sleeping in culverts. Names, he had learned, were like boots: you wore them until they fit, then until they wore through, then you threw them away and found another pair. He had never once been sorry to throw a pair away.

"Skills?"

"Bridges. Trestles. I can tell you what's rotten and what's only tired."

That earned him a look over the top of the ledger. "The Lowmark spur," she said. "The consortium closed it two years back. Nobody's inspected it since, because nobody's paid to. You want to work for free?"

"I want to work for whatever the town can pay."

"Small notes, then." She almost smiled. "Everyone here pays in small notes. It's a town of small notes."

He took a room above a chandler's for four coppers a week, paid two in advance, and did not unpack. Unpacking was a habit for men who intended to be somewhere. He hung his coat on the back of the door, and before he did anything else — before the bed, before the water, before the window — he turned the coat inside out and checked the lining.

The seam above the left hip had been restitched so many times the thread had gone from white to gray. He pressed his thumbnail along it and felt the stiff rectangle beneath: oilcloth, and inside the oilcloth, folded into eighths, a survey map. Not the forged papers in his breast pocket — those were replaceable, a shilling's work from a man in Marrowgate with a steady hand. The map was the other thing. The map was nine years old and it had his own handwriting on it, and his own signature, and eleven names written on the back in a different, shakier hand, added later by a man who could not sleep.

He had never been able to burn it. He had told himself it was evidence, leverage, insurance — that a day would come when the consortium's lawyers came for him and he would need to prove what he'd been made to sign and what he'd refused to. That was the story he told himself, and he told it so often it had gone smooth as a river stone. But at three in the morning, in whatever room he was not unpacking, he knew the truth was simpler and worse. It was the only honest thing he owned.

He turned the coat right-side out and went to look at the bridge.

* * *

The Lowmark spur left the main line at a junction overgrown to the waist with bindweed, ran two miles along the river bluff, and ended at a loading dock that hadn't loaded anything since the timber quota collapsed. The trestle crossed a side-channel where the river slowed and dropped its silt, and it was, in the engineering sense of the word, a corpse.

Vesper walked it the way other men walked into churches.

He started at the abutment, because abutments told you what the builders had thought of the ground, and this one told him they'd thought little. Dry-laid stone, no grout, founded on gravel that the river had been quietly stealing for thirty years. He could see the theft in the scalloped bank below. He knelt, pressed his palm to the stone, and read the temperature of it — cold at the base, warming toward the waterline, which meant seepage, which meant the frost would be levering it apart every winter like a patient burglar.

Then the piles. He walked the low tide of silt beneath the trestle and put his knife to each pile in turn, the old timber-man's test, the blade angled and the wrist loose, listening with his hand. Sound wood rang. Rotten wood went *thock*, a dead note, a knuckle on a coffin lid. Six of the fourteen piles in the second bent went *thock*. Two didn't answer at all, because they were no longer piles; they were memories of piles wearing the clothes of piles, the river having eaten them from the waterline up and left the shells standing out of habit.

He climbed. The deck planks were silvered and sprung, and the handrail had gone the color of old cheese, but the ironwork was the interesting thing. He crouched at the first connection and saw the bolts — square-shanked, hand-cut threads, the work of a forge shop, not a mill. And stamped into the head of each bolt, worn nearly smooth but still legible when he tilted it against the light:

**K.R.**

He sat back on his heels. Kestrian Rail. He knew the mark from textbooks and from one museum case in the capital, behind glass, under a card that said *DEFUNCT UNDERTAKING*. Kestria had been a mountain country east of the Empty Quarter, annexed forty years ago in a war so short the newspapers had barely filled a column with it. The maps had been redrawn. The name had been scraped off the atlases the way you'd scrape a burnt edge off toast. And here was its ironwork, holding up a trestle in a river town nobody had ever heard of, holding it up badly.

"Everyone asks about the bolts."

Vesper came off his heels and around before he'd decided to move, which annoyed him. A boy stood on the bank below, maybe twelve, barefoot, holding a fishing line with no hook on it. The boy had been there a while. Vesper had not heard him, and he heard everything. He filed the failure away with the seriousness other men reserved for money.

"Do they," Vesper said.

"The old man Doss. He says the bridge is older than the town." The boy squinted up. "He says the Kestrians built it before the war, when the river ran different. Are you the new inspector?"

"Who says there's a new inspector?"

"The harbormaster. She told the chandler, and the chandler told my mother, and my mother told everyone." The boy shrugged, as if to say: that's how a town works, you'll learn. "Are you going to fix it?"

"No one's paying to fix it."

"Then why are you here?"

It was a fair question and Vesper had no honest answer, so he gave the one he kept polished. "Work is work. Even counting what's dead." He climbed down, and the boy watched him the whole way with the flat, unblinking attention of the young, who have not yet learned to pretend they aren't looking.

* * *

He spent three days on the trestle, and by the third day he had stopped pretending he was doing it for the small notes.

There was a discipline to inspection that suited him the way prayer was said to suit other men. You could not think about yourself and read a structure at the same time; the work demanded all of you, every sense pointed outward. The grain of the timber, the rust weeping from a connection in orange tears, the way a bent leaned a quarter-degree off plumb and told you which way the current had been pushing it for a decade. A bridge was a confession. Every mistake its builders had made, every corner they'd cut, every flood they'd underestimated — it was all written there, and it had all already happened, and that was the comfort of it. Bridges failed in the past tense. You walked a dead structure and read its autopsy, and nothing in it could hurt you, because it was already over.

The Vell Bridge had not been over. That was the difference. On the Vell Bridge he had read the confession in advance — the fatigue cracks, the undersized chords, the load rating he'd been told to raise and had raised, because the directors' names were nowhere on the paperwork and his name was everywhere — and the bridge had failed anyway, at six in the evening, with the eleven of them on it, the evening shift walking home across the span because the footpath was faster than the long way around.

He did not think about it in daylight. In daylight there was the trestle, and the trestle was enough.

On the fourth day he wrote his report. He did it in the chandler's back room by lamplight, on paper the harbormaster had scrounged, and he wrote it in the plain language he'd learned from an old inspector in his apprenticeship, a man named Cade who said that reports were written for the man who'd have to answer for them at the inquiry. *The second bent will not survive another flood season. The third bent will not survive this one. Recommend closure of the channel to all traffic and demolition of the structure before the river rises.*

He signed it *Corben Ash*, and the false name went down on the paper smooth as water, and something in his chest pulled tight, the way it always did. Nine years of false names and the hand never stopped wanting to write the true one.

He delivered the report at dusk. The harbormaster read it standing in her doorway with the lamp behind her, and he watched her face do the small arithmetic of a town with no money: the cost of demolition, the cost of doing nothing, the odds the river would do the demolition for free.

"You're certain?"

"I'm certain."

"The channel can't close. Half the town's boats moor up that side-channel."

"Then half the town's boats are moored against a bridge that's coming down. Move them."

She looked at him a long moment. "You talk like a man who's watched something come down."

"I talk like a man who reads bridges."

"Where did you work before here?"

"Everywhere the work was." He touched his cap. "I'll want my pay."

"Two days. Small notes." And then, as he turned: "Ash. There's a stranger came in on the morning boat asking after salvage rights on the river. Cargo broker, she says. Asking who's new in town, too. Asking about you."

The evening went cold along Vesper's spine, one vertebra at a time, the way it had learned to.

"What did you tell her?"

"That a fitter named Corben Ash was inspecting the spur." The harbormaster's eyes were steady and unbothered, and that was the worst of it — she had nothing to hide, so she couldn't understand a man who did. "Why? Is there a reason I shouldn't have?"

"No reason," Vesper said. "It's a small town. Everyone tells everyone."

"That's right," she said. "That's exactly what it is."

* * *

He walked back along the bluff in the dark with his collar up and his mind running the problem the way he'd run a load calculation, fast and cold.

A cargo broker did not ask about a trestle fitter. A trestle fitter was the least interesting man in any town; that was the whole architecture of the name *Corben Ash*, load-bearing dullness, a man built to be forgotten. Someone asking after the new arrival was either a constable, a creditor, or a scout — and he owed no money anywhere, and constables did not come by boat asking about salvage.

Which left scout. And a scout meant someone was looking, and someone looking meant someone had a reason, and in nine years the reasons had always come down to the same two: the man he had been, or the paper in his coat.

He stopped at the top of the chandler's stairs and made himself run the check anyway, the nightly liturgy: papers in the breast pocket, correct, unmemorable, paid for. Map in the lining, present, pressed flat against his hip like a second bone. Room as he'd left it, window latched, nothing moved, nothing taken, the dust he'd sprinkled on the floorboards unbroken. He had been doing this check in one room or another for nine years, and he did it now with the ease of long habit, and the ease itself was the saddest thing about him, though he had never let himself think so in daylight.

He lay down on the bed without undressing and listened to the river.

It was louder tonight. He had noticed it that morning and told himself it was the wind, and noticed it again at dusk and told himself it was the rain upcountry, and now, in the dark, with nothing left to tell himself, he let the engineer in him say what the engineer had been saying since the ferry: the river was up more than the season accounted for. There was rain in the high country, a lot of it, and it was coming down this brown water toward Lowmark, and it would arrive when it arrived, and the second bent would not survive it, and he had said so in a report signed by a man who did not exist, to a town that could not afford to listen.

Sleep came the way it always came, late and thin. And at three in the morning, in the gray seam between, he did the thing he did every night, the thing Sable Idris would one day know about and he would never forgive himself for confessing: he lay still with his eyes closed and drew the Vell Bridge in his head. Every chord, every rivet, every span, from memory, line by line, the way it should have been built — the way he should have refused to sign it — and he rebuilt it nightly, and it fell nightly, and eleven men and women walked home across it into the river, and he lay in the dark in a room above a chandler's in a town called Lowmark under a name that was three weeks old, and waited for morning.

The river rose all night. He could hear it taking the banks a foot at a time, patient as a creditor.

And just before dawn, through the floorboards, through the fog of half-sleep, he heard a sound he would remember for the rest of his life: a knock at the chandler's door below, and the chandler's voice, and then a woman's voice, low and even, asking — not about salvage rights, not about the trestle, not about the river at all.

Asking, politely, whether the new man had come into town with a boat.

Because something had come down the river in the night, the voice said. Something the flood had let go of. And it had washed up against the mooring pilings at the foot of the old Kestrian trestle, and it was bronze-bound, and it was stamped with a two-headed hawk, and the woman wanted to know who had found it first.
