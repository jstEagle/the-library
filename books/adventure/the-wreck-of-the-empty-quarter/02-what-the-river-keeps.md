## 2. What the River Keeps

The storm came up the valley at dusk like something being chased.

Vesper felt it before he heard it—a pressure change in his ears, the kind of drop that made old river men look at their mooring lines and say nothing. He was on deck of the *Cormorant*, his boat in the way that a rented room is yours: he'd bought her cheap three years ago from a widow in Callow's Bend who wanted nothing but to be rid of the smell of her dead husband's tobacco, and he'd kept her moving ever since, because a boat that moves is harder to find than a man with no name.

He checked the lines twice. Then a third time, because the habit was older than the fear that had built it. The mooring pilings at Lowmark were black ironbark driven into the riverbank by some engineer forty years dead, and they groaned when the wind found them, a low animal sound that Vesper had learned to sleep through the way other men slept through church bells.

By full dark the rain was coming down in ropes. He sat below with a lamp turned low and the survey map unrolled across his bunk—the stolen one, sewn originally into his coat lining until the damp of three monsoons had taught him better—and went over it again the way he did most nights, not because there was anything new in it but because the act of reading it kept certain other thoughts from finding him. Lines and grades. Cut depths. The closed mountain spurs of the Empty Quarter, drawn in a consortium draftsman's hand so precise it looked printed rather than drawn.

Somewhere past midnight, the storm reached down into the valley floor and put its shoulder against the world.

He heard the river change before anything else. A flood river has a voice, and this one climbed an octave—water coming down off the high country all at once, carrying with it whatever the high country no longer wanted. Trees. Fences. Once, on the Serrault, he'd watched a whole boathouse go by upright, its doors still latched, as if it were simply relocating. The *Cormorant* bucked against her lines. The lamp swung on its gimbal and threw shadows in circles around the cabin, and Vesper sat very still with both hands flat on the chart table and let the boat do what boats do, which is argue with water and lose slowly.

He didn't sleep. That wasn't unusual either.

* * *

Dawn came gray and rinsed-out, the rain thinned to a drizzle that seemed less like weather than residue. The river had risen four feet in the night and gone the color of strong tea, thick with silt, running fast enough that driftwood hit the pilings with knocks he could feel through the hull.

He was on deck with coffee he'd boiled too long when he saw it.

At first he took it for a barrel—one of the big salted-fish casks the canneries upstream lost every spring. It hung against the downstream face of his second piling, snagged where a cross-brace caught it, turning slowly in the eddy. But barrels don't sit that deep in the water, and they don't turn that heavily, and after a while the current shifted and showed him a corner. Square. Bronze-bound. Wood gone dark as old blood.

Vesper stood at the rail with his cooling coffee and performed the small internal argument he always performed, the one between the part of him that wanted to know things and the part of him that had learned, expensively, what knowing things cost. Nine years ago the first part had won, and eleven people had gone into the Serrault with a bridge folded under them, and the second part had been running the arithmetic ever since.

Curiosity is a debt, he told himself. You pay it later, with interest.

He got the boathook anyway.

It took the better part of two hours. The chest was heavier than it looked—he rigged a line under it with a slipknot and hauled hand over hand, the *Cormorant* listing toward it, the river trying to keep what it had carried. Twice the line slipped. The third time he took a turn around the bollard and let the boat's own lean do the work, and the chest came up out of the water streaming brown, and he understood immediately why it had sunk: it was lined in lead. Someone had wanted this thing to go down and stay down.

Which meant someone had failed at wanting that. And failures like that had a way of being noticed.

He set it on deck and stood over it, dripping, breathing hard, telling himself he would leave it on the bank for the salvage warden like any honest man, and knowing even as he thought it that Lowmark's salvage warden was a consortium appointment and everything that washed up on a consortium river belonged, eventually, to the consortium.

The seal stopped him with his pry-bar already fitted to the lid.

Two-headed hawk, wings spread, rendered in bronze inlay gone green at the edges. He knew heraldry the way he knew bridges—by having crossed under too much of it—and he knew this one, or half-knew it, the way you half-know a word in a language you studied as a child. Two heads facing away from each other, sharing one body. A bird watching both directions because neither direction could be trusted.

Kestria.

A name that wasn't a name anymore. He'd seen it once, years ago, in a border office in a country that had since redrawn itself: a customs clerk stamping an old atlas with DISUSED across whole pages, and Kestria had been one of them, its mountains and rivers struck through in red ink like a cancelled account. He remembered thinking, with the particular coldness of a young man who believed systems worked, that a country could be erased the same way a line could be decommissioned. Paper over paper. He'd been right about the mechanism. He'd just never expected to be standing over the paperwork.

The lock was bronze too, and old, and it gave to the pry-bar with a crack that sounded obscenely loud in the post-storm quiet.

* * *

He opened it alone, in the cabin, with the hatch battened and the lamp trimmed low. Not because he intended to steal it—though he understood, setting hands on the contents, that intention and outcome had already parted company—but because whatever was inside had been lead-lined and drowned on purpose, and he wanted to know it before anyone else did. Self-preservation dressed up as caution. It usually was.

Documents. Dozens of them, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, the ink beneath miraculously dry. He unwrapped them one at a time with the care of a man defusing something.

Certificates of registry, in a script he couldn't read—dense, vertical, beautiful, the letters marching like rail spikes set to gauge. Ledgers with columns ruled in faded violet. A bundle of letters tied with wire, sealed with wax stamped by the same two-headed hawk. And beneath everything, flat and long, a leather case that made his hands go still before he'd even touched it, because he knew the dimensions the way he knew the dimensions of his own hands.

A survey folio.

He drew it out. Opened it. And forgot, for a long moment, to breathe.

Rail line. Grades, curves, cut-and-fill figures, tunnel alignments, bridge sites marked in a notation system he didn't recognize—not consortium standard, not imperial standard, something older and stranger, with symbols like little birds perched on the contour lines. The route ran up out of a valley he couldn't place, climbing northwest through country the atlas in the border office had shown as blank white above the tree line, and then—

Then it went through the glacier.

Not around it. Through. A pass marked with a single symbol repeated three times, and dates in the margin—weeks, specific weeks, a window—and Vesper read those marginal notes three times before the engineer in him surfaced through the fugitive and said, quietly, *this is real*. Whoever had surveyed this had done it properly. The grades were conservative where they needed to be and bold where boldness was justified. The curve radii were the work of someone who had walked every foot of it. This was not a fantasy drawn by exiles dreaming of home. This was a buildable line through impossible country, surveyed by a master, and it was older than any railway he'd ever worked, and it had been sealed in lead and sunk in a river.

He sat back on his bunk with the folio open across his knees and felt the strange vertigo of a professional looking at work better than his own.

There was one more item. A letter, separate from the bundle, sealed but unstamped—as if it had never been meant for any postal system, only for whoever finally pried the chest open. The wax held the hawk. Across the face of it, in careful trade-tongue, in handwriting that trembled only at the end:

*To the one who finds this. If you are reading our letters, then we are fewer than we hoped. What is in this chest is not treasure. It is proof.*

Vesper put it down without opening it. Some doors you knock on; some you leave shut until you've decided whether you want to live on the other side.

* * *

It was the ledgers that did it, in the end. Not the hawk, not the letter, not even the impossible pass. He'd almost convinced himself—almost—that he could repack the chest, row it to the warden's dock, invent a story about finding it snagged, collect whatever finder's fee the consortium paid and forget the whole thing by autumn.

Then he turned a ledger page and saw the watermark.

It was faint. Backlit against the lamp, visible only when he tilted the page, the way such things always are—the private signature of the paper mill pressed into the sheet itself. Most mills used their own marks: a fleur-de-lis, a millwheel, a founder's initials. Vesper had handled consortium paper for nine years, had signed his ruin onto it, had watched their lawyers slide exhibits across tables with this exact watermark glowing sideways in the lamplight of hearing rooms.

The crossed rails and star. Meridian Consolidated. The consortium's own stock paper.

Under the two-headed hawk of Kestria. Inside a chest sealed in lead and drowned.

He set the ledger down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be loaded.

His mind did what trained minds do under stress: it began assembling the structure, load by load. Kestria annexed—when? Twenty-five years ago? Thirty? Before his time, certainly, back when the consortium was younger and hungrier and buying governments the way it now bought closed rail lines for pennies. And here was consortium paper bearing a Kestrian state seal, which meant the consortium had been printing documents *for* Kestria—or *about* Kestria—or selling Kestria's maps and surveys to whoever swallowed it, and this chest was the receipt. The evidence. Somebody had gathered it all up, sealed it against water and time, and tried to move it somewhere safe, and the river had decided otherwise.

And now it lay open on the bunk of a man with forged papers and no fixed name, in a town where the consortium owned the docks, the warden, and very likely the constable.

Vesper looked at the folio again—at the pass, the weeks, the little bird-symbols perched along the contour lines—and felt the two halves of himself pull in opposite directions like a mooring line in flood. The survivor said: put it back, sink it yourself, deeper, further downstream, let the river finish the job somebody botched. The survivor had kept him alive for nine years and deserved to be heard.

But the engineer was looking at that route through the glacier, and the engineer was already, traitorously, involuntarily, checking the grades in his head. Estimating the cut depths. Wondering about the tunnel alignments, the drainage, the snow-shed intervals. Wondering who had surveyed it, and why, and what had happened to them, and whether the line had ever been built or had died on paper the way Kestria had died on paper.

*Proof,* the letter said.

He repacked the chest. All of it except the folio, which he told himself he was keeping out only to study, which was true, and which was also the first lie he'd told since coming to Lowmark that he didn't fully believe. He rewrapped the oilcloth, lowered the lid, and looked around the cabin for a hiding place, and realized there wasn't one—not on a boat this small, not from searchers who knew what they were doing. The chest would have to go ashore, buried, tonight, somewhere the rising river wouldn't take it back.

He was still standing there, weighing the thing in his hands' memory, when he heard footsteps on the dock boards above.

Light ones. Unhurried. The steps of someone who had every right to be there, which in Vesper's experience was exactly how the wrong people walked.

He blew out the lamp.

* * *

Through the gap in the hatch he watched a figure come down the bank path and stop at the head of his gangplank—a woman, by the set of the shoulders, in a traveling coat dark with drizzle, hands clasped behind her back in the manner of someone inspecting property she hadn't yet decided to buy. She looked at the *Cormorant* for a long moment. Then, unhurried, she counted the mooring pilings. One. Two. Three. Her lips moved slightly on the third.

Then she looked directly at the gap in his hatch, at the darkness where his eyes were, and smiled as if she could see him perfectly.

"Good morning," she called, in a voice pitched to carry over water. "I'm looking for the man who owns this boat. I'm told the river brought him a present."

Vesper stood motionless in the dark of his cabin, the survey folio pressed flat against his chest, and understood that invisibility—the discipline of nine years, the whole architecture of his survival—had come apart sometime in the night, silently, the way bad bridges do.

The woman waited at the foot of the gangplank, patient as floodwater, and did not leave.
