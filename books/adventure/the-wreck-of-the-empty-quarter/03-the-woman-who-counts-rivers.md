## 3. The Woman Who Counts Rivers

The river had eleven mouths at Lowmark, and Sable Idris counted them the way other women counted coins—by touch, by habit, by need.

She stood on the customs wharf in the gray hour after dawn, when the fog still owned the water and the fishing boats came in blind, guided by bells they tied to their masts. Eleven channels braided out from the town's front door into the delta, and every one of them had a name that appeared on no chart: the Widow, the Twins, the Long Throat, the Sifter, and seven more her grandmother had taught her before she could read. Her grandmother had learned them from a man who had been born on the far side of the mountains, in a country whose name he only ever whispered, as if the wind might carry it to the wrong ears.

Sable counted the channels twice. Nine were running high with last night's storm-melt; two had gone slack and silted, which meant the flood was already turning, already thinking about leaving. She noted it the way she noted everything—in the ledger behind her eyes, where nothing was written down and nothing was ever lost.

Then she turned to the business of finding a liar.

* * *

The harbor master's office smelled of tar and old tea, and its keeper, a woman named Ostrey with forearms like dock rope, looked up when Sable laid her papers on the counter.

"Cargo broker," Ostrey read aloud, without inflection. "Idris. Out of Corvane."

"Corvane," Sable agreed. It was a real city. That was the trick of good papers—they were true everywhere except where it mattered.

"Broker for whom?"

"Myself. I move freight nobody else will quote. Salvage especially." She let the word sit there between them, plain as a coin on a counter. "There's been flood salvage coming ashore along this stretch. I'm told Lowmark's seen its share."

Ostrey's face did something small and closed, like a shutter moving half an inch. "Told by whom?"

"A shipping agent in Marrowford. He said a chest came up against some mooring pilings after the storm. Bronze-bound. Heavy."

"He said that, did he." Ostrey poured tea she did not offer. "Marrowsford men say a lot of things. Half of what comes up in a flood is junk. Doors. Dead sheep. Somebody's grandmother's chairs. You want me to walk the banks with you cataloguing dead sheep?"

"I want to know who found anything worth keeping," Sable said pleasantly, "and whether they know what salvage law says about unregistered recovery."

"And what does salvage law say?"

"It says a finder who reports within three days is owed a finder's fee. A finder who doesn't report—" she sipped the tea, which was terrible, "—is owed something else entirely."

Ostrey looked at her for a long moment, and Sable watched her do the arithmetic brokers and harbor masters are both fluent in: what is this woman worth to me, what is she dangerous to, and which number is bigger. "There's a boatman," Ostrey said finally. "Lives on his own boat, moored past the old tannery slip. Keeps to himself. Came through a month back with forged-looking papers and too much money for a man inspecting rotten trestles. If anything washed up on this river, it would have washed up on him. He's moored right where the current dumps things."

"Name?"

"That's the funny part." Ostrey almost smiled. "He gave one when he registered. Then the fisherfolk started calling him something else. They call him the Engineer, because he climbed down under the trestle bridge in the rain to look at the pilings, and he told them exactly which one would go first and when. And it went first, and when he said." She set down her cup. "Men like that usually have reasons for not being called what they're called."

"I'll be careful," Sable said, which was a lie, because careful was not the same as cautious, and she had never once in her life managed to be cautious about the thing that mattered.

* * *

She found his boat at the tannery slip that afternoon, and she understood immediately why the current delivered things here. The slip jutted into the river like a cupped hand, and the channel bent around it in a slow eddy that gathered whatever the flood let go: logs, weed, barrels, drowned branches. A boat moored there didn't have to search the river. The river searched itself and brought the findings to your door.

It was a good boat—a thirty-foot river ketch, black-hulled, patched in places but patched well, with the particular neatness of a vessel kept by someone who understood that a boat was the last honest thing a man could own. Tools were stowed in ranks. Lines were coiled in flanders, not hanked. Even the rust streaks below the scuppers had been scrubbed and painted over.

And the man himself sat in the cockpit with a surveyor's chain spread across his knees, cleaning link by link with an oiled rag, and he did not look up when her boots hit the pontoon, which told her he had heard her coming for at least fifty meters and had decided not to care.

"You're moored in a catch-all," she said.

"I'm moored in a quiet place." He still hadn't looked up. His voice was flat and unhurried, a voice used to being heard over machinery. "Quiet places get visitors eventually. I'd hoped for later."

"Sable Idris. Cargo broker, out of Corvane." She stopped at the edge of his cockpit, close enough to talk, far enough that boarding him would be an act, not an accident. "I'm asking after flood salvage. There's a fee in it for you if you've found anything, and trouble if you've found something and sat on it."

Now he looked up, and she got her first full look at his face, and she thought: tired. Not lazy-tired or drunk-tired but the deep-set weariness of a man who sleeps the way soldiers sleep, in shifts, with one ear open. He had the hands of an engineer—scarred knuckles, ink stains that never fully washed—and the eyes of someone reading her the way she'd read the delta that morning, counting her channels, deciding which ones ran and which had silted shut.

"Flood salvage," he repeated. "You're the second person this week to use that exact phrase. First one was a fisherman who wanted to sell me a drowned bicycle."

"Was the bicycle bronze-bound?"

"No." He went back to his chain. "Nothing's come up on my lines but driftwood and a dead pig. I don't know anything about any chest."

That was the first lie. She knew it the way she knew the river—by feel, by the way the current of his voice changed around the word *chest*, a half-second hitch, a stone in the stream. She filed it without letting it show.

"Well," she said cheerfully, "if a chest does come up, salvage law's very generous to finders who report promptly. Twenty percent of assessed value, plus costs. More if the contents turn out to be historical. There are collectors who pay absurd money for pre-Annexation documents—maps, seals, letters. Anything with an old provenance."

Something moved behind his eyes at the word *seals*. Small. Fast. Gone.

"I'll keep it in mind," he said, "if a chest floats into my cockpit."

Second lie. Two lies in under five minutes, and both of them clean, professional lies—the kind a man tells when he has told so many they come out smooth as planed wood. Sable felt something she rarely felt with marks, which was respect. This was not a smuggler or a coward or a fool. This was a craftsman.

She decided to show him hers.

* * *

"The thing about salvage," she said, settling onto his pontoon edge uninvited, pulling off one boot to shake out grit, an intrusion calculated to look careless, "is that everyone thinks it's about luck. Flood takes a thing, river gives it back, finder gets rich. But it's never luck. Water has habits. Debris travels in lanes—you can predict where a floating object will beach within a hundred meters if you know the channel geometry and the discharge rate." She nodded at the tannery slip. "This eddy, for instance. Anything the river lets go of between the second bend and the ferry crossing ends up right here, against your hull. You didn't moor here because it's quiet. You moored here because it's a net."

For the first time, the man put down his rag.

"You count rivers," he said. Not a question.

"I grew up on one."

"Which one?"

"One with more names than maps," she said, and watched him take that in, watch him decide—not yet—what it meant. "My grandmother taught me the channels before she taught me letters. Said a river was the only honest map left, because it redraws itself every season and never pretends otherwise. Everything else—" she shrugged, "—everything else is drawn by people who want something."

He was quiet a moment. "Your grandmother sounds like an engineer."

"She sounded like a refugee," Sable said, "but yes, same skill set."

And there it was—the flicker. Not recognition exactly; he was too controlled for that. But a recalculation. She could nearly see the abacus behind his eyes sliding beads: *refugee, broker, counting channels, asking about seals.* She let him calculate. Letting a careful man think he's figured you out is how you learn what he's figured.

"You inspect bridges," she said. "That's what the fisherfolk say. You looked at the trestle and told them which piling would fail, and it failed on schedule."

"Trestles aren't complicated. Rot is rot."

"They also say you wouldn't give your name. Just signed the register with initials and paid in small notes. New notes. Counted twice."

"Some people count twice."

"I count everything twice," Sable said. "It's why I'm good at my work. So let me save us both some time, Engineer. Something bronze-bound came up on this river four nights ago. Someone found it. Someone is either sitting on it or trying to move it quietly, and I will find it, because finding things is the entire content of my character. The only variable is whether the finder deals with me on good terms or bad ones. Good terms involve money. Bad terms involve the consortium's salvage agents, who arrived in Marrowford yesterday on the morning packet—two of them, gray coats, traveling as insurance assessors—and who are considerably less pleasant than I am."

She said it plainly, watching his face, and this time she got what she was fishing for: not a flicker but a stillness. A total, deliberate absence of reaction, held a beat too long, the way a man holds his breath passing a graveyard.

"You know a lot," he said at last, "for a cargo broker."

"I know a lot for a lot of things." She pulled her boot back on. "Here's my card. Corvane address is good for another month. If a chest happens to wash up—or happened to—I pay better than anyone gray-coated, and I ask fewer questions than you'd believe." She stood, balanced easily on the moving pontoon. "One more thing, since we're being honest with each other."

"We haven't been honest once."

"No," she agreed, smiling. "Isn't it restful? Most conversations, you spend all night deciding which truths to spend. With a professional, you can just relax." She stepped back onto the wharf planks. "The seal on that chest, if it exists—it won't be a merchant's mark. It'll be a two-headed hawk, wings down, standing on a broken crown. And if you've seen it, then you already know this isn't about money, and so do I, and that makes us the only two people in Lowmark having the same conversation."

She walked away before he could answer, because the answer didn't matter. What mattered was that she'd spent the seal like a coin tossed into dark water, just to hear the splash—and the splash had told her the depth.

Behind her, the river ran on through its eleven mouths, saying nothing, keeping everything.

* * *

She rented a room above the chandlery and spent the evening doing what she always did in a new town: learning it from the window. By dusk she had the rhythms—when the night-soil cart ran, when the tavern crowd thinned, which lane the constable avoided after dark. And she had the boat, black against the last light, with its single lantern burning late.

He was awake. Of course he was awake. Men who lied that smoothly slept badly.

She made herself a pot of the chandlery's terrible tea and laid out her grandmother's letters on the table—the real ones, the ones she carried sewn into her own coat lining, because apparently that was simply what people like them did. Twelve letters, water-stained, in a script that existed nowhere on any living map. Her grandfather's hand. The surveyor who had walked the glacier pass with brass instruments and a nation in his breast pocket, measuring a railway for a country that had nine years left to live.

*They will erase the name first,* he had written, *and the roads second, and the graves last of all. Paper outlives borders. Get the paper over the mountains.*

The paper had gotten over the mountains. The paper had gotten into a chest. The chest had gotten into a river.

And the river, faithful old honest map, had carried it straight to a man who inspected bridges and paid in small notes and lied like a craftsman—and who had, unless Sable missed her guess entirely, opened it alone in the dark and seen the two-headed hawk and understood exactly none of what he was holding, except that it was dangerous.

She should have felt frustration. Instead she felt the cold bright excitement she always felt at the start of a difficult passage, when the channel narrows and the water quickens and every instinct says turn back.

Below her window, the lantern in the cockpit went out.

A minute later it came back on. Went out again. On.

Not a signal for her—he couldn't know her window. A signal for someone else, or a nervous habit, or—

No. She leaned closer. Out. On. Out. On. Steady rhythm, patient, deliberate. Three long, two short.

Downriver, near the customs wharf, a lantern answered. Three long, two short.

Sable stood very still in the dark room, teacup forgotten, and watched the two lights speak to each other across the sleeping town, and revised every number in her ledger at once.

The engineer wasn't hiding from the consortium.

He was signaling them.

Or—she made herself consider it, because a counter who counts once is a fool—he was signaling *someone* pretending to be them, or warning them off, or feeding them a false position, because a man that careful never used a code he hadn't already double-crossed.

Either way, the quiet place at the tannery slip had just become the loudest spot on the river.

She blew out her candle, sat down in the dark, and began, methodically, to plan how to follow a man who was better at disappearing than she was—at least until morning, when the gray coats came off the packet boat at the customs wharf, and the counting would stop being a game.

The river had eleven mouths. By dawn, she intended to be watching all of them.
