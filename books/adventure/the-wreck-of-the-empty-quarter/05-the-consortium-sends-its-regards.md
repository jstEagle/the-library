## 5. The Consortium Sends Its Regards

The knock came at dusk, three measured raps, the kind a man makes when he has already decided the door will open.

Vesper was on his boat, mending a lantern shutter with wire and pliers, and for one long moment he simply stopped moving. In nine years of drifting he had learned that the first instinct—freeze, listen, count exits—was worth more than any forged paper. He set down the pliers without a sound. The river muttered against the hull. Somewhere across the water a fisherman was hauling nets, singing tunelessly, and the smell of frying oil drifted off the lowmark streets where the town's lower lanes were already ankle-deep in the season's first floodwater.

Three raps again. Then a voice, pleasant, unhurried: "Mister Corben. I know you're aboard. Your lamp's lit."

*Mister Corben.* That was the name on his current papers—Josiah Corben, bridge inspector, out of a jurisdiction that no longer checked. The lamp was a mistake. He killed it out of habit, then cursed himself for the tell and relit it. If the man already knew he was aboard, dousing the light only proved he was the kind of man who doused lights.

He opened the hatch.

The stranger stood on the mud-slick planks of the mooring walk with the ease of someone who had never once worried about footing. He was perhaps fifty, broad through the shoulders in the way of men who had been soldiers and then something worse, dressed in a gray traveling suit that had been expensive two seasons ago and was now exactly right—good enough to signal money, worn enough to signal that the money didn't need to shout. Rain beaded on the brim of his hat. He held a bottle of wine in one hand like a peace offering and a ledger under his arm like a warrant.

"Mister Corben," the man said again, and smiled. It was a good smile. It had been practiced on people with less to lose. "Halloran. Northern Consolidated Rail & River. I'm told you've been inspecting our trestle."

"Your trestle." Vesper kept his voice flat, bored. "I was told it was the town's."

"The town's on paper. Everything on this river is ours on paper. That's rather the point of paper." Halloran glanced past him into the boat's dim cabin, and his eyes did the thing that Vesper had spent nine years dreading and nine years learning to read: they inventoried. Chart tube. Tool chest. The cot. The shelf of tinned food arranged by size, because a man who arranged his tins by size was a man who needed the world to have edges, and a man who needed edges was a man who had once lost something without edges to catch it. "May I come aboard? It's the rain. And I dislike conducting business through a hatch like a fishmonger."

Everything in Vesper said no. But *no*, said badly, was a confession, and so he stepped back and let the man down into his boat, into the small sealed world he had kept clean of strangers for three years on this stretch of river, and hated himself a little for the courtesy of it.

Halloran sat where he was pointed, set the wine on the chart table without being offered a glass, and looked around with the mild appetite of a man appraising a horse. "Comfortable. I've always admired the drifting inspectors. No wife, no lodge, no debts a man can trace. You people are the only honest ones left in the trade—no stake in anything, so no reason to lie." He smiled again. "Of course, that's also what makes you so very easy to buy."

"There's nothing I'm selling."

"No. Not yet. That's why I'm here first, before the others." Halloran opened the ledger across his knee, unhurried, and Vesper saw that it was not a ledger at all but a folder of thin, expensive paper, and that the top sheet had a name on it. His name. The real one. *V. Ravensworth, engineer of record, Vell Bridge, third survey, second signature.*

The river seemed to go quiet under the hull.

"You have the wrong man," Vesper said.

"I have the right man and the wrong name, which in my experience is the same man twice over." Halloran tapped the sheet once, gently, as if soothing it. "Vell Bridge. Eleven dead. The consortium's lawyers made you the signature while the directors stayed nameless—that's how it works, you know, it's not personal, it's architecture. Somebody has to be the man at the bottom of the building, and the building is always built downward." He looked up, and for a half a second the practiced warmth dropped away and something older and wearier looked out. "You were made to carry it. I know how that feels."

"You don't know anything about me."

"I know you've been moving for nine years. I know you've held eleven names that I've found and I'd wager three that I haven't. I know you take inspection work in dying river towns because rotting timber doesn't ask questions about your references." Halloran folded his hands. "And I know that three nights ago, after the storm, the river gave you a gift against your mooring pilings, and that you were seen at dawn dragging it aboard with a boat hook, alone, at an hour when honest men were still asleep."

The cabin walls, which had always felt like armor, suddenly felt like a box. Vesper made himself breathe. *He knows the chest exists. He doesn't know what's in it. He can't know what's in it—the seal was under river mud, the lock was rusted shut, I pried it open below decks with the lamp shaded—* but the arithmetic of it was already running in his head and it came out wrong. A bronze-bound chest, two days in the water, hauled out by the one man in Lowmark who turned out to be a fugitive engineer. If Halloran knew that much, then someone had watched the river all night, or someone had talked, or both.

"Salvage," Vesper said. "River salvage. A chest of drowned cargo. Half of Lowmark drags things out of that river every flood."

"Half of Lowmark drags out drowned pigs and broken furniture and once, memorably, a church bell." Halloran's voice stayed pleasant. "Half of Lowmark does not drag out a bronze-bound strongbox stamped with the two-headed hawk, and half of Lowmark, if it did, would be selling the bronze for scrap by now instead of keeping it aboard and lying about it with its face doing that."

"Doing what."

"That." Halloran almost laughed. "Mister Corben. Mister *Ravensworth*. Let's not spend the whole night pretending. I'm going to make you an offer, you're going to refuse it, and then we'll both have learned something about each other, and the evening will have been a great success. That's how these meetings always go. I've sat on the other side of enough of them."

He turned a page in the folder. Vesper saw figures. Real figures, written in a clerk's careful hand, with the consortium's watermark ghosting through the paper when the lamp caught it—that same watermark, that same ghost-hawk-in-a-shield, and his stomach turned over, because he had seen that mark once before, two nights ago, beneath the Kestrian seal, on the back of a survey map older than any line he had ever worked.

"Triple wages," Halloran said. "Your last recorded rate, which I have here, was four marks a day with a hardship allowance. Call it twelve. Paid quarterly, in advance, into any bank you care to name—or in cash, since I suspect banks and you have an understanding. Duration: as long as you care to keep drifting. In exchange: the chest. Contents unexamined by you, condition unimportant, and—" he raised a hand, "—before you say it, no, you don't need to know what's in it. That's the beauty of the arrangement. Ignorance is the one thing in this world that never depreciates."

"And if I don't know what's in it, why is it worth triple wages?"

"Because you're not being paid for the chest. You're being paid for the *absence* of the chest. There's a difference, and the difference is what I'm paid to manage." Halloran closed the folder. "The Northern Consolidated is a large organization, Mister Ravensworth, and large organizations have long memories and short tempers, and I am the part that stands between the two. I would much rather write you a quarterly draft than write you into a report. The report is a very dull document. It has your real name in it, and your real name is a thing you've spent nine years teaching the world to forget, and I promise you the world learns faster than you'd like."

Vesper looked at the wine bottle, at the folder, at the man's gray suit darkening at the shoulders where the rain had come through the hatch. He thought about the chest below his bunk, wrapped in oilcloth, its documents in their oiled sleeves, the sealed letter he had not opened, the survey with its strange notation system that had kept him up two nights running—not with dread, at first, but with the old electric joy of a route that *worked*, a line that climbed where no one had thought to climb, and then dread after all when he'd found the consortium's watermark beneath the hawk. The two marks together meant one thing and he had known it at once, the way you know a bone is broken: the consortium had mapped Kestria before Kestria was erased, and had sold the map, and the chest was proof, and proof was the one cargo that sank men.

"I don't have your chest," he said.

It came out wrong. He heard it come out wrong—half a beat too fast, an octave too high, the voice of a man who had rehearsed innocence and never performed it. Nine years of drifting and he had practiced every skill except the one that mattered tonight.

Halloran sighed, and it was almost kind. "There it is," he said softly. "You know, I'd have taken a good lie. I'd have taken a *mediocre* lie. I've been paid to accept worse. But you've given me the truth wearing a false mustache, and now I have to write that down." He rose, collected the folder, and left the wine. "Keep it. Drink it or don't. I'll call again in three days, and the offer will be the same, and the price of the report will not."

He paused at the hatch, rain streaming past him, and looked back with that dropped-mask expression for just a moment.

"For what it's worth—and it isn't worth anything, I know—I read the Vell Bridge findings. All of them. The third survey was altered before it ever reached your desk. You signed a drawing you were never allowed to see whole." He said it flatly, without sympathy, the way a man reports the weather. "I mention it only so you understand that I do my reading. Good evening, Mister Corben. Or the other one."

Then he was gone, up the walk, his footsteps unhurried on the planks, and the rain closed behind him like water closing over a stone.

* * *

Vesper did not touch the wine. He sat on his bunk with the lamp turned low and listened to the river, and did the arithmetic he had been avoiding since dawn two days ago.

The chest sat beneath him, wrapped in oilcloth, and he could feel its weight through the bunk boards like a passenger. He had told himself, when he pried it open alone in the dark, that he was only curious. That was a lie he had told himself in the same voice he'd used on Halloran, and it had come out just as badly. He had opened it because the seal had hooked something in him—the two-headed hawk, a country he'd only ever seen in the margins of other maps, *here be a nation, lately removed*—and because a rail survey older than any line he knew was, to a man like him, the closest thing to scripture the world still printed. He had wanted it. That was the whole truth. He had wanted it the way drowning men want river water.

And now the consortium knew it existed, and knew it had washed up in Lowmark, and knew—because he had just confirmed it with his face and his voice—that the drifting inspector Corben had it.

He thought about the options the way he thought about a failing span. Sell it: triple wages, quarterly, forever, and the consortium's goodwill, and the knowledge that somewhere in a locked drawer the last proof of Kestria would be filed under *destroyed*, and that he would have been the hand that filed it. He had already been the signature on one erasure. Eleven names. He could not carry a twelfth country. Run: load the chest tonight, cut the mooring, take the flooded river down—no. Down was the consortium's country, the locks and the tolls and the company towns; down was where Halloran's report would be waiting. Up, then—up the river narrowed into the gorge, and the gorge in monsoon was a meat grinder, and the survey maps said the upper channels were impassable at flood, and he had spent nine years believing what survey maps said because believing them was what had kept him alive.

Or stay, and lie better, and wait for three days, and discover that three days was exactly as long as Halloran needed to confirm what his face had already confessed.

He got up, took the lantern, and went down into the bilge where the chest lay, and put his hand flat on the oilcloth, and felt the bronze binding cold through it. The sealed letter was still unopened. He had told himself that was discipline. Tonight it looked like cowardice with better posture.

*You were seen at dawn.* Who watched a mooring at dawn on a flooded river? Not consortium men—the consortium bought things, it didn't crouch in reeds. Someone else had been counting the river's channels. Someone with a cargo broker's papers and a boatman's eyes, who had asked him twice about salvage rights with questions shaped exactly like nets, and he had lied to her twice, and both times she had smiled the way Halloran smiled, and he had told himself she was nothing.

The chest had been seen at dawn by more than one pair of eyes, then. The consortium had its watchers and the river had its own, and he was moored in the middle of a channel that everyone was reading and he had never learned to read back.

He blew out the lantern and sat in the dark and did the last piece of arithmetic, the one he'd been circling all evening. Halloran had said *the price of the report will not* be the same in three days. But reports took time to write and longer to act on, and men like Halloran preferred purchase to force, which meant the quiet was not quiet—it was a countdown with the numbers painted over. And there were other men, cheaper men, the kind the consortium kept for the dull documents Halloran had mentioned, and those men did not knock three times and bring wine.

They came at night, and they searched first and asked questions after, if they asked at all.

Vesper looked at the black square of the hatch, at the rain coming down past it in gray ropes, at the lights of Lowmark shivering across the floodwater. Somewhere out there, a woman who counted rivers was counting his boat too. He had lied to her twice. He was going to need a third lie, a better one, or—his hand tightened on the oilcloth, on the cold bronze beneath—or something that wasn't a lie at all, and he didn't yet know what that would cost.

The knock, when it came, was not three measured raps.

It was a pebble against the hull, twice, from the water side, in the dark.
