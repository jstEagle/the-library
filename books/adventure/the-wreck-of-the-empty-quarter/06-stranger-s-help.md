## 6. Stranger's Help

Sable Idris had learned to count footsteps the way other people counted money, and by the third night in Lowmark she could walk the riverfront with her eyes shut and know exactly who was awake behind which shutter. The town had a rhythm like a slow engine: the bakery at four, the fish carts at five, the consortium warehouse clerks stumbling out of the Salt House at eleven smelling of gin and self-importance. She had catalogued all of it. What she hadn't catalogued—what she was still working on, the way a surveyor works on a stubborn ridge line—was Vesper Ravensworth.

He wasn't sleeping either. She could tell because his boat's lantern burned past midnight again, a low amber smudge through the fog that came off the river after dark, and because she'd watched him pace the deck twice already, hands jammed in his coat pockets, head down like a man arguing with someone only he could see.

She sat on the pilings of the old ferry slip across the channel, forty yards of black water between them, and did what she'd been doing for six days now: she watched him back.

* * *

The trouble with watching a man for six days was that you started to learn things you didn't want to know. Sable had come to Lowmark on the strength of a rumor—a bronze-bound chest, hawk-stamped, pulled from the flood—and she had expected to find a salvager, some river rat who'd crack it open, sell the paper by the pound, and never understand what he held. Instead she'd found an engineer.

She knew engineers. Her grandfather had been one, before there was no country left to engineer for. Engineers had a particular way of moving through the world, hands always half-curled as if gripping something invisible, eyes that measured everything—doorways, loads, the sag of a rope, the angle of a stranger's shoulders. Vesper Ravensworth measured everything. And he lied beautifully, which was its own kind of tell, because honest men lie badly.

He'd told her he was a trestle inspector passing through. He'd told her the map sewn into his coat lining—which she'd seen the outline of when he'd stripped to his shirtsleeves patching his hull—was "old work papers." Two lies, both delivered with the calm of a man reading a freight manifest. She'd let them stand. You didn't correct a man's lies until you understood why he needed them.

What she couldn't yet work out was whether he was a danger or a door.

The chest mattered. The chest was the first physical proof in twenty years that Kestria had existed outside the heads of exiles and the margins of forbidden documents. Sable had been raised on a country she'd never seen—raised on her grandfather's voice drawing borders in the air over their kitchen table in a port city three thousand miles away, on children's songs about towns whose names weren't printed anywhere anymore, on the careful, exhausting discipline of remembering. The chest bore the two-headed hawk. The chest had been carried by someone who died getting it downstream, if the water damage meant anything, and it had washed up against the mooring lines of a man with forged papers and a liar's steady voice.

So she watched. And on the seventh night, watching saved him—or so she would later tell herself, though the truth was probably that it saved her too.

* * *

It was the dog that tipped her first. There was a yellow dog that slept under the Salt House steps, and it never barked at clerks, but at half past eleven it stood up and pointed itself downriver like a compass needle finding north, and made a sound low in its chest that Sable had heard before, in another town, on another night, the night the gendarmes came for the exile quarter in Marenholt. Men who moved in groups of four and walked like they were owed something.

She slipped off the pilings and crossed the channel in a skiff she'd rented under a name that wasn't hers, oars muffled with rags, and she was alongside Vesper's boat before his lantern even flickered in surprise.

"Company," she said, keeping her voice below the river's murmur. "Four men, downriver walk, moving like they're being paid by the hour. They'll be here inside ten minutes."

Vesper came to the rail fast—but not panicked, she noted. He looked downriver first, correctly, then at her, and his face did something complicated. "You've been following me."

"You've been lying to me. We're even." She tossed him the bowline. "Argue later. Do you have a second way off this boat?"

"There's the bilge hatch—"

"They'll know that. Where do you go when you don't want to be found?"

A pause. She watched him decide something, watched the decision move through him like a load shifting on a crane. "Under the trestle," he said finally. "The inspection staging platform. Nobody goes there but me."

"The rotting one you're being paid to condemn?"

"It's rotten. It isn't going anywhere." A ghost of something that wasn't quite a smile. "That's the whole point of good engineering. Knowing exactly how much failure you can stand on."

There was no time to decide whether she liked him. The lantern light was coming around the bend upstream—no, downriver, four hand-lamps hooded to throw their beams low, which meant professionals, which meant Halloran's people, which meant the consortium had stopped sending letters and started sending men. Sable killed her own lamp and dropped into the shadow of the pilings while Vesper went over the side with a canvas bag and moved along the bank like a man who had done this before, unhurried, economical, gone.

* * *

She stayed. Later she would not be able to explain to herself why she stayed—prudence said follow him, prudence said be elsewhere—but she wanted to see what they took, and knowing what a man loses tells you what he is.

They boarded his boat without knocking, because boats can't refuse. Four of them, and a fifth on the bank holding the lamps, and she lay flat along the slip planks above them with rain beginning to spot the wood around her face, close enough to hear the search. It was thorough and it was wrong. They went through the berth and the chart locker and the bilge and the false bottom under the galley floor that Vesper apparently thought she didn't know about—they found nothing in any of it, because whatever he kept, he kept on his body—and then the tall one, the one doing the talking, put his boot on the table and said, quietly, in the tone of a man reading charges:

"He opened it. Has to have. Salvage law says report within three days, and he hasn't reported, which means he knows what it is, which means he's worth more talked than beaten. But check anyway. Paper burns, paper floats, paper gets buried. Look for anything with a hawk on it."

Hawk. Her heart went hard and small in her chest. They knew. Not guessed—knew. Which meant the consortium had been waiting for that chest longer than the flood had been rising, which meant the chest hadn't been lost at all. It had been running.

They left the boat turned inside out and a chalk mark on the mooring post—a circle with a slash, the consortium's way of saying *we were here, we'll be back*. Sable waited until their lamps had folded away into the streets, until the yellow dog settled back onto the Salt House steps, until the rain had soaked her through twice over. Then she crossed the channel again, alone, and stood at the foot of the rotten trestle stairs in the dark and called up softly:

"Inspector. Your boat's still floating. That's the good news."

* * *

He came down the stairs with the lamp unhooded, which was either trust or a dare, and up close in the bad light she saw what six days of distance hadn't shown her: a tired man. Not soft-tired. Worn-down-to-structure tired, the way a bridge is tired, still standing, every rivet accounted for, nothing left over.

"They took nothing," he said.

"I know. I watched."

"You watched." He set the lamp on a crossbeam and looked at her, and she braced for the question—*why, who are you, what do you want*—the questions she had answers prepared for, three layers deep. Instead he said: "You warned me. Before they arrived. Ten minutes, you said, and it was nine."

"Counting's a habit."

"What else is a habit?"

And there it was—the real question, dressed in work clothes. Sable leaned against the stair rail and considered him, and considered the rain, and considered how many years she had spent being exactly nobody from exactly nowhere, fluent in four accents, owner of no objects that told stories. He was asking her to trade. He'd just lost his invisibility; he was offering to acknowledge hers was gone too.

"My grandfather," she said, "was a surveyor. On the northern lines. Before."

Before. The word did something to his face—she watched it land, watched him understand it the way you understand a load rating stamped on a beam. *Before.* Everyone from the old country spoke in before and after; it was the grammar of erased places.

"He taught me to count rivers," she went on, because she had decided, somewhere in the last hour on the wet planks, that if she was going to burn herself she'd burn clean. "All eleven channels of the delta, and which ones silted in drought and which ones drowned villages in flood. He had maps. Real ones, ink ones, with a country's name on them. I grew up thinking every family had those. Then I grew up enough to learn that ours was the last one that did, and that men had died carrying them, and that my father changed our name twice before I was ten." She looked at the lamp flame. "I recognized the seal on your salvage the day you pried it open. Two-headed hawk, wings up, crown between. I've drawn it in charcoal on fences since I was six years old. So no, Inspector—I'm not a cargo broker. I'm the granddaughter of a country that doesn't exist, and that chest is the first piece of it I've ever touched."

The rain filled the silence. Vesper didn't offer sympathy—she noticed that, and was grateful; sympathy would have been unbearable. What he did was worse and better. He nodded slowly, like a man confirming a measurement.

"The watermark," he said. "Inside the seal. You know about the watermark."

Her breath stopped. "Show me."

"Not here." He glanced upriver, toward the town, toward the chalk mark on his mooring post. "They'll be back, and next time not to search. And—" he hesitated, and she saw the cost of the next words before he spent them, saw him pay it anyway, "—there are things in that chest I need to show someone who'll understand what they're looking at. I've been telling myself I was going to sell it. To them, or anyone. Buy my way clear."

"And now?"

"Now I've met someone who draws hawks on fences." He picked up the lamp. "Come at dawn. The east landing, where the willows are. Bring nothing that has your real name on it."

"I haven't owned my real name in years," Sable said. "But I remember it. Does that count?"

Something moved across his face—not quite a smile, closer to relief wearing a smile's coat. "That counts," he said. "That's the only thing that counts."

* * *

She rowed back across the channel with the rain easing and the town dark behind her, and she made herself run the arithmetic coldly, the way her grandfather had taught her—trust nothing, verify everything, sentiment sinks cargo. A man with forged papers. A man the consortium had marked. A man who admitted he'd considered selling the last proof of Kestria to the people who helped erase it.

And a man who, offered a clean chance to lie to her one more time, had instead said *come at dawn*.

Sable shipped the oars in the lee of the ferry slip and sat for a moment looking at the black water, and let herself feel the thing she'd been refusing to feel for six days: hope, sharp and dangerous as a chisel. Tomorrow she would see the watermark. Tomorrow she would know whether the consortium's fingerprints were on the death of her grandfather's country in ink she could hold.

Tomorrow, for the first time in her life, she might not be the only one who remembered.

She took up the oars. Dawn was five hours off, and she intended to be early.
