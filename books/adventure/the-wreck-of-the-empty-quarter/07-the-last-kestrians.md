## 7. The Last Kestrians

I had brought strangers to the quarter before—never on purpose.

There was a fishmonger once, during the second year of the flood taxes, who had seen old Tam Vey writing in the water-script and asked him, kindly enough, what language it was. Tam said it was nothing, a family cipher, and the fishmonger believed him because people believe what costs them nothing to believe. But I had walked that man's route home afterward for three nights to be sure he told no one, and what I learned was this: you do not bring outsiders to the quarter. You bring them only as far as the tannery wall, and then you decide.

Vesper Ravensworth—or whatever his name was this month—walked beside me through the tannery district with his collar up and his eyes doing the thing they always did: reading everything, claiming nothing. He counted rooflines. He noted the drainage channels cut into the cobbles, the ones that ran wrong for rain but right for something carried. He was an engineer down to his walk; he could not cross a bridge without judging it, and I suspected he could not enter a room without measuring its exits.

"You're taking me somewhere," he said. Not a question.

"I'm taking you to see photographs," I said. "You'll want to see them before you decide what the chest is worth."

"And if I'd rather not know where we're going?"

"Then you should have lied better when I asked about salvage rights." I turned down the alley behind the glue-works, where the smell of rendered hide covered every other smell in Lowmark, which was precisely why the quarter had chosen it. "You didn't. That tells me you wanted to be found."

He said nothing to that, but his jaw tightened in the way it did when I was right and he resented the evidence.

* * *

The door was a door in a wall that had no business having one—a plank door painted the same gray as the tannery brick, with a handle worn to brightness by forty years of the same hands. I knocked twice, paused, knocked three times. Somewhere inside, a bolt drew back, and a voice said a word in Kestrian that meant, literally, *the river brings*, though it had come to mean simply *who is it*.

"It's me," I said in the same tongue. "And I've brought the engineer."

The word *engineer* did more work than any password could have. The door opened wide.

Inside, the gray brick gave way to a courtyard I had known since before I could speak: a square of packed earth no larger than a warehouse floor, ringed by connected rooms, strung with lines of drying laundry that were not all laundry. Some of those hanging cloths were maps—Kestrian survey silks, printed on fabric so they could be burned in seconds or worn as shawls in an emergency. A child of perhaps six ran past us underfoot, chasing a hoop, shouting numbers as she went. Not counting her laps. Reciting elevations.

"Six hundred and forty at the col, six hundred and ten at the tarn—" she chanted, and vanished through a doorway.

Vesper stopped walking. I felt him stop, the way you feel a train brake—the whole air of him changing pressure.

"What is she—" he began.

"Elevations," I said. "Of the Serel Pass. She doesn't know yet that there's anywhere else to memorize. We start them early."

An old man came out of the eastern room wiping his hands on an apron, and behind him two more, and behind them the courtyard filled the way courtyards do when a stranger arrives—not quickly, but completely. Old Tam Vey first, of course, because Tam appointed himself gatekeeper decades ago and no one had ever bothered to relieve him of the post. Then the Osten brothers, identical in their grayness except that one had lost an eye to a quarry blast and the other had never forgiven the quarry. Then a dozen faces I could have named in my sleep, because I had named them in my sleep, in the dead language, during the worst year of the monsoon before last.

They looked at Vesper the way you look at weather coming over a ridge. Not hostile. Assessing.

"This is the man who has the chest," I said, in Kestrian, so that he would understand none of it and understand everything from our faces.

"The man who *had* the chest," Tam corrected mildly. "Halloran has it now. What this man has is worse." His good eye—it was his left; the right had gone white as river fog years ago—fixed on Vesper. "What does he have, Sable?"

"He has the survey in his head," I said. "Or most of it. He read it for four nights."

A murmur went around the courtyard like wind through the laundry lines. One of the silk maps stirred on its line.

"That's not possible," said the younger Osten brother. "Nobody reads a Kestrian field survey in four nights. The notation alone takes—"

"He's a railway engineer," I said. "A real one. Whatever else he is, whatever name he's wearing this season—he reads lines for a living. Lines are lines." I looked at Vesper, standing very straight among us with his hands in his coat pockets like a man holding onto something. "Tell them what you told me. About the notation."

He hesitated. I watched him weigh it—I had watched him weigh everything since the night he pried open that chest alone on his rotting boat, and I was beginning to learn the geography of his hesitations. This one was not fear. It was the flinch of a man being handed something he did not believe he could carry without dropping.

"The notation system," he said finally, in careful trade-accented Kestrian that made several of the old men blink, "isn't a cipher. Or it is, but it's a working cipher—the kind survey crews invent when they're moving fast and can't risk the drawings falling into the wrong hands. Your grandfather—" he nodded toward me, and I realized with a small cold shock that he had already understood whose chest it was, had probably understood since the seal, "—built it out of railway conventions. Bench marks, chainages, curve radii. Anyone trained on rails could crack it given time. Given four nights and no sleep—" he shrugged, a small bitter motion, "—given no sleep, it's just arithmetic."

"You read it," Tam said. It was not a question either. Old men in hiding get to skip questions.

"I read it," Vesper said. "I can't promise I kept all of it. But the route through the pass—the glacier route—" He stopped. His eyes went, involuntarily, upward, as if the pass itself hung over the tannery courtyard, beyond the smoke and the laundry and the low gray sky. "It's buildable. That's what I keep coming back to. Whoever surveyed it wasn't dreaming. The grades work. There are three places you'd need snowsheds and one place you'd need a gallery tunnel, and the rock there is bad granite, I'd want cores before I swore to it—but it's buildable. Someone measured it like it mattered."

"Someone measured it," repeated the elder Osten brother, softly, "because it mattered."

* * *

They fed him first. This was policy older than me: no decision in the quarter was ever made with a hungry guest at the table, because hunger made men agree to things and the quarter had survived ninety years by agreeing to almost nothing. So we sat on benches under the eaves while women I had called aunt my whole life brought out river fish cooked with a spice blend that existed nowhere on any customs manifest, ground fresh from seeds smuggled in sewing kits across three borders, and flatbread on a griddle older than the annexation.

Vesper ate carefully, the way he did everything, and I watched the old men watch him eat, and I understood that I was watching a negotiation conducted entirely in bread.

It was Tam who broke it, as Tam broke everything, gently, with a hammer wrapped in wool.

"You knew the seal," he said to Vesper. "When Sable described your face when you opened the chest. Tell me what you saw."

"A two-headed hawk," Vesper said. "Facing east and west. Stamped into bronze, and struck again in wax on the letter inside." He set down his cup. "I didn't know it. That's the truth. I've seen a thousand seals and I couldn't have told you Kestria's from a wine importer's. But the watermark underneath it—" and here his voice changed, dropped into the register I had learned to dread, the one he used for the bridge, for the consortium, for the parts of himself he kept locked like signal boxes, "—the paper under the wax was Hollis & Grange stock. Consortium mill. Same stock they use for their internal surveys. I've handled enough of it to know it blind."

The courtyard went quiet. Even the child with the hoop had gone still somewhere out of sight, listening the way children in hiding learn to listen.

"So you understand," Tam said, "what the chest is."

"It's proof," Vesper said. "That the consortium mapped Kestrian territory before the annexation and sold the maps to the power that annexed it. The survey is dated eleven years before the border closed. It's signed by a consortium chief engineer. And it's countersigned—" he paused, and I saw him choose whether to say the next part, and say it, "—by the same directors whose names were on the papers after Vell Bridge. The same signatures. They made me take the blame for a span I warned them about, and twenty years earlier they sold a country off the face of the map with the same pens."

No one moved. The laundry lines creaked.

Then the elder Osten brother began, very quietly, to laugh—not cruel laughter, but the laughter of a man who has waited a long time at a station and finally hears a whistle. "The same pens," he said. "You hear that, Tam? The same pens. Ninety years we've been telling our grandchildren the mapmakers came first and the soldiers came second, and here sits a foreigner who can prove it from the paper stock."

"We don't need proof for ourselves," said one of the aunts, sharply. "We know what happened. Proof is for the world that erased us."

"Proof is for the pass," Tam said, and the sharpness went out of the courtyard at once, because everyone knew what he meant, and now—looking at Vesper's face—I saw that Vesper was beginning to know too.

* * *

Afterward, in the eastern room, they showed him the photographs.

They kept them in a tin tea box lined with oilcloth, and there were eleven of them, and I had seen them so many times that I could have recited them like the child recited elevations. My grandfather at thirty, standing beside a theodolite on a ridge with cloud pouring over it like milk. My grandfather at fifty, grayer, squinting into sun, a chain crew fanned out behind him down a slope of scree. My grandfather's crew at the mouth of the glacier valley, all of them hatless in defiance of protocol, grinning like men who had found the end of a treasure map rather than the beginning of one. And the last photograph, taken the winter before the annexation: my grandfather alone at a table, the finished survey rolled and sealed beside him, looking directly into the camera with an expression I had spent my whole life trying to translate.

Vesper held that last one for a long time.

"He knew," he said at last. "In this picture. He knew what the survey would be used for—or that it might be—and he sent it out anyway."

"He hid it," I said. "The copy in the chest was his master field copy. The clean drawings went to the consortium, because he was their employee and refusing would have cost him his family. But he kept the field copy, and he wrote the notation system himself, and he taught it to my mother, who taught it to me, badly, the way you teach a language you're forbidden to speak." I took the photograph from him gently, because his hands had gone very still. "He died the year after the border closed. The chest went into the river with the last courier, sixty-one years ago. We thought it was at the bottom of history."

"It nearly was at the bottom of the river," Vesper said.

"Yes. Rivers are patient. So are we."

He looked around the room then—at the oilcloth-lined tin, at the silk maps drying on their lines, at the doorway where the elevation-child stood half-hidden, watching the stranger with the frank appraisal of the young—and I saw the flinch come over him. I had been waiting for it. I had seen it the night before, when I told him the old men would want to meet him, and I had seen its smaller cousins a dozen times: whenever anyone thanked him, whenever anyone assumed he would still be in Lowmark next week, whenever kindness approached him from an angle he hadn't braced against.

"You should understand what we're asking," Tam said, following my gaze, missing nothing. "Not tonight. Tonight you eat and sleep and in the morning you tell us honestly how much of the survey you kept. But you should understand it now, so you can begin deciding."

"Deciding what?"

"That we mean to send someone up the pass at calving season," Tam said. "With the route in whatever form survives. To the valley where the archives went—every census roll, every treaty, every birth record of a country the world agreed to forget. My grandfather helped carry them up there. Sable's grandfather surveyed the rail line that was meant to bring them home." He spread his ruined hands, palms up, an old man's gesture of offering everything and holding nothing. "The chest is gone. Fine. Chests are gone; rivers take them, empires burn them. But a route lives in a head, and yours is the head it lives in now. We're asking whether you'll draw it for us. Whether you'll give Kestria back its road."

The silence stretched. Rain started on the courtyard flags, soft as static.

And Vesper Ravensworth—whoever he truly was, under the forged papers and the sewn-in map and the nine years of small notes and borrowed names—looked at the photograph of my grandfather, and at the child in the doorway, and at the space between them where a family ought to stand, and I watched him do the thing I had already learned to recognize and already, against all my training, begun to grieve.

He flinched.

"I'm not staying in Lowmark," he said quietly. "I never stay. When the river drops, I go. You should find someone whose name isn't going to be different in the spring."

"No one's name is different here," I said. "That's rather the point of the quarter."

But he shook his head, slow, like a man refusing food he needed, and set the photograph down on the table between us with exaggerated care, as if it might go off.

Before anyone could answer him, the bolt rattled in the outer door—twice, fast, the knock we used for warning instead of welcome—and one of the Osten brothers slipped in from the courtyard, rain-wet to the shoulders, and said the words that ended the evening and began everything else:

"Halloran's men are at the tannery wall. They're asking after the engineer."
