## 9. Halloran's Ledger

The ledger was bound in green goatskin, and it smelled of the sea, though it had never been nearer the coast than a director's private library in Corvane. Halloran had noticed that about the consortium's records: everything smelled faintly of salt, as if the money itself had come off ships. He turned the pages with cotton gloves and a lamp turned low, because some habits outlive their reasons, and this room had once belonged to a man who read other men's secrets by lamplight and died badly for it.

*Consolidated Mountain & River Trust, Annexation-Era Correspondence, Restricted Series K.*

Series K. He had wondered for years what the K stood for. Now he knew, and he wished he didn't.

The entry was dated forty-one years back, in a clerk's hand so neat it looked printed:

> *Sold to the Office of the Governor-General, Territory Survey of the Kestrian Highlands, comprising rail alignments, river fords, glacier passes, and seasonal routes, 214 sheets. Payment received in full. Note: sellers advised that the native survey is superior to anything our own engineers could produce at ten times the cost. The pass at Serac Col is marked but not named, per instruction.*

Per instruction. Meaning: don't write down what we both know this is for.

Halloran sat back in the creaking chair and let the arithmetic assemble itself, because that was his gift and his curse — he could not look at a thing without pricing it. The consortium had sold a country's bones to the power that meant to strip its flesh. Every ford, every pass, every place a people could hide or flee or stand: itemized, priced, delivered. And when Kestria was annexed — when its name was sanded off treaties and its towns rechristened — the consortium had done what consortia do. It had kept the receipts.

Not out of sentiment. Out of leverage. A receipt is only paper until someone comes asking questions; then it becomes a weapon you point at yourself or someone else. For four decades the chest's contents had been a liability sleeping in some Kestrian archive vault, and the consortium had paid, quietly, year after year, for men like Halloran to make sure it stayed asleep.

Then the river had woken it up.

He closed the ledger and pressed his palm flat on the goatskin, feeling the grain under the glove. Somewhere downstream, in a mud-stained river town whose name changed with every flood season, a bronze-bound chest sat against a dead man's mooring pilings, stamped with a two-headed hawk, and a washed-up engineer who thought himself invisible was learning that no one is ever invisible to a company that owns the rivers he floats on.

Halloran took out his notebook — black oilcloth, half ledger, half diary, entirely his — and wrote the day's figures the way he wrote them every night. *Assets. Liabilities. Exposure.* Under *Exposure* he wrote: *One chest. One engineer. One town full of ghosts.*

Under a column he did not label, because labeling it would be the first step toward insubordination, he wrote: *I knew a language once too.*

* * *

His employers did not think of themselves as villains. That was the first thing a man learned working for them, and the hardest thing to unlearn afterward. They thought of themselves as stewards. In the boardroom above the freight yards in Corvane, behind glass that kept out the smell of coal and cattle, they used words like *continuity* and *stakeholder confidence*, and if you listened long enough you could almost believe that erasing a country was a form of housekeeping.

Director Vane had said it to him personally, three days ago, in a voice like a man discussing drainage. "The documents are an anachronism, Halloran. The annexation was ratified by every signatory that mattered. What we require is not concealment — concealment implies guilt — but tidiness. History is a filing problem."

"A filing problem," Halloran had repeated.

"Eleven sheets of correspondence and a survey. Burn the correspondence, melt the seal off the chest, sell the bronze. The survey has commercial value; our cartography division will want it. Nothing else survives." Vane had smiled then, the small dry smile of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice. "You've handled messier."

And Halloran had nodded, because nodding was the job, and because the alternative to nodding was unemployment, and unemployment, for a man with his history, was not a neutral condition. A man without papers and without wages does not drift. He sinks. He had watched men sink. He had been close enough to the waterline himself, once, to know the sound it makes.

But walking out of that office, past the framed photographs of bridges and viaducts the consortium had built across three nations — handsome iron things, honest-looking, each one signed by some engineer whose name the company later made sure to forget when the lawsuits came — Halloran had caught himself doing something new. He had caught himself counting.

It was Sable Idris who had taught him the habit, though she'd never know it. Years ago, in another life, in another port, before either of their names meant anything to anyone, he had watched a girl no older than twelve stand on a breakwater counting the channels of a shifting delta with her lips moving, patient as prayer, while grown men argued around her. He'd asked a dockhand who she was. *Idris girl,* the man had said. *They count rivers where she's from. Country got ate up, but the counting stuck.*

Country got ate up. Four words, tossed off like gull-cry, and Halloran had carried them for twenty years without knowing why, the way a man carries a splinter too deep to dig out.

Now he stood in the archive with the salt-smelling ledger and understood: the splinter had finally found the nerve.

* * *

He worked from a hotel room in Lowmark because the consortium preferred its fixers visible in respectable rooms and invisible everywhere else. From the window he could see the river, high and brown with early monsoon, carrying whole trees past the town like a parade of drowned giants. The lower streets were already sandbagged. The townspeople moved through the flooding with the bored efficiency of people performing an annual ritual, which told him something the briefing files hadn't: these were people who had made peace with impermanence. He recognized the type. He was one.

On the desk lay the file on Vesper Ravensworth. Halloran had read it twice, which was once more than necessary. The consortium's version was thin: engineer, disgraced, the Vell Bridge collapse, eleven dead, scapegoated with the cooperation of his own signature. *Subject is compliant under financial pressure. Subject avoids fixed residence. Subject's professional competence rated exceptional; judgment rated poor.*

Judgment rated poor. Halloran snorted. Any man who survived nine years of drifting with his skills intact had judgment of a very particular and excellent kind — the judgment of prey animals, honed nightly. No, the file was wrong about Ravensworth, and Halloran distrusted files that were wrong, because a wrong file meant the man inside it was more complicated than the payroll required, and complicated men were the ones who burned offices down.

There was a second folder, thinner, unmarked. Sable Idris. Cargo broker, various registrations, most of them plausible, none of them older than six years. Photograph attached: a woman with river-gray eyes looking slightly past the camera as if it owed her money. The consortium had flagged her only after the chest surfaced — *possible Kestrian interest, monitor* — which meant somewhere in Corvane there was a list of possible Kestrian interests, maintained for decades, updated dutifully by clerks who presumably never asked what they were monitoring for.

Halloran poured himself two fingers of the hotel's bad brandy and did not drink it. Another habit from the old country, wherever the old country had been; he had left at seven, and memory had worn the name of the place smooth as a river stone. There had been soldiers, and a night train, and his mother pressing a folded document into his hand — not money, never money, they had none — a document, in a language the soldiers were burning books to forget. He had kept it through three borders and two false names and one year of genuine hunger. Then, at seventeen, needing bribe money, he had sold it.

He had spent thirty years telling himself it was just paper. That what it proved didn't need proving; that he remembered; that memory was enough.

He had believed it right up until the moment a flooded river delivered a bronze-bound chest to a stranger's pilings, and the consortium's entire apparatus had shuddered into motion over *paper*. If paper were nothing, why had Director Vane used the word *tidiness* in that soft voice? Why was Halloran being paid triple rates to burn letters written before he was born?

Because paper was testimony. Because paper could be read aloud. Because a nation lived in its documents the way a man lived in his signature, and the consortium had spent forty years holding a pillow over the proof, and now the proof had floated free, and men like Halloran were the pillow.

He drank the brandy after all. It tasted like the hotels he'd lived in: adequate, anonymous, faintly of other people's regrets.

* * *

The instructions from Corvane arrived by courier that evening, sealed with the trust's interlocking-rails device. Halloran broke the seal standing at the window, watching the floodwater take a chicken coop whole and carry it off like a toy.

The instructions were brief, as Vane's always were.

> *1. Recover the chest intact. Cartography division values the survey.*
> *2. The correspondence is to be destroyed, witnessed.*
> *3. The engineer Ravensworth is to be compensated and released. If compensation fails, he is to be discredited — a man of his record requires little effort.*
> *4. The woman Idris is to be detained and questioned regarding exile networks. Detention methods at your discretion.*
> *5. Upon completion, submit final expenses. This engagement will not be renewed.*

Item five was the knife between the ribs, delivered with the courtesy that was the consortium's signature move. *This engagement will not be renewed.* Thirty years of service, and they were retiring him the way one retired a bridge: quietly, before it became a liability, with no ceremony and less gratitude. He understood the logic. He had seen too much. Men who saw too much became line items under *Exposure*.

He read item four again. *Detention methods at your discretion.*

A woman who counted rivers. Exile-born. A girl on a breakwater with her lips moving, patient as prayer, while her country's name was being sanded off the maps.

Halloran set the telegram down on the desk, weighted it with the brandy glass, and stood looking at it for a long time while the rain came on outside, first in gusts, then in earnest, hammering the tin awnings of Lowmark like applause for something terrible.

Here was the truth he had been circling for three days, the figure at the bottom of the ledger he had refused to add up: he did not want the job anymore. Not because his conscience had suddenly bloomed — conscience was a luxury he'd pawned decades ago — but because the arithmetic had changed. The consortium was retiring him regardless. A man with no future owes his employer nothing, and a man with no past owes himself everything. For the first time since a night train carried him out of a burning province, those two conditions overlapped in Halloran's person, and the overlap felt less like freedom than like standing on ice and hearing it speak.

But wanting was not doing. He was still, tonight, the consortium's man. Tomorrow he would go down to the river town, put on his reasonable face, find the engineer Ravensworth, and offer him triple wages for river salvage. He would watch the man refuse badly, because frightened men always refused badly, and the refusal would confirm everything. And then he would do his job, because that was what he knew how to be, and because the alternative — the alternative was a seven-year-old's language he could no longer speak, a mother's folded document sold for passage, a country-shaped hole he had papered over with ledgers for thirty years.

He opened his black oilcloth notebook and made the night's entries. *Assets:* one chest, location known. *Liabilities:* one engineer, one broker, one town. *Exposure:* considerable.

Then, in the unlabeled column, in a hand smaller than his usual, he wrote: *Grandfather's orchard terraces, north slope. Census roll would have listed them. Chest may contain census rolls.*

He stared at what he'd written. Then he struck it through, once, firmly — and found that striking it through did not strike it out. The words remained legible under the line, the way a buried country remains legible under its conquerors' maps, the way a man's first name remains legible under thirty years of aliases.

Rain hammered the window. Downriver, against rotting pilings, a two-headed hawk waited on bronze for whatever Halloran decided to be.

He packed the notebook away, checked his revolver out of habit, and slept badly, dreaming of channels — a whole delta of them, shifting, and a girl's voice counting, and somewhere in the count, a number that was his own name, spoken correctly, for the first time in thirty years.

In the morning he would begin the hunt. But tonight, alone, with the flood rising and the ledger open in his mind, Emory Halloran — a name no living soul in Lowmark or Corvane would recognize — admitted the thing he had struck through: he was not hunting the chest for the consortium.

He was hunting it for the census rolls. And God help whichever side reached it second.
