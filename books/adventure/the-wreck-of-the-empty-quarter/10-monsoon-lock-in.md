## 10. Monsoon Lock-In

The first sign was not the rain. It was the birds.

I noticed it on the morning the passes closed—how the swifts that had been stitching the air above Lowmark all summer suddenly went quiet, gone inland in a single night, as if they'd read a schedule no one had shown me. The old men in the exile quarter said the same thing happened every year. *The birds know before the river does,* one of them told me, in that language of theirs that sounded like water going over stones, and then he said it again in trade-tongue so I'd be sure to understand.

Three days later the river knew too.

* * *

I had been in Lowmark eleven weeks by then. Long enough that the ferryman's boy had stopped watching my boat for strangers and started watching it for me, out of something like ownership. Long enough that the woman who sold me coffee at the dockside stall had begun setting aside the unburnt ends of the roast without being asked. Long enough, I told myself, for a man to leave whenever he wanted.

That was the lie I kept in the same pocket where I kept the survey map sewn into my coat lining: folded flat, always ready, never quite reached for.

The rain came up the valley on a Tuesday, if the day still meant anything in a town that measured time by river stages instead of calendars. It did not arrive so much as descend—a gray ceiling lowering over the whole basin at once, and then the sound, which I will hear for the rest of my life: not the patter of weather but the roar of a thing with weight and intention, hammering the tin roofs of the lower streets until speech became impossible and everyone simply stopped talking and let the sky do it.

I stood under the awning of the chandlery and watched the first hour of it come down and understood, with the particular clarity of a man watching a door close, that I was not leaving this valley until spring.

The mountain passes north of Lowmark closed with the first snowline drop. The southern road drowned under the same water that fed the river. Every year, for four months, the Empty Quarter's river towns became islands—reachable by nothing, governed by nothing but the flood gauge nailed to the customs house wall. I had known this when I came. I had told myself it suited me: a season of enforced stillness, a place to wait out the consortium's attention, because surely Halloran would not linger past the first hard rain.

But knowing a trap is a trap does not make it comfortable to sit inside it.

* * *

The gauge at the customs house was painted in bands—green, yellow, orange, red—and the whole town organized itself around those colors the way other towns organize around church bells. Green meant the docks worked. Yellow meant the lower streets moved their goods upstairs. Orange meant the lower streets moved themselves. Red meant the river owned everything below the second story, and the town simply agreed to let it, sweeping the mud out again in October like hosts tidying after a rowdy guest.

I watched them do it with something close to disbelief the first time the orange band went under. There was no panic. A man named Oskar Vey, who kept the chandlery, walked his inventory up two flights of stairs over the course of an afternoon, whistling. The coffee woman stacked her tables on the roof of her stall and weighted them with stones. Children were sent into the flooded lanes in shallow-draft punts to fetch things people had forgotten, shrieking with joy, poling through what had been a street on Monday.

"You're staring," Sable Idris said.

She had appeared beside me at the awning rail the way she appeared everywhere—without announcing herself, as though she'd been standing there all along and had only now decided to be visible. She was counting the channels again. I'd learned to recognize it: her eyes moving across the brown water, left bank to right, reading the current the way I read a bridge truss, looking for the load paths.

"It floods on purpose," I said. "They built for it."

"They built *with* it." She nodded at the punts. "Kestrian river towns did the same. My grandfather wrote about it—the annual drowning as a civic institution. He thought it was the most honest thing a settlement could do. Admit once a year that the land was never really yours."

I said nothing. It was getting harder, that silence. Every week she gave me another piece of Kestria—a word, a custom, a photograph—and every piece made the chest sitting under my floorboards heavier, and made the lie of my neutrality thinner.

"The gauge says red by Friday," she said. "You should bring your boat up. The mooring pilings at your berth will be three feet under."

"I've moored in floods before."

"Not here." She turned and looked at me directly, and there was no broker's polish in it now, just the flat assessment of someone raised on rivers. "Here the current takes boats off pilings like teeth from a comb. Come up to the quarter. We have a slip above the orange line. Old Kestrian stonework—it's held for sixty years."

"Why would you do that?" I asked. It came out more suspicious than I intended, but suspicion had become my native language and I no longer bothered translating it into courtesy.

Sable didn't take offense. She never did; it was one of the most unnerving things about her. "Because you're going to be here all winter regardless," she said, "and I'd rather you spent it where I can see you."

* * *

I moved the boat. That was the thing I kept returning to, in the nights afterward—I moved the boat, and moving it meant staying, and staying meant becoming legible. A man anchored in a flood is a man whose neighbors learn his habits. They learn when he drinks his coffee, how he ties his lines, whether he flinches at thunder. Invisibility is a discipline of motion, and the monsoon took motion away from me the way the consortium had once taken my name.

The exile quarter sat on the high shelf of the town, above the orange line, a warren of stone houses built by hands that remembered mountains. The slip Sable offered was cut into the living rock behind a chandler's shed, and when I ran my palm along its masonry I felt the tool marks—fine, regular, confident. Surveyor's work. Her grandfather's generation had built infrastructure in exile the way other men said prayers: faithfully, daily, without any expectation the country would come back to use it.

The chest stayed under my floorboards. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is the whole currency of this account: even after the raid, even after old Tomas's blood on the cobbles, even after Halloran's offer and my refusal and the certain knowledge that refusing had only sharpened their interest—I still kept the chest. Partly because giving it up felt like surrender. Partly because some stubborn engineer's part of me wanted to keep reading the survey inside it, the dead man's route through the glacier pass, tracing those impossible gradients with my finger at night like a monk with scripture.

And partly, I think, because it was the only thing in nine years that had made me matter to anyone, and I hated how much I liked it.

* * *

Red week came, and the river rose to meet it.

I have stood on bridges in failure and watched water destroy things I built, so believe me when I say I know what rising water sounds like. But I had never heard it sound like *this*—not violent, not hungry, just vast and patient, climbing the customs house wall a hand's width at a hour while the entire town went about its business above the line. The lower streets vanished. Not ruined—vanished, their roofs becoming stepping stones, their doorways becoming fish habitat. The coffee woman's stall showed only its stone-weighted roof, and someone had already tied a lantern to it, so at night the drowned street glowed faintly green under six feet of current, like a lamp lit in a sunken room.

"First flood?" Oskar Vey asked me. He'd found me at the high seawall at dusk, watching the water eat the town I'd learned to walk.

"First one I've stayed for."

"That's the difference." He handed me a cup of something hot and alcoholic that the exiles brewed and refused to name in trade-tongue. "Everyone runs from their first flood. The ones who stay learn the secret."

"Which is?"

He looked out at the drowned streets, at the lantern on the coffee stall's roof, at children poling punts between chimney tops. "It comes back," he said. "Every October, the water leaves, and everything's still there. Muddy, but there. You spend your whole life thinking that whatever goes under is gone forever. Then you live somewhere that floods on purpose, and you learn that some things survive being submerged." He drank. "Towns. Languages. Countries."

He said the last word lightly, the way a man drops a coin in a well to hear how deep it is, and walked off before I could decide whether I'd been comforted or threatened.

* * *

The strange truth of that season—which I set down now without fully understanding it—is that the terror and the comfort arrived together, inseparable, like the two heads of the hawk on the chest.

The terror came at night. It came as the sound of the river against the seawall, a sound like breathing, enormous and slow, and it told me: *you cannot run.* Nine years of running, and the monsoon had done what Halloran's fixers and the consortium's lawyers never managed—it had made standing still compulsory. I lay awake in the loft above the chandler's shed and felt the trap close, and my heart did what trapped hearts do. Some nights I got up and checked the boat twice, three times, running my thumb along the seams as if readiness were the same as escape. Some nights I took out the survey sheets and read the pass route until dawn, because a route is a promise of somewhere else, and I needed the promise the way other men needed sleep.

But the comfort came in the mornings, and it came from the same source.

It came from being greeted. From the exile children—who had drilled geography of a country that existed nowhere—deciding I was fair game for their lessons, quizzing me on mountain names that appeared on no map, correcting my pronunciation with the mercilessness of the young. From the old men nodding to me at the bakery as if my presence there were a fact of the landscape, like the seawall. From the coffee woman, whose stall was underwater, setting up a temporary brazier in the quarter square and handing me a cup each morning without my asking, the unburnt ends already set aside.

Nobody in Lowmark asked who I was anymore. That was the comfort and it was also, I understood dimly, the danger. Because they had stopped asking not because they'd lost interest but because they'd decided, and what they'd decided was that I was theirs. Neighbor. The word got used openly. *Neighbor, hold this. Neighbor, the gauge is climbing. Neighbor, taste this and tell me if it's spoiled.*

A man can hide behind a false name. He cannot hide behind a neighbor's face.

* * *

Mid-season, the river threw its one tantrum.

The scheduled flood was one thing—orderly, anticipated, almost gentle in its bureaucracy. But halfway through the red weeks a storm cell parked itself over the headwaters and the river jumped eighteen inches in a single night, over the red band, over the mark painted above the red band that nobody had ever explained to me, and the whole town woke at three in the morning to the sound of the seawall working.

I was on the wall before I'd properly decided to be, in the rain, with forty other people, passing sandbags down a human chain by lantern light. And here is what I remember most: nobody ordered it. There was no authority present worth the name. The chain assembled itself because everyone in Lowmark understood, without discussion, that the wall was the town and the town was the wall, and a gap anywhere was a gap everywhere.

Sable worked two positions down from me, hauling bags with a rhythm that never faltered, and at some point in the small hours our eyes met across the chain and she shouted—not words, just a sound, the wordless shout of laborers acknowledging each other—and I shouted back, and something in my chest that had been clenched for nine years loosened half a turn.

At dawn the river crested a hand's width below the top course of stones and began, grudgingly, to fall. We stood along the wall in the gray light, soaked through, filthy, laughing in the exhausted way of people who have survived something together, and old Tomas—his ribs still taped from the raid, his eye still yellowed—put his arm across my shoulders and said, in his accented trade-tongue:

"Now you are one of us. You have fought the river."

"I haven't decided that," I said.

He laughed. "None of us decided. That is how it works."

* * *

The water retreated in September the way it had risen—by inches, on its own schedule, indifferent to anyone's plans. The lower streets emerged one roof at a time, then one doorway at a time, and the town went down into the mud with brooms and buckets and began the annual resurrection, and I went with them, because by then I did not know how else to behave.

My boat had survived on the rock slip, exactly as Sable promised. Sixty-year-old Kestrian stonework, holding true while newer moorings downstream had been chewed to splinters. I stood on the deck and looked at the damage the season had done her anyway—seams opened, caulking sprung, a list to port that hadn't been there in June—and I understood that the boat and I had taken the same beating from the same months, and that neither of us was going anywhere until we were repaired.

Sable came down the slip that evening with a carpenter's bag over her shoulder and a statement rather than a question.

"She'll need re-caulking stem to stern if you mean to run the post-monsoon water," she said. "High melt. It'll be rougher than the flood, in its way. I've done this work before. Two hands go faster than one."

I looked at her, and at the bag, and at the boat that was currently my only means of leaving and therefore my last possession worth the name freedom.

"You're assuming I'm staying long enough to need a seaworthy boat," I said.

"No," Sable said, setting the bag down on the boards with a thump of finality. "I'm assuming the passes open in five weeks, the chest has to reach them, and sooner or later you're going to stop pretending you haven't memorized that survey. So we may as well start on the boat."

I opened my mouth to deny it—to deny all of it, the memorization, the intention, the whole architecture of reluctance I'd built plank by plank all season.

Nothing came out. And she smiled, the way a person smiles at a lock they already have the key to, and picked up her first caulking iron.
