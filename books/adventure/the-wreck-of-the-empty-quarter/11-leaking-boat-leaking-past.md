## 11. Leaking Boat, Leaking Past

The boat was dying by inches, and Sable Idris had decided she was not going to let it.

She'd known hulls like this one all her life—river-built, river-rotted, held together by stubbornness and tar. This one had a name scraped off the stern and a history scraped off its owner, and it sat on the mudflat behind Vesper Ravensworth's rented room like a patient who had stopped believing in doctors. The monsoon had gotten into her seams. Every morning the bilge held two fingers of brown water and a smell like drowned rope. Every morning Vesper bailed her out, said nothing, and went back to pretending he hadn't decided to stay.

Sable had been counting. Not the days—she'd stopped counting days when she was nine, on the theory that exile was easier to survive in hours. She was counting the things Vesper did that a man planning to leave did not do. He'd mended his mooring lines twice. He'd bought a second tarp. He'd started saying *after the water drops* the way other people said *tomorrow*, without flinching.

He was staying. He just hadn't told himself yet.

So she came down to the mudflat on the fourth morning of the flood season with a pot of pitch, a bundle of oakum, and the flat, unanswerable tone of a woman who had already made up everyone's mind, and she said, "We're caulking her today."

"We?" Vesper was already there, of course. He slept like a cat—she'd learned that much—waking at every sound, dressed before his eyes were fully open. He looked at the pitch pot, then at her, then at the boat, and she could see the refusal assembling itself behind his eyes, brick by careful brick.

"You can't run the post-flood current in a hull with three open seams and a plank that moves when you press it." She crouched, pressed the plank. It moved. "There. She'll open like a purse in the first set of rapids, and you'll spend a week drying your papers on rocks, if you're lucky."

"I'm not running rapids."

"Everyone runs rapids eventually. The river decides when." She set the pot on a flat stone and began feeding the little spirit stove beneath it. "Sit down, Ravensworth. Or Vesper. Or whatever the paper in your pocket says today. You can hold the oakum."

He held the oakum. That was the thing about him she'd understood by the second week: you couldn't argue him anywhere, but you could hand him a task, and his hands would go where his mouth wouldn't. He picked up the caulking iron and the mallet like a man picking up a language he'd sworn off, and within ten minutes his strokes had the clean economy of someone who had done ten thousand of them, and Sable felt something in her chest loosen and tighten at the same time.

Because she knew who he was. She'd known since Lowmark, since before the chest, since the night she'd stood in the doorway of the ferry office and heard the clerk call him *Mr. Pemberton* and watched the man flinch at the name like a horse at a whip-crack. She'd gone back to her room and written the name down and stared at it until midnight. Vesper Ravensworth. The Vell Bridge. Eleven dead. The signature at the bottom of the plans that every newspaper in three countries had printed, and the consortium's lawyers had made sure was the *only* name printed.

The exile quarter knew the story too. Old Tomas had recognized him before she did—Tomas, who had spent forty years reading railway men the way she read rivers, because Kestria had been a country of railways before it was a country of memories. *That's the one who signed the bridge,* Tomas had said, and Sable had said, *then why does he look like a man carrying eleven coffins instead of one signature,* and Tomas had said nothing, which from Tomas was a great deal.

She hadn't told Vesper she knew. Not yet. Knowledge was a tool, and tools got dull if you used them too early.

The morning went the way mornings go when two people work with their hands: the talking happened sideways, in the gaps. He showed her how to drive the oakum so the seam swelled rather than split. She showed him where the river rats had gnawed the sternpost and how to pack wire mesh into the wound. The pitch bubbled. The rain came and went in the thin, warm, monotonous way it had all season, and neither of them bothered to stop working when it fell.

"Your hands," he said at one point, watching her lay a bead of pitch. "You've done boatwork before."

"I've done everything before. That's what being born on a river gets you." She worked the bead flat. "My mother's people ran cargo on the Serein. Before."

"Before," he repeated. He didn't ask before what. That was its own kind of courtesy, and she filed it away with the other small evidences: the man was not indifferent to other people's losses. He was only determined not to trade his own.

* * *

By midday the third seam was closed and they'd turned the boat on her side to reach the garboard, and Vesper had gone quiet in the particular way he went quiet—not the silence of a man with nothing to say, but of a man holding something down. Sable had learned to read his silences the way she read water: by what moved underneath.

She handed him the mallet, took the iron herself, and said, casually, to the hull, "The Vell Bridge had a scissor truss, didn't it. Over the main span."

The mallet stopped.

She kept her eyes on the seam. "I read the inquiry transcript. All of it. The load calculations, the wind data, the testimony about the ice jam." She drove the iron into the seam, one slow inch. "You know what struck me? Everyone testified about the steel. Nobody testified about the drawings. Eleven dead, three countries of newspapers, and not one engineer asked to see the original drawings."

"They saw them." His voice was flat as the mudflat. "They saw what they were shown."

"And what were you shown?"

The silence stretched long enough that she heard a kingfisher somewhere up the cut, and the rain starting again on the tarp they'd rigged over the stern.

"Plans," he said finally. "I was shown plans. I checked them. I signed them." He took the mallet back from her—she let him—and set it down, and looked at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger he was tired of. "That's the story. That's the whole story. I checked the plans and I signed them and the span came down in a wind it should have shrugged off, and eleven people went into the Vell with the morning mail."

"And the consortium's directors—"

"Were never named. I was the name. The signature was the product." He almost smiled; it was the ugliest thing she'd seen him do. "They didn't even fire me. They gave me a settlement and a reference and a suggestion that I might be happier working somewhere with, ah, fewer journalists. That's how you learn you've been made into a door. You walk through your own ruin and find the room behind it is full of men you've never met."

Sable laid the iron down and sat back on her heels. The rain ticked on the tarp. She had rehearsed this conversation a dozen ways in the dark, and now that it was here she found she didn't want the rehearsed version. She wanted the true one, and she suspected—she had suspected since the night she'd watched him bail the bilge by lamplight, moving with the automatic patience of a man who did not expect to sleep—that the true one was stranger.

"That's not what I asked," she said.

He looked at her.

"I didn't ask what they showed you. I asked what you were shown. And you gave me the same answer twice, which means you've given it so many times it's worn smooth, like a coin." She turned to face him fully. "I'm not the inquiry, Vesper. I'm not a journalist. I'm not Halloran." His jaw tightened at the name. "I'm a woman caulking a boat with you, and I'm asking you what you *do*. At night. Because I've seen your lamp burning past two o'clock, and I've seen the papers you burn in the stove afterward, and paper that burns that fast and that often is drawing paper, and a man who isn't an engineer anymore doesn't burn drawings every night for nine years."

The river said what rivers say, which is nothing, endlessly.

Vesper Ravensworth looked at the boat between them—the boat he had not decided to keep, the boat he was caulking anyway—and Sable watched him arrive at the understanding that she was not going away, that the flood had closed the passes and the roads and every exit he'd ever kept oiled, and that the only thing left in the world was this mudflat and this conversation.

"Graph paper," he said.

It came out rough, like something hauled up from depth.

"Every night. Graph paper. I draw the span." He rubbed his thumb along the caulking iron's handle, back and forth, a man touching a talisman. "The Vell main span. From memory. Every member, every plate, every rivet line. I've drawn it—" he stopped, recalculated, and she saw him decide to stop lying by rounding down— "I've drawn it more than three thousand times. I could draw it on the back of a postage stamp in the dark."

"Why?"

"Because I'm looking for the flaw." The words came faster now, a sluice gate opening after years of rust. "The plans I signed were sound. I've checked them three thousand times and they are *sound*. The truss geometry, the dead load, the wind bracing—sound. So either the steel was bad, or the plans I signed were not the plans that were built, and if the plans I signed were not the plans that were built, then someone swapped them, and if someone swapped them—" His hand closed around the iron. "Then I didn't kill eleven people through negligence. I was *used*. And I can't prove it, because the original drawings went into the consortium's archive the week after the collapse, and the archive is in a building I can't enter, in a city where my face is known, and so I draw the span instead. Every night. Looking for the flaw. Because the flaw is the proof, and the proof is the only thing that would make the drawing stop."

The rain came down harder. Neither of them moved to the tarp.

Sable found that her hands had gone still in her lap, and that her heart was doing something complicated and painful, because she had come down to the mudflat to extract a confession and instead she'd been handed a key—and the key fit a door she'd been staring at for months without seeing.

"The consortium's archive," she said carefully.

"Their central records. Vellmark. Why?"

"Vesper." She made herself say it slowly, so it would come out steady. "The chest that washed into your pilings. The Kestrian chest. You told me you recognized the consortium's watermark under the seal. On the survey documents."

"Yes."

"Where do you think a Kestrian survey, commissioned by a government that no longer exists, ended up with a consortium watermark on it?" She watched it land in him—watched the professional mind, the one he couldn't switch off, take hold of the question the way his hands took hold of a task. "The consortium didn't just buy maps of Kestrian territory, Vesper. They *kept* the originals. Their archive isn't a records office. It's a vault of everything they've ever made disappear. Treaties. Borders. Whole countries." Her voice caught and she hated it and let it. "Maybe even the original Vell drawings."

He had gone very still. The kind of still that comes before either flight or transformation.

"That's why they want the chest," he said. "Not because of what it proves about Kestria. Because of what it proves about *them*. The watermark. The chain of custody. If the chest surfaces, someone could subpoena the archive, and the archive—"

"—is full of doors," Sable finished. "And you've been made into one of them."

For a long moment neither of them spoke, and the boat lay between them on its side like a witness, half-caulked, half-open, its seams stopped and its wound still showing.

Then Vesper did something she had never seen him do. He reached into the breast of his coat—she flinched, one hand going to her knife, nine years of exile reflex—and came out not with a weapon but with a stub of carpenter's pencil, worn to the length of a thumb, and a folded square of graph paper gone soft at the creases from unfolding.

He didn't show her what was drawn on it. He just held it, and she understood that she was looking at the nightly ritual itself, carried against his heart for years, the flaw he was still looking for, the proof he couldn't finish.

"Three thousand times," he said quietly. "And last month, reading the Kestrian survey, I found their notation system in an afternoon. A dead country's shorthand, older than anything I'd ever trained on." He looked at her with an expression she was going to spend a long time learning the name of. "Sable. If the consortium's archive holds the original Vell plans—and the chest says it might—then helping your people get to their valley isn't charity. It's the first job I've ever had where the work and the amends point the same direction."

"That's not why we're asking you," Sable said. "Tomas would tell you the same. We ask because you can read the survey. What you get out of it is yours."

"That's what frightens me about it," he said, and almost smiled, and this time it was not the ugly version.

* * *

They worked until the light went copper and the rain settled into its evening rhythm. The last seam took the pitch, and Sable pressed the plank, and it held—solid, silent, a boat again instead of a rumor of one. Vesper ran his palm along the hull the way a man touches a thing he has decided, without announcing it, to keep.

From up the cut came the sound Sable had been listening for all day without letting herself hear it: voices, many voices, low and urgent, in the old language. The exile quarter's evening call, except the evening call was two hours early and it was not a call for supper.

Tomas came down the mudflat path fast for a man of seventy, his lantern swinging, and the look on his face made Sable stand before she'd decided to.

"The old men are meeting," he said, in Kestrian, then, seeing Vesper's face, again in the trade tongue. "Tonight. The whole council." His eyes went to Vesper, and stayed there, and something passed over the old man's face that Sable had never seen on it before—hope, worn smooth as a river stone, being picked up again after years in the drawer. "They're voting on the pass. And they want the engineer to hear it."

Vesper looked at Sable. She looked at the boat—caulked, sound, ready—and then at the lantern light bobbing up the path toward a room full of old men who spoke a dead language and remembered a living country.

"They want *me*," he said. Not a question. A man checking, one last time, whether the door he was standing in was real.

"The whole quarter wants you," Tomas said. "Come and find out what that costs."

Sable picked up the pitch pot and the tools, and the three of them went up out of the flood together, and behind them the mended boat rocked gently at its mooring, holding water for the first time in weeks—holding it, and keeping it out.
