## 12. The Vote of the Old Men

The rain had settled into its long argument with the town by the time the summons came. Not the violent monsoon downpours of the first weeks—those had passed like fevers—but a steady grey drizzle that made the whole of Lowmark sound like paper being slowly torn. Vesper heard it through the wall of the exile quarter's meeting house, where he sat on a bench older than any nation he could name, waiting to be judged.

They had asked him politely. That was the worst part. An old woman named Ferren—she of the hands like knotted rope, who had once, in another life, been a schoolteacher in a country that no longer appeared in atlases—had come to his door with a bowl of rice and dried fish and said, *The council would hear you tomorrow evening, if you're willing.* As if it were an invitation to tea. As if they hadn't all watched him burn his cover to cinders three nights ago when the consortium men came for the chest and old Tammas Vey stood in their way with nothing but his body and his stubbornness.

Vesper had broken a man's nose for that stubbornness. He was still not sure whether it had been courage or simply the last straw of a man tired of watching other people bleed for things he refused to stand behind.

The chest sat at the front of the meeting house now, bronze bindings green with river rot, the two-headed hawk of Kestria staring out from its lid with both faces at once—the watcher and the watched. Candles burned at either end of the long table where the council sat: nine elders, most of them past seventy, arranged in a semicircle like a jury that had been rehearsing for fifty years.

Sable stood beside the chest. She caught his eye as he entered and gave him nothing—not encouragement, not warning. Just acknowledgment. She had stopped trying to read him days ago, or perhaps she had simply decided she'd seen enough.

"Sit," said Ferren, indicating a chair facing the council. They had placed it so that he would face them with the chest at his shoulder. He understood the geometry. Whatever he said tonight, he would say it beside their country's remains.

He sat.

* * *

The vote came late, after two hours of talk in Kestric—a language like water over stones, all rounded vowels and buried consonants—and then again in trade-tongue for his benefit, which struck Vesper as a courtesy he hadn't earned. The old men argued the way engineers argue about load tolerances: patiently, with numbers, with the assumption that the truth would hold if they built the argument right.

The facts were these. Calving season would open the glacier pass in roughly five weeks—maybe six, depending on how the summer heat worked at the icefall. The window would last perhaps twenty days. Sable would take the chest upriver with a small party, follow the dead surveyor's route through the pass, and descend into the valley where Kestria's archives had been buried before the annexation: census rolls, treaties, land grants, the parliamentary minutes of a government that had surrendered its own existence rather than let it be stolen outright. Enough paper to prove a nation had lived. Enough paper, maybe, to make it live again.

That much had been decided before Vesper ever walked into the room. What remained was the question they'd been circling all evening, the one Ferren finally set down in front of him like a card turned face-up.

"The survey," she said. "You've read it."

"I've read what I could." Which was true, and also a lie by understatement. He had read all of it. He had read it four times, and twice more in his sleep.

"And the notation system. The surveyor's shorthand."

"It's a private cipher. Family-built, I'd guess—passed down, refined over years. Grid references folded into elevation marks, water sources encoded as punctuation. Whoever drew it didn't want just anyone following his line." Vesper kept his voice level, professional. "It's elegant work. Better than anything the consortium ever produced."

A murmur went around the table. One of the old men—Brey, who had a voice like a landslide starting—said something in Kestric that made several others look at Vesper with new attention.

"He says you speak of it like a man speaks of a lover's handwriting," Ferren translated, dry as chalk.

"I speak of it like an engineer," Vesper said. "Which is the only honest way I have left."

* * *

Here is what he did not tell them, though every word of it pressed against his teeth:

He knew the route. Not approximately. *Exactly.* Every gradient, every switchback, every tunnel length and bridge span and snow-shed interval between the flooded river town and the valley beyond the pass. He had memorized the survey during the insomniac weeks after Tammas Vey first showed him the chest—no, earlier than that, he realized; he had begun memorizing it the second night, alone on his boat with a candle stub and the documents spread across his chart table, telling himself he was only studying, only assessing buildability, only doing what any engineer would do with a document like this in his hands.

He had memorized a country's spine because he couldn't sleep, and now the country wanted it back.

And here was the other thing he did not tell them: he had already drawn the maps. Three nights running, after the raid, after the old man's blood on the stones of the quarter, he had sat up producing fair copies—route profiles, elevation charts, the pass approach in four sections, water crossings annotated with the surveyor's own coded notes translated into standard convention. They were good maps. They were the best work he'd done in nine years, and he had made them in secret, in the small hours, the way he used to redraw the Vell Bridge plans night after night, line by line, as if perfect repetition could undo a single moment of failure.

The maps were wrapped in oilcloth under his bunk. He had brought them to the meeting house tonight. They were sitting against his leg right now, and he had not mentioned them, because mentioning them would be a form of answering, and he had already decided what his answer would be.

* * *

"We ask you to guide them," Ferren said.

The candle flames leaned in the draft from the door. Somewhere outside, the river spoke its one long sentence.

"You've read the cipher," Brey continued, in his grinding voice. "Sable cannot. None of us can—we are weavers and fishermen and one bad accountant. The surveyor was my cousin's husband, and even I never learned his marks. If the party takes a wrong branch above the icefall, they die in a crevasse field that has swallowed better map-readers than any of us. You are the only living person who can walk that line and know it."

"Not the only one," Vesper said quietly.

"The surveyor is dead."

"His notation isn't. It can be taught. I can teach it—to Sable, to whoever goes. A week of evenings. The core of it is simple once you see the logic; the rest is practice." He heard his own voice doing what it always did in rooms like this: retreating into competence, building walls out of technicality. "I'll draw the full route. Every section. Redundant copies. I'll mark the hazards so plainly a child could follow them."

Ferren studied him for a long moment. She had eyes the color of wet slate, and they did not blink enough.

"That is not what we asked."

"No," Vesper said. "It isn't."

* * *

He should have expected the silence to be worse than anger. Anger he could have handled—he'd spent nine years marinating in other people's anger, lawyers' anger, widows' anger, the cold procedural fury of men whose signatures were cleaner than their consciences. But the council simply looked at him, nine old faces in candlelight, and waited, as if the truth might yet arrive on its own if given room.

It was Sable who broke it, and her voice was careful in a way that cost her something.

"Say the rest of it, Vesper."

"There's nothing else to say. I'll draw the maps. I'll teach the notation. That's what I can give."

"Why not more?"

Because. Because the last time he put his name to a route, eleven people fell four hundred feet into a gorge while he stood on the bank listening to the sound a bridge makes when it decides it is finished. Because the consortium had taken that signature and used it as a shield, and he had learned—had *learned*, had paid tuition in eleven lives—that a man's name on a plan is a promise, and promises are the first thing that gets sold. Because if he went up that mountain and someone died on a line he had vouched for, there would be no coming back from it, no drifting to some other river town under some other name, no next chapter. There would only be the number going up. Twelve. Thirteen. Fourteen.

Because cowardice, he had discovered, fits like an old coat. You stop feeling the weight of it.

What he said was: "My presence adds risk without adding capability. The maps carry everything I know. A guide who can't be replaced is a liability the party doesn't need."

"Liar," said Brey, without heat, the way a man identifies weather.

Vesper looked at the old man. Brey looked back, unbothered, his big hands flat on the table like things set down permanently.

"You think we don't know what you are?" Brey said. "We've had nine weeks of you. We watch how a man walks away from things. You walk away slow. You look back. Cowards don't look back, engineer. Cowards run clean. You've been standing at the edge of this since the night you pried open that chest, and every week you stay, you sink in a little deeper, and every week you pretend the water isn't rising." He tapped the table once. "So no. We don't believe your risk calculus. We believe you're frightened. And we'd like you to say so, because we are old, and we haven't got the patience left for anything but the truth."

The candles hissed. Vesper became aware of his own heartbeat, absurdly loud, like footsteps on a trestle.

* * *

He thought about the Vell Bridge. He thought about it the way he thought about it every night—not the collapse itself, which his memory had mercifully blurred into noise and rotation and the terrible slow tilt of the far span, but the morning after, when the consortium's lawyers had laid the drawings in front of him and explained, with great kindness, that the fatigue cracks in the primary truss members had been documented in an internal memo fourteen months prior, and that the memo's distribution list contained no names, only titles, and that the signature block at the bottom of the certification page contained exactly one name, and it was his.

They hadn't threatened him. That was the thing people never believed. They had simply shown him the shape of the trap and let him climb into it himself, because a man will always choose the version of the story in which he retains some agency, even if the agency consists entirely of choosing his own destruction. He signed. They thanked him. The directors stayed nameless. He took the settlement money, changed his name, and began the long discipline of leaving.

Nine years of leaving. Nine years of forged papers and small notes and boats that leaked and towns he could describe but never claim. And in all that time he had told himself the wandering was survival. Necessary. Prudent. He had never once, until this candlelit room, allowed himself to call it what Brey had just called it in front of everyone.

Frightened.

"I'm frightened," Vesper said.

It came out smaller than he intended. He made himself go on.

"I'm frightened that I'll sign my name to something again and it will break. I'm frightened that I'm the kind of man things break under. I'm frightened—" and here his voice did fail him briefly, and he hated it, and pushed through anyway, "—I'm frightened that if I go up that mountain and lead those people onto ice I've only seen on paper, and the ice is wrong, or the season is wrong, or I am wrong, then everything I am becomes the thing that killed them. You want me to say it plainly? There it is. I would rather hand you a perfect map and stay safely worthless than stand on that glacier and find out what my judgment actually weighs."

The silence afterward was different. Not softer, exactly. But it had shifted from judgment to recognition, and recognition, Vesper thought, was almost harder to bear.

Ferren folded her hands. "Thank you," she said, and meant it, which was somehow the cruelest thing anyone had said to him in nine years.

* * *

The vote, when it finally came, was not about him. That was the surprise. The council voted—nine hands, eight raised, one abstention—to send Sable and the chest upriver at calving season, with whatever guides the exile quarter could spare, navigating by whatever maps the engineer would provide. His refusal was accepted without comment, folded into the record like a known quantity, a coefficient in an equation. Nobody argued. Nobody pleaded. They simply planned around the hole where a guide should have been, the way water plans around a stone.

It was, Vesper realized as the meeting dissolved into Kestric murmurs and the scraping of benches, the most dignified rejection he had ever received. They had not needed his courage. They had only hoped for it, the way you hope for rain, and they would plant regardless.

He stayed until the room emptied except for Sable and the chest and the guttering candles. Then he pulled the oilcloth bundle from against his leg and set it on the table between them.

"What's this?" she said, though her hands were already reaching.

"The route. Complete. Four sections, hazard-marked, redundant copies. The notation translated throughout." He kept his voice in the register of delivery, of business concluded. "There's a teaching schedule inside too. Seven evenings. Start with the grid-folding; everything else hangs off it. By the time the pass opens, you won't need me."

Sable unwrapped the oilcloth slowly. Her fingers moved over the drawings the way they had moved over the boat's hull during repairs—reading material through touch, testing for flaws. She reached the third sheet, the icefall approach, and stopped.

"This is exact," she said. "This isn't interpretation. This is the line itself, every switchback, every—" She looked up. "When did you do this?"

"Does it matter?"

"When. Did. You. Do. This."

"After the raid. Nights." He shrugged, and the shrug felt like the flimsiest structure he'd ever built. "I couldn't sleep anyway."

She stared at him for a long moment, and he watched her arrive at the same conclusion Brey had voiced aloud, and he braced for her to say it—to say *you memorized it, you've had it in your head the whole time, you could lead them yourself*—

But Sable Idris, who counted rivers and read men like cargo manifests, only rolled the maps closed, tied the oilcloth, and held them against her chest the way you hold something that has been handed to you across a very long distance.

"Seven evenings," she said. "Starting tomorrow. Bring the candle stubs—you'll need them."

She carried the maps out into the rain. Vesper sat alone in the meeting house with the chest and the dying candles, and told himself the tightness in his chest was relief.

Outside, the river rose a finger's width against the pilings, patient as a creditor, and somewhere upstream, five weeks' hard travel away, a glacier waited with its few weeks of mercy already counting down.
