## 14. The Chest Changes Hands

Halloran had been in a hundred raids and this was the first one he'd hated before it started.

He stood in the doorway of the exile quarter's meeting house with rain running off his hat brim and four consortium men at his back, and he watched an old man with a face like crumpled paper put himself between them and a floorboard, and something in Halloran's chest went wrong. Not softness—he'd had that cut out of him years ago, or told himself he had—but recognition. He knew what it looked like when a man decided a thing was worth his ribs. He'd seen it in mirrors once.

"Nobody has to be hurt," Halloran said, and meant it, which was the problem. The men behind him hadn't come for meaning.

The old man didn't move. He said something in the dead language—Kestrian, they called it, though the word meant nothing to the consortium's ledgers—and one of the younger exiles translated without being asked, voice shaking: "He says you're standing on the graves of people who are still alive."

"Poetic," said Voss, the big one, the one the regional office had sent along because Halloran's last three reports had been, in their words, insufficiently decisive. Voss carried a truncheon the way other men carried opinions. "Move him."

What happened next took eleven seconds and Halloran counted every one of them, because counting was how he kept himself from doing something about it. Voss went through the old man like weather. The exile folded around the truncheon and went down onto the floorboard he'd been protecting, and Halloran heard—not imagined, heard—the hollow knock of wood on wood beneath him, the sound of a cavity under the boards.

"There," Voss said, breathing hard, pleased with himself. He knelt, wrenched up the plank, and hauled out the chest.

Bronze-bound. Hawk-stamped. Heavy as a sin.

* * *

The raid had been three weeks in the making, and Halloran had built it himself, which was another thing he'd have to answer for eventually, if there turned out to be an eventually where answers were taken.

The order from the regional office had been elegant in the way of all ugly orders: *Recover the salvage. Ensure no documentation survives in private hands. The historical record is a matter for the company.* He'd read it twice and understood it completely. The chest that had washed against Vesper Ravensworth's mooring pilings contained papers, and the papers contained the consortium's oldest crime, older than the Vell Bridge, older than Halloran's employment: forty years ago, the consortium had sold Kestria's territory surveys to the power that annexed her. Rail grades. River crossings. The passes. They had drawn the invader a map of a country and let the country be erased, and then they had erased themselves from the transaction, and now the transaction had floated downriver out of the flood and landed at the feet of a disgraced engineer with nothing left to lose.

The chest could not exist. That was the whole of the strategy. Not seized—seized things could leak, could resurface, could end up in a courtroom or a newspaper. It had to go into the river again, or better, into a furnace, with witnesses enough to swear it had never held anything but mildew and old clothes.

Halloran had planned it cleanly. Enter at dusk during the monsoon lock-in when the lower streets flooded and the exiles couldn't scatter. Take the chest. Burn the contents on the spot in the meeting house stove, in front of everyone, so the lesson landed: your country is not coming back, and we can prove it isn't, and proving it is our business, not yours.

Clean. He'd done clean work his whole career. He'd been exiled from somewhere himself once—a border province nobody could find on a map anymore either—and he had learned early that the world did not reward men who hesitated over other people's paperwork.

So why, standing in the rain with the chest finally in his hands, did his own hands shake?

Because of the old man on the floor. Because the old man had gone down protecting not the chest itself but the floorboard, and Halloran had spent twenty years reading rooms and he understood what that meant: there had been something else under there, or the chest had been hidden under there so long the hiding had become sacred. These people had kept a dead country alive in a floorboard for a generation. And Halloran's employers wanted to kill it a second time, quietly, with a stove.

Voss hefted the chest. "Boat's waiting. You want it opened here or—"

"Stove," Halloran said. "Here. In front of them. That was the instruction."

One of the younger exiles made a sound like something tearing. An old woman began to sing, low, in the dead language, and two more joined her, and the sound filled the meeting house the way water fills a hull—not fast, but certain.

"Make them stop," Voss said.

"No." Halloran set his hand flat on the chest lid. The bronze binding was cold despite the wet heat of the monsoon night. "Let them sing. It costs us nothing and it looks worse if we don't."

It costs us nothing. He heard the words as he said them and heard, underneath them, the ledger-voice of the regional office, the voice that had promoted him six times by teaching him that everything cost something and most things cost other people. He was tired. He was fifty-one years old and he was tired in a way that sleep didn't touch, and he had begun, in the last month, to dream about a kitchen he hadn't seen since he was nine years old.

* * *

The stove in the meeting house was iron, squat, fed with driftwood because coal was dear in Lowmark. They put the chest on the table beside it and Halloran told the exiles to stay back and they didn't, exactly—they pressed inward instead, a slow tide of old bodies and children, and Voss raised the truncheon and Halloran caught his wrist.

"Not the children," Halloran said. "Not ever the children. If you can't do this job without hitting children, the job is wrong and so are you."

Voss looked at him with something new in his face—not respect, calculation. Halloran filed that away as a debt coming due. Men like Voss reported upward. Men like Voss survived by smelling weakness, and Halloran had just shown him some.

He pried the chest open with a pry-bar from his kit. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age: documents. Bundles of them. A sealed letter bearing a wax hawk. A survey folio with a spine cracked like a knuckle, its cover stamped in faded gilt—*Directorate of Lines and Grades, Royal Survey of Kestria*—and Halloran felt his breath go short, because he had started his career as a junior draftsman copying surveys exactly like this one, in a different country, for a different master, and he knew the handwriting style the way a priest knows liturgy.

He lifted the top bundle. Census rolls. Names, ages, parishes. Thousands of names in a careful clerical hand, each one a person who had been told their nation had never existed, and here was ink saying otherwise.

"Burn pile," Voss said. "Come on. Rain's picking up."

Halloran sorted. He told himself he was sorting for efficiency—burn the bulk, verify nothing incriminating escaped—but his hands were moving with a purpose he hadn't authorized. Treaty. Correspondence. And then, third bundle down, a thin folder tied with ribbon gone gray, marked in the corner with a provincial seal he recognized so hard it was like a fist.

His province. His erased province. The one that wasn't on maps anymore either.

He untied the ribbon with fingers that had stopped pretending to be steady.

Inside: an expulsion order. Dated the year he was born. *By authority of the occupying administration, the families herein listed are remanded across the border, holdings forfeit.* And below the list of names, in the column of forfeited holdings, a farm he knew the shape of even now, even after forty years—a long field, an orchard gone wild, a kitchen with a window over the sink where his mother had stood washing dishes and singing in a language the schoolmaster beat out of him within a year.

And beneath the expulsion order, a second document, the one that made the room tilt: a consortium bill of sale. The consortium had bought the forfeited holdings. His family's farm, and eleven others, sold at auction to the same company that now paid him to burn proof of what it had done. His wages had been paid, all these years, out of ground his family had been marched off of.

"Halloran." Voss's voice, closer now. "What've you got?"

Halloran closed the folder. The motion was smooth. Twenty years of clean work.

"Nothing. Chaff. Parish records." He set the folder on the table, and while Voss turned to shove the first armload of census rolls toward the stove, Halloran's hand came back and took it, and it went inside his coat, against his chest, flat and thin and burning cold, and he buttoned the coat over it like a man closing a wound.

He would tell himself later that he didn't decide. That his hand acted alone. But he had spent his whole life watching men claim their hands acted alone, and he wasn't going to join them. He decided. In the space of one breath, in a flooding room full of singing old people, Aldous Halloran decided that the consortium's fire was going to burn everything except one page, and that page was going to be his.

* * *

They burned it all. It took the better part of an hour, feeding the stove in bundles while the rain hammered the roof and the exiles sang until their voices gave out and then sang anyway, quieter, the way you keep a lamp lit by cupping it. Halloran worked methodically. He checked each bundle—really checked, because if Voss found a gap later it would go badly for everyone—and the folder stayed against his ribs, warming slowly to his body heat until he could almost believe it had always been part of him.

The sealed letter he held longest. Wax hawk, unbroken. Addressed in the dead language, but a translation slip was pinned to it in someone's modern hand: *For whoever finds this. We were real.*

He read those four words maybe thirty times while the stove ate a country.

We were real.

So had his province been. So had the orchard. So had the song his mother sang, which he could hum but no longer translate, and which he had not hummed aloud in thirty years because a man in his line of work could not afford a homeland, even a dead one, even a tune.

He dropped the letter in the stove. Watched the wax bubble. Watched the hawk go black, then orange, then nothing.

"You all right?" Voss asked, watching him sideways. "You look ill."

"The smoke," Halloran said. "Old paper. Go see to the boat."

When Voss was gone he stood alone in the meeting house with the ashes and the exiles, who watched him from the walls with eyes that had catalogued everything, missed nothing, and would remember forever. The old man who'd taken the beating was propped in a chair, lip split, and he looked at Halloran with no hatred at all, which was infinitely worse, and said something in Kestrian.

"He says," the young translator began, and stopped, and tried again. "He says the fire doesn't burn what's written in people. He says you'll find that out."

"I don't know what he means," Halloran said, and walked out into the rain with a stolen page against his heart and a stolen chest under his arm, and both statements were true, and only one of them was a lie.

* * *

The boat waited at the flooded lower landing, riding high, lanterns hooded. Voss had the chest stowed amidships under tarpaulin before Halloran reached the gangplank, and the two hired men from the regional office sat in the stern with the particular stillness of men who had been told to watch the man who hired them.

Halloran noticed that. He noticed everything; it was the job. What he noticed now, specifically, was that the chain of custody had already slipped out of his hands. The chest was no longer his recovery—it was the office's, witnessed, logged, and any deviation he attempted between here and the furnace barge downstream would be reported before he finished attempting it. He had planned this raid to the minute and had somehow failed to plan the ten minutes after success, because he had assumed success would feel like victory instead of like this: standing in the rain, empty-handed, holding a secret that was eating through his coat.

"Course?" the helmsman asked.

"Downstream. Furnace barge at Graylock Landing, dawn." He climbed aboard. The river took them and the town slid away behind them, lamplit streets drowning on schedule, and somewhere back in the dark of the exile quarter a man named Vesper Ravensworth—who had burned his own cover tonight stepping between Voss and an old man, according to the report that would reach the office by morning—was learning what Halloran had learned twenty years too late: that there was no such thing as invisible, only not-yet-noticed.

Halloran sat in the bow with the rain on his collar and the folder dry against his skin, and he did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding for three weeks.

If the chest burned at Graylock, the consortium was safe, the exiles had nothing, and Aldous Halloran had one page of proof of his own ruin and no way to use it, because a man who burned countries for a living did not get to stand up in court and say *but my country was real*. They would laugh. Then they would bury him. He'd buried enough men to know the depth of the hole.

But if the chest did *not* burn—if it went astray, upstream, toward a glacier pass that opened only weeks a year, into the hands of exiles and a disgraced engineer who had already shown tonight that he chose sides with his body before his brain could vote—

Then the hunt changed shape. Then it wasn't the consortium retrieving property anymore. Then it was three parties racing for the same valley, and Halloran—holder of one page, keeper of one flame, traitor-in-waiting to whichever side won—would get to choose, for the first time in thirty years, what his name was worth.

The river ran high and brown and fast, carrying the whole drowned season toward the sea. Somewhere ahead, at Graylock Landing, a furnace barge waited with its fires already lit.

"Hard to starboard at the split," Halloran told the helmsman. "We'll take the north channel."

The north channel bent upstream. The helmsman frowned. "Graylock's south, sir."

"I know where Graylock is." Halloran pulled his hat low and put his hand in his coat pocket, on the ribbon-tied folder, and felt the paper that proved his mother had owned an orchard. "Change of plans. Wake me when you see the pass road."

Behind him, under the tarpaulin, the bronze-bound chest rode north into the dark, and the hunt—three-sided now, and nobody entirely sure yet who was hunting whom—began.
