## 15. Downstream in Flood

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and that was the worst of it, because the silence let us hear the barge.

It came down the flooded channel like a rumor—no running lights, no bell, just the deep animal groan of a hull shouldering water it had no business displacing at that speed. I stood on the roof of the customs shed with Sable's spyglass to my eye and watched it pass the burned-out ferry landing, and even through the rain-blurred glass I could see the chest lashed amidships under a tarpaulin, bronze corner-caps catching the first gray light like the eyes of something waking.

"He's running the Narrows," Sable said beside me. She said it flatly, the way you'd say *the bridge is out*.

"Nobody runs the Narrows in flood."

"The survey maps say nobody runs the Narrows at all." She took the glass from my hand without asking. "Halloran isn't anybody."

I had spent nine years being nobody, and I knew exactly what she meant.

The boat was ready because we'd made it ready—three days of caulking and cursing in the flooded lower streets while the town emptied its cellars into the current, Sable driving oakum into seams with a mallet, me re-stepping the mast and arguing with her about whether a leaking hull could survive what we both knew we were going to ask of it. The answer was no. We went anyway. There is a kind of arithmetic that has nothing to do with numbers: a chest full of a country against one rotten boat and two people who had already lost everything else worth counting.

We put out from the customs steps as the light came up the color of dishwater. The river had swallowed the lower quay whole; the mooring posts stood up out of brown water like drowned men's fingers, and where the market street had been there was only current, fast and muscular, tugging at our keel the moment we cleared the shelter of the sheds.

Sable took the tiller. I took the sheet. Neither of us said anything about which of us was better at this, because we'd already settled it with three days of silence on the subject: she read water the way I read grades and curves, and I could keep a boat's guts together longer than anyone she'd met. Between us we might make one competent riverman. The Narrows would not care.

* * *

The flood had turned Lowmark's river into something I barely recognized, and I had spent two seasons learning its moods. Channels that had been knee-deep in August were now ten feet of churning brown; islands I'd fished from were submerged except for the crowns of trees, which broke the surface like the backs of swimming animals. The current ran maybe eight knots in the main channel, and every eddy line was a seam of white foam a hundred yards long, marking where the water argued with itself.

The barge was a mile ahead and gaining. It was built for cargo, not speed, but it drew too much water to bother dodging, so it ran straight lines while we hunted the faster seams along the banks. Slowly—grudgingly, the way the river gave up anything—we began to close.

"There," Sable said. "Past the church spire. He's swinging wide for the cut."

The old canal cut entered the river below the drowned church, a straight shot of half a mile that would save him the long bend. It was also, according to the survey maps I'd memorized until they lived behind my eyelids, choked with debris since the last calving season. But the flood had changed that calculus too. Floodwater lifts things. A channel that was choked in low water might be scoured clean at high—and might have new snags laid across it by the same hand.

"He'll ground on the cut bar if the scour didn't take it," I said.

"And if it did take it, he's a half-mile ahead of us forever." She leaned on the tiller, reading the water ahead the way I imagined her grandfather had read his survey sheets—looking for the truth underneath the surface. "Vesper. We're not catching him on the flat. We catch him at the Narrows or never."

The Narrows. Two miles downstream, where the valley pinched between slate bluffs and the river dropped twelve feet in a quarter-mile through a slot no wider than a wagon road. In summer it was a chute of green water that local boatmen portaged around rather than argue with. In flood, the maps—the consortium's own surveys, the ones I'd signed my name near once upon a time—marked it with a single word in red ink: *impassable.*

Red ink had never stopped Halloran. It wasn't going to stop us.

* * *

We caught sight of them properly for the first time as we came abeam of the drowned orchard—four figures on the barge's deck, one at the sweep rudder, one forward with a boathook, and Halloran himself standing amidships beside the tarpaulined chest with his hands folded, watching us come. Even at two hundred yards I could feel him looking. He raised one hand—not a wave, exactly. An acknowledgment. *I see you. I know what this costs you. Come and pay it.*

"He's enjoying this," Sable said, and there was something in her voice I hadn't heard before—not fear, colder than fear. Recognition. She'd grown up among people who had lost a country to men who enjoyed things.

"Hold your anger," I said. "You'll need both hands."

"I'm holding plenty."

The barge swung into the canal cut and vanished behind the levee willows. When we reached the mouth thirty seconds later, the channel was empty water and moving shadow, and then—there—he was, emerging from the far end, still afloat, still ahead. The scour had taken the bar. Luck was on his side, or he'd bribed the river the way he bribed everything else.

But the cut had cost him time in ways he hadn't counted. As he came out into the main channel again, the current seized his unmanageable length and slewed him sideways, and for ten glorious seconds the barge lay broadside to the flood, wallowing, the sweep rudder cavitating uselessly, and we closed half the gap between us.

Sable didn't waste it. She put us into the seam along the north bank where the flood backed up against the bluff and ran backward in a long slick tongue, and we rode it like a hand pushing us from behind, and suddenly the barge was fifty yards off our bow instead of five hundred, close enough that I could see the rust streaks on his hull and the tarpaulin straining against its lashings.

"Halloran!" I shouted across the water. My voice sounded strange to me, ragged, younger than I was. "Ground her! Ground her and walk away!"

He turned his head and considered me with the mild patience of a man being pestered by weather. Then he said something to the man at the sweep, and the barge bore away toward the south bank—toward the Narrows entrance, taking the line no sane pilot would take, hugging the bluff where the current piled up fastest and deepest, because Halloran had understood something I was only now understanding: that in flood, the fastest water was also the deepest water, and depth was survival. The red-ink word on the map assumed a normal river. This was not a normal river. This was a river gone mad, and madness favored the committed.

"He's going to run it," Sable said. Not a question.

"We're going to run it."

She looked at me then, really looked, rain streaming off her jaw, and whatever she saw in my face must have satisfied her, because she nodded once and put the tiller over.

* * *

The Narrows announced itself before we saw it—a sound like continuous thunder, but wrong, too steady, the sound of a great volume of water forced through a space that hated it. Spray hung over the slot in a permanent gray veil. The bluffs on either side rose sheer and black, streaked with runoff, and between them the river dropped away into white.

I got the boat ready the only way I knew how: everything lashed, everything sealed, the pump handle tied within reach of Sable's knee, the spare oar rigged as a steering sweep aft. The leak we'd never fully beaten was seeping again, a cold wet insistence around my boots, and I wedged myself against it with one foot on the bilge boards.

"Line," Sable said. "Follow my line. Don't look at the rocks, look at the water."

"That's what I'd tell you."

"Then we understand each other."

The barge went in first. For a moment it hung at the lip of the drop, absurd and huge, a cathedral falling—and then the river took it and it simply ceased to be a thing that floated and became a thing that fell, stern-up, buried to its gunwales in foam, and the spray closed over it and I lost it entirely.

Sable put us in on its wake.

I will not pretend I remember those ninety seconds the way a narrator remembers them, in orderly sequence. I remember fragments. The bow lifting until the sky was all there was. The first rock—a fang of slate the size of a chapel—appearing off the starboard side close enough to touch, sliding past with a hiss like something exhaling. Sable's voice, calm as a schoolmistress, saying *left, left, LEFT*, and my hands hauling the sheet and the sweep together, and the boat pivoting on its own center in a way boats are not supposed to pivot. Cold water over the gunwale in a solid sheet. The pump handle knocking against my knee. And through all of it the roar, the total, annihilating roar, so that Sable's voice reached me less as sound than as vibration, instructions delivered through the hull itself.

Then the slot widened, the water smoothed from white to gray to a bruised brown, and we were through, wallowing, shipping water, alive.

Ahead of us, the barge was not.

* * *

It had grounded. Or half-grounded—there was a reef below the Narrows exit that the flood had turned from a hidden menace into a breaking seam of standing waves, and the barge lay across it with its back broken, canting hard to port, the flood tearing at it in long brown ropes of current. Its crew had gotten into the water already—I could see heads bobbing in the eddy behind the wreck, three of them, four, being carried down toward the slack water at the bluff's foot where a man could swim if the river allowed it.

And the chest—

The chest had broken loose. The tarpaulin was gone, the lashings snapped, and it sat on the barge's tilting deck, bronze caps flashing, held there only by friction and the angle of the wreck. Halloran stood beside it. He had not gone into the water with his men. He stood on a sinking ship next to the thing we had chased through a flood, and he looked at us coming down the channel toward him, and I watched him do the arithmetic.

I watched him choose.

He put his shoulder to the chest and heaved. It went over the side on the upstream edge of the wreck, into the seam of current that ran between the reef and the bluff—and the river took it the way the river takes everything, instantly, totally, rolling it under and punching it back up, spinning it downstream toward the slack water where his crew swam.

"No," Sable said. Just that. One syllable, and everything she was inside it.

"Sable—the eddy—"

"I see it."

She put us at it. The eddy behind the wreck was a slow revolving pool perhaps forty feet across, ringed by current, and the chest was circling in it like a planet, dipping and rising, one bronze cap winking above the surface. If we could get a line on it—if I could get into the water—

I was already stripping off my coat. The survey map sewn into its lining flashed through my mind—nine years of impermanence, of keeping nothing, and here I was about to jump into floodwater after a box. Some things you don't decide. They decide you.

"Get me alongside," I said. "Close as you dare."

"Vesper, the water—"

"Close as you dare."

She brought us in on the eddy's rim, and the boat's gunwale kissed the current-line, and I went over the side.

The cold hit me like a blow to the sternum. The flood wasn't water anymore; it was a living weight with opinions, and it wanted me dead with the impersonal enthusiasm of all large forces. I got a hand on the chest—one bronze cap, slick as ice—and the current tried to peel me off it immediately, dragging my legs out sideways, my body streaming downstream from my grip like a flag. The chest rolled. I rolled with it. Somewhere above me Sable was shouting words the roar ate whole.

I got my other hand on the second cap. I got my forearm through the rope loop that Halloran's crew had left knotted at the end—for carrying, probably, on some dry day in some dry country that no longer existed. The loop held. The chest held me. Together we circled the eddy, and I breathed when the river let me, and I did not think about the Vell Bridge, about eleven names, about what the river does with things it takes. I thought about a dead surveyor's handwriting. I thought about children drilled in a geography that existed nowhere. I thought: *not this. Not this too.*

"Line!" I screamed, and heard my own voice crack apart in the roar.

A rope splashed across my shoulder. I wrapped it twice around my wrist, and the boat surged, and Sable—God, Sable—put her whole weight against the tiller and dragged us all, boat and chest and fool, out of the eddy and into the slack water under the bluff, where the current forgot about us and let us live.

* * *

We got the chest aboard by main strength, heaving it over the gunwale while the boat sank lower and lower around us, the leak winning at last, the bilge water slopping over the floorboards at every movement. The chest sat in three inches of river, and I sat beside it, coughing, shaking, my hands sliced open on the bronze edges and not caring.

Sable stared at me across the chest. Water ran off us both. Behind us, the barge settled by degrees onto the reef, and Halloran's crew scrambled up the bluff path like ants, and Halloran—

Halloran was gone. I scanned the bank, the wreck, the water, and found him standing on the shingle a quarter-mile downstream, soaked to the skin, having swum the eddy-line while we fought for his prize. He was looking at us. Even at that distance I could read the set of his shoulders, and it was not the set of a defeated man.

"Vesper," Sable said quietly. "Look at the boat."

I looked. I had been refusing to look. Now I looked, and the truth was plain: we were sinking. Not leaking—sinking, the seams opened somewhere in the Narrows, the hull flexed beyond forgiving, water coming in faster than grief. The chest, waterlogged, weighed as much as a coffin. The pump was a joke. The shore was fifty feet away and the boat had perhaps three minutes of floating left in it, and between us and any landing place lay the full indifferent muscle of the flooded river.

"We can't take it and ourselves," Sable said. Her voice was very level. That was how I knew what it was costing her. "Not both. Not through that current."

"We can try—"

"If we try, we lose everything. Us, the boat, the chest." She put her hand flat on the bronze lid, on the two-headed hawk stamped there, worn nearly smooth by a century of hands. Her grandfather's hands, maybe, among them. "Or we save ourselves and let the river have it."

The river had the chest's weight in its teeth already; I could feel the boat slewing, wanting to swing downstream, wanting to give up its burden. Fifty feet of current. A chest full of treaties and census rolls and a nation's proof, going soft in the water even now, paper drinking the flood.

Halloran was walking upstream along the shingle. Toward the pass road. Toward the consortium team waiting somewhere above the Narrows with pack animals and gear, because of course he'd planned for losing the barge; he planned for everything. He didn't need the chest anymore. He needed only to reach the pass before we did, and seal the valley, and burn whatever the exiles hoped to find. The documents were never the weapon. The route was the weapon. And the route lived in one place only now—in the head of a drowning man clinging to a sinking boat.

"Let it go," Sable said. "Please. Let it go."

I put my arms around the chest one more time. The bronze was cold as the glacier water that waited higher up. I thought of the old men in the exile quarter, of the boy who had recited Kestrian rivers to me like a psalm, of Sable's grandfather's photograph, stiff-collared, holding a surveyor's transit like a scepter.

I let go.

The river took it gently, almost courteously, the way it takes everything eventually. It rolled once, showed its two-headed hawk to the gray sky, and slid under, and the current carried it away downstream into the brown immensity, and the surface closed over it the way a page turns.

We swam for the bank, towing nothing. The boat died behind us with a sigh, folding into the flood that had made it and unmade it. I stood on the shingle dripping, hands bleeding, watching the water where the chest had gone, and felt the mission die with it—felt eleven chapters of hope go under with that bronze flash, felt the old familiar tide rise in me, the one that said *walk away, vanish, become nobody again, it's what you're good at.*

Sable stood beside me, staring at the same water. She was crying, I think, though the rain made it impossible to swear to, and she would have denied it.

Then she turned and looked at me, and her eyes went wide, and she said:

"You read it. You read every page."

"What?"

"That night in the exile quarter—you had it open for six hours. You wouldn't let anyone else touch it. You turned every page yourself." Her voice was climbing, gathering speed, a flood of its own. "Vesper. What do you remember?"

The river roared past us, carrying a country away. And somewhere beneath my ribs, under nine years of ash, something answered her—something that had been waiting, patient as a seed, for exactly this question.

"Not everything," I said. "But more than Halloran could burn in a lifetime."
