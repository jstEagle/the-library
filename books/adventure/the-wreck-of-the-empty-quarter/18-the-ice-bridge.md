## 18. The Ice Bridge

The glacier spoke all night.

Not a sound you could name at first—Vesper lay in the tent listening to it the way you'd listen to a house settling, except no house ever settled like this. It was a groan under everything, deep and slow, the sound of a mountain's worth of ice deciding whether to move. Beneath it came smaller voices: cracks like rifle shots, the tick and hiss of meltwater finding its way down through blue corridors older than any nation on any map.

He'd slept badly for nine years. He was used to the dark hours being full of bridges that fell down. Tonight they were full of a bridge that might hold.

*The pass opens,* Sable had said three days ago, standing at the mouth of the valley with her face turned up toward the white wall of the Col de Verre, listening to it calve with an expression he could only call homesick. *Listen. That's Kestria clearing its throat.*

Now they were on it, twelve exiles and one disgraced engineer, roped in pairs across the icefield below the pass, and the glacier was talking to all of them.

Vesper crouched at the edge of the crevasse field and did what he had done his whole life: he read the ground.

The surveyor's notation had called this stretch *the Loom*—a grid of hidden crevasses bridged by snow, some of them wide as streets, some thin as a hand's width, none of them marked on the surface. The old man's cairns were still here, seventy years on: stacked stones with a sliver of slate set into the top, each slate scratched with a symbol from the dead notation system. Two lines crossed by a dot meant *sound footing.* A single line broken meant *hollow beneath—test before weight.* Three dots descending meant *the bridge narrows; cross singly.*

Twelve exiles behind him, and every one of them watching the back of his coat the way passengers watch a driver on a mountain road. He could feel their trust like a weight sewn into the lining, where a survey map used to be.

"Two lines crossed," he said, tapping the slate in the nearest cairn. "We go straight out, forty paces, then the notation breaks. From there we test every step. Probes out. Nobody moves until the person ahead has full weight on solid ice. Understood?"

Old Tomas, who was sixty-eight and had walked out of Kestria as a boy, nodded solemnly. The youngest of them, a girl named Wren who'd been born in Lowmark and had never seen her grandparents' country except in charcoal drawings, grinned like this was a festival.

"Understood," said Sable, beside him, and her voice carried something else underneath it—the same thing he heard in his own chest. They were two days ahead of Halloran's consortium team. Two days, and the pass would stay open maybe ten more. But two days was not safety. Two days was a lead that could be spent as fast as it was earned.

He drove his first probe into the snow bridge and felt it bite stone-solid ice beneath.

They went out onto the Loom.

* * *

For an hour it was almost beautiful.

That was the thing about ice country that no one told you, Vesper thought—that it was gorgeous the way a loaded gun is beautiful. The crevasse field caught the morning sun and threw it back in colors that had no business existing above the tree line: cobalt in the depths, green fire along the fracture lines, the snow bridges glowing faintly gold as if lit from underneath by some buried sun. Between the hidden gaps the surface was firm and squeaking underfoot, and the line of them moved across it in careful silence, twelve exiles going home over the backs of frozen rivers.

Sable walked point-probe beside him. She had learned the notation system in four days flat, drilling herself at night by lamplight the way her grandfather must once have drilled apprentices, and now she read the cairns half a beat before he did.

"Broken line," she said, dropping to one knee at a seam in the snow that looked to Vesper exactly like every other seam. She probed. The probe went through to its ring and kept going. Below them, somewhere down in the blue, meltwater chuckled.

"Hollow." She stood, sidestepped three paces east, probed again. Solid. She looked back at him. "Your grandfather-in-law was good."

"He wasn't my—" Vesper started, and stopped, because arguing the point felt like denying something true. "He was good," he said instead. "Seventy years and his marks still hold. That's the whole profession, right there. Work that outlives your name."

"You'll have that too," Sable said quietly. "What you're building down from the pass. It'll outlive everything they wrote about Vell Bridge."

He didn't answer. There was nothing to say to that yet—not with eleven names still folded in his breast pocket, written out in his own hand, carried up a glacier because he couldn't bear to leave them at sea level. Some debts you don't get to declare paid. You just carry them to higher altitudes.

Behind them, far down the white slope they'd climbed since dawn, Vesper stopped and turned.

He'd been hearing it for twenty minutes without letting himself hear it: a sound too regular for the glacier, too rhythmic for wind. Boots. Many boots, moving fast, unafraid of the ice because they hadn't learned to fear it yet.

At the base of the climb, small as ink flecks, the consortium team had reached the foot of the Loom.

"Down," Vesper said. "Everyone down and quiet."

They knelt among the cairns, twelve exiles and one engineer, and watched the men below come on. Halloran led them—he was identifiable even at distance by the set of his shoulders, that particular forward lean of a man who has spent his life walking into rooms where he isn't wanted. Eight men behind him, roped carelessly, probes carried like walking sticks rather than instruments. Consortium gear, consortium pace, and worst of all, consortium confidence: they were following the old roadbed grade visible under the snow, the ghost of a Kestrian survey track, and they were moving fast because they trusted maps drawn by men who'd never stood on this ice.

"They're making better time than us," Sable murmured. "They'll close the gap by the col."

"Only if they live that long." Vesper watched the eight-man rope swing wide around a cairn none of them bothered to read. "They're cutting inside the marked line. That puts them over the deep gallery—the notation calls it the Cathedral, if I'm reading the old man right. Snow bridges over open depth. You can cross it. Once. If the cold holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then the Loom collects its toll."

Below, Halloran's rope reached the entrance to the deep gallery and went in without pausing.

* * *

It happened the way disasters always happen, which is to say slowly and then instantly.

Vesper saw it first as a change in light—a flicker in the gold glow of the snow bridges, as though something vast had shifted its shoulder beneath the surface. Then the glacier spoke again, louder than it had all night, a groan that rose through the soles of his boots and up his spine, and the middle of the consortium's rope simply ceased to exist.

No explosion. No drama. One moment there were eight men crossing a white street; the next there was a hole, thirty feet across, breathing cold air from the bottom of the world, and the rope was sliding into it link by link with men still tied to it.

The screams came a half-second later, thin and human across all that grandeur.

"They're alive," Sable breathed. "Some of them—the rope's caught on the lip—"

She was right. Vesper could see it through the probe glass: three men dangling on the taut line over the void, the rope jammed against a shelf of ice at the crevasse rim, the other five scattered on firm ground beyond the collapse, hauling, shouting, doing everything wrong. And Halloran—Halloran was on the near side, thrown clear at the first lurch, flat on his belly at the crumbling edge with his arms out over the void, holding the wrist of a man whose legs swung over nothing.

Vesper was already moving.

"Vesper—" Sable's hand closed on his pack strap.

"Their rope's jammed but the lip is failing." He was uncoiling his own line as he talked, hands working on knots his fingers knew better than his mind did. "When that edge goes, all three go down. And when they go down, the rest of that team panics, and panicked men on the Loom die by the dozen—and they're between us and the col, Sable. We can't go around them. We can't wait them out. The pass doesn't wait."

"So we let the glacier do the consortium's accounting—"

"I signed papers once that killed eleven people because it was cheaper than the alternative." He met her eyes, and found his voice had gone very level, the way it went in the last seconds before a span took load. "I'm not doing the arithmetic twice. Anchor me."

For one heartbeat she held his gaze, and he watched her weigh it—all of it, the raid on the exile quarter, the lost chest, nine years of a man running—and then she turned and drove the picket into the ice with three blows, fast and hard, and clipped his line.

"Twelve exiles are watching," she said. "Whatever you're about to teach them, teach it well."

Vesper went down the slope on his belly, the way Halloran had, edging out onto the fractured apron at the crevasse lip. Up close the damage was worse than it had looked. The snow bridge under the stranded men was sagging in slow motion, a white hammock rotting through, and the jammed rope had sawed a groove into the ice shelf that deepened visibly as the load swung. Below, the dangling men had gone silent in the way of people conserving breath for screaming later. Halloran's arms shook. His face, when it turned up, was gray.

"Pemberton." Not *engineer*. Not *you.* Just the name, dragged out of him like a tooth. "The lip's cutting. I can't—I can't hold him and myself both."

"Then don't hold yourself." Vesper got his boots wedged against a rib of blue ice, dropped flat, and slid his own rope out across the gap. "Let go of the edge. Take his wrist with both hands and put your whole weight backward. My line takes you both."

"You're insane. This ice is—"

"This ice held a Kestrian survey team with iron tools and hemp rope in 18-something-or-other, and it'll hold a steel core line and one guilty engineer. *Move.*"

And Halloran—Halloran, who had offered triple wages for a stolen chest, who had burned a quarter of a river town looking for paper, who had spent his whole career being the man who arrived after the decision was made—let go of the ice and put his life entirely into the hands of the man he'd been hunting.

The lip groaned. The groove in the shelf deepened. Vesper felt the load come onto his line—two men, gear, gravity, all of it transmitted down his arms and into the anchor behind him where Sable and old Tomas and three others leaned their whole bodies against the picket—and for one endless second the world consisted of tension, of the difference between a rope that holds and a rope that parts, which was a difference he had thought about every night for nine years.

The rope held.

They came up out of the hole one at a time—first the young consortium man Halloran had been holding, hauled over the lip coughing and weeping; then Halloran himself, rolling onto solid ice and lying there breathing like a landed fish. Beyond the gap, the five survivors of the team stood in a stunned row, their rope cut loose, their expedition gutted, staring at the man their employer had sent to destroy them being pulled out of the dark by the man they'd been sent to destroy.

Halloran sat up. Snow fell off his shoulders. For a long moment he didn't look at Vesper at all. He looked at the hole, at the blue depth of it, at the place where he had very nearly become a line item in someone else's ledger.

Then he started to laugh. It was a terrible sound, ragged and involuntary, the laugh of a man whose body had decided for him.

"You came out," he said. "You absolute fool. You came *out* onto the lip for us. We burned the exile quarter. We took the chest. I'd have taken the map out of your skull with a knife if they'd asked me to."

"They asked," Vesper said. "You're here instead."

Halloran's laugh died. He looked past Vesper, at the line of exiles kneeling at the cairns, at old Tomas and little Wren, at Sable standing over the anchor with the picket hammer still in her fist. Something moved across his face that Vesper recognized, because he'd worn it himself for nine years: the look of a man seeing the cost of his work laid out in faces instead of figures.

"There's something you should know," Halloran said. "Since you've gone and made it so I owe you a life." He wiped his mouth with the back of his glove. "The archive. Your valley of paper. You think you're going to find it by wandering the floor of that valley reading cairns. You won't. It's not in the valley floor."

Vesper went very still. "The survey says the cache chamber is below the old terminus grade—"

"The survey is right and you're reading it wrong, because the terminus *moved.*" Halloran pushed himself up on his elbows, and now his voice had the flat urgency of a man spending something he couldn't take back. "The annexation commission regraded the valley in the third year. Buried the original station under fill. Your archive isn't under the ruins—it's under the *embankment*, forty meters north of where your map points, behind a retaining wall with a keystone cut like a two-headed hawk. I know because I've read the commission files. I'm the only man alive outside this rope who knows where they are." He laughed again, shorter, harsher. "I was supposed to find them and burn them. That was the actual contract. The chest was insurance. The burning was the job."

The wind moved along the icefield. Somewhere below, the collapsed bridge finished its settling with a long sigh, and the Loom closed another street forever.

"Why tell me?" Vesper asked. "You could trade that at the col. Buy your way back into their good graces. Buy a pardon."

"Because they left me on the wrong side of a hole today, Pemberton." Halloran got to his feet, slowly, testing his legs like a man learning to walk again. "Eleven years I've done their arithmetic. Today I finally heard it spoken out loud, and it turns out the number they got to was *me.*" He looked at the crevasse a last time, then at Vesper, and stuck out a gloved hand—an absurd gesture, halfway up a glacier, bloodied and frost-rimed. "I'm not asking to be forgiven. I'm asking to be useful. There's a difference, and I know exactly how wide it is."

Vesper looked at the hand. Behind him, Sable had come down the slope and stood with her arms folded, watching, saying nothing, leaving the choice where it belonged.

He took it.

"Forty meters north," Vesper said. "Behind a hawk-cut keystone."

"Forty meters north," Halloran agreed. "And Pemberton—" his grip tightened, just once, "—when we get down there, and you see what's in those crates, you're going to want to sit down. There's a census roll in the archive with my family's name in it. Expelled. I've carried proof of it in my pocket for a month." His jaw worked. "Turns out I was exile-born too. I just picked the wrong side of the border to be born on."

Above them, far up the white wall of the Col de Verre, the sun struck the pass and the glacier boomed like a struck bell—one of the great calving reports, the sound Sable had called Kestria clearing its throat. The opening was narrowing. Every hour counted now.

Vesper turned to the twelve exiles waiting at the cairns, and to the five shaken consortium men who had begun, uncertainly, to gather up their gear—to follow, it seemed, wherever the rope that had saved their leader chose to lead.

"Twenty of us now," he said to Sable. "One pass. Maybe six hours of light."

Sable looked up the slope, at the blue-white wall with its thread of a route glinting along it, and smiled for the first time since the ice had opened.

"Then we'd better move like Kestrians," she said, and led them up toward the col.
