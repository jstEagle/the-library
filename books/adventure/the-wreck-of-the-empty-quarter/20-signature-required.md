## 20. Signature Required

The pass had been open eleven days when Vesper Ravensworth began to build a railroad on top of a glacier's grave.

Not the glacier itself—the old surveyor had been clear about that, and the dead man's notation system, which Vesper now read as fluently as his own handwriting, had underlined the warning three times in fading violet ink: *No iron on the ice. The ice keeps its own schedule.* The spur would run from the saddle of the pass down the eastern moraine, four and a half kilometers of temporary track to the valley mouth where the archive crates waited in their century of dark. Temporary was the word that mattered. The exiles said *first*, but Vesper heard *temporary* every time he looked at the grade stakes, because temporary was the only kind of line he could bear to sign.

He had told Sable this once, at dawn, standing over the profile drawings pegged flat against the wind with stones.

"A permanent line is a promise," he'd said. "A temporary one is just an apology you can take back."

She'd looked at him for a long moment, her breath smoking in the cold. "Then build the best apology anyone ever saw," she said, "and let them decide later what it turned into."

That was Sable all over. She counted rivers; she counted everything—crates, days, calories, the number of times Halloran's rescued team had eaten at their fire without being asked twice (nine, as of last night). But she never counted what a thing cost before deciding what it was worth. It was the arithmetic of someone who had already lost a whole country and found that the ledger still balanced. Vesper, who had lost only eleven people and never stopped auditing the debt, could not make his mind work that way. He could only make his hands work. So he worked.

The crew was thirty-one strong: twelve exiles, nine of Halloran's former consortium men who had chosen the losing side of the mountain over the winning side of the ledger, six local porters who had appeared out of the valley villages once word went round that someone was paying in food and gratitude both, and four mules who answered to no one. They called him *injinyér* in the exile tongue—a word the old men pronounced with a weight that made Vesper's neck prickle, because in Kestrian it apparently meant not just builder but *one who answers for*. One who answers for. He had spent nine years making sure no one would ever again hold him to that definition, and here were twelve people handing it back to him like a gift they didn't know was a knife.

The rail itself had come up with them: forty lengths of light strap-iron salvaged from the consortium's own abandoned survey camp below the pass, plus the timber Halloran's men had stockpiled for a cableway they'd never built. The irony was not lost on anyone. The consortium had come to erase a country and had instead supplied the materials of its resurrection. Tamás, the youngest of the exiles, had painted a two-headed hawk on the side of the first mule's pannier in white lead, and the mule had bitten him for it, which everyone agreed was auspicious.

* * *

On the twelfth day, the grade was done.

Vesper walked it end to end alone, as he always did, though there was no regulation left anywhere in the world that required him to. Four and a half kilometers of ballast and tie and rail, dropping through switchbacks cut into the moraine, each curve sighted by eye and checked by level and then checked again by the memory of a dead surveyor who had walked this same ground seventy years ago and written, in violet ink, *the mountain forgives a gentle curve; it does not forgive pride.* Vesper had taken the man's advice. The worst gradient was one in twenty-five. The tightest curve was eighty meters radius. Every culvert was oversized. Every embankment was wider than it needed to be. He had built the line the way a penitent builds a church—extravagantly, obsessively, with more than the structure required, because the excess was the point.

At the bottom of the last switchback, where the spur would meet the valley floor and the sledges waited to haul the first crates up, the whole company had gathered. Someone had set out the good tea, the brick-red stuff the porters drank, in tin cups for everyone. The morning was hard and blue. Above them the glacier ticked and groaned like a house cooling at night, calving season at its height, the pass open perhaps another ten days, maybe twelve. Nobody said so. Everybody knew.

Sable stood at the front with her grandfather's field case open on a crate lid. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the documents that remained: the letter she'd read aloud in the Valley of Paper until her voice gave out, the census rolls they'd carried up by hand, and—one sheet, kept apart—the original survey cover sheet, the one bearing the two-headed hawk and, beneath it in smaller print, the consortium watermark that Vesper had once taken for a curse and now understood as evidence. Proof that the railway men had known. Proof that they had sold the map of a living country to the power that erased it.

And beside it, on the crate lid, Vesper had laid out the thing he had been avoiding for two days: the engineering plan.

It was a single large sheet, drawn in pencil over three nights in the tent he shared with the theodolite, inked on the fourth. Plan and profile, grades, curves, culvert schedules, timber specifications, load ratings for the strap-iron, a drainage scheme keyed to meltwater pulses, and in the margin, in small careful letters, the words *TEMPORARY WORKS — SEASONAL SPUR — SUBJECT TO REVISION.* He had drafted it the way he drafted everything now: twice, then a third time from memory with the first copy covered, to prove he hadn't made an error he'd have to live inside of. It was, he knew with a professional certainty that felt almost like peace, a sound piece of engineering. A modest one. An honest one. Nothing about it was a lie.

Except the space at the bottom right, which was blank.

"Vesper." Sable's voice, quiet, carrying anyway in the thin air. "The people are waiting."

"I know what they're waiting for."

"Then tell me why you're looking at the paper like it's going to bite you."

He almost laughed. Almost. Instead he picked up the plan and held it, and the wind took the corner and fluttered it, and thirty-one people watched him stand there holding a drawing the way another man might hold a snake he wasn't sure was dead.

"You should know what this is," he said, and his voice came out lower than he intended, so he cleared his throat and started again, louder, the engineer's voice, the one that carried across cuttings and over the noise of ballast trains. "You should know what you're asking me to do, because I don't think most of you do, and you deserve to."

Silence. Even the mules seemed to stop shifting.

"There was a bridge," Vesper said. "Nine years ago. The Vell Bridge, over the Vell gorge, on the Corrunel mainline. I designed the span. I calculated the loads. And when the consortium's directors decided that the pier foundations were too expensive to build properly—when they ordered the footings shortened by four meters to save eleven thousand in currency—I signed the revised plans anyway. My stamp. My signature. My name."

He had never said it aloud in front of more than two people. Saying it to thirty-one, with a mountain listening, was different. The words came out of him like ballast tipping from a hopper—all at once, unstoppable, heavier than he'd remembered.

"The bridge stood for fourteen months," he went on. "Then a freight train came across at dusk with a locomotive and forty cars of ore, and the south pier settled, and the span came down into the gorge. Eleven people died. Six crew, five passengers. One of them was a girl going home from school. Nine years old." His hands were steady. He made them be steady. It was the only part of the work he had never lost. "There was an inquiry. The directors had lawyers whose entire profession was the manufacture of distance—they'd signed nothing, instructed nothing on paper, existed nowhere. But the plans needed a signature. Signatures are how responsibility becomes *findable.* So they fed me to the inquiry, and the inquiry ate me, and the directors paid a fine the size of a rounding error, and I lost my license and my name and I started walking, and I have been walking for nine years."

Old Ferenc, the eldest of the exiles, who spoke the dead language like a man keeping a fire alive one coal at a time, said something soft in Kestrian. Tamás translated, not quite whispering: "He says, *we know. We have known since the boat.*"

"I know you know," Vesper said. "That's not why I'm saying it. I'm saying it because of this." He lifted the plan. "This paper has a blank space at the bottom. On the Vell Bridge, that blank space got filled in, and filling it in was the worst thing I have ever done—not because the bridge fell, but because I knew, when I signed, that I was signing something wrong. I traded my name for my place. That's what a signature is. It's the moment you agree to be *found* by whatever happens next. And I swore I would never—" His voice caught. He hated it, and let it happen. "I swore I would never put my name on a line again. Any line. I drew maps for nine years and signed none of them. I inspected trestles and stamped nothing. I became very good at being nobody, and I told myself it was discipline, and it was actually just fear wearing discipline's coat."

Sable had come to stand beside him. She didn't touch him. She stood the way she stood at a river confluence, reading the water, letting him find his channel.

"So understand what you're asking," Vesper said to all of them. "You're asking me to become findable. If this spur fails—if a culvert washes, if a curve slips, if one of your people is hurt because I miscalculated a grade—there will be a paper with my name on it, and the name will mean something, and it will mean it forever. That's the contract. That's what a signature is. I need you to hear that I am not signing this because I've stopped being afraid." He looked down the line, at the switchbacks climbing the moraine, at the small white cairns of the dead surveyor visible above them on the shoulder of the pass, marking a route no living nation had ever sanctioned. "I'm signing it because I finally found something worth being found for."

* * *

He had brought the stamp up the mountain himself, and this was the part no one knew yet.

It was not his original stamp. That had gone into the inquiry's evidence box nine years ago, photographed, catalogued, entered as Exhibit Forty-One, and presumably melted or filed or buried in some consortium records room along with the conscience of everyone involved. What Vesper had was a replacement—cut for him in Lowmark during the monsoon, by a die-sinker named Oskar Vey who owed him a favor from a life neither of them mentioned. Brass, hand-engraved, two inches across. Vesper had carried it in his coat lining all the way up the pass, sewn into the same seam where the stolen survey map had once ridden, and he had told himself it was a tool, nothing more, the way a man tells himself a loaded thing is a tool right up until the moment he decides what it's for.

Now he took it out, and unwrapped the cloth, and the sun caught the brass.

Tamás made a sound. Old Ferenc crossed himself in the Kestrian fashion, thumb to brow, thumb to sternum, thumb to each shoulder—an X over the heart, sealing something in.

Because the stamp was not Vesper's design. He had dictated the lettering to Vey over three nights of monsoon rain, and Vey had engraved exactly what he was told, and what Vesper had told him was this: not *Ravensworth*, not *V. Ravensworth, Engineer*, not any of the formulas by which a man signs away his solitude. Around the rim, in letters Vey had cut deep enough to outlast the brass, ran a single line of Kestrian—the old men had checked it four times, sounding it out syllable by syllable—and it read:

*VESPER RAVENSWORTH — ONE WHO ANSWERS FOR.*

Inside the ring, where a monogram or a registration number would go, Vey had cut a two-headed hawk. Small, fierce, wings spread, facing both ways at once: toward the past and toward whatever came next.

"I couldn't sign it with the old stamp," Vesper said. His voice was rough now, and he had stopped fighting it. "The old stamp belonged to a man who signed things to keep his place. This one belongs to—" He looked at Sable. At Ferenc. At the twelve, at the nine turncoats, at the porters, at the mules with the hawk painted on its pannier. "This one belongs to whoever I am if I stay."

He laid the plan flat on the crate lid. He weighted the corners with stones, the way he'd weighted the profile drawings against the wind, and the gesture was so familiar, so purely his own craft, that someone in the back of the group laughed softly and then hushed themselves.

The ink was ordinary. He'd bought it in Lowmark, a bottle of cheap copying ink, and it had ridden up the glacier in his kit between a spare chisel and a bag of salt. He filled the pad, pressed the stamp, rolled it once against his palm the way he'd done ten thousand times in another life, and set it above the blank space at the bottom right of the sheet.

His hand did not shake. He noted this with a strange, distant surprise, the way one notes weather in a country one doesn't live in anymore. Nine years of nightly redrawing the fatal span in his head—every girder, every shorted footing, every load path of his guilt—and his hand had never once shaken, and he had taken that steadiness for a wound. Now, pressing brass to paper above a line he had built to carry a country's memory home instead of ore, he understood it had never been a wound at all. It had been the instrument, waiting.

The stamp came away wet and black and exact. The hawk. The words. *One who answers for.*

Then he took the pen and signed beneath it, in full, slowly, each letter a separate act: **Vesper James Ravensworth**, and after it, because the form of it mattered, because bureaucracy was the religion of railways and he had come back to the faith: *Engineer of Works. Seasonal Spur, Pass Route. Certified to the best of my knowledge and skill. This line is built to carry what must not be lost.*

He blew on the ink. The wind helped.

"It's done," he said.

For a moment nobody moved, and Vesper had time to think, absurdly, *they're going to check my culvert schedules,* and then Ferenc began to sing.

It was the song from the train, the one the exiles had sung when the first engine crossed the pass—or rather, the one they would sing, the one they had sung in kitchens and cellars and hidden quarters for seventy years, the song that was a timetable and a prayer and a map all at once. The others joined, the old voices cracked and the young ones sure, and the porters hummed the parts they didn't know, and even Halloran—who had come up with them a broken ally and was learning, visibly, day by day, what restitution weighed—stood with his hat off in the cold, mouthing syllables he didn't understand as if memorizing them for evidence in some better court.

Sable touched the ink with one fingertip, carefully, the way she'd touch a document in the archive. "It's real now," she said. "You know that. The moment it dried, it stopped being yours."

"That was the idea."

"No." She shook her head, and her eyes were bright and terrible and certain. "The idea was bigger. You signed your name to a piece of paper. But the paper isn't the line, Vesper. The line is the twelve hundred crates in that valley, and the census rolls, and my grandfather's letter, and every child in the quarter downstairs who can draw a country that isn't on any map. Your name is on all of it now. Everything we carry down this track today goes out into the world under *your* stamp. One who answers for." She smiled, and it was not entirely kind. "I hope you slept well."

"I haven't slept since the pass opened," Vesper said. "But it's the best insomnia I've ever had."

* * *

They loaded the first sledge at noon.

The strap-iron took the weight without complaint—forty lengths of salvaged rail doing the work of a proper line, singing faintly under the runners, a thin high note that Vesper catalogued automatically (*stress harmonic, acceptable, monitor at temperature swing*) and then, for the first time in nine years, simply enjoyed. The mules leaned into their traces. The grade held. The first culvert passed meltwater with a sound like applause.

Vesper rode the first sledge down himself, standing at the front beside the driver, because he had decided on the second night of drafting that the engineer rides the first movement over any line bearing his signature—it was the oldest rule he had, older than the license, older than the fall, and it was the one rule the consortium had never managed to buy out of him. Below, the valley opened in its long green mile, and the sledges of the archive waited in rows, twelve hundred crates of a nation's paperwork gleaming with frost, and the porters were already harnessing up.

Halfway down the last straight, the driver pointed ahead with his chin. "Injinyér. Look."

Two figures were coming up the track on foot, moving fast, stumbling on the fresh ballast. One wore the patched coat of a valley post-runner. The other—

Vesper knew the walk before he knew the face. He had watched that walk circle his boat in Lowmark, count his channels, warn him of a search party in the dark.

"Sable," he said, to no one, uselessly—because Sable was up at the pass with the council—and then the figures were close enough to see clearly, and it was not Sable at all.

It was a woman he had never seen, gray-haired, exile-born by the cut of her coat, and she was half-carrying the post-runner, who had a bandage around his ribs and blood on his collar and a leather dispatch tube gripped in his fist like something torn from a drowning man.

The sledge ground to a halt. Vesper jumped down. The woman thrust the dispatch tube at him with both hands, the way you surrender a weapon or deliver a verdict.

"From the quarter," she said, in accented trade-tongue, her breath ragged from the climb. "From Sable Idris, sent downriver the day you came up the ice. The consortium's lawyers filed in the colonial capital this week. They are claiming the archive as salvage under annexation law—and worse." Her voice dropped, and the old ferocity in it made the frost seem warm. "They have named the engineer of record on *their* counter-filing. They have dug up the Vell inquiry, Mr. Ravensworth. They are petitioning to have your signature declared void—your person declared incompetent to certify works—on the grounds of the bridge."

The valley was very quiet. Somewhere above, the glacier ticked.

"They want to erase your name," the woman said, "the way they erased ours."

Vesper looked down at his hands, still smelling faintly of cheap copying ink, still steady. Then he looked up the line he had built, at the smoke of the council fire at the pass, and he began, very calmly, to laugh.

"Good," he said. "Let them bring the bridge. I've redrawn it every night for nine years." He took the dispatch tube. "I know exactly what they signed—and now, thanks to this line, so does everyone else. Get me paper. Get me the inquiry transcripts. We ride down tonight."

The first train to Kestria would roll in three days. Apparently, the fight for its tracks had only just begun.
