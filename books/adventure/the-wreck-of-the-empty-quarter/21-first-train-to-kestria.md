## 21. First Train to Kestria

### Vesper

The locomotive had no name yet, so the exiles gave it one in their dead language, and when they said it aloud it sounded like water going over stones. Vesper did not ask for a translation. Some words were better carried than opened.

He stood on the footplate at dawn with his hand on the regulator and watched the glacier pass crack its seasonal mouth. The ice bridge they'd crossed on foot in calving season was gone now—melted back into the mountain's keeping—but the spur line he'd built ran along the rock shoulder above where it had been, forty-one days of labor by men who had never held a spike and women who had never packed a ballast bed, all of them learning the work the way you learn a language you thought you'd forgotten.

"Pressure's good," Sable said beside him, though she wasn't crew and had no business reading the gauge. She read everything anyway. That was her whole character, as far as Vesper could tell, and he had come to love it the way you love weather.

"Pressure's good," he agreed.

Below them, the valley of paper waited with its crates pried open and repacked, census rolls and treaties and eleven thousand names copied out in a surveyor's careful hand. Twelve exiles had gone down as loaders and come up as something else. Vesper had watched it happen over six weeks and still couldn't name the change except that they stood straighter, the way track stands straighter once it's been tamped.

He thought about signatures. He thought about the last time his name had gone onto an engineering document, and what it had cost, and how the eleven dead of Vell Bridge had followed him across nine years and four assumed identities like dogs that wouldn't be called off. Then he looked at the firebox door, at the heat shimmering behind the viewing glass, and understood that this time the name would be a door opening instead of a floor giving way.

Sable touched his elbow. "They're singing."

They were. Down on the flatcar where the archive rode under tarped crates, the old men had begun—one low voice, then another joining, then the children who had been drilled in a geography that existed nowhere until three months ago. The language rose up through the cab floor like vibration through rail steel. Vesper couldn't understand a word of it and understood all of it.

"Not yet," he said, mostly to himself. "We sing when we're moving."

He opened the regulator, and the first train to Kestria breathed, and moved.

* * *

### Sable

Her grandfather had walked this route in 1911 with a theodolite and a mule and a notation system he invented because the standard systems kept getting confiscated at borders. Sable had spent her childhood tracing his field books by lamplight in a rented room in a city that had never heard of Kestria, learning the shapes of valleys she would not see for thirty years.

Now she saw them from the window of a moving train.

She had expected weeping. What came instead was a kind of furious attention—she pressed her palm flat against the glass and matched every landmark to the notation, cairn to cairn, curve to curve, checking him. Checking the dead man's work against the living man's memory, and finding them agree, and only then allowing herself to feel anything at all.

"You're doing it again," Vesper said without turning from the controls.

"Doing what."

"Surveying from a passenger seat."

"Somebody has to check your work." But her voice cracked on *work*, and he heard it, because he heard everything; that was his whole character too, and she had come to love it the way you love a load-bearing wall.

The train took the long curve above the meltwater lake, and there below them, exactly where the field book said, lay the ruins of the Kestrian border station—roofless, moss-eaten, the platform edge still squared and true. Someone had laid flowers on it. Fresh ones. Someone had come ahead, on foot, weeks ago, and left flowers on a platform no train had served in fifty years, on faith alone.

Sable stopped checking. She let the numbers go and just looked, and the old men's song came up through the floor, and her grandfather's handwriting floated up through her memory, and for the first time since the flood brought the chest against Vesper's pilings she cried without deciding to.

Vesper said nothing. He put the train through the curve at exactly the speed the dead surveyor's gradient tables recommended, which was his way of saying everything.

* * *

### Halloran

He rode in the second carriage with his wrists unbound and his testimony memorized, which was, he reflected, the freer arrangement. In the consortium's service he had traveled first-class on four continents with documents that could disappear people, and he had never once felt safe. Here, among exiles who had every reason to hate him, guarded by nothing but their own decency and a boy with a shotgun who kept forgetting to look menacing, he felt—for the first time in thirty years—like a man traveling toward something rather than away.

The folder on his knees held everything. Ledger copies. The 1973 correspondence selling Kestrian territory surveys to the annexation authority. The memo instructing that "the Kestrian question be closed administratively," which was consortium language for *erase them*. And tucked in the inner pocket, where it had lived since the raid, the document he had saved from the fire: the expulsion order for the Halloran family, 1958, from a country whose name he had spent his life pretending meant nothing to him.

He had told himself he pocketed it as leverage. Leverage for whom, he hadn't asked.

Through the window, the mountains unspooled—the pass, the lake, the ruined station with its impossible flowers—and Halloran understood that he was watching a country come back from the dead, and that his testimony was the shovel digging it out, and that there was no version of himself, in any of the cities he'd fled through, who would have believed this morning possible.

The old man across from him—Davorin, ninety-one, hands like tree roots—had not spoken to him the entire journey. Now Davorin leaned forward and said something in the dead language, slowly, twice.

"I don't speak it," Halloran said.

"No," Davorin agreed. "But you will. You're staying."

"I'm turning state's evidence. There will be trials. I'll likely serve time myself—I signed things, old man. I knew what some of them were."

"Yes," Davorin said placidly. "And then you'll stay. We have a word for what you are. It doesn't translate well. It means roughly: *the one who burned the house and then carried water until the burn scar healed.*"

Halloran looked out the window so the old man wouldn't see his face do whatever it was doing. "That's a very specific word."

"We're a very specific people. It's why they tried to erase us." Davorin settled back. "It didn't take."

### Mirela, aged nine

Mirela had been born in Lowmark in a room above a fish-drying shed, and the only Kestria she knew was the one inside the lessons—her grandmother's finger moving over a hand-drawn map, saying *this is the Vell river, this is the high pasture, this is where the archives sleep*, naming places the way you name the dead.

Now the places went past the window in the right order.

She checked them off in her head the way Grandmother had taught her, and when the train came around the shoulder of the mountain and the whole valley opened at once—the river, the terraces, the town with its tower still standing after fifty years of nobody—she forgot to breathe, and then she forgot to check anything off, because checking was for maps and this was not a map anymore.

This was just the world, being where they said it would be.

The singing got louder. Her grandmother was singing the loudest Mirela had ever heard her, and her voice sounded young, and Mirela grabbed her hand and sang the words she'd been drilled in without understanding, and somewhere past the halfway mark of the song she realized she understood them, or almost, or enough—that they weren't really words about geography at all, they were words about coming home, and she had been singing them her whole life without knowing they were instructions.

The tower grew larger. Somebody on the flatcar rang a bell that had been wrapped in oilcloth for fifty years.

Mirela pressed her nose to the glass and watched her country arrive.

* * *

### Vesper

They came down off the pass at noon and onto the old mainline—his line, rebuilt on the grades of the vanished survey, and the rails sang a note he knew from a hundred thousand miles of listening: true. Tamped right. Alive.

Kestria's capital—or what the maps had called its capital, back when maps admitted it existed—rose out of the valley haze with its tower and its terraced roofs and, on the platform, a crowd. Hundreds. Word had traveled down the spur on foot faster than any telegraph: *the train is coming, the train is actually coming.* They lined the platform edge the way people line up for executions or coronations, which is to say completely still, completely certain.

Vesper brought the throttle back and set the brakes the way you set brakes when eleven lives taught you what sloppy braking costs, and the great locomotive sighed down to a stop with its cowcatcher precisely at the painted line someone had refreshed that morning, in a script he couldn't read, on a platform that had waited half a century.

Silence. The kind that has weight.

Then Davorin stepped down first, ninety-one years old, and knelt, and put his palm flat on the platform stone, and said one word in the dead language—and the crowd said it back, hundreds of voices, and the word rolled up the valley and came off the mountains twice, echo answering echo, a country pronouncing itself.

Vesper stayed in the cab. This part wasn't his. He watched through the cab window as the archive crates came down hand to hand, as Sable lifted her grandfather's field book overhead like a lantern, as Halloran stood apart with his folder of confessions and was approached—not arrested, approached—by three elders who spoke to him quietly and then, incredibly, shook his hand.

Sable climbed up to the cab. Her eyes were wrecked and shining.

"They want you on the platform," she said.

"It's not my—"

"They know. They want you anyway. Come on, engineer." She used the title like a key. "Your country's asking."

*I don't have a country,* he almost said. But standing there with the firebank ticking and the rails still humming under his boots, he found the sentence had expired. Somewhere between the pass and the platform, between the chest lost in the flood and the map redrawn from the inside of his own skull, he had acquired one—not by blood, not by birth, but by the older method: by showing up, and staying, and signing.

He climbed down.

The crowd parted. An old woman with a ledger—of course a ledger, these were people who fought erasure with paperwork—opened it on a plank table someone had carried out, and beside it lay a document Vesper recognized with a jolt: the restoration charter, drafted in three languages, waiting at its final page for two things.

An official stamp.

A signature.

His hands did not shake. Nine years of shaking had apparently used themselves up on the ice. He inked the stamp, pressed it firm, and wrote his name—Vesper Ravensworth, full, legible, unhidden—in the space provided, and the crowd made a sound he had only ever heard before at the completion of difficult bridges: relief and awe braided together, the sound of people trusting a structure with their lives.

"There's one more thing," the old woman said, and turned the page.

* * *

### Sable

Later—after the singing, after the feast of preserved things and garden things, after Halloran was formally invited to stay and formally accepted, after the children fell asleep in piles on the station benches—Sable found Vesper at the end of the platform, looking down the mainline into the dark south, where the rails ran on toward the annexing power's territory and the consortium's lawyers were, even now, learning what Halloran's folder contained.

"The council met while you were being fed," she said. "You've been appointed."

"Appointed."

"Chief engineer. Restoration lines. All of them—the pass route, the southern trunk, the branch to the high pastures my grandmother used to describe until she cried." She handed him the paper. "It pays badly. The treasury is currently eleven crates of census records and a bell."

Vesper read it twice. She watched him do the thing he always did with documents—checking the load paths, the fine print, the places where floors give way—and then watched him stop doing it.

"There's a pardon offer coming," he said. "From the consortium. There always is. Full reinstatement, pension, my old name cleared in exchange for silence about the Kestrian surveys."

"Will you take it?"

He looked down the dark line a long moment. Then he folded the appointment carefully, the way you fold something you intend to keep, and put it inside his coat—inside the lining, where a stolen map had once lived sewn against his heart.

"I'm going to need a place to unpack," he said.

Sable smiled, and took his arm, and together they walked back toward the light and the singing, where a country that had refused to die was setting extra places at the table, and the first train home stood cooling on the rails, stamped, signed, and staying.
