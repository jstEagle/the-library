## 1. Miles, Not Years

The river did not care that it was Wren Okonkwo's birthday. It didn't care about anything, which was, he had come to understand, the whole point of it.

He stood on the deck of the *Magpie* at first light, watching the mist lift off the water in slow rags, and did what he always did: measured. The wreck lay forty feet down in the green murk of the anchorage, a navy corvette sealed by court order three years ago, and his job — the only job anyone would give him anymore — was to go down and bring up whatever the lawyers decided could be brought up. Instruments. Documents. A ship's bell, once, crusted white with corrosion, that had made a junior officer weep when it came over the rail.

Forty feet down. Two hundred yards from the mooring buoy to the seal line. He counted these things the way other men counted years.

His brother had taught him the habit, or maybe his brother had been the reason for it. Daniel had never once asked how old they were getting; he'd asked how far. How far to the next set of rapids. How far down the coast before the fuel got thin. How far a man could swim in cold water before his hands stopped answering him. And when Daniel went under — eleven years ago now, off a charter boat in a stretch of river Wren still could not say aloud without tasting iron — the last thing anyone knew for certain was that he'd been four miles from the confluence. Four miles. Wren had walked the bank for six days measuring it, pacing out the distance like a penance, as if grief were something you could survey and fence.

Since then he had stopped counting years altogether. Years were treacherous; they accumulated whether you lived them or not. Miles were honest. You earned every one, and when you stopped moving, you simply stopped.

"Okonkwo." The harbormaster's voice carried across the water, flat with official boredom. "You've got a visitor. Says he's government."

Wren looked at him a long moment. Nobody visited the *Magpie*. Salvage divers working a sealed wreck were the maritime equivalent of lepers — useful, paid, and not invited anywhere. He finished his coffee, which was mostly silt anyway, and said, "Send him down."

* * *

The man who came down the pontoon walkway wore a linen suit that had given up trying to stay clean around the cuffs, and shoes that had never touched mud in their lives until this morning. He was fifty-ish, silvering at the temples, with the practiced warmth of someone whose job was to be liked just long enough to be believed. He carried a leather folder and nothing else, which told Wren more than the folder's contents would.

"Mr. Okonkwo." He extended a hand. "Emil Voss. Ministry of Resources."

"There's no ministry pier here," Wren said, not taking it. "You walked past two checkpoints to reach a boat nobody's supposed to know is crewed."

Voss smiled as if Wren had passed a test. "I'm told you're discreet. I'm also told you're good. The navy seal doesn't extend to your contract — you're a civilian recovery operation, technically. Which makes you exactly what we need."

"For what?"

Voss opened the folder and laid a photograph on the deck box between them. It showed a survey camp seen from the air: five tents, a boat at a crude dock, the Upper Fly River winding away north into country so blank the mapmakers hadn't bothered contouring it. "A hydrographic survey team. Nine people. They went in eight weeks ago to chart the upper reaches ahead of the calving season. Their last scheduled transmission was fifty-one days ago. Nothing since."

"People go quiet up there," Wren said. "Weather kills radios."

"Weather doesn't kill nine people." Voss produced a second photograph, closer: equipment crates stenciled with ministry markings. "We need their gear recovered. Survey instruments, data drives, field records. Everything they took in, everything they generated. We're prepared to pay triple your standing rate, plus a completion bonus that will let you retire the *Magpie*'s engine debt twice over."

Wren looked at the money. Then he made himself look away from it, because looking at money first was how men like Voss won.

"Why me? You've got patrol boats. You've got helicopters."

"Helicopters can't land in the gorge this time of year. Patrol boats draw too much for the upper shallows, and—" Voss paused, delicately, the way a man pauses before stepping over a body, "—there are sensitivities. We'd prefer a private contractor. Someone with no institutional ties. Someone who understands that some questions are best recovered along with the equipment and left in the crate."

There it was. Wren had heard variations of that sentence his whole career, usually right before something went wrong that somebody wanted forgotten. His instincts, dulled by three years of lifting corroded brass out of green water, stirred like something waking at the bottom of a lake.

"And if I find the team?" he asked. "Nine people don't evaporate."

"If you find the team," Voss said evenly, "you'll be compensated for rescue expenses as well. But Mr. Okonkwo — eight weeks. In monsoon country. Let's both be honest about what you'll actually find."

Wren turned and looked at the river. The mist was burning off now, and the current ran brown and patient toward the delta, carrying everything downstream eventually — leaves, logs, boats, men. Somewhere up in that blank country, past the gorge, past the calving lake where the glacier shed its ice into the headwaters, nine people had stopped transmitting. And somewhere closer than that, in water he'd been diving for three years without letting himself think about why, his brother had stopped breathing.

Daniel had gone missing within thirty miles of that survey camp. He'd been running charter supplies upriver the same season the navy suddenly sealed a wreck that, Wren had always privately thought, sat in oddly shallow water for a corvette. He had never pursued the thought. Pursuing thoughts required wanting answers, and Wren had arranged his life very carefully around not wanting anything — not answers, not company, not years. Just miles. Just the next job, the next descent, the next forty feet of green silence where nothing could ask him anything.

Triple pay. Enough to clear the engine debt. Enough to stop.

Or enough to start.

"When do I leave?" he said.

Voss smiled again, and this time it reached all the way to his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "Tomorrow, if you can. There's a supply flight to the lower staging post. A man named Deng will meet you with fuel and charts." He gathered his folder, then paused, one hand flat on the deck box. "One condition. You recover the equipment intact. Whatever state it's in. No reading, no copying, no curiosity. The ministry pays for discretion, Mr. Okonkwo, and it remembers who provided it."

"I don't read other people's mail," Wren said. "I lift it out of the water."

"That's precisely why it's you."

Voss went back up the walkway, his ruined cuffs flapping, and Wren stood alone on the deck with the photograph of the camp. Five tents. A boat at a dock. Country so blank it made the eye slide off.

He worked through the day the way he always did — checking the bilge, servicing the winch, testing the dive regulator against his own palm until his lips went numb — but underneath the routine something had shifted, some small tectonic movement far below the mudline. He caught himself, twice, standing still with a wrench in his hand, staring north.

At dusk he went below and took the tin down from behind the spare parts shelf. Inside: a river stone, worn smooth, that Daniel had pocketed from the confluence the last summer of his life. Wren held it the way he always did, briefly, without prayer, because he'd never learned one.

"Four miles," he said to the tin, to the stone, to the dark water sliding past the hull. "Maybe I'll finally count the rest."

He slept badly, which was normal, and woke before dawn, which was also normal, and cast off the *Magpie* from the mooring while the mist was still thick enough to hide in. The engine turned over on the second try, coughed, caught. The current took the bow and swung it north, upriver, toward the gorge.

Two hundred and sixty miles to the survey camp, by his reckoning. He wrote the number in the logbook in his careful diver's hand, and beneath it, after a moment, added the only other thing worth recording:

*Conditions unknown.*

Then he opened the throttle, and began to earn them.
