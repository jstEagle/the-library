## 2. The Quiet Camp

The river told him where the camp was before he ever saw it.

Wren had learned to read water the way other men read faces, and for two days the Upper Fly had been speaking in a language he didn't like. It ran too clean below the second gorge — no silt plume, no diesel sheen, none of the gray scum a survey camp of nine people should have been bleeding into its own current. Either the team had packed out with military thoroughness, or they'd never been there at all.

He found them on the third morning, or rather he found what was left of where they had been.

The camp sat on a gravel bench above the confluence, tucked against a wall of mossy granite the way any competent surveyor would tuck it — out of the wind, above flood line, with a clear view up and down the channel. Wren killed his outboard a quarter mile downstream and let the current carry him in silent, one hand on the tiller, eyes working the bank. Nothing moved. No birds lifted from the tents. Even the sandflies seemed to have made other arrangements.

He grounded the bow on the shingle and stepped out with the wrench already in his hand. Not a weapon, exactly. A habit.

* * *

The first thing wrong was the silence of the place. Not empty silence — abandoned silence has a texture to it, a soft decay of canvas and cold ash. This was different. The camp looked like it had been paused mid-sentence.

Nine dome tents stood in their rows, guy lines taut, rainflies still rigged. He walked the perimeter twice before he touched anything, reading the ground the way his brother used to read tide tables — patiently, without expectation, letting the details arrange themselves. The fire pit held the black remains of a substantial blaze, banked under a scatter of ash that hadn't been disturbed by rain. Under a tin awning, a folding table still bore the morning's leavings: three enamel plates with rice gone hard as plaster, a kettle, a jar of instant coffee with the lid off. A spoon stood upright in a mug of tea that had dried to a brown ring.

Nobody leaves tea standing. Nobody leaves coffee open in fly country unless they mean to come back to it in ten minutes.

Wren crouched at the fire pit and touched the ash. Cold, but not old-cold. There had been no real rain here in maybe four days — the monsoon hadn't pushed this far up-valley yet — and the ash was dry all the way through. Five days, six. He looked at the plates again. Three plates. Nine tents. Where were the other six meals?

He stood very still and listened to the river, and the river said nothing helpful.

* * *

The radio was the thing that finally turned his blood cold, and it took him an hour to find it because it wasn't where a radio should be.

The comms tent stood at the camp's center, the way protocol demanded — last thing packed, first thing checked. Inside, the folding cot was neatly made. A paperback lay face-down on the pillow, spine cracked, holding its owner's place like a dog waiting at a door. On the field desk, the HF set sat exactly where it belonged.

Except it had been destroyed from the inside.

Wren had seen radios smashed before. Salvage work taught you what violence did to equipment — hulls stove in from outside, casings crushed by falling cargo, the honest damage of accident and force. This was something else. The casing was intact, unscratched, sitting square on the desk. But when he tipped it, the guts came loose in his palm: the circuit board snapped clean across, the transformer crushed flat, every tube and capacitor reduced to fragments that had been *poured* back inside like a man filling a bottle with sand. Someone had opened the case, methodically murdered the machinery, sealed it up again, and set it back in its place.

From inside. With the lid closed. So that anyone glancing into the tent would see a working station and move along.

That wasn't rage. Rage throws radios off cliffs. That was housekeeping.

Wren set the dead radio down and wiped his palms on his trousers, though there was nothing on them. He became aware of his own breathing, loud in the canvas quiet. Somewhere upstream, water fell over stones, patient as a clock.

People don't evaporate. His brother's voice, or his own — after enough years alone on the water the two had merged. People leave tracks, or bodies, or notes, or debts. Nine trained surveyors do not walk out of a functioning camp leaving their tea to dry.

Unless they left in a hurry. Unless they left *sideways*.

He went back to the mud.

* * *

The ground around the camp was the kind of fine gray silt that records everything. After the first day of the job, Wren had stopped noticing it; now he knelt over it like a priest over scripture. Around the tents, around the fire pit, down to the waterline where the boats had been beached — nothing. No boot prints newer than weather could explain. No drag marks. No scuff where a heavy case had been hauled over the shingle. The beach itself showed only the high-water line and the faint, week-old ghost of keels that had launched and never returned.

It was as if nine men had been lifted off the earth by the collar.

And yet. He circled wider, out past the latrine trench, past the water filter hung from its tripod, and there — half-hidden under an overhang of granite where the bench met the cliff — a smear in the silt. Not a footprint. A knee print, deep, both knees, pressed hard enough to have squeezed the moisture up dark around the edges. And beside it, a long furrow, as if something heavy had been dragged a few feet and then lifted.

Or pulled.

Wren stood over the mark for a long time. The monsoon would erase it within the week; the river would take everything else eventually, the way it took everything. But right now the mud was talking, and what it said was: someone had gone down on their knees here, recently, either praying or being made to, and then been taken away without walking.

He made himself stop looking at it.

* * *

He found the journal in the leader's tent, and he knew it was the leader's tent because of the boots — size twelve, resoled twice, the kind of economy that belongs to a man who spends his salary on maps instead of shoes. The cot was made. The footlocker was unlocked. And wedged under the mattress, the way men who work in dangerous country hide things — badly, close to where they sleep — was a yellow waterproof notebook, the kind with the elastic band and the grease-pencil pages that laugh at rain.

Wren sat on the cot with it and did not open it for a full minute. Some part of him understood that once he read it, the job changed shape. Triple pay bought gear recovery, not knowledge. Knowledge was the kind of cargo that didn't fit in a boat.

He opened it anyway. He was, he admitted to the empty tent, never going to be a man who didn't look.

The early pages were ordinary and therefore precious: barometric readings, flow rates, soundings, a running argument with the cook about rice portions. The handwriting was small and exact, an engineer's hand. Dates marched forward. The river rose in numbers. Then, eleven days ago, the entries shortened. The sentences got tighter, like a man writing with someone looking over his shoulder.

*Third pass on the east branch confirms anomaly. Flow data inconsistent with mapped basin. There is more water coming out of that tributary than goes in.*

*Took the dinghy up the east branch as far as the gorge. Found dredge tailings. Fresh. Kilometers of it. Grey spoil stacked on both banks like snow. No concession posted. No permit number on the markers. You do not run dredges that size without permits unless the permits are the kind you never write down.*

*Sampled the headwater pools. Don't want to say what's in them. Sending full assay to the ministry with tomorrow's scheduled transmission. If we're wrong, we'll laugh about it in Port Moresby. If we're right—*

The entry stopped mid-sentence. The next page was torn out. So was the one after that, and the next five, torn cleanly at the binding, the ragged stubs still visible like the roots of pulled teeth. What remained after the gap was a single final entry, dated four days ago, written fast, the letters leaning hard:

*They came at night. Two boats, no lights, no markings. R. says soldiers but I think worse — soldiers ask questions. They knew our names. They knew my daughter's school. Told us we surveyed a dead river and we would confirm same in Port Moresby and forget the east branch exists. Amos wouldn't sign. Amos took the drive and went up-country in the dark. God keep him. They are waiting for us to pack so it looks natural. I am writing this and hiding it because if I am wrong about being allowed to live, someone should find the east branch. Coordinates follow. Do not trust the fixer who sent you. Do not trust anyone who arrives by boat.*

Below it, in grease pencil, a hand-drawn map.

Wren turned the page slowly. The map filled the whole spread: the main stem of the Upper Fly rendered in confident strokes, and branching off it to the east, a tributary that appeared on no chart he'd ever salvaged, drawn in detail — depth soundings, gorge narrows, a falls marked with a caution symbol, and at the head of it, in letters small and deliberate, a notation that was not hydrographic at all:

*DREDGE WORKS — SEE ASSAY*

He sat with the journal open on his knees while the light through the canvas went from silver to grey. Outside, the river kept up its one long sentence, and somewhere far up the east branch, according to a dead man's pencil, machines were eating the headwaters of a river that officially did not exist.

Do not trust the fixer who sent you. Too late for that. The fixer had paid triple, in cash, and asked no questions about method, and Wren had taken the money because taking the money was easier than asking why a government that seals a wreck would also lose a survey team and send one diver to find them. One diver. Not a search party. Not a patrol. Him — a man with a boat and a grief, cheap and deniable, hired to fetch equipment and ask nothing.

Hired, perhaps, to be the last person to see the camp and the first to report it tidy.

He closed the journal and put it inside his jacket, against his chest, where the waterproofing was best. Then he did what he had come to do, because a man should finish the work in front of him even when the work turns out to be a door into somewhere else. He inventoried the camp: the sounding gear, the theodolite, the sample cases — all present, all serviceable, all of it worthless compared to eleven grams of paper now riding over his heart.

He was loading the last case into his boat when he saw the smoke.

Not from the camp. From downstream — a thin thread of it rising off the far bank, two kilometers below the confluence, where he had seen no camp and no boat and no reason for fire. Woodsmoke, deliberate, fed in small amounts. Somebody was keeping a low fire low, which meant somebody was hiding, which meant somebody alive.

Wren stood in the shallows with a sample case in his hands and did the arithmetic he didn't want to do. Whoever it was had watched him arrive. Had watched him spend the day among the tents of their friends, or colleagues, or crew. Had a rifle within reach, most likely, and a bullet already spent once on someone.

The journal's last line sat cold against his sternum. *Do not trust anyone who arrives by boat.*

He looked at his own boat, sitting honest and battered in the shallows, and felt the strange vertigo of being the thing another person had good reason to fear.

He pushed off anyway. Downstream. Toward the smoke.
