## 3. The Stranger on the Water

The river gave up its dead slowly, and it never gave them back whole.

Wren had learned that at nineteen, dragging the Upper Fly for three days after his brother's charter went down, hauling up silt and snagged line and once, horribly, a boot that wasn't even the right size. The river kept what it took. So when he rounded the bend below the survey camp and saw smoke rising thin and gray from behind a fallen strangler fig, his first thought was not *survivor*. His first thought was *bait*.

He killed the outboard and let the boat drift, hand on the rifle he'd bought in Port Moresby from a man who'd asked no questions because Wren hadn't offered any answers. The current carried him past the smoke, close enough to smell it — woodsmoke and something under it, chemical, a field dressing's sharp antiseptic bite. Someone was hurt. Someone was hurt and cooking anyway, which meant either very brave or very stupid, and both kinds ended up dead on this river.

He grounded the bow on a gravel bar and came ashore low, moving through the ferns the way his father had taught him before the navy took the rest of his education over. The camp, when he found it, was barely a camp: a tarp strung between roots, a fire pit the size of two cupped hands, a woman sitting with her back against the fig's buttress and a pistol loose in her lap like an afterthought.

She heard him before she saw him. He knew it because her head didn't turn but her whole body changed, weight shifting onto her left hip, the pistol's grip rotating into her palm without her fingers closing around it. Good instincts. Bad position. She'd chosen the tree for cover from the water and forgotten the land existed.

"Don't," she said, when his boot cracked a branch.

"Could've shot you ten seconds ago."

"Then you're either friendly or you want something." Her voice was hoarse, scraped raw. "Both mean come out where I can see you."

He stepped into the open with his hands visible and the rifle slung, which was itself a kind of lie, because a slung rifle is four seconds from a raised one. She was younger than he'd expected, mid-thirties maybe, dark-haired, mud to the knees, and there was a dressing on her upper arm that she'd tied one-handed and tied badly. Blood had wicked through it in a long brown smear.

"Wren Okonkwo." He said it flat, the way you read a name off a shipping manifest. "Salvage diver. I was hired to recover equipment from the government survey team."

Something moved behind her eyes — not fear exactly. Recalculation. "Hired by who?"

"A fixer named Deng. Works out of the ministry annex in Kiunga."

"And they sent one man? In a tinny with a twenty-horse?"

"They sent one man who works alone and doesn't ask what happened to the last crew." He crouched, slow, and sat back on his heels so his height stopped being a threat. "You're Reyes. Ilsa Reyes. Your name was on the manifest Deng gave me. Listed as equipment, along with everything else I was supposed to bring back."

That landed. He watched it land — watched her understand that on some office floor in the capital, she had been converted from a person into a line item, and that the man in front of her had been paid to collect the line item or its remains.

"My team," she said. "You were at the camp."

"This morning. Tents still standing. Meals half-eaten. Radio smashed from the inside." He paused. "No tracks. Not in that mud. People don't evaporate, Ms. Reyes, but your people did."

"Not my people anymore." She laughed, and it was an ugly sound, all edges. "I missed the final leg. Food poisoning, if you can believe it — forty-eight hours bent double in Kiunga while they went upriver without me. I was supposed to rejoin them at the confluence." Her thumb moved across the pistol grip, back and forth, a metronome. "I flew as far as the patrol would let me, then walked in. Found the camp like you found it. And then I found the men who did it, or rather—" she touched the dressing on her arm, almost casually, the way you'd touch a bruise you hadn't decided to own yet— "they found me. Two of them. Government plates on their boat. One of them apologized before he fired. Did you know that? He said *sorry, miss* like he was bumping my elbow on a bus."

Wren said nothing. Downriver, the light was beginning to fail, the gorge walls going purple, and the monsoon clouds stacking up over the divide like freight cars coupling.

"So here's my problem," she went on, conversational, terrifying. "A fixer hires a stranger to recover a vanished survey team's gear. But gear isn't why they send divers. They send divers to make sure nothing comes back up. Bodies. Documents. Evidence." The pistol was in her hand now, not pointed anywhere, just present. "So you'll forgive me if I need to hear it from you straight: are you the cleanup crew?"

"If I were, you'd already be in the river."

"That's what a cleanup crew would say."

"It's also what a man says who's standing in the open with his rifle slung because he's tired and wants to sit down." He did sit down then, cross-legged in the mud, deliberately, making himself small and slow. "My brother drowned on this river six years ago. Navy sealed a stretch of it afterward — wreck site, they said, hazard to navigation. I've spent every season since diving other people's losses, and I took this job because triple pay buys a lot of diesel and I don't ask questions. That's not clean, Ms. Reyes. But it's not what you think it is."

She studied him for a long time. The fire ticked. Somewhere upstream, a bird made a sound like a hinge swinging.

"You measure things," she said finally. "Divers do. Depth, bottom time, air. You count everything because counting keeps you alive."

"I measure miles," he said, before he could stop himself. "River miles. It's how I keep time."

Something in her face shifted — not softening, nothing so generous, but recognition, the look of one solitary animal sighting another across a clearing. She holstered the pistol, which was not trust, only arithmetic.

"There's a tributary," she said. "Unmapped. Officially. Amos — our team lead — found it on old aerial photos, a drainage nobody surveyed since the Australians flew it in '63. We ran it. There's a dredge operating up there, Wren. A full-scale dredging operation, no lease, no license, tailings going straight into the headwaters. Mercury in the water you could taste. And the men running it have ministry protection, which means the survey that found them has to stop existing." She looked at her arm. "Amos took the data drive and went to ground upstream. I took half the records and got shot for it. That's the whole story. That's what evaporated your survey team."

* * *

They argued about the boat, because of course they did. She wanted to go upstream after Amos; he pointed out that upstream meant past the camp, past whatever force had erased it, in a boat drawing too much water for the narrows. She called him a coward with a price list. He told her that dead cartographers mapped nothing. She said, "You sound like a man rehearsing his own eulogy," and he had no answer for that, because it was true — he had been rehearsing it for six years, one mile at a time, a life measured in distances instead of days.

They compromised the way drowning people compromise: badly, and together. Downstream first, to her cached supplies and the second boat the survey had left at a fishing village, then a decision. She slept in the bow of his tinny with the pistol under her hand and he sat the night watch listening to the rain start, fat drops at first, then the long steady roar of the monsoon arriving like a debt collector.

It was nearly dawn when he heard the engine.

Not the loose rattle of a village longboat — this was a governed, disciplined idle, an engine being handled by someone trained, holding station against the current. He put the binoculars on it and felt the cold move up his spine. Patrol hull, gunmetal gray, no markings, two figures on deck with the patient posture of men who are very good at waiting.

"Reyes." He said it quietly and she was awake instantly, which told him she'd been sleeping the way soldiers sleep, one ear open. "Company."

She looked, and he watched her do the math faster than he did. "They followed me downstream yesterday. Lost me in the dark. Now they're sweeping the banks." Her voice had gone flat and fast. "They'll check every fire scar, every landing. This stretch funnels into the gorge in about four kilometers. After that, no banks to sweep — just walls and white water."

"And if we stay here?"

"Then by noon we're a line item each." She was already moving, throwing her gear into the bow, tearing down the tarp, kicking dirt over the fire pit with a methodical fury. "Gorge runs maybe eleven kilometers. Class four at the top, worse in flood. This rain will have it at flood."

"The monsoon just started."

"Yes," she said, swinging into the boat and fixing him with a look that dared him to be sensible. "Isn't that lucky. Nobody sane will follow us into it."

He got the engine going. The patrol boat, hearing them, swung its bow downstream with the unhurried certainty of something that has done this many times.

The gorge took them the way a fist takes a fly. Walls rose on either side, black basalt streaked with waterfall threads gone violent in the rain, and the river narrowed and stood up and began to run. Wren drove with his whole body, reading the water the way he read wrecks — for structure, for the thing underneath that kills you. Ilsa bailed with a cut-down jerry can and shouted depths at him off the chart she'd memorized, and somewhere in the middle of the worst rapid, with the boat half airborne and the patrol boat's engine note falling away behind them, swallowed by the roar, she laughed — a real laugh this time, wild and unwilling.

They shot out the far end into a widening valley under a sky the color of wet slate, both of them soaked, shaking, alive. She looked at him across the bilgewater and said, "Amos is up there with the drive, and those men are between us and him, and the rains have closed every road out of this valley for a month. You understand what that means?"

He understood. It meant the river had stopped being a workplace and become a sentence. It meant the woman who'd held a gun on him twelve hours ago was now the only other living soul in a drowned world. It meant that for the first time since the boot came up out of the water, he was counting something other than miles.

"We find your Amos," he said, and throttled up toward the headwaters, where the rain was falling harder and something worse than rain was waiting.
