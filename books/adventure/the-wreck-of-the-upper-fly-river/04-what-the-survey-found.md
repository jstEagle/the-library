## 4. What the Survey Found

Ilsa Reyes had learned to read water the way other people read faces, and the water below the drowned church was telling her something she did not want to hear. It had gone the color of milk tea overnight — not the honest brown of a river carrying mountain, but a chemical, opaque white-grey that no flood alone could make. She stood at the rail of Wren's leaking boat with her arms crossed against the cold and watched the current slide past like something anesthetized.

"You see it," Wren said behind her. Not a question. He had been watching her watch it for most of an hour, patient in the way of men who worked alone underwater, where patience was not a virtue but a survival requirement.

"Tailings," she said. "Sediment from processing. That's not silt. Silt has weight to it, it settles in the bends. This just *hangs*." She turned. Her shoulder throbbed under the field dressing she'd taped over the graze, a dull hot pulse that kept time with her heartbeat, and she was tired enough that keeping the truth behind her teeth felt like more work than saying it. "You pulled me out of the reeds because you wanted answers. Fine. But you're not going to like them."

"I never like them," Wren said. "I just collect them."

* * *

They went below, into the boat's cramped cabin where rain drummed the deck overhead like fingers on a coffin lid, and Ilsa sat across the chart table from him and told it the way she had rehearsed telling it a hundred times in her head and never once aloud.

"The survey wasn't supposed to find anything," she began. "That's what nobody understands about government surveys. We're not explorers. We're auditors with boats. The ministry gave us a corridor — Upper Fly headwaters, forty kilometers, hydrology, sediment load, bank stability. Routine. The kind of assignment you draw when someone senior wants a line item for next year's budget." She laughed, and it came out wrong, too sharp. "Except Amos — our team leader, Amos Okere — he's the kind of man who reads the fine print on his own instructions. And the fine print didn't add up."

"How so?"

"The corridor boundaries. They were drawn to exclude a tributary." She pulled the hand-drawn map from inside her jacket — the one she'd taken from the camp, the one with the unmapped fork inked in Amos's careful cartographer's hand — and spread it flat between them, weighting the corners with two tin cups and a coil of rope. "Not avoided. *Excluded.* There's a difference. Avoided means inconvenient terrain. Excluded means somebody drew a box around a place and wrote *do not look here* in bureaucratic language."

Wren leaned over the map. Up close she could see what the river had made of him: the permanent squint, the hands scarred at the knuckles, the way he held still entirely, as if motion were something he rationed. "So Amos looked anyway."

"So Amos looked anyway." She traced the tributary with one finger. "We ran it on the fourth day. And the water—" Her voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through. "The water was wrong. Same as out there right now, only worse. pH off the scale. Heavy metals you could taste through the hull. Dead fish belly-up for kilometers, and not scattered dead — *arranged* dead, like the river itself had been lined with them. And then we came around a bend and there it was."

She stopped. In her memory she was back on that bend, the morning light flat and silver, the diesel stink arriving before the sight did: three dredges lashed to the far bank like great rusting insects feeding, conveyor belts running slurry into settling ponds that stretched away into the forest, ponds the color of old bruises, unlined, leaching straight into the groundwater. Men in company coveralls with no company name. A generator compound. A fuel dump the size of a village square.

"Dredging at industrial scale," she said. "In a watershed that feeds half the province's drinking water. No permit posted, no remediation plan, nothing. Illegal operations usually hide. This one didn't bother hiding, and that scared me more than anything else on that river. You only stop hiding when you know nobody's coming to look."

"And your survey was coming to look."

"Our survey was *supposed* to be the nobody. Except we looked. Amos sampled everything — water, sediment, fish tissue, soil cores. He photographed the dredges, logged barge registrations, got close enough to read serial numbers on the processing equipment. Four days of documentation. The most complete evidentiary record of environmental crime I've ever seen assembled, and I include the case files I studied at university." She pressed her palm flat against the table, steadying herself or the table, she couldn't tell which. "And then he called it in. Satellite phone, encrypted channel, direct to the ministry contact who'd signed our tasking order. He reported what we'd found and requested instruction."

Wren was quiet a moment. Rain filled the silence. "What did they say?"

"They said, and I am quoting Amos verbatim because I've heard it in my head every night since: '*Cease documentation. Return to base. Await transport.*'" She met Wren's eyes. "You don't tell a survey team to stop documenting unless the documentation is the problem. And you don't say *await transport* unless you already know what kind of transport is coming."

* * *

The story came out of her in pieces after that, the way she'd been carrying it — not as a narrative but as shards, each one cutting on its own edge.

The patrol boat had come upriver two days later. Not a ministry vessel — a black-hulled thing with no markings, running dark at dusk. Amos had seen it first through binoculars and had understood instantly, the way a cartographer understands a contour line closing into a cliff. He'd split the team. Half the data drive — the photographs, the barge logs, the chain-of-custody notes — stayed with him, sealed in a waterproof case, and he went upstream into the gorge country where a boat couldn't follow and a man could vanish if he knew how, and Amos knew how, he'd done fieldwork in worse places than this.

The rest of the team — Daniel, Priya, Marcus, old Tevita who'd been surveying rivers since before Ilsa was born — took the second data set and the radios and started downstream for the extraction point, slow, official, visible. A decoy without being told they were a decoy, because Amos hadn't told them, because telling them would have made them complicit in a choice none of them should have had to make.

"And you?" Wren asked quietly.

"I was late." Two words, and they cost her more than everything she'd said before them combined. She looked down at the map, at Amos's precise ink lines, because looking at Wren was impossible while she said it. "I'd stayed behind at the third campsite to re-shoot a benchmark. My theodolite had a hairline crack in the prism and the readings were drifting and I'm — I'm the kind of person who can't leave a bad reading alone. Twenty minutes. Maybe thirty. By the time I got to the river, the others were gone downstream and the patrol boat was already between me and them, and I heard—" 

She heard it now. She would always hear it now. Voices through the rain, amplified by a loudhailer, formal and calm, asking the survey team to stand by for inspection. Then not-calm. Then a single shot, flat and small over the water, the way gunfire sounds when it's real and not a movie. Then the sound of a boat being boarded, boots on aluminum, and Tevita shouting something in a language she didn't speak, and then nothing. Nothing for a long time.

"I went into the mangroves," she said. "I cut my arm open on the way — that's the graze, or part of it; the rest came later, from a man in a skiff who decided I was worth one bullet but not two. And I've been moving downstream ever since, staying off the water in daylight, waiting for—" She gestured vaguely at the cabin, the rain, the whole collapsing apparatus of her situation. "Waiting for anything. And then your boat showed up, and I thought you were the cleanup crew, and I nearly put a machete in you, and instead you fed me rice and asked me questions."

"Lucky for both of us my face doesn't invite machetes."

"Don't flatter yourself. I was exhausted." But she smiled, briefly, and it hurt her face, and she let it fade. The smile faded and left the true thing underneath, the thing she had carried alone down sixty kilometers of river. "Four people, Wren. Daniel had a daughter. He showed me pictures at every camp, same three pictures, like a ritual. Priya was twenty-six. Marcus was going to quit after this season — he'd bought land, he talked about planting things. And Tevita taught me how to read a river when I was a rookie and he treated every survey like a pilgrimage." Her voice went flat and careful, the voice of someone walking on ice over deep water. "They are dead because I re-shot a benchmark. If I'd been on time, I'd have been on that boat. And maybe I'd be dead too, which would be fair — or maybe four people alive would have fought differently than one woman alone in the mangroves, which would have been better. Either way, the arithmetic doesn't come out clean. I survived by accident. Being late. Being *late*, Wren. That's my whole epitaph."

Wren didn't say *it wasn't your fault*. She watched him decide not to say it, watched him understand that the sentence would be ash in her mouth, and something in her chest loosened a fraction at being known that precisely by a stranger.

"What happened to your brother?" she asked instead, because fairness demanded it, because he had fed her and sheltered her and not yet asked why she mattered enough for anyone to shoot.

His jaw moved. For a moment she thought he wouldn't answer. "Charter boat," he said finally. "Navy-sealed zone on the lower river. They told everyone it was a wreck recovery. My brother was running supplies to the survey region the night it happened." His eyes went to the deck above them, to the river beyond, and she saw the grief in him arrange itself around something new and harder. "I think your excluded tributary and my sealed wreck might be the same story."

"They are," Ilsa said. "The seal, the vanished team, the patrol boat with no name — it's all one operation. Somebody very high up sold the headwaters, and everybody who found out is being subtracted." She tapped the map. "Amos has the other half of the data. Photographs, registration numbers, the chain of custody that makes it evidence instead of rumor. Without his half, mine is just a woman's word against a ministry. With it—"

"With it it's a case."

"With it it's a *reckoning*." She folded the map along Amos's creases, carefully, the way you handle the last intact thing from a dead man's desk. "He went upstream. Into the gorge country above the tributary. If he's alive, he'll be moving high and slow, staying ahead of searchers, heading for the calving lake eventually because it's the only way out of the upper basin once the glacier opens."

"And if he's not alive?"

"Then I carry his half of the story out myself, and it dies smaller, and four people stay unavenged, and I live with being late for the rest of my life." She looked up at him, and the exhaustion in her had burned down to something harder underneath, a coal that the rain couldn't reach. "But I don't believe he's dead. Amos Okere reads fine print. He read this one early."

Outside, the monsoon redoubled, hammering the river into froth, and beneath the drumming of it Wren had gone very still in the way she was learning meant a decision forming. He was looking at the map's upper edge, at the gorge country drawn in Amos's confident ink, at the long climb toward the calving lake.

"That tributary," he said slowly. "Before we go anywhere near it — there's something I need to show you. Something I have to dive for first."
