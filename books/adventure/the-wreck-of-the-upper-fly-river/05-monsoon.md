## 5. Monsoon

The rain did not fall so much as arrive, a gray wall sweeping up the gorge like something with intent. Wren heard it before he saw it — a hiss in the canopy upstream, growing to a roar, and then the world simply dissolved into water.

"Get the tarp!" Ilsa was already moving, hauling the canvas over the fuel drums while he cranked the outboard and swung the boat's nose toward the bank. The river had changed character in an hour. What had been a green channel threaded with gravel bars was now a brown animal, swollen and muscular, carrying whole trees past them at the speed of a galloping horse.

He found a side slough behind a collapsed levee of silt and rooted logs, nosed the boat in, and killed the engine. The silence afterward wasn't silence at all. Rain hammered the jungle with a sound like static turned all the way up, and beneath it ran a deeper note — the river itself, working.

They rigged the tarp over the cockpit and sat under it, shoulder to shoulder, watching the water climb the bank in visible increments. Wren marked it against a root: two inches in twenty minutes. He'd seen monsoons on three rivers in his life and this one was behaving like all of them and none of them, as if the sky had decided this particular gorge owed it something.

"The bilge," Ilsa said.

He'd been listening to it too. The pump cycled every four minutes that morning. Now it came on every ninety seconds, whining through its cycle, spitting a thin rope of water over the side.

"She's taking it faster than she can give it back."

"So we bail."

"So we bail," he agreed, and handed her the dented pot they cooked rice in.

* * *

The church appeared out of the rain like a rumor. They'd been bailing in shifts for six hours — Wren at the pot, then Ilsa, then Wren again, the water in the bilge holding steady only because their arms refused to quit — when Ilsa spotted the roofline through the trees: corrugated iron gone orange with rust, a concrete bell tower leaning five degrees off true, and no bell. A mission church, abandoned decades ago by whatever order had planted it here, half-swallowed by the forest and now, in flood season, drinking the river itself.

Wren poled the boat up what had once been the road and was now a canal between strangler figs, and they tied off to the tower's base. The nave stood in eighteen inches of standing water, brown and still, pews floating in loose rafts or wedged against columns. Above the waterline, whitewashed walls climbed toward a ceiling furred with mold, and someone long ago had painted a mural there — saints or apostles, faces washed to pale smudges, hands still raised in blessing or surrender. It was impossible to tell which.

"It's beautiful," Ilsa said, and meant it, and Wren found he couldn't argue.

They hauled everything above the flood line onto the altar platform — dry stores, the fuel drums, the journal wrapped in its oilcloth, Ilsa's survey case — and set the tent up where the priest would have stood. Then, because the boat still needed them, they went back out into the rain and bailed until dark, by which time the water had risen another foot and the slough had become part of the river again, and there was nothing left to do but let the boat fight for herself overnight, pump running off the battery, and trust.

Dinner was rice and tinned fish eaten cross-legged on the altar, rain drumming the iron roof so hard they had to raise their voices to be heard. Wren caught himself almost enjoying it — the absurdity of the table they'd made, the candle stub Ilsa had found in a vestry drawer stuck to an upturned collection plate. She noticed him noticing and raised the plate an inch, like a toast.

"To the parishioners," she said. "Sorry about the mess."

* * *

It was during the second night that the argument started, though later neither of them would have called it that. It began with the journal.

Ilsa had it open on her knees, reading by candlelight, and Wren watched her finger stop on a page and press flat, the way you press on a wound.

"He could still be alive," she said. "Amos."

"You said the patrol hit your camp with automatic weapons."

"I said I *heard* automatic weapons. From two kilometers downstream, in fog." She looked up. "You've heard gunfire on a river. Tell me you can identify the caliber from two kilometers in fog."

Wren said nothing. The rain filled the pause.

"He took the drive and went upstream because upstream is where the tributary goes. Where the caves are. He grew up in karst country — he told me once, in Sulawesi, caves everywhere, kids playing in them. If anyone in the world could disappear into limestone and stay disappeared, it's Amos." Her voice had the bright, brittle quality of someone arguing against their own knowledge. "He's alive. Someone has to say it out loud or it isn't true."

"And if he's not?" Wren kept his voice level. "What does believing cost you?"

She flinched as if he'd struck her. "That's a hell of a question from a man who keeps his brother's ashes in a dry bag."

The words landed harder than she could have known — or maybe she knew exactly, which was worse. Wren looked at the candle flame and let the silence stretch until it hurt them both.

"Danny drowned eleven years ago," he said finally. "Charter dive, navy-sealed wreck on the lower Fly. Body never recovered. You know what I did for the first year? I planned dives. Every night. Charts, tide tables, gas mixes. I had him coming home. Because as long as the plan existed, he wasn't dead yet. The plan was keeping him alive." He looked at her. "I know what hope costs, Ilsa. I paid it for a year. And the interest nearly took me with it."

"That's not the same."

"No. It's worse. Danny's dead and I know it, and I still keep the ashes in a dry bag because putting them somewhere means choosing a place, and choosing a place means it's finished." He laughed, short and without humor. "So believe Amos is alive. But believe it with your eyes open. Believe it the way you'd bet money you need to live on."

Ilsa closed the journal. For a long moment the only sound was the rain and, faintly, the bilge pump far away in the flooded nave, cycling, cycling, faithful as a heartbeat.

"He taught me to read contour lines properly," she said quietly. "Everyone else on the team treated me like the junior who'd wandered in from the mapping office. Amos sat me down with a 1:50,000 sheet and said, *the map is a promise the land makes you break*. That's how he talked. Like the terrain owed him honesty." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, fast, angry at herself for it. "If he's dead, I want to know. If he's alive, I want to find him. Either way I'm done not knowing. Is that believing with my eyes open?"

Wren reached across and refilled her tin cup with the last of the coffee, cold and bitter as ditchwater.

"That'll do," he said.

* * *

On the third day the rain slackened to a steady, businesslike drizzle, and the river began — slowly, grudgingly — to give back what it had taken. They spent the morning bailing the boat and the afternoon doing nothing, which was its own kind of labor, and it was in that nothing that the stories came out, the way water finds its level.

Ilsa told him about her father, a road engineer who'd redrawn half the passes in the Cordillera and never once been home for her birthday; how she'd learned maps as a love language, tracing his routes on the wall atlas, touching the places he was instead of the places he wasn't. Wren told her about the wreck — not Danny's death, but the wreck itself, the navy seal on the charts, the permit office that stonewalled every application, the way the exclusion zone had a shape on paper that didn't match any hazard he'd ever sounded. He hadn't meant to say any of it aloud. It came out anyway, in the drowned church, to a woman measuring the water line on a column with a pencil, marking the river's retreat like a countdown.

By evening the water had dropped below the third pew. The saints on the ceiling watched from their ruined faces, hands still raised. Wren lay in the tent listening to the pump cycle — slower now, every ten minutes, the boat winning her fight — and thought about the shape of that exclusion zone, and about Amos somewhere upstream in the limestone dark, alive or not, and about how a man could spend eleven years diving one question and have the answer sitting in a government file the whole time.

"Wren." Ilsa's voice from outside, low. "Come look at this."

He crawled out. She was standing at the flooded doorway with the candle, looking down at the water in the nave — clearer now as the sediment settled, brown going to amber. And on the floor beneath the water, revealed by the dropping level, laid out in tiles some mission mason had set a century ago, was a map.

Not a decoration. A chart. Rivers, tributaries, a gorge, a lake — rendered in blue and white tile, accurate enough that Ilsa's breath caught audibly.

"That tributary," she whispered, pointing. "The unmapped one. From the journal. It's *here*, Wren. It's been here the whole time. Somebody knew this river system well enough to put it in a church floor — and it's not on any government survey I've ever seen."

Wren crouched at the edge of the water, candle held low, and looked at the tiled lake at the head of the tiled gorge. Below it, small and deliberate, the mason had set a single black tile. A mark. A warning, or a destination.

The rain had stopped. In the silence, far upstream, they both heard it at the same moment — a sound carried down the emptying gorge like a message: the deep, cracking boom of a glacier letting go of itself.

The calving season was opening early.
