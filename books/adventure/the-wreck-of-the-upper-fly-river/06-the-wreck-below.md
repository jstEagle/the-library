## 6. The Wreck Below

The rain had stopped at dawn, and the river lay flat as poured pewter, which was the only reason Wren agreed to go down.

He stood on the stern of the *Marianne K*, working neoprene over his shoulders while Ilsa watched from the wheelhouse with her arms crossed and her jaw set in the particular way she had when she thought he was being reckless but couldn't argue with the arithmetic. The hull patch below the waterline had failed overnight. The bilge pump had run all night like a small animal begging, and the boat sat three inches lower than it should have, the river already tasting what it could not yet swallow.

"You don't have to dive," she said. "We could beach her. Haul her out."

"Beach her where?" He nodded at the banks — sheer black shale on one side, drowned forest on the other, both still weeping monsoon runoff. "There's no gravel bar for twenty miles. And if the patrol comes back upriver, a beached boat is a confession." He pulled the hood up, checked the seal at his throat twice, the way his hands remembered even when his mind wandered. "I'll patch it. Forty minutes' work."

"And the wreck?" She said it carefully. They'd both been circling it since the church, since Amos's fevered words about the seal, about the navy line drawn around this stretch of the Fly like a scar nobody would explain. "You said you'd dived it before. Before all this."

Wren looked down through the water. From above, on a day like this, you could see the shadow of it lying on the bottom thirty feet down: a long dark hull, settled into the silt at a slight list, its superstructure furred with weed. The navy had sealed the site after the sinking four years ago. No salvage permits issued. No explanation given. Just a buoy with a stenciled number and a fine large enough to ruin any diver who asked questions.

His brother's charter boat had gone down eleven miles downstream of that buoy, on the same night, in water that should have been safe.

"I've dived it once," Wren said. "From the outside. Took photos for a man who wanted them and never paid. The hull was intact. Sealed hatches. Nothing to see." He picked up the patch kit, the epoxy, the mallet. "Today I'm going inside."

Ilsa was quiet a moment. Then: "If you find something down there—"

"Then it wasn't an accident," Wren said. "Any of it."

She didn't say *your brother*. She didn't have to. That was the thing about Ilsa Reyes that had surprised him, somewhere between the church and the long gray days of bailing: she understood grief the way a cartographer understands terrain. She didn't try to flatten it. She just marked it, and worked around it, and never pretended it wasn't there.

He went off the stern without ceremony, the cold closing over him like a door.

* * *

Thirty feet down, the Upper Fly became another country.

The silt hung in the water like breath held too long, and his torch beam carved a yellow tunnel through it. Below him the wreck waited exactly as he remembered it — and not. Four years of river had dressed it in silk: pale curtains of algae streaming from the railings, a soft gray pelt of silt along the deck, a school of small silver fish that scattered and reformed around his light like quicksilver deciding whether to trust him.

He swam the length of the hull first, out of habit, out of ritual. This was how he'd measured every job since Daniel died: not in hours or money but in miles of river and feet of depth, in the distance between himself and the place where his brother's lungs had filled. Diving was the closest he could come to visiting him. Every wreck was a grave; every grave was a conversation he kept failing to finish.

*Daniel, look at this. Look where they sent me.*

He found the breach in the *Marianne K*'s hull at the turn of the keel — a seam split by the ice strike two nights ago, edges peeled back like a tin can opened by a patient thumb. Good news, relatively. A clean tear, reachable, patchable. He scraped the seam, mixed the epoxy in the cold — it cured slower down here, he gave it double time — and pressed the fiberglass plate home, hammering the fasteners in a rhythm he felt more than heard, a heartbeat transmitted through steel into the body of the river.

It should have taken forty minutes. It took fifty, because halfway through, his torch swept across the seabed beyond the wreck, and he stopped breathing.

Equipment. Not scattered, not dumped — *stowed*. Crates, maybe forty of them, laid out in rows on the riverbed in the lee of the sunken hull, each one lashed to concrete ballast blocks, each one shrouded in mesh netting against the current. Organized. Maintained. Someone had visited this place, recently and often, and arranged the riverbed like a warehouse floor.

He drifted toward the nearest crate. The mesh had rotted at one corner, and through the gap he saw metal, machined and oiled, gleaming under his light. He knew machinery the way Ilsa knew maps. This was dredge hardware. Pump impellers the size of dinner plates. Sluice sections. Hose couplings stamped with a maker's mark he recognized from salvage auctions — heavy industrial equipment, the kind that moved mountains of river gravel and left tailings dead zones behind it.

Not cargo lost in a sinking. Cargo *stored* in one.

His heart was going hard now, and he made himself slow down, made himself think like a diver instead of a brother. The wreck itself. He'd photographed the exterior once; intact, sealed. But sealed hatches on a scuttled vessel were a choice. Ships sunk deliberately are opened first so they'll flood and settle clean. Sealed hatches meant someone wanted the interior dry, or private, or both.

He found the main hatch aft. The padlock on it was new — bright brass, uncorroded, absurd down here in the green dark, like a doorknob on a coffin. Beside it, bolted to the coaming, a small stainless box. He knew those boxes. Tracking transponders, the kind mining companies used to monitor equipment caches in remote country. Its indicator light blinked a slow, patient red, visible even through the murk.

Someone was watching this place right now. Or had been, until very recently.

Wren hung there in the cold, the river pressing on him from all sides like a hand, and let the pieces come together the way they always came together at the end of a salvage job — not one at a time but all at once, the whole shape of the thing surfacing like a body.

A navy wreck, sealed, off-limits. But not a wreck. A *scuttle* — a patrol boat put down here on purpose, over a cache of illegal dredging equipment, in the middle of the headwaters the survey team had been mapping. The seal wasn't mourning. The seal was a lock. And the government fixer who'd hired him hadn't wanted the survey gear recovered. He'd wanted a licensed, insured, deniable diver poking around this stretch of river — because something had spooked them. Because Amos had run upstream with half their data, and they needed to know what else might surface.

And Daniel. Daniel's charter had been running this reach the night the patrol boat went down. Eleven miles downstream. In the official story, a rogue swell, a mechanical failure, bad luck. But if there had been boats moving equipment at night — if there had been men who needed the river dark and empty—

Wren's eyes burned behind the mask, and for a moment the torch beam wavered and the whole yellow tunnel of the world trembled. Four years he'd carried it like a stone in his chest: the not-knowing, the sealed file, the navy line on the map that said *nothing happened here, stop asking.* He had thought his brother drowned in an accident. He had thought grief was the price of living near rivers.

But accidents don't get sealed. Crimes do.

He pressed his palm flat against the sunken hull, against the cold steel, the way he'd once pressed his hand to Daniel's headstone and felt nothing but granite. Down here it wasn't granite. It was evidence. It was the reason. His brother hadn't been unlucky. His brother had been *in the way* — or worse, had seen something, a night convoy riding dark, crates going overboard, men who couldn't afford witnesses.

*I'm sorry,* he thought, and for the first time in four years the words weren't an ending. They were a beginning. *I'm sorry it took me this long.*

Above him, faint through thirty feet of water, he heard it before he saw it: engines. Deep, diesel, deliberate. Not the *Marianne K*'s single tired lump. Multiple engines, upstream, coming down.

The patrol.

They'd known exactly where to look, because the transponder on the hatch had told them a diver was here.

Wren kicked for the surface, hauling his tool bag, the epoxy forgotten, the patch holding or not holding — none of it mattered now. The silver fish scattered like flung coins. The wreck fell away beneath him, its brass padlock glinting once in his fading beam, and he rose through the pewter light with his heart slamming and one thought burning brighter than the torch:

They hadn't erased the survey team because of maps.

They'd erased them because Amos had found *this* — and Amos was still alive, still upstream, still carrying the proof in a feverish fist.

The surface broke open above him, gray sky and rain beginning again, and Ilsa's voice came down to him across the water, sharp with alarm:

"Wren — boat, coming fast — get out of the water, *now*—"
