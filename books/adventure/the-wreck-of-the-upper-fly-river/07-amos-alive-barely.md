## 7. Amos, Barely Alive

The tributary announced itself before we saw it — a cold breath coming down the gorge, the kind of air that has been sitting on ice for a thousand years. Ilsa felt it through her wet shirt and thought: *glacier*. Which meant the calving lake was close. Which meant the way out was close. Which meant everything now depended on a man who might already be dead.

They had left the boat that morning where the gorge pinched shut, wedged into a seam of rock above the white water, camouflaged under cut ferns. Wren had spent an hour making it look like driftwood wreckage, and when he finished he stood back with his hands on his hips and said nothing, and Ilsa understood that he was saying goodbye to something. A salvage diver loves his boats the way priests love their churches — not because they are beautiful, but because they hold you up over the abyss.

Now they climbed.

The map from the survey journal showed the cave as a pencil tick and two letters: *A.T.* Amos Teller. She had stared at those letters for three days of rain, refusing to believe them, because believing them meant believing Amos had made it out of the camp alive while six others hadn't, and hope was a currency she couldn't afford to spend twice. She had spent it once already, the night she'd waited at the rendezvous bend until dawn, watching the black water for a boat that never came.

"Here." Wren's voice, low, ahead of her. He crouched at the base of a granite shoulder where the jungle thinned to moss and scree. "Smoke smell. Old."

She smelled it too — woodsmoke gone sour with damp, soaked into stone. Someone had kept a fire here. Someone had kept *fires*, plural, over days.

The cave mouth was hidden behind a curtain of hanging roots, and Wren went through it first, pistol drawn, moving like water finding its level. Ilsa followed with the flashlight cupped in her palm, its beam strangled by her fingers, giving them only enough light to be dangerous in.

Inside, the air was warm. That was the first wrong thing. The second was the smell — sweat and rot and the sweetish chemical reek of an infected wound left too long untreated.

Then the flashlight found him, and she stopped breathing.

Amos Teller lay against the far wall of the cave like a man the river had tried to return and half succeeded. He was thinner than the photograph in her memory, hollowed at the cheeks, his beard grown into something feral, his survey jacket stiff with dried mud and darker stains. His right leg was propped on a rolled sleeping mat, wrapped in strips of shirt gone yellow-brown at the center. And clutched against his chest, held the way a drowning man holds driftwood, was a hard case no bigger than a paperback book.

His eyes opened. They took a long time to focus, traveling from the light to her face, and when recognition finally landed it hit him so hard that he began to cry without any change in his expression at all — just tears sliding down into the beard, his mouth working around words that wouldn't come.

"Ilsa," he managed. "Ilsa Reyes. You're — they said — I heard the shots and they said—"

"I was late." Her voice came out flat, strange. "I missed the final leg. The helicopter slot fell through and I took the next one, and by the time I got to the bend—" She stopped. Kneeling beside him, she reached out and touched his forehead, and snatched her hand back. He was burning. Not warm — *burning*, heat pouring off him like a stove door left open. "How long have you been feverish?"

"I don't — days. The days aren't — they don't come in order anymore." He laughed, a dry terrible sound. "Monday keeps happening."

Wren had moved to the cave entrance and was looking out at the gorge, listening. Now he camped by the wall near the fire pit — a neat ring of stones, a hoard of split wood under plastic sheeting, tins of survey rations stacked with the labels facing out. Even delirious, Amos had been an organized man. It broke her heart in a place she didn't know was still soft.

"The others?" she asked, though she knew. She had known since the quiet camp, since the half-eaten meals and the radio smashed from inside. But knowing a thing and hearing it were different animals, and she needed to hear it from someone who had been there.

Amos closed his eyes. When he spoke again the fever-slur was gone, replaced by something precise and exhausted, a man reading from a report he'd written in his head a hundred times.

"Dekker came upriver on the second night. Not a patrol launch — one of theirs, unmarked, matte hull. Men in plain clothes with military rifles. They knew our positions. They knew *where our tents were pitched*, Ilsa. That's not surveillance, that's someone feeding them our coordinates from inside the ministry." His hand tightened on the case. "Okafor ran for the treeline. They shot him in the open. Marsh and Lindqvist they took aboard. Chen and Priya — Priya fought, and they—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "I was upstream taking flow readings at the gauging station. Half a kilometer away. I heard it. I hid in the water under the station pilings with my nose above the surface and I listened to my team die, and then I swam downriver in the dark with the drive strapped to my chest, and I've been climbing ever since."

The cave was very quiet. Somewhere outside, water dripped from the canopy in slow metronome time.

"You could have gone downstream," Wren said from the shadows. His voice had no judgment in it, which somehow made it worse. "To the villages. To anyone."

"To whom?" Amos turned his head, and even wasted by fever his gaze was flint. "The district commissioner signed the dredging permits under a shell company. The provincial police commander flew to the capital the week before we found the tailings ponds. Every official channel is either bought or terrified. There was exactly one thing I could do with this—" he lifted the case an inch off his chest, arms trembling with the effort, "—and that was keep it out of their hands and stay alive long enough to pass it on. So I climbed. Upstream, because they'd expect a runner to go down. And my leg—" he gestured at the bandage without looking at it, the way a man avoids looking at a thing he's afraid of, "—the leg objected."

Ilsa knelt and unwound the bandage with hands that stayed steady through sheer professional discipline. Beneath it, the wound was a gouge along the calf — shrapnel or a ricochet, weeks old — angry red streaks climbing toward the knee like lightning frozen mid-strike. Sepsis, early but advancing. He needed antibiotics, real ones, within a day or two, or the question of whether he survived would be taken out of everyone's hands.

"There's a med kit on the boat," she said. "Broad-spectrum. It'll buy time."

"It won't buy enough." Amos caught her wrist. His grip was shockingly strong for a dying man. "Listen to me. Both of you. The patrol — Dekker's people — they're not hunting the gorge anymore. They're waiting."

Wren went still. "Waiting where."

"At the calving lake. It's the only exit. One channel out, and they know it, and they've had two weeks to sit on it." Amos's laugh again, that dry awful sound. "You want to run? Run. They'll scoop you out of the water like leaves. I watched them do it to a smuggler's barge on the fourth day — I was up on the ridge, watching through field glasses, learning what they do to people who use this river without permission."

Ilsa sat back on her heels. The cold glacier breath moved through the cave, and she thought about the map spread in the bottom of the boat, the route she and Wren had argued over for three days — down the gorge, across the lake, past the checkpoint at the outflow, home. It had always been a thin plan. Now it was no plan at all.

"So there's no way out," Wren said.

"There's no way out *with this*." Amos tapped the case. "Which brings me to the bad news, if you can believe there's worse." He worked the latches with fingers that shook, and inside, cradled in foam, lay the data drive — and beside it, a portable terminal with a cracked screen. "Impact damage when I went down the scree slope on day nine. The file structure's scrambled. Encryption keys fragmented across corrupted sectors. I've tried every recovery protocol I know." His voice dropped. "It's not readable. Not here. Not anywhere except—"

"The uplink shack," Ilsa said.

He looked at her, and something in his face almost smiled. "You remember."

"I built the redundancy spec. Of course I remember." Her mind was already running the architecture: the survey's primary uplink station, four kilometers below the glacier terminus, solar-backed, hardened, designed to push raw data through the ministry's own satellite window precisely because the ministry would never think to look for the truth inside its own pipe. The shack's decryption array held the master keyset. Plug the drive in, rebuild the index, transmit. "If the shack's intact. If the solar array survived the rains. If—"

"If, if, if." Amos let his head fall back against the stone. "That's all any of us are now. A chain of ifs."

* * *

They worked on him by flashlight while the afternoon died outside. Wren cleaned the wound with surgical patience, saying little, and Ilsa dosed Amos with the antibiotics from the boat kit and forced water and ration paste into him until he waved her off. The fever didn't break, but it plateaued — the fire banked rather than extinguished.

Through it all, Amos talked. Fever-talk, mostly, drifting: names of the dead recited like a rosary, fragments of survey data read aloud from memory — turbidity levels, mercury concentrations, parts per million — numbers he had carried so long they'd worn grooves in him. Once he grabbed Ilsa's sleeve and said, very clearly, "Priya had a daughter. Make sure whatever you do, it's worth Priya's daughter knowing about." Then his eyes rolled back and he slept, truly slept, for the first time since they'd found him.

Ilsa stepped out of the cave into the blue hour. Below her, the tributary ran gray-green with rock flour, glacier milk, and beyond the gorge's bend she could see it: the pale wall of the icefield, and at its foot, a smudge of silver that was the calving lake. Somewhere on that silver water, a matte-hulled launch sat waiting. Had been waiting for two weeks. Would keep waiting, patient as a spider, because the men aboard were paid by the month and the month was paid by men who owned ministers.

Wren came to stand beside her. For a while neither spoke.

"We could leave him," she said at last, because someone had to say it, and it should be her. "Take the drive, take the map, go down fast and light. Two people move quicker than three, and one of the three can't walk."

"Yes," Wren said.

"He'd tell us to. He *has* told us to — you heard him."

"Yes."

She turned and looked at him. In the failing light his face was unreadable, river-carved, a man who had spent years measuring distance instead of feeling things. But she had shared a bilge pump with this man through a monsoon; she knew what lived under the flatness.

"You're going to say we carry him," she said.

"I'm going to say the shack is four klicks below the glacier and the lake is between us and it, and whichever way we go, we go through them." He looked out at the icefield. "So the question isn't whether Amos slows us down. The question is what we're willing to be when this is over. I've already been the man who didn't go back for someone." His jaw worked once. "I'm not being him twice."

Behind them, from deep in the cave, Amos began to talk in his sleep — clear, urgent, the cadence of a man dictating a transmission: *repeat, repeat, headwaters contaminated, repeat to all stations.*

And far off across the silver water, faint as a heartbeat, came the first crack of the glacier letting go.
