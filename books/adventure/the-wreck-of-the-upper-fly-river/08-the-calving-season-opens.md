## 8. The Calving Season Opens

The sound woke Wren before the light did — a crack like the sky splitting along a seam, rolling down the valley and bouncing off the gorge walls until it seemed to come from everywhere at once.

He was out of his bunk and on deck before he'd decided to move, bare feet slick on wet planking, breath fogging in air that had turned cold overnight. Upstream, where the river narrowed into the throat of the calving lake, the glacier stood pale against the dark — and it was moving. Not flowing, not creeping. *Calving.* A wall of ice the size of a cathedral facade leaned, groaned, and let go, dropping into the lake with a detonation he felt through the soles of his feet. The wave it threw came downriver minutes later, lifting their boat on a swell of gray water studded with brash ice, and setting it down again as gently as a hand.

Ilsa was beside him now, hair loose, eyes wide. "It's open," she said. "The route. It's *open*."

Behind them, from the cave where they'd carried him two days ago, Amos's voice floated down, thin but lucid for the first time since they'd found him: "How long?"

Wren didn't answer. He was doing the arithmetic himself, watching the ice. The calving would keep the channel scoured for a few days — three, maybe four — before the floes jammed the narrows downstream and sealed the river shut again until next season. Three days to run forty miles of rapids choked with bergs, reach the uplink shack above the tributary confluence, decrypt and transmit everything Amos had nearly died carrying. And somewhere behind them, closing fast, the patrol boat that had been hunting them since the drowned church.

"Three days," he said finally. "Maybe less."

"Then we don't waste one of them standing here admiring the scenery." Ilsa was already moving, coiling lines, kicking the bilge pump to life. The pump coughed, sputtered, caught. The boat had been taking water since the monsoon; he'd patched the hull twice with epoxy and prayer, and both patches were holding the way wet paper holds.

They cast off as the sun cleared the ridge, running with the current the glacier had given them. For the first hour it was almost easy — the river swollen and fast, carrying them past cliffs streaked black with runoff, past waterfalls that fell so far they dissolved into mist before they landed. Wren took the wheel. Ilsa stood at the bow calling the ice.

And there was ice everywhere. Growlers the size of cars spun lazily in the eddies. Whole shelves of blue glacial ice drifted with their undersides showing, glowing like something lit from within. A berg the size of their boat grounded itself briefly on a gravel bar ahead of them, tipped, and slid free just as Wren threaded past it close enough to read the strata frozen in its flank — centuries of winters, compressed and released in a single season.

"The river's helping us," Ilsa said, half to herself. "All this time it's been the thing keeping us trapped, and now—"

"Don't thank it yet." Wren kept his voice flat. "Rivers don't take sides. They just move."

But privately, watching the water carry them at eight knots toward the shack, he felt what she felt. For weeks the river had been a prison — monsoon, patrol boats, the navy seal downstream, every mile of it owned by men who wanted them gone. Now the same water was running them upstream of all of it, faster than any engine could push against the current, hidden inside a fleet of drifting ice no patrol would dare enter. The river wasn't taking sides. But today, by accident or grace, it was theirs.

* * *

The narrows came at midday, where the gorge pinched to less than fifty meters and the current doubled. The ice had begun to jam there already — floes grinding against each other, backing up into a shifting dam that left only a single winding lead of open water, and even that was closing as he watched.

"We portage?" Ilsa asked.

"With Amos? Over that?" The portage trail climbed four hundred meters of scree. Amos could stand, barely, leaning on Wren's shoulder, but he couldn't climb. "We run it."

He brought the boat in slow, nose into the current, reading the lead the way he'd once read wrecks — looking for the shape beneath the surface. There. A gap between two grounded floes, maybe a boat's width, the water through it white but deep. He committed before he could think better of it, because thinking better was how you died in water like this.

The gorge roared around them. Ice ground against ice on either side with sounds like grinding teeth, close enough to touch. A slab calved off the left wall and dropped into the lead twenty meters ahead, and Wren put the wheel over and slipped through the gap it left behind before the boil settled, the hull shrieking along a submerged shelf of ice he felt through the deck plates rather than heard. Then they were through, into open water below the jam, and Ilsa was laughing — actually laughing, high and ragged — and Amos, wedged against the transom with the data drive strapped to his chest like a child, was staring at them both as if they'd lost their minds.

"You're insane," Amos said.

"Yeah," said Wren. "Hold on."

* * *

The patrol found them an hour later.

Wren heard the engine first — a low diesel thrum echoing down the gorge, wrong somehow, too steady, too mechanical amid all that chaos of water and ice. He looked back and saw it rounding the bend behind them: the gray-hulled patrol boat, armored wheelhouse, a man on the bow with binoculars who raised them, then lowered them and shouted something back over his shoulder.

"They've seen us," Ilsa said.

"They can't catch us in this." Wren fed throttle. "Not through the ice. Their draft's too deep, their hull's too stiff. They know it too — watch."

He was right, mostly. The patrol held back at the edge of the ice field, shadowing them, herding them, waiting for the river to do its work. Twice more the engine surged closer where the channel widened, and twice Wren dodged into water too broken with ice for the bigger boat to follow. But the third time, the patrol didn't surge. It stopped instead, at the mouth of a side channel, and Wren understood a heartbeat too late.

"It's not chasing us," he said. "It's cutting ahead. There's a straight run below the confluence — they'll be waiting at the shack."

Ilsa went still. "Then we can't go to the shack."

"We have to go to the shack." Amos's voice cracked across the deck like a whip. He'd dragged himself upright, fever-bright but ferocious. "The drive is useless anywhere else. You understand? Everything — the dredge surveys, the contamination data, the shipping manifests tying it to the ministry — it's encrypted to our uplink keys. Without the shack there is no story. There's just us, dead in the bush, and them, lying about it forever."

Silence, except the river.

"There's a landing below the falls," Wren said slowly, working the map in his head. "Two miles short of the confluence. From there it's a climb — six hours on foot with a healthy man. With Amos, call it ten."

"I can walk," Amos said, and then his knees buckled, and Ilsa caught him, and nobody argued the point further.

* * *

The ice did the rest.

It happened below the falls, where the river spread wide and shallow over a gravel fan. Wren saw the growler late — a block the size of a truck, submerged except for a shoulder of green ice, spinning slowly in the outwash current. He put the wheel hard over. Too hard. The boat slewed, the current took the stern, and the growler opened the hull below the waterline the way a can opener opens tin.

The impact threw them all down. Water came up through the deck boards immediately, cold and fast, and Wren knew before he got his feet under him that no patch was saving this one. The river they'd been riding as an ally reached up through the wound in the boat's belly and claimed it, and them, in the space of thirty seconds.

"Landing!" he shouted. "Everything you can carry!"

They came ashore in waist-deep water, hauling duffels and fuel and the medical kit, and turned to watch their boat settle onto the gravel bar with the dignity of a dying animal, stern rising, bow buried, the current combing white foam down its flanks. Everything they had left in the world fit into four bags.

Amos couldn't manage the bank, so Wren carried him. He got the man across his shoulders in a fireman's lift — Amos weighed nothing, nothing, the fever had eaten him down to wire and skin — and started climbing. The trail was a goat track cut into the gorge wall, slick with meltwater, switchbacking up through cloud forest dripping like a wet sponge. Ilsa broke trail ahead with the machete, and Wren put his head down and climbed, one step, another step, measuring the distance not in miles now but in breaths, in the burn of his thighs, in the weight on his back that was heavier than any salvage he'd ever raised.

Somewhere below and behind, faint through the trees, he heard the patrol boat's engine again. Running the rapids now that the ice had done their work for them. Heading for the shack.

"They'll beat us there," Ilsa said, stopping at a switchback, chest heaving.

"Probably." Wren shifted Amos higher. "So we don't go to the shack."

She stared at him. "What?"

"The shack has a radio mast, right? Visible from the water. That's why they'll sit on it." He nodded up-slope, where the ridge line climbed toward a shoulder of rock he remembered from the survey map — a knoll above the tree line, bare granite, wind-scoured. "But Amos told us the encryption keys are in the equipment, not the building. If we can pull the transceiver and the key module out of the shack—" He shrugged. "There are other high places."

Isla followed his gaze to the ridge, and he watched her do the cartographer's math — sightlines, elevation, distance to the nearest relay. "The knoll," she breathed. "Line of sight to the provincial repeater. We could transmit from open rock, anywhere, if we had power and the gear."

"So we send them to guard an empty building," Wren said, "while we climb."

Above them the clouds tore open briefly, and he saw the knoll clearly for the first time: a bare shoulder of granite catching the last of the light, snow-dusted, impossibly far. Below it, at the confluence, the gray shape of the patrol boat was already nosing into the current, small as a toy, heading for the shack they would never reach.

Wren resettled his burden and started climbing.
