## 9. Broadcast

The shack sat on the shoulder of a ridge like something the mountain had coughed up and then regretted — corrugated iron gone to rust-lace, solar panels furred with moss, an antenna guyed to a dead tree that leaned over the drop as if it, too, were tired of holding things up. Ilsa had carried the map of this place in her head for two years. She had drawn it herself, from memory, on the back of a fuel requisition form, and she had never believed she would stand inside it.

Now she stood inside it, dripping monsoon, her hands shaking so badly she could not work the latch.

"Sit down," Wren said behind her. He set Amos against the wall beneath the one window that still had glass, propped the feverish man's shoulders on their folded tarp, and turned back to the door without another word. He had the machete in one hand and the salvage bar in the other, and he looked like a man arranging furniture in a house he intended to defend.

Ilsa made herself sit at the desk. The chair was missing a leg; she braced it with a ammunition crate and pulled the terminal toward her. The screen woke with the patience of old technology, amber letters crawling up out of black. The uplink was there. The encryption suite was there. Everything Amos had promised was here, humming on trickle-charged batteries through two years of nobody coming.

Two years of nobody coming because everyone who knew about this place was dead or running.

*You survived because you were late,* she told herself, the old litany, the litany that had kept her alive and kept her small. *Late for the boat. Late for the last leg. Late for everything that mattered.*

She fed the data drive into the reader. The light went red. Amber. Red again.

"Come on," she whispered. "Come *on.*"

Outside, down through the cloud, the river was running brown and enormous, carrying the glacier's calendar in its current. The calving had opened the route three days ago. The patrol boat would have come up through the ice gap within hours of it clearing — they always did, Amos had said, they always watch the calving season, it's the only door. Which meant the patrol was not chasing them anymore. It was ahead of them, waiting, and Wren had spotted the wake of its tender forty minutes ago cutting across the bend below.

Forty minutes. Maybe less now.

The drive light steadied to green. Files began to unspool across the screen: bathymetric surveys, sediment cores, water chemistry readings with mercury numbers that made her stomach fold over on itself. Dredge schedules. Fuel manifests signed by names she recognized — ministry names, the kind of names that appeared on plaques at ribbon-cuttings. Photographs. She opened one and had to close her eyes for a moment before she could look: the tributary running grey-white with tailings, dead fish belly-up along the banks in such numbers that the water seemed paved with them.

Her team had seen this. Her team had documented this, carefully, professionally, with all the rigor she had taught them. And then someone had decided that rigor was a threat, and sent men with guns upriver to erase it, and the only reason Ilsa Reyes was not lying in the mud of that quiet camp with the rest of them was that she had been twenty minutes late to the dock.

Twenty minutes. She had spent two years wishing she'd been on time. Now she understood she owed her whole life to a delay, and there was no clean way to carry that.

She began building the transmission. Every news desk she knew — the national papers, the regional broadcasters, the wire services, the foreign correspondents who still answered her emails out of politeness. Every ministry address, including the ones implicated, because shame worked best when it was delivered directly. An ombudsman's office in the capital that had once actually investigated something. A university hydrology department. She attached everything. The files climbed the progress bar like men climbing out of a well.

Behind her, Amos groaned.

"Ilsa." His voice was gravel and fever. "Is that—"

"It's going out. All of it."

He laughed, a wet, broken sound. "Marta said you'd come. She said, Reyes is never late twice." He coughed. "Tell her—" He stopped. They both knew what he'd stopped saying.

"You're going to tell her yourself," Ilsa said. "When your fever breaks. When we're down off this ridge."

His eyes were bright and unfocused, but he found her hand and gripped it with surprising strength. "The shack has a backup battery," he managed. "Under the floor. If they cut power mid-send—"

"I know. Wren told me. We checked."

"Marta trusted him?"

"He hasn't left the door in an hour," Ilsa said. "Yes. She'd have trusted him."

Amos closed his eyes. His breathing evened, and for the first time since the cave, the terrible furnace-heat of him seemed to bank itself. Later she would understand the fever broke in that moment, between one transmission packet and the next, as if his body had only been waiting for permission. But she did not understand it then. Then there was only the progress bar, and the rain on the tin roof, and the sound of Wren moving something heavy against the door.

* * *

Wren had held doors before. Salvage work was mostly holding doors — hatches, bulkheads, the pressure door on a flooded compartment you sealed with your own shoulder while the water argued on the other side. You learned that a door is just a decision made physical. You lean into it, or you don't.

He leaned into it.

Through the gap under the door he could see the trail switchbacking down through the wet ferns, and beyond it the grey churn of the river, and — when the cloud tore briefly open — the tender, beached on the shingle below, four men coming up through the trees in file. They moved like professionals. No shouting, no lights. That was worse than shouting. Shouting meant they thought you might run.

He had wedged the ammunition crate, the desk, and a fifty-kilo drum of diesel against the door, and he had pried loose the ridge beam brace to use as a ram, and none of it would matter if they simply shot through the walls. Corrugated iron stopped rain and little else. So he had done the other thing, the thing his brother Teo used to do when the odds went bad on some charter gone wrong: he had made noise. He dragged the diesel drum across the floor so it boomed like a man repositioning a machine gun. He hammered the machete flat against the roof strut, three sharp reports, pause, three more. He let them hear a defended position and hope they'd rather wait than bleed for it.

Every minute he bought was a minute of upload. That was the arithmetic. He didn't look at the screen. Looking would make it real, and real things could be lost. He just leaned on the door and listened to the rain and thought about Teo, who had died in brown water above a secret that wasn't his, and how every mile Wren had measured since had really been a search for this ridge, this shack, this hour.

The footsteps came up the switchback. Stopped. He heard a low voice, the click of a radio handset, a reply crackling thin through weather. Then silence, which was the worst sound yet, because silence meant they were deciding.

Inside, Ilsa's voice, low and steady, reading addresses aloud to herself like a rosary. *Newsdesk. Newsdesk. Ministry. Ombudsman.*

The first blow against the door came without warning — a rifle butt, then a boot, then the flat concussion of something heavier. The diesel drum jumped. Wren threw his weight into the brace and felt the whole structure flex around him like a hull taking sea.

"They're at the door!" he shouted.

"Eighty percent!"

Another blow. The hinge screws shrieked. Through the widening gap he saw a flashlight beam and beyond it a face, young, wet, frightened — a boy in a uniform doing what boys in uniforms are told to do, and Wren felt no hatred, only the old cold clarity of a man underwater watching the surface get further away.

"Seventy seconds," Ilsa called. "Maybe less. It's slow — the storm's eating the signal—"

"Then send it twice," Wren said. "Send it until the building falls down."

He heard her laugh — one short, fierce note — and then the sound of her hands on keys, fast, certain, and he understood that courage was not what he'd thought it was all these years. It wasn't the dive, the cold water, the dark. It was this: a woman with shaking hands typing anyway. A dying man gripping a stranger's fingers. Himself, leaning on a piece of scrap iron against the whole weight of a state, because someone had to witness, and witness was the only law that reached this far upriver.

The door burst inward six inches. An arm came through, groping for the bar. Wren brought the salvage bar down on the forearm and the arm withdrew with a scream, and then the muzzle of a rifle appeared in the gap and he flattened against the wall as the first shots tore through the shack, punching daylight holes through the far wall, shredding moss off the rafters, and Amos — God help him — Amos was laughing from the floor, hoarse and delirious and free.

"Ninety-eight percent," Ilsa screamed over the gunfire.

Wren grabbed the diesel drum and shoved it into the doorway, into the bodies there, and the whole mass of it went over with a sound like a felled tree. Screaming outside. Rain pouring through the new holes in the roof onto the terminal, onto Ilsa's shoulders, and she didn't move, didn't flinch, just hunched over the keyboard like a mother over a child, shielding it with her body.

"One hundred," she said.

And then, quieter, almost gentle, as the progress bar filled and the queue emptied and two years of erased people climbed into the sky on a carrier wave and scattered themselves across every desk and screen and conscience in the country:

"*Sent.*"

* * *

The silence afterward lasted perhaps three seconds.

Then the door gave way entirely, and the first man through it stepped on the fallen drum, slipped, and went down hard, and Wren was already moving — not toward the fight but away from it, hauling Ilsa up by the arm, dragging Amos's good arm across his shoulders. There was a back way; there is always a back way on a ridge, because ridges don't care about buildings. A gap in the rust where a wall panel had been scavenged years ago, and beyond it the steep fall of fern and rock toward the river's blind bend.

"They'll call it in," Ilsa gasped as they went, Amos stumbling between them, his fever-sweat cold now, his eyes suddenly, shockingly clear. "The patrol will radio ahead — roadblocks at the confluence—"

"Then we don't go to the confluence," Wren said.

Below them, through the tearing cloud, the great river ran high and brown and unstoppable, carrying the glacier's melt down toward the coast, past the drowned church, past the scuttled patrol boat, past the quiet camp, out into the world. Somewhere downstream, in offices with air conditioning, screens were beginning to light up with photographs of a poisoned tributary and a list of signatures.

"The long way down, then," Ilsa said.

Wren shifted Amos's weight and looked at the mountains, at the rain, at the country that had tried to bury them all in brown water — and started walking.
