## 10. The Long Way Down

They came down off the mountain the way water comes down — finding every crack, taking the steepest line, refusing to stop.

Wren carried Amos on his back for the first six hours, a harness improvised from survey webbing and the last of Ilsa's rope, the man's fever-hot weight pressed between his shoulder blades like a second heartbeat. Amos had stopped talking somewhere above the tree line. He mumbled now, fragments of coordinates and names, a litany of the dead he'd left behind at the quiet camp. Wren had learned on the river that you could carry almost anything if you stopped asking how far was left. You measured in miles, not years. You put one foot past the other foot and let the arithmetic do itself.

Ilsa walked point with the rifle slung across her back and the data drive in an inner pocket against her skin, as if it were warm-blooded, as if it might bolt. Twice she heard engines on the switchbacks below them and pulled them into the fern shadow of the cutbank to wait, her hand flat on Wren's chest, both of them breathing through their mouths while the patrol's radios crackled up through the wet green dark. Both times the engines passed. The mountain was doing what mountains did for people who knew how to be small in them.

By the third dawn they reached the mission station at Kavali Bend, where an old lay brother named Tomas looked at Amos's leg without questions and at their faces with only one. "You're the ones from the radio," he said. Not a question either. The broadcast had gone out four days ago, and it had gone everywhere at once — every news desk, every ministry address, every inbox Ilsa could remember from a career of filing reports no one read. The truth had traveled faster than they had. It always did, once it finally started moving.

* * *

The world, it turned out, had been waiting to be told.

Wren sat in the mission's kitchen on the fifth morning, drinking tea that tasted of woodsmoke, watching the story unfold on a radio with a coat-hanger antenna. The mining consortium's name came first, then the officials who'd signed the dredging permits under shell companies, then the photographs from the survey's own cameras — the tailings ponds bleeding orange into the headwaters, the dredge barges working the tributary that appeared on no map because no map was allowed to show it. Then the inquiry. A national commission, announced by a minister whose voice shook in a way that Wren recognized. It was the shake of a man reading words someone else had written for him, hoping the arithmetic would hold.

And then, smaller, buried in the middle of the bulletin like a stone in a riverbed: *a fixer operating out of Port Moresby, one Darius Cole, taken into custody this morning in connection with the disappearance of the government survey team.*

Wren set down his cup. His hands were steady. That surprised him. He'd imagined this moment — if he'd imagined it at all — as something louder. But it arrived the way the calving had arrived: a sound you felt in your feet before you heard it, and then the whole face of things coming loose.

Cole. Triple pay, cash, no paperwork. A handshake that had felt wrong the way ice feels wrong underfoot before it gives. Wren thought about the night he'd signed on, sitting in that waterfront bar with a man who never drank and never blinked, and how some part of him — the part that had been asleep at the wheel of his own life for three years — had known exactly what he was climbing into and had climbed anyway, because grief is a kind of gravity and falling feels like movement when nothing else does.

"He'll give up everyone above him," Ilsa said from the doorway. She was thinner than when he'd found her on the water, all tendon and salt, but her eyes hadn't changed. They still weighed everything. "Men like Cole keep lists precisely so they can trade them."

"You think it holds?"

"I think," she said, sitting across from him, "that forty million people can read those photographs now, and commissions are cheap but retraction isn't. I think the truth doesn't need the law to survive it. It just needs witnesses." She turned her cup slowly on the table. "That's what Amos kept saying, in the fever. *Witnesses.* Like a prayer."

Through the window Wren could see the mission garden, and beyond it the brown gleam of the Kavali, running high with monsoon melt toward the confluence twenty miles downstream. Toward the place where the Upper Fly met everything else and became a different, larger river. Toward the place where, three years ago, a charter boat had gone down in weather no one should have been flying boats in, carrying salvage gear and a navy seal order and a man named Daniel Okonkwo, who had laughed even in his sleep and dove like the water owed him money.

Toward the place where Wren had promised himself, standing on a bank with a tin box in his hands, that he would come back when there was something worth saying.

* * *

They went down to the confluence on the seventh day, the three of them, because Amos insisted on walking and because Ilsa said she wanted to see the water that had tried to kill them doing something peaceful for once. The rains had broken. The sky over the gorge was rinsed blue, and the river ran wide and glittering, braided with light, carrying the whole watershed toward the sea without hurry or malice.

Amos sat on a driftwood log with his crutch laid beside him, still gaunt, still yellow around the eyes, but upright, which two weeks ago had seemed like a rumor. He watched Wren walk down to the gravel bar alone and understood, the way river people understand each other, exactly what the tin box was.

Wren knelt at the waterline. The current pushed against his shins, patient, insistent, the same push it had always been. He'd spent three years measuring his life in miles — miles of river between him and the wreck, miles between dives, miles of distance he could put between himself and the night the radio went silent. Miles were honest. Miles never asked you what you were running from. You just counted them and called the counting a life.

He opened the tin.

The ashes were gray and fine as river silt. Of course they were. Everything came back to the river eventually; his brother would have found that funny, would have made a joke about sediment and eternity, and Wren found he could hold that joke in his chest now without it cutting him. That was new. That was the work of a mountain and a church full of bailing water and a woman who'd survived by being late and couldn't forgive herself until she saved two men instead.

"Hey, Danny," he said. The wind took most of it. That was all right. The river would take the rest.

He didn't say anything about justice. Justice wasn't his to announce — it belonged to the inquiry rooms and the news desks, to Ilsa's transmission still echoing through a hundred thousand screens, to Amos's testimony already being typed up in a capital city none of them could see. What Wren had was smaller and older than justice. He had witness. He had stood at the door of the radio shack with shaking hands and held it, and what came through that door had changed the shape of the country, and no one could ever again say it hadn't happened, because too many people had seen it happen.

He scattered the ashes slow, a handful at a time, letting the current take each one before the next. The gray dissolved into the brown and was gone, and the river didn't change at all, and that was right too. Rivers don't change for us. We change our way of measuring them.

When the tin was empty he stayed kneeling a long time. Behind him he heard Ilsa come down the bank and stop a few paces off, not intruding, just present, the way she'd been present through the whole long fall downstream — testing him with sharp words, holding him with flat hands, becoming, without either of them deciding it, the person whose absence would now have a weight.

Finally he stood and turned around. She was looking at the water, and the light was on her face, and she looked, for once, unburdened.

"So," she said. "What now? Cole's arrested, the inquiry has your name in it twice, and there's a ministry official offering Amos a consulting contract as damage control. You could go anywhere. You could stop diving entirely. Most people would."

Wren looked at the river. The confluence braided and unbraided itself, channels joining, splitting, joining again, the whole moving body of it headed somewhere larger than any of its parts. He thought about the scuttled patrol boat on the riverbed, the mining equipment under its hull, the wreck that was no wreck — evidence still down there in the cold and the dark, and divers needed to document it properly, legally, for the record, for the inquiry, for the truth to have roots instead of just wings. He thought about telling someone else's story underwater and calling it work.

Then he thought about the dive after that. And the one after that. Not for a navy seal order. Not for triple pay from a man who never blinked. For the thing he'd felt at nine years old, hanging off a dock with his brother laughing above him — the pull of the green deep, the way the world went quiet and honest beneath the surface, the way a man could know himself down there in a way the dry world never allowed.

"Dive," he said.

Ilsa raised an eyebrow. "For the inquiry?"

"For me." He said it and heard how strange it sounded, how new — a sentence with himself at the center of it, no ghost and no debt attached. He smiled, and it didn't hurt. "There's a wreck downriver that needs documenting. Officially. And after that—" He shrugged, and the shrug felt like setting down something heavy at the top of a long stair. "After that, whatever water's next."

Ilsa looked at him for a moment longer, weighing, the way she weighed everything. Then she nodded once, and picked up his pack along with her own, and slung them both over her shoulders like it was nothing, like carrying things was simply what they did for each other now.

"Well," she said, turning toward the trail, toward the mission, toward whatever came after the end of the story. "You'll need a cartographer. Rivers move. Maps go stale. Someone has to keep track of you."

Wren followed her up the bank, the empty tin in his pocket, the river at his back going on without him, and for the first time in three years the miles ahead didn't feel like distance.

They felt like direction.
