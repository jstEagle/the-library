## 1. The Man Who Stopped Moving

The salt flats taught you one lesson above all others: nothing out here stops moving unless it's dead.

Casper Vasquez had learned it the hard way, his first season as a ranger, when he'd come upon a coyote lying flat in the white glare and thought it dead until it turned its head and looked at him with eyes like chips of amber. Even dying things kept moving. The heat shimmered the horizon into a lie of water, and everything that lived on the flats — the lizards, the vultures riding thermals, the wind itself — was always going somewhere else.

Which was why the man at the Halvard Waystation bothered him before Casper even knew who he was looking at.

He'd ridden up through the basin in the gray hour before dawn, when the flats went the color of old bone and the air smelled of dust and cold stone. The waystation was a low huddle of buildings around a well — a stable, a bunkhouse, a cook-shed with its chimney breathing smoke. Ordinary. Except that a horse stood saddled at the rail, packed for travel, and no one had lit the yard lamp even though the sky was still dark enough to need it.

A man waiting to leave in the dark didn't want to be seen leaving.

Casper dismounted a quarter mile out and walked the rest, boots whispering through salt crust that broke like thin ice under each step. He'd done this a hundred times in the year since — approached a hundred fires, a hundred waystations, a hundred strangers bent over maps or mending tack — and a hundred times he'd been wrong. But there was a posture he'd learned to recognize the way a shepherd recognizes a certain gait in a wolf: shoulders slightly forward, weight on the balls of the feet, a body that never quite finished sitting down. A man who had stopped moving only because movement would betray him.

The cook-shed door stood open a hand's width. Lamplight leaked through the gap in a thin gold seam.

Casper stood in the dark beside the woodpile and listened. Inside, someone was eating — the small, unhurried sounds of a man who took his meals seriously. A spoon against a bowl. The creak of a bench. And beneath it, almost lost, a voice humming. Low, tuneless, more habit than music.

His heart did something strange then. It didn't speed up. It slowed, the way a river slows before a drop.

Because he knew that hum. Not the melody — there wasn't one — but the *habit* of it. He'd heard it across a card table in Perdition Bend, through the wall of a bathhouse in Corvo Flats, once from a ridge two hundred yards off while the man below him gutted a fish and hummed to keep himself company, alone in the middle of nowhere, the way lonely men do.

A year. Three hundred and forty days of dust and false trails and paying informants who lied to him cheerfully, and here the thief sat ten feet away, eating beans by lamplight, humming.

*Theo.*

Casper's hand found his revolver and rested there without drawing. That was the ritual now, the year-long ritual: stand in the dark, hand on the grip, and say the words silently, the way his father had taught him to say grace. *In the name of Aldous Vasquez, ranger of the Saltmark, I take you.* His father had been dead eleven years, but Casper said the words anyway, because they were the last thing his father had asked of him, and a son honors the last thing he's asked.

Then he moved.

He came through the door fast and low, shoulder leading, revolver up, and the lamplight hit him all at once — and the bench where the man should have been was empty except for a bowl still steaming, still rocking gently from the touch of a hand withdrawn not three seconds before. The back window stood open to the night, curtain lifting in the wind like something waving goodbye.

"Damn it—" Casper was through the window before the thought finished forming, landing hard in the salt, rolling, coming up with the gun leveled at the yard.

Empty. The saddled horse still stood at the rail, patient as furniture. The man had left his own horse behind, which meant —

Footsteps behind him. Casper spun.

Theo stood at the corner of the shed, half in shadow, hands loose at his sides. Up close, after a year of glimpses, he was smaller than Casper had built him in his head — lean, weathered, somewhere past thirty, with a face that might have belonged to any drifter on any road in the Saltmark. Only the eyes were particular: dark, calm, and watching Casper with what looked horribly like recognition. Like sympathy.

"You've gotten faster," Theo said. His voice was quiet, roughened by dust. "Or I've gotten slow."

"Don't." Casper leveled the revolver at his chest. His breath was coming too hard and he hated that, hated that the man could see his hands, see how badly he wanted this. "Hands where I can see them. You're under arrest for theft — the property of Aldous Vasquez, deceased, recovered to his lawful heir—"

"You say that every time." Theo didn't move. Didn't reach for the knife Casper knew was at his belt. He just stood there in the gray light with the steam of somebody else's breakfast rising behind him, and his voice carried no fear at all, only a tired kind of gentleness that made Casper want to shoot something. "Every town. Every trail. *The property of Aldous Vasquez.* You've got it written on the warrant so many times the ink's worn smooth. Don't you ever wonder why a man keeps running a year for one brass compass?"

"I don't wonder anything about you."

"That's a lie," Theo said, "and we both know it, and it's the only thing we've ever agreed on."

Casper fired — high, deliberately high, a warning crack that scattered roosting birds off the stable roof — and Theo moved at last, fluid and unhurried, sliding along the shed wall, and Casper chased him around the building's blind corner into the open yard where the pre-dawn had begun to lift, the flats emerging pale and endless in every direction, and Theo simply *stopped*. Stood in the open. Spread his arms an inch from his sides.

"No horse," he said. "No cover. One shot, ranger. Take it."

And God help him, Casper almost did. His finger was on the trigger, the sights settled on the center of that calm chest, and the whole year funneled down to this one clean moment — the arrest made, the debt paid, the shape of his life completed—

He didn't fire.

He told himself later it was procedure. Rangers brought them in alive; that was the law; his father had believed in the law the way other men believed in God. But standing there with the hammer cocked and the man offering himself like a gift, Casper knew the truth with a clarity that felt like falling: if he pulled the trigger, tomorrow morning he would wake up and there would be nothing left to chase. No trail. No warrant. No reason to be out on the flats at all, no reason to be anyone at all.

And Theo saw it. Saw the whole arithmetic happen behind Casper's eyes. Something in his face shifted — not triumph, nothing so simple. Pity, maybe. Or grief.

"There it is," Theo said softly. "There it is. Same as last time."

Then he ran — straight out onto the open flats, no cover, no plan, just long easy strides eating the white ground — and Casper stood with the gun up and let him go, tracking him down the sights, tracking him and tracking him, until the shimmer took the man the way the shimmer takes everything out here, and the flats were empty again, and Casper was alone with a cooling bowl of beans and a horse that wasn't his quarry's and a silence that pressed on him like deep water.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat in the cook-shed with the dead man's abandoned breakfast and drank the waystation's bitter coffee and turned the compass over in his hands — no. Not the compass. He'd never held the compass. He held the memory of it, which after eleven years was nearly the same thing, worn smooth as the ink on his own warrant.

His father had kept it in the top drawer of the desk in the ranger station at Dry Hollow, wrapped in oilcloth, though it was far too fine for oilcloth. Brass case, lid engraved with a rose of sixteen points, and inside the glass a needle that didn't point north. Casper had discovered that when he was nine, sneaking into the drawer while his father was out on patrol. The needle swung lazily, aimlessly, settling on nothing — until you held it near the big survey map on the wall, and then it strained, quivering, toward a blank space in the southeastern corner where the mapmaker had written, in faded ink, *unsurveyed — do not chart.*

"What is it?" Casper had asked, when his father caught him with it.

His father had taken it from him carefully — carefully, the way he handled nothing else in the world — and put it back in the drawer, and locked the drawer, and said, "It's mine. It's the only thing that's mine. Don't ask me again."

But Casper had asked again, the way sons do, over years, in a hundred small ways, and he'd pieced together what little his father would give: that the compass had come from the south, from the drowned country, from before the war that nobody in the Saltmark talked about. That it had been *saved*, his father said — saved from something. His father's face when he said that word, *saved*, had been the face of a man describing a rescue he hadn't managed for anyone else.

And then, eleven years ago, his father had died — fever, the doctor said, though Casper had always privately doubted it; rangers didn't die of fevers, they died of rivers and guns and bad luck — and the drawer had been empty. The compass gone. Taken sometime in the last weeks, by person unknown, and Casper had torn the station apart looking, had torn himself apart looking, until a year ago a fence in Perdition Bend had described a piece he'd bought and sold: a brass compass, sixteen-point rose, needle that wouldn't find north, sold to him by a soft-spoken drifter who called himself Theo.

So Casper had made a warrant with his own hands and sworn it with his father's name and gone out onto the flats.

Now, sitting in the lamplight with the thief's abandoned breakfast going cold, Casper laid it all out in front of himself the way you lay out tools before a repair, and looked at it honestly for the first time.

A year. He'd crossed the Saltmark four times. He'd spent his savings, worn out two horses, forgotten the name of the last woman who'd waited for him in Dry Hollow, and she'd been right to stop waiting. He'd caught Theo twice — twice! — and both times the man had slipped him, and both times Casper had noticed something he'd refused to look at directly: that Theo never hurt anyone. Never stole anything else. Just ran, and let himself be chased, and sometimes — Casper had started to suspect, in the black hours — sometimes made sure he *could* be chased. Left tracks a careful man wouldn't leave. Took rooms with windows facing the obvious street.

As if the chase suited him too. As if two men were keeping each other alive out here, each convinced he was the pursuer.

Casper set down the coffee. Through the open window the flats were turning pink at the eastern edge, and the wind had come up, carrying the smell of wet dust — a strange smell, a wrong smell, a smell the Saltmark almost never gave. He frowned at it without understanding.

*Don't you ever wonder why a man keeps running a year for one brass compass?*

He wondered it now. He wondered it with the force of a man stepping off a ledge. Because the honest answer, the answer that had been waiting for him at the bottom of every empty trail and every wasted month, was that he didn't care about the compass. Hadn't cared, maybe, since the day he'd first seen the thief's face. The compass was a key that fit a lock in himself, and the lock was this: that chasing Theo was the last duty his father had ever given him, and as long as the chase went on, Casper Vasquez was still somebody's son, still a ranger's heir, still a man with a shape to his life. Catch the thief, and the shape collapsed. Let him go, and it collapsed slower.

Either way, he'd already lost. He'd just been refusing to read the ground.

Outside, the wind strengthened, and somewhere far off to the southeast — toward the blank corner of the map, toward the *unsurveyed* — thunder muttered, low and long, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Rain, out here, in the dry season. Rain on the salt flats.

Casper stood up slowly, every joint aching, and looked east at the bruise-colored clouds stacking on the horizon, and felt — though he couldn't have said why — that the world had just tilted a few degrees, the way a needle swings when you bring iron near it.

Whatever was coming off those flats, it was coming for him.
