## 2. What the River Gives Up

The rain came out of a sky that had been empty for eleven months.

Casper felt it before he saw it — a pressure change in his back teeth, the smell of wet stone rising from ground that hadn't known water since the last dry season. He was three days out from the waystation, still tasting the dust of Theo's escape, when the first drops hit the salt crust around him and hissed like something scalded.

By dusk it was a wall of water.

He heard it coming up the arroyo — not a sound so much as a presence, a low grinding roar like the earth clearing its throat. He'd grown up on flood stories; every ranger did. The desert didn't take rain, it took revenge. He ran for high ground with the water chasing him, and he made it — barely — to the shoulder of a hill where an old stone structure crouched against the dark, half-swallowed by centuries of silt.

A chapel. Or what had been a chapel once, before the river changed its mind about where it wanted to live. Casper could see the arch of a doorway breaking the floodwater's surface like a drowning man's hand, and above it, a bell tower leaning at an angle that defied gravity through sheer stubbornness.

He climbed inside as the world below turned to brown chaos.

* * *

For two days the chapel was an island.

Casper sat in the bell tower with his bedroll and his dwindling rations and watched the flood do its work. It tore fences out of the earth and sent them spinning. It lifted a wagon whole and rolled it over like a child playing with a toy. It moved boulders the size of houses and sang to itself while it did it, a deep subterranean hum that Casper felt in his spine more than heard.

He thought about Theo.

He always thought about Theo. That was the problem. A year ago, the man had been a name on a warrant — *the thief Vasquez, one Theodore, wanted for grand theft* — and Casper had been a ranger with a purpose, clean and simple as a drawn line. Now the name was a shape carved into the inside of his skull. He knew the way Theo walked, light on the outside of his feet. He knew the way he camped, fire small enough to hide under a hat brim. He knew the way he looked back over his shoulder when he thought no one was watching — not afraid, exactly. *Checking.* Like a man keeping accounts.

Casper had nearly caught him at the waystation. Had been close enough to see the stubble on his jaw, the gray coming in early at his temples. Close enough to hear him say, almost gently, as he slipped the rope: *"You don't even know what you're carrying, ranger."*

Then gone, into the dark, with the compass.

His father's compass.

Casper turned the loss over for the thousandth time and found nothing new in it. Brass case, worn smooth at the edges where a thumb would rest. The needle that didn't point north — had never pointed north, though his father had sworn it worked fine, *just needs a steady hand, mijo*. A crack across the crystal face like a frozen river. His father's last gift, and Theo had taken it off his father's grave-marker, of all the low, godless things—

No. Not the grave-marker. Casper corrected himself, as he always did. The memorial case in the ranger station, glass-fronted, ceremonial. Still a violation. Still a wound.

The rain thinned on the second night. By dawn the water was going down, peeling back off the land in long brown sheets, leaving everything slicked and gleaming and rearranged.

That was when the chapel gave up its dead.

* * *

The receding water exposed them one by one, the way a tide exposes shells: a fence post here, a foundation stone there, and then — at the base of the bell tower, where the silt had been scoured away in a great fan — a flight of steps leading down.

Down. Into the earth. Toward whatever the chapel had been built over.

Casper told himself he was checking for structural damage. Told himself a flooded foundation was a hazard and a ranger's duty was assessment. He was lying, and some part of him — the part that had spent a year learning to read another man's tracks — knew he was lying. But knowing didn't stop his boots from finding the steps.

They went down farther than they should have. The walls were stone, older than any masonry the settlements used, and carved — he realized as his lantern-light caught them — with lines. Not decoration. *Writing*, or something like it, flowing left to right in characters that looped and knotted like waterweed. The same script, he understood with a slow cold turn of his stomach, that was inlaid in brass on the back of his father's compass. The script he'd spent his childhood being told was just *old family markings, nothing, mijo, eat your supper.*

At the bottom of the stairs stood a door, swollen shut by decades of mud. The flood had done his work for him; the frame had shifted, and the door hung open by a hand's width. Casper squeezed through into a room that smelled of river-bottom and old iron.

It had been a crypt, maybe. Or a vault. The ceiling was low and ribbed like the inside of a boat, and along one wall ran shelves, and on the shelves sat the thing the water had uncovered: a chest, black wood bound in green-bronze bands, sitting in a puddle of drained silt like something freshly born.

Stamped into the lid was a seal: a circle broken by a wavy line, crossed beneath by a needle-shaped blade.

Casper knew the seal. Every child in the territories knew it, the way they knew ghost stories. It was on the warning plaques at the old ferry crossings. *DANGER — UNSURVEYED WATERS — FORMER VESSIAN CHANNELS.* Vessia. The drowned nation. The country that wasn't a country anymore, erased from the maps so thoroughly that schoolchildren learned the word only as a synonym for *gone*.

His father had helped erase it. Casper had grown up on those stories too — proud ones, campfire ones. *Your old man rode with the survey crews, mijo. Drew the maps that put the river country right after the Vessian wars. Good honest work.*

Good honest work. Casper stood in the dark with his heart going strange and slow, and reached for the chest.

The lock was bronze and it gave without a fight, as if it had been waiting a century for someone to ask. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that crumbled at his touch, lay papers. Charts. Dozens of them, ink faded to the color of weak tea but legible, and every one of them was a map of the river channels — not the channels as the empire's maps drew them now, but as they had *been*, a labyrinth of deep-water routes threading between settlements that no longer existed. Salvage charts. Marked, in that same looping script, with depths and bearings and tide-tables for waters that were supposed to be dry.

And beneath the charts, fitted into a recess cut into the chest's floor, a hollow.

Casper went very still.

It was a shape he knew better than his own face. An oval, worn-smooth at the edges where a thumb would rest. A shallow channel for a hinge. A notch for a latch. A space exactly, precisely, perfectly the size and shape of a brass compass with a cracked crystal face.

The compass his father had carried. The compass his father had sworn was a family heirloom, *older than the wars, mijo, older than anything*. The compass Theo had stolen, and Casper had chased him a year for stealing.

It wasn't an heirloom. It was a key. It belonged here, in this hollow, in this chest, in this drowned vault under a chapel in a nation his father had helped drown. Someone had cut this recess to hold it. Someone had built these charts around it, because the compass was how you read them — the needle that didn't point north, the *steady hand*. It pointed at salvage. It pointed at the Amaranth, if the fragmentary legends were true, the great Vessian vessel lost with a nation's records aboard. It pointed at everything the maps had been redrawn to bury.

And his father had taken it out. Had carried it away. Had lied about it for thirty years and handed the lie to his son like an inheritance.

Casper sat down slowly on the flooded floor, not caring that the water soaked through to the bone. The lantern guttered. Somewhere above, the river kept moving, indifferent, rearranging the world.

*You don't even know what you're carrying, ranger.*

Theo had known. Theo had known what the compass was, and where it came from, and what it opened — and Casper had spent a year calling him *thief* while the man tried to tell him otherwise. Tried to tell him at the waystation, gentle as a confession, and Casper had answered with a rope.

He laughed, once, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle like the compass crystal. Grief, maybe. Or fury. He couldn't find the seam between them anymore.

Because here was the vertigo of it, the thing that made the flooded room tilt around him: if the compass was a key, then the theft was not a theft. And if the theft was not a theft, then the chase was not justice. And if the chase was not justice, then Casper Vasquez — twelve years a ranger, his father's son, honest work, good honest work — had spent a year of his one life pursuing a lie his father had built brick by brick and left to him like a house.

He gathered the charts with hands that almost shook. Almost. He folded them into his coat, against his chest, where they lay heavy as a second heartbeat.

Above, the sun was climbing, and the desert was already forgetting the rain. Somewhere out there, a man who walked light on the outside of his feet was carrying the key to all of this, running from a law that had no claim on him.

Casper climbed the stairs out of the vault. At the top he paused, looking south, toward the river forks, toward the dying channels, toward the six tides he didn't yet know were coming.

He wasn't chasing a thief anymore.

He was chasing the truth — and for the first time in a year, he hoped to God he wouldn't catch it alone.
