## 3. Six Tides

The old man was dying the way the river died — not all at once, but in channels, one vein of him closing after another until what remained ran thin and shallow over the stones.

Casper found him in a reed hut on the last habitable bend of the Vessa, where the water went from brown to bronze to something like glass. The settlements here had no name anymore; the maps Casper's father had taught him to read called this stretch *unproductive alluvial flats*, as if a hundred years of lives could be summarized by sediment. But smoke rose from three chimneys, and smoke meant people, and people meant someone who remembered.

He carried the salvager inside on a woven mat and set him near the brazier, and the man's eyes — milk-pale, river-cataracted — found Casper's face anyway, the way blind things find warmth.

"You have his jaw," the old man said. "Vasquez. The younger one."

Casper's hand went to his belt before he could stop it. A year of chasing a thief had made reflexes of him. "You knew my father."

"Everyone on this river knew your father." The salvager coughed, wet and long, and when he finished he smiled with too few teeth. "Some of us knew him better than he'd have liked."

* * *

His name was Odilon, though he said names were like boats — useful only until they leaked. He had been a salvage diver before the empire's dredges came, before the Vessian wrecks were declared imperial property and the men who knew where they lay were declared trespassers on their own grandmothers' graves. Now he lay in his granddaughter's hut with water on his lungs that would never leave, listening to the river he could no longer enter.

Casper had come for information about Theo. That was the story he told himself on the walk down from the drowned chapel: the thief would come here eventually, because the chest bore Vessia's seal and thieves follow seals the way jackals follow blood. Casper would wait. Casper would take back what was stolen. Simple as a snare.

But Odilon did not want to talk about thieves.

"Show me the compass," he said.

"I don't have it."

"No." The pale eyes didn't blink. "But you've held it. You dream about it. I can see it in how you sit — like a man missing a limb, guarding the space where it was." He gestured with a hand gone translucent at the edges. "My grandfather dove the Amaranth. Before she sank, I mean. He was her channel-master. He used to say the river keeps its promises the way a drunkard does — eventually, and never quite the way you asked."

"What is the Amaranth?"

Odilon laughed, and the laugh turned into the cough again, and Casper waited through it, watching the firelight move on the old man's skin like current over sand.

"The pride of the Vessian fleet," Odilon said at last. "Her hull was sheathed in green copper, and when she came upriver the whole valley turned the color of new leaves. She carried the crown records out of the capital when the empire's guns reached the harbor — every deed, every lineage, every name of every family the maps were about to erase. The plan was to run her into the high marshes and hide her until the war turned." His voice dropped, went reverent and bitter at once. "The war did not turn. And she did not reach the marshes."

"She sank."

"She was *sunk*. There is a difference, ranger's son, and your father knew it better than anyone." Odilon turned his head, and for a moment the cataracts seemed to clear, as if the river behind them had briefly surfaced. "The empire couldn't catch her in open water. So they bought the pilot of the inner channels. Paid him in gold and in a name — a clean name, an imperial name, a name with no drowned nation hanging off it. He ran her onto the shoals at Deadman's Reach and lit the signal fires, and the fleet came down on her like gulls on a gutted fish. They took what was portable. They left the records in the hold, because paper means nothing to men who have already decided history."

Casper's mouth had gone dry. Outside, the river made its endless small sounds against the reeds, patient as a witness.

"And the pilot," Casper said. Each word cost more than the last. "Who was he?"

Odilon smiled again, and it was the kindest expression Casper had ever been hurt by.

"You already know. You've known since the chapel. Why do you think you came all this way down a dying river carrying a hollow-shaped question?" He closed his eyes. "Your father sold Vessia, boy. And the compass you're mourning — that wasn't his. It was taken off a dead woman's body at the Reach. A navigator's key. It doesn't point north. It points *home* — to whatever it was keyed to last. And it was keyed to the Amaranth."

* * *

They talked through the afternoon while the light went from gold to copper to ash. Odilon's granddaughter brought salt tea and flatbread and said nothing, but she watched Casper the way people watch weather they've decided not to trust.

Here was what the old man gave him, in pieces, between coughs:

The Amaranth did not lie still. The lower Vessa shifted its channels every season, and the wreck traveled with them — buried, uncovered, buried again — so that no chart older than a year was worth the vellum. Except one instrument. The compass, cut from Vessian lodestone by navigators who had learned the river's moods across ten generations, would hold true to the wreck the way a hound holds true to blood. Wherever she drifted under the silt, the needle's dark end would strain toward her.

And she surfaced. Once a year, when the dry season bottomed out and the river dropped to its bones, the lowest tides pulled the water off the shoal country for a few hours at a time. Six tides, the last six of the season. On those six, divers had touched her deck. On those six, anything was possible.

"This year," Odilon said, "you will not be alone down there."

"The fleet." Casper had heard the rumors upriver, half-disbelieved. Salvage barges flying the imperial double-eagle, working their way down from the delta.

"Captain Ilsa Rehn." Odilon pronounced the name the way other men spat. "She stripped the sunken treasury at Corvane in nine days. She stripped the temple barges at Selm. She has charts your father helped draw, licenses signed by men who signed away Vessia, and two hundred divers who are paid by the crate. When the tides turn, she'll be at Deadman's Reach. And everything in that hold — every name, every deed, every proof that we ever existed — goes north in crates stamped with eagles, and Vessia dies twice."

The brazier popped. Somewhere outside, a bird cried once over the water and got no answer.

"Why tell me?" Casper asked. "You knew my father. You know what he was. Why give this to his son?"

Odilon was quiet a long time. The light failed by degrees, and his granddaughter lit a stub of tallow candle, and its flame stood perfectly straight in the windless hut.

"Because you sat with a dying man all afternoon instead of riding past," he said finally. "Because you flinched when I said *sold*, which means you didn't know, which means your father kept you clean of it, which means some part of him was ashamed. And because—" the old breath caught, held, released— "because the compass isn't yours and it isn't mine. It belonged to a woman named Maren Oduya, who piloted the Amaranth's escape and died at the Reach holding it. Her blood is still on this river. If anyone should carry it to the wreck, it should be someone who understands it was never a trophy."

Casper thought of Theo then — the flash of him at the waystation, a year of glimpses, always one ridge ahead, always moving like a man following instructions only he could hear. A thief who stole a compass and never sold it. Who never pawned it, never traded it, though Casper had seen him hungry enough to eat boiled leather. Who carried it the way Casper himself had carried the memory of it.

*He wasn't stealing it,* Casper thought, and the thought landed like a dropped anchor. *He was keeping it.*

"When do the tides begin?" he asked.

"Four days. First tide at dawn on the fourth." Odilon's hand found Casper's wrist, grip surprisingly strong, a diver's grip, built for rope and cold water. "Deadman's Reach is six days' hard ride by the east bluffs. By the river, with the channels failing—" he shook his head— "no one makes it in four. No one but the man who holds Maren Oduya's key."

"I don't hold it."

"Then you'd best find the man who does." The pale eyes fixed on him, unerring. "And you'd best decide, before you do, whether you're going to arrest him or ask him. Because the fleet will not ask."

* * *

Casper walked out into the blue dusk and stood at the bank where the reeds broke the current into threads. Downstream, the river widened toward the shoal country, toward Deadman's Reach, toward a ship full of ghosts and a captain full of eagles. Behind him, upstream, the waystations and bluffs and salt flats stretched back a year — a year of tracking a man whose crime, it now appeared, was inheritance.

He took out the charcoal rubbing he'd made in the chapel, the seal of Vessia, the hollow shaped exactly like the thing missing from his own life. His father's face rose in memory: steady, honorable, the man who taught him to read water and to keep his word. Every lesson now had a second ledger written beneath it.

*I built you a name,* his father might have said. *I paid for it. Isn't that what fathers do?*

The first stars came out over the water. Four days. Six tides. One thief somewhere ahead on this same river, moving toward the same wreck for reasons that were, Casper was beginning to suspect, better than his own.

He went back for his horse. In the hut doorway, Odilon's granddaughter stopped him with a look.

"He says to tell you," she said, "the man you're looking for passed through here two days ago. Bought bandages and dried fish. Heading downstream." She paused. "He paid with a compass bearing. Drew it in the dirt for my grandfather and wouldn't say why."

Casper stood very still.

Downstream. Toward the Reach. Ahead of Rehn, ahead of the tides, ahead of him — but only just.

He swung into the saddle, and the river ran dark beside him all night, going the same direction, fast.
