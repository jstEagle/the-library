## 4. The Thief's Version

Theo built his fire small, the way his grandmother had taught him — three stones and a handful of dry tamarisk, just enough to warm the hands without announcing itself to the dark. She used to say a fire was a letter written to everyone who could see it, so you'd best know what you wanted the letter to say. *I am here. I am careless. Come take what's mine.*

He wanted his letter to say nothing at all.

The river ran somewhere below him in the dark, a sound like slow breathing. He'd made good distance from the waystation, put the salt flats behind him by noon, climbed into the broken country where the mesas leaned together like old men sharing secrets. The ranger was back there somewhere — Vasquez, the son, with his father's jaw and his father's hunger — and Theo had learned over a year of running that the man did not tire the way other men tired. He pursued the way water pursued low ground. Patiently. Inevitably. Without malice and without mercy.

Theo turned the compass in his fingers.

It was a beautiful thing even now, even scuffed, even with the glass clouded where a thumb had rubbed it ten thousand times across ten thousand years of weather. Brass the color of late honey. A rose engraved on the lid — not decoration, his grandmother had told him once, in the last year of her life, when her voice had gone thin as onion skin. *A compass rose, mijo, but also a rose, because Vessia loved its river the way a bride loves a groom.* Inside, beneath the needle, a second ring of markings no mapmaker alive could read: channels that moved, depths that breathed, the secret grammar of a drowned nation.

His grandmother had held it on her lap the evening she told him, her hands folded over it like a birdkeeper's.

"They took everything," she said. "But they couldn't take it out of me. So they took this instead, and thought that was the end of it."

*They* meant the rangers. *They* meant the empire. *They* meant, though she never said the name with anything but tenderness, Casper Vasquez's father — Aldous Vasquez, First Ranger, the man whose signature sat at the bottom of the edicts that erased Vessia from every chart, every ledger, every memory the empire permitted itself to keep.

Theo had been nineteen when she died, and he had taken the only inheritance she had left to give: the truth, and the long grief that came with it. The compass itself he'd taken later, from the Vasquez house, from a case above a dead man's hearth where it sat like a trophy. He'd told himself it was theft. His grandmother would have called it repatriation. The distinction mattered less each year; what mattered was that he still had it, and that somewhere behind him a man was spending his whole life trying to take it back for reasons Theo was only beginning to suspect were lies.

The fire ticked. Somewhere upslope, a stone clicked against another stone.

Theo went still.

Not wind. Wind didn't choose its footing. He killed the fire with two fingers of sand, closed his hand around the compass, and listened — and heard it now, the soft wrongness of men moving carefully, two of them, maybe three, coming down through the rocks with the particular silence of people paid to be quiet.

He was moving before the thought finished forming, rolling his bedroll off the ledge and down into the shadow of a fallen slab, and that was what saved him, because a moment later a lantern flared where he'd been sitting and a crossbow bolt buried itself in the sand where his chest had been.

"Thief!" The voice was a woman's, hard as boot leather. "Captain Rehn wants a word."

Theo came up running.

They were on him fast — three shapes resolving out of the dark, fleet colors at their throats, the grey-and-crimson of the salvage companies that worked the dying river like carrion birds work a carcass. The woman again, tall, a net-iron cudgel in one hand. A big man with a hooked pole sweeping low to catch his ankles. And a third, younger, hanging back with a lantern and a look on his face like he hadn't yet learned to enjoy this work.

Theo enjoyed it enough for all of them. He'd been ambushed before; he'd been ambushed by professionals. You didn't survive a year of Casper Vasquez by being easy to corner. He went under the hooked pole, let its owner's momentum carry him past, and put his shoulder into the big man's knee on the way — heard the wet pop of something giving, heard the roar — and then the woman was there, cudgel whistling, and he caught it on his forearm and felt the bone sing and nearly went down.

The compass. That was all they wanted. He could feel it in how they moved, the way they angled, always angling toward his left hand where the brass rode inside his coat. Not robbers. Not killers, not yet. Salvagers, doing salvage math: one thief, one artifact, minimal damage to the merchandise.

"The captain knows what you're carrying," the woman said, circling. "She knows what it opens. Give it up and walk away rich, little ghost. Keep it and drown with it."

"You have me confused," Theo said, backing along the slab, blood ticking warm down his forearm, "with someone who trusts a captain."

"We burned your little camp," she said. "We'll burn more. There's nowhere on this river—"

He threw the sand.

It wasn't much — a handful scraped from the slab's edge, thrown high across the lantern light — but the young one flinched, the woman blinked, and a blink was a country wide to a man who'd spent his life in doorways and drainpipes and the space between one heartbeat and the next. Theo went over the slab's far side into darkness, took the drop in a roll that lit his shoulder up like a struck match, and ran.

They came after him. Of course they came after him. But he knew this country the way he knew his grandmother's kitchen — every goat track, every dry wash, every place the rock overhung enough to swallow a lantern's reach — and by the time the moon stood two hours higher, the sounds behind him had thinned to nothing, and he was crouched in a slot canyon with his heart slamming and his arm screaming, listening to the river breathe below.

*There's nowhere on this river,* she'd said.

Which meant Rehn knew about the wreck. Which meant Rehn knew about the six tides. Which meant the race Theo had been running alone for a year had just acquired a fleet.

He laughed, quietly, because the alternative was something less useful.

* * *

Dawn found him two miles downstream, washed and bound and miserable, sitting on a shelf of red rock above the water with his sleeve cut open and his forearm splinted from his own supplies. The break wasn't clean but it wasn't bad. He'd had worse. He'd had worse from falling off his grandmother's roof at eleven years old, chasing a heron she swore was an omen.

He thought about her a great deal, lately. More than usual. It was the ranger's fault — the son's face wore the father's face, and every time Theo looked at it he saw the man from the stories, Aldous Vasquez, standing in his grandmother's doorway with the empire's paper in his hand.

She'd told him about that too, near the end. How the rangers had come to the Vessian quarter in the spring of the drowning year, going door to door with lists. How they'd collected heirlooms "for cataloguing" and returned ash. How her own mother had put the compass in a bread loaf and sent her to market, and how the bread had come home uneaten and the compass had stayed hidden under floorboards for thirty years while Vessia became a rumor and then a lie and then nothing at all.

"And then?" Theo had asked, aged nine, legs swinging.

"And then a ranger found it anyway," she said. "A patient one. He didn't arrest anyone. He just took it, and smiled, and said it was evidence." Her hands had tightened on the brass until her knuckles went white. "Evidence, mijo. Of what? Of us. We were the crime, do you understand? Existing was the crime. And he carried our existence home over his fireplace like a deer's head."

Theo had grown up on those stories the way other children grew up on saints' lives. He had believed, for most of his life, that the ranger's son must know — must carry the same knowledge like a coal in the chest, must wake some nights choking on it. It had taken a year of pursuit to teach him otherwise. Vasquez chased him with the pure, unclouded fury of a man avenging a theft he believed real. Nobody had ever told him what his father was. Nobody had ever shown him the second ring of markings, or said the word *Vessia* in his hearing except as a curse.

They were both orphans of the same lie, Theo thought. The difference was that Theo had been raised on the truth and stolen toward it, while Vasquez had been raised on the lie and was chasing it — chasing *him* — with his dead father's compass-shaped hole in his heart, and no idea that the hole had been dug by the very man whose memory he was defending.

It should have been funny. Mostly it was sad. Sadness, his grandmother said, was just anger that had learned to swim.

Below him, the river ran brown and swollen with the season's last melt, carrying silt from country neither of them would ever see. Somewhere down that water lay the Amaranth, his grandmother's nation in timber and iron, surfacing on the sixth tide like a drowned man's last breath. Somewhere upstream, a fleet was making ready. And somewhere behind him — closer than yesterday, closer every day — a man who hated him for reasons that were about to stop being true was following the only trail he had left.

Theo looked at the compass lying in his lap, its needle swinging lazy and certain toward a bearing no living chart acknowledged.

The scouts had known what it opened. Rehn knew. Which meant the secret was rotting loose in the world, and a secret that rots draws scavengers, and scavengers draw —

Hoofbeats. Faint, upstream, single rider, moving fast along the benchland trail.

Theo knew the rhythm of those hoofbeats the way he knew his own pulse. A year of listening had taught him. He stood, wincing, and watched a dust plume rise against the morning sun, and felt the strange double weight settle over him that had been settling for weeks now — half dread, half something he refused to name.

The ranger was coming. And for the first time since this chase began, Theo found he didn't want to run.

He wanted to talk.
