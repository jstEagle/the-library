## 5. Partners of Necessity

Casper found him at the fork of the Alder and the Gray, where the water split like a sentence interrupted mid-thought. Theo had made camp on the shingle bar between the channels — a fire no bigger than a cupped hand, a bedroll already rolled, everything staged for a departure that could come at any sound. A man who had been running for a year learned to sleep in pieces.

Casper had the drop on him. He'd crept the last half-mile through the tamarisk with his boots in his hands, and now he stood ten paces off with his rifle leveled and the river talking softly on either side, and he felt nothing. That was the strange part. He'd imagined this moment a hundred times — the lunge, the tackle, the cuffs, the long ride back to the fort with his father's compass riding in Theo's coat pocket and Casper's name finally restored to him like a document returned from some distant office. In all those imaginings he'd felt triumphant. Now he only felt tired, and a little sick, and aware of how thin the man looked across the fire.

Theo didn't startle. He'd known, probably, since the tamarisk. He turned his head slowly, firelight catching the hollows under his eyes, and said, "You look worse than I do."

"Hands where I can see them."

"They're where you can see them." He held them up, palms out, fingers spread. "You going to shoot me, ranger? Over breakfast?"

"I'm not a ranger."

The words came out before he'd decided to say them, and they hung there over the fire smoke, and Theo's eyebrows went up a fraction — not mockery, something more careful than that.

"No," Theo said quietly. "I don't suppose you are. Not anymore."

* * *

They sat across the fire because it was easier to hold a knife conversation sitting down. Casper kept his rifle across his knees; Theo kept his hands visible and his knife sheathed at his hip, which was its own kind of message. The river ran past them in two voices, the Alder high and brown with the flood's leavings, the Gray low and clear, and between the two voices the shingle bar was an island that belonged to neither channel.

"You read the chest," Theo said. It wasn't a question. He'd seen it somehow — in the way Casper carried himself, maybe, in the week since the drowned chapel. "At the chapel."

"How do you know about the chest?"

"Because you stopped chasing me like a man avenging his father and started chasing me like a man following a map." Theo fed a twig to the fire. "There's a difference. The first one looks at your face. The second one keeps looking at your pocket."

Casper's hand went to his coat before he could stop it. The compass rode there against his ribs, warm from his body, its needle swinging slow circles when he moved. Theo watched the hand go and nodded once, as if a debt had been confirmed.

"Tell me about her," Casper said.

"My grandmother?"

"Your grandmother."

Theo was quiet for a while. Out on the Gray a heron stood in the shallows, still as a post, waiting for the water to give it something.

"Her name was Maren Vess," he said at last. "Not Vessian — nobody says the whole word anymore, it sounds like a story. She was born on the Amaranth, third berth off the galley, in the year the empire started calling the river theirs. Her father ran cargo. Legitimate cargo, mostly." A dry twist to his mouth. "When the fleet burned, she was eleven. She swam out with the salvage charts sewn into her jacket, because her mother told her: whatever else drowns, the charts swim. And the key to the charts—" he tapped his own chest, where the compass used to ride against his ribs for twenty years before Casper's father took it back, "—the key was already gone. Stolen three nights before, by a man who came aboard wearing a surveyor's coat and a smile."

"Vasquez," Casper said. His father's name in his mouth tasted like silt.

"Emil Vasquez," Theo agreed. "Who then spent forty years building a legend about the honest ranger who lost his compass to thieves, so that no one would ever ask why a surveyor needed a Vessian salvage key in the first place. So that his son would grow up hunting the wrong crime." He looked up, and his eyes were steady and very tired. "I didn't steal from you, Casper. I reclaimed. There's a difference too."

Theo's POV

He watched the boy take it — because that's what he was, this Vasquez, a boy grown tall around an empty place — and Theo knew exactly what the taking cost, because he'd taken the same blow years ago from his grandmother's deathbed stories. It broke something in a man, learning his inheritance was a costume. Theo had spent thirty years deciding what to do with the break. He'd chosen theft, and spite, and a long cold orbit around the family that had erased his mother's mother. He wasn't sure yet what the boy would choose, and that uncertainty was the most dangerous thing on the shingle bar.

"You could arrest me," Theo offered, when the silence had gone on long enough. "That's still an option. Drag me to the fort, hand over the compass, get your name back. Rehn's scouts would write you a commendation themselves — they've been on my trail since the chapel, did you know? Three of them, gray slickers, working downriver. They want the key for the fleet. Your father's employers."

"I know. I've seen their sign."

"Then you know what happens if you arrest me. The compass goes to Rehn, Rehn strips the Amaranth to the rivets, and every name in the crown records goes into an empire ledger and dies there. Maren Vess becomes a line item." He leaned forward. The firelight put shadows in his face like watermarks. "Or you do the harder arithmetic."

"Which is?"

"We go together. Sixth tide. We reach the wreck before the fleet does, we take the records, and we give them to the river people — the settlements along the lower Gray, the ones with Vess blood in them who don't even know it. You get the truth about your father. I get my grandmother's name cleared. Neither of us gets our old story back, because neither of our old stories was true." He spread his hands. "That's the trade."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you shoot me, or you don't, and either way I've slept worse than this bar."

Casper almost smiled. It was a small thing, quickly gone, but Theo catalogued it — the first expression he'd seen on the man's face in a year that wasn't hunger or hate.

* * *

Casper's POV

The negotiation took until the fire burned down to coals, and it was conducted the way men trade knives: slowly, edge first, each truth laid on the shingle like a blade offered hilt-outward and trusted not at all.

Casper gave him the chapel. Every detail — the seal on the chest, the hollow shaped like the compass, the salvage charts with their channels marked in a hand that might have been his father's. He watched Theo's face while he told it and saw the thief's certainty harden into something like grief, and understood that Theo had hoped, somewhere, to be wrong.

Theo gave him the scouts. Names, numbers, the signal marks they chalked on stones — a gray slash for sighted, a circle for cornered. He gave him the route the fleet would run and the day Rehn's barges would pass the trading post at Marrow Bend. And then, when Casper pushed, he gave him the hardest thing he had:

"Why didn't you ever sell it?" Casper asked. "A salvage key like that. You could have bought a province."

"Because it isn't mine to sell." Theo said it flatly, without performance. "It's hers. I'm just the hand it lives in until the wreck comes up. My grandmother made me swear on her deathbed — not to use it, not to spend it. To return it." He looked at the fire. "I've been returning it for thirty years. I keep failing, because every road to the Amaranth runs through a Vasquez."

The words landed harder than any accusation. Casper found he couldn't look at the man. He looked instead at the two rivers — the Alder running high and blind with flood-water, the Gray running clear and low over its stones — and thought about how a river forks and both branches still believe they carry the same water.

"If we do this," Casper said, "there are terms."

"Name them."

"The records go to the river people. All of them. Not sold, not traded, not held back for leverage. If I find out you've kept so much as a page—"

"You'll hunt me again," Theo said. "I'd expect nothing less. It's the only skill you have left."

It should have stung. What stung was that it was true, and that Theo said it without cruelty, the way you state the weather.

"And my term," Theo said. "When this is done — if we live — you let me walk. No arrest, no reckoning, no legend. The thief Theo drowns in the sixth tide and never surfaces. Whatever name I take after that, it's mine."

Casper thought about a year of salt flats. A year of sleeping in pieces, of measuring his life in the distance between himself and another man. He thought about the waystation, and the flash flood, and the hollow in the velvet where the compass fit like a hand in a glove, and his father's voice saying *we are rangers, Casper, we keep things* — when all along what his father had kept was stolen.

"All right," Casper said. "On the sixth tide, Theo drowns."

They didn't shake on it. Men like these two didn't shake; the word was the bond and the knife was the notary. Theo kicked dirt over the fire, and the dark came down over the shingle bar, and the two rivers went on talking past each other in the black.

They broke camp before dawn, moving down the Gray together, and it was Theo who spotted the chalk mark on the boundary stone first — a fresh gray slash, still damp, no more than a day old.

"They're ahead of us," Theo said softly.

Casper looked downriver, toward Marrow Bend, toward the sea, toward the six tides that were already counting down somewhere beyond the reed flats. "Then we'd better be faster," he said, "than anything my father ever built."
