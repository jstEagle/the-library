## 6. The Drowned Archive

The archive had been a lighthouse once, before the river took the coast.

Theo saw it first as a broken tooth rising from the brown water, half a league off the channel's edge — a cylinder of black stone furred with silt and pale weed, its lantern room collapsed inward like a skull caved at the brow. The Vessians had built it to burn over the shallows for ships coming home. Now it burned nothing, and the water that drowned it was the same water Casper's father's fleet had made.

He didn't say that. Not yet. He said, "We dive at slack water. An hour, maybe less."

Casper stood at the bow of the borrowed skiff with his arms crossed against the morning chill, watching the tower as if it might watch him back. He hadn't slept. Theo could tell by the way he held his jaw, like a man keeping something behind his teeth through sheer force. Since the fork of the river, since the knives and the negotiation and the uneasy arithmetic of two men who hated each other's fathers agreeing to row in the same boat, Casper had spoken maybe forty words, and Theo had counted them.

"Slack water," Casper repeated. "And if the fleet—"

"The fleet is two days behind us. Rehn doesn't know about this place." Theo shipped the oars and let the skiff drift. "My grandmother did. She wrote it in the margin of her prayer book, in Vessian script, small enough to hide in a psalm. *The light remembers.* That's all she wrote. I spent six years figuring out what it meant."

"And what does it mean?"

Theo looked at the drowned tower, at the way the current combed around its base in long silver threads. "It means the Vessians didn't trust paper on land. Paper burns. Paper gets confiscated, gets read by magistrates, gets used to hang people. So they sank their records. Sealed them in lead and wax and set them where only someone who knew the channels could reach them." He tapped the compass where it hung inside his coat, against his breastbone, warm from his skin. "That thing isn't just a salvage key. It's a liturgy. It points the way down."

Casper's face did something complicated. Theo watched him try to make it flat and failed.

They anchored in the lee of the tower, where the current slackened into a slow green murk. Theo stripped to his linen shirt and bound his knife to his calf, then wrapped the compass in oilcloth and knotted it to his chest harness so it rode over his heart. Casper watched every motion with the particular attention of a man cataloguing evidence, and Theo let him, because there was no point pretending anymore what either of them was.

"You go first," Casper said. "I'll be right behind you."

"No," Theo said. "You'll be exactly behind me. One arm's length. If we get separated down there, you follow the compass line, not your instincts. Your instincts are a ranger's, and rangers drown in rivers because they think rivers work like deserts." He held Casper's eyes. "Water doesn't forgive, Vasquez. It just keeps what it takes."

Something moved behind Casper's eyes — the memory, maybe, of a father who'd taught him the desert's forgiveness was equally thin. He nodded once.

The water took them cold, then colder, then dark.

* * *

Down was a direction Theo's body understood before his mind agreed to it. Pressure closed on his ears; he worked his jaw, blew against pinched nostrils, felt them pop open into pain. The green light thinned above them like a ceiling being painted shut. Below, the tower's base spread out into foundations, and the foundations into rooms — the lighthouse had been more than a light. It had been a vault with a lamp on top, a library disguised as navigation, and the Vessians had sunk their whole memory into its cellar.

The compass pulled.

Not north. Down and slightly east, tugging at the oilcloth like a fish on a line, and when Theo unknotted it underwater and held it out, the needle swung through its arc and settled, quivering, toward a doorway choked with river clay. Through the doorway: a corridor. Along the corridor: niches, dozens of them, and in each niche a lead case the size of a bread loaf, sealed with black wax stamped with the seal he knew better than his own heartbeat — the ship and the seven stars of Vessia.

His grandmother had sewn that seal into his first coat. He'd worn it until the thread rotted, never knowing it was a country.

He heard Casper's breathing change behind him — the quick shallow rhythm of awe or dread, hard to tell apart at depth. They worked the nearest case free together, and it came loose with a sigh of disturbed silt, heavy as sin, and they carried it up through the green silence into the shocking white afternoon.

* * *

They got three cases up before the air ran thin and the shivering started. Theo built a fire on the tower's exposed foundation shelf, a hump of black stone just breaking the surface, and they sat hip to hip for the warmth neither would admit to needing while the lead cases steamed dry.

Casper broke the first seal. His hands were steady — ranger's hands, rope and trigger hands — but Theo saw the tremor start in his forearms and climb.

The documents inside were wrapped in waxed linen, and the ink had survived the decades the way ink survives when it's been written by people who expected to be betrayed: deep, dense, permanent. Shipping manifests. Fleet registries. And then a page headed with a date Theo had grown up hearing whispered like a curse — the night the Amaranth went down, the night the river swallowed the last Vessian warships rather than let the empire take them.

Casper read it aloud, because he had to, because some things need a voice to become real.

"*On the orders of Ranger-Commander Emeric Vasquez, the channel buoys were removed under cover of dark. The Amaranth and her consorts, drawing too deep to pass without them, grounded on the shoals at Gray Teeth. Fire arrows followed.*"

He stopped.

Theo didn't move. He'd known. He'd carried the knowledge for years like a stone in his boot — his grandmother's stories, the names, the shape of that night. But knowing a thing yourself and watching a man learn it in front of you are different hungers entirely, and Theo found he couldn't look away.

"There's more," Theo said quietly. "Keep reading."

Casper kept reading. His voice flattened as it went, the way voices do when they're carrying too much weight — "*Commander Vasquez personally boarded the flagship to secure the navigator's instruments. Among these, a bearing-compass of Vessian make, recovered from the person of one Maren Oduya, pilot, who resisted and was—*"

"Don't," Theo said, and his own voice cracked on it, and he hated that it cracked. "*Was.* Just leave it as *was*. I've imagined the rest of that sentence my whole life."

Silence, except the fire and the river slapping the stones. Maren Oduya. His grandmother, young, a pilot, holding the compass that had been hers by right of blood and craft, and a desert ranger prying it from her hands on a burning deck. Theo had built his whole life out of that sentence. He'd thought he was prepared to hear it confirmed.

He wasn't. Grief, he discovered, doesn't care how long you've rehearsed it.

But it was Casper who broke.

It started as stillness — total, terrible stillness, the kind Theo had seen in men an instant before violence. Then Casper set the document down on the stone with exaggerated care, the way you set down something that might detonate, and put both hands over his face, and made a sound Theo had never heard a man make: low, throttled, like something dying quietly in a trap.

"He told me they were pirates," Casper said through his fingers. "Every story. Every night by the fire. The Vessians were pirates, the fleet was full of thieves, and he *saved* the river from them, and the compass was his — was mine — was a *trophy*, and I grew up on that. I built myself on that." His hands came down. His eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "He led them onto the shoals. He took the light away first. You don't even do that to wolves."

"No," Theo said. "You don't."

"I arrested people for lesser thefts. I dragged men to the magistrate for stealing *bread*." A laugh came out of Casper that wasn't a laugh at all. "And the whole time I was hunting you across the salt flats, you were—" He looked at the compass between them, at the needle still trembling in its glass, faithful to a dead country. "It was hers. It was always hers. My father stole it off a drowning woman and I chased the man who took it *back* for a year. I called it justice."

Theo should have felt triumphant. He'd imagined this moment — the honest son of the butcher learning the ledger, the look on his face. In his imagination, Casper Vasquez had argued, denied, reached for his father's version like a drowning man reaching for driftwood.

Instead the man sat in the wreck of everything he believed and grieved honestly, and Theo found there was no triumph in it at all. Only the strange, unwelcome ache of recognition. Two boys raised on lies, one on each side of the same crime, both handed a compass-shaped hole and told it was an inheritance.

"My grandmother used to sing," Theo said, after a while. "Vessian songs. She'd stop when anyone came near. I thought she was ashamed." He turned a lead case over in his hands, feeling the weight of a nation packed into bread loaves. "She wasn't ashamed. She was keeping it alive somewhere it couldn't be confiscated."

Casper wiped his face with the heel of his hand, rough, like punishing himself. "How many cases are down there?"

"Dozens. Maybe a hundred. Names, charts, the crown records, everything the empire wanted erased." Theo looked south, down the failing river, toward the sea and the shoals called Gray Teeth, where the Amaranth still waited under the silt for the season's last tides. "Everything Rehn's fleet will strip for scrap and burn as evidence if we don't move."

Casper stood. He moved like a man whose skeleton had been replaced overnight — unfamiliar, careful — and picked up the compass, and held it out to Theo. Handle first. The old courtesy, the one that meant *it's yours.*

"It was hers," Casper said. "Take it."

Theo didn't take it. Not yet. Because as his fingers closed near the brass, the needle swung — sharply, decisively, away from the archive below and pointing hard south-southeast, downriver, quivering like a struck bell.

And far downstream, faint on the wind off the water, came the sound Theo had been dreading for two days: oars. Many of them, in rhythm. A work song in a language he knew — the empire's tongue, sung by Captain Ilsa Rehn's fleet, and they weren't two days behind anymore.

"They found the chapel," Theo said. "They've been following our wake."

Casper closed his fist around the compass — around his father's crime, Theo's inheritance, the only key to the shifting channels — and his grief hardened into something Theo recognized at last, because he'd been wearing it for years himself.

Purpose.

"Then we stop grieving and start rowing," Casper said. "Fourth tide won't wait for either of us."
