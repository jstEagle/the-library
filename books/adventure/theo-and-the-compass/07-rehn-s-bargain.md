## 7. Rehn's Bargain

The trading post at Sallow Bend smelled of tallow smoke and river rot, and it was the last place on the dying river where a man could buy rope, lamp oil, or lies in equal measure.

Casper saw the fleet's colors before he saw her — the black-and-brass pennant of Rehn's salvage company snapping from the mast of a moored barge, three more hulls behind it riding low with dredging gear. His stomach went cold and stayed cold. They had lost two days to Theo's wound, two days of slow channels and fevered sleep, and the fleet had eaten the distance like the river ate its banks.

"We can go around," Theo said quietly beside him. His left arm was bound tight against his chest, the bandage already browning at the seam. "There's a back trail through the reed beds. Ugly walking, but ugly is free."

"They're not here for us by accident."

"No," Theo agreed. "They're here because someone talked. And the only someones who knew we were coming downriver are dead, drunk, or paid." He said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse.

They should have gone around. Casper knew that even as he walked through the post's crooked gate, past drying nets and a dog too old to bark, into the long low hall where traders shouted over crates of salt fish and lamp oil. He told himself he was going in for supplies — rope, oil, bread for four days — and he let himself believe it for exactly as long as it took him to cross the room and find Captain Ilsa Rehn sitting at the corner table like she'd been carved there.

She'd cleared the space around her. Not by asking — by existing. Traders gave her table a berth the way they'd give a hole in the ice. She was drinking nothing, eating nothing, her hands folded on the scarred wood, and when she looked up at Casper she smiled the way a ledger smiles: all columns, no warmth.

"Ranger Vasquez." She pronounced it carefully, each syllable weighed. "Or should I say — *the* Vasquez. Sit down. You're letting the heat out."

Theo had melted toward the door the moment he saw the pennant; Casper felt his absence like a pulled thread. Good. One of them free was better than none.

He sat.

* * *

Rehn poured two cups of something dark from a clay jug without asking whether he wanted any. The cup she pushed across was chipped at the rim. Everything about her was deliberate in that way — the shabby table, the plain coat, the cheap wine. A woman who could afford brass fittings on three barges choosing to look poor. It was a costume, and she wore it better than most men wore their faces.

"You've been hard to find," she said. "A year, my people tell me. A year chasing one thief across the flats. That's either devotion or obsession, and I've never learned to tell them apart."

"It was justice."

"Was it?" She sipped. "Because justice has an end date, Ranger. Obsession doesn't. And here you are, a year later, sharing a campfire with the man you swore to hang. My scouts watched you pull him out of the channel at Fourth Bend. Carried him, I'm told. Quite tenderly."

Casper kept his face still. It cost him. "Your scouts were closer than I thought."

"My scouts are always closer than people think." She set the cup down and turned it slowly, once, twice, watching the wine swirl. "Let's not fence. You know what I want. The compass. The Vessian charts your father kept. And the man who's been feeding you his grandmother's bedtime stories about drowned kingdoms."

"He hasn't fed me stories. I've seen the archive myself."

Something flickered across her face — quick, professional annoyance, gone before it finished arriving. "Then you've seen paper. Paper rots. Paper burns. What I have is real." She reached into her coat and laid three things on the table between them.

The first was a letter, sealed with the imperial crest. The second was a small leather case, and inside it, when she opened it, a commission document bearing a name that made Casper's ears ring: **Vasquez**. His father's name, restored, countersigned by the Surveyor-General himself. Salvage Master, First Class. Authority over all river operations north of the delta.

The third thing was a photograph, if such a crude thing deserved the word — a tintype, actually, soft-edged and silvered with age. His father. Younger than Casper had ever known him, standing on the deck of a survey barge, laughing at something outside the frame. Beside him, a woman with dark hair and a compass hanging at her throat on a cord.

Casper knew the compass. He'd grown up with it. He did not know the woman.

"That's Maren Oduya," Rehn said softly. "Vessian. Your father's partner, before the war, before the drowning. Before he took everything she had and called it salvage rights." She tapped the tintype. "You've spent a year hunting the grandson of that woman, Ranger, because your father taught you the theft was yours to avenge. It wasn't. It never was. You've been guarding a locked box your whole life without once asking what was inside."

"And what's inside?"

"A lie. A big one, well-built, load-bearing." She leaned forward. "Here's what's on offer. Hand over the compass and the man, and the commission is yours. Your father's name comes off the traitor rolls — yes, we know what's in the archive, we've known for years, why do you think the fleet moves now? — and onto the honor roll where it belongs. You get a salary, a crew, a boat. You get to be what you were raised to be. Salvage Master Vasquez. Legitimate."

"And if I don't?"

"Then your father stays a traitor, you stay a ranger with no jurisdiction on open water, and I take the compass off his corpse-friend there in whatever condition the taking requires." She shrugged, easy as shifting cargo. "I'd rather not. Corpses make paperwork."

* * *

Casper looked at the wine and didn't drink it. Outside, the light was going amber through the slats, and somewhere a trader was arguing about the price of pitch, and the river moved past the pilings with that low endless whisper it made when the season was dying.

His father's face looked up at him from the tintype, young and laughing, and beside it the woman whose compass Casper had carried for a year like a piece of his own heart.

*Whose heart?* he thought. *Whose heart have I been carrying?*

"I need time," he heard himself say.

"You have until the tide turns. Two hours."

"The tide won't turn for six."

"Then you have six hours, and I'll spend them buying every rope and barrel of oil in this post so you can't resupply." Rehn smiled again, and this time it reached her eyes, and there was nothing warm there at all. "I'm generous, Ranger. Ask anyone I've ruined."

He stood. At the door he stopped, because stopping was part of it, because a man who leaves too fast looks guilty and a man who lingers looks like he's weighing gold.

"The commission," he said. "It names me Salvage Master. Over what waters, exactly?"

"All of them. North of the delta."

"Then it names me master of a graveyard." He turned his head just enough to catch her expression. "My father held that same authority once. Did you know that? The archive says he did. It says what he used it for." He let that sit. "I'll give you your answer before the tide."

He walked out into the amber light and did not look back, and it was only halfway down the dock, with the boards groaning under him and the river breathing its rot-breath around his ankles, that he understood what he'd done in that room.

He'd lied to her. Smoothly, easily, with his face still and his voice level — lied about needing time, lied about giving an answer, bought six hours with words that meant nothing. And it had felt *good*. It had felt like competence. It had felt like his father's voice coming out of his own mouth, that old easy barter of promises against people, inheritance against lives.

*Trading a man for a name.* That was the family trade, wasn't it? His father had traded a nation for a reputation. Rehn was offering Casper the same exchange rate, and he had sat at her table and haggled.

He gripped the dock rail until his knuckles ached. Downriver, the water ran low and brown over the bones of the channel, and somewhere out there the Amaranth waited under the silt with the truth nailed shut inside it, and the sixth tide was coming whether he was ready or not.

Theo was waiting in the reeds beyond the post, pale and fever-bright, holding his wounded arm like something borrowed.

"Well?" Theo said. "Do I run, or do we?"

Casper looked at him — the thief, the grandson, the man with the truer claim — and thought: *she offered me everything I was raised to want, and the price was you.*

"We," he said. "But not the way we planned. She'll have the river watched. We go through the burn country instead, the old survey cuts — my father's routes." He almost laughed, and it came out wrong. "We use the liar's maps to beat the liar's buyers."

Theo studied him for a long moment, fever-sharpened eyes missing nothing. "What did she offer you?"

"Everything."

"And?"

Casper pushed off from the rail and started up the bank, toward the dry hills and the burning light.

"She asked the wrong question," he said. "Come on. Four days to the wreck, and the fourth tide won't wait for grief."

Behind them, down at the moored barges, a bell began to ring — Rehn's signal, crisp and businesslike, the sound of a woman who had counted her six hours and decided not to spend them waiting.
