## 8. Fourth Tide

**CASPER**

The river was dying by inches, and we were racing its funeral.

From the ridge above the lower channels I could see the whole slow catastrophe of it: the water pulling back from its banks like a man retreating from a debt, leaving mudflats that steamed in the morning heat, leaving fish gasping in shrinking pools, leaving the ribs of boats that had sunk so long ago the wood had gone the color of bone. Somewhere behind us — two hours, maybe three — the salvage fleet was burning its way down the main channel, Rehn's dredges churning the shallows into broth.

"Third tide's gone," Theo said beside me. He was crouched at the edge of the overlook, and even now, even wounded, he crouched like water — all balance, no weight. "We have three left. Maybe. If the season's feeling generous."

"Seasons aren't generous."

"No," he agreed. "They're Vessian."

I looked at him. He almost smiled.

The wound was in his left side, a scout's blade that had opened him from hip to ribs two nights ago at the trading post, when he'd turned back — turned *back*, the fool, the stubborn, impossible fool — to hold the alley mouth while I got the charts out. He'd killed one of Rehn's men to do it. I'd seen his face after. He hadn't enjoyed it, and he hadn't apologized either, and somehow that was the thing that made me trust him.

"You should be lying down," I said.

"I should be a great many things." He pressed his palm flat against the bandage and stood anyway. "The channel at Sorrow's Bend closes at low water. If we're going to run the inside route — your father's route — we run it now, while there's still water to run."

My father's route. I carried it the way I carried everything of his: without wanting to, without being able to put it down. Twenty years of summer nights hunched over those charts, the old man's finger tracing channels that appeared on no other map, his voice low and certain — *this is how you move through a country that wants you dead, boy. You know it better than it knows itself.*

I had thought he meant the desert.

He had meant the river. He had meant the country he helped drown.

"Then we run it," I said.

* * *

**THEO**

The inside route was a graveyard with a current.

I'd grown up on my grandmother's river stories, and I had imagined the channels of drowned Vessia as mournful places — quiet water, sunken bells, the patient dark. I had not imagined *this*: the smell of exposed silt, sweet and rotten at once; the herons standing in the shallows like mourners who'd forgotten why they came; the water itself running thin and fast over ground that had been streets, plazas, the foundations of a nation, so that you poled through what had been someone's kitchen, someone's church, someone's life.

Casper poled like a man reading. I watched him from the stern, gray-faced, one hand clamped under his ribs, and I watched his eyes move over the water the way scholars move over old script — and I understood, finally, what I'd stolen a year ago.

Not a compass. A translation.

"Sandbar," he said. "Port side. The chart shows a cut behind it, but the cut's silted since — we go over the bar, hard, and pray."

"You pray," I managed. "I'll bail."

We went over the bar. The boat screamed, we screamed, and then we were through, into a channel barely wider than the hull, walls of reed and ruin closing in, the water so shallow the keel carved a furrow in the riverbed itself.

"You knew this," I said. It wasn't a question. "You've never been here, and you knew this."

"He made me memorize it." Casper's jaw was tight. "Every summer. He'd quiz me at dinner. I thought it was a game." A pause, and then, quieter, with something in it I recognized because I'd heard it in my own voice at my grandmother's grave: "He was making me a tool and calling it an inheritance."

I wanted to tell him it wasn't the same. That my grandmother had given me her stories freely, that my theft had been a reclamation, that the moral arithmetic of the two of us came out different. But the blade in my side said otherwise, and the truth — the truth we'd pulled out of the drowned archive with our own blue-cold hands — said otherwise too. His father had stolen the compass from a woman who trusted him. My hands had stolen it from her grandson's killer's son. Nobody in this boat came out clean.

"Casper." I said his name and he looked back. "What he taught you — it's the only map of this river that exists. Rehn's fleet has imperial charts. Imperial charts say this channel doesn't exist." I let that sit. "Your father lied to everyone. But he told *you* the truth, in his way. Use it."

He was quiet for a long moment, poling, the reeds whispering past.

"He'd have hated this," Casper said at last. "Me, using his river against his employers."

"Good," I said. "Hate him a little. It keeps you honest."

* * *

**CASPER**

We heard the fleet before we saw it — the deep industrial cough of the dredge engines rolling up the water like thunder that had learned patience.

They'd found the inside route. Of course they had; Rehn wasn't a woman who accepted that a channel didn't exist, she was a woman who made her divers find one. Through a gap in the reeds I saw them: three barges and the dredge, lanterns strung stem to stern like a carnival come to rob a grave, and ahead of them, skimming the shallows in a long low gunboat, the picket boat, running our wake.

"They're following our channel," Theo breathed.

"Then we give them a channel that stops existing."

I closed my eyes and let my father's voice rise, that low certain dinner-table voice, and I hated how easily it came. *The Cut at Vesper's Knee, boy. Mark it well. The river takes it back every dry season — by fourth tide there's not a hand's width of water, and by fifth the mud would swallow a mule. Anyone who runs it late is dead. Anyone who runs it late is dead, do you hear me—*

I heard him. For the first time in my life, I heard him all the way through.

"Theo. How fast can you bail?"

He looked at me, and he understood — I don't know how, but he understood — and he grinned through the gray of his face like a man about to do something magnificent and stupid. "Fast enough."

We ran Vesper's Knee with the fourth tide dying under our keel, water thin as spilled tea, the boat grinding, lurching, the riverbed rising to meet us — and behind us, gaining, the picket boat, its engine snarling, its pilot confident in a channel that had maybe forty minutes of life left in it.

We came through the far side as the boat kissed mud. We came through as the water behind us began, gently, terribly, to leave.

* * *

**THEO**

I heard the picket boat ground before I saw it — a long tearing groan of hull on silt, then shouting, then the flat crack of a rifle, and the bullet took me high in the shoulder, spinning me into the bilge water, and the world went white and then red and then very simple.

Simple like this: Casper's hands on me. Casper dragging me behind the gunwale. Casper's voice, raw, saying my name — my *real* name, the Vessian one, the one I'd given him at the fork in the river like a knife handle offered hilt-first, *Teodoro Amaranth-Vess*, the name of a drowned nation and a sunken ship, the name I hadn't spoken aloud in twenty years.

"Stay down," he was saying. "Stay down, Theo, stay with me—"

"I'm not going anywhere," I told him, and it came out as a laugh, which was the wrong sound, because blood was coming with it. "You're stuck with me. That was the whole point of the alliance. Terrible negotiation on your part."

"Shut up."

"Make me. You're the one with the charts." I pressed my hand to my shoulder and felt the heat of it and thought, distantly, *grandmother, I'm sorry, I'm doing this wrong.* "Fifth tide's coming. And Casper—" I caught his sleeve, made him look at me, made him see it. "Rehn's fleet is stuck behind a dead channel. We have one tide on her. One. Don't you dare waste it grieving."

He looked at me for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes — I'd seen it building for days, the slow collapse of a man's whole architecture of loyalty, and now, kneeling in bilge water with my blood on his hands, I watched it finish falling and something else stand up in the ruin.

"Fourth tide," he said quietly. "He told me the fourth tide kills Vesper's Knee. He told me and I never asked *why he knew*. Why he knew every channel of a river he was never on." His hand closed over mine, over the wound, steady as a tiller. "He knew because he drowned it. And I'm going to use every channel he drowned to take his wreck back. That's not his inheritance, Theo. That's mine."

* * *

**CASPER**

We made the last reach of the inside route as the sun went down and the fifth tide began to rise — the last water this river would give us, the season's final gift, already pooling in the low channels, already breathing.

Theo lay in the stern, bandaged, feverish, alive, and he watched me pole with an expression I couldn't name until I realized I'd seen it before: on my own face, in a waystation mirror, a year ago. Recognition. The end of a chase.

Ahead, through the last stand of drowned cypress, the water opened wide and dark and still, and far out in the middle of it, where no chart in the empire said anything but open marsh, the evening light caught something just beneath the surface — a long pale shadow, a spine, a *hull*, the length of a chapel.

The Amaranth. Rising with the tide that would bury it forever.

Behind us, far off, the sound of Rehn's dredges grinding against a channel that no longer existed.

"Sixth tide," Theo whispered.

"Sixth tide," I said, and poled us toward the wreck my father had sunk and my father's compass had promised me — toward whatever was waiting in the hold with my name on it.
