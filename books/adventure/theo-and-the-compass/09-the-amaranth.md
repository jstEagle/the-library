## 9. The Amaranth

The fifth tide came in gray, without wind, the river lying flat as poured pewter between its failing banks. Casper stood at the bow of the stolen skiff and watched the water begin to move backward — not toward the sea, but away from it, drawn into some old wound in the earth that had been waiting a century to drink.

Theo crouched at the stern, one arm bound tight against his ribs where Rehn's scouts had opened him two days ago. The wound had gone hot. Casper could smell it under the tar and river-stink — a sweetish rot that meant fever was coming whether they finished this or not.

"There." Theo's voice was rough as gravel dragged through water. He lifted his chin toward the channel ahead, where the current bent wrong, curling against itself like a snake swallowing its tail. "The compass wants us to go against it."

Casper took out the compass. It sat in his palm the way it always had — warm, heavier than brass had any right to be, the needle trembling toward something no other instrument could feel. His father's compass. Theo's grandmother's compass. A key wearing the skin of a keepsake.

The needle swung hard left, then steadied, pointing down and slightly forward, into the water itself.

"It's not pointing at a direction," Casper said quietly. "It's pointing at a depth."

* * *

They found the Amaranth by its bones showing through the mud.

The drought had dropped the river lower than anyone living remembered, and where the channels braided apart there was a stretch of silt plain exposed like a stripped bed — and rising from it, black ribs. Timber, huge as cathedral rafters, furred with a century of river moss, running in a curve so long Casper's eye couldn't hold it all at once. The hull of a ship that must have been three hundred feet from stem to stern, sunk whole, preserved whole, in the cold anaerobic dark beneath the shifting channels.

The Vessian wreck. The drowned fleet's last great ship. The thing his father's charts circled and never named.

And moored above it, riding the last deep water like vultures on a carcass: the salvage fleet. Four barges, cranes folded like insect legs, lanterns burning yellow along their rails even though the sun was barely down. Divers' lines ran off the lead barge's stern and vanished into the murk, taut as fishing line, patient as hooks.

"They beat us here," Theo said. There was no despair in it. Just arithmetic.

"No," Casper said. "We're exactly on time."

He explained it while they beached the skiff in the dead reeds downstream, out of the lantern-glow. The tide was still coming in — the sixth tide would be the season's last, and after that the channels would close for good and the wreck would sleep another lifetime. Rehn could strip what she'd reached tonight. But the crown records — the manifest, the registry, the proof that Vessia had existed, had governed, had owned these waters before the empire redrew its maps — those were kept in the strongroom, amidships, below the waterline. Casper knew because he'd spent three nights with his father's charts spread over a waystation table, reading the annotations in the margins. *Strongroom aft of the third mast. Sealed under imperial writ. Do not breach.* His father had known exactly what was down there. Had guarded the knowledge for thirty years like a man sitting on a grave.

"Rehn's divers will go for cargo first," Casper said. "Silk, plate, anything that floats to justify her commission. She won't risk the strongroom until she has time. We take it tonight, while she thinks she's already won."

"And if she catches us?"

"She'll offer me a bargain again." Casper checked the edge of his knife. "I've had practice saying no."

* * *

The water swallowed them at forty feet and turned the world the color of old glass.

Casper had dived wrecks before — small ones, honest ones, boats that had died of weather rather than war. This was different. The Amaranth hadn't sunk. She had been *drowned*, driven under by the raid his father led, scuttled with her people's records locked aboard because the empire wanted more than her cargo. It wanted her erased. A nation isn't destroyed when its cities burn; it's destroyed when its paperwork burns, when its names come off the maps, when the grandchildren can't prove their grandmothers ever lived.

His lamp swept across a corridor door hanging open, and inside it, silt-silhouettes: a chair. A desk. A ship built with offices, with archives, with the furniture of a government that had expected to keep existing.

Theo swam ahead of him, favoring his side, trailing a thin ribbon of pink that the water took and diffused until it looked like nothing at all. They passed through the great cabin — chandeliers still hanging, crystal furred white — and Casper felt the vertigo rise in him again, the same vertigo from the drowned chapel. He had spent a year hating a thief. He had been robbing a graveyard the whole time.

The strongroom door was iron, and it was already open.

Rehn's divers had cut the seal and gone deeper, chasing whatever the manifest promised. Beyond the door, lamplight moved in the dark like something alive. Voices came distorted through the water, through the hull, through Casper's own skull.

He pulled himself through.

The strongroom was smaller than the chapel where he'd learned the truth — a low vaulted chamber lined with shelving, and on the shelving, wrapped in oilcloth and wax-sealed against the century, rows upon rows of documents. Ledgers. Registers. Charts bearing the Vessian seal — the coiled river-serpent he'd first seen stamped in lead on a chest the flood gave up. An entire nation's memory, shelved and waiting.

And on a reading stand in the center of the room, placed there deliberately, weighted down with a brass paperweight shaped like a compass rose — a single letter.

His father's handwriting. He knew it the way he knew his own pulse. The heavy downstrokes. The way the capital T crossed like a blade.

*Casper.*

He should have grabbed the records first. Some part of him understood that — the tactician, the ranger, the man who'd crossed a desert and a dying river to get here. But his hands went to the letter, because his hands had been reaching for it his whole life without knowing.

*If you are reading this, then you found the chest, or you found the wreck, or you found Theo — and I am past explaining myself. I will not ask your forgiveness. I will only give you the truth, which is the only inheritance I have left that isn't stolen.*

*I led the raid on the Vessian fleet. Not as a soldier following orders — as an architect. The erasure of Vessia was my design, drawn up in this very river's name. And the compass — I did not win it. I took it from a woman named Sera Vane, who had carried it through the burning of her city, who used it to guide her people's survivors into hiding along these banks. Your mother's stories about how I earned it in honorable salvage were mine. I let you build yourself on top of the lie because the lie was load-bearing. Take it out and see what falls.*

*What falls is this: everything I told you about duty was true except its object. I served the map, not the river. If you have come this far, you have already chosen better than I did. The records in this room belong to the people the maps forgot. Give them back.*

*One more thing. Rehn's operation is charged. Her divers set salvage charges along the hull to crack the cargo decks free — powder kegs, chained and fused, port side, near the second mast. She means to blow the wreck open and take everything at once, and the blast will bury the strongroom under a thousand tons of hull. Whatever you decide about me, decide fast.*

*Your father, who was not your father in any way that matters. — E. Vasquez*

Casper read it twice. The second time the letters swam, and he understood distantly that he was crying underwater, which shouldn't have been possible, tears and river mixing at the mask's seal, grief dissolving into the same water that held his father's crime.

Theo's hand closed on his shoulder. Through the glove, through the neoprene, through a year of hatred: just pressure. Just presence. The thief watching his enemy grieve, and staying.

Then the hull shuddered.

A low boom rolled through the water, felt in the teeth, in the sternum, in the shelves themselves — dust and silt blooming off the ceiling in slow gray clouds. Not the main charge. A test shot. Somewhere up the port side, Rehn's divers were setting the rest, and the fuse of the whole operation had begun to burn.

Casper put the letter inside his jacket, against his chest, where it lay over his heart like a hand.

Then he took out the compass.

It glowed faintly in the lamplight, the needle swinging, restless, alive. And Casper saw it clearly for the first time — not as his father's keepsake, not as Theo's grandmother's heirloom, but as what it had always been: a salvage key. A master-key to the drowned fleet's own workings. The Vessians had built their ships to answer it. Which meant —

"The charges," he said aloud, forgetting the water wouldn't carry it, and Theo read his lips and his eyes and nodded.

They swam for the second mast.

* * *

The powder kegs hung in a chain along the port side like a rosary — six of them, lashed to the hull's ancient timbers, fuses running back toward a firing line on Rehn's lead barge. Two of Rehn's divers knelt at the chain, splicing the last connections, lamps bobbing.

Casper came at them out of the dark with the compass held out before him like a lantern, like a weapon, like a name.

The needle didn't point north. It pointed at the kegs — at the Vessian detonation locks built into each one, salvaged tech the empire's engineers had copied but never understood, locks that answered to one frequency, one signature, one key. The compass pressed to the nearest lock made it shiver, click, and die — disarmed, its charge suddenly just powder in a barrel.

The first diver spun, knife out. Theo hit him from the flank like a thrown anchor, and the two of them went rolling through the silt in a slow, silent violence of elbows and breath. Casper didn't watch. He pressed the compass to the second lock. Click. The third. Click. Click. Click. Four dead charges, five, and the last diver — the one fighting Theo — broke away and swam hard for the surface, for his captain, for the alarm.

The sixth lock was jammed with river grit. Casper worked the compass against it, needle spinning, and heard — felt — above him, through a hundred feet of water, the muffled thunder of Rehn's crew realizing their operation was being murdered from inside. Boots on decks. Cranes swinging. A bell starting to ring.

Click.

Six for six. The chain of charges hung dead along the Amaranth's hull, harmless as fishing floats.

Casper hung in the black water beside the drowned ship, the compass glowing in his fist, his father's letter against his heart, and above him the fleet's lanterns wheeled and scattered like a kicked nest of hornets.

Theo surfaced beside him, gasping, blood threading from his side into the river, grinning through the pain with a wildness Casper recognized now as kinship.

"They'll send boats," Theo panted. "Armed ones. And we're standing in their graveyard holding their prize."

Casper looked down through the clearing water at the vast pale shape of the Amaranth, at the strongroom full of a nation's memory, at the sixth tide already beginning to turn.

"Then we'd better be quick," he said. "The river's only going to give her up once."

Behind them, across the flat gray water, Captain Ilsa Rehn's launch came around the lead barge's bow, lanterns blazing, running straight at the dark where they floated.
