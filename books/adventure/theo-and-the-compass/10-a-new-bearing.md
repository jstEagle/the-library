## 10. A New Bearing

**CASPER**

The sixth tide came in gray.

Not the gold of the first tide, when the Amaranth had risen like a rumor made solid — gray water, gray sky, the river running slow and heavy with silt from the drowned country upstream. Casper stood on the mudbank where the fleet's fires still smoked and watched the current carry the last of Rehn's ambition out toward the salt flats. The salvage barges sat low in the shallows, holed and settling, their cranes bent like broken reeds. Somewhere beneath that brown water lay the charges he had turned against their makers, and somewhere deeper lay the Amaranth herself, sinking back into the dark she'd kept for a hundred years.

She would not rise again. He understood that now. Six tides, the old salvager had said, and the river gave nothing twice.

Behind him, the Vessian descendants were carrying the records ashore.

They came from every settlement along the lower reaches — families who had kept their grandmothers' songs without knowing what the songs were for, who baked bread with a cross-hatch pattern they called luck and called it nothing else, who had names they didn't speak aloud because speaking them had once been dangerous. Old Marta from the ferry crossing. The brothers who ran the eel weirs at Tallow Bend. A girl of maybe twelve, barefoot in the mud, holding a lantern though the sun was up, because her family had always lit lamps for the drowned and no one remembered why.

Casper had spent the night hauling crates out of the wreck's hold while Theo swam the flooded corridors with a knife between his teeth, cutting loose the sealed tubes of crown records — deeds, registers, the census of a nation that officially had never existed. His arms ached. His lungs burned with river. He had never been so tired or so certain of anything in his life.

Marta opened the first tube on the mudbank with hands that shook. She read the name inscribed inside — *Vessia, Keeper of the River Roads* — and then she sat down in the mud and wept without sound, the way people weep who learned long ago that grief must be quiet to survive.

Casper looked away and found Theo watching him from the waterline, wringing out his shirt, his grandmother's compass hanging open in his other hand like a small brass heart.

* * *

**THEO**

Theo had imagined this moment a hundred times over the years, usually in the hour before dawn when the lies he told himself wore thinnest: the moment he got the compass back, the moment the name *Sable* meant something again besides a mark on a wanted list. In his imaginings it had always felt like winning.

It felt like weather instead. Something vast moving through him that he could do nothing about but stand in.

He turned the compass over. The case was warm from the water, from his hand, from a hundred years of waiting. On the underside, worn nearly smooth, the engraving he'd traced with his thumb since childhood: *For Lira Sable, who keeps the bearing.* His grandmother had been nine years old when the rangers came upriver. She had hidden in an eel weir while men in empire green burned the archive boats. She had lived to be ninety and had never once said the word *Vessia* above a whisper, and when she died she left Theo a compass and a story he'd thought was a fairy tale until the day he saw the same compass hanging from the belt of a ranger's son and understood it wasn't a tale at all. It was loot.

"You could keep it," Casper said beside him. Theo hadn't heard him cross the mud. The man moved quietly for someone built like a door. "After everything. It's as much yours as mine. More."

Theo looked at the compass. Then at the fire already stacked on the shingle — driftwood and barge-timber, the descendants building it without being asked, the way you build a pyre when you've finally got something worth burning.

"No," he said. "That's the whole point, isn't it? Your father used it to find things to take. Rehn wanted it to find things to take. If I keep it, I'm just the next one in line." He weighed it in his palm one last time — a year of chasing, a lifetime of wanting, all of it fitting in a hand. "My grandmother kept the bearing. She didn't need brass to do it. Neither do I."

* * *

**CASPER**

They burned it at slack water, when the river went still as held breath.

Marta spoke first — not Vessian, none of them knew enough Vessian anymore, just the bread-blessing her grandmother had taught her, the cross-hatch words — and then the girl with the lantern stepped forward and set the compass on the wood with both hands, careful as laying an egg. Theo struck the flint. Casper watched the flame take the brass housing, watched the glass crystal crack and cloud, watched the needle swing wild inside the heat — north, south, east, every direction at once, all of them true, all of them useless — and felt something in his chest come loose that had been cinched tight since he was nineteen years old and knelt at his father's grave promising to be worthy of the name.

*Worthy of the name.* That had been the whole catechism of his life. Vasquez. Ranger. Honest man's honest son. And underneath it, all along, the rot: a father who had led the raid that drowned a fleet, who had pried a compass from a child's inheritance, who had built his son's identity out of stolen timber and called it legacy. The letter from the hold was folded against Casper's heart now. He hadn't shown it to anyone. He wasn't sure he ever would. Some confessions were between a dead man and the person he'd wronged most — and that person wasn't Casper. That person was standing three feet away watching the fire.

"I'm sorry," Casper said. It was too small. He knew it was too small even as he said it. "For my father. For the year I spent hunting you for owning what he stole."

Theo didn't look away from the flames. "You know what's strange? I spent that year hating you. The whole time. Every trail you almost caught me on, every waystation — I hated you like you were him." He finally turned. Mud to the knees, hair stiff with river salt, eyes red-rimmed and clear. "Then I watched you read those records in the archive and grieve like the grief was yours to carry. And I thought — he doesn't know. He's been chasing his own ghost the same as me." A short breath, almost a laugh. "We were both just boys following our fathers' maps."

"Yours led somewhere true."

"So did yours. Eventually." Theo nodded at the fire, at the ring of quiet people around it, at the river beyond them running silver-gray toward the sea. "You could go back, you know. Tell the Rangers' Guild what happened. They'd probably even believe you. Rehn's gone; there's a hole where a captain used to be, and holes get filled fast."

Casper thought about it honestly, which was more than he'd managed in a year of thinking about anything else. The guildhall at Fort Meridian. The roll call. The name Vasquez spoken with respect by men who respected only the name. He tried to feel the pull of it and found — nothing. Not peace exactly. Just the absence of a hook that had been set in him so long he'd mistaken it for bone.

"They gave my father a medal for Vessia," he said. "I'm done taking medals from liars."

* * *

**THEO**

They parted at the top of the tide, because the tide was going out and so was Theo, downriver on a borrowed skiff with a sail patched from a salvage balloon.

The descendants had argued with him — stay, help us rebuild, you're one of us, you're *Sable* — and he'd told them the truth: he'd spent his whole life being somebody's grandson, somebody's thief, somebody's ghost. He needed a year of being nobody before he could be somebody new. But he took the name back. That he did. When Marta asked him what to write in the record book she'd started — the first Vessian ledger in a century — he said, "Lira Sable's grandson," and watched her write it, and something in his chest that had been clenched since he was nine years old finally let go.

Casper walked him down to the skiff through the mudflats, both of them silent, because some farewells don't want talk.

At the waterline Theo stopped. "The letter," he said. "Your father's letter. What are you going to do with it?"

"Burn it with the rest, eventually. Read it a few more times first." Casper's jaw worked. "There's a part where he says he meant to give the compass back. Twenty years he meant to. Kept meaning to." He looked out at the water. "That's the thing I can't forgive. Not the raid. The *meaning to*. My whole life was built out of his meaning-to."

"He wrote it to you," Theo said. "Whatever else he was, he wanted you to know. That's not nothing."

"It's not enough. But it's something." Casper put out his hand — ranger's grip, rough palm, steady — and Theo took it. "Go see the sea, Sable. Your grandmother never did. Somebody should."

Theo held the grip a moment longer than he needed to. Then he stepped into the skiff, shipped the oars, and let the outgoing tide take him.

He didn't look back until the bend. Casper was still standing on the flats, getting smaller, one figure alone on a gray shore — and Theo realized with a start that for the first time in a year, he wasn't running from the man behind him. He was just leaving. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole width of his life.

* * *

**CASPER**

Casper stayed.

He told himself it was temporary — a week, a season, until the settlements were steady — but he'd stopped lying to himself somewhere around the fourth tide and saw no reason to restart. He mended the eel weirs at Tallow Bend because nobody else knew how. He taught the girl with the lantern to read the old charts, and she taught him the bread-blessing, and neither of them mentioned how strange it was that a ranger's son should end up keeper of the river roads his father helped drown. Marta started calling him *the warden*, half-joking, and the name stuck the way useful names do.

On the first evening of the seventh tide — no tide at all now, just the ordinary breathing of the river — he climbed the chapel hill where the flood had started everything and looked down at the water. Somewhere under that surface lay the Amaranth, keeping her secrets, keeping her dead, keeping the bearing in the only way that mattered: by staying.

The letter was in his coat. He took it out, read the last lines once more — *if you are reading this, Casper, then you chose the river over me, and it is the only right choice I ever gave you* — and fed it to the lantern flame.

The paper curled to ash. The wind off the water took it apart.

Below, lights were coming on in the settlement — real lamplight now, not memorial light, the first lamps lit for the living instead of the drowned. The girl was waving up at him from the shingle, shouting something about a boat coming downriver, unfamiliar, flying no colors he'd recognize.

Casper pocketed the letter's ashes, shouldered his pack, and started down the hill to meet whatever the river brought next — not a ranger's son, not a chaser of thieves, just a man with a bank to walk and a light to answer.

The boat, when he reached the shingle, was flying no colors at all. But the man at its tiller raised two fingers in a salute Casper knew from a year of distant trails, and grinned like a man who'd seen the sea and come back anyway.

"You're a hard man to leave behind," Theo called across the water. "Mind if I anchor here a while?"

Casper looked at the river, at the lights, at the life he'd chosen — and found, to his own surprise, that the answer had been waiting in him the whole time, steady as a needle finding north.

"Mind?" he said. "I'll hold your line."
