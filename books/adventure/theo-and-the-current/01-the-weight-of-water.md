## 1. The Weight of Water

The river had opinions about my schedule, and this morning it was shouting.

I'd been flying the Kavere run for six years—medicine out, mail back, a crate of engine parts if the mission station had been frugal enough to order them. Forty minutes each way in the Heron, up the green throat of the Sess, past the drowned church at Tolo Bend where the bell tower still stood like a man wading chest-deep and refusing to admit defeat. Routine. The kind of flying you could do with your hands asleep. That was the point of it. I had built myself a life that ran on routine the way the Heron ran on avgas: measured, unremarkable, invisible.

The flood had other plans.

It had come up three days ago, fast and brown, eating the low fields whole, and now it was going down just as fast, leaving everything it touched wearing a coat of silt. From eight hundred feet the country looked like something being erased—a map dissolving from the edges inward. The strip at Kavere was fine, high ground, but the backwater channel that looped off the Sess below my own airstrip had jumped its banks and turned half the bottomland into a shallow brown lake.

I dropped the medicine at Kavere, drank the terrible coffee Sister Anselm insisted on making, refused the second cup I wanted, and lifted off into air so wet it felt chewable. The Heron climbed the way she always did, grudgingly, like an old woman rising from church. Good plane. Best thing I owned, which is another way of saying the only thing I owned that mattered.

I should have gone straight home. The weather was building in the west—that bruised, stacked look the sky gets before the monsoon commits—and every year I told myself I'd stop pushing the margins, and every year I pushed them anyway, because the margins were where the money lived and the money was thin as communion wine.

But the light did it. The sun came under the storm clouds at a low angle and lit up the flooded bottomland, and there, snagged against a fence line that was now a reef, sat a shape that didn't belong.

Floods carry things. That's what floods are for. They carry away the loose, the dead, the abandoned—the things nobody anchored well enough. In six years I'd seen the water deliver refrigerators, coffins, one entire outhouse, and a goat that had opinions about being rescued. So a box in a fence wasn't strange.

What was strange was that it looked built to be carried by water. Squat, iron-banded, no corners showing rot. It sat in the shallows like it had been waiting for me specifically, which is the kind of thought you're supposed to laugh off and pour a drink over, and instead I banked the Heron around and looked again.

Chest-sized. Man-portable, barely. And the fence had caught it at an angle that said it hadn't come tumbling down the Sess with the driftwood—it had come up out of the backwater channel itself, from upstream, from the drowned country where nobody lived anymore because nobody was allowed to remember why.

I told myself I was going home. Then I told myself I'd just circle once more. Then I put her down on the flooded grass at the edge of the bottomland, which I will not pretend was good airmanship, and waded out thigh-deep through water the temperature of soup, telling myself all the way that I was only going to look.

* * *

The chest weighed what a chest full of paper weighs, which is to say nothing much and everything. Waterlogged, though. It came up out of the mud with a sucking sound, like the river changing its mind too late.

I got it onto the strip's only dry patch, the gravel apron in front of my shack, and stood there dripping while the afternoon went gold behind the western storms. My plan—I want to be honest that there was a plan, even if it was a bad one—was to set it down, note the location for the district water office, who were supposed to log salvage, and let it sit until someone official came to collect it.

The problem with that plan was the seal.

It was stamped into a lead plate on the lid, the size of a saucer, and the floodwater had scrubbed it clean. A tree with its roots exposed, growing downward instead of up. Around it, a ring of lettering worn nearly smooth but not quite: I've spent twenty years not thinking about that lettering, and I recognized it anyway, the way you recognize a face from a funeral.

Vessania.

Say it slow and it sounds like a place. Say it fast and it sounds like a mistake. There used to be a nation by that name upriver, in the high valley country where the Sess is born—fertile, stubborn, famous for its archivists and its orchards, gone now. Not conquered, exactly. Erased. Twenty years ago the government redrawn the maps, dissolved the provinces, scattered or disappeared anyone whose name began with a Vessian vowel, and then spent two decades pretending the whole business had been a border adjustment. You can buy a school atlas today that shows the valley as empty pasture. I have one. I checked, once, years ago, drunk enough to be curious and sober enough to never do it again.

Nobody says the word. Not because it's illegal—it isn't, technically—but because saying it marks you. I learned that early. I came here to be unmarked. Bush pilot, medicine runs, a shack with a leaky roof and a radio I kept tuned to the civil band and nothing else. Six years of being nobody from nowhere, and I'd gotten so good at it that some mornings I almost believed it myself.

And now the river had handed me a sealed chest wearing the name of a dead country, and it was sitting on my gravel apron looking at me.

I made dinner. Rice and tinned fish, eaten standing up, watching the chest through the window the way you watch a dog you don't trust. The storm front walked in from the west right on schedule, and the rain hit the tin roof like applause from a very large, unfriendly audience.

Here's the thing about a carefully invisible life: it doesn't take much to crack it. Not a bomb, not a betrayal. Just a word on a lead plate, and suddenly every silence you've maintained starts to feel load-bearing.

I went outside in the rain. I told myself it was to make sure the chest wasn't washing away, which was true, and also a lie, because what I actually did was kneel down in the flooding dark with rain hammering the back of my neck and read the lettering on the seal properly for the first time.

*ARCHIVUM VESSANIAE.* Below it, smaller, a date. Not twenty years old. Three months.

Three months.

A nation dies and stays dead for twenty years, and then somebody stamps a fresh date on its old seal and seals a chest full of—of what? Paper, by the weight. Paper that someone built to survive a flood, because they knew the flood was coming, or because they knew the water was the only road left that nobody watched.

I sat back on my heels in the rain and felt the first real fear of the evening, which was not fear of the chest. It was the understanding that somewhere upstream, in the drowned valley country, someone had recently gone to a great deal of trouble, and trouble like that travels. It comes downstream. It looks for hands.

Mine, apparently.

I left the chest on the apron, under the tarp I threw over it, and went inside, and did not sleep, and listened to the rain rehearse for the monsoon, and at some hour past midnight the radio on the shelf hissed awake all by itself—not a voice, not yet, just the carrier wave opening and closing like something breathing on the far end of the band, patient, waiting for me to answer.

I didn't answer.

But I lay there in the dark and understood, with the clarity that only comes at the worst hours, that the life I'd built—the measured, unremarkable, invisible one—had developed a hairline fracture running straight through the middle of it, and that everything after this night would be decided by whether the crack held or spread.

Outside, under the tarp, the chest sat in the rain with its dead country's name, and waited.
