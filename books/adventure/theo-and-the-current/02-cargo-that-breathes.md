## 2. Cargo That Breathes

The chest sat in the corner of the shack for two days before I touched it.

I want to be honest about that, because later—when people asked how a man like me got mixed up in all of it—I liked to say I never had a choice. That's not true. I had two days of choices. Two days of flying my medicine runs and eating tinned beans and telling myself the thing in the corner was salvage, was freight, was somebody's drowned luggage that would eventually get claimed or wouldn't. Two days of walking past it with my eyes fixed on the coffee pot like a man avoiding eye contact with a dog he knows is going to bite.

It didn't work. Curiosity is a current. You can fight it for a while, but you're swimming against it, and sooner or later it takes you where it wants.

* * *

The shack wasn't much. Corrugated walls gone rust-bloomed at the seams, a cot, a stove, a radio set I'd rebuilt three times from parts of four dead radios, and a workbench where I kept the tools that kept the *Kestrel* alive. The plane sat outside on the strip under a tarp, one wheel chocked with a stump, looking like a heron asleep on one leg. Rain came through the roof in a thin diagonal line when the wind swung north, and I'd positioned a bucket under it so long ago I'd stopped hearing its drip.

The chest sat opposite the cot, where I could see it from bed. That had been deliberate, though I told myself it was just the only clear floor space.

It was iron-banded wood, river-dark, heavier than its size suggested. The flood had scoured it but not opened it—the lock was brass, green with age but sound, and the bands were riveted in a pattern I didn't recognize. Stamped into the lid, plain as a brand on a steer, was the crest: a circle broken by a vertical line, with a star nested in the break.

Vessania.

I'd grown up on maps that didn't have Vessania on them. Everybody my age had. There was a shape on the old school atlases—a country wedged between the river delta and the highlands—and then there wasn't. One year the atlases came back from the printers without it, and the teachers taught around the blank space like it was a stain they didn't want to point at. You learned early not to ask. My father asked once, at a bar in Port Lachlan, and a man bought him a drink and told him a story about his family, and my father never asked again. He told me that story years later, drunk, as a warning. *Some questions are doors, boy. You don't know what's behind them until you're standing in the room.*

I'd built my whole life on keeping doors shut. Fly low, file honest manifests, carry what you're paid to carry, ask nothing about the parcels that don't rattle. Twenty years of being nobody important to anybody. It was a good life. It was a small life, and I'd made it small on purpose, because small things don't get noticed, and unnoticed things survive.

And now there was a door sitting in the corner of my shack with a dead nation's face stamped on it.

* * *

On the second night I gave up pretending to sleep, lit the lamp, and got out my pry bar.

The lock fought me. Brass doesn't rot like iron does; it just sits there being stubborn while everything around it dissolves. I worked the bar into the seam below the hasp and leaned, and the wood groaned, and somewhere in the walls something skittered away from the noise. The hasp screws tore free one by one, each with a small wet pop, and then the lid came up with a suck of trapped air that smelled like river mud and, underneath it, something older—paper, glue, and the faint chemical ghost of old film.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, were documents.

Not many. Maybe forty sheets, plus a bundle of photographs and a book bound in gray board. But the weight of them—when I lifted the oilcloth, my arms actually registered it differently, the way you feel a change in an engine before you can name it. Some cargo breathes. Any pilot who's flown long enough knows. Ammunition breathes. Livestock breathes. Certain kinds of paper breathe too, though it took me until that night to learn it.

The top sheet was a typed order, official letterhead, a government seal I did recognize—the Republic's eagle-and-wheat, still current, still on every customs form I filed. The date was twenty-two years old. The text was short. I read it three times and it didn't get shorter.

It authorized, in bureaucratic language so clean it might have been describing a drainage project, the *liquidation of administrative assets* in the Vessian territory, with a list of attached schedules. Liquidation. Assets. Words doing heavy lifting. At the bottom, a signature block, and above the signature block, a line for countersignature that was filled in with a name I didn't know and a rank I did: Interior Ministry, Office of Special Administration.

Underneath it were more pages. Rosters. Requisitions for trucks and fuel, dated across eleven months. Fuel requisitions are the thing that stopped me cold, if you want to know the moment it curdled. I've hauled fuel. I know what a ton of diesel does and doesn't do. Nobody burns that much diesel moving administrative assets. You burn that much diesel moving people, or burning them.

Then the photographs.

They were in a separate envelope, and my hands were shaking enough by then that I tore it getting it open. Black-and-white, mostly, a few faded toward sepia. The first ones were almost ordinary—streets, a market square, a church with a cracked bell tower. A town. Then the same square with trucks in it. Then men kneeling at the edge of a trench, coats over their heads, and soldiers standing behind them with rifles, and the composition of the photograph so casual, so workmanlike, that it was worse than any horror could have been. This was documentation. Somebody had been sent to document.

The last photograph was different. A column of people on a road, women and children and old men, stretching back past the frame into haze. On the back, in pencil, in a hand that pressed hard: *Column 4, day 3. Where are they taking them?*

Nobody had answered that question in twenty-two years. The pencil mark hadn't even smudged. It had been waiting.

I put the photographs down carefully, the way you set down something sleeping, and picked up the gray book.

A ledger. Bound pages, ruled columns, entries in several hands. Names. Columns of names, each with a village, a date, and a disposition code—letters and numbers I couldn't read but whose rhythm I understood anyway, the way you understand a language you've heard your whole life without speaking. Page after page after page. I turned maybe ten pages and stopped counting names because counting felt like a kind of participation.

Somewhere in those columns were hundreds of people who had been erased twice—once from the world, once from every map and record and memory their neighbors were permitted to keep. And here they were, written down. Named. Filed. The bureaucracy of forgetting had accidentally produced the perfect instrument of remembering. They'd kept receipts.

I sat back on the stool and looked at the chest and understood, finally, why it breathed. Because it wasn't cargo. It was a witness. It had been thrown in a river by someone who wanted it dead, and the river had changed its mind, and now it was lying in my shack breathing at me.

* * *

The radio crackled at half past two.

I'd left it on low, the way I always did—night frequency, weather and traffic chatter, the murmur of the bush. Most nights it's static and the occasional voice reading coordinates, and you sleep through it like surf. But this voice cut through clean, and clean voices at 2:30 a.m. mean money or government.

"—calling stations on the east channel. Repeat, calling all stations, east channel." A pause, professionally measured. "This is a survey coordination broadcast. We are seeking information regarding waterborne debris displaced in recent flooding. Specifically, a sealed container, iron-banded, approximately one meter, bearing no commercial markings. Reward offered for recovery or sighting. Report to survey office, Port Lachlan. This message will repeat."

It repeated. I listened to it three times, my hand flat on the ledger like I was holding it down against wind.

Survey coordination broadcast. At half past two. On a band used by bush pilots and mission boats. Offering a reward for a container described in detail that matched the thing under my hand down to the iron bands—but *bearing no commercial markings*. No mention of the crest. Whoever wrote that script knew exactly what the crest meant, which is exactly why they'd left it off. You don't advertise that you're hunting a dead nation's secrets. You call it debris. You call it a survey.

I thought about my father in the bar in Port Lachlan. *Some questions are doors.*

Too late. I was already in the room.

I keyed the mic, then didn't. What would I say? *Found your debris. Come collect it.* And then what—a truck, a polite man with a clipboard, and Silas Pemberton quietly added to a requisition schedule somewhere? The fuel math in those papers had already taught me what happened to items on lists. I let go of the mic.

But I didn't turn the radio off. I sat there until dawn listening to the message cycle, and around four o'clock another voice joined the band—a woman's voice, far south, weak through the static, asking the survey station to repeat its grid references. Asking, casually, about flood debris sightings along the middle river. Not reporting anything. Just asking. And there was something in how she asked—patient, precise, like someone who had been asking the same question for a very long time—that made the hair stand up on my arms.

She signed off with a callsign I didn't catch, and the band went back to static, and I sat in the graying light with forty sheets of paper and a book of the dead and the growing certainty that the river hadn't delivered me salvage.

It had delivered me a choice. The kind my father warned about. The kind that, once you're standing in the room, you find the door has locked behind you.

Outside, the rain started again, soft on the tin roof, and down at the strip the *Kestrel* shifted on her chocks like something dreaming. I wrapped the papers back in their oilcloth, closed the lid, and set the chest where I could see it from the cot.

I didn't sleep. Somewhere out in the rain, someone was already coming to ask me questions I'd have to decide whether to answer—and I had the feeling she'd been asking them for ten years.
