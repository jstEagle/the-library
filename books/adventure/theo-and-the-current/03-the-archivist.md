## 3. The Archivist

Mara Okonjo had learned to read a man's silence the way other people read weather.

Silas Pemberton's radio had gone quiet three days ago — not dead, but *careful*, the way a frequency goes careful when someone has found something on it they don't want found. She'd been listening to this band for eleven years. She knew every voice that drifted across the river country at night: the missionaries, the smugglers, the government relay out of Fort Calloway with its cheerful canned announcements about crop yields and civic pride. And she knew the sound of a bush pilot who had stopped transmitting because he was afraid of what he might say into an open microphone.

So when the mail skiff dropped her at Pemberton's strip on a gray Tuesday morning — uninvited, unbudgeted, against every rule of her own hard-won caution — she wasn't guessing. She was answering.

The strip sat on a shelf of packed earth above the river, hemmed in by kapok trees that dripped even when it hadn't rained. A corrugated shack leaned against the treeline like an old drunk against a wall. Beside it, under a tarpaulin strung between two poles, sat a single-engine plane with its cowling open and its guts exposed, and beside *that* — she saw it before she saw him, because of course she did; she'd have seen it through concrete — a chest.

Iron-banded. Water-stained. Sitting in the shade like something resting after a long swim.

Mara stood very still at the edge of the strip and let herself feel it: ten years of rumor compressed into one rusted rectangle. The archive clerk in Kessuma who'd whispered about a courier who never arrived. The defrocked priest in Halversport who'd shown her a rubbing of a seal half worn off a tombstone. The old woman on the delta ferry who said her grandmother used to sing songs in a language no map had a name for anymore. Every thread had led here eventually, or nowhere, and she had followed them all, and now one of them had ended in mud and iron forty feet from where she stood.

She made herself walk normally. She had learned that too — how to approach a thing without letting it know you wanted it.

"You're not the mail," said the pilot.

He came around the nose of the plane with a rag in his hand and grease to his elbows, and up close he was younger than his voice suggested and more tired than either. His eyes went to her face first — cataloguing, she noted, the way pilots do, reading conditions before committing to a landing — then past her shoulder to the empty river road behind her.

"No," she agreed. "I'm not."

"Skiff dropped you?"

"It did."

"Pemberton." He didn't offer the hand. Smart. "And you are?"

"Mara Okonjo." She watched the name land. It didn't land anywhere — no flicker, no recognition, nothing for a polygraph to catch. Either he didn't know it or he was better than she was. "I'm looking for the chest."

The rag stopped moving.

For a long moment the only sound was the river below the bank, moving brown and patient, and somewhere in the trees a bird making a noise like a hinge that needed oiling. Then Silas Pemberton did something she hadn't expected. He laughed — short, humorless, aimed mostly at himself.

"Eleven days," he said. "I've had it eleven days, and I haven't slept since night two, and I keep telling myself nobody knows, and here you are standing on my strip saying *the chest* like we're discussing a delivery of engine parts." He tossed the rag onto the wing strut. "Who sent you?"

"Nobody sent me."

"Nobody sends nobody four hundred miles upriver in monsoon season."

"I sent me." She took a step closer, and let him see it this time — let him see what ten years does to a person's face when they've spent them on a single question. "I've been chasing rumors of that chest since before you probably knew Vessania existed, Mr. Pemberton. Which, given that it hasn't existed on any map in twenty years, is maybe not a high bar. But I'm asking you plainly: don't make me beg, and don't make me lie to you. I've done enough of both."

He studied her. She let him. There was a version of this conversation where she performed competence, credentials, harmlessness, and she had run that version a hundred times in a hundred offices, and it had gotten her expelled from the national archive and blacklisted from three universities and a file with her name on it in a building she was no longer allowed to enter. She was done performing. Whatever happened next would happen to the actual woman.

"The seal," he said finally. "You know what it is."

"A crowned reed over crossed keys." Her mouth was dry. "The Vessian state crest. It was struck from the national registry in Year Twenty, along with everything else. Officially the design never existed. Unofficially—" she nodded at the chest "—that's the third one I've ever seen. The other two were on paper. Photographs of photographs. Copies so degraded the archivists argued about whether the keys were original or added later."

"And this one?"

"Is real." The word came out of her like something pulled. "That's why I'm here."

* * *

He made tea because there was nothing else to make, on a single burner in a shack that smelled of kerosene and canvas and, faintly, of river water that had come inside and never fully left. The chest sat on the table between them, and he kept his hand flat on its lid while she talked, as if it might swim away.

She told him the shape of it — not everything, not yet; some things you rationed even to yourself — but the shape. That twenty-two years ago, the province called Vessania had appeared on maps and then hadn't. That the official history, the one taught in schools with the government's stamp on the spine, said *administrative dissolution*: borders redrawn, populations resettled, a regrettable period of civil disorder during which, quote, excesses occurred. That the word *excesses* had done more work than any word in the language, carrying floods and fires and an entire people's disappearance on its narrow bureaucratic back.

"That's the version I was raised on," Silas said. "Excesses occurred. My father flew relief in during the last year of it. He never talked about what he saw. Just said the rivers ran wrong that season."

"They ran wrong," Mara said quietly, "because of what was put in them."

His hand tightened on the lid.

"I worked in the national archive for nine years," she went on. "Cataloguing. The most boring job in the capital, and I loved it, because archives are where governments hide things in plain sight. You want to bury a crime, you don't burn the documents — burning leaves a hole, and holes get noticed. You misfile them. You put the massacre report inside a folder about drainage permits, and you wait thirty years, and by then everyone who could recognize it is dead or retired and the paper is just paper." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on the table. "I got good at finding misfiled things. Too good. I found a shipping manifest from Year Nineteen — freight tonnage out of the Vessian district, classified as agricultural equipment, routed to a smelting works. Nobody ships plows to a smelter, Mr. Pemberton. They ship metal. And the tonnage was wrong. It was wrong by a factor that keeps you awake."

"What did you do?"

"What any idiot would do. I asked questions. Out loud. In a building full of people whose careers depended on no one asking questions." She smiled, and knew it wasn't a warm smile. "They didn't fire me. Firing creates paperwork. They *reassigned* me, twice, to posts that didn't exist, and then a review board determined I had falsified credentials I had not falsified, and I was escorted out with a box of my belongings while my colleagues studied their desks very carefully. That was six years ago. Since then I've funded my research by copying documents for lawyers and teaching children their letters, and I've traveled every river and road in this country following the same rumor: that someone in the Vessian administration understood what was coming, and gathered the proof, and sealed it, and sent it downstream to safety. And that the courier never arrived."

Silas was silent for a moment. Outside, the light had thickened; the monsoon months announcing themselves in the color of the air.

"How do you know it never arrived?" he asked. "How do you know this isn't it — that it arrived, and was buried, and you're the first person in twenty years to dig in the right mud?"

"Because if it had arrived," Mara said, "someone would have used it. Someone always uses proof. That's the whole point of proof." She set down the cup. "Open it. Please. I've imagined what's inside for ten years. I'd like to stop imagining."

* * *

He opened it the way a man opens a door he's been avoiding — fast, once decided — and folded back the oilcloth lining, and the smell rose out of it: cold river silt, old paper, wax, and beneath those something older and sweeter, like a room closed for a generation.

Documents. Bundles of them, wrapped in waxed linen, swollen with water but intact — someone had known what they were doing when they packed this, someone had planned for immersion, which meant someone had planned for interception. Photographs in a waterproof sleeve, their edges furred but their images legible. And at the bottom, wrapped separately, a ledger bound in gray board with a stamped number on the spine: **VR-114**.

Mara did not touch it at first. She looked at it the way you look at a person you've mourned.

Then she lifted it out, and the table seemed to tilt under the weight of it, though it weighed almost nothing.

"Registry of Actions Undertaken," she read. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "Provincial Directorate, Vessania. This is an administrative ledger, Mr. Pemberton. This isn't a diary or a manifesto. This is a *government record*. Dates, authorizations, requisition numbers, signatures." She turned pages with fingers she kept deliberately steady. "Here — grain requisitions that emptied whole districts. Here — transport allocations. Here—" she stopped.

Here was a page headed, in a clerk's neat hand: **Resettlement Operations, Phase Three. Authorizing Officer:** and beneath it a signature, and beneath the signature a printed name, and beneath the printed name a notation in the margin, initialled, that read: *Proceed per directive. No further documentation required.*

"Sanctioned," she said. The word cracked coming out. She said it again, properly. "*Sanctioned.* Do you understand what this means? For twenty years they've told us it was chaos — disorder, excesses, regrettable fog of civil conflict, no one's hands clean, tragedy on all sides. And this—" she pressed her palm flat on the open page "—this is a *requisition form*. Phase Three. Numbered. Initialled. You don't requisition a massacre by accident. You requisition it the way you requisition gravel."

Silas had gone very still. "The photographs?"

She drew one from the sleeve and held it where the lamplight reached. A road. A ditch. Figures in it, and figures beside it, and men with rifles standing in the attitude of men who are working, not fighting. On the back, in pencil: a date, a grid reference, and a photographer's initials.

"Evidence of the act," she said, "and evidence of the paperwork that ordered it, in the same box. That's what makes this chest worth killing for. Either one alone, they can argue with. Together—" she looked up at him "—together they're a verdict."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere out on the water a boat engine changed pitch, and both of them turned their heads toward the sound without meaning to, and held still until it faded.

"We need to talk about who else knows," Silas said at last, low. "Because someone's been asking about this on the radio band. Not by name — fishing. Asking after salvage, flood debris, anything unusual washed down from the high country. And yesterday a voice I didn't know asked specifically about iron-banded chests, and then signed off before I could get a bearing."

"Then we're already late." Mara began wrapping the ledger back in its linen, and her hands were shaking now and she let them be. "Mr. Pemberton — Silas. Listen to me. I have spent ten years believing this proof existed and six years being destroyed for believing less than that. I am not going to pretend I'm calm about it. But I'm telling you the truth when I say the safest thing you could do — the thing every instinct you have is going to recommend — is to put me back on a skiff and sink this chest in the deepest hole in the river and go back to flying medicine and forgetting my face."

"Is that what you'd advise?" he asked. "If our positions were turned?"

It was a fair question and she honored it with honesty. "No. I'd advise exactly what I'm about to advise, which is that this goes to the capital, to the only press house the ministry doesn't own, before the monsoon closes the rivers and before whoever is fishing that band stops fishing and starts hunting. And I'm telling you it will cost us both everything we have, and possibly everything we are, and I will understand completely if you choose your quiet life over my obsession."

Silas looked at the chest. At the plane with its open cowl. At the river, where the light was failing and the first fat drops of the season spotted the dust outside the door.

"My quiet life," he said slowly, "ended eleven days ago when I pulled this thing out of the water. I've just been waiting for someone to tell me what it cost." He met her eyes. "You tracked a ghost for ten years, Ms. Okonjo. I think you'd better tell me the rest of it — all of it — because if we're going to run this river together, I need to know what's chasing us."

Mara closed her hand around the ledger's gray cover, and outside, the rain began in earnest — the long rain, the season's rain, drumming on the tin roof like a countdown.

"There's a name in that ledger," she said, "that I haven't been able to make myself look for yet. And I think, before we take another step, that I have to."
