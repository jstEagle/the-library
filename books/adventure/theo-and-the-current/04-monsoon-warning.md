## 4. Monsoon Warning

The barometer on my wall had been falling for two days, and by the third morning it had given up all pretense of being a scientific instrument and become an omen instead. The needle sat in the red like a man refusing to leave a losing table. I tapped the glass twice, the way my father used to tap gauges he didn't trust, and the needle didn't so much as shiver.

"Fine," I told it. "I hear you."

Outside, the sky over the river country had gone the color of old pewter, low and bruised, and the air had that thick, sweet weight to it that meant the monsoon wasn't coming anymore—it was here, standing just offstage, clearing its throat. The birds knew it. The whole strip was loud with them, more birds than I'd seen all season, feeding frantically before the weather shut everything down. Even the river sounded different, a deeper note under its usual murmur, running high and brown with water from hills I'd never see.

I walked the strip out of habit more than hope. Four hundred meters of laterite clay, graded flat twenty years ago by some colonial engineer who never imagined anyone would still be using it, baked hard as brick through the dry months and now beginning to soften at the edges where the grass grew thin. By next week it would be soup. By the week after, a shallow lake with pretensions. Every pilot in the river country knew the arithmetic: you flew your last run before the monsoon or you didn't fly again until it ended, and the ones who pushed it too far were mostly names on memorials now, or worse, stories other pilots told each other at fuel stops, half laughing, not meaning any of it.

I had one run left in me, maybe. A medicine drop upriver to the mission at Kalende—quinine, penicillin, sutures, the usual small arsenal against the usual miseries—and then I'd tie the plane down, double the anchors, and let the sky do what it wanted for three months.

Three months. With a chest full of Vessania sitting in my shack.

I'd been trying not to think about it, which is a way of thinking about it constantly. Two days since I'd pried the lid open, and I'd slept maybe six hours total, and most of what I remembered of those hours was the ledger's columns marching behind my eyelids. Names. So many names. Dates beside them, neat as a shipping manifest, because that's exactly what it was—a manifest of people, received, processed, disposed of, the bureaucratic language doing its filthy work of making slaughter sound like inventory.

And the seal. That blue-and-gold crest stamped into wax that had somehow survived twenty years underwater, stubborn as the truth it guarded. Vessania. A country that no longer existed, that my schoolbooks had called a "territorial reorganization," that the maps had quietly digested the way a snake digests a meal, leaving no bulge, no trace. I'd flown over that part of the world a hundred times without knowing I was flying over a graveyard the size of a nation.

The radio had been quiet since the stranger's voice asked his questions on the band two nights ago. Too quiet. In my experience, silence on a frequency is never emptiness—it's listening. Somebody out there had stopped transmitting because they'd gotten what they needed, or because they were waiting for me to make a mistake and say something useful. Either way, every time I keyed the mic to file my flight plan, I imagined the signal going out into the wet dark and finding ears I couldn't see.

I filed anyway. Silence would have been louder.

* * *

Mara spent the morning with the documents spread across my workbench like a surgeon laying out instruments, and she worked the way I flew—methodical, total, nothing wasted. She'd arrived with one bag and no explanation beyond the rumor that had led her here, and in two days she hadn't unpacked so much as a toothbrush, as if staying ready to leave were a discipline she'd practiced so long it had become her skeleton.

She'd made a system of my chaos. Photographs sorted by watermark. Letters arranged by date. The ledger open on a wooden stand she'd improvised from a crate and two wedges, its pages interleaved with strips of rice paper to keep the damp from welding them together. She wore cotton gloves she'd brought herself, which told me something about how long she'd expected to be handling things like this.

"You should eat," I said, putting coffee on the burner. It was the third time I'd said it. It was the third time she'd heard it.

"After this page."

"That's what you said about the last four pages."

"This page has a signature." She didn't look up. Her finger hovered over the bottom margin of the ledger, not quite touching the ink. "Silas. These aren't orders from above. Look at the routing stamps. These decisions were made locally. Authorized in-country. Someone signed their name to this, right here, in this room of a building that doesn't exist anymore, and then went home to dinner."

"Maybe that's the point. Maybe they knew nobody would ever read it."

"Everyone who writes thinks someone will read." She finally looked up, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before—not the hunger I'd come to expect, but a kind of tired fury, old and banked like coals. "That's the whole horror of it. They weren't hiding. Hiding is for the guilty who fear discovery. This—" she touched the ledger stand, light as a benediction, "—this is filing."

I poured the coffee and set her cup within reach without comment, and she drank it without thanks, and we understood each other perfectly, which frightened me more than the radio silence did.

By noon the light had gone strange—the flat, sourceless glare that comes when the clouds are too thick to find the sun in them. The heat pressed down like a hand. My knee ached, an old crop-dusting injury that forecast weather better than any instrument, and it was singing.

"Weather's turning," I said. "Real turning, not monsoon-turning. Something ahead of the main body."

Mara glanced at the window. "How long?"

"Hours. Maybe less. And once it starts, the strip's got days, not weeks. After that, this whole valley is boat country until October."

She absorbed that with a slow nod, and I watched her do the same math I'd been doing—the chest, the papers, the miles, the mud. Whatever she intended to do with all this, whatever decade-long hunt had brought her to my door, the window for doing it by air was slamming shut.

"I need to send a message," she said quietly. "To a contact in the capital. Before the lines go down."

"The radio's monitored. You know that."

"I know that." Her jaw tightened. "But if I wait for the monsoon, I'm cut off for a season, and whoever was asking questions on your band won't be."

I hated that she was right. I hated more that I'd already drafted the message in my head twice and deleted it twice, the words about the chest dying somewhere between my fingers and the key.

"We'll send it from Kalende," I said. "Mission has a transmitter, and Father Anselm owes me enough favors to look the other way while it transmits. We fly up tomorrow at first light, drop the medicine, send your message, and come back before the front hits. One last run."

"And after that?"

"After that," I said, looking out at the pewter sky, "we're grounded, and so is everyone hunting us. Monsoon evens the odds. Nobody moves fast in the wet season."

Mara almost smiled. "You say that like it's a comfort."

"It's the only comfort I've got."

* * *

The stranger came at four in the afternoon, walking up the strip from the north with a canvas satchel and a surveyor's confidence, and every animal instinct I owned stood up and started screaming.

He was maybe forty, sun-faded khakis, a wide-brim hat, the lean look of a man who walked for a living. He waved before I could decide whether to meet him halfway or reach for the shotgun, and his smile was easy, professional, forgettable. Everything about him was designed to be forgotten five minutes after he passed. I recognized the design because I'd worn it myself for fifteen years. Invisible men know each other the way dogs know each other, by smell, and this man smelled wrong.

"Afternoon," he called. "Silas Pemberton? The pilot?"

"Depends who's asking."

"Corben. District survey office." He produced a folded paper, official-looking, creased soft from handling. "Flood mapping. New hydrology program, post-rainy-season assessment. We're surveying every airstrip in the valley—drainage, elevation, load capacity. Yours is on the list."

I took the paper and read it slowly, which bought me time to read him. The letterhead was right. The stamp was right. The reference numbers were plausible. But the paper was too clean for a document that supposedly came from an office two hundred miles away, and the ink hadn't smudged the way district-office carbon does, and Corben's boots—good boots, city boots, broken in on pavement rather than clay—told a different story than his hat.

"Flood mapping," I said. "In the last week of the dry season."

"Pre-monsoon baseline. Have to know what it looks like before to measure what happens during." He said it smoothly, the answer of a man who'd rehearsed it, and maybe it was even true. Maybe. "Mind if I take a few measurements? Won't take an hour."

"Strip's all yours."

What I wanted was to tell him to walk back the way he came. What I did was stand in the doorway of the shack and watch, because the chest was inside, and Mara was inside, and a man who walks onto a remote airstrip claiming to measure drainage is either exactly what he says or precisely what he isn't, and either way the only safe move is to keep him in sight.

He worked convincingly, I'll give him that. He paced distances with a tape. He took readings with a level on a tripod, jotting figures in a notebook. He knelt at intervals and pressed a soil gauge into the clay, twisting it, withdrawing a core, examining it with a professional squint. If I hadn't known better—if my life didn't depend on knowing better—I'd have gone back to my coffee.

But he kept drifting. Every circuit of the strip brought him a little closer to the shack, the way water finds a low place, and each time he drifted near, his eyes did a quick, hungry pass over the building—the windows, the door, the lean-to where I kept tools. Not the eyes of a man measuring land. The eyes of a man casing it.

On his fourth circuit, I stepped out. "Find what you're looking for?"

"Just finishing the near benchmarks." He straightened, smiling, sweat cutting clean tracks through the dust on his face. "Interesting soil out here. Laterite holds water differently than the alluvial stuff downriver. Makes for unpredictable subsidence."

"Mm." I leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, between him and the shack. "Funny thing about surveys. Usually there's a team. Usually there's a vehicle. You walked ten miles of clay in city boots to measure a strip nobody's surveyed in twenty years, two weeks before the monsoon makes the whole question academic."

His smile held, but something behind it recalibrated. "Budget cuts. Man does what he can."

"Sure." I nodded toward his tripod. "Level's a nice piece of equipment. Case looks expensive."

"Borrowed."

"From whom?"

"A colleague."

"In the survey office?"

Something flickered in his face then, quick as a fish turning, and I knew. I knew the way you know a storm is on you before the first drop lands—in the pressure change, in the animal part of the brain that predates language. He wasn't a surveyor. He might not even be named Corben. He was a question wearing a lanyard, and the question was: where is the chest?

"Tell you what," I said pleasantly. "It's getting hot, and I've got cargo to prep for a dawn run. You want water before you walk back?"

"No, thank you." He began folding his tripod with crisp efficiency. "I've got what I need."

"I bet you do."

He shouldered his satchel and started down the strip, and I watched him go, counting his steps without meaning to, noting that he never once looked back—which no real walker ever fails to do, because real walkers always check what's behind them. At fifty meters he paused, bent as if adjusting a bootlace, and in that one motion, casual as breathing, angled something small and dark from his satchel toward my shack.

A camera.

The shutter clicked once, twice—a sound too soft to carry, but I saw the geometry of it, the line of his arm, the lens finding the window where the workbench sat, where the chest sat, lid open, documents fanned out in the sun because Mara liked the light for reading. Photographing the evidence of a massacre through the window of a bush pilot's shack, and calling it flood mapping.

Cold moved through me, slower than anger, heavier. Because here was the thing I understood in that moment, standing in my own doorway with my heart going like a propeller at full throttle: the paperwork didn't matter. The letterhead didn't matter. Whether he was government or remnant or freelance, whether he answered to an inspector or a ghost of the old regime—he'd found me. The invisible life I'd built, the careful nothing I'd been for fifteen years, was over. Somewhere in a file, in a dossier, in somebody's memory, Silas Pemberton had stopped being a medicine run and started being a location.

He finished his bootlace and walked on, unhurried, and I let him. Following him out onto that empty strip would accomplish nothing except confirming how much I'd seen. Better to let him believe the fool had watched a fool work.

I waited until he dropped below the tree line at the strip's north end. Then I went inside, fast, and started closing the shutters.

Mara looked up from the ledger, gloves still on, pen still moving. "What is it?"

"How much did you have in the window?"

"The documents? All of it. Why?"

"Because a man just photographed them through my glass, and he'll be back with friends, and friends don't walk ten miles in city boots." I pulled the shutters closed one by one, plunging us into lamplight and gloom. "We fly tonight. Not dawn—now, while there's still light and the front's still twenty minutes out. Medicine, message, and we don't land here again."

Mara was already moving, sweeping the photographs into their folder with hands that didn't shake, and I loved her a little for that, though I didn't have time to know it yet. "The strip will hold?" she asked.

"For another hour, maybe. After that the clay goes soft and we're not going anywhere by air at all." I hauled the chest from under the bench—lighter than it looked, that was the terrible thing, all those dead reduced to forty pounds of wood and paper—and wedged it behind the cargo netting. "Weather's coming, Mara. The real kind and the other kind. Both at once."

Outside, the first wind hit the strip, flat and hot and smelling of iron, bending the grass flat in waves, and far to the southwest the sky had turned a color I'd only seen twice in my life and hoped never to see again—the green-black bruise of a storm that means to kill something.

I fired up the engine while the light lasted.
