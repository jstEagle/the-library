## 5. Grounded

The engine coughed on the third try, and I should have listened.

That's the thing about machines—they tell you the truth before they kill you. The Lycoming had been sulking since dawn, a wet rasp in the lower registers that I'd filed under *monsoon humidity* and nothing more. You get good at filing things in my line of work. Medicine crates. Radio chatter you pretend not to hear. A sealed chest with a dead nation's crest sitting under a tarp in your shack while the rain comes down like it's trying to drown the whole idea of dry land.

I ran up the throttle anyway. The strip at Kestrel Bend was half mud already, water standing in the wheel ruts like small brown mirrors, and I wanted one clean circuit before the sky closed for good. Just to know the plane still answered. Just to feel like a pilot instead of a man guarding a box of ghosts.

The Cessna rolled out through the puddles, tail light, and lifted off into a ceiling the color of wet slate. For about ninety seconds, everything was exactly as it had always been. The river unspooled below me, braided and swollen, the forest drowned to its knees, and the old familiar arithmetic of the air—drift, altitude, distance—settled over me like a coat I'd worn so long I'd forgotten it wasn't skin.

Then the engine stumbled.

Not coughed. *Stumbled.* A hard mechanical hitch, metal finding metal where metal should never meet, and the propeller's smooth disc went ragged for half a heartbeat before catching again. My hand was already moving—mixture, carb heat, fuel selector—running the drill the way you run your own name, without thinking.

It caught again. Worse this time. A grinding note underneath the stumble now, deep in the case, the sound of something inside deciding to stop being an engine and start being scrap.

I was at eight hundred feet, downwind of the strip, and I did the math fast. Wind fifteen knots down the valley, strip behind me and to the left, mud at both ends but mud I knew. I put the nose down, traded altitude for speed, and flew the ugliest, quietest approach of my life with the engine shuddering like a sick animal between my knees.

The wheels touched at the far end, plowed spray, and the engine died for good just as I pulled off onto the grass—no sputter, no fade. One moment it was running. The next it simply refused, the way a door refuses, the way a country refuses. Silence rushed in so complete I could hear the rain ticking on the cowling and my own pulse knocking in my ears.

I sat there with my hands still on the yoke, not moving.

You have to understand what that airplane was. Eleven years I'd flown her. She'd carried vaccines through hail, landed on sandbars when the strips were flooded out, kept me alive through weather that should have folded her wings like paper. Every dent had a story. Every hour on that tachometer was an hour I hadn't spent anywhere else, doing anything else. People talk about freedom like it's a feeling. It isn't. It's a machine that starts when you turn the key, and mine had just died under me in a flooded field.

I got out and stood in the rain and opened the cowl, because grief makes you do stupid procedural things.

The oil was glittering. That's the phrase mechanics use—glittering—and it means bearing metal, means something inside has been chewing itself, means the engine is done in a way that no bush mechanic with a crate of spare parts can fix. I stared at it for a long time. Then I checked the oil screen, and there it was: flakes of silver in my palm, fine as fish scales.

Sabotage or bad luck. That was the question, and I turned it over all the way back to the shack, and I couldn't make it land on either side. An engine can seize on its own; monsoon season eats engines, everyone knows that. But three days after a stranger who claimed to be a surveyor photographed a chest stamped with a nation that doesn't exist? Three days after he'd stood in my shack drinking my coffee and asking friendly questions about my fuel supply and my flight schedule?

Bad luck doesn't ask about your fuel supply.

Mara was waiting under the awning when I came in, dripping, and she read my face before I said a word. She'd been reading documents her whole life, Mara Okonjo—faces were just another text, and apparently I was an easy one.

"It's dead," she said. Not a question.

"Seized. Metal in the oil." I hung my soaked jacket on its nail and found that my hands wanted to shake, so I made them hold a kettle instead. "Could be the season. Could be our surveyor friend dropped something in the tank while I was sleeping."

"You'd know which?"

"I'd know if I tore it down. Which takes two days and tools I don't have." I lit the burner and watched the flame wobble in the draft. "Either way, the answer's the same. I'm not flying anywhere. Not this week, maybe not this month. Parts come upriver by boat, boats don't run in this, and even if they did—" I nodded at the tarp-covered shape in the corner, the chest, patient as a coffin. "Even if they did, whoever killed that engine knows exactly where we are and exactly how we were planning to leave. The air route's burned."

Mara didn't say anything right away. She did what she always did, which was go still, the way she went still over a page, letting the information settle into whatever vast internal filing system she kept behind those tired, sharp eyes.

"The river," she said finally.

I laughed. It came out wrong. "Three hundred miles of floodwater, Mara. Checkpoints every fifty. Half the bridges are underwater and the other half are guarded by men whose salaries depend on nobody asking questions about Vessania."

"Yes," she said. "That."

"That's not a plan, that's an obituary."

"It's the only route they haven't closed yet, because closing it would cost money and admitting there's something to close it *for*." She stood and came to the window, looking out at the rain hammering the strip, the dead plane sitting crooked in the grass like a shot bird. "Silas. Think about what they've done already. They sent someone to photograph the chest. They killed your engine—or got lucky, and either way they'll assume they did. The next step isn't sabotage. The next step is men with rifles arriving on a boat, asking us to hand it over, and then asking us a second question we won't be able to answer twice."

She was right, and I hated her a little for it, the way you hate anyone who tells you the truth while you're still grieving.

"So we run the river," I said. "In what?"

"There's a ferryman at the bend below the falls. Odile. One eye, half a reputation, and a longboat she'll sell to anyone who doesn't ask where it's been." Mara turned from the window. "I've done my homework on this stretch of country for ten years, Silas. Ten years of maps and manifests and shipping records, all of it leading here, to a bush pilot who pulled a chest out of the current. Don't you dare look surprised. The flood that gave you that chest took a village's dock apart upstream. Things wash downstream. Truth included."

I looked at her—at this woman who'd appeared out of the rain four days ago with nothing but a bag and an obsession, who slept in her boots and read by lamplight until two in the morning and spoke about a murdered country the way other people speak about a parent. And I understood, sitting there with engine metal in my pocket and rain coming down like a verdict, that the life I'd built—the careful, invisible, well-filed life—was over. Had been over since the day I hooked that chest out of the backwater. Everything since was just the sound of the door closing behind me.

"How long to the capital?" I asked.

"By river, in this water, with checkpoints?" She calculated, lips moving slightly. "Six days if we're lucky. Ten if we're not. Longer if Odile's boat leaks as badly as her prices suggest."

"And if we're caught?"

Mara held my eyes. There was no performance in her, no bravado. Just a woman who had already answered this question years ago and saw no reason to dress it up.

"Then the ledger goes in the river, and I go with it, and at least the names will be together."

The kettle screamed. I took it off the flame and poured, and my hands were steady now, which surprised me. Grief does that too, sometimes—it burns off everything except the next thing, and the next thing, and the next.

"I need to pull the propeller through tonight," I said. "Drain the tanks, fog the cylinders. If she's going to sit, she sits right." I heard my own voice and recognized it: the voice of a man making promises to a corpse. "And tomorrow we walk down to see your one-eyed ferryman."

Mara took the cup I offered. Her fingers brushed mine, brief and cold from the window draft, and neither of us mentioned it.

"For what it's worth," she said quietly, "I'm sorry. About the plane."

I glanced out at the Cessna, gray wings dark with rain, drooping in the grass like something that had lain down to die. Eleven years. Every dent a story. And now the stories belonged to the mud.

"She got me out of worse than this," I said. "More than once."

"Then she'd want you to keep moving."

"That's what I'm afraid of."

We drank our tea while the light failed, and outside the rain kept up its work on the strip, softening the edges of everything, erasing the tire tracks, the footprints, the last evidence that anything with wings had ever left this place. By morning there would be no sign at all. By morning the ground itself would have agreed to forget us.

Which left only the water.

And the water, as I was about to learn, remembers everything.
