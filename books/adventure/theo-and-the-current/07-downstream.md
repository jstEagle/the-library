## 7. Downstream

**Silas**

The ferryman's dock sat where the river bent around a drowned orchard, and the woman who ran it had one eye and no patience.

"Odile," Mara said, stepping onto the planks like she'd walked them before. "I was told you might have a boat."

"Told by who?" Odile didn't look up from the net she was mending. Her hands moved with the economy of someone who'd been fixing things longer than most people had been alive. One eye — the other a puckered seam of old scar tissue — but the one that worked missed nothing.

"A friend in Kessara."

"There are no friends in Kessara." She bit through a knot, spat it into the water. "There are people who owe you and people you owe. Which are you?"

"The second kind," Silas said.

That got her attention. The good eye came up and took him apart in about four seconds: the mud on his boots, the way he stood between Mara and the open water without meaning to, the bandolier of tools slung across his chest because he hadn't felt right leaving them behind since the engine seized.

"You're the pilot," she said. "The one whose plane died at the strip."

News traveled fast out here, or slow and then all at once. "Word gets around."

"Word is the only thing that travels faster than this river, and both of them drown people." She set down the net. "What do you want?"

"A longboat," Mara said. "Something that can run at night."

"Everything runs at night now. The checkpoints sleep during the day." Odile rose from her stool, joints cracking like green wood, and led them along the dock to where three boats lay tied in the shallows. The first was a coffin. The second was worse. The third was forty feet of weathered teak with a canopy frame stripped bare and an outboard bolted to the stern that looked older than the war.

"That one leaks," Odile said, tapping the hull with her boot heel. A dull sound, not hollow. Honest.

"How bad?"

"Bad enough that you'll bail every hour. Not bad enough that you'll sink if you bail every hour. There's a difference, and the difference is whether you're lazy." She looked at Mara. "You're paying how?"

Mara named a figure. Odile laughed, which was apparently a negotiation tactic. They went back and forth while Silas drifted to the boat's edge and looked down into the brown water, where his own reflection wavered over something darker — the chest, wrapped in oilcloth, lashed under the tarp they'd carried down from the shack on a handcart. Forty pounds of paper that weighed like lead ore. He'd started thinking of it as cargo again. That was safer than thinking of it as what it was: twenty years of names, and every one of them a debt somebody powerful still owed.

"Done," Odile said finally, and shook Mara's hand once, hard. Then the good eye swung back to Silas. "One condition. You don't tell anyone where you got this boat. Not where you bought it, not who sold it. If the inspector's men come asking, I sold to strangers I never saw."

"Fair enough."

"It's not fair. It's survival. Fairness is for cities." She was already untying the lines. "You're leaving tonight?"

Silas looked at the sky. It had been building all day — a bruise-colored wall stacked up over the western hills, silent and enormous, the kind of cloud formation he'd learned to respect the hard way in ten years of flying. The monsoon wasn't coming. It had arrived; they just hadn't been informed yet.

"Tonight," he said.

* * *

**Mara**

They loaded in the last grey light, and Mara learned the first lesson of the river within the first hour: everything takes twice as long and costs three times what you planned.

Odile sold them fuel in dented jerry cans, a tarp, a bilge pump with a cracked handle, rice and dried fish packed in wax paper, and a hurricane lamp with half a bottle of kerosene. She refused to sell them a weapon, which told Mara she knew exactly what kind of trouble they were carrying, and refused to take the extra money Mara offered, which told her something else entirely — something she filed away and didn't examine yet.

"Keep to the middle channel past the orchard bend," Odile said at the dock, holding the lamp high so its light jumped across the black water. "After that, follow the current and pray. The river knows where it's going even when you don't."

"And the checkpoints?" Mara asked.

"Two on this stretch. The first is a floating platform at Mile Thirty — a rope across the channel, two soldiers, a lantern. They'll wave you down and search you unless you look bored. Boredom is camouflage on the water. The second—" Odile paused, and for a moment the lamplight made her single eye look almost gentle. "The second you won't see coming. Nobody does. Move fast and stay low."

Then she did something Mara didn't expect. She put one rough hand flat against the hull of the longboat, the way you'd touch a horse's neck before a hard ride, and said something low in a language Mara didn't recognize — clipped, liquid, nothing like the trade tongue or the capital dialect. It lasted three seconds. Then she straightened and spat over the side.

"What was that?" Mara asked.

"Old words. From a country that isn't on maps anymore." Odile's face closed like a door. "Go. Before I change my mind and keep the boat."

Mara climbed aboard. Silas took the tiller, tested the outboard twice — it caught on the second pull with a cough and a blue stutter of smoke — and eased them off the dock into the current.

Behind them, Odile's lamp shrank to a spark, then to nothing. Ahead, the river ran wide and brown and indifferent, and the western sky had gone the color of a healing bruise.

Mara sat amidships with the chest between her knees and her arms around it, and felt the boat begin to move — really move, the bow lifting, the water peeling away in long V-shaped wrinkles — and thought: *this is the first time in eleven years I've actually been going somewhere.*

She had spent a decade standing still. Filing papers in a provincial records office after the archive expelled her, reading other people's histories by day and hunting her own by night, writing letters to dead men's colleagues, chasing rumors down alleys that always ended in walls. Every road out of the question had circled back to the same place: the proof existed, somewhere, and she couldn't reach it.

Now it was sitting in her lap, leaking river water through the oilcloth, and the whole country was between her and the printing press.

"Comfortable?" Silas called over the engine noise.

"No," she said. And then, because it was true and because the dark made truth cheaper: "But better than comfortable."

Silas glanced back at her. In the dim glow of the running lamp she could see just enough of their face — the set jaw, the eyes flicking constantly between the water ahead and the sky above, a pilot's habits surviving the death of the plane.

"Hold on, then," Silas said, and opened the throttle.

* * *

**Silas**

The first monsoon wall hit them at Mile Twenty-Six.

No warning worth the name — just a wind that arrived sideways out of nowhere, warm and wet and smelling of iron, and then the rain, not falling so much as arriving, a solid grey curtain sweeping up the river valley like a door closing behind them. Visibility dropped to fifty feet, then thirty. The channel markers vanished. The world contracted to the width of the boat and the sound of water hammering the canopy frame.

Silas steered by feel. It was strange how much of flying translated — read the surface, anticipate the gusts, never fight the medium, work with it. The river in flood wasn't so different from a storm front at altitude, except that the ground was underneath instead of below, and it wanted them.

"Bail!" they shouted.

Mara was already moving, working the cracked-handled pump, then a tin pan when the pump clogged, throwing water over the side in steady arcs while the rain replaced it nearly as fast. The leak Odile had promised announced itself as a seep along the port-side seam, a thin silver thread of river climbing the bilge. Manageable. Barely.

Lightning walked across the sky in long white strides. Somewhere ahead, thunder answered — or maybe it wasn't thunder. Hard to tell the difference out here, with the army moving hardware up and down the valley.

They ran blind for an hour. Then the rain slackened from wall to heavy curtain, and Silas saw the lantern.

It hung off a platform built on barrels mid-channel, maybe sixty feet ahead, and stretched across the water was a rope — thick, tarred, sagging under the current's push, with a bell tied at its center that was already starting to ring in the chop. Two soldiers under a lean-to, one standing, one sitting, both looking very cold and very bored, exactly as advertised.

"Checkpoint," Silas said quietly. "Mile Thirty."

"I heard the bell." Mara stopped bailing. Her hands were shaking — from the cold, or the ledger in her lap, hard to say. "What do we do?"

Silas looked at the rope. Looked at the soldiers. Ran the arithmetic they'd already run a hundred times since the shack: turn back and lose the night, run the bank and risk the shallows, or go through.

Going through meant being seen. Being seen meant being described. Being described meant Hale would have a heading and a hull color by morning.

But there was another option, and it was the one Odile had handed them without seeming to: boredom.

"We're medicine runners," Silas said. "We've been medicine runners all week. We're tired, we're wet, we're hauling supplies upriver to the mission station at Tolo Bend, and we resent the delay. Can you look resentful?"

Mara blinked. Then something shifted in her face — the archivist surfacing, the woman who'd spent years performing harmlessness in rooms full of men who could end careers. "I can look resentful," she said. "I've had practice."

Silas cut the throttle to an idle and let the current carry them the last stretch, unhurried, a boat with nowhere urgent to be. The bell clanked as the rope scraped the hull. A soldier in a poncho too big for him leaned over the edge of the platform, lantern raised, and squinted down at them through the drizzle.

"Papers."

Silas passed up the forged transit card they'd bought in Kessara from a man who charged double and delivered single-use. The soldier held it under the light for a long time. Long enough that Silas could hear the bilge water sloshing, could see Mara out of the corner of their eye sitting perfectly still with her hands folded on the oilcloth bundle, wearing an expression of magnificent, bone-deep inconvenience.

"Cargo?"

"Medical stores. Rice. Lamp oil." Silas jerked a thumb at the tarp. "Mission at Tolo Bend is short since the flood. You want to count it, be my guest, but we'll be here till Thursday and your relief will find you two very popular with the mosquitoes."

A pause. Rain drummed on the lean-to roof. The seated soldier said something Silas didn't catch, and the standing one snorted, and whatever it was, it was funny in the way misery makes things funny.

"Move along," the soldier said, and threw the card back.

Silas caught it, nodded like a man mildly inconvenienced, and let the current take them under the rope and out the far side. They didn't open the throttle until the lantern was a smudge behind them and the channel bent away north.

Only then did Mara exhale — a long, controlled breath, the kind you practice.

"'Your relief will find you popular with the mosquitoes,'" she repeated.

"I improvise."

"You *lie* well."

"Ten years of telling air traffic controllers the weather is fine." Silas kept their eyes on the channel. "Turns out most of life is telling someone in authority the situation is fine."

Mara laughed — short, surprised, real. It was the first time Silas had heard her laugh, and it did something to the air in the boat, loosened a knot neither of them had mentioned.

Then the lightning came again, closer, and lit up the whole western sky, and showed them what was waiting downriver.

* * *

**Mara**

The flash lasted half a second, but Mara's memory held images the way her mind held documents — completely, involuntarily, forever.

In that half-second she saw: the river widening into a brown sea, flooded fields on both banks gone to one continuous sheet of water. She saw a village roofline with water lapping at the eaves. She saw, far downstream where the channel narrowed toward the gorge country, a line of lights that was not a town — too regular, too evenly spaced, strung across the water like beads.

A boom. A barrier. Or gunboats at anchor, picketing the narrows.

And she saw, closest of all, directly ahead of them where the storm light fell, a shape half-submerged in the flooded treeline: a longboat, capsized, its hull pale as a bone in the dark. No one clinging to it. No one visible at all.

The darkness came back. The rain filled the silence.

"Did you see it," she said. Not a question.

"The boat," Silas said. "Yes."

"Anyone—"

"Couldn't tell. Current's got to be four knots through there. If anyone went in..." Silas didn't finish. Didn't need to. They both understood the arithmetic of floodwater: minutes, sometimes less.

Silas turned the boat toward the treeline anyway. Of course they did. Mara was learning that about them — that beneath the dry deflections and the pilot's fatalism lived a person who turned toward wreckage the way other people turned away. It was going to get them killed someday. It was also, she suspected, the reason she was sitting in this boat at all instead of drowning in a records office in a town whose name she'd already forgotten.

"Easy," Silas murmured, easing the throttle down, nosing into the flooded trees where the current slackened. The capsized hull came up out of the dark by degrees — twelve feet of longboat, identical make to theirs, swamped and snagged on a submerged fence line. Gear floated around it in a scatter: a jerry can, a woven basket, a child's sandal turning slowly in an eddy.

Mara's chest went tight. She reached for the boat hook.

"Careful," Silas said. "Snags. And if someone's under there—"

"If someone's under there, they've been under there a while." But Mara hooked the hull gently anyway, rolled it, and the floodwater gave up its dead-or-living answer:

Nothing. Empty. Whoever had ridden this boat had gone into the water and the water had kept them, or they'd made the bank somewhere downstream, or — her mind supplied, helplessly, professionally, the archivist cataloguing outcomes — they'd never existed and the boat had drifted empty from some drowned landing upstream.

They searched the near bank with the lamp for twenty minutes, calling out over the rain until their voices went hoarse. They found a second sandal. Nothing else.

When they finally pushed back out into the main channel, neither of them spoke for a long time. The chest sat between Mara's knees, and for the first time it didn't feel like cargo or evidence or history. It felt like a passenger. Something riding with them that had already cost more lives than either of them could count, and might cost more before the capital.

"Odile's second checkpoint," Mara said at last, quietly. "The one we won't see coming."

"Yeah."

"Do you think that was it? The empty boat?"

Silas was quiet for a moment, reading the water, reading the sky, reading the line of lights far downstream that the lightning had shown them and the dark had taken back.

"I think," they said, "that somebody on this river tonight found out what happens to boats that carry things the government doesn't want carried. And I think we should learn the same lesson faster." They opened the throttle. The bow lifted. "Hold on. We run the narrows before dawn, or we don't run them at all."

Mara held on. The rain came down like a verdict, and the river ran fast and brown and hostile toward the gorge country, and somewhere behind them — patient, certain, closing — she could almost hear the sound of a gunboat engine idling in the dark.

Ahead, the first lights of the blockade flickered through the rain.

And ahead of those, three hundred miles on, the presses waited.
