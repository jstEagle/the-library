## 8. The Port of Small Mercies

The port announced itself by smell before sight—diesel, fish rot, woodsmoke, and under it all the green rot of the river itself, which never left your clothes no matter how long you boiled them. Then the bend opened and there it was: a crescent of corrugated roofs sagging over a waterfront street, cranes like herons frozen mid-step, and a hundred boats moored gunwale to gunwale in the brown water as if the whole town had rafted up for safety.

"Port of Small Mercies," Odile said from the stern, spitting tobacco into the wake. "Named by a priest who drowned here. He said God sent small mercies to this place because He knew we couldn't handle the big ones."

"How comforting," Silas said.

"It's not meant to be comforting. It's meant to be accurate."

We came in low and slow, Odile running the boat through the moored fleet with the casual brutality of someone who had done it ten thousand times, our hull kissing other hulls, hands reaching down to shove us along. Nobody looked at us twice, which was the point. In a town this size, looking was a currency, and everyone was poor.

Odile tied us off behind a fish-packing shed whose walls wept brine, and led us up a stairway that smelled of cat and kerosene to a room above it: low-ceilinged, windowless except for one square of glass fogged with river breath, furnished with a mattress, a cot, a table, three chairs, and a fourth chair missing its seat. A single bulb burned behind a wire cage.

"You'll stay here," Odile said. "You'll not go out. You'll not stand at the window. If you must talk, talk quiet, because the walls here are paper and the neighbors are ears." She set a key on the table, then didn't let go of it. "There's a sweep coming. Two days, maybe three. Police boat from the capital, going deck by deck. They're looking for something."

"For us?" Silas asked.

"They're looking for *something*," Odile repeated, which was not an answer, and I understood then that she had already decided how much she would tell us and that the amount was fixed. "My cousin Bess runs the packing shed below. She'll bring food. You pay her in stories, not money—money leaves fingerprints."

She paused at the door, one eye on me, one on Silas, though of course she only had the one eye total; it simply moved between us like a lamp on a swivel.

"The archivist," she said. "The pilot. A chest full of dead men's names. You know what happens to towns that shelter cargo like that?"

"No," Silas said.

"Neither do I. That's the problem." And she was gone, boots ringing down the stairs, leaving us alone with the bulb and the fogged window and the sound of the river working at its pilings like a patient animal.

* * *

The sweep came early.

I woke to Silas's hand on my shoulder—not shaking, just pressing, the way you'd still a frightened animal—and to the sound of engines idling in the fog outside, big diesel engines in no hurry at all, which is the most frightening kind.

"Deck by deck," Silas whispered. "Starting at the far end. We have maybe twenty minutes."

"Odile said two days."

"Odile said maybe."

Below us, Bess's shed came alive—the scrape of crates, the low murmur of men arranging things that wanted arranging. I heard a crate dragged across the floor directly beneath our feet, then another, then the particular hollow knock of a false bottom being fitted into place.

"Get dressed," Silas said. "Not your clothes. There's a bundle on the chair. Fish worker's gear. Put your hair under the cap."

I did as I was told, fingers clumsy, heart going like a bird in a sack. The clothes smelled of fish and diesel and someone else's sweat, and I found, absurdly, that the smell steadied me. Disguise is a kind of armor, even when it's only cotton.

They came up the stairs at half past five.

Three of them. The first was young, wet-eared, holding his rifle like it might go off if he thought about it too hard. The second was older and bored, which was worse. The third stood in the doorway and said nothing at all, and it was the nothing that told me he was the one who mattered.

"Papers," the bored one said.

Silas produced papers. I watched them pass from hand to hand—river trade permits, transit stamps, a medical courier's license with Silas's photograph and a name that wasn't quite Silas's name but was close enough to be a cousin of it. Forgery, and a good one. I filed that away. There were things about my companion I had not yet been told.

"And you?" The bored one turned to me.

Bess materialized behind them, broad and flour-dusted, carrying a tray of dried fish as if delivering it to us were the most natural act in the world. "That's my sister's girl," she said. "Came down with the last run. Deaf in one ear, poor thing, don't waste your breath."

The bored one looked at me. I looked back with the flat, slightly strained attention of someone reading lips they can barely catch. Inside my chest my heart was doing something structural.

He waved the papers. "Courier work. What do you carry?"

"Medicine mostly," Silas said. "Sometimes mail. Sometimes parts."

"Arms?"

"Do I look like I could afford arms?"

Something almost like amusement crossed the man's face and died there. The silent one in the doorway shifted, and his eyes went past all of us, slow, methodical, around the room—the mattress, the cot, the table, the fogged window—and stopped, for exactly one second too long, at the floor beneath the table, where a scuff mark showed where something heavy had been dragged recently.

Because it had. Because at four that morning, Bess and her sons had taken the chest down through a trapdoor I hadn't known existed and buried it under forty kilos of dried mackerel, and mackerel, whatever else you say about it, does not hide a drag mark.

The silent one crouched. Touched the scuff. Stood.

And then, from below, from the shed, came a crash—a whole stack of crates going over, cursing, laughter, Bess's eldest son bellowing at his brother about carelessness—and the silent one's head turned toward the sound, and the moment passed through the room like a fish through reeds, and was gone.

"Move along," the bored one said, handing back the papers. "Curfew's at eight. Don't be on the water after dark."

They went down the stairs. The engines idled away into the fog. Bess set the tray of fish on the table, sat down heavily on the broken chair, and laughed until she had to wipe her eyes.

"Deaf in one ear," she wheezed. "Mara, love, you want to know a secret? So am I. In this family it's practically traditional."

* * *

That evening Odile came back, and with her came six other people, and with the six other people came cards, rum, and a stew of river catfish that tasted like the bottom of the world in the best possible way.

"This is how it works here," Odile said, dealing. "Sweep comes, we close the shutters, we play cards. Police see a room full of honest people losing money to each other, they move on. You—" she pointed a finger at me, then at Silas, "—you are tonight my cousins from upcountry. Names are on the cards. Learn them."

The six were a dockhand named Ferro with forearms like mooring rope; twin sisters who ran the net loft and finished each other's insults; a boy of maybe seventeen called Pip who dealt second-hand off the bottom so smoothly even Odile missed it twice; Bess; Bess's eldest son; and a silent old woman introduced only as Grandmother, who won more than everyone combined and never once changed expression.

I have spent most of my life in rooms with fewer people in them. Archives are quiet that way—you and the dead and the dust, and the dust doesn't cheat at cards. But somewhere between the third hand and the fifth glass of rum, with Pip accusing the twins of signaling and the twins accusing Pip of everything, I realized I was laughing. Actually laughing, ugly and loud, at something Ferro had said about a customs officer and a goat, and the sound of it came out of me strange, like a language I'd studied but never spoken.

Silas noticed. Of course Silas noticed. Those eyes missed nothing, which I'd known since the airstrip, since the chest, since the first night we'd sat up until dawn with the ledger open between us like a patient on a table.

"You're staring," I said.

"I'm watching you lose money."

"I'm winning."

"You're cheating."

"I'm *archiving*," I said, sweeping in the pot. "It's different."

Grandmother took the next three hands without comment, and the table groaned, and the night thickened around us like syrup.

Later—much later, when the twins had gone and Ferro was asleep sitting up and only Odile, Silas, and I remained among the wreckage of cups—I felt it come. The way it always comes, after enough warmth and enough rum: the loosening, the truth rising like silt after a boat passes.

"Ask me," I said to Silas.

"Ask you what?"

"Why I was expelled. From the archive. You've been polite about it for two hundred miles and it's starting to feel like pity."

Silas set down the cup. Odile, ostentatiously, began studying her cards.

"I figured you'd tell me when you wanted to."

"That's the problem with you pilots. All this patience. It's unsettling." I turned my cup in a circle on the table. "Eleven years ago. I was thirty-one, senior cataloguer, special collections. I'd found a gap—a gap in the transfer records, 20XX, the year of the purge. Fourteen boxes logged out of the Vessian provincial registry and never logged back in. Signed out by an authorizing officer whose signature appears—" I stopped. Started again. "Whose signature matters. I filed a formal inquiry. Then another. Then I went over my director's head to the ministry."

"And?"

"And the ministry thanked me for my diligence. And three weeks later I was dismissed for 'unauthorized access to restricted materials'—materials I had accessed through every proper channel, materials that by the time of my hearing had been *reclassified* so that accessing them properly was now impossible. Do you understand? They didn't deny the gap. They built a wall around it and charged me with touching the wall." I heard my own voice go flat and hard, the archive voice, the voice I used for cataloguing atrocities. "Twenty-two years in that building. My whole life in card catalogs. And they walked me out past the reading room and I saw my own desk through the glass with someone else already sitting at it."

Silas said nothing for a while. Outside, the river kept up its work on the pilings.

"My father," I said. "Emmanuel Okonjo. He was a schoolteacher in Vessania. Provincial capital. He wrote letters—he wrote *so many letters*, to the ministry, to the newspapers, to anyone. Organizing teachers. Demanding the government acknowledge what was happening in the interior districts. When the purge came, he was on a list. Not an enemy list. A *tidiness* list. People whose disappearance would make the record cleaner." I looked up. "I've spent eleven years looking for the list, Silas. Not justice. Justice is a word for people with power. I wanted the list. The actual paper. Because paper is the only thing those people ever respected, and paper is the only thing that can't be disappeared twice."

"And when you find it?"

"Then the names come back. All of them. Including his." I swallowed. "Especially his."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, Bess's family was arguing cheerfully about nothing.

"You know what I did before flying medicine?" Silas said quietly. "Before any of it?"

I shook my head.

"I flew survey for a mapping company. Contract work. One season, the contract was Vessian border country—post-purge, resurveying land titles. And every title I helped draw, every boundary line, was drawn over villages that weren't there anymore. Fields gone to bush. Roads to nowhere. And the company knew. The ministry knew. Everyone knew, and everyone drew their clean lines anyway, and I took the paycheck." Silas's jaw worked. "So when I pulled that chest out of the water, it wasn't curiosity. It was arrears. Eleven years of it. I just hadn't known who to pay."

We looked at each other across the ruined card game, across the rum and the lamplight and the smell of fish, and something settled between us—not resolved, nothing so neat, but *aligned*, the way two bearings align on a chart to fix a position. Here. This place. These people.

"Well," Odile said, gathering the cards with a snap. "Now that everyone's confessed, somebody pour the last of the rum before I drink it out of spite."

* * *

I slept badly and woke before dawn to a sound I couldn't place—a low, rhythmic thudding from the floor below, too steady for argument, too irregular for machinery.

I crept down the stairs in my socks.

In the packing shed, lit by a single hurricane lamp, Bess's family was at work—but not on fish. Crates stood open. And inside them, wrapped in oilcloth, packed in straw: rifles. Dozens. Bess's sons moved among them with the tenderness of midwives, and Bess herself stood over the lot with a manifest in her fist, checking numbers against numbers.

She saw me on the stairs and didn't startle, which somehow frightened me more.

"Morning, love," she said. "You sleep?"

"What is this?"

"Small mercies," she said, and tapped the manifest against her palm. "Town like this, the police take and the army takes and the tax man takes. Somebody has to give some back." She looked at me a long moment, one working woman to another, and lowered her voice. "Your friend Odile says you're headed to the capital. To the press house."

I said nothing.

"There's a man coming through here tomorrow," Bess said. "Runs guns south, prints pamphlets north. Calls himself a printer. Owes Odile a debt he'd rather pay than discuss." She rolled the manifest closed and tucked it into her apron. "If I were a woman carrying a chest full of dead men's names, I'd want to know him. And if I were the police—" she nodded upward, toward the fogged window, toward the river, toward the capital and everything waiting in it, "—I'd already know you were coming. Sweeps like today's aren't fishing expeditions, love. They're nets. And somebody, somewhere upstream, has told them exactly where to cast."

The lamp hissed. Below the floor, beneath the mackerel, the chest sat dark and patient, holding its thousand names.

"Tomorrow," Bess said, and handed me a cup of coffee strong enough to strip paint. "Best you hear it from the printer himself."
