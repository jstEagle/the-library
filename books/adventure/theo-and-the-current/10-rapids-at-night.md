## 10. Rapids at Night

The Gorge Narrows didn't have a name so much as a reputation. Odile had given us the shape of it with her one good eye and a scarred finger dragged across the chart: a twelve-mile throat where the river dropped forty feet through black stone, the water compressed into something less like a current and more like a fist.

"Nobody runs it at night," she'd said.

"That's the point," Mara had said.

I hadn't argued. I was learning that arguing with Mara Okonjo was like arguing with weather—technically possible, practically pointless, and you always ended up wet anyway.

Now it was two hours past sunset and we were tied up in a side slough below the last checkpoint, waiting for the moon to go behind cloud. The longboat sat low in the water, riding heavy with fuel drums, food, and the chest lashed under a tarp amidships like a sleeping animal we were all pretending not to hear breathing. The chest had developed a gravity of its own over the past hundred miles. You caught yourself looking at it. Checking the lashings. Touching the tarp the way you'd touch a wound to see if it was still bleeding.

Odile's cousin's boy, Teo—a wiry fourteen-year-old who piloted the boat with the casual contempt of someone who'd been born on brown water—crouched at the stern tiller, chewing a stalk of sugarcane and watching the sky.

"Cloud's coming," he said. "Twenty minutes, maybe."

I checked the engine for the fourth time. A twenty-horse longtail, shaft propped, propeller nicked but true. It would do. It had to. There was no version of this where it didn't do.

Mara sat on the gunwale with her knees drawn up, the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, her arms around it the way you hold a child or a bomb. She'd barely spoken since the checkpoint lights came on an hour ago—three boats strung across the downstream mouth of the gorge, floodlights raking the water, soldiers moving slow and bored behind sandbags. Hale's work. We'd seen the gunboat's silhouette upstream at dusk, big and patient as a crocodile.

"They'll expect us to wait out the night and run at dawn," she said now, to no one in particular. "So we won't."

"Right," I said.

"You think it's madness."

I thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty more than comfort. "I think the river doesn't care what anyone expects. That's the only reason I'm not terrified." I paused. "That's a lie. I'm terrified. But the arithmetic still works. Twelve miles of white water beats three checkpoints with radios."

Teo spat sugarcane fibers into the water. "My uncle ran it once at night," he said. "Lost the boat. Kept his life. He says the trick is to not fight it. The river already knows where it wants you."

"And if where it wants you is upside down?" I asked.

"Then you were fighting," Teo said, with the serene cruelty of the young.

The cloud arrived. The moon went out like a lamp snuffed between fingers, and the world contracted to the width of our running light—a single dim red bulb Teo had wired low on the bow, legal enough to pass as a fishing boat, faint enough to be invisible past fifty yards. Mara stowed the ledger in the waterproof satchel strapped to her body, cinched her life vest, and looked at me across the dark.

"Silas."

"Yeah."

"If we founder—the chest comes first."

I looked at her. In the red glow her face was all planes and shadow, and there was nothing soft in what she was asking. She wasn't asking me to save her. She was telling me the order of things, in case the order of things came due.

"No," I said.

"It's twenty years of dead people. It's my father's name. It's the only copy in the world of—"

"I know exactly what it is. And no." I kept my voice level, which took more effort than running the gorge would. "You can't testify from the bottom of a river, Mara. You're the courier. The papers are cargo. Cargo gets replaced; couriers don't. If it goes down, it goes down, and we surface, and we find another way. That's the whole reason I copied—" I stopped.

Her head turned slowly. "Copied what?"

The red light buzzed. Somewhere upstream, a frog made a sound like a rusty hinge.

"Nothing," I said.

"Silas."

"We're burning moonless dark, and you want to have this conversation now?"

"Yes," she said. "Because if we capsize, I want to know whether I'm diving after a ledger or after a lie."

I exhaled through my nose. There are moments in a cockpit when you realize you've been flying on instruments that disagree with each other, and you have to pick one and commit before the ground picks for you. This was one of those, except the ground was water and the instrument was a woman who could gut me with a sentence.

"The cartographer," I said. "In Kessoum. While you were haggling over the channel map, I traced six pages. The names. The authorizations. Hale's signature. Enough to hang the truth on even if the original sank." I met her stare. "Insurance. Not betrayal. There's a difference, and the difference is that I did it so you'd never have to choose."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then, quietly: "You should have told me."

"Yeah. I know. I'm telling you now."

"Why now?"

"Because you asked. And because—" I gestured at the black throat of the gorge ahead, the distant sodium glare of the checkpoint, the whole indifferent machinery of the state arranged against two people in a leaking boat. "Because after tonight, everything's either over or it isn't, and I didn't want to walk into that carrying something from you."

She held my eyes a beat longer. Whatever moved behind them, she folded it away, unspent. "Teo," she said. "Take us in."

* * *

The first mile was almost kind.

The river narrowed by degrees, banks sliding close, walls of stone rising on either side until they blotted out even the starlight, and the current picked us up the way a hand picks up a leaf. Teo stood at the tiller with his weight forward, reading the water by feel and memory and some sense I didn't have a name for, feathering the throttle, swinging the nose left, then hard right, around rocks that announced themselves only as a change in the sound of the current—a hiss becoming a growl becoming a roar.

I crouched amidships with a paddle, keeping us off the walls. The air went cold and mineral. Spray came off the waves in sheets. Every few seconds the bow would lift and slam and the whole hull would shudder like a struck drum, and beneath my feet, through the floorboards, I could feel the chest shifting in its lashings, a dull knock like a heartbeat.

"Left channel!" Teo shouted over the noise. "Stay left! Right side has a hole!"

"A hole?"

"A hole!" he repeated, as if that explained anything, and maybe it did, because thirty seconds later the right half of the river simply ended—a seam of smooth green water rolling over nothing, pouring down into a churning pit that ate the moonlight even though there wasn't any. The left channel was maybe eight meters wide and boiling. Teo threaded it. I will spend the rest of my life being grateful to a fourteen-year-old with a sugarcane habit.

At mile four the river dropped.

There was no warning I could read—no horizon line, no pale water ahead, just Teo suddenly shouting "Big one! Paddle! PADDLE!" and then the bottom fell out of the world. The boat went weightless. For one full second we were flying, spray streaming off the bow, the red running light carving a smear through the dark—and then we hit, and the impact came up through my boots and my spine and my teeth, and the world became water.

Not water around us. Water *through* us. A standing wave broke over the bow and filled the hull knee-deep in a single blow, and the current took the loaded boat sideways and drove us at the rock wall like it was angry we existed. I jammed my paddle against stone and shoved, shoulders screaming, and the hull ground along the wall with a sound like tearing sheet metal, and then we were off, spinning, shipping water, the engine screaming as Teo fought the throttle.

"Bail!" I roared. "Bail bail bail—"

Mara was already bailing. Of course she was. She'd found the gourd scoop somewhere in the chaos and was throwing water over the side with both hands, methodical, furious, the ledger satchel strapped tight to her body, and I had one clear flash of thought—*she hasn't let go of it once*—and then the next wave hit.

It came from the left, off a rebounding wall, a slab of green water taller than the gunwale, and it didn't break over us so much as pick us up and fold us. The boat rolled. I remember the specific physics of it with horrible clarity: the slow tilt, the moment of suspension when the water and the boat and I were all briefly agreeing on an angle that couldn't possibly hold, and then the cold closing over my head and the world turning into noise and pressure and brown.

The river took me down. It spun me—I felt my boot strike something soft, the gunwale, maybe, or Teo—and then the current had me properly, and I learned very quickly that a flooded gorge at night does not want you dead. It just doesn't care. It tumbled me end over end, drove me against stone, scraped me along the bottom, and spat me up for half a breath so I could fill my lungs before dragging me under again.

I've spent my whole life around water. Bush pilots learn rivers the way sailors learn stars—they're the map, the runway, the emergency landing strip, the thing that kills you. I knew the rule: don't fight it, float feet-first, keep your head up. Knowing the rule and obeying it while the gorge hammered you against its own walls were different animals entirely.

I surfaced near a rock fin, got an arm over it, and hung there coughing, deafened, blind, the roar pressing in from every direction. The red running light was gone. The boat was gone. The engine noise—

No. Not gone. Faint, downstream, stuttering. Someone had the engine running.

Someone was in the water.

I heard her before I saw her—a cry cut short by a wave, swallowed, gone. I put my face into the spray and listened the way you listen in fog for another engine, stripping out the river's voice layer by layer, and there: a gasp, downstream-left, close to the wall where the eddy lines tangled.

"MARA!"

Nothing. The roar ate it.

I went in.

I don't remember deciding to. That's the part I keep returning to, later, in the quiet. There was no decision. The arm over the rock opened and the river had me again and I was swimming—not well, not bravely, just *toward*, hauling myself down the eddy line hand over hand along the slick stone while the main current tried every second to peel me off and add me to its inventory. My shoulder hit something. I grabbed cloth. Life vest strap. I hauled, and she came up out of the water coughing, hair plastered across her face, and her first act above the surface was to grab my collar with both hands and scream directly into my ear:

"THE SATCHEL—"

"Let it GO—"

"IT'S ON MY BODY, YOU IDIOT—"

And it was. Strapped to her chest, cinched down, waterproofed, exactly where she'd promised it would be. She hadn't lost it. She'd lost everything else—her shoes, her knife, probably several years of her life—but the ledger rode her ribs like a second heart.

The eddy behind the rock fin caught us both. I got an arm around her chest from behind, locked my grip, and kicked for the shallows at the fin's tail, and the river, having made its point, let us go with the grudging indifference of a giant releasing two insects it had merely been examining.

We crawled out onto a gravel bank in the dark, and I lay there on my back with the rain starting to fall on my face, and I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came up out of me like water from a cracked pipe—shaking, hysterical, half terror and half something I didn't have a word for yet.

"What," Mara wheezed beside me, "is funny."

"You," I gasped. "Capsized. Half-drowned. Nearly eaten by a gorge. And your first word to me was about the paperwork."

She pushed herself up on her elbows. Rain ticked on the stones. Downstream, faint through the roar, the longtail engine stuttered and steadied—Teo, alive, keeping the boat alive, God knew how.

"It's not paperwork," she said. "It's twenty years of—"

"—dead people. I know. I know." I turned my head and looked at her. She was shaking, whether from cold or adrenaline I couldn't tell, mud to the eyebrows, one cheek bleeding where the rocks had written their signature, and she was holding the satchel straps with both hands like a woman holding a rail in an earthquake. "You scared ten years off my life tonight."

"You jumped in after me."

"You told me the chest comes first. I told you no. I was establishing policy."

Something happened to her face then. It was small—it was the kind of thing you'd miss entirely unless you'd spent a hundred miles learning to read her—but it happened. The fury drained out of it, and underneath the fury there was just a tired, soaked, frightened woman lying on a gravel bar in the rain, and for a moment she let me see that. Just me. The rain filled the silence between us, and neither of us moved, and the space of about three seconds contained more unspoken material than the entire previous week.

Then she reached over and punched me in the arm, weakly, with a closed fist that carried roughly the force of a falling leaf.

"For copying my ledger," she said.

"Noted," I said.

"And thank you," she said, so quietly the river nearly took it, "for jumping."

I wanted to say something worthy of that. Something clean and certain, the kind of line that belongs in a book. What came out instead was: "Your teeth are chattering. We need fire, or we need to move, and we can't have fire."

"We move." She stood, swaying, and pulled me up, and her hand stayed on my forearm a moment longer than balance required. Neither of us remarked on it. Some things you carry without naming, like cargo lashed under a tarp.

We limped downstream along the bank, following the engine note, and found Teo a quarter mile on, beached in a backwater, bailing the longboat with the same gourd scoop Mara had used, his face gray with shock and set with a stubbornness that clearly ran in the family. The boat had a new crack in the port strake and had lost a fuel drum and most of our food. The chest's lashings had held. Of course they had. Mara retied every knot herself, twice, by feel, in the rain.

We ran the last seven miles of the Narrows at dawn-gray, half-sunk, silent, and slid out of the gorge into the wide brown lower river as the sun came up behind the rain clouds like a coin behind smoked glass. No checkpoints here. Not yet. The water stretched out flat and endless toward the capital, three hundred miles downstream, and for the first time since Kessoum I let myself believe we might actually make it.

Mara sat beside me in the bilge, wringing out her hair, and gave me a look I couldn't parse—something settled, something decided.

"There's a port two days downstream," she said. "Small mercies, the boatmen call it. Odile has friends there. We resupply, we hide, we push on."

Behind us, far back upriver, faint as a memory, I thought I heard the low diesel thump of a gunboat coming down from the checkpoint to count its drowned.

I told myself it was the engine. I told myself a lot of things that morning.
