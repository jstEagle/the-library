## 11. Odile's Price

Odile had one eye and a hundred ways of seeing.

The missing one she'd given to the river, back when she was young enough to think the river took payment in flesh instead of secrets. The hundred others she'd spent forty years building: a boy on the fuel dock who counted strangers twice, a woman in the customs shed who read telegrams upside down, a fisherman who anchored where he could see the police jetty and pretended to be drunk by noon. By those means Odile knew everything that moved through the Port of Small Mercies before it finished moving, and most of what only intended to.

So she knew about Hale's gunboat three days before it came. She knew about the checkpoints at Gorge Narrows and the sweep teams walking the levee with photographs of two faces she'd fed breakfast to not a week ago. And she knew—because the fisherman heard it from a customs man who owed her his brother's life—that Inspector Hale had sent a private message along with the official ones. Not to her superiors. To her.

*The pilot and the archivist. You know where they are. You know what happens to ports that harbor them.*

No threat in it. That was the worst part. Hale wrote like a man doing arithmetic.

Odile sat in her back room with the message burning a hole in her shirt pocket and listened to the rain come down on the tin roof like applause for something terrible, and she thought about Vessania.

She thought about it more than she let anyone believe. In the port they called her the One-Eyed Ferrywoman, hard as a cleat, a woman who'd sold passage to smugglers and priests and once, famously, a fleeing cardinal, and who never asked why because asking was bad business. They thought the eye she'd lost had made her blind to everything but profit. Let them think it. Blindness was also good business.

But Odile had been born Vessian. Her name hadn't always been Odile. There had been a mother who sang off-key and a brother who wanted to be an engineer and a father with a boatyard on the Serein bend, and all of them had gone into the ground—or into the river, which was the same ground, wetter—in the spring of the purge, when Odile was nineteen and away trading upriver. She'd come home to a boatyard burned to the waterline and neighbors who wouldn't meet her eye, and she'd understood, with the clarity that only orphans get, that the whole world had agreed to forget, and that forgetting was contagious, and that she would catch it or die of it.

She'd caught it. She'd renamed herself after a saint nobody worshipped anymore, bought a leaky ferry with money she didn't ask too many questions about, and built a port where every kind of fugitive could drink without explaining themselves. It wasn't penance. Penance implied you believed you'd done wrong. It was just the shape survival took when it hardened: a woman with one eye, watching everything, saying nothing, keeping a ledger of names in her head that no government would ever pay her for.

Until now. Until a bush pilot with a busted plane and a disgraced archivist with a chest full of dead men's names had walked into her back room and started dealing cards, and Odile had looked at Mara Okonjo across the table—at the hunger in her, the same hunger Odile had smothered in herself twenty years ago—and felt the old thing stir under its scar tissue like a fish under ice.

And then she'd gone out to the radio shack and told Hale exactly where they were.

* * *

The card game was still going when she made up her mind. It was always going; it was the port's heart, this room behind the chandlery where Odile settled debts and spread rumors and decided, night by night, who the river would be kind to. Tonight the players were Silas, Mara, the fuel-dock boy, and a netmender who cheated so badly everyone let him.

Odile stood in the doorway with the rain hammering behind her and watched them a moment. The pilot laughed at something—the netmender's cheating, probably—and it was a real laugh, unguarded, the laugh of a person who'd spent years being invisible and had briefly forgotten how. The archivist didn't laugh. Mara Okonjo held her cards like documents, studied them like testimony, and when she glanced up and caught Odile watching, there was nothing soft in her face at all. Just the question she carried everywhere, the one that had eaten her career: *do you know something I don't?*

Everyone knew something Mara didn't. Odile knew the most.

"Deal me out," she said. "Pilot. A word."

Silas followed her into the chandlery, wiping rain from their forearms, easy in the way of people who lived alone with machines. "If this is about the tab—"

"It's about the gunboat." Odile set a lamp on the counter between them and let the silence do its work. She watched the laughter drain out of the pilot's face by degrees, like water finding level. "Ninety miles downstream, maybe less in this current. Two more at the Narrows checkpoint. Your friend Okonjo has made you famous."

"How long have you known?"

"Three days." She let that sit. Let the pilot do the arithmetic she could see happening behind their eyes: three days of sleeping under Odile's roof, three days of repairs and resupply and planning, three days of telling her things.

"You told them," Silas said quietly. Not a question either. Pilots were quick that way; they had to read weather in a sky that gave them nothing.

"I told them where you were. Not what you carry." Odile poured two fingers of the rough cane spirit she kept for funerals and negotiations, which in her experience were often the same event, and pushed the glass across. "There's a difference. There has to be, or this port drowns."

"With you in it."

"With four hundred people in it. With the widow Amara and her six orphans. With the customs man whose brother I ransomed. With every soul who ever ran to me because nowhere else would take them." Odile drank. The spirit tasted like it always did, like punishment accepted gladly. "You two are two. I am four hundred. That is the whole of my morality, pilot, and I've never pretended otherwise."

Silas turned the glass but didn't drink. "Mara can't hear this."

"Mara already knows."

That landed harder than the gunboat news. Silas stared at her. "How?"

"Because she watches me the way I watch other people. Because she saw me come back from the radio shack Tuesday night with my collar wet and my hands wrong, and she filed it." Odile almost smiled. "Your archivist misses nothing except how to be happy. Go get her. This concerns her more than you."

* * *

They stood together at the counter, the three of them, lamplight and rain, and Odile told the rest of it plainly because she was done pretending she was a simpler woman than she was.

"The message said hand you over or the port pays. But there was a second line. Private. Hale's own hand, not the ministry cipher." She laid the paper on the counter between them and did not watch them read it, because she had learned long ago that you learn more from people when you aren't looking at them.

Mara read it aloud, because of course she did, because words were her trade and she trusted them more than faces. "*'Tell the archivist the names in that ledger include a man named Okonjo, senior registrar, Vessian civil archive. Tell her the signature beneath the authorization order is mine.'*" Mara stopped. The rain filled the pause. "He's telling me he signed it."

"He's telling you he wants you to know he signed it," Odile said. "There's a difference, and I've spent my whole life living in differences. A man who hides his crimes wants you ignorant. A man who shows you his signature wants something else. Absolution, maybe. Or he wants you to understand that he is not the top of this thing, and never was."

"He could be lying."

"He could." Odile shrugged with the shoulder that still worked properly. "But I've traded messages with that man for six years, ever since the ministry decided my port was useful and my discretion was purchasable. He has never once lied to me. He's delayed me, misled me, threatened me, and once saved my fuel dock from a fire that was meant to look accidental—but he has never lied. Men who lie run out of true things to spend. He hoards his."

Silas spoke carefully. "You've been reporting to him for six years."

"Every boat that stops here, every cargo, every name worth selling. And in exchange, this port stays off the lists. No sweeps. No 'inspections' that end with buildings burned and boys disappeared." Odile met the pilot's eye and did not flinch from what she found there, because flinching was for people with something left to lose. "You want to judge me, wait until you've buried everyone you love and see what bargains you cut to keep the graves safe."

"I'm not judging," Silas said, and the strange thing was that Odile believed them. "I'm trying to figure out why you're telling us."

"Because I'm done." It came out of her before she'd decided to say it, twenty years of dammed-up water through a crack, and Odile hated how it sounded—not hard, not clever, just old and tired and true. "Do you know what I was doing Tuesday night at the radio shack? I was reading him your position, and my hands were shaking, and do you know why? Because your archivist reads names out loud in her sleep. I've heard her through the wall. Okonjo. Serei. Almaz. A hundred names, every night, like a rosary. And every name she says, I know the family. I know the boatyard. I know the bend in the river where they lived." She set down the glass with great care, because if she didn't she'd throw it. "I built a whole life on not knowing, pilot. On selling the knowing and keeping none of it. And then you two walked in here carrying the one thing in the world I swore I'd never touch again, and I found out that not-knowing was just a room I'd locked myself in, and the door was never actually locked."

Mara hadn't spoken since the message. Now she said, very quietly, "You're Vessian."

"My name was Odile Serein-Bend before it was anything. My father built boats. My brother was going to build bridges." Odile tapped the counter once, twice, the way she tapped the hull of her ferry before a crossing, an old habit, a knock on wood that had never once answered. "The ledger you carry has my family in it. Page nine, probably. I've never seen it and I don't need to. I've carried page nine in my head for twenty years."

The rain went on. Somewhere out on the dock the fuel boy was singing to himself, badly, happily, four hundred reasons wrapped in one bad voice.

"So here is my price," Odile said. "Not for the boat—I gave you the boat, that was trade, that was clean. This price is for what I'm about to do, which is dirty, and I want witnesses to the dirt so I can't take it back."

* * *

What she did was this.

She went to the radio shack with both of them watching, keyed the frequency Hale used, and reported—truthfully, precisely, in the flat cadence of six years' practice—that the fugitives had taken the eastern channel above the Narrows, heading downstream, making poor time in the flood water. All of it true. What she did not report was the rest: that the eastern channel flooded first and drained fastest, that by dawn it would be a brown ribbon through drowned forest too shallow for a gunboat's draft, while the main channel—the one every checkpoint watched—would stay deep and slow and full of patrol boats chasing a report that was already twelve hours stale.

Then she went to the chandlery and pulled a chart from under the floorboards, one of only three copies in existence, drawn by a cartographer who'd died owing her money, and marked the channel with a grease pencil in three strokes.

"Dawn," she said. "The eastern water drops fast. If you're not past the second bend by full light, you'll be mud-bound in sight of the checkpoint, and no price I pay will cover you. And pilot—" she fixed Silas with the one eye, the eye that had watched everything for forty years and finally, tonight, had looked inward and found something there worth saving, "—the engine on that boat of mine is older than both of you put together. Pray louder than you steer."

Mara stood holding the chart like it might dissolve. "Why?" she asked. Not accusing. Worse—genuinely asking, an archivist requesting a source. "After six years. After everything it cost you. Why now?"

Odile looked at her for a long moment. Then she reached into her shirt and took out the folded paper she'd carried for twenty years, soft as cloth from handling, and put it in Mara's hand.

It was a photograph. A boatyard on a river bend. A broad woman with sawdust in her hair, laughing, one arm around a skinny boy holding a model bridge. On the back, in a young man's careful hand: *Papa's yard, the last good spring.*

"That's my price," Odile said. "You carry that name too, archivist. Serein. When you read them out loud—and you will read them out loud, I've known that since the night you got here—you say that one clear. You say it like it's somebody." She turned away before her face could do anything unforgivable, and busied her hands with the chart, folding it against the rain. "Now go. Dawn won't wait, and neither will the gunboat, and neither, God help me, will I."

Behind her she heard them gathering their gear, quick and quiet, and the fuel boy's bad singing, and the rain coming down on the Port of Small Mercies like the sky itself had finally decided to testify.

At the door, Mara paused. "Odile. The name on the back—who wrote it?"

"My brother," Odile said. "The one who was going to build bridges." She kept her back turned, kept her hands moving. "Build yours fast, archivist. Some of us are standing on the ones that fell."

She listened to their footsteps fade down the dock, to the rope creak, to the old engine cough twice and catch, and then she went to the window and watched the boat slide out into the dark brown water, small and stubborn, carrying her father's boatyard and her brother's bridge and every name she'd ever refused to say.

Then she picked up the radio handset, and called Inspector Hale, and told him the truth.

All of it. Except the part that mattered.
