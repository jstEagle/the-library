## 13. Interception

The reeds began an hour before dawn, and by full light they had swallowed the river whole.

We'd been running the marsh channel since midnight—Odile's map, the cartographer's channel, all of it a rumor stitched together with pencil lines—and for a while I let myself believe we'd slipped the net. The water here was the color of strong tea, flat as poured glass, broken only by the wake of our longboat and the occasional explosion of a heron going up out of the reeds like something shot. Mara sat forward with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, the way she'd held it for two days straight, sleeping sitting up with her arms around it like a child around a dog.

Odile's cousin's boy, Teo, poled us through the narrows where the channel braided into three and none of them were marked. He was maybe nineteen, silent as his aunt, and he read the water the way I read weather—which is to say without thinking about it at all. Every so often he'd shift the pole to one side without explanation, and thirty seconds later we'd slide past a sandbar or a drowned fence post sitting just under the surface, patient as a landmine.

"Smoke," Mara said.

I'd smelled it too. Woodsmoke, faint, riding on a wind that shouldn't have been able to carry anything across that much open marsh. Diesel under it. Gunboats burned a particular mix—I'd ferried enough fuel drums upriver in my years of invisible flying to know it the way you know a person's cough.

Teo heard it a moment after we did. He stopped poling. We drifted.

"How many engines?" I asked him.

He held up two fingers, then thought about it, then held up three.

Three gunboats. In a marsh with one navigable channel that Odile had sold us fair and then sold us out anyway—that was the part I kept circling back to, the way a tongue finds a chipped tooth. She'd given us the head start herself. She'd stood on her dock with her one eye wet in the lamplight and told us to run, and she'd told Hale exactly which direction we'd run in, because the port would still be standing tomorrow either way, and Odile had buried people at Vessania too, and dead people don't get to choose between betraying someone and saving everyone else.

I didn't blame her. That was the ugly thing. I understood her completely, and understanding made it worse.

"They'll have the main channel blocked," Mara said. Her voice was level. She'd been leveling her voice for three hundred miles, and I was starting to hear the effort underneath it, the way you hear the strain in a rope before it parts. "The cartographer's channel comes out—" She stopped. Looked at me.

"Where?"

"It comes out into the open river." She said it flat, the way you hand someone bad news you've already finished grieving over. "Half a mile downstream of the Narrows checkpoint."

So there it was. Run the gauntlet blind or turn back into a marsh with three gunboats combing it, and behind us the monsoon coming up the valley like a debt collector. I looked at the sky. It was the color of old pewter, low and soft-edged, and the air had that thick, sweet pressure to it that meant the real rain—the walls of it, the kind that erased horizons—was hours away, not days.

I'd spent twenty years making myself small. Invisible flights, unmarked strips, cargo manifests written in pencil so they could be rewritten. A man builds a life like that the way you build a house on a riverbank: carefully, knowing the flood is coming someday, telling yourself that someday is a word that means never.

The chest sat between Mara's feet, and under the oilcloth under the canvas under the lock, four hundred names waited to be read aloud.

"Teo," I said. "How shallow can this boat go?"

He gave me a look that said: how stupid are you, pilot man, and then answered anyway. "Knee-deep. Once. After that we are walking."

* * *

We came out of the reeds at nine in the morning into a wall of gray rain you could stand inside of, and the open river was not open—it was a moving brown sea with whitecaps on it, and somewhere in that violence, half a mile downstream, three gunboats rode at anchor across the channel like a fence made of iron.

Hale had done his homework. Of course he had. He'd locked down every exit from the marsh except the one nobody sane would take, and then he'd anchored his boats wide enough apart that a longboat couldn't slip between them but close enough that their guns overlapped. I could see the shapes of them through the rain, blurred, running lights burning yellow in the murk. Soldiers on the decks under tarps. A searchlight sweeping the water even in daylight, more habit than use.

Mara crouched beside me under the boat's canvas awning, both of us soaked through anyway, watching the cordon through a gap in the reeds.

"He'll parley," she said.

"You know that how?"

"Because I know him." She said it without flinching, and that was its own kind of wound. "He had me arrested once, and he brought me coffee first. He reads people the way I read documents. He'll want to look me in the eye when he asks. He'll want me to say yes to my face, because if I say yes to my face, then it isn't coercion, it's agreement, and he can live with himself."

"And if you say no to your face?"

She was quiet for a moment. Rain drummed the canvas. Downriver, the searchlight swept and swept, patient as time.

"Then he has a problem," she said. "And Hale solves problems with procedure. Procedure says: sink the boat, recover the chest, file the report. Procedure does not account for—" she gestured, at me, at the boat, at the whole ridiculous enterprise of us, "—pilots."

There was a loudhailer crackle from the nearest gunboat, distorted by rain, and then a voice, amplified, formal, absurdly courteous across all that brown water:

*"Longboat in the reed line. This is Inspector Hale of the Interior Ministry. You are requested to hold position. No action will be taken while talks proceed. You have my word."*

His word. I looked at Mara. She looked at the rain.

"He keeps his word," she said quietly. "That's the worst thing about him. He just keeps it in service of burying children in unmarked files."

* * *

They sent a launch. One officer and four marines with rifles slung—not aimed, pointedly not aimed, the whole choreography of restraint—and the launch idled alongside our longboat in the seething rain, and a man stepped across onto our gunwale like he was stepping onto his own porch.

Inspector Hale was taller than I'd imagined and younger. Forty, maybe, gray coming in early at the temples, rain running off a black rubber coat that probably cost more than Odile's boat. He took off his cap, which was some kind of courtesy, and looked at Mara Okonjo for a long moment before he said anything, and what passed over his face in that moment wasn't cruelty. It was grief wearing a uniform.

"Mara."

"Elias."

"I'm authorized to offer you amnesty," he said. "Full amnesty. Your record expunged. Your archive access restored. Your father's file released to you personally—all of it, everything Interior holds." His voice stayed level, official, but his eyes didn't. His eyes were doing something desperate and quiet, like a man reading a document by failing light. "In exchange for the chest and its contents. Unopened. Verified sealed by me, personally, and destroyed under witness."

"The contents are already known," Mara said. "I've memorized the ledger. Killing the paper kills nothing now."

"Paper persuades. Memory doesn't." He said it gently, almost kindly, like a teacher correcting a favorite student. "You know that better than anyone. Twenty years, Mara. Twenty years and no one acted on rumor. Paper with seals and signatures—that's different. That's a weapon. Give me the weapon."

"The weapon belongs to the dead."

"Mara." And there it cracked, just slightly, the official voice splitting along an old seam. "They will sink this boat. If you refuse, I cannot stop what happens next, and I am telling you that as the man who—" He stopped. Started again, lower, the loudhailer men pretending not to listen, the rain covering us like a curtain. "As the man who signed the authorization order because the alternative was being removed and replaced by someone who wouldn't have warned you at the port. Someone who wouldn't have let Odile's people live. I have been slow-walking this since the day the chest surfaced. I am at the end of what slowing looks like."

I watched Mara take that in. I watched her hands, wrapped around the oilcloth bundle, go white at the knuckles, and I watched her lift her chin, and I understood suddenly, completely, the way you understand a river current—without argument—that whatever she said next was going to be no, and that no was going to cost everything, and that she was going to say it anyway, because that was who she'd been since she was a girl reading her father's name off a disappeared-persons list in a basement archive.

"You signed the order," she said. "You could have refused. You could be in a cell right now instead of on this boat, and you'd be clean, Elias. Instead you're here offering me my father's file like it's currency. Like his name is yours to trade."

"Mara—"

"No." Quiet. Terrible. Final. "Take your amnesty. Bury it with the rest of them."

Hale closed his eyes. When he opened them, the grief was gone, packed away, and the inspector was back, and behind him on the gunboat deck I saw a marine raise a signal flag, and I saw Hale see me seeing it, and for one suspended second the three of us hung there in the rain—him knowing, me knowing, the machinery starting whether he willed it or not.

"Last chance," he said, to her, softly. "Both of you. Walk away. There's no shame in—"

"There's nothing left but this," I said.

He looked at me then, really looked, the way you look at weather you've decided to fly into anyway. "Pilot," he said. "You're carrying something heavier than cargo."

"Yeah," I said. "Finally."

* * *

Here is what twenty years of bush flying teaches you that no flight school can: commitment is a thing you do before you're ready, always, or not at all. You don't commit to the strip when you see it—you commit forty miles out, because past the point of no return there is no decision left to make, only execution, and execution is calm. Execution is the easiest part.

I put my hand on Mara's shoulder and pushed her down below the gunwale with the ledger, and I said, "When I say, you hold on to Teo and you do not let go," and she said, "Silas, what are you—" and I said, "Now," and swung the longboat's nose downstream and opened the throttle.

The engine caught with a cough and a roar. The launch alongside us was still tied alongside, its officer mid-turn, and I will spend the rest of my life knowing exactly what I did in the next four seconds: I rammed it.

Not hard—we were a wooden longboat and they were steel—but hard enough. Our bow ground up their hull with a shriek like the world's largest nail being pulled, threw the officer off his feet into the bilge, spun their launch broadside into the current, and bought us ten seconds of pure chaos in which three gunboats' worth of soldiers were all looking at each other instead of at us.

Then the river took us, and the rain came down like the sky had been waiting for permission.

"Down! Everyone down!" The first shots came ragged, panicked, firing at a wake instead of a boat. Something smacked the transom an inch from Teo's head and went through the far rail. I hauled the tiller hard, putting the reed line's edge between us and the middle gunboat, running the whitecaps at an angle that would have made any sane boatman weep. The longboat slammed, slammed again, shipped a ton of brown water that Mara bailed with a rice pot, screaming something I couldn't hear over the engine and the rain and the guns.

The third gunboat was ahead of us. I hadn't seen it—anchored farther out, in the open channel, and it was turning now, its wake unrolling toward us like a sentence being written.

"Hold on!" I hauled the tiller over and we ran straight at it.

Not at it—at the gap behind it, the fifty meters of churned water between its stern and the reed line on the far side, a gap that was closing as it turned, a gap that existed for maybe eight seconds, and I flew the boat the way I'd flown a thousand mountain passes: committed, forty miles out, no decisions left.

Bullets stitched the water. Something tore across the outside of my left arm—a hot bright line, shockingly casual, the way a whip cracks—and I heard Mara scream my name and I heard myself say, "I'm fine, bail," in a voice that sounded like somebody else's, and then we were through, through the gap, through the cordon, into the open river with three gunboats astern and the storm ahead and blood running warm down my wrist into the bilge where it vanished instantly into all that brown water, as if the river had already agreed to keep the secret.

Behind us, the guns fell silent. Not because they'd lost us. Because Hale had called them off—I knew it the way I knew weather. Somewhere back there in the rain, a man who loved her was standing on a steel deck holding a radio handset, choosing, second by second, how slowly to obey.

Ahead of us, the horizon was gone. The monsoon had arrived in earnest, a gray wall miles high, and the river ran straight into the heart of it.

Mara pressed her hands against my arm, tearing a sleeve into strips, tying them tight above the graze, her face inches from mine, shouting over the wind: "You rammed a government launch!"

"It seemed polite!" I shouted back. "Under the circumstances!"

She laughed—one wild, disbelieving bark of it, half terror, half something else entirely—and then she leaned her forehead against my shoulder, careful of the arm, and stayed there while Teo drove us into the storm, and I understood that whatever happened next, however this ended, there was no version of my life anymore that didn't have her in it. The point of no return was forty miles behind us. Maybe three hundred. Maybe twenty years.

The rain swallowed the gunboats. The river carried us on.

By nightfall we'd need shelter, dry cloth for the wound, and a place the storm couldn't find us—and the only country ahead was the fever country, the stilt villages, the deep green nowhere where the maps themselves gave up.

My arm throbbed like a second heartbeat. I steered us into the dark.
