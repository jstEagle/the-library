## 14. Fever Country

The stilt village had no name on any map, which was exactly why Odile's cousin had sent them there.

Mara counted the houses from the bow of the dugout as they came in through the drowned forest: eleven structures on ironwood poles, connected by walkways that rose and fell with the water like the ribs of some sleeping animal. Smoke from cooking fires threaded up through gaps in the thatch. Children stopped mid-shout to watch the boat slide beneath their feet. An old woman looked down at Silas slumped against the gunwale, at the bandage around his ribs gone brown and stiff, and said something in a language Mara didn't know to a man who was probably her son.

The son nodded once and pointed to the house at the far end of the village, the one built apart from the others, closest to the deep channel.

"They know why we're here," Mara said.

"Odile's cousin sent word ahead." Silas tried to sit up and failed, his breath going thin and wet. "Or they're just kind."

"Both can be true."

He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and Mara put her arm behind his shoulders and held him steady while the boat bumped against the ladder poles and hands reached down to help her haul him up into the light.

* * *

The house was one room above the water, floorboards worn smooth by generations of bare feet, a cookstove in the corner vented through a hole in the roof. The old woman—her name was Amara, which made Mara flinch the first time she heard it, and the old woman noticed and smiled as if she'd done it on purpose—set out a mat, a basin, a clay pot of something bitter and green.

"Fever bark," Amara said in trade tongue, tapping the pot. "Steep it strong. He drinks it or he dies. Those are the choices."

Silas lay on the mat with his shirt off and Mara finally saw the wound properly for the first time since the reed marshes, since Hale's gunboat, since the moment she'd pressed her palms against the graze and told him it was nothing, it was a scratch, keep steering. It was not nothing. The furrow across his ribs had gone angry red at the edges, the center weeping, the skin around it hot and tight as drumskin. When she cleaned it he didn't cry out. He just gripped the edge of the mat and stared at the ceiling beams and breathed through his teeth, and somehow that was worse than screaming.

"You should have let me look at this days ago," she said.

"You were busy memorizing a ledger."

"I could have done both."

"You were busy staying alive," he corrected, gently, and turned his face away so she couldn't argue with his eyes.

The fever came that night. She knew because he started talking—not to her, not to anyone in the room, but to someone standing just past the lantern light. His voice was the easy radio voice she'd heard him use with tower operators and ferry captains, warm and unhurried, except now it kept snagging, repeating itself, apologizing to no one.

"I know, I know. I'll be there. I'm coming. Tell them I'm coming."

Mara sat beside him with a wet cloth and did not sleep. Outside, rain worked the thatch in long slow sheets. Somewhere across the water a dog barked twice and gave up. She thought about the ledger wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floorboard Amara's son had pried up for them, three hundred names in a dead nation's hand, and how strange it was that she could hold all of that in her head and still be undone by the sound of one pilot apologizing to a fever dream.

Near dawn he surfaced enough to drink. She got half the bitter green draught into him before he pushed the cup away and found her wrist with his hand and held on.

"Mara."

"I'm here."

"Don't tell them where we're going. Even if they ask nice. Even if—" His eyes focused on her, briefly, terribly clear. "Even if it's me asking. If the fever makes me ask. Don't tell me."

"I won't," she said. "Go back to sleep."

He slept. She stayed awake and watched the lantern gutter and thought: *this is what it costs.* Not the running, not the bullets, not even the dying. The watching. She had spent ten years preparing to lose everything for this story—her name already lost, her career already lost, her father already lost—and she had never once imagined she would have something new to lose, something that had walked into her life out of a flood-swollen river carrying a chest full of ghosts, something that talked in its sleep and flew broken airplanes and bled for her without being asked.

She wrung out the cloth. She pressed it to his forehead. She began, quietly, to be afraid in an entirely new way.

* * *

The village fed them without being asked, which was its own kind of miracle. Fish smoked over green wood. A porridge of something like rice that grew wild in the flooded fields. Amara's granddaughter, a girl of maybe nine with a gap in her smile, brought Mara a chipped enamel mug of tea every morning at first light and refused payment with the gravity of a customs official.

On the third day Mara asked Amara, carefully, why they were doing this. Strangers. Fevered. Hunted. The inspector's gunboats had been seen at the mouth of the channel; Odile's warning had said so in code, four words that meant *they are looking.*

Amara was mending a net, her fingers moving without her eyes. "In the purge year," she said, "the soldiers came up this channel too. Looking for Vessans who'd run south. My husband hid two families under these floors. Three weeks. Babies crying in the night, and us praying the rain would cover it." She pulled a knot tight. "One family lived. The other was taken when they went for food. So." She shrugged, a small terrible shrug. "Now we hide people. That is the whole arithmetic."

"The risk—"

"There is always risk. There is risk in being the kind of person who hides people and risk in being the kind who doesn't. Only one kind gets to look at herself in the morning." Amara bit the thread free. "Your pilot. Will he live?"

"If the fever breaks."

"And if it doesn't?"

Mara looked out at the rain. "Then I bury him here, and I go on to the capital alone, and I say his name along with the other three hundred."

Amara studied her for a long moment. "Good answer," she said. "Wrong answer. But good."

"What's the right one?"

"The right one is you don't decide yet. You steep the bark. You change the cloth. You stay." She went back to her net. "Deciding is for people with nothing left to lose. You still have things. I can see them from here."

* * *

The fever broke on the fifth night.

Mara knew before she knew—the quality of his breathing changed, the wet rattle smoothing out into something ordinary, human, boring. She had never been so grateful for boredom in her life. She sat back against the wall and realized her whole body was shaking and let it shake.

He woke near midnight, lucid, hollow-eyed, and asked for water. She gave it to him. He drank the whole cup and then lay looking at the underside of the thatch, listening to the rain.

"How long?"

"Five days."

"The ledger—"

"Is where we left it. The floorboard hasn't been opened. The girl brings me tea and reports on the village gossip, which currently consists of a dispute over a missing chicken. We're safe."

"We're never safe."

"No," she agreed. "But tonight we're safe *tonight*, and I've decided that counts."

He turned his head to look at her. In the low lantern light his face was thinner than she'd ever seen it, the cheekbones standing out, a gray stubble on his jaw, and he was still the most solid thing in her world.

"You stayed," he said.

"Where would I go?"

"People leave. People get smart. You had every reason to take the chest and run—you could have hired a guide, you could have—"

"Silas." She said his name the way you set down something heavy. "I watched you talk to the fever for five nights. Do you want to know what you said?"

He winced. "God. No."

"You apologized. Over and over. To somebody about a delivery." She kept her voice level. "You promised you'd come. And on the worst night, the night I thought—I really thought, Silas—you said my name. Just once. Just *Mara*, like a question. Like you were checking whether I was still in the room."

The rain filled the silence.

"So no," she said. "I wasn't going anywhere. I'm apparently the kind of person who stays. Amara saw it before I did. She says I still have things left to lose and she could see them from across the room." Her voice did something she hadn't authorized. "And she's right, and it terrifies me, because I built my whole life so that nothing could be taken from me. No family to threaten. No home to burn. No one whose death could be used against me. That was the design. Ten years of it. And then you fished a chest out of a river and now there's—" She gestured helplessly at him, at the mat, at the lantern, at the whole drowning world. "*This*. And I don't know how to protect it. I only know how to protect a story."

Silas reached over and took her hand. His grip was weak from the fever but it did not waver.

"For what it's worth," he said, "you protect the story pretty well."

She laughed—a cracked, wet sound, closer to crying than laughing—and he pulled, gently, until she came down from the wall and sat on the mat beside him, her back against his shoulder because his ribs couldn't take more than that, and they stayed that way while the rain went on working the roof.

"I love you," she said.

Not loudly. Not dramatically. She said it the way you read names from a ledger—one after another, each one mattering, none of them performed. "I've loved you since the narrows, probably. Maybe since the shack. I'm telling you now because the fever scared me straight, and because if we die in the capital I refuse to let the record show I kept quiet about the one true thing I had."

His hand tightened around hers.

"I love you too," he said. "Since the shack. You opened that chest and your whole face changed and I thought, oh no. Oh no, I'm in trouble." A breath of a laugh. "For the record."

"For the record," she agreed.

They sat in the lamplight while the storm moved over them, and neither of them said anything else for a long time, because there was nothing left that needed saying, and because both of them understood that words spoken aloud in a fever country are promises, and promises are debts, and the capital was still ninety miles downstream with a blockade between here and there.

* * *

Two mornings later, the girl with the gap-toothed smile came for her tea delivery and did not bring tea. She brought a strip of oilcloth, tied with the particular double knot Odile used, and would not meet Mara's eyes.

Mara untied it with cold fingers.

Inside, a scrap of paper in Odile's cramped hand:

*Gunboat passed the mouth at dawn. Second one behind it. They're sweeping channel by channel. Your friends in the capital say the press house is watched — do not come the front way. Ask for the map man's channel. Trust the boy with the ink-stained fingers. Go soon. — O*

Below it, in different ink, smaller, pressed hard:

*Mara. Whatever happens — H.*

Mara read it three times. Then she folded it small, tucked it into her shirt, and climbed down the ladder into the grey morning light, where Silas stood at the water's edge testing his legs against the sway of the walkway, thin as a heron and upright, watching the far bend of the channel as if he could already hear the engines coming.

"We need a boat," he said.
