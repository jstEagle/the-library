## 16. City of Rain

**Silas**

The capital announced itself the way a fever announces itself—not all at once, but as a slow wrongness in everything you touched.

They came upriver through the last miles of drowned farmland, past rooftops standing in water like teeth in a gum, past a church steeple whose bell had gone silent under the flood. The rain fell so hard it stopped being weather and became a condition of existence. Silas stood at the tiller of Odile's longboat with a tarpaulin over their shoulders and watched the city assemble itself out of the murk: first the cranes of the harbor district, then the smokestacks, then the gray cliff-face of the government quarter rising above the waterline like something that intended to outlast them.

"Three days," Mara said beside him. She had to lean close to be heard over the drumming on the canvas. "Odile said three days from the stilt village. She was right."

"She usually is."

"That's what worries me about her."

He didn't answer that. There wasn't an answer that didn't lead back to Odile's confession—the head start she'd given them, bought with whatever she'd already sold to Hale—and he'd spent the last hundred miles trying to decide whether gratitude or fury was the correct response. He'd settled on neither. Gratitude and fury both required energy he needed for other things.

The river channel into the capital had been closed. They could see it from two miles out: a boom of chained barges across the brown water, gunboat tied alongside, searchlights cutting cones through the rain even though it was barely noon. So they did what Odile's map said to do—they turned off into the flooded streets of the outer districts, where the water ran chest-deep between warehouse walls and nobody official bothered to look.

The boat couldn't go all the way. Nothing could go all the way anymore. They paid a boy with a flat-bottomed skiff to take them the last stretch, wading the final quarter mile themselves, the chest wrapped in oilcloth and strapped to Mara's back like a hump, Silas carrying the false bottom panel that held the copied pages. His wound had closed but the flesh around it still pulled tight when he lifted his arm, a hot wire of reminder stitched into his body.

*You should have stayed in the stilt village,* he thought, not for the first time. *You should have let her carry this alone.*

But that thought never survived contact with reality. He'd watched Mara read names aloud by lamplight until her voice gave out. He knew exactly how alone she would have been.

The streets of the capital were rivers now, and the people moved through them like fish—wading, poling, ferrying goods on their heads, children swimming between the doorways of submerged shops. Nobody looked at two more soaked figures hauling themselves along a second-story walkway. The monsoon had made everyone anonymous. It was the only mercy left in the city.

"There." Mara pointed.

Silas followed her finger across the drowned intersection and felt his stomach drop, though he couldn't have said why at first. The Free Press building stood four blocks away—a narrow stone tower, older than everything around it, its upper windows lit against the gloom. He could see the presses' vibration in the light, a faint shudder, like a heartbeat behind a wall.

It was beautiful. It was exactly what they'd come three hundred miles to find.

And around its base, in a loose ring, were soldiers.

Not many. Not yet. A checkpoint at each corner of the block, sandbags and a truck, men in ponchos smoking under an awning. Casual. Patient. The posture of people who had been told to wait rather than told to hunt—which was worse, because waiting meant someone expected them.

Mara's hand found his arm. Her grip went slowly from nothing to painful.

"No," she said. "No, no—"

"Mara."

"They're not even hiding it. They're just *standing there.*" Her voice cracked down the middle. "Hale warned us the press house was watched. I thought—I thought we'd have a chance. I thought we'd come in through the water and there'd be a back way and—"

"There might be."

She turned on him, rain streaming off her chin, eyes wild with three hundred miles of spent hope. "Look at it, Silas. Look at it!"

He looked. Four checkpoints. One truck. And between the checkpoints, gaps—gaps filled with floodwater and ruined buildings and the kind of chaos that only a city drowning can produce. He'd flown into places no map admitted existed. He'd landed on strips shorter than his own doubt. His whole life had been built on finding the gap in the fence.

"I'm looking," he said. "I see four doors they're watching. That means there are five they're not."

Mara stared at him. Then, slowly, the wildness in her face banked itself into something harder and colder and more useful. She adjusted the straps on her shoulders. The chest shifted against her back.

"Then we find the fifth," she said.

* * *

**Mara**

They took shelter in the shell of a print shop two blocks from the Free Press building—a smaller place, long dead, its type cases rotted and its windows broken out. Water sloshed ankle-deep across the floor. But the roof held, mostly, and from the upstairs window Mara could watch the ring of soldiers through a gap in the brickwork while the rain tried to hammer its way in after her.

She counted. It was what she did. When the world became unbearable, Mara Okonjo counted things, because things could be known, and known things could be used.

Four checkpoints. Sixteen visible soldiers, rotating on some schedule she hadn't yet cracked. One supply truck, which meant they planned to stay. Two officers, distinguishable by their dry uniforms—one of whom spent an unusual amount of time looking up. At the windows. At the roofline.

*They know the presses are running,* she thought. *They know the story is being set in type right now, forty feet above their heads, and they're under orders not to storm the building. Which means someone above them is afraid of what happens if they do.*

That was something. That was maybe everything. A cordon under orders to wait was a cordon that could be reasoned with, delayed, deceived. A cordon under orders to shoot was just arithmetic.

Footsteps on the stairs. She turned, hand going to the knife at her belt, and relaxed by degrees when Silas came up into the room with a young man in tow—early twenties, ink-stained fingers, a canvas satchel slung across his chest and rain sheeting off his oilskin coat.

"This is Tomas," Silas said. "He found me on the walkway downstairs. Says he knows the press house."

"I work there," the young man said. His voice was steady but his eyes kept flicking to the window, to the soldiers, back. "Compositor. Night shift. I've been stuck on this side of the water since the levee broke—can't get to my shift, can't get home, everyone's stuck everywhere." He looked at Mara directly then, and something in his gaze steadied. "Odile sent word ahead. A week ago. She said two people might come asking, one of them carrying something that weighed more than it should. She said if they came, I should help them."

Mara's throat tightened. *Betrayal braided with loyalty.* Even now, even having sold them once, the old woman was reaching out of the swamp to pull them toward shore.

"And you believed her?" Mara asked.

Tomas almost smiled. "My grandmother worked the river ferries. She used to say Odile was the only honest crook on the water." He shrugged. "I believe my grandmother."

Silas crouched by the window, studying the checkpoints. "Tell us about the building. Every entrance. Every way in that isn't a door."

"There aren't any ways in that aren't doors," Tomas said. "That's the point of the Free Press. Old man Ferro built it like a fortress—stone walls, iron shutters, one cellar. The soldiers know it. They're not trying to get in. They're trying to make sure nobody gets *out* with anything." He paused. "Except."

Mara turned. "*Except.*"

"The cellar floods." Tomas said it carefully, like a man handling something fragile. "Every monsoon, the lower level takes water—it's why the archive is on the third floor. There's a drainage tunnel, runs under the street to the old canal, built a hundred years ago when the district was warehouses. Ferro keeps it cleared because the pumps need the outflow. It comes up inside the building, behind the paper store. There's a grate. I've carried reams of stock through it when the delivery boats couldn't dock." He swallowed. "It's flooded now. Waist-deep, maybe higher. And it's dark. And the canal mouth opens—" he pointed east, toward a collapsed embankment half a mile away, "—there. Where the old tannery used to be. No checkpoint there. Nobody watches a hole full of black water."

Silas was quiet for a moment. Then: "How deep is the canal?"

"At this stage? Over your head in places. You'd swim sections. In the dark. With—" his eyes dropped to the oilcloth bundle strapped to Mara's back, "—that on you."

Mara felt the weight of it settle onto her shoulders all over again, as if the chest had heard itself discussed. Twenty pounds of proof. Twenty pounds of names. Her father's name among them, in a dead man's careful ledger hand.

"I'll carry it," she said.

"You don't have to—"

"Tomas." She kept her voice level. "I have carried this thing for ten years. Through archives that burned, through borders that closed, through every year they told me Vessania never existed. I will carry it through a flooded sewer if that's the last mile. The only question is whether you two are coming with me."

Silas rose from the window. In the gray light his face looked carved, tired beyond tiredness, and she saw him weigh it—the same weighing she'd watched him do since the rapids, since the marshes, since the night by lamplight when he'd finally said aloud what they'd both been refusing to say. He was counting costs. He was very bad at it, because he always arrived at the same total.

"We're coming," he said.

* * *

**Silas**

They waited for full dark, which under the monsoon was less a time than a depth—the city sinking into a blackness so complete that the soldiers' lanterns became the only geography. Rain hammered the tin roofs. Somewhere across the drowned districts, a transformer blew with a green flash and a sound like the sky cracking, and half the grid went down, and Silas decided to take it as an omen in their favor.

At the collapsed tannery embankment, the canal mouth gaped like a wound. Black water breathed in and out of it with the pulse of the flood. The smell hit him first—oil, rot, a century of the city's secrets dissolved and suspended.

"Ladies first is a tradition I'm happy to break," he said.

Mara was already fastening the chest's straps tighter. "I go first. I'm smallest, and if the grate's blocked I'm the one who can turn around in the pipe." She caught his look. "Don't argue with me tonight, Silas. I haven't got the strength and you haven't got the time."

Tomas handed them each a stub of candle and a striker, waterproofed in wax. "Follow the left wall. There's a junction at two hundred meters—keep left again. When you feel the current strengthen, you're near the outflow; climb before it, there's a rung ladder. The grate's padlocked but the lock's been rusted through since March. I reported it. They never fixed it." He hesitated. "Inside, ask for Ferro. Tell him the night compositor sent you. He'll want to put you on the radio before he puts you anywhere else."

"The radio," Mara repeated.

"Ferro's been waiting twenty years for a reason to use it."

Silas took Mara's hand in the dark. Just briefly. Her fingers were cold and did not let go quickly enough, and he understood that this was her version of everything he'd said by lamplight, compressed into a grip.

"Two hundred meters," he said. "Left at the junction."

"Left at the junction," she echoed.

And then she stepped off the embankment into the black water and was gone, and Silas stood in the rain for exactly three seconds—three seconds of terror, of the certain knowledge that the world was too large and too wet and too well-armed for two people and a box of paper—before following her down.

The tunnel was worse than imagined and better than feared. The water was neck-deep, then chest-deep, then, in one endless stretch, over his head entirely, and he swam blind with one hand tracing the left wall and his lungs burning and the candle useless in its wax cocoon against his chest. Once—only once—he lost the wall, spun in the dark, swallowed canal water that tasted like the whole drowned century, and panicked with a completeness that shamed him even as it happened. Then his fingers found brick again and he breathed, and swam, and counted strokes because counting was what you did when the world became unbearable.

Ahead, faintly, a candle flared. Mara, upright on some ledge, holding the flame high, her face a smudge of gold in the vast dark.

"Junction," her voice echoed, warped by the pipe. "Going left."

They climbed the rusted rungs. They shouldered the grate aside with a shriek of metal that seemed loud enough to wake the army outside. And then they were through—into a paper store stacked floor to ceiling with newsprint, dry, dim, smelling of ink and dust, alive with the muffled heartbeat of the presses somewhere above.

Mara slid the chest off her back and stood over it, dripping, shaking, laughing a small broken laugh that was not quite crying.

"We made it," she whispered. "We're *inside.*"

Silas opened his mouth to answer.

And the lights went out.

Not all of them—the emergency lamps down the corridor, red as embers—but the main power died with a thud that he felt through the floor, and the press rhythm overhead stuttered, faltered—

—and stopped.

In the sudden silence, boots. Many boots. On the stairs above, descending fast, and a voice calling down through the building, amplified and cold:

"The premises are surrounded. Anyone inside the Free Press building is ordered to the ground floor. This is your only warning."

Mara's hand found Silas's in the red dark. Her grip was the same grip as the canal's edge—desperate, unletting.

"They're coming down," she said.
