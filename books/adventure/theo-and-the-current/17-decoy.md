## 17. Decoy

The rain had a rhythm now, and Silas had learned to hate it the way you hate a song stuck in your head. It came off the harbor in long gray sheets, drumming on the tin roof of the fish-cannery loft where they'd been hiding for two days, and underneath it, always, the sound of the city: sirens, loudspeakers, boots in water.

Mara sat by the one window with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth across her knees, watching the street below like it owed her money. She'd been watching it for hours. The Free Press building stood six blocks east, close enough to see its rooftop antenna against the rain—close enough to count the soldiers at its doors if you had good eyes, and Mara's eyes were very good.

"Fourteen," she said, without turning around. "There were nine yesterday. They've added a checkpoint at the alley entrance."

Silas said nothing. There was nothing useful to say. They both knew what fourteen soldiers at the doors of the last free press in the country meant. It meant the plan was dead. It meant three hundred miles of river, a bullet graze gone septic and then slowly better, a fever village, Odile's betrayal, Hale's warning—all of it spent, all of it burned as fuel—and here they were, six blocks from the finish line, staring at a wall of rifles.

The chest sat under the floorboards where the cannery workers once hid contraband tobacco. Silas could feel it through the planks, the way you feel a storm cell on the horizon before you can name why the light looks wrong.

"We go at night," Mara said. "Through the flooded cellar. That printer boy said there's a way up from the water."

"The printer boy also said the cellar was watched from inside," Silas said. "Hale told us that himself. Whoever sent us that warning didn't send it so we'd die beautifully."

"Hale sent it because he's coming apart," Mara said. "I read people for a living, Silas. I read ledgers the way other people read faces. He's not warning us. He's asking us to hurry."

"And if he's wrong? If his superiors want us walking into that building so they have an excuse?" Silas crossed the room and crouched beside her, rain hammering the roof like applause for something terrible. "Then we hand them the chest and the archivist both, and twenty more years of silence."

Mara finally looked at him. Her face was thinner than when he'd pulled her out of the flood country, cheekbones standing out like the ribs of a boat run aground, but her eyes hadn't changed. They were still the eyes of a woman who had spent fifteen years being told *no* by every archive, every ministry, every polite functionary with a stamp, and who had answered each one by digging deeper.

"So we wait," she said. "We wait for the monsoon to break, for the checkpoints to relax, for—"

"They're not going to relax." Silas stood and went to the window himself. Below, the drowned street ran brown and fast, carrying debris toward the harbor: a door, a chair, a child's toy boat that spun once and vanished. "Look at them. Fourteen soldiers. Loudspeakers. They're not guarding a building, Mara. They're guarding a story. And stories don't get tired."

* * *

It was the plane that gave him the idea, which was a joke in itself, because the plane was the reason he'd been grounded in this city at all.

He'd gone down to the harbor district that afternoon to buy antibiotics from a pharmacist who asked no questions—the kind of errand he'd been running his whole life, medicine and secrets, secrets and medicine—and on the way back he cut through the old municipal airfield. He hadn't meant to. His feet took him there the way feet take a drowning man toward anything that floats.

The *Kestrel* sat on the flooded apron where the authorities had impounded it after the crash-landing, half-submerged in a foot of gray water, tail tied down to a rusted ring, canvas cover rotted through in three places. His plane. His beautiful, stubborn, impossible plane, the one constant of his invisible life, the thing that had carried medicine and mail and now a nation's murdered memory across three hundred miles of hostile water. Shot down over the harbor—or nearly; he'd put it down on the mudflats hard enough to bend the propeller shaft and crack the cowling, and walked away from it while the gunboats circled.

They hadn't scrapped it. That was the thing he couldn't stop looking at. They'd impounded it, catalogued it, left it sitting in plain view like evidence in an open case—which, he supposed, is exactly what it was.

A mechanic's shed stood at the field's edge, roof half-gone, tools scattered by looters years ago. But some things survive looters. Silas found a torque wrench, a set of spanners, a hand pump for the fuel drums. In the cannery loft that night, turning the wrench over in his hands, he understood what his own mind had been building ever since he saw the plane sitting there in the rain, patient as a loaded question.

"Mara," he said. "How much do fourteen soldiers care about a plane?"

She looked up from the ledger. "What?"

"The *Kestrel*. It's impounded at the municipal field. Sitting right there. Every soldier in this city knows my face by now—they've been broadcasting it since the marshes. They know I'm a pilot. They know the plane is mine." He set the wrench on the table between them. "So ask yourself what happens the moment a stolen, wanted aircraft takes off from that field at dusk, low, loud, flying straight down the government quarter with no flight plan and no shame."

Mara went very still. He watched her get there ahead of him—he watched her arrive at the shape of it and flinch away from it, the way you flinch from a hot stove even when you need the fire.

"No," she said.

"It doesn't have to be me."

"Don't." She stood up. The oilcloth slid off the ledger. "Don't you dare stand there and offer me some version of this where you hand yourself to them, because I have done the arithmetic on what you are to me, Silas Pemberton, and I am not trading it for a diversion."

"There's no other version." He kept his voice level, the way he talked to frightened passengers in bad weather. Level voice, steady hands, tell them the truth gently. "Every road into that press house runs past guns. Every boat in the harbor is being logged. The only asset we have that they can't ignore is noise. A plane over the government quarter at dusk—that's not a diversion, that's a five-alarm event. Every soldier at those doors gets pulled toward the sky. Gunboats turn their searchlights up instead of out. For maybe forty minutes, that building has a soft side."

"Forty minutes." Her voice cracked on it. "And then they shoot you down. Again. Except this time they'll be aiming."

"Maybe."

"*Maybe?*" She laughed, and it was an awful sound, wet and short. "Silas. They shot you down over open water with a warning burst. This time you'll be the whole war."

"I know."

"You *know*, and you're standing there with your calm pilot face telling me *maybe* like it's a weather report—"

"Because it is one!" He heard his own voice rise and made himself bring it back down. Rain on the roof. Boots in water. Somewhere a loudspeaker droning curfew rules to a city that had stopped listening. "Mara. Listen to me. I have spent my entire life being careful. Invisible. Low and slow, no flight plans filed under my real name, medicine runs and quiet favors, a man who ferries other people's secrets because having none of his own kept him safe. Twenty years of flying under everything. And you know what I learned?"

She didn't answer. Her jaw was tight, her arms crossed, rain-light moving on her face.

"I learned that invisible men don't get to matter," Silas said. "Invisible men watch. We ferry. We keep our heads down and our tanks full and we tell ourselves it's enough, that staying out of it is a kind of virtue. And then a chest floats out of a flood with a dead country's seal on it, and I find out that all my careful years were just a longer way of doing nothing." He picked up the torque wrench again, turned it once, set it down. "I'm not doing nothing anymore. Not tonight. Not when we're six blocks from the only place in this country where those names can become true."

Mara pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. When she lowered them, she was crying without any drama about it, the way she did everything—efficiently, furiously, like even grief had a deadline.

"There has to be another way," she whispered.

"If there is, I haven't found it, and neither have you, and we're out of time. The inquiry commission arrives Thursday. After that, the government has legal cover to seal the case forever. Tonight is the window. Tonight or never." He reached out and took her hands away from her face and held them, and her fingers were cold and did not resist. "Here's how it works. I go to the field at four. The *Kestrel*'s engine seized from sabotage, but sabotage bends things—it doesn't usually break them. I've got a torque wrench and six hours and a mechanic's hands. If she turns over, I take off at dusk, fly the government quarter low and slow, lights blazing, transponder screaming my name. They will come for me with everything they have, because I'm not just a fugitive in the air—I'm the story they've been trying to bury, flying over their rooftops. While every eye in this city points up, you take the chest through the flooded cellar with the printer boy and you climb."

"And if the engine doesn't start?"

"Then we think of something else."

"And if they shoot you down over the crowd—"

"Then I go down loud," Silas said, "and everyone in this city sees exactly what their government does to a man carrying the truth. You said it yourself, Mara. Stories don't get tired. So let's give them one they can't bury."

* * *

They argued until midnight. Or rather, she argued and he held steady, the way a seawall holds—not winning, just refusing to move. She offered alternatives: splitting up, going separately, waiting for the printer boy's contacts to smuggle them in with a coal delivery. He shot each one down with the flat patience of a man who had already flown the route in his head a hundred times.

At some point past one in the morning she stopped arguing and started planning, because that was who Mara Okonjo was—a woman who could hold heartbreak in one hand and a checklist in the other. She drew the cellar route on the back of a flour sack. She timed the guard rotations from the window. She packed the ledger into the oilcloth, then unpacked it, then packed it again.

At two, she said, quietly, not looking at him: "If you die, I will spend the rest of my life testifying, and every word of it will be for you, and I will never forgive you for making my testimony a love letter."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me," Silas said.

And she laughed—really laughed, once, cracked and bright in the dark of the cannery loft—and then she kissed him, hard, with the rain coming down and the soldiers counting themselves outside and the chest waiting under the floorboards, and it tasted like salt and like a promise neither of them would say aloud, because saying it aloud would make it a goodbye.

At three-thirty, they split the last of the bread.

At four, Silas shouldered the tool bag and went down the stairs into the drowned dark, and Mara stood at the window and watched him until the rain took him.

* * *

The municipal airfield smelled of jet fuel and rot. The *Kestrel* waited in the gray pre-dawn, water sloshing around the wheels, canvas cover hanging off her like a wet shroud. Silas peeled it back and put his palm flat against the cold engine cowling, the way you'd touch the forehead of someone you weren't sure was still alive.

"All right, old girl," he murmured. "One more dishonest day's work."

He worked through the dark hours with a borrowed flashlight clenched in his teeth, draining the water from the fuel lines, tracing the sabotage—someone had dropped grit into the intake, scored the cylinders, bent a fuel line just enough to starve the mixture. Sloppy work, rushed. He straightened the line, flushed the system twice, re-torqued what needed torquing, drained the crankcase and refilled it from a drum of oil he found behind the mechanic's shed. His hands knew this work the way they knew nothing else—better than they knew his own signature. By the time the sky turned the color of wet slate, the spark plugs were clean, the magneto was dry, and the propeller, straightened as best he could with a hammer and a wish, still wobbled a little at the tips.

It would fly. It wouldn't fly well. It would fly loudly, which was the entire point.

At 6:40 in the evening, with the monsoon pressing down like a lid, Silas climbed into the cockpit of the plane that had carried him through twenty years of invisible miles, and buckled in, and put his hand on the starter.

Below, six blocks east, a woman with a chest on her back stood in a doorway in the rain, waiting for the sky to make its noise.

He thought about the chest floating in the backwater. About the seal of a country that no longer existed. About a ledger of names, and a woman who had spent her life reading names aloud to rooms that refused to hear. About Odile's one eye and the fever village lamplight and the taste of river water in his lungs.

He thought: *invisible men don't get to matter.*

Silas Pemberton turned the starter.

The *Kestrel* coughed, caught, roared—rough, ragged, gloriously alive—and the sound rolled across the flooded airfield like thunder answering thunder, and every soldier in the government quarter looked up.

He taxied into position, pushed the throttle forward, and rose into the rain, low and slow and lit up like a signal fire, banking hard toward the towers of the capital, trailing noise and history behind him.

Six blocks east, Mara stepped out of the doorway and began to walk.
