## 18. The Long Way Up

The plane went up at 4:17 in the morning, and the whole city looked at it.

Mara knew because she was looking too—standing in the drowned mouth of an alley off Cordwainer Street, chest strapped to her back like a coffin she'd chosen to carry, watching Silas's old Cessna claw through the rain with its running lights blazing and its engine roaring loud enough to wake every soldier in the capital. It flew badly. It flew bravely. It flew like a man who had decided that being seen was worth more than being safe.

*That's my pilot,* she thought, and then corrected herself: *that's the signal fire.*

She turned away before her eyes could betray her by filling.

"Keep moving," said the boy.

His name was Teo, which made her laugh once, a short ugly sound, when he told her. *Theo and the Current*—she'd written a novel under a false name years ago, before the archive expelled her, before Vessania became the only word in her life. She'd named a fictional boy Theo because it sounded like someone who survives rivers. Now a real one stood in front of her, seventeen maybe, rain sheeting off his oilskin hood, a printer's apprentice who owed Odile a debt he wouldn't name and who had been waiting three days in a flooded cellar for them to arrive.

"Teo," she said. "Fine. Fitting."

"Fitting for what?"

"For getting us killed."

He grinned. He had a gap in his teeth and rain in his lashes and the unbothered cheer of someone too young to have learned what fear costs over time. "Odile said you'd be difficult."

"Odile said I'd be armed."

"You're not."

"I'm not," Mara admitted. "But I'm heavier than I look." She shifted the straps on her shoulders. The chest rode against her spine, forty pounds of water-stained paper and iron-banded wood, wrapped in waxed canvas and tied down with the kind of knots Silas used—overhand, doubled, obsessive. Every step she took, it pressed against her like a hand between her shoulder blades. *Go on,* it seemed to say. *You don't get to put me down.*

Above them, the plane banked hard over the harbor district, engine stuttering, then catching, then roaring again—loud, deliberate, impossible to ignore. Somewhere across the drowned city, searchlights would be swinging up. Soldiers would be spilling out of barracks. Hale's net, the one Odile's warning had described, would be hauling itself toward the sky.

And underneath that net, in the flooded dark, two people would be walking.

"This way," Teo said, and dropped into the water.

* * *

The cellar entrance was a loading door set below street level, half-submerged since the third week of the monsoon. Teo went through it like an otter—a breath, a slip, gone—and Mara stood at the lip with the rain hammering her hood and the chest dragging at her back and thought, with perfect clarity: *I can't swim with this.*

She'd never learned properly. Archive work didn't require swimming. Reading did not require swimming. Twenty years of chasing dead men's signatures through dead men's filing systems required many things, but buoyancy was not among them, and now she was about to descend into black floodwater carrying the only surviving proof of a massacre, guided by a teenager, while the man she loved drew gunfire over the harbor.

Silas could swim. Silas had pulled her out of the Gorge Narrows with one arm around her ribs and the current trying to peel them apart, and afterward neither of them had said anything about it, because some things you say and some things you just keep doing.

*He's flying so you don't have to drown,* she told herself. *So walk.*

She felt for the steps with her boot, found the first one slick with silt, and went down.

The water came to her waist inside the doorway, then her chest as the floor sloped away. Cold found every seam in her clothing. The chest tried to float, which was worse than if it had sunk—it pressed upward against the straps, tilting her, shoving her face toward the water—and she grabbed a pipe bracket on the wall and held on until Teo's hand closed around her wrist.

"Breathe," he said. "I've got you. There's a line."

There was a line—a rope strung at chest height through the dark, knotted every arm's length, run by Odile's people through the cellars of half a block of Cordwainer Street. Mara hooked her elbow over it and moved hand over hand, boots finding nothing, body trusting rope and rope alone, the chest surging and settling against her back like something alive. The water smelled of diesel and drains and river. Overhead, muffled by stone, she heard the plane again—closer now, circling, its engine note ragged and defiant.

*Fly loud, love,* she thought. *Be the loudest thing in the sky.*

They came up in a coal chute, gasping, into a cellar that wasn't flooded—one of the high ones, on the ridge of the street—with crates stacked along the walls and a single storm lamp burning behind a baffle of tin. Two men sat among the crates playing cards by lamplight, and they didn't even look up, and Mara understood suddenly what Odile had built here: not a route but a household, scattered through the city's bones, ordinary people keeping lamps lit for strangers.

"Rest five minutes," Teo said. "Then the stairs."

"I don't need—"

"You're shaking."

She was. She sat down on a crate anyway, arms wrapped around her knees, and let the shaking happen, because fighting it cost more than admitting it. One of the card players poured something from a bottle into a tin cup and handed it to her without a word. It tasted like kerosene cut with fruit. It helped.

On the wall above the card game, someone had pinned a page torn from a newspaper. Not today's paper—the presses hadn't dared print anything true in years—but an old one, twenty years old, yellowed and soft at the folds. A headline about flooding, nothing more. But beneath it, in pencil, in several different hands, someone had written names. Dozens of names. A list that grew like moss in the dark, added by whoever passed through, whoever carried one.

Mara read three of them before she had to stop.

They were ledger names. Names from the chest on her back. Someone in this city had been writing them from memory all these years, the way you scratch a notch for every day you're imprisoned—not because it changes anything, but because stopping would mean agreeing to forget.

"Teo," she said. Her voice came out wrong. "Who wrote those?"

"People do," he said, like it was weather. "My uncle started it. He worked the docks at Vessania before. Come on. Stairs."

* * *

The stairs went up through the building's throat—a printer's shop, dead these ten years, its machinery shrouded in sheets like furniture in a house where everyone died. Rain came through the roof in threads. Teo moved through it confidently, touching things as he passed: a press frame, a type case, a hanging chain, checking each like a man greeting animals.

"The Free Press is two buildings down," he said. "But you can't go at street level. They've had the block watched since the warning went out—your inspector's warning, Odile says. So we go through."

"Through what?"

"The old delivery tunnel. Paper came in by canal, back when there were canals. The tunnel runs under the street and comes up inside their bindery." He glanced back at her, lamplight catching the gap in his teeth. "It's flooded."

"Of course it is."

"Only to here." He held a hand at his chin. "Chest floats. You hold it in front of you, tip your head back, breathe off the air pocket at the ceiling. Forty feet, maybe fifty. I'll pull the rope; you follow the knots."

Mara stood in the dripping dark and did the arithmetic. Fifty feet underwater, in the dark, holding forty pounds of proof, breathing off a pocket of trapped air, following knots, behind a boy who owed a favor to a one-eyed ferrywoman.

Every version of her life that ended well had ended somewhere else—in an archive, in a quiet room, in a chair by a window with the ledgers finally shelved and done. None of those versions included this tunnel. All of them had required other people to pay.

"Teo," she said. "Why are you doing this? Really."

He was quiet a moment. Rain ticked on the shrouded press.

"My grandmother's name is in your chest," he said. "I've never seen it written anywhere. Nobody writes it. When Odile sent word—a chest, Vessian papers, a woman coming—I walked here in one night." He shrugged, and the shrug broke a little in the middle. "I want to see it printed. That's all. I want to see her name in ink, in a newspaper, where it can't be erased. Is that enough?"

"It's more than enough," Mara said. "It's the whole thing."

She unslung the chest and checked the knots, checked the waxed canvas, checked the strap Silas had doubled at the buckle because he doubled everything. Then she swung it around and held it against her chest, arms locked through the straps, the way you'd carry a child through deep water.

"Okay," she said. "Show me the knots."

* * *

The tunnel swallowed them at a sloped mouth of standing water, and the world became four things: rope, knot, cold, and the small grey light of Teo's lamp ahead, wavering on the ceiling of the passage.

She went under at the low point with the chest hugged tight and her face tipped up into the trapped air, and it was exactly as bad as she'd imagined and also strangely, horribly calm. The water pressed on her ears. The rope ran smooth and sure through her fist. Knot. Breathe. Knot. Breathe. The chest buoyed her, actually lifted her, and she understood that Silas had known this—he'd packed the chest to float, or it floated because truth does—and the thought arrived with such force that she nearly laughed underwater, a burst of bubbles, *he'd have tested it, of course he'd have tested it, he tests everything, he ties everything twice—*

Her boot caught a submerged shelf. The chest lurched. For one full second her mouth went under and the dark came in cold at her lips—

—and the rope hauled taut ahead of her, Teo pulling, steady as a heartbeat, and she got her face back into the air pocket and breathed.

Knot. Breathe. Knot.

Somewhere far above, muffled through meters of brick and earth and rain, she heard it: engines. Many engines. Trucks, maybe, or armored cars, moving along the streets overhead. And beneath them, fainter, higher, still going—still *up there*—a single aircraft engine, circling, circling, refusing to land, refusing to fall, being the loudest thing in the sky.

*Hold them, Silas. Hold them just a little longer.*

The rope rose. Light bloomed ahead—not lamplight but daylight, grey and thin, coming through a hatch. Teo's hand reached down through the water, gripped her forearm, and hauled, and she came up out of the tunnel into the bindery of the Free Press like a diver surfacing, chest first, gasping, alive.

* * *

The bindery smelled of glue and paper and wet wool, and it was full of people.

Not soldiers—people. A dozen of them, maybe more, moving quietly among the machines: an old woman feeding type into a composing stick, a man coiling cable, two young women hand-cranking a press that shouldn't have had power and somehow did, a boy Mara's own age sharpening a knife against a strop with the unhurried rhythm of someone who has decided how he will spend his last hour. No one spoke above a murmur. Everyone glanced at the hatch when she came through it, and then, seeing only her and the chest, went back to work.

"They've been ready for three days," Teo said softly. "Since Odile's warning. They know the block is watched. They know what happens when the soldiers come." He wiped his hands on his apron. "They're printing anyway."

An older man detached himself from the press and came toward her—broad, grey-bearded, ink to the elbows, with the calm of a man who has already grieved himself and come out the other side usable. He looked at the chest on her back for a long moment.

"You're Okonjo," he said. "The archivist."

"I'm the archivist," she agreed. "Was."

"You're whatever tonight makes you." He held out both hands. "Give it here. Careful—it goes on the flat table, center room. We set type from the ledger pages, name by name. Front page, full run, ten thousand copies before dawn if the machines hold." His eyes went past her, up, toward the sound that was still faintly circling the city. "Your pilot bought us the quiet to start. We won't waste it."

Mara unslung the chest and laid it on the table, and her hands were steadier than she expected. She broke Silas's double knots gently, respectfully, the way you open a letter from the dead, and lifted the lid, and the smell of old paper rose up—twenty years of it, river-damp and stubborn, the smell of things that refused to rot.

The ledger lay on top. She opened it to the first page and turned it toward the compositor.

"Start here," she said. "Read them aloud as you set them. All of them. If we only get one page printed before they breach the doors, make it the names."

The old woman at the type case looked up. "Aloud?"

"Aloud," Mara said. "Names are the point. Everything else is argument. Names are evidence."

The compositor began to read. *"Ademar Voss. Ilka Voss. Tomas Brandt—"*

And the typesetter's hands began to move, letter by letter, and the press crew gathered close, and one by one they picked up the rhythm, each name repeated down the line as the type was set, so that the name was spoken six times before it ever touched paper—spoken into the air of the room, into the ears of everyone present, made real by breath before it was made permanent by ink.

Mara stood with her hand flat on the open ledger and listened to the dead being introduced to the living, and terror and purpose stopped being two things. They were one thing. They had always been one thing. It felt like the current of the Narrows, like the rope in the dark, like a hand on her wrist hauling her up: *knot, breathe, knot, breathe.* You didn't conquer fear. You moved through it, holding on to what was true.

Outside, the plane's engine changed pitch—climbed, strained, rang thin and desperate.

Inside, the press crew kept reading. *"Serafina Oduya. Miklos Ferren. Ana—"*

Then the young man with the knife stopped stropping, tilted his head, and said, quietly:

"Do you hear that? The trucks are stopping."

Everyone froze. Through the walls, through the rain, through the ringing aftermath of the engine overhead, came the sound Mara had been hearing in nightmares for twenty years: boots. Many boots. On pavement. Getting louder.

The old editor looked at Mara, and at the chest, and at the half-set line of type gleaming in the lamplight.

"How long do you need?" he asked.

Mara looked at the press. At the names. At the door.

"Print faster," she said.
