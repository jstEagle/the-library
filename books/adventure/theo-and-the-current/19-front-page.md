## 19. Front Page

The cellar smelled of ink and river water, and Mara loved it the way you love a thing that is about to kill you.

She came up the last ladder rung with the chest strap biting into her shoulders and her palms raw from the climb, and the printer boy—Tomas, nineteen, ink to his elbows, a debt to Odile he'd never once mentioned—pulled her through the floor hatch into a room that hummed. Not machinery, not yet. People. A dozen of them, maybe more, moving through the yellow lamplight of the Free Press house like a crew that had been waiting twenty years for a cargo and had only just been told it was coming.

"Is that it?" A woman with grey in her braids and a compositor's hunch stepped forward. Her eyes went to the chest the way other people looked at children.

"That's it," Mara said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "The Vessian ledger. Original. Unopened since the flood."

The room went quiet in a way that had weight. Somewhere above them, rain hammered the roof, and beneath the rain, faint but unmistakable, the low diesel note of an engine idling in the street.

Tomas heard it too. His head tilted like a bird's. "That's been there since midnight," he said. "They're not coming in. Not yet. They're waiting to see if anyone else comes out."

"Then nobody comes out," Mara said, and set the chest on the composing table, and broke the seal she had sworn to Odile she'd preserve, because seals were for museums and the dead, and the dead had waited long enough.

* * *

They worked the way water works—without argument, without ceremony, filling every space available.

The grey-braided woman was named Adaeze and had set type for the Free Press since before Mara was expelled from the national archive, which meant she had set type through the purges, the trials, the quiet years when the word *Vessania* could get a printer's fingers broken one knuckle at a time. She read the first ledger page Mara handed her and did not gasp or weep. She read it twice, then said, "We lead with the names. Numbers let them argue. Names make them count."

"Names," Mara agreed. "All of them. Every page."

"It's four hundred pages."

"Then we'd better start."

The presses were old Heidelbergs, two of them, kept oiled against a day like this by men who had been told for two decades they were being sentimental. The type had to be set by hand for the front page—there was no time for plates—and so the room became a kind of orchestra: the tick of composing sticks, the hiss of the dampening rollers, Adaeze calling measurements, Tomas running copy up and down the stairs to the radio room where a man called Ferro was rigging the broadcast transmitter that had been dismantled and hidden, piece by piece, in a false wall since the last crackdown.

Mara set type herself. She had not touched a composing stick in eleven years—not since the archive, not since the day she'd asked one question too many about a country that no longer existed—and her fingers remembered anyway, the way her father's hands had remembered nets and knots, the way grief remembers. She set the headline in twenty-four point: *THE VESSANIA LEDGER: THE NAMES THE STATE PAID TO ERASE.* Below it, the first column of names, set in a clean roman face, each one spelled exactly as the ledger spelled it, because accuracy was the only monument she knew how to build.

Silas was not there. That was the bargain, and every hour the presses didn't roll she heard it again: the roar of the old plane lifting off the flooded parade ground at the edge of the city, loud, visible, transponder screaming a false identity, drawing the net skyward while she went into the ground. She had kissed them on the mouth in the rain and said *come back* and Silas had said, with that crooked river-country smile, *I always come back, I just don't always come back whole,* and then the wheels had left the water and Mara had turned and run toward the cellar and had not let herself look up. Not once. Looking up was how you lost people. She had a lifetime of evidence.

"Page two," Adaeze called. "Give me page two."

Mara opened the ledger to the middle signatures and her breath went out of her.

She had read these names a hundred times—in Odile's back room by lamplight, in the stilt village while Silas burned with fever, on the boat, in her sleep. But reading them to set them in type was different. Type was permanent. Type was a promise. She lifted the first name to the light.

Her father's.

*Okonjo, E. R., archivist, second class. Transferred for interrogation. Disposition: unresolved.* Dated the eleventh of March, twenty years ago, signed below by three authorizing officers whose signatures she knew by heart now, the way you know the shape of a scar. The third signature was Hale's. Younger, harder-edged, but his—the careful, schoolmaster's hand of a man who believed he was keeping order.

"Mara." Adaeze's voice, gentle. "You don't have to set that page."

"Yes I do." She heard her own voice steady itself, brick by brick. "That's the whole point. I set it. I set all of it."

She set her father's name into the column with hands that did not shake until the fourth line, and then only a little, and no one in the room pretended not to notice, and no one said anything, which was its own kind of mercy.

* * *

The first press rolled at 4:17 in the morning, and the sound went through the floorboards and up through Mara's boots and into her chest, and she stood with her hand flat on the machine's iron flank like it was an animal she was calming, or an animal calming her.

The copies came off damp and warm. Adaeze held the first one up to the light and read the front page top to bottom in total silence, and then she folded it with a compositor's precision and said, "It's good," which Mara would learn later was the highest praise the woman gave, ever, to anyone.

They printed three thousand. It was nothing—it was a rounding error in a city of two million—but the plan had never been the paper. The plan was the broadcast. Ferro's transmitter, reassembled behind its false wall, could carry the press house's frequency across half the capital on a clear night, and a monsoon night with the government's own relay towers struck by lightning was as clear as it was going to get. At dawn, when the city woke to find wet bundles of newsprint appearing on doorsteps and under doors and in the hands of market women who owed Odile favors three ports downstream, anyone who wanted to know more could tune to a frequency that had been silent for eleven years and hear the ledger read aloud, name by name, in a living voice.

Mara was going to be the living voice.

"You could leave," Adaeze said, quietly, while Ferro tested levels and the room emptied of everyone who had families and exits. "The paper's out. The story's loose. They can't unprint it. You've done the thing. Go out the cellar, take the channel, be on the river by noon."

"And the broadcast?"

"I can read. Ferro can read. We've all got voices."

"Not the same voice." Mara touched the ledger. "I've spent my whole life on these names. I was expelled for asking about them. My father is in this book. If they're going to be said out loud, finally, properly, into the ears of the city that forgot them—" She stopped. Started again. "I need it to be me. You understand? Not for the cause. For me."

Adaeze looked at her for a long moment. Then she took off her own press apron and hung it on a nail, which Mara understood, somehow, was a benediction. "Then read slowly," Adaeze said. "Names go down better slow."

* * *

The first grey light came through the high windows at ten past six, the color of dishwater, and with it the sound in the street changed.

The idling engine had been joined by others. Boots on wet stone—not marching, nothing so theatrical, just the unhurried percussion of a cordon being set by professionals who had done it many times. A loudspeaker coughed, tested, and a voice with the flat courtesy of a recorded announcement began informing the building that it was surrounded, that the printing of seditious material was an offense under Emergency Statute Nine, and that occupants who exited through the front door with hands visible would be processed fairly.

"Processed fairly," Ferro repeated, from the false wall, where he was coaxing the transmitter's needles to life. "That's what they said about Vessania."

"Levels," Mara said.

"Levels are good." He swung a microphone on its arm toward her. "Whenever you're ready. The whole city's listening or nobody is. Either way, it's the same job."

Mara opened the ledger to page one and looked down at the first name, and thought of Silas somewhere over the harbor in a plane that was a signal fire, and thought of Odile in her port with her one eye and her terrible arithmetic, and thought of her father walking out of the national archive on the eleventh of March and never walking back in, and she leaned to the microphone and said, in a voice she did not recognize as her own until she heard it:

"This is the Free Press. My name is Mara Okonjo. Twenty years ago, the state of Vessania was erased from every map in this country, and four thousand of its people were erased from every record. This morning, the record comes back. I'm going to read you the names."

She read the first one. Then the second.

The loudspeaker outside stopped its recorded courtesy. In its place, a human voice, angrier, ordering the door breached. She kept reading. Somewhere below, the front door boomed like a struck bell, and boomed again, and the third boom was splintering wood, and she kept reading, because Adaeze had told her to read slowly, and she had promised herself she would not stop until the soldiers were close enough to touch the microphone, and she was a woman who kept her promises to the dead.

* * *

They came up the stairs at a run and she heard them coming and did not stop.

That was the strange part, the part she would carry for the rest of her life: how calm it was. The room filled with grey uniforms and black rifles and the wet smell of men who had stood in the rain all night, and the sound of the presses—still rolling, Tomas and Adaeze feeding them, bless them, bless them forever—and Mara's voice on the transmitter, and the soldiers stopped, actually stopped, because the room was so full of sound that their orders seemed to have lost their footing in it.

An officer pushed through. Young. Rain on his shoulders. "Shut it down," he said. "Shut it all down."

Mara read the next name.

He crossed the room in four strides and put his hand on the transmitter's switch, and Ferro stepped into his path, an old man with bad knees and no weapon, and said, "Son, that's a live circuit, you'll blow the tube," which was a lie, and bought them forty seconds, and Mara read three more names into those forty seconds.

Then they pulled her back. Not gently. A hand in her collar, a wrist twisted behind her, and the microphone swung away from her mouth and the ledger slid off the table and hit the floor with a slap that she felt in her teeth, and she was shouting now, finally shouting, the calm gone, the names pouring out of her at double speed like water through a broken dam—*say them, say them, they're listening, the whole city is listening*—and the officer yelled at someone to get the transmitter, and someone else kicked the ledger across the floor, and Adaeze went down on her knees over it and covered it with her body, an old woman in a rain of boots, and the presses kept rolling, kept rolling, because no one had thought to stop them.

Mara twisted around in the soldier's grip and looked at the microphone, still live, still hot, hanging at the end of its arm, and she did the only thing left.

She kept reading.

Not into it—toward it, at it, her voice pitched to carry, to be caught by the live element, garbled and half-swallowed but *there*, names tumbling out of her across the frequency in a broken, breathless litany while they dragged her toward the door: *Achebe, T. — Aduba, K. — Okonjo, E. R.*—her father, last, saved like the best line of a poem—*my father, you bastards, his name is right here, say it back, all of you, say it back—*

The door. The rain. The street, full of soldiers and floodwater and, God, *people*—market women, dockworkers, a knot of them at the cordon line holding the wet newsprint over their heads like shields, like banners, and someone in the crowd had a radio pressed to their ear and was repeating her names into the rain for the ones who couldn't hear.

The last thing Mara saw of the press house, over a soldier's shoulder, was the high window: behind it, the presses still turning, and Adaeze still on her knees, still holding the ledger, and above the roofline, far off to the east over the harbor, a single aircraft climbing through the monsoon, loud and visible, exactly as promised.

They put her in the truck. The doors slammed. The dark came down.

And through the truck's thin walls, faint but unmistakable, threaded with static and rain and the diesel growl of the convoy, she heard it: her own voice, or the ghost of it, coming from the radio of the truck's cab, where the driver had never turned the dial—still broadcasting, still repeating, because Ferro had cut the transmitter to a loop before they breached the door, an hour of names on an endless reel, rolling out over the drowned city like a tide that would not go back out.

Mara put her forehead against the cold metal wall, and in the dark, in the wet, in custody, she laughed—once, cracked, triumphant—and began, softly, to read the names again from memory.
