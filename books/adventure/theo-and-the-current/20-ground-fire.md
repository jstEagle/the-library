## 20. Ground Fire

The first round took the tail.

I felt it more than heard it—a shudder through the airframe like a fist against a door—and then the Cessna went loose in my hands, the way a horse goes loose when it decides it's done with you. The harbor spread out below, gray and boiling with rain, and somewhere down there was the Free Press building with Mara inside it, and somewhere behind me was the whole machinery of a government that had just decided I was worth spending ammunition on.

Good, I thought. That means she's still moving.

The engine coughed. Not seized—coughed, like a man clearing his throat before he says something final. I'd flown her for eleven years. I knew her moods the way I knew weather: not by instruments but by feel, by the small honest tremors of a machine that couldn't lie. She was telling me we had maybe ninety seconds of powered flight left, and the harbor was coming up fast, all that water waiting to fold around us like a closing hand.

I'd made my peace with this at the airstrip, three hours ago, when I'd wired the cowling shut with baling wire and told myself the vibration didn't matter because I wasn't planning to land so much as arrive loudly. But peace is a thing you make in daylight, in a dry hangar, with your hands busy. It's different when the water is rising toward you at two hundred feet per second and your hands are full of a dying airplane.

"Come on, old girl," I said. "One more favor."

She gave me what she had. I banked wide over the container terminal, low enough to read the crane numbers, loud enough to wake every soldier in the district, and then I pointed her nose at the widest stretch of open harbor I could find and cut the mixture.

The stall warning screamed. I let it scream.

* * *

Water hits like concrete if you're going fast enough. Everyone says that. What they don't say is that it also hits like a decision—the world's oldest decision, the one the river makes every spring, the one I'd been dodging my whole life by staying in the air, by keeping miles between me and everything that could hold me still.

The hull slapped. The nose dug. The world went white, then brown, then upside down, and the harness bit into my shoulders hard enough to crack something in my collarbone, and then there was only the cold and the roar and the strange green light of the harbor ceiling above me, rippling like the skin of a drum.

I've pulled men out of rivers. I've never been the man in the river. It's a different education.

The harness release stuck. Of course it did. Everything sticks when it's underwater and full of silt, and I hung there inverted, lungs burning, thinking with a clarity that felt almost funny: *So this is how it ends. Not in some dramatic gesture over the capital. Drowned in the harbor, tangled in my own seatbelt, while the real work happens without me.*

Then I thought about Mara reading names into a microphone, and I got angry instead of tired, and anger is better than adrenaline for working a jammed buckle.

It gave. The water took me, rolled me, spat me up.

I came out gasping between two moored barges, one arm dead from the shoulder down, blood thinning pink in the rain-washed water around me. The Cessna was gone under already—all but the tail, which stood up out of the harbor like a grave marker, streaming brown water, and I remember thinking that even drowned she looked stubborn.

Then the shouting started.

* * *

You'd think a city under martial law goes quiet when a plane falls out of its sky. You'd be wrong. A city under martial law is a pot with the lid rattling, and I had just dropped a lit match down the spout.

They came out of the warehouses first—dockworkers, night shift, faces under tarps and oilskins. Then the tenement windows along the waterfront lit up one by one, yellow squares in the rain, and people leaned out into the monsoon to look at the wreck floating in their harbor, and someone—somebody always does—started to cheer.

Not for me. They didn't know who I was. For the plane. For the sheer audacity of an airplane flying over their occupied city like the occupation was a rumor. Word travels faster than bullets in a place where everyone's been listening at doors for twenty years, and by the time I'd hauled myself onto the barge deck using my good arm and a mooring line, the story had already outrun the facts: *a plane, a pilot, shot down by the garrison, right over the harbor, in front of God and everybody.*

A woman in a rubber apron crouched next to me. Dockworker's hands, a face like a closed ledger. She looked at my shoulder, at the blood, at the tail of the plane standing in the water behind us.

"You the one they were shooting at?"

"That would be me."

"Then get off this barge." She said it without heat, the way you'd tell a man the tide's coming in. "Garrison boat's already moving. But—" She hesitated, and something moved behind her eyes that I recognized, because I'd seen it in Odile's face and in Mara's and in the stilt-village lamplight: the arithmetic of risk, done fast, done anyway. "There's a fish house on Pier Nine. Say Odile sent you."

"Odile," I said. "Of course."

"Everyone's cousin eventually," the woman said, and pushed a tarp into my hands, and turned back to her work as if a shot-down pilot were cargo like any other.

* * *

Pier Nine smelled of diesel and brine and old ice. The fish house was a shed with a corrugated roof drumming under the rain, and inside it were six strangers, a shortwave radio, and a kettle, and by the time I'd gotten my ruined jacket off and someone had splinted my collarbone with a slat of packing crate and tape, the radio was telling me what my crash had actually bought.

"—garrison confirms the aircraft was engaged after violating restricted airspace over the harbor district. The pilot's identity has not been released. In related developments, workers at the container terminal have walked off—"

"They walked off *before* the plane came down," said a young man with ink-stained fingers, one of the printer's people, sent down to watch the water. "Half shift, no announcement, no union call. They just put down their tools and stood there looking at the sky. Like they knew."

Like they knew. Twenty years of a nation pretending another nation had never existed, twenty years of ledgers burned and maps redrawn and grandmothers telling children not to ask, and apparently the whole time, the whole city had been holding its breath, waiting for somebody to break the surface first.

Mara used to say that. Back at the shack, over the chest, her hands flat on the lid like it was a pulpit: *"Silas, you can't bury a country. You can only sit on the grave. And sitting is exhausting."*

"How long since the presses started?" I asked.

The ink-stained man checked his watch. "Two hours. If she got in."

"If she got in."

"She got in." He said it flat, certain, the certainty of a man who needed it to be true. "Odile's cousin doesn't miss."

* * *

What happened over the next four hours, I mostly experienced through a radio, a kettle of terrible tea, and a window the size of a porthole, watching the city catch fire—not with flame, but with light. Every window on the waterfront. Then, across the harbor, the tenement blocks, floor by floor, like someone climbing a staircase turning on lamps. Then the sound, which I will hear for the rest of my life: not cheering, not yet, but a low continuous noise, thousands of people making the same sound at the same time, the sound a crowd makes when it stops being afraid all at once.

The radio announcer's voice cracked twice. The second time, he stopped trying to hide it and simply read the names.

Vessian names. From the ledger. Read slowly, spelled out, over state frequency—which meant someone at the transmitter had decided, on the air, in real time, to commit treason or journalism, and in this country those had finally become the same word.

"The Ministry of Information denies—" a second voice began, and was cut off mid-sentence, and the first voice came back and kept reading names.

In the fish house, nobody spoke. The dockworkers took off their caps. The woman with the ledger face, who'd followed me in—I saw her later, she was Odile's actual cousin, because of course she was—stood with her eyes closed, lips moving, following along like a prayer.

And I sat on a crate with my arm strapped to my chest and my clothes steaming in the kettle-heat and understood, with a clarity that hurt worse than the collarbone, that we had won. That it was already over, whatever happened next. You can arrest a woman. You cannot arrest a frequency. Somewhere in the dark above the drowned streets, the truth was loose and breeding, and every gun in the garrison was suddenly a shovel trying to un-dig the sea.

* * *

Hale came at dawn. Not Hale himself—his name, on the radio, in the worst possible way for his superiors.

We heard it live. A garrison order, broadcast in error on the open band—or leaked deliberately; by then I'd stopped believing in errors—commanding units to "neutralize all persons involved in the unauthorized transmission, including press-house staff and detained subjects." Neutralize. The word did its work in the room. Even the dockworkers, who'd grown up on euphemism, went still.

And then, forty minutes later, a second transmission. Calm, clipped, unmistakably an officer's voice:

"This is Inspector Hale, Internal Security Directorate. The prior order is countermanded. All detainees are to be held unharmed pending public inquiry. Any unit executing the prior order will answer to me personally." A pause, long enough to contain an entire career being set on fire. "That is all."

The fish house erupted. I didn't. I sat very still, listening to the rain, thinking about a man in a reed marsh offering amnesty with a gunboat's shadow across the water, and about an anonymous warning passed through three hands to reach a press house door: *they are watching the building.* He'd been choosing sides for longer than any of us knew. He'd just finally said it out loud where his own side could hear him.

"He's a dead man," the ink-stained printer said, half awe, half horror.

"No," I said. My voice came out rougher than I expected. "He's a free one. There's a difference, and it's about to matter."

* * *

They came for me at midmorning—but not soldiers. A delegation: the dockworker woman, two terminal stewards, a priest whose parish apparently included piers, and the ink-stained printer carrying a message written on butcher paper.

*The inquiry convenes Thursday. The archivist asked for you by name. She says: bring the rest of the pages. She says: you promised.*

I hadn't promised. I'd copied them in secret, in a cartographer's back room, ashamed of the copying even as I did it—insurance, betrayal, prudence, love, all braided together the way everything true is braided. I'd never told her. I'd carried those pages through a fever and a blockade and a burning sky, sewn into the lining of a jacket that was currently dripping on a fish-house floor.

I fished the lining out with my good hand. The pages were warped with harbor water, ink feathered at the edges, but legible. Names. Signatures. The whole rotten architecture of a massacre, folded small enough to survive a crash.

The priest looked at them, then at me. "You could have destroyed these," he said. "Saved yourself."

"I could have," I agreed. "But I'm a pilot. We don't jettison cargo. We just choose what we're willing to go down carrying."

Outside, the rain had thinned to a fine gray drift, the monsoon loosening its grip at last, and through the fish-house doorway I could see the harbor—my harbor now, our harbor, littered with the floating debris of a morning's history—and beyond it the towers of the capital, half-lost in cloud, where a woman with a chest-shaped bruise on her shoulders was presumably sitting in a cell, or a hearing room, or wherever they kept people who had just renamed a nation.

Thursday. Two days.

I looked down at the salvaged pages, then at the butcher-paper message, and felt the strangest sensation of my life: not fear, not grief, not even triumph. Just the quiet, unfamiliar weight of having somewhere to be, and someone waiting for me to arrive.

"Get me a boat," I said. "And something dry to wear. I have testimony to deliver."
