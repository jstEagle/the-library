## 22. Still Water

The river in the dry season was a different animal than the one that had tried to drown us. Where it had been brown and furious last year, all muscle and silt, it ran now like poured glass, green-gold in the morning light, so clear in the shallows that you could count the scales on a fish holding station against the current. I'd spent twenty years learning the sky over this country—every thermal, every dead calm, every lie the horizon told at dusk—and I was only now, in my second year of living on the water, learning to read it the same way.

Mara stood at the bow with one bare foot up on the gunwale, reading the river ahead of us the way she used to read ledgers: fast, skeptical, missing nothing.

"Sandbar's moved," she called back. "Left channel after the bend. Odile's chart is out of date."

"Odile's chart has been out of date since she drew it."

"That's what makes it hers." She turned, squinting against the sun, and I felt the old familiar jolt—the one that hadn't dulled in fourteen months, that I'd stopped pretending I wanted to dull. "There's a new cut past the fish traps. Deep enough if we hug the reeds."

"You trust the reeds?"

"I trust the man who mapped them." She said it lightly, but we both knew who she meant. The cartographer from the gorge country had sent word downriver in the spring—a hand-drawn sheet of the channels above the Narrows, delivered by a boy in a dugout, no price asked. *For the archivist,* the note said. *The truth keeps its own accounts.* Mara had traced every line of it twice, then folded it into the oilskin wallet where she kept things that mattered: her father's name copied out fair, Hale's letter, a photograph of the two of us taken at the inquiry by a newspaperman who'd asked us to stand closer together.

We hugged the reeds. The boat drew thirty inches loaded, and the new cut gave us four feet, black water sliding under the hull, and then we were through into the long straight reach below Odile's port, and there it was—the smoke of the cook fires, the tin roofs catching sun, the crane arm of the new dock standing up against the sky like something planted and growing.

And above the dock, on the low bluff where the flood line marked its highest ambition in old driftwood, the memorial.

You could see it from three miles off. That was the point of it. A stone pavilion, open on all sides, built by volunteers from six villages and one contrite government that had finally, grudgingly, voted funds for a thing it couldn't take credit for. Inside it, under glass, lit by a skylight no bigger than a coffin lid, sat the chest.

I still dreamed about pulling it from the water sometimes. Not the seal, not the ledger, not the names—just the weight of it coming up the rope hand over hand, water streaming off the wood, my arms burning, some animal part of me already knowing that whatever was inside would end the life I'd built. It always woke me calm, though. That was the strange thing. In the dream I never once thought about letting go of the rope.

* * *

Odile met us at the dock with her one eye narrowed against the glare and her good hand already reaching for our mooring line before I'd thrown it.

"You're late," she said.

"We're early," Mara said, stepping onto the planks. "Your calendar's out of date too."

"My calendar says the mail boat comes when it comes, which today means late." But she pulled Mara into a one-armed embrace anyway, rough as a hatch closing, and held it a beat longer than she'd ever admit to. Then she looked at me over Mara's shoulder. "Pilot. You look terrible."

"I've been told I look well."

"Then they're lying to you." She released Mara and jerked her chin toward the cargo. "Medicine?"

"And mail. Forty-one sacks. The capital post office finally acknowledged the route exists."

"A miracle. Two years ago they didn't acknowledge the river exists." She hoisted a sack with her good arm, wincing, and I took it from her without making a production of it. Her shoulder had never healed right since the night she'd stood between us and a soldier's rifle butt outside the press house—not that she'd been there; not that anyone official would say she had. The inquiry had needed her testimony more than the army had needed her punished, and in the end the arithmetic had come out in her favor, mostly. Mostly was the national average these days.

We worked the cargo up the ramp in the easy rhythm of people who'd done it a hundred times. The port had grown since the inquiry—two new warehouses, a proper clinic with a zinc roof, a school whose morning lessons we could hear drifting down through the palms, thirty children receding something in unison. Odile's back room still hosted the card game on Saturdays, but now half the players were clerks and teachers and one very retired gunboat captain who came upriver twice a season and lost money cheerfully and never once mentioned the night he'd ordered the river locked down.

Hale. He'd be here tomorrow, actually. He came every few months, ostensibly to inspect the memorial's security, actually to sit across from Mara at Odile's table and drink rum and talk about archives until midnight. He'd testified for eleven hours. He'd named his own superiors, his own orders, his own signature on the authorization page, in a voice so flat the transcript read like a weather report. They'd given him a desk job in the capital and he'd resigned it inside a month. Now he did what we did, in his way—carried things upriver, put them down where they belonged. Some debts you don't pay off. You just keep making payments.

"He'll want the new pages," Mara said, as if I'd spoken aloud. We'd gotten good at that—finishing each other's manifests.

"There are no new pages."

"There are always new pages." She looked up the bluff toward the pavilion. "The Vessian families in the eastern settlements have been sending documents to the archive. Letters. Photographs. A woman in Kessari found her brother's service record in a wall. The truth didn't end with the ledger, Silas. It just started there."

I watched her face while she talked about it—that particular brightness, the one I'd first seen in my shack two years ago when she'd laid the opened chest between us and said *this is sanctioned, do you understand, this wasn't chaos, someone signed it*—and I thought, not for the first time, how lucky I was that she'd never learned to stop.

"Come on," I said. "Before the whole port descends. Let's pay our respects first."

* * *

The path up the bluff was worn smooth now, red clay packed hard by a thousand feet. Someone had planted frangipani along it, and they were blooming out of season because frangipani doesn't care about seasons, only about deciding it's time. At the top, the pavilion stood open to the wind, its stone columns cool even in the heat, and inside, under glass, the chest sat exactly where we'd placed it eighteen months ago, on a plinth of river rock, its Vessian crest facing the door.

They'd left the lid open. That had been Mara's insistence, argued through three committee meetings and one shouting match with a ministry architect. *A closed chest is a secret,* she'd said. *An open one is an invitation.* So the lid stood propped, and inside, instead of the documents themselves—those lived in the national archive now, behind better locks than the ones that failed before—you saw facsimiles. The photographs. The first page of the ledger, with its column of names. And beneath the glass of a separate case, arranged in a spiral that the architect had hated and everyone else loved, the original list itself, handwritten fair by Mara over one long week, every name legible, every name spelled the way the families spelled it.

People came up here every day. That was the thing I hadn't predicted. I'd thought a memorial was a place you visited once, the way you visit a grave. But the women from the fish camps came weekly, and the schoolchildren came in noisy crocodiles, and old men came alone at dawn and stood a long time, and travelers off the mail boats—we weren't the only route anymore; there were three now, and Odile's port was becoming what ports become, a hinge—travelers came and read the names and went down the hill quieter than they'd come up.

Mara walked the spiral slowly, as she always did, lips moving over the names. I hung back by the door column, the way I always did. This part was hers. My name wasn't in the spiral. Neither was hers. We'd survived, and the spiral was for the ones who hadn't, and there was a rightness to standing outside it.

But halfway around, she stopped. Touched the glass over one name near the center. Okonjo, E. Disappeared 19 Vessian purge, confirmed. Her father. She'd written his name herself, in ink she'd mixed from soot and gum arabic because she wanted it to be ink made the way ink was made when he was alive.

I crossed to her and put my hand on her back, between the shoulder blades, and she leaned into it without turning, the way a boat leans into a current it trusts.

"He'd have liked the pavilion," she said. "He hated doors."

"He'd have liked that the lid's open."

"He'd have complained about the letter spacing." She laughed, wet-eyed, and wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, done. Then she looked at me, and the brightness was there, banked now, steady, a lamp instead of a flare. "Two years ago I knocked on your shack door ready to beg a pilot to fly me into a war zone."

"It wasn't a shack door. It was a shack wall. There wasn't a door."

"There was a hole where a door should be, and a madman living behind it with a stolen chest and a broken airplane."

"The plane wasn't broken yet."

"No," she said softly. "It wasn't."

We stood a moment with that. The plane. The *Kestrel*. They'd hauled her out of the harbor mud eleven days after I put her down in it, shot through the engine cowling, one wingtip stove in, and for a while there'd been talk of restoring her, putting her in a museum next to the chest. I'd gone to look at her once, sitting on trestles in a government hangar with a rope around her tail, and I'd lasted four minutes. Museums are where machines go to stop being themselves. I'd signed the release papers that afternoon and donated the proceeds to the clinic here, and Odile had bought the boat we run now with what was left, and named it, without asking either of us, the *Second Current*.

Some sacrifices you make once. Some you keep making, in smaller denominations, forever. I'd stopped counting which was which.

"Come on," Mara said. "Odile will feed us whether we're hungry or not, and the children will want to see the mail."

* * *

The afternoon went the way the best afternoons go, which is to say it disappeared. Mail sorted on the dock tables while kids orbited us like gulls, snatching at envelopes, demanding news of the capital, of the inquiry, of anything. Medicine carried up to the clinic, where Sister Amara—no relation to Mara, a coincidence that had delighted them both upon meeting—put me to work fixing a shelf bracket within ninety seconds of arrival, because in this country a visitor with working hands is a resource, not a guest. Supper at Odile's long table under the awning: river fish, rice, the green mango pickle that Odile made fierce enough to strip paint, and twelve people talking over each other in three languages, and nobody at that table invisible, nobody at it careful, everybody in it exactly as loud and wrong and right as they pleased.

Found family, Mara called it once, and I'd winced at the phrase because it sounded like something off a printed page—and then I'd looked down the table at Odile arguing with the retired captain about cards, at Sister Amara laughing, at the printer's apprentice from the capital who'd guided Mara up through the flooded cellar and never left, at Hale due tomorrow on the evening boat, at Mara beside me with pickle juice on her thumb and the whole ruined, rebuilt story of us sitting quietly in her eyes—and I'd decided the phrase could stay.

After dark, we walked out the point past the fish traps, where the river goes wide and slow and holds the stars double. The dry-season air smelled of woodsmoke and water. Somewhere upstream a drum was going, patient as a heartbeat.

"Mosquito season starts in six weeks," Mara said.

"It does."

"The eastern settlements want the route extended past the Narrows. Odile thinks the boat can handle it if we rebuild the stern gland first. Hale wants to come as far as the gorge—he's never seen the channel the cartographer drew."

"You've been planning this longer than six weeks."

"Since breakfast." She leaned her head on my shoulder. "Is that all right?"

I looked upriver, into the dark where the water ran on toward places I'd flown over a hundred times and never once known. Two years ago my life had been miles—miles instead of years, miles instead of names, miles as a way of staying nowhere enough that nothing could find me. Now the miles had shrunk to a river's width, and the years had opened up like the channel past the fish traps, deep water where I'd braced for sand.

"Mosquito season lasts four months," I said.

"So we'll need the netting fixed."

"So we'll need the netting fixed." I turned and kissed her hair, salt and river and woodsmoke. "And the stern gland. And probably everything else."

"Everything else," she agreed, "in that order."

The drum upstream changed rhythm. Down at the dock, a lantern swung—Odile, signaling, though there was nothing to signal about at this hour except habit and care. The river slid past us, dark and unhurried, carrying its doubled stars toward the sea, and I understood, standing there with her weight against my shoulder, that we hadn't arrived anywhere in particular. We'd arrived in the condition of arriving. Every season from here was a place we'd get to together, and none of them, God willing, would require us to disappear.

"Silas," Mara said, after a while, in the voice she used for things she'd been carrying all day.

"Hm?"

"The woman in Kessari. The one who found her brother's service record in the wall." She lifted her head. "She sent a letter with the last mail packet. There are more records. A whole cache, sealed in a mission house that's about to be sold. If nobody collects them before the buyers move in—" She stopped. Not because she doubted. Because she knew I'd already said yes.

I looked upriver, into the dark.

"When does the water rise?" I asked.

"Six weeks," she said. "Maybe five."

"Then we'd better fix that stern gland."

She laughed—the real one, the one I'd crossed three hundred miles of floodwater to earn—and took my hand, and we walked back down the point toward the lantern light, planning the next hundred miles all the way home.
