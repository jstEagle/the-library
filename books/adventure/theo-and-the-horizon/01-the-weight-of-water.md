## 1. The Weight of Water

Fog on the Sorrow was a living thing. It came up off the water before dawn, thick as wool, and it swallowed the banks whole — the alders, the pilings, the drowned villages that showed their rooftops only in the driest summers. Rosalind Vane had learned to read the river through it the way other people read faces: by feel, by sound, by the particular silence the current made when it slid over the third sandbar below Grayling Bend.

She stood at the wheel with her boots planted wide and her hands light on the spokes, and she did not think about the fog at all.

That was the trick of it, three years in. You didn't think about the thing you couldn't change. You thought about depth soundings and coal consumption and whether Milo had remembered to grease the paddle shaft, because a riverboat did not forgive a distracted pilot, and neither did the Sorrow. The river took what it wanted from anyone who looked away too long. She knew that better than most. She'd paid for that lesson in a currency she refused to count anymore.

"Bar's coming up shallow," Milo said from the bow rail, his voice muffled by the mist. He was nineteen and all elbows, leaning out over the water like he hadn't yet learned what rivers kept. "Hear it?"

She heard it. A soft combing sound under the hull's port quarter, gravel whispering against iron. She eased the wheel two spokes to starboard and felt the Kestrel answer, heavy and obedient, the way she always answered. Ninety tons of riveted steel and stubbornness. Rosalind had built her back list into the boat herself, plank by plank, in a yard outside Port Merrow, and there wasn't a seam aboard she couldn't find blind.

The bar fell away behind them. The fog thinned for a moment — just long enough to show the far bank, a smear of dark pines — then closed again like a door.

Three years. She measured them in miles now, not months. Miles were honest. Miles added up clean at the end of a season, and you could point at a number and say: *this is what I've done instead.* Eleven thousand miles last year, running freight and passengers and whatever else paid, up and down the Sorrow and its tributaries, from the sea gate at Vell to the last navigable reach below the mountains. Eleven thousand miles of not thinking about it.

It didn't work. But it filled the days, and full days sank slower.

* * *

They made Grayling Bend by midmorning, tying up at a landing that was really just rotting pilings and a plank walkway someone's grandfather had nailed down. There was a trade post there, a woodshed of a building with a tin roof, run by an old woman named Petra who charged fair prices and asked no questions, which was why half the river traded there.

Rosalind went ashore for stores because she always went ashore for stores. Routine was ballast. You stacked it low and even and the boat rode steady.

Petra had coffee on, bitter and black enough to strip paint, and she poured two cups without asking. She was seventy if she was a day, small and wiry, with hands like tree roots and eyes that missed nothing on the river or off it.

"You look tired," Petra said.

"I look the same I always look."

"That's what I said." Petra slid a folded newspaper across the counter, then stopped, her hand flat on it. "There's a letter, too. Came up on the packet boat yesterday. Been waiting."

Rosalind didn't get letters. That was the truth of her life laid plain: nobody wrote to Rosalind Vane. Her crew got letters sometimes — Milo's mother sent him things wrapped in oilcloth, careful and smelling of lavender. Dree, her quartermaster, got nothing and wanted nothing, which suited them both. But Rosalind's name on an envelope meant either debt collectors or lawyers, and she'd settled both accounts years ago.

"Who's it from?"

"Sealed when it came. See for yourself."

Petra turned the letter around on the counter.

The wax was black. The seal pressed into it was a marsh hawk, wings spread, talons down — a bird stooping to strike.

Rosalind's hand went still on her cup.

Cassian Marsh. Of course it was Cassian Marsh. Nobody else on the river used black wax; nobody else would dare. The marsh hawk had been famous from Vell to the mountain passes ten years ago, back when Cassian ran cargo nobody asked about and made more money in a season than Rosalind made in five. Back when they'd been rivals in the way that fires are rivals — burning the same air, hungry for the same wind.

Back before.

She set the cup down. Her hand was steady. She'd trained it to be.

"What did the carrier say?"

"Said the gentleman paid double for speed." Petra's mouth twitched. "Said he wants an answer soon."

"He can want in one hand and spit in the other."

But she took the letter. That was the thing she noticed later, turning it over in her cabin that night while the Kestrel rocked gently at her moorings: she took it. She could have left it on Petra's counter to gather dust until the old woman burned it with the season's rubbish. Instead her fingers had closed around it before her pride caught up, and by then it was in her pocket, and pockets have a way of deciding things.

* * *

The Kestrel's cabin was eight feet by ten and held everything Rosalind owned that wasn't bolted down: a bunk, a chart table, a shelf of books gone soft with damp, a brass compass hanging above the chart table that she never used because she never needed it, but which she would sooner have sold the boat than parted with.

She sat at the chart table. She turned the letter over twice. Black wax, marsh hawk, Cassian Marsh.

Then she broke the seal, because three years of routine will only carry a person so far, and some doors knock until you open them.

*Captain Vane.*

*You'll want to throw this in the river. I'd deserve it. But I'm asking anyway, and I don't ask twice, so hear me out.*

*I have cargo that needs moving upriver to Highmoor Settlement before the snows close the pass. Sealed crates, medicine, time-critical. Triple your going rate, paid half in advance, no questions asked — and yes, I know what you'll say to that, so let me say this instead: I need you specifically. Not any pilot. Not the fastest boat. You.*

*I won't put the reason in writing. Some reasons shouldn't travel. Come to Port Merrow, the Salt Anchor, within the week, and I'll tell you everything, and you can tell me to go to hell in person, which I suspect you'll enjoy.*

*Either way, I remain, as ever,*

*C.M.*

Rosalind read it three times. Then she set it down on the chart table, next to the compass, and looked at the ceiling of her cabin for a long while.

Triple rate. Medicine. Highmoor.

Highmoor.

The word landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. She waited for the splash and it didn't come — the well was deeper than that, three years deep at least, and counting.

She didn't think about Highmoor. That was the rule. Highmoor was part of the thing she didn't name, filed away with the rest of it, sealed in black wax of its own making. She ran the lower river now. She'd told herself it was economics — the upper reaches were thin water, hard miles, poor pay — but economics was just grief wearing a practical coat, and she'd known it every single day for three years and said it out loud to no one.

And now Cassian Marsh, of all the people on God's green water, was holding out triple pay to carry medicine to the one place on the map she'd sworn never to see again.

He'd have known that. That was the worst of it. Cassian knew exactly what he was doing. He always had. Ten years of rivalry taught her that much: the man never fired a shot he hadn't already decided to win with. If he was asking her to sail into the past, it was because he knew the past was the one cargo she couldn't refuse to carry.

Milo knocked. "Cap? Weather's clearing. We running the evening tide up to Merrow, or laying over?"

Rosalind looked at the letter. At the compass hanging above it, catching lamplight, pointing — as it always pointed — north, toward the mountains, toward the pass, toward Highmoor.

She measured her life in miles. Eleven thousand last year. And here was a man offering her the longest mile of all, the one she'd been walking around in circles for three years without ever actually taking.

"We're running," she said. "Get Dree to break out the good coal. If we're going to Port Merrow, we're going fast."

Milo grinned and thumped away down the deck. Rosalind folded the letter once, twice, and tucked it inside her coat, over the breast pocket, where she could feel it against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

The fog was lifting off the Sorrow. Ahead of her, somewhere past the bend, the river ran north toward the mountains, and for the first time in three years, Rosalind Vane found she was afraid of where she was going — and glad of it.
