## 2. An Old Debt in a New Harbor

Port Merrow smelled of tar and herring and other people's money, and Rosalind Vane had never once been glad to see it.

She brought the Kestrel in on the morning tide with the fog still peeling off the water like old paint. The harbor was half-full — fishing boats mostly, a grain barge or two, nothing that would look twice at a riverboat riding light with empty holds. She took the last open berth at the north quay, tied off her own lines because her crew knew better than to touch them before she was done, and stood a moment at the rail watching the gulls argue over the fish market.

Three years she had measured her life in miles. Miles upriver, miles downriver, miles between the places where the past couldn't reach her. Port Merrow sat at the edge of the map she'd drawn for herself — close enough to the mountains that on clear days you could see the white line of the passes, far enough that nobody asked why a woman who ran cargo alone kept her wheelhouse so quiet.

She went ashore for rope and lamp oil and salt beef, and it was on the way back, cutting through the customs house square with her collar up, that she saw the seal.

It was on a letter pinned under a weight of brass on the harbormaster's board, among the notices about dredging schedules and quarantine flags. Heavy cream paper. A wax stamp pressed with a marsh hawk in flight — wings spread, talons down, the mark Cassian Marsh had used since before either of them could legally drink. She stopped walking the way you stop when a floorboard gives way beneath you: all at once, and already falling.

The letter said only: *Kestrel. North quay. Come at dusk. — C.M.*

No please. No apology. Just the hawk and his initials, as if three years were a thing you could fold flat and slip under a door.

Rosalind read it twice, then walked back to the boat without buying the salt beef, which told her more about her state of mind than she cared to know.

* * *

Cassian Marsh had taken a room above the Salt & Anchor, which meant he was either doing well or pretending to. He opened the door before she knocked, which meant he'd been watching the street from the window like a man waiting on a debt collector.

"You got my letter."

"I got your letter." She stepped past him into the room. It was clean, spare, one chair, one bed, a table with a single lamp turned low. He looked older. Not softer — older the way river stone is older, worn into new shapes by the same water. His hair had gone grey at the temples and he wore it shorter than he used to, and there was a scar along his jaw she didn't remember giving him time to earn.

"Sit," he said.

"I'll stand."

"Rosalind."

"Cassian." She let the silence do its work. It always had. He looked away first, out the window toward the harbor, where the Kestrel rode at her lines with her running lights unlit.

"You look well," he said.

"Liar. What do you want?"

He turned back to her, and whatever softness he'd been rehearsing fell away, and what was left underneath was the man she remembered — quick, careful, dangerous in the way of people who plan three moves ahead and count on you to make two of them.

"One run," he said. "One. Upriver, before the snows close the pass. Triple your usual rate, paid half in advance, half on delivery."

"What cargo?"

"Sealed crates. Medicine."

"To where?"

"Highmoor Settlement."

The name landed in the room like a dropped anchor. Highmoor. Up past the Sorrow Narrows, past the last customs station, up where the river gave out entirely and the mule tracks began. A settlement of maybe two hundred souls clinging to a high valley that the government remembered only when it wanted votes and forgot whenever medicine was concerned.

"That's not a run," Rosalind said. "That's a funeral with extra steps. The pass closes in five weeks. Customs has doubled patrols on the upper river since spring. And you're asking me to take sealed crates — sealed, meaning no manifest I can show if I'm boarded — through all of it, alone."

"Not alone." He almost smiled. "With me."

"Worse."

"Triple pay, Rosalind."

"Quadruple."

He blinked. "You said—"

"I said quadruple. If you're asking me to do something stupid enough to need me specifically, then you're asking me to risk everything I have, which means the price isn't money, it's the boat. And the boat doesn't come cheap." She crossed her arms. "Who paid you? Whose medicine is it?"

Something flickered across his face — the first honest thing he'd shown her. "I can't say."

"You can't or you won't?"

"Won't. There are people whose safety depends on nobody knowing they funded this. Including yours. Especially yours, once you say yes."

"If I say yes."

"When," he said, with the old infuriating certainty. "You'll say yes. You always did hate an unsolved problem more than you loved being safe."

"I love being safe fine. I've gotten very good at it. Three years good." She moved toward the door. "Find another pilot. There are a dozen men in this port who'd run those crates for double and ask fewer questions."

"There aren't," Cassian said quietly. "And you know it. Half of them can't read the Narrows in fog. The other half would sell the crates to the first checkpoint that offered amnesty. I need someone who knows the river like a language and hates me enough to check my work twice." He paused. "I need you, Rosalind. That's the truth of it. I've needed you for three years and I've been too much of a coward to send a letter until now."

Her hand was on the latch. She should have opened it. Every mile she'd put between herself and this man, this life, this particular flavor of trouble — every mile said *open the door, walk down the stairs, go home to your quiet boat.*

"No," she said. "Whatever this is, whoever's paying, whatever guilt you're carrying around looking for somewhere to set it down — it isn't mine. I don't smuggle anymore. I made that decision standing in a place you know about, and I haven't broken it since, and I'm not going to break it for triple pay and a pretty speech."

"It's quadruple now," Cassian said. "You raised it yourself."

"Then it's quadruple times no."

She opened the door. And that was when she saw the manifest.

It lay on the table where he'd left it, half under the lamp, a shipping order folded in quarters with the funding ledger clipped to it. Ordinary paper. Ordinary ink. But the return address was printed at the top in a clerk's careful hand, and Rosalind's eyes went to it the way a compass needle goes north, helplessly, instantly, before the rest of her could object.

*St. Brannoc's Home for Foundling Children, Greywater Road, Port Merrow.*

The orphanage.

The orphanage where a skinny girl with river mud on her boots had learned to read off cargo manifests and steal apples off the customs barge and swear that someday she'd have a boat of her own. Where Wren had arrived two winters after her, all elbows and fury, and where the two of them had lain on the roof tiles counting ships and promising each other the horizon. Where Cassian had come as a charity case with quick hands and quicker arithmetic, and the three of them had become the kind of family that isn't given to you but built, plank by plank, out of shared hunger and shared dreams.

Where her whole sworn-off life had begun.

The room went very quiet. Even the harbor noise seemed to hold its breath.

"They're funding it," Cassian said softly. "The children. The older ones — the ones who've apprenticed out, the ones working the docks. They pooled what they had. Cooked the books of the laundry fund, sold a boat that was willed to the home, went door to door along Greywater Road. It took them eight months to raise enough." He watched her face do what it was doing. "They bought the medicine for Highmoor because of what happened there three years ago. Because somebody told them the story, and children keep stories the way adults keep grudges. They came to me because I owed them. And I came to you because—" he exhaled, "—because there was never anyone else. Not really. Not ever."

Rosalind stared at the manifest. Her pride was a wall she'd built stone by stone over three years, and it was still standing, and it would go on standing, and none of that mattered at all, because behind the wall something old and wounded had lifted its head at the smell of home.

"The crates load tomorrow night," Cassian said. "Tide's at eleven. I'll be at the north quay either way. With you or without you."

She picked up the manifest. Folded it. Put it in her coat pocket, which was not returning it, and was not accepting anything, and was exactly the kind of ambiguity she'd have despised in anyone else.

"I'm not saying yes," she said.

"Of course not."

"And if I change my mind, it's quadruple, and you sleep on deck, and you touch nothing on my boat without asking."

"Naturally."

"And Cassian—" she stopped at the doorway, not turning around, because if she turned around he'd see whatever was written on her face and she wasn't ready to have it read. "If this is some scheme of yours, if there's a profit hidden anywhere inside this, I will put you off the Kestrel at the Narrows and let the river sort out the rest."

"I'd expect nothing less," he said, and she heard the smile in it, and hated it, and climbed down the stairs into the dark with a dead woman's hometown folded in her pocket and the first snows already falling somewhere up in the passes she'd sworn never to see again.

* * *

Back aboard, she lit the wheelhouse lamp and sat a long while with the manifest open on the chart table, next to the brass compass she kept in the drawer and never used.

The compass needle swung, settled, pointed north — upriver, toward the Narrows, toward Highmoor, toward everything.

She was still sitting there when the deck creaked aft, near the cargo hatch, in a way decks don't creak on their own.
