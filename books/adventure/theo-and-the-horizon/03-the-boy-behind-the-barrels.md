## 3. The Boy Behind the Barrels

The barrel smelled of salt fish and lies, and Theo had been inside it long enough to know both intimately.

He'd climbed in at Port Merrow in the dark, when the dockhands were drunk enough to sing and blind enough not to count. The plan had been simple: ride the river a day, slip out at the first stop, find work, work his way upriver. The plan had not accounted for the barrel being *loaded onto a boat that didn't stop*, or for salt brine soaking through his trousers, or for the fact that ten-year-old legs go numb after four hours in a space built for herring.

He shifted, and his elbow knocked the compass where it hung against his chest on its cord. Brass, warm from his skin even in the cold of the hold. His mother's voice came back the way it always did when he touched it: *North is just a promise you make to yourself, little bird. Everything else is weather.*

"Everything else is weather," he whispered, because saying it out loud made it true.

Then the lid came off, and light poured in like an accusation.

Theo looked up into the face of a man with a beard like an uncombed hedge and eyes gone wide as portholes.

"Well," said the man. "That's new."

* * *

They put him in the middle of the deck, which Theo decided was theater — making him sit where everyone could stare, like a caught fish on display. The big one, the one who'd found him, stood behind him with his arms crossed like a fence. Two others had drifted over: a woman with grey-streaked braids and a ledger under her arm, and a wiry boy maybe sixteen who kept glancing between Theo and the cargo crates as if doing arithmetic.

And then she came.

Theo knew her before anyone said her name. He'd seen her once before, three years ago, through the window of a harbor office in Lowmarsh — a woman arguing with men in customs coats, tall and straight-backed, with a scar splitting one eyebrow. Rosalind Vane. His mother had said the name like a curse and a prayer at once. *Rosalind Vane could run this river blindfolded, little bird, and half the time I think she does.*

She stopped in front of him and looked down, and Theo lifted his chin and did not look away, because Wren Okonkwo's son did not look away from anybody.

"A stowaway," Captain Vane said. Her voice was flat as the water at dawn. "On my boat."

"Your boat's going upriver," Theo said. "So am I."

"Is that so." She crouched to his level, which surprised him; most adults loomed. Up close her eyes were the color of old bottle glass, and they moved over him quick and thorough — counting ribs, cataloguing bruises, reading him like a chart. "How old are you?"

"Old enough."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting until we're past the next checkpoint." He'd rehearsed that line for two days and it landed exactly the way he'd hoped: something flickered behind her eyes that might have been respect, or might have been indigestion.

The woman with the ledger snorted. "Oh, I like him."

"You would, Dree," the bearded one said.

Captain Vane held out her hand. "Anything you're carrying, I need to see. Now."

Theo's hand went to the compass before he could stop it. He curled his fingers around it, felt the worn brass, the dent in the rim where — where his mother had dropped it running from the checkpoint guards, the night everything went wrong. He thought about lying. He thought about refusing.

But there was nowhere left to hide it, and some part of him — the part that had been alone since the orphanage turned him out at spring thaw, the part that was tired down to the marrow — wanted someone else to know what he was carrying.

He pulled the cord over his head and set the compass in her palm.

* * *

He watched her face do something strange.

It started small — a blink, a stillness in her hands — and then spread, like frost across a window. She turned the compass over. Ran her thumb along the dent in the rim. Turned it back and read the engraving on the case, the words worn nearly smooth by a decade of thumbs:

*W. OKONKWO — NORTH IS A PROMISE.*

The deck got very quiet. Even the river seemed to lower its voice.

"Where," Captain Vane said, and her voice wasn't flat anymore, it was scraped raw underneath, "did you get this."

"It was my mother's."

"Your mother's name."

"Wren." He said it loud, daring her to flinch. "Wren Okonkwo. And Cassian Marsh owes her. That's why I'm here. He owes her a debt and I've come to collect."

For a moment nobody moved. Then the bearded one — Milo, someone had called him — said, very quietly, "Cap?"

"I know whose it is, Milo." She hadn't taken her eyes off Theo. "I'd know it anywhere. I watched her buy it off a dead man's estate in Casterhal when you were still wetting your bunk."

Something hot rose in Theo's chest. "You knew my mother?"

"I knew *of* her." Captain Vane stood, and the compass stayed in her fist, and Theo's heart lurched — *give it back, give it back* — but she held it out to him almost immediately, handle first, careful as handing over a live coal. "Everyone on this river knew of Wren Okonkwo."

"Then you know Marsh owes her." He jammed the cord back over his head, and the compass settled against his chest, and he could breathe again. "She ran a shipment for him three years ago. Medicine, up to Highmoor. She never got paid. She died waiting on the rest of it, and he never—" His voice cracked and he hated it, hated it, hated it. "He never sent so much as a letter."

The crew exchanged looks like coins passing under a table. The wiry boy mouthed *Highmoor* to Dree, and Dree's ledger-hand went still.

Captain Vane crouched again. This time she didn't look through him. She looked *at* him, and Theo had the sudden, uncomfortable sense of being seen all the way down.

"How did you get out of Port Merrow?" she asked. "Who told you we were sailing?"

"Nobody told me. I can read a tide table." Which was a lie — Milo's cousin had told him at the orphanage gate, for a copper and a kiss on the forehead from a woman who smelled of tar — but he delivered it with his mother's best poker face, and Captain Vane's eyes narrowed like she knew it was a lie and couldn't prove it.

"You're ten."

"I'm *capable*."

"You're a child hiding in a fish barrel on a boat carrying sealed cargo, bound for a checkpoint run, in the last week before the passes close." She ticked each item off on her fingers. "Do you understand what happens if customs finds you? They don't send children back to Port Merrow, boy. They send them to the work houses past Graywater, and nobody comes back from Graywater."

"Then don't get caught."

Milo barked a laugh and strangled it when she glared.

"Here's what's going to happen," Captain Vane said. "Dree will find you dry clothes and feed you. You'll stay where I can see you. And when we reach the next landing, I'm putting you on the mail packet back downstream—"

"No."

"—where the sisters at the mission will—"

"*No.*" Theo was on his feet now, fists balled, the compass swinging against his chest. "You don't get to decide. My mother ran your river. She ran it for *him*. And she died in a customs cell in Lowmarsh while her medicine sat in a warehouse forty miles away, rotting, because some clerk stamped CONFISCATED on it and went home to his supper." His voice was shaking and he let it shake. "Cassian Marsh owes her. Highmoor owes her. *You* owe her, if you knew her name. So no. You don't get to mail me back like a returned parcel. I'm cargo with a claim, and I'm not getting off."

The silence after that was long enough to hear the water slapping the hull.

Dree wrote something in her ledger without looking at it. The wiry boy stared at his boots. Milo scratched his hedge of a beard and studied the sky like it owed him money.

Captain Vane looked at Theo for a long, long moment. And Theo saw it happen — saw the fury in her face curdle and shift and settle into something else, something that looked almost like pain wearing armor. He'd seen that look before, on the faces of the mission sisters when they talked about his mother. Grief that had been carried too long to set down.

"Below decks," she said finally. "Dree, find him a blanket. Milo — you're on watch with him. Nobody talks to him about anything until I say so." She turned to go, then stopped, her back to him, one hand resting on the rail.

"Theo," she said, without turning around.

His breath caught. He'd never told her his name.

"Wren talked about a son," Captain Vane said quietly. "Once. In Casterhal, the day she bought that compass. She said she'd teach him north before she taught him letters." A pause. "She said his name was Theo, and that he was going to be trouble."

Behind her, Dree smiled into her ledger.

"So," Captain Vane said, and now she did turn, and her bottle-glass eyes were wet and furious and something else entirely, something that made Theo's chest ache in a way he didn't have words for yet. "You'll eat in the galley. You'll sleep in the fo'c'sle. And you'll tell me everything you know about Cassian Marsh's debt to your mother — every word, every date — because I have a feeling the man who hired me for this run has been lying to me since the day he stepped aboard my deck."

She walked aft toward the wheelhouse, coat snapping in the wind, and Theo stood among strangers on a stolen deck with a brass compass warm against his heart.

Milo crouched beside him and offered a hand the size of a dinner plate.

"Fair warning, lad," the big man rumbled. "Nobody argues with the captain and wins. Been trying eleven years myself." He grinned. "You lasted longer than most."

Theo took the hand and let himself be pulled up, legs still numb, stomach growling, compass steady.

Upriver, somewhere past the fog, Cassian Marsh was waiting — and Theo meant to make him pay.
