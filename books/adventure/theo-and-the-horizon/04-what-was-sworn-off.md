## 4. What Was Sworn Off

Cassian Marsh had faced down customs cutters, river pirates, and one memorable winter with a bear in his cargo hold, but nothing had ever frightened him like the silence of Rosalind Vane.

She sat across the galley fire from him, arms crossed, watching Theo sleep in the corner nook where Milo had dragged a spare blanket an hour ago. The boy hadn't let go of the brass compass even in sleep. His fingers were curled around it like a fist around a heart.

"You knew," Rosalind said. It wasn't a question. It was an accusation wearing a question's clothes.

"I suspected." Cassian fed a split log to the stove and watched the flames take it. "The compass confirmed it."

"Then you put that boy on my boat knowing whose son he was. Knowing what seeing that compass would do to me." Her voice stayed level, which was worse than shouting. Cassian had heard her shout at storms. This quiet was the sound of a woman deciding whether to throw you overboard. "You used him as leverage."

"I used the truth as leverage," he said. "There's a difference. Small, but it matters to me."

"It shouldn't."

* * *

The Kestrel rode at anchor in a bend of the river called the Long Still, water so deep and slow it held the stars without breaking them. Above deck, Milo kept first watch and Dree kept second, though Cassian had noticed Dree checking her pocket watch more often than watch-keeping required. He filed that away. He'd been filing things away about Dree for two days now, small discrepancies, and the pile was getting heavy enough to rattle.

But that was tomorrow's problem. Tonight there was only the fire, the sleeping boy, and three years of debt sitting between him and Rosalind like a fourth passenger.

"Tell me how she died," Rosalind said. "All of it. Not the version you gave me at Port Merrow. The real one."

Cassian looked into the fire because looking at Rosalind would have been unbearable. "You remember the winter Wren left Highmoor. Three years back. She came downriver with a list as long as her arm — antibiotics, insulin, fever reducers, surgical thread. Half the settlement's stores had spoiled when their cold cellar flooded. She'd been writing to the regional health office for eight months and getting letters back that said *request received, pending review.*"

"I remember the letters." Rosalind's jaw tightened. "I told her pending review meant no."

"You told her to smuggle. So did I. That's the part we've never said out loud." He watched her flinch, just barely. "We both told her we'd run it ourselves, and then we both found reasons not to. You had the salvage contract on the Vane shoals. I had the Merrow grain deal. And Wren — Wren being Wren — she didn't ask twice. She hired some cut-rate runner out of Tarn Hollow, paid him everything she had, and went upriver alone."

"The checkpoint at Grayford," Rosalind said quietly.

"Grayford." The word came out of Cassian like a splinter coming out. "The runner panicked at the inspection. Tried to bribe a junior officer with money he didn't have, got caught, and the whole shipment went into the confiscation lockup. Standard procedure. Thirty days' impoundment while they verified the manifest." He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "Thirty days. That's what the law required. Verification of a manifest for medicine that was already listed, already legal, already bound for a settlement that had filed the request itself. The paperwork was right there. Any officer with eyes could have released it in an afternoon."

"But nobody did."

"Nobody did. Because releasing it would have required someone to admit the impoundment had been pointless, and admitting that would have required someone to care." Cassian spread his hands. "Wren waited at Grayford eleven days. She petitioned. She wrote letters. She stood in the snow outside the customs house until an officer threatened to arrest her for obstruction. On the twelfth day she started home to get more money for the fines, and the fever she'd been carrying since Highmoor — she'd been sick herself the whole time, did you know that? Of course you didn't. She hid it from everyone — turned into pneumonia. She made it as far as the ferry crossing at Lowbridge."

Rosalind closed her eyes.

"She died in the ferry shelter," Cassian said. "With her petition papers in her lap. A boatman found her at dawn. The settlement didn't learn for another week, and by then the thirty-day impoundment had expired and the medicine was released — released, mind you, to be sold at auction, because the original requester was deceased and the claim lapsed. Some merchant in Tarn Hollow bought insulin for pennies and resold it downriver."

The fire cracked. Theo stirred in his sleep and murmured something, and both of them went still until he settled again, compass clutched tight.

"That's how," Cassian finished. "That's all of it. The law didn't kill her. But the law stood there holding the cure while she froze, and then it sold the cure to the highest bidder. And every one of us who told her *just wait, just file again, just go through channels* — we handed her to that checkpoint one polite sentence at a time."

* * *

Rosalind was silent for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice had changed — flattened, stripped, the way it went when she talked about hull damage or dead crew. Inventory voice. Grief converted to tonnage.

"And the crates in my hold."

"Funded by Highmoor itself. Quietly. They've been saving for two years — every family, every wage, the schoolchildren giving up their festival money. They pooled it and sent it downriver with one instruction: buy the same shipment, the same manifest, and get it home before the passes close. No petitions. No offices. No pending review." He leaned forward. "They're not asking the law's permission this time, Roz. They're done waiting for it. The settlement that lost Wren is paying to finish what killed her."

"So I'm carrying stolen goods."

"I'm carrying purchased goods with falsified papers, which is a different crime with the same cell at the end of it. And you—" he pointed at her, firelight catching the scar on his knuckle, "—you're carrying them because I asked, and you haven't thrown me off your boat yet, which tells me your answer is already made and you're just angry that I know it."

"Don't flatter yourself."

"I never flatter myself. I do arithmetic." He counted on his fingers. "One: you took the job after seeing the orphanage return address, which means the wound mattered more than the grudge. Two: you haven't turned back since finding the boy, and the Kestrel burns fuel going nowhere. Three: you let Theo sleep below instead of putting him off at the next landing, and Rosalind Vane does not keep passengers she intends to abandon." He lowered his hand. "Justice outside the law is still justice. That's the whole argument. I don't have a better one. I don't think there is a better one. The law had its chance at Grayford and it spent it verifying paperwork."

"The law isn't wrong because it failed once."

"No. It's wrong here because it fails every time, in exactly this way, and calls the failure procedure." His voice roughened despite himself. "I've spent three years telling myself I'd have gone upriver if Wren had asked me directly. That's a lie I keep polished because it's easier than the truth, which is that she *did* ask, and I answered with a shipping schedule. You and I swore off smuggling after her funeral because we decided the trade was beneath us. It wasn't the trade that was beneath us, Roz. It was our excuses."

Rosalind stood. She walked to the nook and stood over Theo for a long moment, watching him breathe, watching the compass rise and fall against his small chest. Then she came back and sat down, and something in her posture had shifted — not softened, exactly. Settled. Like ballast moved to a new position.

"Here's what happens," she said. "I finish the run. The medicine reaches Highmoor if it costs the Kestrel her keel. That part was decided the moment I saw the manifest, and you knew it, which is why you're insufferable instead of nervous."

"I'm also nervous."

"But hear me clearly, Cassian." She leaned forward, and the firelight made her eyes look like river ice. "Finishing the run is not forgiveness. Wren is dead because everyone around her chose convenience over courage, and you and I were the closest ones. There is no cargo in the world that pays that debt. What I'm doing, I'm doing for her and for that boy — not for you. When this is over, you and I are still whatever we were before Port Merrow. Strangers with history."

Cassian nodded slowly. "Then we're agreed."

"We're agreed on the run. Nothing else."

"Nothing else," he echoed, and offered his hand across the fire.

She looked at it for a long moment. Then she took it — one hard shake, brief as a blade flash — and let go.

"Alliance," she said. "Not absolution."

"Noted for the record." He managed half a smile. "For what it's worth, I'd rather have your anger at my back than anyone else's loyalty."

"That's because you've never had my anger at your back. Give it time."

* * *

He climbed topside near midnight to spell Milo. The river lay black and glassy, the mountains ahead just a darker darkness against the sky, and somewhere up there was Highmoor, and the snows that were coming early this year — everyone said so, the trappers, the ferrymen, the old men on the docks.

Dree stood at the rail where she shouldn't have been, off her watch hour, a lantern shuttered low beside her. She slipped something into her coat pocket as he approached — too quick, too smooth, but not quick enough.

"What have you got there, quartermaster?"

"Nothing. Spare wick." She smiled at him, steady as a charted channel.

Cassian smiled back and said nothing at all, and behind them both, faint through the deck boards, he could hear the boy turning over in his sleep, the brass compass clicking softly against the planks like a small persistent heartbeat.

Three days to Grayford.
