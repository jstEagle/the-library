## 5. The Cutter's Wake

The fog burned off by midmorning, and what it left behind was trouble.

Rosalind saw it first as a smudge on the water astern — a gray shape where no gray shape should be, sitting low and heavy in the channel they'd held for three days. She had the glass up before Cassian finished his coffee. One look through the lens and her stomach dropped through the deck plates.

Customs cutter. Twin screws, black hull, the gold chevron of the Revenue Service painted fresh on her bow. And she was gaining.

"Everyone off the rail," Rosalind said quietly. "Now."

Cassian lowered his cup. "How far?"

"Four miles. Maybe five." She snapped the glass shut. "They didn't find us by accident, Cassian. Somebody on this boat told them exactly where to look."

His jaw tightened, but there was no time for that conversation. There would never be time for that conversation; that was how betrayals worked. They waited until you were committed, until turning back cost more than running forward.

"How fast can you lose them?" he asked.

Rosalind looked at the river ahead. The Kestrel was entering the last stretch of open water before the Sorrow Narrows — twelve miles of braided channels, shifting sandbars, and drowned timber that had sent better boats than hers to the bottom. Every pilot's chart marked it in red ink with a single word: *avoid*.

Her chart had annotations instead.

"I can't lose them," she said. "But I can make them wish they'd never followed me."

She rang for full steam and took the wheel herself.

* * *

The Narrows announced themselves the way grief does — gradually, then all at once. The wide brown river split into threads, silver ribbons winding between bars of pale sand, and the current turned treacherous, pushing one way on the surface and another below. Somewhere under all that water lay the bones of the *Marguerite*, lost in '09 with all hands, because her pilot trusted the charts.

Rosalind did not trust the charts. She trusted the color of the water, the lean of the reeds, the way a sandbar's wake curled left or right depending on which side the deep channel ran. Three years of running this river had written its secrets into her hands.

"Stand by the leads," she called. "Milo, sound for me. Call out anything under six feet."

Milo swung the lead line, chanting depths like a prayer. Behind them, the cutter's engines grew louder — she'd stopped pretending to be a merchantman and was coming on at full power, her wake a white scar across the water.

"They're signaling," Dree reported from the stern. Her voice was steady, but her knuckles were white on the rail. "Revenue pennant. Heave to or be fired upon."

"Noted," Rosalind said. "Tell them we're considering it."

The first bar came up fast — a long spine of sand just breaking the surface, guarded on either side by water that looked deep but wasn't. Rosalind had run this crossing eleven times. She'd grounded once, in her second year, and spent four hours knee-deep in the river hauling the Kestrel off by hand while Cassian stood on the bank and offered advice she still hadn't forgiven.

She took the crossing now at full speed.

It was counterintuitive and it was the only way: slow boats wallowed, caught the bar's suction, and sat down hard on the sand. Fast boats planed over the shallow patch before the river knew they were there. The Kestrel's hull sang — an actual note, high and strained — as she crossed the bar with inches to spare, and then the deep water of the next channel opened beneath her and the note resolved into the familiar thrum of the engine.

Behind them, the cutter slowed. Hesitated. A Revenue captain with a government hull and a government career did not cross an unmarked bar at speed.

"We've bought an hour," Cassian said.

"We've bought twenty minutes," Rosalind said. "Hold something."

* * *

Theo appeared at the wheelhouse door halfway through the second channel. He'd been ordered below — everyone had been ordered below — but there he was, barefoot, gripping the frame, watching the water with an expression Rosalind recognized because she wore it every day of her life. Not fear. Hunger.

"You should be below," she said.

"The mast is fouled." He pointed up. Sure enough, the jib halyard had wrapped itself around the forestay during the last hard turn, and the sail was flogging itself to death against the shrouds, throwing drag across the bow. "Somebody has to go up and cut it loose. Milo can't climb — he told me he gets sick past the crosstrees."

"Milo is forty years old and sensible."

"I'm not afraid of heights," Theo said simply, the way other children said they liked jam. "I never have been. Mama used to say I was part gull."

Rosalind looked at the rigging, then at the cutter astern — visible again now through the gap between islands, closing the distance the bar had cost her. Twenty minutes, maybe less. A fouled headsail was drag she couldn't afford.

"Rig him a line," she told Cassian. "Bosun's chair, safety hitch. If he slips, you catch him or I'll put *you* ashore."

Theo was up the ratlines before the chair was even ready, moving with a quickness that made her chest ache — hand over hand, feet finding holds without looking, small body swaying with the roll of the deck as if the motion were a language he'd been born speaking. He reached the crosstrees, hooked his leg through the shroud, and drew his knife.

"Left side!" he called down. "It's wound twice around!"

"Then unwind it twice!" Rosalind called back, and put the wheel over into the third channel.

What followed was the finest hour of piloting she could remember and the worst hour of her nerves, both at once. The Narrows narrowed. The channels bent like question marks. Twice she threaded gaps so tight the willows brushed both rails, and above her Theo worked the fouled rigging — cutting, hauling, cursing in a vocabulary he had clearly learned from sailors and not from any orphanage matron — while the Kestrel rolled and dove beneath him.

And the boy laughed.

She heard it clearly, once, carried down through the wind: a bright, unguarded whoop as the deck heeled and the mast swung and he hung against the sky with nothing under him but river. It was the laugh of a child who had decided, somewhere in the dark of the hold behind those barrels, that fear was a luxury he'd declined to purchase.

Something in Rosalind's chest, something that had been clenched for three years, loosened half a turn.

"Clear!" Theo shouted, and the jib filled with a crack like a gunshot, and the Kestrel surged forward with clean lines and a fair wind, and Rosalind put her nose dead down the deepest channel on the whole river and *ran*.

* * *

The cutter gained anyway. Of course she did. Government money buys good engines, and the Revenue captain had finally found his nerve, taking the bars at half speed where Rosalind took them at full, losing minutes but never stopping, a patient gray wolf on their wake.

By early afternoon they were down to their last trick, and it was the oldest one on the river.

"There." Cassian pointed to starboard, where the tree line broke and a green haze rose from the water — miles of reed marsh, a drowned forest of cane and cattail stretching to the horizon. No charts. No channels. No bottom worth naming.

"No one's taken a boat that size in there since my grandfather," Milo said.

"My grandfather never met this cutter," Rosalind said. "Dree — take the depth soundings at the mouth and mark them. Theo—" She caught herself. Old habit, ordering the boy below. She looked at him instead, standing at the rail with his mother's knife still in his hand, salt-damp hair plastered to his forehead, eyes bright as coins.

"Theo. Starboard watch. Call me the reed lines when the light changes."

He blinked. Then he grinned, and it transformed his whole face, and Rosalind understood that she had just done something irreversible.

"Aye, Captain," he said.

The marsh swallowed them at the falling tide. Rosalind took the Kestrel in on momentum alone, threading the deeper sloughs by the color of the water — dark copper meant channel, olive green meant mud, and the pale glassy patches meant sandbar, avoid, *avoid*. The reeds closed behind them like a door. Ahead, the cutter's engines thundered past the marsh mouth, huge and frustrated, hunting open water for a target that had vanished into the green.

They heard her anchor drop two miles downstream, patient as winter. They heard her launch boat put out, oars muffled, poking into the reeds and finding nothing but reeds.

Rosalind brought the Kestrel to rest in a pool of open water deep in the marsh, surrounded by ten thousand acres of cane, and let the engine die.

Silence rushed in — frogs, wind, the tick of the cooling boiler. Theo slid down from the foremast, landed on deck like a gull settling, and stood there catching his breath, flushed and triumphant.

"Well?" Cassian asked.

Rosalind leaned on the wheel and let out a breath she felt she'd been holding since Port Merrow. "We'll wait for dark. Then we move up the slough on the far side — there's a backwater that lets out above their anchorage. By morning we'll be thirty miles away and they'll still be fishing their launch out of the cattails."

"And the informant?" Cassian said quietly, too quietly for the boy to hear.

Rosalind glanced at Theo, laughing with Milo over the fouled halyard, showing him exactly where he'd cut it. Then she looked at Dree, standing at the stern rail, watching the marsh with an expression Rosalind had seen once before — on a woman counting crates at a checkpoint, deciding whether to lie.

"Tomorrow," Rosalind said. "Tonight, we're all crew."

Theo came aft and stood beside her at the wheel, close enough that she could feel the warmth radiating off him, smelling of tar and river wind. He didn't say thank you. Crew didn't.

"Captain," he said instead. "When we get to Highmoor — do you think Mama would have wanted to see it?"

Rosalind looked north, where the mountains were beginning to show themselves above the tree line, white-capped and indifferent, holding the settlement and the medicine and the end of every road she knew.

"I think," she said, "she's the reason we're going to find out."

Above the marsh, far off, a lantern moved along the cutter's deck — steady, patient, waiting. And somewhere aboard the Kestrel, Rosalind knew, a ledger with names in it was already open.
