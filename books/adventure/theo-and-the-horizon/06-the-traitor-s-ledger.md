## 6. The Traitor's Ledger

Milo found the ledger by accident, which was how he found most things worth finding.

He'd gone below to the hold for a spare lamp wick — the wheelhouse one had burned down to a stub, and Rosalind had been squinting at her charts through a halo of smoke for two days without complaint, because that was the kind of woman she was. The Kestrel's hold smelled of pitch and river water and, beneath it now, the faint medicinal bitterness of the crates. Milo knew every inch of that hold the way he knew his own pockets. Which was why he noticed, immediately, that Dree's sea chest had been moved.

Three inches. Maybe four.

Dree Voss did not move her sea chest. In eleven years aboard half the boats on this river, Milo had never once seen Dree's chest out of alignment with the deck seam — she squared it to the rivets like a woman squaring accounts, which, in fairness, was exactly what she was doing. Quartermasters were a particular breed. They counted everything: rope, salt, coin, favors. Especially favors.

Milo crouched beside it. He wasn't a snooper by nature; he was a small man who'd survived a hard childhood by being pleasant and unremarkable, and pleasant unremarkable men learned early not to open other people's boxes. But the chest sat wrong against the planking, tilted just enough that its shadow fell differently than it should have, and under the shadow, wedged between chest and hull, was a fold of oilcloth that didn't belong to anything.

He should have called for Rosalind right then. That was the correct sequence of events, and he could already hear the version of himself in some future retelling saying *I knew at once*. But his hands were already on the oilcloth, because curiosity is just fear wearing a braver coat, and Milo unwrapped it there in the lamplight of the empty hold.

Inside was a signal mirror, a shuttered lantern, and a strip of paper covered in Dree's neat, upright hand.

*Position as of dawn. Nine souls aboard incl. boy. Running Sorrow Narrows at first light if fog holds. Cargo intact. Cutter position unknown — request confirmation.*

Milo read it twice. The words didn't change. Below them, unsigned but unmistakably hers, was a tally — because of course Dree tallied even her betrayals — dates, distances, and a column marked simply *owed*, with a figure at the bottom that made his stomach drop: four hundred crowns. Bail money. Prison debt. And beside it, in smaller script, a name: *Tam.*

Dree's brother. The one she sent half her pay to, season after season, in letters Milo had carried to the Port Merrow post office without ever once wondering why a quartermaster with no family ashore wrote so faithfully.

The customs cutter hadn't found them by luck. It had found them by ledger.

* * *

He stood in the hold for a long moment, the paper trembling slightly in his fingers, listening to the boat around him. The Kestrel talked in her sleep — timbers ticking, water chuckling against the strakes, the mast groaning its low note. Above him, muffled through the deck, he could hear Theo laughing at something Cassian said, a bright scrap of sound that fell through the boards like light through a crack.

Nine souls aboard including the boy. She'd written it herself. Counted them like cargo.

Milo climbed the ladder slowly, folding the oilcloth into his jacket, and went looking for Rosalind.

He found her at the rail amidships, watching the reed marsh slide past in long green strokes. They'd made good distance after the Narrows — too good, maybe, because the cutter had fallen back and everyone aboard had started to believe they'd won something. Even Rosalind's shoulders had come down from around her ears. Milo hated what he was about to do to them.

"Captain," he said. "A word below."

She looked at him, and whatever she saw in his face made her push off the rail without a question. That was another thing about Rosalind Vane: she never wasted time asking whether bad news was bad.

* * *

They told Cassian after. Or rather, Rosalind told Cassian, standing over the chart table with the signal mirror between them like a dead bird on a plate, while Milo kept his eyes on the deck seams and wished himself anywhere else. Theo was up forward helping Dree re-coil lines — which, Milo thought grimly, meant the traitor currently had the boy alone, and every second of that thought made the words harder to say.

"You're certain it's hers." Cassian's voice was flat. Not angry yet. Anger came later with him, all at once, like a keg going.

"It's her hand," Milo said. "I've watched her write manifests for three years. Nobody else makes their sevens like that."

"And the tally?"

"Bail for her brother. Tam Voss. Customs prison at Greyford, near as I can tell. Four hundred crowns owed, and the cutter's people were offering remission for information." He swallowed. "She's been selling us since before we left Merrow. The Narrows run — she'd have known our heading before we made it."

Rosalind hadn't spoken. She stood with both hands flat on the chart table, staring down at the mirror as though it might reflect something useful. When she finally lifted her head, her face was the one Milo had only seen once before, three years ago, in a different life: absolutely still, absolutely tired, and underneath it something moving like deep water.

"Get Theo," she said quietly. "Bring him aft. Then bring Dree."

"Rosalind," Cassian started.

"I said get them."

* * *

It happened on the aft deck, in full view of the river, because Rosalind wanted witnesses — or maybe because she wanted the river itself to see it. Milo stood by the rail with Theo pressed close against his side, feeling the boy's heart going like a trapped bird, and watched Dree climb the ladder from below with a coiled line still over her shoulder and confusion turning, degree by degree, into understanding.

She saw the mirror on the deck between Rosalind's boots. Milo watched her take inventory of it — he could see her doing it, the quartermaster's reflex, cataloguing — and then watch her decide, visibly, not to lie.

"Well," Dree said.

"Well," said Rosalind.

Dree set down the coil of line. Squared it to the deck seam. Milo almost laughed at that, except nothing about this was funny, and then he realized she'd done it deliberately, a last act of order in a world coming apart, and he didn't laugh at all.

"How much did you see?" Dree asked Milo directly, and there was no apology in it. Just the question.

"Enough."

"The Narrows." She turned back to Rosalind. "I gave them your heading before you ever cleared Merrow. I want you to know that, because if you're going to hate me, hate me for the true amount. Not the rounded figure."

Theo made a small sound against Milo's ribs. Dree's eyes flicked to him — just once — and something crossed her face that Milo would spend a long time afterward trying to name. Shame, maybe. Or arithmetic. The look of a woman adding a new entry to a column she couldn't close.

"They have Tam," she said. Her voice stayed level, but her hands, at her sides, had curled into fists. "My brother. Nineteen years old. Caught running tar-wood over the border in spring — a boy's crime, a boy's sentence, except customs doesn't do boys' sentences anymore. Four hundred crowns bail, or he sits in Greyford until the thaw, and Greyford in winter—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "Greyford in winter kills one man in five. He's nineteen. He's all I have."

"So you sold us." Cassian's voice cracked across the deck like a whip. "You sold the boy. You sold all of us. For four hundred—"

"For my brother." For the first time her voice rose, and it rose all the way. "Yes. I sold you. I've been writing to that cutter's master since Port Merrow and every night I've lain in my bunk doing the sums and telling myself nobody would drown, nobody would hang, it's just fines and confiscation, it's just *cargo* — and then there was a child aboard, and I kept writing anyway, because Tam is also somebody's whole world and I am all he has, and I chose him." Her breath shuddered once and steadied. "I'd choose him again. That's what I need you to understand, Captain. Not that I'm sorry. That I'd choose him again."

The river ran. Somewhere ahead a marsh bird cried, twice, and went quiet.

Rosalind picked up the signal mirror. Turned it over in her hands. Milo had served under captains who shot informants, and captains who marooned them, and one captain in his younger days who'd made a man walk the shallows behind the boat for an afternoon as a lesson none of the crew forgot. He watched Rosalind weigh all of those options and discard them, one by one, the way she'd once described shedding ballast — deliberately, in deep water, where it could do no harm.

"Here's what I know," she said at last. "People break for love, not malice. Malice I can guard against. Love gets in everywhere, wears your own face, signs your own manifests." She looked at Dree. "You could have run at any port. You didn't. You stayed aboard a boat you were selling, and you slept ten feet from the boy you endangered, and you tallied your debt like a merchant because you needed the betrayal to *cost* you something. That's not a traitor's ledger, Dree. That's a penance one."

Dree's jaw worked. "That doesn't unwrite what I wrote."

"No. It doesn't." Rosalind stepped closer. "So here's how it gets unwritten. The cutter's master expects your next report. He'll get one — tonight, from my hand, in your hand, saying we've holed up in the marshes awaiting a fair wind, cargo intact, no rush. We give him a position forty miles from where we'll actually be. And when we cross the range and deliver this medicine, you will stand on that mountain with us, and every mile you bought with your brother's freedom, you'll spend buying back ours. All of it. To the last crown."

"That's it?" Cassian sounded incredulous. "You're keeping her? After—"

"After what?" Rosalind turned on him, and Milo saw three years fall off her and pile themselves onto him instead. "After she did the worst thing a person can do for the best reason there is? Where were you planning to send her, Cassian? To whom? We're nine souls on a stolen errand past the edge of the law. There's no clean place to put broken people. There's only here." She looked around the deck — at Cassian, at Milo, at Theo's white face against his coat — and her voice dropped to something almost gentle. "This is the crew now. Chosen, not hired. Fragile as eggshell and twice as liable to crack. But a family isn't the people who never fail you. It's the ones still aboard after they have."

Nobody spoke. The Kestrel ticked and groaned beneath them, talking in her sleep.

Dree bent slowly and picked up her line coil. Held it against her chest like a shield, or a child.

"What do you need me to write?" she asked hoarsely.

"Everything true," said Rosalind, "except where we are."

* * *

They wrote the false report together at the chart table that evening, Dree's neat sevens marching across the page while Rosalind dictated and Milo held the lamp. Theo watched from the doorway, silent, and Milo caught the boy watching Dree with an expression far too old for ten years old — the look of someone learning that adults break too, and that the breaking isn't always the end of them.

When it was done, Dree sealed it and handed it to Milo for the morning's post-runner exchange at the marsh trading post, and her hand shook only slightly, and only once.

Later, near midnight, Milo climbed topside to check the mooring lines and found Rosalind at the wheelhouse window, charts spread before her, tracing a route east with one finger — away from the river entirely, up into country where the Kestrel could not follow.

"We'll need mules," she said, without looking up. "And cold-weather gear. And a guide who knows the foothills tracks."

"Theo's mother was from Highmoor," Milo offered. "Boy might know the way better than any guide."

Rosalind was quiet a moment. On the horizon, far off, the mountains had come out from under the cloud cover for the first time in days — white-capped, enormous, indifferent.

"Aye," she said softly. "Then tomorrow we ask him. Because the river ends ahead, Milo, and everything that matters starts where it does."
