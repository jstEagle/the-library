## 7. Where the River Ends

The river told us before the soundings did.

For two days it had been narrowing, shedding its wide brown patience, gathering itself into white water and stone. By the third morning the Kestrel was threading a channel barely twice her beam, and I could read the bottom through six feet of clear mountain water — every rock a pale shoulder waiting to bruise her hull.

I stood at the wheel and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since Port Merrow. Forty miles of this. Then gravel bars, then boulders, then nothing.

"Say it," Cassian said beside me. He'd been watching my face instead of the river, which was either trust or foolishness.

"Not yet."

"Rosalind."

I eased us around a submerged shelf, close enough that Theo — perched on the rail with his mother's compass open in his palm — whooped at the green flash of weed below the keel. "Not yet," I said again.

But the river kept saying it for me. By noon the current had gone shallow and loud, the boat riding high and skittish, and when I finally brought her against the last deep pool above the rapids, the lead line came up reading four feet. Four feet, on a boat that drew five and a half.

I coiled the line slowly. Some things you know in your hands before you say them out loud.

* * *

We beached her at the mouth of a stony creek where the mule track came down to meet the water, just as the charts promised and the charts are always half a lie. The Kestrel settled into the shallows with a sigh, listing gently against the bank like a tired animal leaning on a fence.

Nobody spoke while we unloaded. That's the thing about sailors — we grieve with our backs. Crates of medicine went onto the pack mules Dree had arranged to meet us there, three shaggy beasts with opinions about everything. Spare lines, oilskins, hard bread, the small iron stove Milo insisted on hauling because, he said, cold men make bad decisions. The Kestrel gave it all up without complaint.

Last of all I went below alone.

Her cabin smelled of tar and river and three years of me. I ran my hand along the bulkhead where Wren's name was still carved under a coat of varnish — *W.V., first mate, best navigator on this water* — and I pressed my thumb into the letters until they hurt.

"You carried me far enough," I told her. "Now carry them."

Then I closed the hatch and didn't look back, because looking back is how you drown.

* * *

The track climbed through foothills the color of old bronze, scrub oak and scree, the river shrinking below us to a silver thread that looked like something you could snap with your fingers. We made good time for an hour. Maybe two.

Then Theo stopped.

He didn't announce it. He just slowed, the way boys do when they're ashamed, letting the mules pass him, letting the distance between him and the rest of us stretch until he was walking alone with his head down. I watched him try three times to lengthen his stride and fail. His face had gone the gray of wet ash.

Cassian glanced back at me. I shook my head. This one was mine.

I fell in beside the boy. Up close I could see him counting his own breaths, jaw set, furious at his body for being ten years old.

"I'm slowing everyone down," he said, before I could invent a kind lie. "My legs are too short. You should have left me with the boat."

"The boat's done, Theo. She can't go any farther than you can."

"That's not what I mean." He kicked a stone off the track, hard, and missed. "You should've left me behind. Back at the narrows. Or before. I'm cargo that talks."

I let us walk twenty paces in silence. The wind had picked up, carrying a smell I didn't like — that clean, mineral emptiness that means snow somewhere above the tree line, snow that has no business falling yet.

"When I was your age," I said, "I couldn't swim."

He looked up, suspicious. Grief makes liars of children; they think every story is a trick to make them feel better.

"It's true. Grew up on the water, terrified of it. My father used to say I'd learned to fear the wrong element." I stepped over a fallen branch and offered him my hand for the slick part of the trail. He took it, which was progress. "Wren taught me. Your mother. Did you know that?"

"She said she worked rivers." His voice had gone careful.

"She did more than work them. She owned them. First woman to run the Sorrow Narrows alone, and she did it in fog, with a busted rudder, because there were people downstream who needed what she carried." I laughed, short and surprised at myself. "I was her deckhand then. Seventeen, useless, scared of drowning. And she stood behind me at the wheel one night — I still remember her boots squeaking on the boards — and she said, *Rosie, the river doesn't care how big you are. It only cares whether you keep reading it.*"

Theo walked a few steps, turning this over. "Is that why you're good?"

"That's why I'm good. And here's the other thing, the thing nobody tells you: she wasn't fast, that day in the narrows. She was *right*. Slow and right beats fast and wrong every mile of your life." I stopped and turned to face him, and I made myself say the true thing, because he deserved the truth and so did I. "Your mother died because the law confiscated her medicine at a checkpoint and men in clean uniforms wrote it down in a ledger and called it order. Not because she was weak. Not because she was slow. Because the world is sometimes wrong, and she was standing in front of it."

His eyes had filled but he hadn't cried, holding it the way he held everything, tight and furious. "Did you love her?"

"Like a sister. Like the sister I chose." My throat closed around it. "And when she died, I got so angry I swore off the only life that ever fit me. Three years I measured my guilt in miles, Theo. Miles downriver, miles from anything that reminded me. And then a boy stowed away on my boat with her compass, and every mile since has been taking me back toward her instead of away." I crouched to his level, snow-smell thickening in the air. "So no. I will not leave you behind. You're not cargo. You're the reason any of this is worth carrying."

A tear got loose then, just one, and he wiped it away with his sleeve like it had betrayed him too. "Okay," he said, gruff as an old man.

"Okay," I agreed. "Now walk slow and right. We've got a pass to beat."

* * *

The snow started an hour later, fat and silent, wrong for the season, wrong for the month. Within twenty minutes the track had gone soft underfoot and the world had shrunk to a gray-white room with the five of us in it — me, Cassian, Theo, Milo hunched under the stove-straps, Dree leading the mules with her collar up and her eyes doing arithmetic of her own.

We camped that night in the lee of a granite shoulder, stove lit, mules tethered, and I sat awake long after the others slept, watching snow bury the trail behind us.

Because here is what I knew and no one else did: the passes ahead were already closing. Main pass would go first, then the low routes, and Mercy Pass — if the old stories held — would hold maybe another two days. We had medicine for two hundred people and a window measured in hours.

Cassian stirred across the fire. He didn't ask what I was thinking. He just looked at the snow, then at me, and said quietly:

"Mercy Pass?"

"Mercy Pass," I said.

He nodded once, and I saw him swallow whatever memory lived behind that name, and throw another log on the fire.

Above us, the mountain waited, white and patient, keeping its own ledger.
