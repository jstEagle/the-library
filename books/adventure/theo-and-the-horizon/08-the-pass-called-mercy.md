## 8. The Pass Called Mercy

Cassian Marsh had made a career out of knowing which doors to knock on and which to leave shut. He'd spent three days knocking on the wrong one.

The main pass — the Cartographer's Gate, the maps called it, all confidence and clean lines — was gone. They stood at its mouth in the gray of morning and looked at what the mountain had done overnight: a fan of snow and shattered granite two hundred feet high, still settling, still breathing small avalanches off its shoulders with soft, contemptuous sighs. Somewhere under it was the road. Somewhere under it were the switchbacks, the way-station, the whole tidy idea that a person could simply walk from the river to Highmoor if they were stubborn enough.

Rosalind said nothing for a long time. She had a way of letting silence do her arguing, and Cassian had learned, over thirty years of knowing her, that the silence usually won.

"It's not clearing," Dree said at last. She'd climbed a spur to get a better look, and came back down with snow in her collar and her jaw set like she was chewing on bad news. "The whole face above the gate is loaded. It'll come down again before noon. Anyone on that road when it does—"

"Anyone on that road when it does is a story," Rosalind finished. "Not a person."

Theo stood at the edge of the debris field with his hands jammed in his pockets, staring up at the mountain as if he could shame it into moving. Three days on the mule track had burned the softness off him; his cheeks were raw, his lips cracked, but his eyes hadn't changed. That was the thing about the boy that unsettled Cassian most. Wren had looked at obstacles exactly that way. Like they owed her an apology.

"There's another way," Cassian said.

Everyone turned. He hadn't meant to say it. It had been sitting in his chest since dawn, since he'd first seen the gate choked shut, and he'd been hoping — genuinely hoping — that the mountain would spare him from having to say it aloud.

"You know that," Rosalind said quietly. Not a question. She knew him too well for questions.

"I swore I'd never take anyone over it." He heard his own voice go flat, the way it did when he was handling something fragile. "I swore it to myself, standing on the far side with my hands shaking so badly I couldn't light a pipe."

"Theo," Rosalind said, without turning, "go check on the mules."

"He's twelve now, Roz. He can hear hard things."

"He's ten," she said, "and he can hear them later. Go on, lad. The gray one's favoring her left foot. See to it."

Theo went. He went slowly, and he looked back once, and Cassian understood that the boy would hear every word of this eventually anyway — through the mule-hide walls of the tent, through the way adults' voices carried when they thought they were being careful. Children always collected the debts adults tried to hide from them. Wren had taught him that too, without meaning to.

* * *

Mercy Pass wasn't on any map worth trusting. It existed because a century ago some desperate miners had decided that a vertical mile of cliff on the mountain's western shoulder was preferable to starving, and they'd hammered iron stakes into the rock and strung rope ladders and catwalks between them, and then most of them had died anyway, and the ones who lived named it after whatever they'd found on the other side.

Cassian had crossed it once, eleven years ago, running a debt north ahead of a man who intended to collect it in installments of bone. He'd gone over alone, in summer, with good rope and steady weather, and it had still taken him nine hours and cost him something he'd never gotten back. Halfway along there was a section where the cliff leaned out over nothing and the path was a plank-and-rope bridge no wider than a coffin lid, and the wind came up the valley in long, patient gusts that seemed to be testing your grip the way a thief tests a lock. He had crossed that section on his hands and knees, weeping without any sound at all, and when he reached the far side he'd sat down against a boulder and sworn — out loud, to the mountain, to whatever was listening — never again.

Now here he was, drawing it in the dirt with a stick while Rosalind, Dree, Milo, and four exhausted mules watched.

"Three sections," he said. "The Stairs, which are just stairs, bad ones. The Traverse, which is the part you've heard about if you've heard anything. And the Chimney, a crack in the rock that goes up and up and spits you out at the top. Total crossing, loaded, in the dark — eight hours if nothing goes wrong."

"And if something goes wrong?" Milo asked. He was twenty-two and honest enough to ask.

"Then it takes us," Cassian said. "All of us. There's nowhere on that route to leave someone behind gently."

Dree surprised him. She'd been quiet since the river, quiet since Rosalind had chosen to bind her instead of maroon her — a choice Cassian still didn't fully understand, though he was beginning to suspect it was the only kind of choice Rosalind Vane ever made: the expensive one. Now Dree stepped forward and unrolled a strip of oilcloth from inside her coat. On it, in her cramped quartermaster's hand, was a schedule.

"Customs rotates their high patrols," she said. "I logged their rotations for two years before — before everything. The western shoulder sweep passes below Mercy Pass at dusk and again at first light. Between those sweeps, the route is blind for eleven hours. If we're on the cliff when they come through, they don't just arrest us. They log the route as active again, and Highmoor loses its road forever."

"So we cross in the dark," Rosalind said.

"In winter," Cassian added. "In the dark, in winter, carrying crates."

Nobody spoke. The wind moved through the dead pines around them, and somewhere above, the mountain shed another small white breath off its shoulder.

"We could turn back," Milo offered, to nobody in particular.

"No," Theo said.

They all turned. The boy had come back from the mules without anyone noticing — of course he had — and he stood there with snow melting in his dark hair and his mother's compass in his fist, and his voice didn't shake at all.

"She waited," Theo said. "She waited three weeks for medicine that was sitting in a customs warehouse forty miles away. I know what waiting costs. We're not turning back."

Cassian looked at Rosalind. Rosalind looked at the boy for a long moment, and then at the mountain, and then at Cassian, and something passed between the two of them that had no words in it — thirty years of rivalry and one shared grave and every mile either of them had ever measured.

"First light tomorrow minus eleven hours," she said. "We move at dusk."

* * *

The Stairs were worse than stairs and better than he remembered, which was to say they were terrible and survivable. Ice glazed the treads. They roped together in pairs, mules led singly with their loads broken down into smaller packs, and climbed by lantern-light with the valley dropping away behind them into a blackness so complete it felt structural, like the dark was a place rather than an absence.

Theo climbed like he'd been born to it. That shouldn't have surprised Cassian — Wren had been a dock-rat, half goat by temperament — but watching the boy move up the frozen steps, sure-footed and silent, did something strange to his chest. Fear, he realized. Not for himself. For the first time in eleven years, Cassian Marsh was afraid for someone other than Cassian Marsh, and the feeling was so much worse than self-preservation that he almost laughed.

At the top of the Stairs they rested against a shelf of rock, and Dree checked her watch against the stars, and announced they were on schedule, and everyone pretended that was comforting.

* * *

The Traverse began at midnight.

It was worse than he remembered. Memory had sanded it down, the way memory does — it had kept the shape of the fear and lost the texture. The texture was this: a plank walkway bolted to a cliff that leaned out over a drop so deep the bottom was a rumor, ropes gone gray and bristled with frost, wind coming up the valley in gusts that arrived like appointments. Below them, faintly, the river they'd abandoned roared in its gorge, a sound like the world clearing its throat.

"One at a time," Cassian said. "Hands on the guide rope. Don't look down. Don't stop in the middle. If the wind hits, freeze and hold. It passes. Everything passes."

He went first. Of course he went first. Some debts you pay in money and some you pay in position, and he'd spent eleven years knowing this one would come due in position.

He was halfway across the worst span — the coffin-lid bridge, the one that had broken him a decade ago — when he heard it.

A crack. Small. Above and behind.

Then Theo's voice, very young and very clear: "The rope—"

Cassian turned. The boy was fifteen feet back, on the narrow ledge where the bridge met the rock, and the anchor stake above him — an original iron pin, a century old, older than any law or promise on this mountain — was pulling free of the stone with a grinding whisper of grit. The guide rope sagged. Theo grabbed for the cliff itself, fingers scrabbling on frost-slick granite, and the stake came out all at once, and the rope whipped away into the dark like something alive and offended, and Theo went with it.

Later, Cassian would not be able to reconstruct the next three seconds. There was no sequence of decisions in them, no thought, none of the cold arithmetic he was famous for. There was only his body doing the one thing it had apparently been waiting eleven years to do: lunging back across the planks, flat-out, arms out, catching a fistful of the boy's coat as the child swung out over the gorge like a bell struck too hard.

The momentum took them both. The old planks groaned. One snapped. Cassian's boots skated on frost and suddenly the drop was everywhere, roaring below him, and his right hand held Theo's coat and his left hand held nothing, and he felt — with perfect, lucid clarity — the exact moment the mountain asked him the question it had been saving for him.

*You let go,* the mountain said, *or you fall.*

Eleven years ago he'd crossed this cliff alone, for himself, running from a debt. Now he hung over the same void with a ten-year-old's weight tearing his shoulder from its socket, and the arithmetic was different, and the answer was different, and it wasn't arithmetic at all.

"I've got you," Cassian said. His voice sounded calm. He would remember being amazed by that. "Theo. Look at me. I've got you."

Boots thundered on the planks behind him — Rosalind, Dree, rope uncoiling, Milo shouting — and then a loop settled over Cassian's left wrist, cinched tight, and the load halved, and then halved again, and hands hauled them both back onto the ledge and into a pile of shaking bodies and frost and breath.

For a moment nobody spoke. The wind gusted, tested their grip, found it holding, and moved on.

Then Theo, pressed against Cassian's chest, said into his coat, muffled and furious and alive: "You caught me."

"Somebody had to," Cassian said. His voice finally broke on it. "Your mother used to say I was good for exactly one thing per decade. I'm ahead of schedule."

Above them, faint and far, a lantern swung on the far side of the Traverse — and Dree, checking her watch with numb fingers, went very still.

"That's not our light," she said. "Patrols don't carry storm lanterns with blue glass."

Cassian followed her gaze across the abyss, to where the blue flame bobbed closer along the cliff path they themselves had to take — and understood, with a sinking that had nothing to do with heights, that the eleven-hour window had just closed early.
