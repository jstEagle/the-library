## 9. Highmoor

Theo smelled the settlement before he saw it — woodsmoke and something sour underneath, like milk gone wrong. Fever. He'd learned the smell of sickness on the Kestrel's last run with his mother, when half the crew went down with river ague and she'd dosed them all from a tin box she kept locked.

"Almost there," Rosalind said, but her voice had gravel in it, and Theo knew what almost meant on this mountain. It meant another hour. Maybe two. His feet had stopped feeling like feet somewhere behind the last ridge, and Cassian had been carrying him piggyback for a while now without either of them mentioning it.

The snow came sideways, fine as salt. Ahead, through the white, shapes: rooftops sagging under drifts, a watchtower with no light in it, a palisade gate standing open because nobody had the strength to close it.

A woman met them in the gate with a lantern that guttered in the wind. She looked at the four of them — Rosalind upright by sheer stubbornness, Cassian with Theo on his back, Dree gray-lipped and swaying, Milo half-dragging his mule — and her face did something complicated.

"You're the river people," she said. "Wren's boy?"

Theo slid off Cassian's back before he could think about whether his legs would hold. They mostly did.

"I'm Theo," he said. "We brought the medicine."

* * *

They came down into Highmoor like ghosts walking out of a storm, and the settlement gathered around them the way cold hands gather around a fire.

The doctor was named Sef. He was younger than Theo expected, with burned-looking eyes and hands that shook until he touched a patient, and then they didn't shake at all. He took one look at Dree's frostbitten fingers and one look at Rosalind's face and said, "Tell me you have it."

"Forty crates," Rosalind said. "Quinine, willow bark, fever tinctures, lungwort. Everything on Wren's old list." Her voice caught on the name, just slightly, and Theo watched her push past it. "It's on the mules. Get your strongest people."

Sef closed his eyes for one second — Theo counted it, one full second, like a man taking a breath before diving — and then he was moving, shouting names, and Highmoor woke up around them. Doors opened. Lanterns swung. People who could barely stand came out to haul crates through the snow, and Theo stood in the middle of it holding his mother's compass and understanding, all at once, why she had written the letters she wrote. Why she'd kept the list folded inside her coat until the paper wore soft as cloth.

She'd known exactly who was waiting up here.

* * *

They put Theo in the doctor's house, which was warm, which felt obscene given everything outside it. A girl about his age fed him broth and wouldn't tell him her name because, she said solemnly, names were bad luck during fever season. He decided he liked her.

Through the window he watched the settlement work. Sef moved from house to house like a man putting out fires only he could see. Rosalind stood in the snow directing the unloading even though her hands were wrapped in rags where the frostbite had split the skin, and every time someone tried to make her come inside she pointed at another crate instead.

Cassian found him at the window.

"You should sleep," Cassian said.

"So should you."

"I will. After." He lowered himself onto the bench beside Theo, groaning like an old man, which made Theo feel briefly excellent. For a while they just watched the snow and the lanterns.

"She talked about this place," Theo said. "My mother. Not much. But I knew there was a mountain, and there were people she owed." He turned the compass over in his hands. The brass was warm from his pocket. "I thought maybe she meant money. But it wasn't money, was it."

"No," Cassian said quietly. "It wasn't money."

"The medicine they took at the checkpoint. That was hers. She paid for it with the last of everything we had, and they took it, and she—" His throat closed. He made himself finish. "She died waiting for something that was sitting in a customs warehouse the whole time. Forty miles away. Maybe less."

"Yes."

"And now it's here."

"Now it's here," Cassian said. "Because of you. You know that, don't you? We'd have turned back at the Narrows if you hadn't gone up the mast. We'd be in a customs hold if Dree hadn't—"

"Dree sold us out."

"And then spent three days hauling crates through a blizzard to fix it." Cassian shrugged. "People are more than their worst hour. Your mother believed that harder than anyone I ever knew."

Theo looked down at the compass. The needle swung, restless, finding north and losing it and finding it again.

"I want to give it to Sef," he said. "The compass. She'd have wanted the doctor to have it. She always said a good navigator is worth more than a good map, and he's navigating something worse than a river."

Cassian was quiet a moment. When Theo glanced over, the man's eyes were wet, and he pretended very hard not to notice, because that was the kind thing.

"He'll keep it better than any of us," Cassian said. "Go on, then. Before you talk yourself out of it."

* * *

Sef was in the storehouse, cataloging crates by lamplight, writing numbers in a ledger with a stub of pencil. Up close Theo could see how thin he was. How long it had been since he'd slept.

Theo held out the compass.

Sef looked at it. Then at him. "What's this?"

"It belonged to my mother. Wren." Saying her name out loud in this place felt like setting something down that he'd been carrying up the whole mountain. "She bought medicine for Highmoor once. Customs took it at the checkpoint and she never got it back, and she—" He stopped. Started again. "This is hers. She used it to find her way everywhere. I want the doctor here to have it. So she finally gets to help this place find its way too. Or whatever. I'm ten, I don't know how to say it right."

Sef took the compass in both hands like it might break, or like he might.

"She said that?" he asked. "About navigation?"

"She said it all the time."

Sef laughed — a cracked, exhausted sound, half sob. "Wren Okonkwo told me that exact thing eleven years ago, when I was a first-year medic too scared to take this post. She sat where you're standing and said, *Doctor, a good navigator beats a good map every time.*" He pressed the compass to his chest. "I've been navigating by her ever since. I just didn't know she was coming back."

"She isn't," Theo said. "But the medicine is. And me."

"That's more than enough." Sef crouched so they were eye to eye. "Listen to me, Theo. What your mother started, you finished. In my ledger, in this settlement's memory, that makes you Highmoor's own. Whatever happens next — wherever that boat of yours takes you — you will always have a roof here. Do you understand?"

Theo nodded, because his voice had gone missing again.

Outside, the wind dropped all at once, the way it does, and in the sudden quiet they both heard it: the bell in the watchtower, ringing fast and hard.

Riders on the ridge. Customs blue.

* * *

By the time Theo got to the gate, everyone else was already there. Rosalind stood at the front with her arms crossed and her frostbitten hands hidden in her sleeves, wearing the face Theo privately called her wheelhouse face — nothing getting through, nothing moving her. Beside her, Cassian had gone very still, and Dree had gone very pale, and Milo had positioned himself, not subtly, between Dree and the riders.

There were six of them, customs officers on shaggy mountain ponies, led by a broad woman with a gray braid and a warrant tube slung across her back. Officer's insignia. Senior.

"Captain Vane," the officer said, dismounting. "Rosalind Vane, master of the *Kestrel*, out of Port Merrow. Am I right?"

"You're right," Rosalind said.

"We've been chasing your cargo for nine days. Contraband pharmaceuticals, no manifest, no license, moved through three restricted zones." The officer's breath steamed. "I have authority to seize the goods and detain every soul involved. Including the child."

Theo felt Cassian's hand settle on his shoulder. Heavy. Steady.

"Before you do," Rosalind said, level as still water, "walk with me. Five minutes. Then arrest whoever you like."

Something passed between the two women — some old recognition, some shared language of people who had worked the same rivers. The officer hesitated. Then she nodded once and followed Rosalind toward the storehouse.

Theo, being ten and having survived smugglers, avalanches, and a customs chase, saw absolutely no reason to obey the obvious instruction to stay put. He followed along the palisade shadow, close enough to hear.

Rosalind pushed open the storehouse door. Lamplight spilled out. Inside, rows of crates stood open, and the settlement's sick lay on pallets between them because there was nowhere else — twenty people at least, children among them, sweating and shivering while volunteers measured out the first doses of the medicine that had arrived hours ahead of the fever's worst wave.

The officer stood in the doorway a long time.

A small girl coughed in her sleep. A volunteer wiped her forehead with a cloth. Another carried a cup of willow-bark tea past the door, steam rising off it, and paused to nod at the officer — nodded at her, Theo saw, the way you nod at a neighbor.

"The law says these crates are contraband," Rosalind said quietly. "The law also confiscated the first shipment of this exact medicine and let a woman die waiting for it. I was there. So was he." She tipped her head toward the shadows where Theo stood, and did not pretend otherwise. "You can seize it tonight and carry forty crates of hope back down the mountain to sit in a warehouse. Or you can write in your report that the cargo was lost in the high passes. Nobody's fault. Weather does that."

The officer didn't answer for a long moment. Snow ticked softly against the roof.

"My own daughter had river fever two winters ago," she said at last. "There was no quinine within eighty miles. I watched four children in our own station town buried that spring." She pulled off one glove, flexed her fingers against the cold, and Theo understood that she was deciding something, and that it was costing her. "Where did you cross?"

"Sorrow Narrows, then Mercy Pass," Rosalind said. "You won't find tracks. The snow's taken them."

"The snow's taken them," the officer repeated, like a line she was learning. She turned back toward the storehouse, toward the pallets and the lamplight and the coughing children, and her shoulders came down from around her ears. "Log it as lost in transit. All forty crates. Cause: severe weather, high passes, presumed destroyed." She looked at Rosalind, and something almost like a smile crossed her weathered face. "And Captain — if I ever run cargo of my own, I know who to ask."

She walked back to her riders and gave the order in a voice pitched to carry: nothing up here but weather and ruin, turn the ponies around.

Theo stepped out of the shadows. Rosalind looked down at him, and for once she didn't tell him off for eavesdropping. She just put her bandaged hand on top of his head, briefly, like sealing a hatch.

"Come on," she said. "You heard the officer. There's nothing up here but weather."

Behind them, in the storehouse, Sef opened the next crate, and the needle of a brass compass glowed steady under the lamplight, pointing the way home.
