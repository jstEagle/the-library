## 10. Measuring in Years Again

The river smelled different in spring.

Rosalind stood at the wheelhouse rail of the *Kestrel* and breathed it in — mud and thaw and green things pushing up through the banks, the cold iron tang of snowmelt running fast off the range. Three years she had measured her life in miles of this water, mile by mile, as if distance were a debt she could pay down. She had never once thought to measure it in anything else.

She was measuring it differently now.

The boat beneath her feet was not the same boat that had gone into the shallows at the river's end last autumn. They had left her there on the stones, stripped of everything that could be carried, hull holed where the current had worked her against the gravel bar all winter. Rosalind had grieved her the way you grieve a person — quietly, at a distance, without letting anyone see. Then the settlement's carpenters, men whose children were alive because of what the *Kestrel* had carried up the mountain, had come down with the thaw asking what they could do. What they did was three months of work: new planking from Highmoor pine, a rebuilt stern, a wheelhouse roof patched with copper Dree had traded for in Port Merrow at a price Rosalind chose not to ask about.

She ran her hand along the rail. The wood was pale where the new met the old, a seam like a scar that hadn't learned yet how to be invisible.

"Captain." Milo came up the companionway two steps at a time, sixteen now and suddenly too tall for his own opinions, a coil of line over his shoulder. "Lines are cast off when you say. Tide turns in twenty minutes."

"Then we turn with it."

He grinned and went. Below, she could hear Dree's voice running through the cargo manifest — legitimate freight this time, timber and salt and bolts of cloth, though Rosalind had learned over the winter that "legitimate" was a word best held loosely, like a rope you might need to let go of quickly. Dree's brother was out of the customs prison now, working the Merrow docks, and Dree wore her freedom like a coat that still didn't quite fit. But she wore it. And every stitch of thread on this boat that wasn't rope or sail had passed through her hands.

Including, Rosalind saw when she turned, the small square of canvas pinned to the boy standing at the bow rail.

* * *

Theo had grown half a head since the pass. It seemed unfair, the way children did that — as if the mountain had taken something from him and the river had given it back with interest. He still carried his mother's brass compass on a cord around his neck, though he no longer clutched it. He just wore it, the way you wear your own heartbeat.

The badge at his collar was stitched in red thread on white canvas: a kestrel in flight, wings spread, and beneath it the word *CREW* in Dree's stubborn, slanted hand. He'd been wearing it for six days and had not once let it out of his sight long enough to wash, which meant it was beginning to smell like a boy who'd been wearing a badge for six days.

Rosalind came down from the wheelhouse and stood beside him at the rail. For a while neither of them said anything. That was one of the things she loved about him — he knew when the river wanted silence.

"You're steering us out," she said finally.

His whole face changed. "Me?"

"Tide's turning. Milo's got the lines. I've got the wheel until we clear the mouth, and after that—" she pulled the worn brass key from her pocket and held it out, "—after that she's yours."

He took the key the way he'd once taken the compass from his mother's things: carefully, like it might break, like it might not be real. Then he looked up at her, and behind the excitement there was the other thing, the thing that never fully left his eyes anymore, the question he'd stopped asking out loud somewhere over Mercy Pass.

"She would have liked this," he said. Not a question. A bearing.

"She would have hated it," Rosalind said. "She'd have said I was letting a child run my boat." She smiled. "And then she'd have stood right next to you the whole way, pretending she wasn't proud."

Theo laughed, and the sound went through her like warm water through cold pipe. She put her hand briefly on his shoulder — he was getting too big for even that — and climbed back to the wheelhouse.

* * *

They cleared the river mouth at slack water, the *Kestrel* leaning into the first honest current she'd felt since autumn, and the world opened.

Rosalind had forgotten — no, she corrected herself, standing at the wheel with the wind coming off the open water — she had *told herself* she'd forgotten what the sea looked like from a helm you trusted. For three years every horizon had been a wall. Every mile of river had been a corridor she was walking down away from something, or toward something she couldn't name, and the sky at the end of it had always looked less like an invitation than an invoice.

Now she looked out at the blue edge of the world and felt nothing but the wanting to go there.

Cassian found her there an hour later. He came up the companionway without knocking, because he was Cassian Marsh and knocking was a language he'd never learned, and leaned on the rail beside her with the easy insolence of a man who had nearly died on a cliff face and come down the other side of it a different person who was doing his very best to hide it.

"Theo's at the wheel," he said.

"Theo's at the wheel."

"He's holding her two points off the channel."

"He's holding her two points off the channel because the current sets east past the shoal and I taught him that yesterday."

Cassian grunted, which from him was applause. They watched the boy steer for a while. The morning light came gold across the water and caught the brass compass at his throat and made it flash like a signal fire.

"You know," Cassian said, "I've been thinking."

"That's new."

"I'm serious, Roz." He turned, and under the insolence there was something older and more careful. "Highmoor's paid. The debt's settled — Wren's debt, mine, yours, all of it. There's nothing left to run from. So I've been thinking about what a man does with a boat and no debts." He paused, and she watched him assemble the joke he was going to hide behind, and then watch him decide, barely, not to use it. "I've got a boat. You've got a crew. Mine's faster. Yours is better. Seems to me the arithmetic only works one way."

Rosalind kept her eyes on the horizon. "Are you asking to join my crew, Cassian?"

"I'm asking whether there's room for a rival aboard."

"There's room," she said. "There's always been room. That was never the problem."

"No," he agreed quietly. "It wasn't."

The problem had been a woman named Wren and a checkpoint and a crate of medicine sitting in a customs shed while a fever burned through a mountain settlement, and two smugglers who each blamed the other, and themselves, and the law, and the river, and God, until the blaming wore grooves so deep they couldn't walk anywhere else. The problem had been three years of measuring in miles.

But the problem, she understood now, watching Theo's hands steady on the wheel, had been solved. Not by justice — the officer at Highmoor had logged their crates as lost to the weather, which was mercy dressed as paperwork, and mercy was not justice, but it was close enough kin that Rosalind could live beside it. Not by forgiveness either, not entirely; some mornings she still woke with the old anger in her chest like ballast. But she'd learned something on the mule track through the foothills, telling Theo the truth about his mother with the snow coming down: grief you carry alone is cargo. Grief you share is crew.

"Conditions of employment," she said.

Cassian straightened. "Go on."

"One: you take orders. My boat, my wheel, my word."

"Agreed."

"Two: you tell me who paid for those crates. You never said. I never asked. That account closes today."

A flicker crossed his face — the old secrecy, the habit of a lifetime — and then he let it go like a line paying out. "The settlement itself. Pooled coin, every family in Highmoor, saved over two years. Wren's doctor organized it. They wanted the medicine bought back from the same law that stole it, delivered by the same people who'd have brought it the first time if the law hadn't stood in the way." He shrugged. "I was just the broker. They asked for you by name, Roz. I was the one who had to convince you."

"They asked for me by name."

"Wren told them about you. Before the end." His voice went rough. "Said if anything ever needed carrying, there was a captain on the river who'd get it there if it killed her. Said it like a complaint." He almost smiled. "Said it like a prayer."

Rosalind looked at the horizon for a long moment. The wind had freshened and the *Kestrel* was making good way, the new pine singing faintly in her hull, and at the wheel a ten-year-old boy — eleven now, she supposed, time moved whether you measured it or not — held a dead woman's compass and steered a living ship toward open water.

"Three," she said. "You eat last at the galley table. Crew eats first. Even rivals."

"Now that," said Cassian Marsh, "is tyranny."

"It's my boat."

* * *

At noon they raised the deep-water marks, and Rosalind called Theo up from the wheel and gave the helm to Cassian — a test inside a joke inside a peace treaty — and stood at the bow with the boy while the land fell away behind them.

"Do you feel it?" Theo asked. He had his hand flat on the rail like he was taking the boat's pulse.

"Feel what?"

"Like it's starting." He frowned, working at it. "Not like before. Before it always felt like — like we were getting away with something. Even the good parts." He looked up at her. "This doesn't feel like that."

Rosalind thought about the seam in the rail where the new wood met the old. She thought about a badge stitched in red thread, and a compass given to a doctor, and a customs log reading *lost to weather*, and a man at her wheel eating last tonight and laughing about it. She thought about three years of miles, and set them down the way you set down ballast you no longer need — not thrown overboard, just stowed, part of what kept her steady, no longer all that kept her moving.

"Yeah," she said. "It feels like a beginning."

Ahead, the horizon ran clean and unbroken from one edge of the world to the other, and for the first time in longer than she could count, Rosalind Vane was not running from it, or toward it, or away from anything at all.

She was sailing into it.

"All hands," she called, and heard her own voice come back bright off the water. "Stand by to make sail. We're for the open sea — and after that, wherever the wind says."

Behind her, four voices answered as one, and the *Kestrel* ran on.
