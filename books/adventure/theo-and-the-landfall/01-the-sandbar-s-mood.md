## 1. The Sandbar's Mood

The water told her first.

Paloma Dunmore had been reading rivers and coastlines for thirty-one years, and she had learned long ago that the sea never lied outright — it only spoke in a language most people were too impatient to learn. This morning, off the drowned shoulder of the delta where the charts went vague and the channel markers had rotted to stubs, the water was speaking in a dialect she didn't trust. The color was wrong. Not the honest brown-green of a river carrying its silt to market, but something milkier, paler, stirred from below. Sand in suspension. Sand that had been sleeping on the bottom until something — a tide, a storm two hundred miles out at sea, the mood of the world itself — had decided to wake it up and move it.

"Shoaling," she said aloud, though there was no one on the wheelhouse deck to hear her except the wheel itself, worn smooth as a communion rail under her palms.

Below her feet, in the engine room that smelled of diesel and hot iron, she could hear Theo moving around, singing something tuneless and cheerful, the way sixteen-year-olds sang when they didn't know yet that the world charged admission to everything. She'd found him three years ago on a dock in Puerto Sombra, all elbows and hunger, offering to work for passage and looking at her boat the way other boys looked at cathedral doors. She'd told him then what she told everyone: *I don't stay anywhere, so don't get attached.* He'd nodded like he understood. He hadn't. He still didn't. That was either his tragedy or his genius; Paloma hadn't decided.

The Kestrel came on at six knots through the milky water, her old hull groaning its familiar litany of complaints. Forty years of river service had left the boat with a personality Paloma knew better than her own body — every rattle was a word, every shudder a sentence. Right now the Kestrel was muttering about the current, which was running contrary and confused, eddying where it should have run straight, and Paloma trusted the boat's opinion more than any chart drawn by some surveyor who'd passed through here before the war and never come back.

She throttled down. Watched. The lead line told her eleven feet, then nine, then — with a suddenness that made her stomach drop like an elevator with a cut cable — five.

The bar had moved.

Not grown, not shifted a cable's length the way bars did, patient and geological. It had *moved*, whole sandbanks migrating overnight like herds of pale animals, and it had moved directly into the channel that every chart in her cabin swore was twelve feet deep at low water. The Kestrel drew eight and a half. The arithmetic was simple and terrible.

"Theo!" she shouted down the engine room hatch. "Kill it to idle! Now!"

The singing stopped. "What's wrong?"

"The bottom's wrong." She was already throwing the wheel hard over, feeling the rudder bite into water gone suddenly shallow and treacherous, trying to swing the Kestrel's bow back toward deeper water while she still had steerage way. "Sandbar's migrated. Whole thing's moved into the channel."

"Bars don't move!"

"This one did."

She felt it before she heard it — a shudder through the keel, deep and intimate, the boat's bones telling her what her instruments couldn't. Then the sound: a long, grinding scrape, like the world's largest fingernail dragged down a chalkboard made of the Kestrel's hull. The deck tilted. A coffee mug she'd owned for eleven years slid off the chart table and shattered against the bulkhead, and Paloma watched it go with a strange, detached grief, because that mug had survived four groundings, one hurricane, and a customs raid, and this was how it ended — on a sandbar with no name, outside a town she'd never meant to visit.

The Kestrel settled. Stopped. Tilted fifteen degrees to port and stayed there, groaning once more, almost petulant, and then went silent except for the tick of the cooling engine and the slap of confused water against her hull.

Paloma stood at the wheel for a long moment, hands still gripping the spokes, and let herself feel the full weight of it. Not panic — panic was a luxury, and she'd never been able to afford luxuries. Just the tired arithmetic of a woman who had done this math too many times: *grounding means inspection, inspection means damage, damage means repair, repair means money, money means—*

Money she didn't have. Money she hadn't had since Puerto Sombra, since Mateo, since the night everything went wrong and she'd pointed the Kestrel downstream and hadn't stopped pointing her downstream for three years.

Theo climbed up through the engine room hatch, wiping his hands on a rag that had been white during the previous administration. His face was doing that young-person thing where every emotion arrived without paperwork — alarm, curiosity, excitement, all queued up and jostling.

"Are we aground?"

"We're aground."

"On purpose?"

"Nobody grounds on purpose, Theo."

"Pirates do. In books. They careen their ships on purpose to clean the hulls."

"This isn't a book." But she almost smiled saying it, because Theo's book-brain was one of the few things in her life that ran reliably, like a well-maintained pump. "This is a sandbar that grew legs overnight, and we're standing on it, and I need you in the water checking the hull before I decide whether to swear or pray."

"I can do both."

"Do them in order."

* * *

The tide was going out, which was bad news wearing good news' clothes. Bad news, because a grounded boat on a falling tide settles deeper into the sand as the water leaves, like a tired animal lying down. Good news, because a falling tide would expose the hull, and Theo could see the damage with his own eyes instead of her guessing at it from inside.

He stripped to his shorts and waded in without hesitation, because Theo had never met cold water he respected. Paloma stood on the tilting deck with her arms crossed, watching him work his way along the port side where the grinding had been worst, the water dropping around him inch by inch, revealing the Kestrel's underside the way a surgeon reveals what's wrong.

"Well?" she called.

"Hold on." He ran his hands along the planking below the waterline, pressing, listening. He'd learned that trick from her — *the hull talks, listen with your fingers* — and it pleased her in a way she didn't examine too closely, that the things she taught him stuck. "There's paint scraped off here, big patch. And—" He pressed somewhere near the turn of the bilge and stopped. Pressed again. "Captain. There's a seam working. Starboard side, aft of the midships frame. It's not open, but it's... breathing. When I press, water comes through. Little bit."

"How little?"

"Drip. Not a stream. Yet."

*A seam that breathes.* Paloma closed her eyes. She'd known boats that breathed like that for years, old ladies holding themselves together with stubbornness and caulking. She'd also known boats that breathed like that for six hours before they opened their mouths all the way and swallowed the river. There was no way to know which kind of breathing this was without hauling out, and hauling out meant a slipway, and a slipway meant a town, and a town meant—

"Landfall," she said, tasting the name. It sat on the charts like a comma nobody had bothered to remove: a delta town built on pilings and stubbornness, half-swallowed by the estuary, famous for nothing except being between everywhere and somewhere else. She'd steered wide of it twice before in her life. Both times she'd felt the place watching her go by, the way stray dogs watch a cart pass.

"Captain?" Theo was looking up at her, water to his waist, hair plastered flat. "What do we do?"

What she wanted to say was: *we wait for the tide, we float off, we go around, we go somewhere else, we keep moving, because moving is the only thing I've ever been good at and the only thing that's ever kept me free.*

What she said was: "We wait for the tide to lift us off, and then we go into Landfall and see what a haul-out costs. Because if that seam opens up mid-channel, we won't be deciding anything ever again."

Theo grinned, and she saw that he'd already decided Landfall was an adventure. That was the difference between them, laid bare on a tilting deck: he looked at a stranded boat and saw a story beginning. She looked at a stranded boat and saw a bill arriving.

The tide turned near noon. Paloma spent the waiting hours doing what she always did when the world took something from her: inventory. Diesel: enough for two weeks of careful running. Food: ten days if they ate like monks. Cash, counted twice in the cabin's privacy: enough to buy a haul-out, or enough to buy food and fuel for a month, but not both. Never both. The arithmetic of her life had narrowed to that single cruel equation, and every year the numbers got smaller while the sea stayed exactly the same size.

At slack water she put the engine astern, gentle as waking a sleeper, and the Kestrel slid backward off the bar with a sigh of released sand, wallowed upright, and floated. Paloma stood at the wheel and listened to the hull with her whole body, the way you listen to a child's chest for the rattle of sickness. There it was, faint but unmistakable: a whisper of water finding a seam it liked, aft of midships, starboard. Breathing.

"Landfall," she said again, and rang the engine order telegraph to slow ahead, and the Kestrel limped toward the town she'd avoided her whole life.

* * *

Landfall announced itself the way poor towns do — by smell before sight. Woodsmoke and fish drying on racks, tar and brackish mud, the green rot smell of a mangrove swamp digesting slowly at the town's edges. Then the sight: houses on stilts leaning over brown water, a seawall patched with concrete and prayer, a market street flooded ankle-deep at high tide because the town had simply decided to live with the ocean instead of fighting it. Boats everywhere, most of them older than Theo, several of them older than Paloma, all of them looking like they'd been repaired rather than maintained.

A breakwater of black volcanic rock guarded the harbor mouth, and beyond it the water lay flat and greasy under a sky the color of wet tin. Monsoon season was coming — everyone on this coast could feel it coming, the way you feel a headache arrive — but it wasn't here yet, and the harbor was full of boats that had come in to hide from the weather that hadn't landed.

"Harbor office is on the end of the main dock," Paloma said, eyes narrowing at the building with the faded flag drooping over it. "We'll need mooring papers. And I need to find whoever runs a slipway."

"You're frowning."

"I'm thinking."

"You're frowning *and* thinking. You only do both at once when it's about money."

Paloma glanced down at him. Sixteen years old and he'd learned to read her face the way she read water. She didn't know whether to be proud or worried. Probably both. Probably that was what having a kid was — being proud and worried in the same breath, forever, until one of you died or the other one finally ran far enough away.

"It's always about money," she said. "That's not cynicism, that's physics. Everything costs. Every stop, every repair, every day tied to a dock. The sea is free. It's the edges of the sea that charge you."

"That's the saddest thing anyone's ever said."

"It's the truest thing anyone's ever said. Sadness is extra."

They came alongside the main dock just as the afternoon light went coppery, and the Kestrel — leak breathing softly in her belly, hull scraped raw along one flank, forty years old and tired down to her keel — settled against the tires hung as fenders with a bump that sounded, to Paloma's ear, exactly like a patient registering at a hospital she couldn't afford.

A man in a clean white shirt — too clean for Landfall, ironed sharp, the kind of shirt that expected to stay that way — detached himself from a group of dockworkers and came toward them with his hand already extended and a smile already installed.

"Captain! Welcome to Landfall." His voice carried the practiced warmth of a man who'd shaken ten thousand hands and meant perhaps forty of them. "Harbormaster Bell. We don't get many visitors this time of year — monsoon's coming, you understand. You'll want mooring, I expect. And I'd guess..." His eyes traveled down the Kestrel's scraped flank with the speed of a man appraising livestock. "...you'll want the slipway. Saw you come over the bar. Nasty business, our sandbars. They have moods."

"They certainly do," Paloma said, and shook his hand, and felt the shape of the trap before she could even see its edges — because harbormasters who smiled this much always had a price list hidden somewhere, and she had exactly enough money for one item on it.

Behind Bell, at the end of the dock, a blind old man sat on an overturned crate facing the water he couldn't see, and as Paloma paid her mooring fee into Bell's waiting palm — more than she'd budgeted, less than she feared — she heard the old man's voice drift across the planks, thin and rhythmic, telling a story to no one and everyone:

"...and the tide goes out farther than God intended, and the cliffs stand up naked, and the cave mouth opens like a throat, and inside lies everything Voss ever stole..."

Bell's smile didn't flicker. But his eyes, for half a second, went flat and cold as the water under the dock.

Paloma filed that away, the way she filed everything — in the deep hold, with the rest of the cargo she never unpacked — and turned back to the business of keeping her boat alive.

Every stop cost more than she could pay. She'd known that for thirty-one years.

She just didn't know yet how much this one would cost.
