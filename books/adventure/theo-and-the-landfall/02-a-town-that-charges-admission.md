## 2. A Town That Charges Admission

Theo had counted the money twice before Paloma snatched the roll out of his hands and told him to stop counting other people's business on an open dock. So he didn't know exactly how much they had left after the Kestrel ground herself half-drowned onto that sandbar outside Landfall. He only knew it wasn't much, because Paloma's face had gone through three different weather systems in the space of a minute — fury at the river, fury at the bar, and then something quieter and worse, which was arithmetic.

"Stay with the boat," she'd said, which was what she always said, and which was never really an order so much as a suggestion shaped like one. "I'm going to find a shipwright who isn't a thief."

"And if you can't?"

"Then I'll find a thief who's honest about his prices."

She'd gone up the ramp into Landfall with her shoulders set like a woman walking into weather, and Theo had lasted eleven minutes aboard before the boredom got him. Eleven minutes of listening to the bilge pump tick and the hull groan against the mooring lines, of watching brown water slide past carrying leaves and a drowned hat and somebody's lost sandal, all of it moving on toward the sea without paying anybody anything.

He left a note on the chart table — *Gone to look. Back by noon. T.* — and felt guilty about it for exactly as long as it took to reach the top of the ramp.

* * *

Landfall announced itself before you saw it. The smell came first: salt and diesel and frying plantains and, underneath all of it, the green rot-smell of a town built too close to the water and too stubborn to move. Then the sound — hammers, gulls, a radio somewhere playing a song two beats too fast for the heat. And then the town itself, stacked up the hillside in colors that would have been gaudy anywhere else but here looked like survival: houses painted blue and rust-red and sunflower yellow, their lower floors dark with old flood-lines, their upper floors hung with laundry that snapped in the wind like signal flags.

The market was flooded. Not metaphorically — actually flooded, shin-deep and rising with the tide, which apparently nobody considered a problem. Vendors had simply moved everything up a level. Tables sat on crates. Crates sat on tables. A woman sold mangoes from a rowboat, poling between the stalls like a gondolier, calling her prices over the heads of customers who waded to her and waded away again with their purchases held overhead. Children ran through the shallows on some errand known only to children, splashing everyone equally, and nobody scolded them, because in Landfall getting wet was not an event. It was Tuesday.

Theo stood at the edge of it with his boots already soaked through and felt something turn over in his chest that he didn't have a name for yet.

He'd grown up in places that charged admission. That was how he thought of them now, looking back — the company towns upriver where his mother had worked the canneries, the port towns where you needed papers to walk down certain streets, the whole long geography of his childhood where every door had a price on it and most of the prices were more than he had. But this market had no gate. Nobody checked him. A fishmonger with forearms like mooring cables looked at the strange boy standing frozen in the floodwater and said, "You going to buy something or just marinate?" and when Theo laughed — surprised into it — the man grinned and handed him a piece of dried fish for free, "because you've got a hungry face."

"I'm not—"

"You've got a hungry face," the fishmonger repeated, with the finality of scripture, and went back to gutting.

Theo ate the fish standing in the shallows. It was salty and tough and the best thing he'd eaten in a week, which said less about the fish than about the week.

* * *

He wandered. That was the thing Paloma never understood about him, he thought — she saw wandering as drifting, as the first symptom of a disease she'd spent her whole life inoculating herself against. Stay put, stay small, stay moving; those were her three commandments, and they contradicted each other, and she held all three anyway the way you'd hold three live wires. But Theo wandered the way other people prayed. Every new street was a question. Every stranger was a door he hadn't knocked on yet.

Landfall's streets climbed in switchbacks from the drowned market to the dry heights, and each level told you something about the town's relationship with the sea. The lowest streets were all commerce — chandlers, net-menders, a ropewalk, a chandlery whose window displayed brass instruments polished to a shine that seemed almost defiant given the humidity. The middle streets were homes, close-packed, their walls stained with tide-marks like tree rings, each line a flood remembered. The highest streets were quietest, and it was there, near a little chapel with a bell tower leaning like a tired sailor, that he found the storyteller.

He heard her before he saw her. A voice like gravel rolled in honey, coming from under a breadfruit tree where a dozen people sat on steps and crates and an overturned dinghy, and Theo drifted closer the way water finds a low place.

She was old in the way driftwood is old — bleached, hardened, shaped entirely by what had been done to her. Her eyes were closed and had been, he would learn later, for thirty years. She sat with her hands folded on a walking stick made of some black wood he didn't recognize, and she was telling the story of the founding of Landfall, which was also, it turned out, the story of its drowning, which was also, it turned out, the story of Aurelio Voss.

"—and Voss stood where the harbor office stands today," she was saying, "in a coat worth more than any house on this hill, and he watched his ships come in. Seventeen of them, that season. Seventeen hulls heavy with cargo, and every hull heavier still with what the cargo cost." She paused, and the pause had weight to it, the way a good pause always does. "Because Voss's ships did not sail on wind alone, children. They sailed on debt."

A child near the front — a girl of maybe six, sitting cross-legged in the dust — asked, "What kind of debt?"

"The kind you can't pay," the storyteller said. "The kind that gets paid for you. Indentured crews, the old books called them. Men who signed papers to work off a passage and found the passage grew longer every year. Women who signed to feed their families and were fed barely enough to keep pulling ropes. Voss didn't invent it. He perfected it. There's a difference, and the difference is that invention is an accident and perfection is a choice."

Theo sat down on the edge of the group, on a step worn smooth by decades of sitters, and stopped being aware of his own body. This was the feeling he lived for and could never explain to Paloma — the moment a story reached out and took hold of your collar. He'd felt it once in a chapel upriver, hearing a choir; he'd felt it once on the Kestrel's bow at dawn somewhere off the Sable Reaches, watching the light come up. Stories did to him what the sea did to Paloma, except that Paloma would never believe that, and might be right not to.

"Voss died rich," the storyteller went on. "That's the part the history books will tell you, if they tell you anything. Died in his bed, in his big house up the coast, with seventeen ships' worth of gold in banks across four seas. But the town tells a different story. The town says that on the night the great storm took the old harbor — the night Landfall drowned, sixty years ago — Voss's fortune was not in any bank at all. The town says he kept it close. Kept it *here*." She lifted one hand from the stick and pointed, blind eyes shut, unerringly, toward the cliffs at the north end of the bay. "Down below the old lighthouse point. In the sea cave."

Murmurs. Theo realized he was holding his breath.

"Now, I'll tell you what my grandmother told me, and she had it from a man who crewed for Voss himself, a man who came here after the drowning and never sailed again." The storyteller leaned forward, and though her eyes couldn't see her audience, somehow the whole audience leaned with her. "Voss was afraid. Rich men are always afraid — afraid of thieves, of rivals, of the tax men, of the families he'd bled. So he bought himself a vault no thief could crack. A cave behind rock, behind water, behind a tide that comes and goes like a locked door. And the door only opens—" she raised one finger— "*once*. Once in a great while, when the moon pulls hard and the storms push back and the sea forgets itself for a few hours. The tide goes out farther than it has any right to go. The water drops below the cave mouth. And for one slack stretch of hours, the richest grave on this coast stands open."

"Has anyone ever been in?" someone asked. A young man, skeptical, arms crossed.

"Once a generation," the storyteller said, "the tide opens it. That's what the saying is, and sayings don't survive sixty years by being wrong. My grandmother swore her own father saw the bay drain dry as a salt pan, the year she was a girl. Saw the black mouth of the cave below the cliffs like a missing tooth. And do you know what the men who saw it did?" She smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile. "They talked about it. For years. And by the time anyone worked up the courage to go down, the tide had come home, and the door was shut, and stayed shut. Because that's the trick of it, children. The sea gives you a window just wide enough to see your whole life change through it — and never quite wide enough to climb through."

Silence. Even the gulls seemed to be listening.

Then the skeptical young man said, "It's a story. The cave floods and drains with every tide cycle, same as any sea cave. There's no treasure. Old Voss's money is in banks, if it exists at all."

"Probably," the storyteller agreed pleasantly, and Theo's heart did something complicated, because she said it the way Paloma read weather — agreeing with the safe forecast while watching the horizon for the other one. "And probably the moon is just a rock. You're free to believe whatever keeps you comfortable." She settled back against the breadfruit trunk. "But I'll tell you one more thing, since you sat so nicely. The year the tide opens, the town knows before the town knows. The fish come in strange. The crabs walk where crabs don't walk. The old wells taste of salt days early, like the sea is sending word ahead. The water tells us. It always tells us." She turned her blind face slightly, and Theo had the sudden, skin-crawling certainty that she was looking directly at him. "So when the wells go salty, children, you get down to that bay. Whatever's behind that door won't wait for the timid."

* * *

He meant to go straight back to the Kestrel. He even started back — down the switchbacks, past the chapel with its leaning tower, through streets that now seemed to hum with a frequency they hadn't had an hour ago. But the market pulled at him like a current, and he let it, buying nothing, spending the last coins in his pocket on a bag of roasted peanuts he didn't need, asking questions he hadn't planned to ask.

Had anyone ever seen the bay drain? The peanut seller shrugged — her grandfather used to swear the wells went salty once, back before the war, but her grandfather swore a lot of things. Did people still talk about Voss? A boy hauling ice answered that one with a spit and a curse: the Voss Shipping Company still owned half the warehouses on the waterfront, rented out by some trust in the capital, and half the town worked in buildings that paid rent to a dead man's ghost. Was there really a cave? Everyone agreed there was a cave. Fishermen avoided the north point in rough weather; the swell sucked in there like breath. Beyond that, opinions divided neatly along the line of whether you believed old Odalys — the storyteller — or whether you believed the sea kept secrets better than it kept promises.

By the time Theo reached the harbor, the light had gone long and amber, and he was late, and he knew it, and he climbed the Kestrel's gangway braced for weather.

Paloma was in the cockpit with the repair quote in her hand and a look on her face that stopped him two steps down.

"How bad?" he asked.

"Bad." She smoothed the paper on the chart table, and he saw the number, and understood why her voice came out flat. "New planking below the waterline, re-caulk the seam, replace a strake. The shipwright's honest — I checked him three ways. It's the fee structure that's robbery. Harbor levy, inspection surcharge, 'transient vessel assessment.'" She said the last words like they tasted of copper. "This town charges admission, Theo. To float in its water."

"We could leave," he said. "Run down the coast, careen her on a beach, patch her ourselves."

"On a leaking hull, ahead of monsoon season, with no dry dock?" She shook her head slowly. "We're here until she's fixed. That's the trap. Everywhere is the trap." She looked at him then, really looked, and her expression shifted — the captain receding, the woman who'd fished him out of a worse life than this one surfacing underneath. "Where were you? You were supposed to stay with the boat."

"I went to the market." He hesitated. He knew how she'd take it — knew the exact shape of the sigh, the *stories are how towns rob you gently* speech she'd given him in three ports already. But the words came out anyway, pushed by something stronger than caution. "There's a storyteller up the hill. Blind woman. She was talking about a cave — under the cliffs at the north point. A sea cave. She says Aurelio Voss hid his whole fortune in it, and the tide opens it once a generation. When the water drops far enough, the mouth stands open, and—"

"Stop." Paloma held up a hand, but not unkindly. She rubbed her eyes with thumb and forefinger, and when she spoke again her voice was tired in a way that had nothing to do with the day. "Theo. Every town on this coast has a cave with a fortune in it. Every town has a blind woman who tells it better than anyone, because hope is the cheapest thing a town can sell and the only product that never spoils. I've heard this story in six ports. Six. Different magnate, different cave, same ending — which is that the person who goes looking comes back with empty pockets or doesn't come back."

"But what if—"

"What if what?" She met his eyes, and he saw it there, plain as a chart: not mockery. Fear. She was afraid of the story, or afraid of what the story did to him, or maybe just afraid of one more thing in a life that had too many. "Listen to me. We have a boat to fix, a bill we can't pay, and a storm season coming. We don't have time for legends. Legends are what towns use to keep people like us looking down instead of watching the exits." She folded the quote away. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow I start negotiating, and I need you sharp."

Theo nodded, and said yes ma'am, and went forward to his bunk. But he lay awake in the dark with the bilge pump ticking and the town sounds drifting across the water — the radio, the gulls, a dog, laughter from some deck party ashore — and turned the storyteller's words over like stones.

*The water tells us. It always tells us.*

He wanted roots. He'd wanted them so long the wanting had worn a groove in him, smooth and deep, the way water wears stone. Paloma wanted distance, and he loved her for surviving, and he understood — he truly did, better than she thought — that her running had kept them both alive. But a life of distance was just a slow way of drowning, and Theo was sixteen, and tonight, in a strange harbor that smelled of plantains and salt, he had heard a story with a door in it.

Outside, far off across the black bay, the old lighthouse point stood silhouetted against the last light, and beneath it, invisible, the sea held its breath over whatever it was hiding.

Three days later, the tide would go out farther than it had any right to go — and half the town would come running down to the shore, and Theo would already be there, camera in hand, standing at the edge of the impossible.

But that night, all he could do was listen to the water slap the hull, and wonder if the wells on the hill had started tasting of salt.
