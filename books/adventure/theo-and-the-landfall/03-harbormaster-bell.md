## 3. Harbormaster Bell

The harbormaster's office sat on stilts above the water like a heron that had decided to stop hunting and start judging. Paloma climbed the salt-swollen stairs with the repair quote folded in her jacket pocket, and by the time she reached the top landing she had already read the building the way she read a river: where the current ran, where the rot hid, what it would cost to fight and what it would cost to yield.

The door stood open. Inside, a ceiling fan turned with the lazy conviction of something that had never once been in a hurry, stirring air that smelled of ink, mildew, and old tobacco. Behind a desk scarred by decades of boots and cargo hooks sat a broad man in a linen shirt gone soft at the collar, writing in a ledger with a fountain pen he held the way other men held cigars.

"Captain Dunmore." He didn't look up. "You're the Kestrel."

"I'm the Kestrel," Paloma agreed.

"Ran aground on the Widow's Bar coming in. Two nights ago." He finished his line, blotted it, and only then raised his eyes. They were pale, almost colorless, set deep under a brow built for shade. "That bar shifts every season. Every captain who's worked this delta knows it. You came in anyway."

"The channel marker was lying."

"Markers don't lie. People move them." He said it without heat, as if reporting weather. "Sit down, Captain."

She didn't sit. She had learned long ago that sitting put you at the height of a man's desk, and desks were where towns kept their teeth. Instead she leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, and let the silence do some work while Bell looked her over — the sun-cracked hands, the canvas jacket patched at both elbows, the eyes that catalogued exits.

"You've got a reputation," Bell said. "Comes upriver ahead of boats, usually. Small ports remember you. Cayo Verde remembers you. Puerto Sombra remembers you very well."

"Ports remember whoever owes them money."

"They remember whoever *doesn't pay*." He set down the pen and laced his fingers over the ledger. "But I'm not interested in your history. I'm interested in your hull. You took damage on the bar — opened a seam forward of the keel, cracked a strake or two, from what my surveyor saw when your boy tied off. That's not cosmetic. That's the kind of wound that opens wider the next time you lean on it."

Paloma said nothing. The quote in her pocket felt heavier than paper had any right to be.

"Here's how Landfall works," Bell went on, rising. He was taller than she'd guessed, and moved with the unhurried mass of a man who had never needed to hurry because everything came to him eventually. He crossed to the window behind his desk and looked out at the harbor, so she looked too. The Kestrel rode at her mooring in the gray morning light, listing slightly to port, looking smaller from up here than she ever did from the wheel. "Every boat in this basin pays moorage by the ton-day. Dry dock time is billed by the hour, plus materials, plus the yardmaster's discretion. Your quote" — he nodded at her pocket, and she felt the paper there like a coal — "is fair. For an outsider."

"And for family?"

"For family, we're generous." He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, the kind that had opened many doors and closed just as many. "Landfall takes care of its own, Captain. That's the whole trick of a town like this. The delta eats places that don't hold together. So we hold together. We look after each other. And we expect the same courtesy back."

"I pay my fees."

"You will. Everyone pays their fees." He turned from the window. "The question is whether you pay all of them, or only the ones written down."

Outside, a gull screamed once and thought better of it. Paloma watched Bell's face and did the arithmetic she'd been doing since she was nineteen years old and a harbinger in a river town first told her that nothing in a port was free, only deferred. Mooring fees for a boat the Kestrel's size, at Landfall's rates, through however long the monsoon pinned them here — she could feel the number circling overhead like a bird waiting for something to die.

"How long is the season?" she asked.

Bell's smile widened a fraction. "Now that depends. Some years the rains come early and stay late. Some years they're gentle. A town can't control the sky, Captain. But a town can control its docks." He returned to his chair and sat, folding his hands again, a man settling into comfort. "Which brings me to why I asked you up."

"I invited myself."

"You did. And I'm glad, because now we can skip the part where I pretend this is about paperwork." He opened a drawer and drew out a sheet of paper, turning it to face her. On it was a hand-drawn chart of the upper river — the blackwater channels above the last settlement, where the maps went thin and the banks went wild. A route was inked in red. "There's a landing up there. Old logging pier, mostly rotted, still takes a flatboat at high water. Twice a month, equipment comes down that pier. Crates. Instruments. Survey equipment — the mining concern has interests upriver, and they need it moved quietly."

"Quietly."

"Night runs. No manifest, no lights past the bend, no questions at the lock." He slid the paper toward her across the desk. "Three trips. That's all I ask. Three night runs, and your mooring fees for the season are waived. Dry dock time at cost. Materials at cost. The Kestrel leaves Landfall whole and paid-up, and nobody remembers you were ever here."

Paloma looked at the red line on the chart. It ran upriver through water she knew by reputation — narrow, snagged, unpatrolled, the kind of channel where a boat could vanish and take a season to be found. Survey equipment. In crates. At night. Without manifests.

She had heard the phrase *survey equipment* before, in four languages, on three rivers. It meant whatever the man saying it needed it to mean. Sometimes it actually meant instruments. More often it meant things that weighed the same as instruments and left the same holes in the ground, or the same holes in people.

"No lights past the bend," she repeated.

"Bends hide things, Captain. That's what bends are for."

"And if someone boards me at the lock?"

"Nobody boards. That's rather the point." Bell picked up his pen again, twirled it once between thick fingers. "Look. I'll be straight with you, because you strike me as a woman who respects straight talk. This delta runs on three things: silt, silence, and favors. The silt comes free. The silence costs. And the favors—" he tapped the chart, "—the favors are how a stranger becomes something other than a stranger. Three runs. Then you're not an outsider anymore. You're one of ours, and one of ours doesn't pay outsider rates."

"And if I say no?"

Bell sighed, genuinely, the sigh of a reasonable man confronted with unreasonable weather. "Then you're an outsider. And outsiders pay what the board says outsiders pay." He spread his hands. "I don't make the rates, Captain. I just keep the books."

*You write the books,* Paloma thought. *In that ledger, with that pen, in ink that dries the color of dried blood.* But she said none of it. She had learned that too — that the first lesson of small ports was that the man with the pen was never the man to argue with, because the pen did the arguing for him, later, in numbers.

She pushed the chart back across the desk.

"Thank you for the offer," she said. "I'll pay the rates."

Bell studied her for a long moment, and something shifted behind the pale eyes — not anger; anger would have been almost comfortable. It was more like recalculation. A merchant re-pricing goods that hadn't sold.

"That's a shame," he said mildly. "Theo seems like a good boy. Quick. Polite. The market women like him already." He made a small note in the ledger, unhurried. "It's hard, keeping a boy fed on a leaking boat with no income. Harder when the boat's impounded for unpaid fees, which happens at thirty days. Then it's not your boat at all, legally speaking. Auction rules apply."

"He's not for sale and neither am I."

"Everything's for sale here, Captain. That's what a port is." He closed the ledger with a soft, final sound, like a lid going on a jar. "Take a day. Think it over. My offer keeps." He smiled again, that warm-edged smile. "Weather permitting."

Paloma walked down the stairs without hurrying, which cost her something, because every nerve she owned wanted to run. Running was her oldest skill. But you didn't run from a man like Bell; running told him the price was right.

* * *

The boatyard smelled of tar and wet cedar, and the yardmaster — a stooped woman named Ferro with forearms like dock rope — walked the Kestrel's hull at low tide with a sounding hammer, tapping, listening, tapping again, while Paloma followed and Theo hovered at the gunwale pretending not to listen and listening to every word.

"Strake's cracked here, here." Tap. Tap. "Seam's opened a hand's width forward. You're lucky — another fathom and you'd have found the bottom permanently." Ferro spat over her shoulder into the mud. "Caulk, new strake stock, two days on the ways. Call it—" she squinted at the sky as if the number might be written there, "—what I quoted yesterday. Maybe a hair less, if the rain holds off."

"A hair less," Paloma said. "I'll take the hair."

Ferro grinned, gap-toothed, and clapped the hull twice like a horse's flank. "Good boat. Old bones, but honest ones. I'll have her on the ways tomorrow—"

A boy came down the yard ramp then, one of Bell's runners, red-sashed and breathless, carrying a fresh sheet of paper. He handed it to Ferro without a word, waited while she read it, and left the way he'd come.

Ferro read it twice. Her grin died by degrees, like a lamp running out of oil.

"What is it?" Paloma asked.

Ferro turned the paper so Paloma could see. Same figures. Same itemized lines — caulking, strake stock, labor, ways-time. Every number doubled. At the bottom, in fresh ink, a single stamped line: *Revised per Harbor Board assessment.*

"Assessment," Paloma said.

"Board meets when Bell says it meets." Ferro crumpled the paper slowly, her knuckles whitening around it. "Captain, I quoted you honest. I'll build to the honest quote if you can get me leave to. But I've got a yard, and a lease, and a daughter at the school up the hill, and—" she stopped, jaw working. "I'm sorry. I am. But I can't eat for you."

"I know," Paloma said. "I've been the reason people can't eat before. It looks the same from every side."

She walked the length of the hull while Ferro retreated to her shed, and Theo dropped from the gunwale to fall in beside her, and for a while neither of them spoke. The tide was coming back in, sliding gray-green up the mudflats, erasing the yard's footprints one by one.

"Doubled," Theo said finally. "Just like that? Because you wouldn't—"

"Because I wouldn't." Paloma crouched at the bow, laid her palm flat against the cold planking below the waterline, where the seam gaped like a split lip. "This is how it works, Theo. Towns like this aren't corrupt the way cities are corrupt. Cities have systems; corruption hides inside them. A town like Landfall is just a family that grew too big for its house. Bell's the head of it. You don't fight the head of a family. You join it, or you stand outside in the rain."

"We could take the night runs."

She looked at him. Sixteen years old, all elbows and earnestness, salt-dried hair sticking up where his cap had crushed it, and already learning to price things the way ports taught everyone to price them. It frightened her more than the doubled quote did.

"No," she said. "We don't know what's in those crates. And whatever it is, it goes up that river on our deck, and then it's ours — our name on it, our wake behind it. I've carried cargo like that before. Once." She stood, wiped her hand on her thigh. "Once was enough to teach me what it costs. Not in money. Money's cheap. It costs you the person you were before you carried it."

Theo was quiet for a moment. "So what do we do?"

"We pay," Paloma said. "We sell what we can spare, we take charter work in the harbor while we can get it, we stretch the rice and the coffee, and we pay the doubled rate, because a boat with a broken hull and a clean name floats better than the reverse." She said it steadily, and believed maybe sixty percent of it, and hated that the other forty percent was already doing the math on Bell's chart, the red line winding upriver through the dark.

Because that was the cold truth she carried up the yard ramp that evening, past Ferro's shuttered window, past the market stalls closing against the first spit of rain: towns didn't punish outsiders for being strangers. Strangers were forgiven; strangers left. Towns punished outsiders for *staying strange* — for standing in the square with open hands and refusing the handshake that wasn't a handshake, for making the whole careful machinery of favors feel, for one ugly moment, optional.

She had refused Bell, and the refusal had cost double by sundown. By morning it would cost triple, or it would cost Theo, or it would cost the Kestrel herself, hauled off to auction while the monsoon rolled in over a town that charged admission to its own kindness.

Paloma stood on the dock as the rain thickened, watching the Kestrel rock at her mooring, and let herself want, just for one breath, the thing she never let herself want: to stop. To plant herself somewhere and dig in and fight a place instead of fleeing it.

Then she laughed at herself, short and dry, and went below to count what was left in the strongbox.

Behind her, far up the blackening bay, thunder cleared its throat — early, too early, the monsoon arriving ahead of any schedule Bell's books allowed for.
