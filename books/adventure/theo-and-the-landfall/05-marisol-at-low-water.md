## 5. Marisol at Low Water

Paloma had not slept. The Kestrel sat on her mooring lines like a tired animal, creaking with every swell, and Paloma lay in her bunk listening to the hull argue with the water and losing the argument in her head over and over again. Twice the tide had turned in the night — she'd felt it in the way the boat leaned, the way the lines groaned — and twice she'd thought about the cave mouth below the cliffs that Theo had photographed at dawn yesterday, that impossible drained bay, that black doorway standing open in the rock like a held breath.

By morning light the bay had come back, of course. The sea always came back; that was the one promise it kept. But the town had not forgotten what it had seen. From the wheelhouse she could see the shore road already crowded — carts, bicycles, men carrying coils of rope and crates that clinked like tools, a knot of them gathered around Bell's harbor office with the particular patience of people waiting for permission to do something they'd already decided to do.

"They're going down there," Theo said, appearing beside her with two cups of chicory coffee, handing her the one without sugar because he knew her by now. "The whole town. Marisol said—" He stopped.

"Marisol who?"

Theo had the decency to look caught. "Nobody. A woman I met at the market. She asked a lot of questions about the tide tables." He said it too fast, the way he said everything he didn't want examined.

Paloma drank her bitter coffee and looked out at the crowd on the shore road and did not ask further questions, which was its own kind of answer. "Get the dinghy ready," she said. "If half of Landfall is going to crawl into that hole, I want to see what they're crawling into before Bell decides to charge admission."

"You think there's anything down there? Really?"

"I think," Paloma said, "that when a town this poor looks at a hole in the ground and sees money, somebody's going to get hurt finding out. Better us than them."

It wasn't entirely true. Part of her — the part she kept below the waterline, where the expensive parts lived — wanted to see it herself. Wanted to stand in a place the sea gave up even once a generation, wanted to know what Aurelio Voss had thought was worth sealing behind stone and tide. But she told Theo the civic version, because captains tell their crews the version that keeps the crew rowing.

* * *

The cliffs east of the harbor were old coral limestone, honeycombed and rotten, streaked white with bird lime. The path down was a goat track that became a ladder of iron rungs someone had driven into the rock a century ago and never apologized for. Below, the exposed shelf of the low tide still glistened — the water hadn't returned all the way, as if the sea itself were hesitating — and the cave mouth stood above a terrace of wet black stone, tall enough to drive a cart through, its throat swallowing the morning light.

There were already people on the shelf. A dozen, maybe more, clustered at the entrance with lanterns unlit, arguing. Paloma recognized the butcher's son, two of Bell's harbor men trying to look unofficial, an old woman with a basket who seemed to be selling something — of course she was; Landfall charged admission for everything, including miracles.

And at the mouth of the cave, planted in front of it like a fence post made of muscle and bad temper, stood a stranger in a diving suit.

She was small, wiry, dark-haired, maybe thirty, with the wetsuit peeled to the waist and tied off at her hips, revealing a tank harness and a knife on her thigh that she wore the way other women wore wedding rings. She had a lantern at her feet and a coil of guide line in one hand, and she was saying, in a voice that carried flat and clear across the shelf:

"The chamber floods on the turn. Anyone who goes in without knowing the timings drowns in the dark, and I'm not dragging your bodies out. Go home."

"We've got rights—" the butcher's son started.

"You've got lungs. Those are different things."

Paloma came down the last of the rungs and stood on the wet stone, feeling it breathe under her feet — the whole shelf trembling faintly with the offshore swell, the way a deck trembles. Up close the stranger looked worse than tough; she looked exhausted, the skin under her eyes bruised purple, salt-crusted hair escaping a braid, hands nicked and stained from work in the water.

"And who are you?" Paloma asked. "The toll keeper?"

The stranger looked her over — the riverboat boots, the captain's coat gone soft at the elbows, the face of a woman who'd been negotiating with weather for thirty years — and something in her expression shifted from hostility to assessment.

"Marisol Voss," she said.

The name landed on the shelf like dropped cargo. The butcher's son actually stepped back. One of Bell's men spat, whether in contempt or superstition Paloma couldn't say.

"Aurelio Voss's granddaughter," Marisol went on, louder, daring anyone to enjoy it. "Yes. That Voss. And no, before you ask, I'm not here for gold. There is no gold. My grandfather wasn't rich because he found treasure. He was rich because he stole it, and the proof of what he stole is in that cave, and I intend to take it out before any of you can sell tickets to it."

Silence, except the sea working at the edges of the world.

Then the old woman with the basket said, "Five pesos to hear more," and the spell broke into laughter and argument, and Paloma took Marisol's elbow and steered her aside, toward the cliff base, out of earshot.

"You shouldn't have said your name," Paloma said quietly.

"It's the only thing I own outright."

"It's also the reason Bell will have six men down here by noon. A Voss, claiming the cave? That's not a story anymore, that's a jurisdiction." Paloma glanced back at the crowd, then at the black mouth of the cave, breathing its cold air at them. "I'm Paloma Dunmore. Captain of the Kestrel, currently aground in your grandfather's town. This is Theo—"

"He knows me," Theo said.

"He *met* you," Paloma corrected, and watched Marisol's eyebrows rise a fraction — the stranger filing away that the boy had been asking about tide tables, that the captain had been asking about caves, that neither of them had mentioned it to the other until now. Paloma filed it too: this woman missed nothing, which made her either an ally or a problem, and those categories overlapped more than Paloma liked.

"Why are you really here?" Marisol asked. "Don't give me the speech about protecting the townsfolk. Nobody rows out to a cave at first light out of civic duty."

Fair. Paloma looked at the cave instead of at her. "My boat needs repairs I can't pay for. Bell offered to waive my fees if I moved cargo for him at night, and when I refused, my quote doubled. Whatever's in that cave is worth enough that a harbormaster would rather break a stranger than lose it. I want to know what I'm standing next to before it falls on me."

"That's honest," Marisol said, with faint surprise, as though honesty were a dialect she rarely heard spoken.

"It's expensive. Honesty costs more every year."

Marisol almost smiled. It transformed her face for half a second — made her young, made her someone's daughter — and then it was gone, packed away like gear between dives.

* * *

They walked the shelf while the crowd thinned, driven off by the rising water licking closer to the cave mouth and by Marisol's flat recitation of drowning statistics. She talked as she walked, and Paloma listened the way she read charts: marking depths, hazards, currents.

"My grandfather built his fleet on indentured crews," Marisol said. "You know what indenture means here. It means a man signs a paper in some port upriver because his family's starving, and the paper says three years, and the paper lies. It means debt that grows faster than wages. It means my grandfather owned human beings with extra steps, and when they died — and they died, storms, fever, the holds of his own ships — he wrote them off as losses and hired the next boatload." Her voice stayed level, but her hand had closed hard around the guide line until the knuckles whitened. "When I was nineteen I asked him about it. At dinner. In front of the family. He disowned me before the soup arrived."

"What's in the cave?"

"Crew manifests. Ledgers. His correspondence — he kept everything, the man was a magpie for paper, he never threw away a document that proved how clever he was. Including, I believe, letters where he admits exactly what he did, in his own hand, because he was proud of it. If those ledgers come out of that cave, the families descended from his crews — the ones in this town, the ones scattered up the coast — they can file claims. Real ones. Restitution written in law, not charity." She stopped walking and faced Paloma fully. "That's the treasure. Paper. The most valuable paper on this coast, sitting behind a door that opens once a generation, and I have perhaps two weeks before the monsoon seals it for another twenty years."

"And Bell wants it buried."

"Bell's family name appears in the third ledger," Marisol said. "His great-grandfather was Voss's shore agent. The man who kept the crews' papers locked in a drawer so they couldn't leave." She let that sit. "So yes. He wants it buried, or burned, or 'lost in a survey accident.' Whichever is cheapest."

The water was coming back now. Paloma could see it — the slow green tongue sliding across the shelf, patient as tax. Above them the cave mouth dripped. Somewhere inland, thunder cleared its throat, testing the season.

"You've been diving it already," Paloma said. "Your suit's wet past the knees and you're running line."

"Twice. Outer chambers only. There's a sump at the back — a flooded passage — that goes deeper. I need another diver and better timing, and I need to do it before the storm season fills everything." Marisol looked at her directly. "You dive?"

"I hold my breath and hope. Different sport."

"Your boy said you used to salvage."

"My boy talks too much." But Paloma sighed, because lying to this woman felt like lying to a depth gauge — technically possible, practically stupid. "I salvaged for six years off the northern reefs. Wrecks, mostly. Some recovery work nobody paid me for. I'm not cave-trained. Cave diving kills trained divers every year, and it does it politely, without warning."

"I know what it kills. I grew up on these stories." Marisol crouched suddenly and pressed her palm flat against the wet stone of the shelf, as if taking its pulse. "The tide opened this once when I was a child. My grandfather brought me here — the only time he ever brought me anywhere — and he stood right where you're standing and looked at that door, and he was afraid. Not of the water. Of what was inside. I've thought about that face for twenty years." She stood. "I don't trust you, Captain Dunmore. You're a drifter with debts and a warrant-shaped history — yes, I hear things too — and drifters leave. That's what drifters do; it's the whole job description."

"Flattery will get you everywhere."

"But you're the only person in this town who isn't afraid of Bell, and the only one with a boat fast enough to move documents if it comes to moving them. So." She stuck out her hand — rope-scarred, salt-split, steady. "Wary alliance. We watch each other, we don't lie to each other, and we get those ledgers out before the monsoon. After that, we go back to not trusting anyone, professionally."

Paloma looked at the hand. Twenty years of reading people had taught her that the dangerous ones were the ones with nothing left to prove, and this woman had everything to prove and nothing to lose, which was a combination Paloma had been herself, once, and knew exactly how much damage it could do.

But the Kestrel needed money she didn't have. Bell wanted the cave sealed. And somewhere in the dark behind that drowned door was a stack of paper that could buy a hundred families their history back — including, apparently, the family of the man currently doubling her repair bills.

She shook the hand. Grip like a cleat.

"One condition," Paloma said. "Nobody goes past the outer chambers without me on the surface with a line and a boat. I don't care how good you are. Dead divers find nothing."

"Agreed. And one of mine: the ledgers go to the families, not to you, not to me, not to whoever pays most."

"Noted." Paloma glanced at the returning tide, already ankle-deep and climbing the shelf with the confidence of something that owned the deed. Behind them, up on the cliff road, she saw a glint of glass — someone watching with field glasses, and the angle of the watcher put them squarely above the harbor office. Bell already knew. Of course he already knew. By tonight he'd know Marisol's name, her berth if she had one, and Paloma's involvement, and the price of everything in Landfall would adjust accordingly.

"We should go," she said. "Separately. You first, along the shore. I'll take the rungs."

Marisol shouldered her tank and paused. "Captain. The sump at the back of the second chamber — the water in it was warm today. Warmer than the sea. Something feeds it from inland, which means there may be air on the far side. Which means the inner chambers might be reachable without scuba." Her eyes were bright and slightly mad with the beauty of it. "If the monsoon holds off eight more days, we won't even need to dive."

"And if it doesn't hold off?"

Marisol looked south, where the horizon had begun assembling its dark furniture — cloud stacked on cloud, patient, enormous, indifferent to ledgers and warrants and the plans of small determined women.

"Then the sea keeps my grandfather's confession," she said, "for another twenty years. And I'll spend them here, waiting for the tide to open the door again, getting older, watching Bell's grandchildren collect the fees." She pulled her hood up against the first fat drops of rain. "Eight days, Captain. Bring your boat and your breath."

She went up the shore road quick and low, a shadow among shadows, and Paloma stood on the vanishing shelf with the rain starting and the tide reclaiming the miracle inch by inch, and thought: *I came here to patch a hull and leave.*

Instead she climbed the rusted rungs toward a town that was already choosing sides, with a warrant somewhere upriver and a granddaughter of Aurelio Voss expecting her back in eight days, and the first true storm of the season rolling in behind her like a debt collector who knew where she lived.
