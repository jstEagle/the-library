## 6. First Descent

**Paloma**

The tide table lied.

Paloma had spent thirty years trusting tide tables the way other people trusted scripture, and now she stood at the mouth of a cave that wasn't supposed to exist until next spring, watching slack water go glassy and green in the throat of the cliffs below Landfall. The almanac said neap. The sea said otherwise. The sea had been saying otherwise since the day the Kestrel ground herself on that sandbar, and Paloma had stopped arguing with it.

"Slack's maybe forty minutes," Marisol said, crouched on the wet rock with her dive bag open, checking seals with quick, unromantic hands. "Maybe fifty if we're lucky. After that the water starts coming back through that arch like it owes somebody money."

"It usually does," Paloma said.

Theo crouched beside her, lantern packed, notebook already out — of course the notebook was already out — sketching the cave mouth in fast strokes. The opening was taller than the Kestrel's mast, a black keyhole cut into limestone that the impossible low tide had peeled open like a letter nobody had wanted delivered. Barnacles climbed its walls in pale ridges, marking every high water for the last hundred years, and above them, dry as bone, something carved into the stone caught the morning light.

Letters. Rows of them.

"Not gold," Theo breathed. He'd been saying it all morning, half prayer, half dare.

"Don't jinx us," Paloma said, but her heart wasn't in it. She looked down the cliff path they'd scrambled, at the bay lying drained and glistening like the floor of some god's abandoned house, and felt the old itch between her shoulder blades. The one that said *move*. Boats were leaving harbors all over the delta this morning; hers was leaking in Landfall's mud, held hostage by a repair quote that had doubled overnight because she wouldn't ferry Bell's crates upriver in the dark. Every hour she stood here was an hour the town could tighten another screw.

And still she didn't move. That was the strange part. That was the part she kept examining like a splinter under the skin.

Marisol went first, as agreed. She slid into the shin-deep water at the cave's threshold without ceremony, waded to where the floor shelved down, and turned back. "Floor rises inside," she called. "You'll be dry within twenty meters. Watch the left wall — there's a shelf of rock that'll skin you if you hug it."

Paloma followed with Theo behind her, lanterns lit once they passed out of daylight, and the sound changed around them — the hush of the outside world replaced by drip and echo and the deep, slow breathing of a cave filling with tide somewhere below. The air tasted of salt and stone and something older, mineral and cold, like drinking from a well.

The passage opened into a chamber the size of a chapel, and Paloma stopped so abruptly Theo walked into her back.

Lantern light climbed the walls and found *names*.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Carved into the limestone in ranks and columns, some in careful chiseled capitals, some scratched by hands that had clearly been shaking, some so worn by time and damp that only fragments remained — a *MAR*, a *—ELLA VOSS*, a single *J* repeated down one column like a fence picket. They rose from the waterline to the vault of the ceiling, and in the lantern glow they seemed to move, the way names do when there are enough of them.

"Oh," Theo said. Just that. Just *oh*, small as a child.

Paloma had seen mass graves from the deck of riverboats after flood years. She had seen the way towns carved their dead into stone when the dead outnumbered the living's ability to remember them. This was not that. This was worse, or better — these people hadn't been buried here. They'd been *recorded* here. Filed. Like cargo.

She lifted her lantern higher and read the nearest column aloud, because someone had to, because silence in front of that many names felt like complicity.

"*Ondine, brigantine, 1841. Crew manifest.*" A pause. "*Abioye, Kwame. Able seaman. Term: seven years. Wages: deferred.*"

"Deferred," Theo repeated.

"Meaning never paid," Marisol said quietly. She'd come up beside them, dripping, her own lantern raised. Her face in the light was very still. "Keep reading."

"*Achebe, Nnamdi. Cook. Term: seven years. Wages: deferred.*" Paloma's voice sounded strange in the echo, flattened and multiplied. "*Ferreira, Luzia. Deckhand. Age: fourteen.*"

Fourteen. Theo was fourteen. She felt him go rigid beside her and didn't look at him, because looking would make it true in a different way.

"They weren't crew," he said finally. His voice cracked on it. "They were — this is a ledger. The whole cave is a *ledger*."

"Aurelio Voss didn't hide his treasure down here," Marisol said. There was something raw under the steadiness of her voice, something she was holding down with both hands. "He hid his *receipts*. Every ship he bought cheap because the men sailing her couldn't leave. Every contract where the wages were 'deferred' until a voyage that never ended. He carved them himself, near the end — my grandmother said he came down here with masons for two years and told everyone he was building a tomb." She laughed, short and terrible. "He was. Theirs."

Paloma lowered her lantern slowly, and the names swam in the moving light. Somewhere deeper in the chamber, water dripped with metronome patience. Forty minutes of slack, Marisol had said. It suddenly seemed like nothing at all.

"We're not taking gold out of here," she said. "We're taking witnesses."

* * *

**Theo**

Theo had imagined treasure his whole life, the way boys did — chests, coins, the bright clink of it. What he had never imagined was standing inside the treasure and feeling like he was trespassing at a funeral.

He copied names. That was the job now, the one he'd given himself without anyone assigning it, and he did it with numb fingers and a pencil going blunt, kneeling on the dry shelf of rock with his notebook open across his knees. *Abioye, Kwame. Achebe, Nnamdi. Ferreira, Luzia, age fourteen.* He wrote *age fourteen* twice because the first time his hand shook too badly to read.

There were ships' names too, and dates, and little marks beside some entries that Marisol said meant *died in service* and others that meant *ran* — and beside the ones that meant *ran*, sometimes, a second mark. A small circle. Marisol had gone quiet when she found the first one, then quieter when she found the pattern.

"Circles mean they made it," she said. "Somebody was tracking them. Somebody down here knew who got free." She touched the circle beside a name — *Okonkwo, Adaeze* — the way you'd touch a scar. "Adaeze Okonkwo ran from the *Ondine* in '43. My grandmother used to sing a song about her. I thought it was a lullaby." Her jaw worked. "It was a news report."

Theo wrote that down too, though he wasn't sure a notebook could hold it.

The chamber went back farther than their light reached. Side passages branched off into dark, and the air moved through them faintly, carrying the smell of deeper water. Twice Theo thought he heard something that wasn't drip — a low shifting groan, stone settling, the cave clearing its throat — and both times Marisol tilted her head, listening, and both times she said, "Slack's still holding. Keep writing."

So he kept writing. Names became hours. His knees ached against the rock. And somewhere in the middle of copying a column off the *Sea-Lark, 1856*, he realized he'd stopped being afraid of the cave and started being angry, and that the anger was warm, and that he couldn't remember the last time he'd been angry *on purpose*, about something that mattered, instead of just angry at the wind and the work and the endless moving.

Paloma appeared at his elbow with a strip of dried fish and made him eat it. "Scribes who faint are useless scribes," she said, but she sat down next to him while he chewed, her lantern between them, and for a while neither of them talked.

"My ma's name is probably down here somewhere," Theo said, before he could stop himself. "Not literally. But — people like her. People who signed things because there was nothing else to sign." He'd never told Paloma much about his mother. There hadn't ever been a dock long enough. Now the words came out easy as water finding a crack. "She worked a cannery upriver for three years. Said the contract was fair. Said it right up until they docked her pay for the bunk and the blanket and the boots."

Paloma was quiet a moment. "Voss didn't invent it," she said. "He just wrote it down prettier than most."

"And nobody ever hung for it."

"Nobody ever even *read* for it." She looked up at the walls, at the thousands of names climbing into the dark. "Until somebody reads it out loud, none of this happened. That's how the world works, Theo. History isn't what happened. It's what somebody was brave enough to say happened."

He thought about that the rest of the time he copied. He thought about it when Marisol called from the far wall — "Here! There's more, there's a whole inner gallery, the manifests continue through an arch —" and he thought about it when the first thing went wrong, which was the sound.

Not the groan this time. A scrape. Above them, back toward the entrance, rock grinding under a boot that hadn't wiped itself clean.

Theo killed his lantern so fast he nearly burned his thumb.

* * *

**Marisol**

Marisol had grown up in houses with Voss money in the walls, and the first thing such houses taught you was that silence was never empty. So when the sound came from the entrance passage — boot on stone, deliberate, wrong — she was already moving, hand flat on Theo's notebook to close it, the other snuffing her lantern to a coal.

Three lights, she counted, bobbing in the entrance tunnel. Oil lanterns, good ones, harbor-issue. She knew harbor-issue when she saw it. She'd grown up under men who carried them.

"Bell," she breathed, barely voicing it.

"He can't have followed us," Theo whispered. "We watched the shore — there was nobody —"

"There was nobody on the *path*." Marisol eased along the wall, away from the entrance, putting stone between herself and the light. "But the tide's out further than it's been in a generation, girl. There's a whole new shoreline down there. And Bell's been harbormaster long enough to know exactly what an impossible low tide means." She heard Paloma come up beside her in the dark, silent as ballast. "He's not here for the cave. He's here to make sure whatever's in it stays his secret or nobody's."

Through the gap between two flowstone ribs, Marisol watched the lights enter the outer chamber. Three men. She recognized the big one — Bell's wharf rat, Corben, who broke strikes and fingers in equal proportion. They moved carefully, sweeping their lanterns across the walls, and she saw the moment the light found the names, saw Corben stop dead, saw him raise his lamp higher and higher trying to find the top of the columns.

"What is it?" one of the others whispered.

"Names," Corben said. His voice bounced around the chamber, huge with echo. "Walls full of — captain, it's names. Hundreds of 'em."

"That's not what Bell sent us for. Find the strongroom. Old man Voss kept a vault past the galleries — iron door, brass fittings. Move."

*Iron door.* Marisol's heart went cold and quick. She'd heard that phrase her whole childhood — *the iron door, the iron door* — family legend, half joke, the place Aurelio supposedly kept the real fortune. She'd assumed it was a story adults told children to keep them from asking about money. She had not assumed it was *forty meters ahead of her, past an archway she'd already found.*

They had maybe ten minutes before the searchers worked their way back here. Less, if the big man was competent, and he moved like he was.

She turned to the other two. In the starless dark their faces were just shapes, but she could feel Paloma thinking — could practically hear the machinery of her, running routes and odds the way she read water.

"Slack ends soon," Marisol murmured. "When it turns, the entrance floods to the ceiling in under six minutes. We go out through the water or not at all."

"Theo stays put," Paloma breathed. "He knows these walls better than they do now. He keeps the notebooks flat and quiet. You and I make noise on the far side — draw them deep, toward the arch, away from the entrance. Then we swim."

"You're volunteering me for night swimming in a flooding cave."

"I'm volunteering you for the only swim you've trained your whole life for, and I'm coming with you, which makes it a party."

Despite everything — despite the lights creeping closer, despite the iron door waiting beyond the arch like a held breath — Marisol felt something crack open in her chest that might have been laughter. Two days. She'd known this woman two days, and already Paloma Dunmore talked like they'd shared a boat for years.

"All right, Captain," she whispered. "On my count."

Behind them, Theo's hand found her sleeve in the dark and squeezed once. *Go,* the squeeze said. *I'll keep the names safe.*

The lanterns rounded the last flowstone rib. Marisol counted three, two—

And Paloma, instead of slipping away into the side passage as planned, stepped out into the open chamber, lifted her lantern high, and said, in a voice built for shouting across water:

"Gentlemen. You're standing in the middle of the evidence."

* * *

**Theo**

Theo pressed himself into the hollow behind the manifest wall with the notebooks against his chest and listened to history happen.

Paloma's voice filled the chamber the way it must have filled a hundred decks — level, unhurried, carrying. "Every name on these walls is a debt Aurelio Voss never paid. Bell knows it. That's why he doubled my repair quote and offered me night work. That's why he sent *you*." A pause, and then, almost gently: "Corben, isn't it? You broke the cannery strike at Saltmarsh in '09. Men who break strikes for a living ought to know better than anyone what those walls are saying."

Silence. Long enough that Theo's heart hurt with it.

Then Corben's voice, smaller than Theo had ever heard a big man's voice: "My granddad's name is on the *Sea-Lark*."

Nobody answered that. Nobody needed to. Theo heard the lanterns shuffle, heard one of the other men say "Captain said—" and get talked over, heard boots turn — *toward the entrance*, away from the arch, away from the iron door — and then Paloma's voice again, following them out, giving them the dignity of retreat:

"Tide's turning, boys. Swim fast."

Theo waited in the dark until the last light died in the entrance tunnel, until Marisol's whistle sounded soft from the flooded passage, until he'd stuffed the notebooks into the oilskin pouch and slung it tight. Then he ran for the water, and the water was rising, cold climbing his chest as he plunged through the entrance tunnel where the ceiling came down to meet the flood —

— and then he was through, out, gasping in the gray afternoon light of the drained bay, hauling himself onto the rocks beside Paloma and Marisol while behind them the cave mouth swallowed itself, black water pouring into the keyhole like ink into a well.

For a moment none of them spoke. The bay lay flat and shining. Far across the mudflats, three tiny figures were picking their way toward Landfall, walking fast, not looking back.

"They'll tell Bell," Marisol said at last.

"They'll tell Bell their granddad's name is on the wall," Paloma corrected. She was wringing out her sleeves, and there was something in her face Theo had never seen there before — something that looked almost like hope wearing a disguise. "Whether that's good news for us depends entirely on whether Bell believes his own men."

"And the iron door," Theo said. He pulled the oilskin pouch around and patted it. "It's real. Marisol — the stories were real. Past the arch. We saw the fittings through the gap before the lights came."

Marisol stared at the drowned cave mouth for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was steady, but her eyes weren't.

"Then we have four weeks," she said, "until the dry season closes the tides. One window. And Paloma — Bell doesn't send three men to a cave he thinks is empty." She turned, and the last light of the day caught the water on her face and made it look, for a second, like she was crying. "He sends three men to a cave he's already decided to seal."
