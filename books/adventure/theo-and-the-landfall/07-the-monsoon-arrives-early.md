## 7. The Monsoon Arrives Early

The first sign was the birds.

Paloma had spent thirty years reading water, but she'd learned long ago that the water only told you what it intended. The birds told you what the sky intended, and the sky always got its way in the end. That morning the egrets had gone silent and inland, all at once, like a congregation called by a bell no one else could hear. The frigatebirds that usually wheeled over Landfall's harbor in lazy spirals had climbed so high they were specks, then vanished entirely.

She stood on the Kestrel's aft deck with her coffee going cold and watched the horizon to the southeast, where a bruise-colored line had begun to assemble itself out of nothing. It didn't move like weather. Weather drifted. This *built* — stacking itself cloud on cloud, patient as masonry, a wall going up between Landfall and the rest of the world.

"Captain." Theo came up the companionway with the chart case under his arm and his hair still wet from the dock pump. He'd been scrubbing the deck plates since dawn, working off some of the restless energy that had lived in him since the tide went out too far and showed them all the cave. "Marisol's at the harbor office. She says the barometer's dropped forty points since midnight."

"I know."

"She says that's—"

"I said I know, Theo."

He shut up, but he didn't leave, and after a moment Paloma sighed and gestured him over to the rail. She pointed the neck of her bottle at the southeastern sky.

"What do you see?"

He squinted. "Clouds."

"What kind of clouds?"

"The... tall kind?"

"The tall kind." She nodded slowly. "Cumulonimbus, if you want the book word. But it's not the clouds themselves that matter. It's how they're standing. See how straight the leading edge is? Like somebody drew a line with a ruler?" She traced it in the air. "That's not a storm front, boy. That's a wall. Storms drift through. Walls come to stay."

"How long?"

"If it's what I think it is? Three days before it lands. Then four, five days of it sitting on top of us, wringing itself out." She took a swallow of cold coffee and grimaced. "Monsoon season. Except it wasn't supposed to start for another three weeks. The almanac says—"

"The almanac also said the tide would be at two feet this morning," Theo said quietly, "and it was at minus six."

Paloma looked at him. He'd grown up fast in the last week; there was something in his face now that hadn't been there when she'd fished him off a dock in Corvane six months ago — something steadier, and sadder, and older. The cave had done it. Or maybe the town had. Either way, he was right, and they both knew it.

"The almanac's dead wrong about everything this year," she said. "Which means the monsoon isn't coming early because the calendar slipped. It's coming early because the whole season has shifted, and nobody knows by how much." She turned back to the wall of cloud. "And that, Theo, is a problem. Because we have a hull with a bad seam, a repair bill I can't pay, a harbormaster who wants me arrested or bought — possibly both — and now a clock that just started running whether we're ready or not."

* * *

By noon the wind had arrived ahead of the rain, the way it always did — a hot, dry, pushing wind out of the southeast that smelled faintly of ozone and made the harbor chop stand up in short, angry peaks. The fishing fleet had all come in by ten. Paloma counted them from the wheelhouse: eleven boats, twelve, thirteen, every one of them local, every one of them tied down with double lines and storm hitches, their owners moving with the unhurried urgency of people who had done this drill a hundred times and knew exactly how long it took.

The Kestrel was the only boat in the harbor still flying a repair flag.

Paloma stood in the shipwright's yard at the head of the tidal flats and listened to old Ferro Delacruz deliver the worst news of the season with the gentle apologetic tone of a man announcing someone else's funeral.

"—and that's the truth of it, Captain. I'd take her tomorrow if I could. You know I would. But the dry dock's got the *Marta Cielo* in it, and the *Marta Cielo*'s got a cracked keel shoe, and I can't pull her out until the new casting comes up from Solano, and the new casting can't come up from Solano because the river road washed out in the spring floods and won't be rebuilt until—" He spread his hands. "Until the rains end."

"When do the rains end?"

Ferro looked at her with genuine pity. "October. Maybe November, if it's a heavy year."

"September," Paloma said flatly. "It's September."

"And the monsoon's here three weeks early." Ferro shrugged, a small defeated motion. "Everything's early this year, Captain. The tide. The storm. Even the fish ran early — ask any of the net men. The whole delta's shifted somehow, and none of us knows why." He glanced toward the cliffs east of town, where the cave mouth had shown itself and then been swallowed again by the returning water. "Some say it's the Voss fortune stirring. Some say it's a curse. Me, I say it's weather, and weather doesn't care what we say about it."

Paloma pressed her palms flat against the yard's splintery counter and made herself breathe. Behind Ferro, through the open doors, she could see the dry dock: a great timber cradle half-full of river water, and in it the *Marta Cielo*, squat and wounded, waiting for a part that might as well be on the moon.

"What are my options?"

"Options?" Ferro scratched under his cap. "Well. There's careening — beaching her on the flats at spring low and patching the seam from outside. But the tides are all wrong for it now, and even if they weren't, a careened hull in monsoon surge is a hull that gets broken worse than it started. There's the harbor pool — you could anchor in the lee of the breakwater and ride it out, but that seam of yours will open under that kind of pounding, guaranteed. Or..." He hesitated.

"Or?"

"Or you run for it. Up the Serrano channel, into the mangroves. There are backwaters in there deep enough to float a boat your size, sheltered enough that a hurricane couldn't reach you. But you'd need a pilot who knows the channels, and the channels shift every flood, and—" He stopped, because Paloma had already gone very still.

"I know the mangroves," she said.

"You'd need more than knowing, Captain. You'd need luck."

"I've never once had luck, Ferro. I've had competence, and competence has kept me alive this long." She pushed off from the counter. "How much time do I have before this thing makes land?"

Ferro walked to the door and studied the sky with the professional detachment of a man who had watched fifty seasons come and go. The wall of cloud had doubled in height since morning. Its base had gone a strange greenish gray, and beneath it the sea had flattened into an oily, glassy calm — the sea holding its breath.

"Wind starts tonight," he said. "Real wind, not this. Rain behind it by dawn. And the main body of it—" he shook his head slowly, "—tomorrow night. Maybe the night after. If I were you, Captain, I'd make my decision by dark."

* * *

She made it by mid-afternoon, which was to say she made it by refusing to make it, which was its own kind of decision and the worst one available.

The problem was arithmetic, and Paloma Dunmore had never in her life been able to make the numbers work. Running for the mangroves meant abandoning the harbor — abandoning the mooring she'd fought Bell over, abandoning the repair yard, abandoning the town where the manifests were hidden and Marisol was diving and Theo had, God help her, started putting down roots. It meant becoming a fugitive in fact rather than merely in reputation, fleeing a storm instead of facing it, and it meant doing it with a hull that might open up entirely in the Serrano's shifting channels.

Staying meant riding out a monsoon in an open roadstead with a leaking seam, in a harbor controlled by a man who wanted her gone or broken, in a town where half the population now knew about the cave and the other half was arguing about who owned it.

She stood in the wheelhouse with the chart spread out before her and did the arithmetic anyway, pencil scratching, numbers refusing to cooperate. Fuel for the run upchannel: yes, barely. Draft clearance in the Serrano at current tide: marginal, and falling. Time to beat the storm's leading edge: possible, if she left within two hours. Probability of the seam holding: she wrote down a number, crossed it out, wrote a smaller one, crossed that out too.

Theo found her there an hour later, still standing over the same chart, the pencil worn down to a stub.

"We're staying," he said.

It wasn't a question, and that was what made her look up sharply. "What gave you that idea?"

"You sharpened the pencil twice and haven't erased anything. When you're actually deciding, you erase. When you've already decided and you're pretending otherwise, you just... keep writing." He leaned against the doorframe. "Marisol dove the outer chamber this morning. Before the swell came up. She says the inner passage is flooded higher than last week — the whole cave system's filling with the seasonal water table. If we want the ledgers, we dive during the eye of the first storm, when the pressure drops and the water table sucks back for a few hours. That's our window. Our only window. And it's in the middle of the monsoon."

"So we stay for the storm, and then we dive in the eye of it."

"That's the plan."

"That's not a plan, Theo, that's a suicide note with extra steps."

"Maybe." He came into the wheelhouse and put his hand flat on the chart, over the little penciled cross that marked the Kestrel's anchorage. "But it's ours. And Captain — the harbor's the safest place for the boat anyway. Breakwater lee, good holding ground, and every fishing family in Landfall riding it out alongside us. Whatever else Bell is, he's not going to let the harbor fleet sink in a monsoon. Not with the whole town watching. We're safer inside his jurisdiction than outside it right now, and I hate that as much as you do."

Paloma stared at the chart until the lines blurred. He was right. That was the worst part — the boy was right, and she hated being cornered by correctness almost as much as she hated being cornered by Bell.

"Double anchors," she said finally. "Bow and stern, plus a third off the quarter as a kellet. Storm hatches dogged, everything below lashed or emptied. We pump the bilge on rotation, four hours on, four hours off, starting tonight. And if that seam opens—"

"It won't."

"It opened last time, Theo. In a *squall*. This is a monsoon."

"Then we patch it again." He said it simply, without bravado, the way you'd say *the sun will rise*, and something in his certainty frightened her more than the storm did. Because she remembered being that young and that certain, and she remembered exactly what the sea had done with her certainty, and she opened her mouth to tell him so—

—and the first rain hit the wheelhouse windows like a handful of thrown gravel.

* * *

It came in sideways, warm as bathwater and hard as hail, and within twenty minutes the harbor had dissolved into a gray roaring chaos where the breakwater was a rumor and the anchored fleet swung at their cables like ghosts on strings. Paloma stood her first watch alone in the wheelhouse while Theo and Marisol slept in shifts below, and she watched the fishing boats materialize and vanish in the rain-squalls, each one a dim lantern swinging in the void, and she felt the claustrophobia settle over her like a wet coat.

This was the thing nobody understood about storms, she thought. People imagined openness — big sky, big sea, room to run. But a real storm was the opposite. A real storm was walls. Walls of rain, walls of wind, walls of sound so total that your own thoughts got crushed against them. The world shrank to the size of your hull, and the hull shrank to the size of your next decision, and the next decision shrank to the size of your next breath. There was nowhere to go. There was only here, and now, and the water coming in faster than you could send it out.

At midnight she went below and woke Theo for his watch, and by the light of the single swinging lantern she checked the bilge herself before turning in. Two inches of water. An hour ago it had been one. The seam was weeping again — not open, not yet, but weeping, a thin bright thread of harbor water finding its way through the caulking along the garboard strake, persistent as a debt collector.

She stood in the dripping dark of the engine space with her hand pressed against the hull, feeling the sea push against the planking like a living thing, and she thought: *this is what it costs. Every place I've ever stopped, every harbor I've ever trusted, it ends with me standing in a leaking boat listening to the water come in.*

The thought should have made her want to run. Thirty years of running had been built on thoughts exactly like it. But standing there in the dark, with the storm hammering the deck above and two people asleep in the cabin who had, without asking, become hers to protect — she found the old instinct wouldn't fire. The engine of escape turned over and caught and then died, caught and died, like an motor with bad fuel.

Because there was nowhere to run *to*. That was the truth of it. The warrant would follow. Bell would follow. The ledgers were here, Marisol's claim was here, and Theo's ridiculous, stubborn, growing roots were here. Landfall had become the center of the compass whether she liked it or not, and every direction outward was just a longer way around to the same point.

She climbed back up to the wheelhouse and relieved Theo after all, sending him grumbling back to his bunk, and she stood the middle watch alone with the rain screaming past the glass and the barometer crawling down and down like something dying.

Toward dawn the wind backed two points and built, and the Kestrel began to yaw at her anchors, and Paloma stood at the wheel with her arms locked and her teeth set and rode her out — bow into the chop, fall off, bring her back, bow into the chop — hour after hour, the same three motions, the same three prayers, until her shoulders burned and her eyes streamed and the eastern sky showed nothing at all, no dawn, no color, just a slightly less black version of the same gray roar.

And in the middle of that endless gray, the bilge alarm sounded.

Not the steady tick of the weeping seam. The real alarm — the high, insistent shriek she'd rigged herself two years ago and never once heard until now. She tore below with the lantern, braced herself against the heave of the deck, and threw open the engine space hatch.

Water to her knees. Cold, black, rising as she watched — a full inch while she stood there, gushing now, not seeping, the seam split along a hand's width of strake somewhere below the waterline where no lamp could reach and no hand could follow.

Above her, through the deck, she could hear the storm building toward its full voice, and the harbor was a trap with the walls closing, and the dry dock was full, and the rains would not end until October, and the eye — Marisol's window, their one chance at the ledgers — was still a day away.

Paloma put her lantern down on a shelf, pulled off her jacket, and waded in.

"Get up!" she shouted at the deck above her, at the sleeping cabin, at the boy and the stranger who had become her crew. "Both of you! Get up and get down here — we're taking water!"

The storm answered her, a long rolling boom of thunder like a door slamming shut at the far end of the world, and the bilgewater rose another inch around her waist, cold and certain as the future.
