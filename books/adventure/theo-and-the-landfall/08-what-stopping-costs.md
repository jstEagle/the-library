## 8. What Stopping Costs

The rain had found a rhythm by the third day of the monsoon's first week — not the violent drumming Theo had expected from a proper storm season, but something steadier, almost patient, like a hand tapping on a shoulder that wouldn't stop until you turned around. He'd grown up in towns where rain was an event, something you ran through and laughed about. Here in Landfall it was a condition. It got into everything: the salt in the air went sweet with rot, the market planks swelled so the boards buckled like ribs, and the whole town seemed to hunch its shoulders against a sky that had decided to stay.

Theo didn't mind it. That surprised him.

He sat under the awning of the chandlery where he was supposed to be inventorying coils of rope for Señora Ibarra, who paid him in coffee and gossip instead of coin because the Kestrel's repair account had gone from thin to imaginary since Harbormaster Bell's men had "reassessed" the mooring fees. The rope work took his hands; his eyes kept drifting across the flooded street to the dry-dock slip where the Kestrel sat propped on cribbing like a patient waiting for a surgeon who kept canceling.

She looked wrong out of the water. He knew that was a silly thing to feel about a boat, but there it was. The Kestrel was a riverboat the way some people were river people — she only made sense moving. On land her hull showed every scar, every patch of old caulking gone gray, the long gouge from the sandbar that Paloma had threaded them off in the dark their first night here. Theo had run his palm along that gouge yesterday and felt how deep it went. Not through the planking. But close. Close enough that Paloma had stood at the waterline for a long time saying nothing, which for Paloma Dunmore was the same as screaming.

"You're staring again," said Señora Ibarra without looking up from her ledger. She was seventy if she was a day and could splice wire cable with her teeth, or so she claimed, and Theo believed her. "Staring doesn't caulk seams."

"I know."

"Your captain knows it too. That's why she's been down at the harbormaster's office twice today pretending she isn't begging." The old woman made a mark in her ledger with a pencil worn to a stub. "Bell enjoys the pretending. Makes him feel generous."

Theo turned a coil over in his hands. Manila hemp, good stuff, worth more than he'd earn in a month. "Do you think we'll get out of here?"

Señora Ibarra finally looked up. Her eyes were pale as shallow water and missed nothing. "That depends on what you mean by *out*. Boats leave Landfall all the time. People are different." She tapped the ledger. "People either arrive or they stay. This town eats the ones who try to do both."

He wanted to ask what she meant, but the awning gutter chose that moment to overflow and dump a cold sheet of runoff down his collar, and by the time he'd stopped swearing and wrung out his shirt, the old woman had moved on to berating a delivery boy about tarred twine, and the moment had closed like a door.

Still, the question stayed with him all afternoon, riding along while he carried rope and drank too-sweet coffee and watched the rain stitch silver threads into the harbor water. *Do you think we'll get out of here?* He'd asked it casually, but underneath it was another question he hadn't let himself say out loud, not even to himself, because saying it would make it real and wanting things made them hurt when they broke:

*Are we going to stay?*

Because Theo had done the arithmetic that Paloma refused to do. The Kestrel needed three hundred in repairs minimum, probably four. They had sixty-one dollars and change in the tin box bolted under the wheelhouse floor. Even if Bell dropped the mooring fees tomorrow — which he wouldn't, because Bell was the kind of man who fed on leverage the way lampreys fed on fish — even then, they couldn't pay. Which meant the Kestrel would sit in the slip until the yard sold her for scrap to cover the debt, and then Paloma would walk down the dock with one bag, the way she always did, and Theo would follow, because following Paloma was the only life he had.

And the thing was — the thing that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone — he didn't want to follow anymore. Or rather, he wanted to want it less. Landfall was the first place in two years where anyone had learned his name without him leaving before they could use it. The blind storyteller, Old Ferro, had started calling him *el timonel*, the helmsman, and saved him a seat by the brazier every evening. Marisol had begun correcting his knots instead of just redoing them, which she claimed was a compliment. Even the rain felt like it knew him now.

But Paloma didn't stay anywhere. That wasn't a flaw in her character; it was more like a law of nature, one of those rules you built your whole understanding around, like water being wet. Paloma moved. Two years, eleven ports, and never once more than six weeks in any of them. She had a phrase for it — *the tide turns, we turn with it* — and she'd say it lightly, laughing, already counting lines to cast off, and Theo had laughed along because he'd been fourteen and alone and she'd pulled him off a dock in Puerto Sombra like pulling a drowning kid out of surf, and you don't question the person who saves you.

Except lately, watching her stand at the dry-dock rail looking at the Kestrel's wound like it was her own, he'd started to wonder whether the rule was really a rule. Whether it was more like a scar. And scars, he was learning, had stories behind them.

* * *

He found out the story on a Thursday, though he didn't know what he'd found until long after.

It happened because of the mail skiff. The skiff came upriver twice a week when the water allowed, piloted by a round, cheerful man named Dominguez who treated the mail like cargo and cargo like mail and lost both with equal regularity. That Thursday the water allowed, barely, and the skiff wallowed into the harbor trailing weed, and half of Landfall turned out on the docks because in a town cut off by monsoon, the mail boat was theater, carnival, and news service all in one.

Theo was there to collect the Kestrel's spare parts order — a long shot, since nobody had paid for it — and he was standing near the customs shed when he saw the constable come down the ramp.

He wasn't from Landfall. Theo could tell by the uniform, a darker blue, and by the way he walked like a man on duty rather than a man wading through mud. He carried a leather document case and a paper-wrapped parcel, and he stopped the first person he saw — a fishwife Theo half-knew — and asked her something, and she pointed.

She pointed at Paloma.

Paloma was twenty feet away, at the chandlery counter visible through the open doors, arguing with Señora Ibarra about the price of oakum. Theo saw the constable look at her, and then look again, and then do something strange: he put the document case back inside his coat. All the way back inside, out of sight, like a man deciding not to show his cards yet. Then he bought a cup of coffee from the cart by the ramp and stood drinking it, watching the chandlery door, patient as the rain.

Theo's stomach did something complicated.

He told himself it was nothing. A warrant officer upriver checking manifests, maybe. Bell's paperwork, catching up. But he'd spent two years learning to read Paloma Dunmore the way she read water, and when she came out of the chandlery ten minutes later with a roll of oakum under her arm, she paused on the threshold — just a half-second, a hitch no one else would notice — and her eyes swept the dock once, quick, cataloguing. And they passed over the constable in the dark blue and came back to him and stayed there for exactly one heartbeat too long.

Then she turned and walked the other way, toward the cliffs, unhurried, a woman taking a stroll in the rain.

Theo followed her. He didn't decide to; his legs just did it.

She took the switchback path above the harbor, the one that climbed toward the cliff road, and the rain thickened as they rose until the town below became a smear of lamplight and gray water. She stopped at the overlook where the mangrove channels fanned out below like green veins, and she stood looking at nothing, and when Theo came up beside her she didn't act surprised, which somehow made it worse.

"Rope inventory go slow?" she said.

"Señora Ibarra pays in coffee."

"Mm." She handed him the oakum roll to carry, automatic, the way she'd handed him things since Puerto Sombra. For a while neither of them spoke. Below, the mail skiff was warping out again, its wake flattening in the rain.

"There's a man on the dock," Theo said. "Dark blue uniform. Upriver cut. He's been watching the chandlery since the skiff came in."

Paloma didn't move. Rain ran off the brim of her hat in a beaded curtain. "I saw him."

"How long have you known he was coming?"

That got her. Not much — a tightening at the jaw, a fractional shift of weight — but Theo had two years of practice reading small things. "Since the last skiff," she said finally. "Dominguez carries more than letters. Somebody upriver pays him to report who's where."

"Who? Who's upriver?"

"The past," Paloma said, and almost smiled, and it was the worst smile Theo had ever seen on her, because it was the one she used to nail a door shut. "Come on. Weather's turning."

"No." He heard his own voice come out harder than he meant it, and he was fifteen years old and soaked to the skin and suddenly furious in a way that had been building for weeks, since the doubled repair quote, since the sandbar, since every port town where she'd stood at the rail counting days. "No, we're not doing this. You don't get to do this anymore."

"Theo—"

"I live on your boat. I've lived on your boat for two years and I've never once asked you anything, and now there's a man with a document case on that dock and you looked at him like you were looking at a ghost, and I'm asking." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it but he let it crack. "What did you do, Captain?"

For a long moment she just looked at him. Rain drummed on her hat. Below them the harbor lights shivered on black water, and somewhere out in the channel the buoy bell clanged its slow idiot rhythm, and Theo stood his ground with his heart going like a bilge pump.

Then Paloma Dunmore sat down. Right there, on the wet stone of the overlook, hat brim dripping, and looked out at the water, and when she spoke her voice had none of the captain in it at all.

"His name is Emilio Varga," she said. "And I didn't do anything. That's the whole trick of it. I didn't do anything, and it cost me everything anyway."

* * *

She told it plainly, which was somehow worse than if she'd dramatized it. Paloma didn't dramatize; she reported, the way she read soundings, flat numbers over deep water.

"Eleven years ago I ran cargo out of San Baltasar, upriver. You've seen the name on charts — the port with the broken crane. I had a partner. Not a lover, nothing like that. A partner. Rafa Ochoa. We had a boat together, the *Cormorant*, thirty feet of trouble, and we were young and stupid and good at it. Good captains, both of us. Honest ones, mostly. We ran fruit, timber, mail. Whatever paid."

She turned the hat brim in her hands, working it, an old habit Theo had seen a thousand times without ever wondering what it was for.

"Rafa had a brother-in-law. Charming man. Everyone loved him. He came to us with a run — sealed crates, top freight, double rate. Said it was machine parts for the mill at Vado Alto. And I looked at the crates and I looked at Rafa, and Rafa looked at me, and I said no. First time in my life I said no to money, and I said it because the crates were too heavy for machine parts and the manifest was too clean. Clean manifests are like clean alibis, Theo. Nobody writes them unless they need one."

"What was in them?"

"Doesn't matter. That's the point. It never mattered what was in them, because I never carried them. Rafa did. Took the run himself while I was laid up with fever for nine days. Nine days, Theo. I was delirious in a rooming house in San Baltasar while my partner ran contraband on my boat under my name — my registration, my papers, my signature forged on the manifest, because Rafa's handwriting looks enough like mine when you're not looking hard, and the customs man at Vado Alto wasn't looking hard, because Rafa had paid him not to."

The rain filled the pause. Theo sat down slowly beside her on the wet stone. He didn't feel the cold.

"It went wrong, of course. It always goes wrong. Patrol launch at the narrows. Rafa scuttled the crates in the channel and swore blind he'd acted alone, but the paperwork said otherwise. The paperwork said *Paloma Dunmore, master of the Cormorant.* And Rafa —" she stopped, and worked the hat brim, and started again. "Rafa stood in front of the magistrate and cried and said he'd tried to stop me. Said I'd forced him. Said I'd threatened his family. And everyone believed him, because everyone loved Rafa, and nobody loved me, because I was the woman who'd said no to money and then somehow ended up with contraband on her deck anyway, and that's the shape guilt takes when people want it to take a shape. It doesn't fit the truth. It fits the story."

"So there's a warrant."

"There's a warrant. Smuggling, falsified manifest, flight to avoid prosecution. Eleven years old. Every port from San Baltasar to the delta has a copy in a drawer, and most of them have forgotten it, and every few years somebody remembers, and I move." She shrugged, a small terrible shrug. "That's the arithmetic. Six weeks, sometimes eight, and then the tide turns and we turn with it. You thought it was a preference. It's a sentence."

Theo stared at the water. Everything reorganized itself in his head — two years of it, the whole architecture of his life with her, every light departure, every joke about tides, every time she'd refused to learn a shopkeeper's name or let him keep a stray dog for more than a week. He'd built a hundred explanations and torn them down again: she was restless, she was free, she was running from grief, from a dead husband, from debts. He'd never once guessed the truth, which was so much smaller and colder than any of his stories.

*She runs because staying means jail.*

"Why didn't you tell me?" he said. It came out quieter than he intended. "Two years, Captain. I'm not — I'm not your crew. I'm not even really your crew, I'm a kid you pulled off a dock. But I've patched your hull and held your wheel in weather and I never asked you for anything except—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Except to stop. Once. Just once. And you laughed and said the tide turns."

"I know what I said."

"And you were going to let me find out from a constable with a document case. You were going to let them take you off the Kestrel in front of me and I'd find out from Bell, or from a newspaper, or never." His voice shook. "That's the part I can't — you'd have let me think you just left."

"Yes," Paloma said.

No excuse attached. No explanation, no softening. Just *yes*, hanging in the rain between them, and Theo realized with a jolt that it was the most honest thing she'd ever said to him, and possibly the cruelest, and that both of those things could be true of the same person at the same time.

"Why?" he managed.

"Because if you knew, you'd have stayed with the boat when they took me. Or worse, you'd have done something stupid and brave and gotten yourself a file of your own." She finally turned and looked at him, and her eyes in the gray light were the color of the channel before a storm. "You want to know what stopping costs, Theo? It costs exactly this. It costs the people who love you finding out that everything they believed about you was a story you told to keep them safe. Staying means being seen. Being seen means being caught. I have been caught exactly once in my life and it was by a man I trusted with my boat, and I swore it would never happen again, and then—" she gestured, a short helpless motion that took in Theo, the rain, the whole drowned world below them, "—then I went and picked up a deckhand. Because apparently I never learn anything."

"You picked me up because I was drowning."

"I picked you up because you were drowning," she agreed. "And then I kept you, which was the mistake. Cargo you can throw overboard when the patrol comes. People sink the whole boat."

They sat in silence after that. The rain eased, the way it did in the evenings, dropping from a roar to a whisper, and below them the lamps of Landfall burned steady in the wet dark, and the buoy bell counted out the swell. Theo turned it all over, the betrayal and the truth of it, and found — to his own surprise — that the anger was draining out of him and leaving something else behind, something heavier and more durable.

Because he understood it now. That was the awful thing. He understood it completely. If someone had taken the Kestrel from him — forged his name, stolen his boat, made the whole world believe he was the thief — he'd run too. He'd run forever. Running wasn't cowardice. Running was the only honest response to a world that had already decided your story for you.

"You should have told me," he said at last. "Not for the boat. For me. I'd rather know the true Paloma than the story."

Something moved in her face — quickly, gone again, but he saw it. "Careful," she said roughly. "Say things like that and I might start believing I can stay."

"Maybe you can."

"Theo." She said his name the way she said *shoal* — a warning, a depth sounding. "There's a constable downstairs drinking coffee and waiting for his moment. There's a harbormaster who smells blood in the water. There's a warrant with my name on it in a drawer in San Baltasar, and warrants don't expire, they just wait. Staying isn't a choice I get to make. It's a door that's locked, and I lost the key eleven years ago in a rooming house with a fever."

"Keys get found," Theo said.

She laughed — a real one, short and disbelieving — and stood, and pulled her hat down against the drizzle, and for a second she was the captain again, all iron and tide-logic. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we talk to Marisol about the cave, and if the low water holds, we go down and see what the Voss family left in the dark. Evidence changes things. Ledgers change things. Maybe—" she waved a hand at the whole impossible idea, "—maybe paper can do what eleven years of running couldn't."

She started down the switchback. Theo stayed a moment longer at the overlook, looking at the harbor lights, at the customs shed where a man in dark blue was surely still waiting, patient as the rain.

Then he saw it. Down at the ramp, the mail skiff's lanterns swung as Dominguez cast off — early, urgent, out ahead of the weather. And standing at the skiff's rail, talking quietly with the constable in the dark blue uniform, hatless in the drizzle, smiling his slow crocodile smile, was Harbormaster Bell.

Bell had brought the past upriver himself. Bell had fetched it like a tool.

Theo turned and ran down the switchback after Paloma, boots slipping on wet stone, the rain coming back in earnest now, hard and sudden, as if the sky had been waiting for exactly this moment to close its fist.
