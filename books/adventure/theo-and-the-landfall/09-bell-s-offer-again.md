## 9. Bell's Offer, Again

The rain came down like a debt collector.

Paloma stood under the awning of the chandlery on Rope Walk, watching it hammer the flooded street into white noise, and told herself she was only there for the pitch. Pine tar, a coil of oakum, a sheet of copper she couldn't afford and would buy anyway. That was all. The Kestrel's seam had been weeping since the storm warnings went up on the harbor board, a slow dark tongue of water in the bilge that she'd been bailing out of her sleep every night this week, and the monsoon was still three days out by the barometer's sullen fall.

She was counting coins into her palm when the shadow fell across the counter.

"Captain Dunmore."

Harbormaster Bell didn't use umbrellas. He stood in the rain as if it were a formality he'd long since stopped acknowledging, his oilskin collar beaded silver, his face dry where it mattered — around the eyes, which never got wet at all. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges the way driftwood does, and he carried himself with the particular patience of someone who owned the dock you were tied to.

"Bell." She turned back to the chandlery's boy. "The tar, the oakum. Hold the copper."

"Hold nothing," Bell said pleasantly. "Put it on my account, Tomás."

The boy looked at Paloma. She looked at the copper. The Kestrel needed the copper. That was the whole architecture of the man, she thought — he built his traps out of things you already needed.

"I don't take credit," she said.

"It isn't credit." Bell smiled with the bottom half of his face only. "It's an advance against services rendered. Walk with me, Captain. Ten minutes of your time, and the copper rides home on your shoulder."

* * *

They walked up Rope Walk together through the rain, past the shuttered salt sheds and the net lofts dripping their lines like sad beards, and Bell talked about nothing with the ease of a man laying cards face down one at a time. The price of diesel. The new customs schedule. How the mail skiff from upriver had come in Tuesday, riding low, how the constable aboard had spent a night at the Anchorline drinking rum he hadn't paid for and talking about paperwork.

Paloma kept her stride even. Her heart did not.

"You know," Bell said, as they came up under the eaves of the customs house, where the rain broke apart above them into a gray curtain, "in thirty years of running this port I've learned there are only two kinds of boat people. The ones who want to stay, and the ones who can't afford to. You've been telling everyone you're the second kind. Leaky hull, empty purse, first fair wind south." He stopped beneath the customs house lamp, which buzzed and threw his shadow long across the wet boards. "But here's what interests me. The wind's been fair twice since you grounded, and you're still here."

"The repairs aren't done."

"No," Bell agreed. "They're not. Funny thing about that."

He reached inside his coat — slowly, theatrically, a man who wanted to be watched doing it — and drew out not a weapon but a folder. Cheap paper, swollen at the edges from river damp, tied with the red string the upriver courts used. He held it out flat on his palm between them like a card he was letting her read.

She didn't touch it. She'd seen enough warrants in her life to recognize one by its weight alone, and this one had her name on it. She knew without looking. She'd known since the mail skiff came in, since the constable drank free rum and talked too loud, since the cold feeling had settled into her chest Tuesday night like ballast she couldn't pump out. A port town upriver. A partner named Edda Vane who'd signed Paloma's name to manifests full of untaxed cargo while Paloma slept, and then vanished south with the profits, leaving Paloma holding both the boat and the blame. Eight years ago now. She'd been running ever since — not from the law, exactly. From the certainty that the law was a door that only opened inward.

"Smuggling," Bell said, reading her silence instead of the paper. "Conspiracy. A two-year sentence if they take you alive and polite about it. More if you run and they have to chase." He tucked the folder away again, unhurried. "Now. Here's the part where you ask me why I'm showing you this instead of just handing you over."

"Why are you showing me this instead of just handing me over?"

"Because handing you over gets me a constable's thank-you and a line in the ledger." He leaned back against the customs house post, arms folded, rain streaming off the eave between them like a bead curtain. "But the Voss cave gets me a future. And you, Captain Dunmore, are currently the only person in this town who's been inside it."

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere down the hill, the harbor boards knocked against each other in the swell, a sound like knuckles on a door.

"There were no manifests," Paloma said. "We looked. There's a chamber with carvings on the walls, names cut into stone, and then the tide came back and we left. That's all."

"Names cut into stone." Bell nodded slowly, as though she'd confirmed something. "Aurelio Voss didn't carve those names for decoration. Every man and woman he stole a fleet from is listed there — and more than names, if Marisol Voss is half the researcher her grandfather feared. Ledgers. Contracts. Confessions, maybe. Paper that says the great Aurelio Voss built his empire on crews he never paid and families he drowned in debt." His voice stayed level, almost bored, but his eyes had gone bright and fixed. "Paper like that doesn't stay buried once it surfaces. It gets read. It gets argued over in courtrooms for a decade. It makes lawyers rich and harbormasters poor, because do you know what happens to Landfall when the Voss fortune gets redistributed? The memorial commission comes. The surveyors. The historians. They'll want a new pier, a proper customs station, oversight." He spread his hands. "Oversight, Captain. Me, thirty years of running this port my way, ended by a stack of soggy paper some dead man hid in a hole in a cliff."

"So you want it destroyed."

"I want it *managed*." The word came out quick and sharp, the first honest edge she'd heard from him. Then he smoothed it over again, patient as tide. "I want it brought to me, quietly, before it becomes everyone's business. And in exchange—" he tapped his coat, where the folder sat, "—this goes in my stove tonight. Your warrant dies unopened. Your repair bills go back to what they were before you made the mistake of refusing me the first time. And when the monsoon passes, the Kestrel sails south with a clean hull and a clean captain, and nobody in Landfall remembers your name."

* * *

That was the trap laid out whole, and Paloma stood inside it and looked around, because the terrible thing — the thing she would not say aloud, not to him, not ever — was that it was a good offer.

That was what she hated most about men like Bell. Not the cruelty; cruelty she understood, cruelty was just fear wearing boots. What she hated was the arithmetic. She ran the numbers the way she ran a channel in bad light, honestly, because lying to yourself on the water got you drowned. Two years in an upriver cell, maybe four. The Kestrel impounded or rotted at a mooring no one paid on. Theo — sixteen years old, all elbows and questions, who'd followed her aboard two seasons ago with a duffel bag and a look like she was something worth following — left standing on a dock with nothing but a story about a captain who'd gotten herself caged. Against that: a folder in a stove. A clean hull. The open sea, and the old life, the running life, the one that had never once actually saved her but had at least kept her moving fast enough to feel like freedom.

Justice inside the law. She'd tried it. She could count on one hand the times she'd trusted a court, a constable, a contract, and she still had fingers left over. Edda Vane had sworn testimony would clear her — *tell them the truth, Paloma, they'll see you're innocent* — and the truth had taken eight years to catch up with her anyway, and it hadn't caught Edda at all. The law worked late, worked slow, and worked mostly for whoever paid its fees. Bell was offering her the same deal the world had always offered her: your honor for sale, cheap, and everyone pretending the price was generous.

And the other side of the scale. Marisol Voss, standing hip-deep in the drained bay with her grandmother's name carved in stone above her head, saying *they were owed*. Theo, who photographed the cave mouth with the reverence other boys reserved for saints. A drowned town's forgotten families, waiting a hundred years for somebody to carry their names into the light. If she handed Bell the manifests, that light died in a stove on a rainy night, and the Voss families stayed robbed forever, and Paloma Dunmore would sail south free and know, every day of every year, exactly what her freedom had cost.

She found, examining herself with a captain's cold honesty, that she already knew what she was going to do. Had known since the folder came out of the coat. But Bell was watching her face the way he watched the harbor bar, reading every ripple, and a good captain did not show the water her course.

So she let herself consider it. Genuinely. That was the trick of surviving men like Bell — you didn't refuse them fast, because fast refusals told them where your price wasn't, and they'd keep digging until they found where it was. You considered. You made them sweat a little. You made them believe the hook might hold.

"How do I know you'll burn it?" she said. "Paper changes hands. Warrants get copies."

Bell's smile widened a fraction. "You don't know. Same as I don't know you won't copy those manifests before you bring them to me. That's what makes it a bargain, Captain. Trust, between professionals." He pushed off the post and stepped closer, under the eave, close enough that she could smell wet wool and the tobacco he chewed and never spat in public. "You have until the eye of the storm. When the monsoon hits, the bar closes, the boats tie down, and after it passes, the dry season seals that cave for another generation. One dive window, Captain. The eye. Six hours of still air, maybe less. Bring me the paper during the eye, and by the time the rain starts again you're a ghost with a clean name."

"And if I bring you nothing?"

"Then when the rains end, the mail skiff comes back, and this time the constable won't stop for rum first." He said it gently, almost kindly, which was somehow worse. "I'd hate that. Truly. You strike me as a woman who's paid enough."

* * *

He walked her back down Rope Walk through the rain, because of course he did; he wanted to be seen with her, wanted the town to watch the outsider woman and the harbormaster talking under one eave, wanted the seed of *she's negotiating* planted in every window they passed. At the chandlery he paid Tomás for the tar and the oakum and the copper with notes counted out slow, and touched the brim of nothing, since he wore no hat, in a gesture that managed to be one anyway.

"Until the eye," he said.

"Until the eye," Paloma agreed, and took her copper, and felt it heavy on her shoulder all the way down to the water.

The Kestrel rode her mooring in the graying afternoon, decks slicked, rigging singing a low note in the building wind. Theo was below, by the sound of it, arguing cheerfully with the bilge pump, and Marisol's gear hung drying in the cockpit like shed skin. Paloma stowed the copper without a word, went aft, and stood at the wheel though there was nowhere to steer, looking out at the harbor where the water was already climbing the pilings, restless, brown, rehearsing.

The barometer had fallen another tenth. The monsoon was coming early. She could feel it in the halyards, in the ache of an old break in her left wrist, in the way the gulls had gone silent and inland.

One dive window. The eye. Bell thought he'd given her a deadline because deadlines broke people, because a woman with a warrant and a leaking boat would always, always choose the stove and the open sea.

He didn't know her. Nobody ever had, really — that was the price of the running life, and she'd paid it gladly for twenty years. But standing there at the wheel of the Kestrel with the first fat drops of the true rain starting to ring on the deck around her, Paloma admitted the truth she'd been circling for days: somewhere between the sandbar and the cave and the girl with her grandmother's name in stone, running had stopped being the plan. She just hadn't told her own body yet.

Below her feet, faint under the wind, the bilge pump changed its rhythm — working harder now, sucking longer between strokes.

The seam had opened a little more.

The rain came down like a debt collector, and this time it was calling on her.
