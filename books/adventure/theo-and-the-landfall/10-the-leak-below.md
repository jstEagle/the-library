## 10. The Leak Below

The storm did not arrive so much as it arrived *at* them, the way a debt collector arrives at a door — with weight, with purpose, with no interest in what you were doing before it knocked.

Theo felt the change in the Kestrel's bones before he heard it. The old riverboat had been moored in Landfall's harbor for three days now, riding out the season's first true monsoon blow, and she had been complaining the whole time — groaning at her lines, ticking as the rain hammered her deck, muttering in that language of stressed timber that Paloma claimed to understand fluently. But at some hour past midnight, in the black heart of the gale, the complaints changed key. They went from grumbling to grieving.

Theo woke in his bunk in the forward crew cabin with water slapping cold against his shoulder.

At first he thought he was dreaming of the river. Then the slap came again, deliberate, insistent, and he rolled out of his bunk into six inches of bilgewater that hadn't been there when he'd gone to sleep. The lantern hanging from its gimbal was swinging wild arcs, throwing shadows like fleeing birds across the bulkheads.

"Captain!" His voice cracked on the second syllable. He tried again, louder, bare feet splashing toward the companionway. "CAPTAIN PALOMA!"

He found her already on deck, or rather he found the aftermath of her having been on deck — the hatch banging, rain coming through sideways, and Paloma herself hauling herself back down the ladder with her oilskin streaming and her face set in that expression Theo had learned to fear more than any storm: the flat, measuring look of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"Bilge pump's running," she said, over the wind. "It's losing."

That was all she said. That was enough.

* * *

The leak was in the port side, aft, along a seam that had been weeping since the sandbar outside Landfall had chewed the Kestrel's hull — the damage from chapter one of their misfortunes, the wound they'd patched with putty and prayer because neither of them could afford real repairs. Harbormaster Bell's doubled quote had seen to that. Three days of rain and swell had softened the putty, worked at the caulking like a patient thief picking a lock, and now the lock was open.

Paloma got the lantern down into the hold and held it low over the water, and Theo saw it: a seam maybe two feet long, just above the turn of the bilge, breathing seawater in pulses. Each time the hull flexed on a swell, the gap widened, gulped, and closed. In, out. Like the boat was drowning slowly and couldn't decide whether to hurry.

"That's not a weep anymore," Paloma said quietly. "That's a drink."

"What do we do?"

"We do what boats have always been fixed by. We get underneath it and we push back." She was already moving, pulling the spare canvas from the locker, the soft pine plugs, the tallow and oakum, the hammer. Her hands knew where everything was without looking, and watching her, Theo felt the strange calm that always came off her in crisis — the same weary competence she'd shown on the sandbar, except now there was something under it, something he'd only recently learned to see. Fear. Not of the water. Of stopping. Of being stopped.

"Theo." She pressed the hammer into his hand and looked at him, rain still dripping off her chin inside the hold, her voice pitched under the storm's roar so he had to lean close to hear it. "I can't leave the wheel. If I let her swing, she goes onto the breakwater rocks, and then none of this matters. You and Marisol patch. I steer. Nobody stops until she's holding. Understood?"

"Understood."

"And Theo—" For half a heartbeat the arithmetic left her face, and something else was there, something older and rawer. "Don't be brave. Be careful. Bravery is how boats lose people."

Then she was gone up the ladder, and the hatch slammed behind her, and Theo stood chest-deep in the rising dark and understood that the captain had just told him, in her way, that she trusted him with her ship.

* * *

Marisol Voss arrived ten minutes later, which should have been impossible, which meant she had been watching the Kestrel all along.

She came down the ladder in a fisherman's slicker with her dive bag over one shoulder and her braid plastered to her neck, and she took one look at the waterline climbing the stringers and said, "How long has the pump been losing?"

"Since midnight," Theo shouted over the hull-groan.

"Then you've got an hour, maybe two, before it finds the floorboards." She dropped the bag, shrugged out of the slicker, and waded toward him without another word, and Theo had a moment — absurd, in the middle of all that terror — of noticing how completely unafraid she looked. Not reckless. Unafraid, the way deep water is unafraid. Cave divers, he was learning, made a religion of fear; they didn't banish it, they *organized* it, filed every terror into its proper drawer so their hands stayed free to work.

She organized his fear too. "Canvas first," she said, pressing the folded square against the seam while he fumbled with it. "Hold it flat. The pressure wants to suck it in — let it. That's your plug until we can drive the oakum. Now the chisel — there, your left — wedge it under the seam lip, gently, gently, you're opening a mouth, not breaking a jaw."

The work was brutal and intimate and nothing like anything Theo had ever done. The bilgewater rose past his waist, then his ribs, black and cold and full of floating debris — a boot, a coil of line, an empty oil can turning lazily like a little moon. Every swell that hit the hull drove a fresh pulse through the seam, and each pulse came harder than the last, as if the sea had noticed them and taken it personally. The lantern swung. The timbers screamed. Somewhere above them, faint through the deck, they could hear Paloma's voice calling engine orders to nobody — talking the Kestrel through it, the way she talked to everything, as if the whole world were a crew that might listen if you spoke firmly enough.

"Oakum," Marisol said, and Theo handed her the fibrous rope unraveled into loose strands, and she began driving it into the seam with the mallet and a caulking iron, packing the fibers tight, her arms shaking with effort, salt water streaming off her elbows. "Now say something."

"What?"

"Distract me. My hands cramp when I'm scared, and I'm very scared, and if my hands cramp we both drown in a riverboat's basement, which is a stupid way to die." Her grin flashed white in the swinging light. "Talk."

So Theo talked. He told her about the blind storyteller in the flooded market, about the legend of the cave that opened once a generation, about the morning he'd stood on the drained flats with his camera and photographed the black mouth below the cliffs and felt, for the first time in his drifting young life, that the world had a secret worth staying for. He told her he'd never had roots anywhere, that the Kestrel was the closest thing, that Paloma treated the boat like a promise she kept meaning to break.

"She won't break it," Marisol said, driving oakum. "People who run aren't the ones who let go. It's the ones who stay comfortable who let go. Running is holding on too hard." She set the iron, swung the mallet, drove the strand home. "My grandfather taught me that without meaning to."

The seam was closing. Pulse by pulse, the water coming through weakened from a gulp to a seep to a thin cold thread. They got the pine plugs driven over the oakum, then the tallowed canvas nailed over the plugs, Marisol holding the nail while Theo swung the hammer in the rising water, and somewhere in the middle of it Theo realized his arms had stopped shaking — not because the fear was gone but because it had changed shape. It wasn't terror anymore. It was purpose. He and this stranger he'd known five days were moving like two hands of the same body, passing tools without looking, reading each other's balance in the lantern light, and above them Paloma was holding the whole arrangement together with a wheel and a voice.

Three strangers functioning as one crew. He thought: *this is what it's supposed to feel like.* He thought: *I want to keep this.*

* * *

Near dawn the pump began winning.

They heard it change pitch — that small mechanical triumph — and then Paloma's boots on the ladder, and then she was down among them, taking the scene in with one sweep: the plugged seam, the falling waterline, the two of them half-collapsed against the stringers, gray-lipped and shaking, grinning like idiots.

She crouched and put her palm flat against the patched seam and held it there a long moment, the way you'd check a child's forehead.

"Holding?" Marisol asked.

"For now. Until the next blow works it loose, or the one after." Paloma sat back on her heels, and in the steadying lantern light Theo saw how exhausted she was — truly saw it, the shadows under her eyes, the tremor in the hand she tucked away. "This boat needs a dry dock and a shipwright and three weeks, and the storm season just started. We're going to be fighting this seam all the way to the rains' end."

"Then we fight it," Theo said.

Paloma looked at him. Something moved across her face — surprise, maybe, or the beginning of a different kind of arithmetic, one that included him in the answer instead of the cost.

"Aye," she said finally, and it was the softest word he'd ever heard her use. "Then we fight it."

But even as she said it, her eyes went up, through the deck, through the rain, toward the shore — toward the harbor office where a man named Bell kept his ledgers and his grudges, toward the cliffs where a drowned magnate's cave waited under the water for the tide to open it once more. And Theo, following her gaze without knowing why, felt the seam of something else shift — the sense, sudden and cold as bilgewater, that the storm outside was only the first leak, and that the worst floods were still coming from land.
