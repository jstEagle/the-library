## 11. Manifests and Ghosts

The ledgers came out of the cave wrapped in oilcloth, and they smelled like the inside of a drowned man's pocket.

Marisol carried them up the cliff path herself, refusing Theo's help twice before her arms gave out on the third switchback and she let him take one end of the bundle without meeting his eyes. The storm had passed through in the night — not the true monsoon yet, only its herald, a squall that had come off the sea like a fist testing a door — and the rocks were slick with a green film of new water. Below them, the bay was still wrong. Still too full, too restless, the color of old pewter. The tide had not come back to its proper bed; it had come back angry.

"Careful," Paloma said from behind her, and it was not a warning about the footing. It was about everything else.

Marisol knew what she meant. Word had spread through Landfall the way dye spreads through water — first a thread, then all at once. Half the town had walked out to the cliffs at dawn to stare down at the place where the sea had briefly shown them its secret, and now those same faces watched the three of them climb with bundles under their arms, and the watching had weight to it. Hunger has a look. She'd seen it before, on other faces, in other towns, whenever anyone spoke the name Voss aloud.

She had spent thirty-one years trying not to speak it aloud herself.

* * *

They spread the documents across the Kestrel's cabin table because it was the only dry, flat, private surface in Landfall, and because Paloma's boat — leaking, stranded, half-repaired — was still the safest room any of them knew. The lamp swung gently overhead as the riverboat breathed against her mooring lines, and the light moved across the papers like something alive.

There were eleven ledgers. Marisol counted them twice, because her hands were shaking and she did not trust the first count.

Aurelio Voss had been a careful man. That was the thing no one told you about monsters — they were rarely careless. The ledgers were bound in oiled leather, their spines stamped with the fleet's old house mark, the wave-and-anchor that still stood rusting over the customs house in Landfall as if it were a coat of arms instead of a confession. Inside, the handwriting was small and immaculate. Columns of figures. Tonnage, insurance, cargo, crew.

*Crew.*

Marisol turned to the first manifest and felt the cabin tilt, though the boat was barely moving.

It wasn't a list of employees. She had known that, in the abstract way you know a thing you've spent your life refusing to know. But abstraction is a kind of mercy, and the page took it away. Names. Ages. Terms of indenture — five years, seven years, ten. Debts incurred for passage, for tools, for food, debts structured so they could never be worked off, each year's wages swallowed by the year before's interest. And beside nearly every name, in Aurelio's tidy hand or his clerks', a notation. *Lost overboard.* *Deserted — debt transferred to kin.* *Sold to northern contract.* *Drowned, no recovery.*

"They weren't sailors," Theo said quietly. He was standing at her shoulder, seventeen years old and trying very hard to be older. "They were — "

"Yes," Marisol said.

She found the name on the fourth page of the second ledger, and she would spend the rest of her life knowing exactly how her eyes went there, the way water finds the lowest ground.

*Vidal, Rosa. Age 24. Indentured: 6 years, passage debt. Transferred from San Reymo depot.*

Her grandmother.

Rosa Vidal. Who had married well, according to the family story — married the magnate's own son, risen from nothing, a fairy tale told at every Voss dinner table until the tables stopped being set for Marisol. Rosa who had died when Marisol was nine, leaving her a single possession: a cheap tin locket with no picture in it, which the family had let her keep because it was worthless.

Marisol had worn that locket into the cave two days ago. She wore it now, and it lay against her sternum like a small cold coin, and she understood, with a clarity that felt like drowning, why there had never been a picture in it.

Because pictures could be traced. Because Rosa Vidal had been bought, transported, worked, and renamed by marriage, and the woman who had signed her passage debt with a thumbprint in San Reymo had been erased so thoroughly that even her granddaughter's inheritance of grief had arrived with the name filed off.

"She's here," Marisol said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. She had expected it to break. Instead it came out flat and precise, the voice of someone reading a shipping document, because that was what she was doing. "My grandmother. Page four. She was twenty-four."

Paloma said nothing. The riverboat captain sat across the table with her forearms on the wood and her hands loose and open, the way you hold them when you're showing an animal you won't grab. Marisol had seen her do it with strangers, with harbormasters, with the sea itself. She did it now with Marisol's grief, giving it room.

"Theo," Marisol said. "Get me the third ledger."

He slid it across. She turned the pages with a care that cost her — her fingers wanted to tear, wanted to rend, wanted to give the rage somewhere to go — until she found the cross-reference column, the one where clerks recorded what happened to the people after their terms expired or ended. Most entries were dashes. Some were the cold shorthand of disposal.

Beside Rosa Vidal's transfer number, in ink slightly darker than the rest, someone had written: *Retained household. Married into ownership line. Debt cancelled by A.V., personal dispensation.*

Personal dispensation. As if a woman's whole stolen life were a fee that could be waived by the man who stole it.

Marisol closed the ledger. Very carefully, the way you close a door on a room where someone has just died.

* * *

She had asked him once. That was the whole of it, the crime that had unmade her life — she had asked him once.

She was nineteen. It was the last summer the family still pretended to want her, and the whole Voss remnant had gathered at the house above San Reymo, the white house with the wave-and-anchor carved over the door, and Aurelio was ninety-one and mostly a monument of himself, propped in his chair on the veranda like a figurehead salvaged off his own fleet. He liked her, then. That was the terrible part. He called her *mi capitana* because she'd gotten her dive certification before any of the cousins, because she talked about currents and wrecks with a hunger he recognized. She thought it was pride. Later she understood it was recognition — the way a man who has spent a life around deep water knows another creature drawn to it.

And she had asked. Not accusingly. She'd been careful; she'd rehearsed it for a week. There were stories, she said. Old stories in the port towns, about the early fleet, about the crews. Were they true?

The veranda had gone quiet the way a harbor goes quiet before weather. Her father had looked at his shoes. Her aunt had made a small sound and left. And Aurelio had looked at her for a long time with eyes that were still, even at ninety-one, the color of deep water over rock, and he had said:

"You want to know what I built the fleet on."

Not a question. He'd said it almost gently, the way you confirm a diagnosis.

"I built it on the ones nobody counts," he said. "Every fortune on this coast stands on them. The difference between me and the families whose names are on the banks is that I never pretended otherwise." He had reached out and taken her hand, and his grip had still been strong, a working grip, a rope-handling grip. "You can carry that or you can put it down, mi capitana. But you can't ask me to carry it for you, and you can't ask me to be sorry. Sorry is for things you didn't mean to do."

Then he had disowned her. Not with shouting — with paperwork, because he was Aurelio Voss, and even his cruelty kept immaculate books. By morning there was a lawyer's letter. By winter her name was struck from the trust. Her father, who had chosen the money as everyone always chose the money, did not write to her again for six years, and then only once, to tell her that Aurelio had died, and that the funeral had been lovely.

Marisol had spent twelve years diving other people's wrecks, telling herself she had put it down. She had been lying. You don't put down a thing like that. You just swim away from it far enough that you can't see it, and the current does the rest, and one day the current sets you down outside a delta town called Landfall where the tide has gone out too far and exposed your grandfather's oldest sin to the daylight, and you understand that you didn't run from the inheritance at all.

You were carrying it the entire time. Inheritance isn't what's left to you. It's what's left *in* you.

* * *

"You don't have to read the rest tonight," Paloma said.

It was much later. Theo had fallen asleep sitting up in the corner bench, one hand still resting on the closed ledgers like a boy guarding a hoard, and Paloma had draped a blanket over him without waking him, moving through her own cabin with the soundless economy of a woman who had raised herself aboard moving vessels. Now she sat across from Marisol with two cups of something hot and bitter that she called coffee the way you'd call a shipwreck a boat.

"I have to read all of it," Marisol said. "Every page. If we're going to use this — if it's going to stand up in front of a judge, in front of the town — I need to know what's in it before Bell tells them a prettier version."

"Bell doesn't deal in pretty versions. He deals in burned versions."

"Then I need to know it first."

Paloma studied her over the rim of her cup. Outside, the rain had started again, soft for now, a rehearsal. The hull ticked and groaned around them, that language of stressed timber that Marisol was learning to hear the way Paloma heard it — not as noise but as sentence structure.

"My grandmother's in there," Marisol said. "Did you understand that? The woman whose locket I wear. My grandfather wrote her name in a debt book between a boy of sixteen and a man listed as *drowned, no recovery*, and then he married her into the family and called the debt cancelled, and the whole family sat around dinner tables for sixty years telling a story about a girl who rose from nothing." She laughed, and hated the sound of it. "She rose from nothing because everything was taken from her first. That was the rising."

"And now you have her name back."

"Now I have her *number* back." Marisol touched the ledger without opening it. "Transfer 114. That's what she was to him. A unit of tonnage." She looked up. "Do you know what the worst part is? I loved him. When I was small. He used to lift me onto the rail of his chair and tell me about the deep water, and I thought — this is what having a grandfather is. This warmth. And it was real, that's the obscene thing, it was genuinely real, and it was built on *that*—" she tapped the ledger, once, "—the way a house is built on pilings. You live in the warm rooms and you never go underneath."

"Most people don't," Paloma said. "Going underneath costs."

"What did it cost you?"

The question hung there. Marisol hadn't meant to ask it; it had come out the way questions do when you've been underwater too long and everything surfaces at once. She braced for the deflection, the captain's shrug, the change of subject — she'd watched Paloma deflect better interrogators than herself.

But Paloma only looked at the lamp for a while, and the rain filled the silence, and when she spoke her voice was low, pitched under Theo's sleeping breath.

"A partner," she said. "Years ago. What I did and didn't do for him — that's a story for a different night, and maybe not any night. But I'll tell you the shape of it. I learned that the law keeps different books than the ledgers do. Same binding, same neat columns. And sometimes the person written down as guilty is just the one who couldn't afford a better pen." She turned her cup slowly on the table. "I've been running from my entry ever since. So I know something about inheriting a name you didn't choose."

"Yours is a warrant," Marisol said. "Mine is a fortune."

"Both are debts," Paloma said. "That's what nobody tells you about inheritances. Everybody thinks they're gifts. They're bills. The only question is whether you pay them or pass them down."

Marisol thought of her cousins in San Reymo, spending the interest on the fleet money, raising children in the white house under the wave-and-anchor, passing the bill down unopened, generation to generation, like a letter no one dares to read. She thought of the blind storyteller in the flooded market, whom Theo quoted endlessly, who said the tide opens the cave once a generation. She had thought it was poetry when she first heard it. Now she understood it was bookkeeping. Once a generation, the sea audits the account.

"I'm paying mine," she said.

"I know," Paloma said. "That's why I'm still here. That's why the boat stays moored in a town that's charging us admission." She rose, collected the cups, paused at the little stove with her back turned. "One more thing, since we're going underneath. The manifests aren't the prize. You know that, yes?"

"The confession letter. Theo saw the reference in the chamber index — a sealed packet, Aurelio's own seal, in the inner vault."

"A man like that doesn't leave a confession by accident," Paloma said. "He leaves it because he wants it found by the right hands, late enough that it can't hurt him, soon enough that it can hurt everyone else. Which means whatever's in that packet, he chose every word of it. Don't trust it because it's true. Trust it because it's *useful* — and know the difference before Bell makes you choose."

Marisol looked at the sleeping boy, at the blanket rising and falling, at the eleven ledgers stacked like a small dark city on the table of a leaking boat.

"He named her among the stolen," she said. "In his own hand. Whatever else he wrote in that letter, he already confessed the part that matters. He wrote her down. Transfer 114. Rosa Vidal." She said the name aloud for the first time in her adult life, and the cabin seemed to lean toward it, the way a room leans toward a bell. "Everything else is just him negotiating with the dark."

Outside, the rain thickened. Far off across the black bay, where the cliffs dropped to the drowned mouth of the cave, a light appeared — brief, low, moving along the waterline where no honest boat would idle at this hour, in this weather. Then a second light beside it.

Paloma saw it too. She set down the cups without a sound.

"That'll be Bell's men counting our lanterns," she said quietly. "Sleep fast, Marisol. Tomorrow the water starts coming for all of us — and I don't think it's waiting for the monsoon."
