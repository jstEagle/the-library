## 12. The Warrant Catches Up

The mail skiff came on the morning tide, and Paloma knew before it rounded the breakwater that it was carrying something with her name on it.

She couldn't have said how she knew. Twenty years of running had taught her the shape of bad news the way a sailor learns the shape of weather — not by seeing it whole but by reading its edges, the small wrongnesses that gather at the horizon long before the storm itself arrives. The skiff was riding low for a mail run. The man at the tiller wore a constable's grey coat, buttoned to the throat despite the wet heat. And Bell was already on the dock.

That was the detail that settled it. Harbormaster Emory Bell did not greet mail boats. He greeted cargo, officials, and opportunities, and he stood there now at the end of the pilings in his pressed uniform, hands clasped behind his back like a man waiting for a delivery he'd ordered personally. The monsoon clouds stacked up behind him, bruise-purple over the drowned hills, and his smile was the only dry thing in Landfall.

Paloma set down the coil of line she'd been splicing and watched the skiff come in. Her hands were steady. She made a point of noticing that they were steady, the way you check a knot twice when you already know it will hold.

Behind her, inside the Kestrel's cabin, Theo was arguing with Marisol about dive weights — their voices rising and falling through the open port in the easy rhythm of people who had become, without anyone announcing it, a crew. Three weeks ago they'd been strangers. Two days ago they'd been fugitives hiding a boat in the mangroves. This morning they were something else again: three people with saltwater ledgers wrapped in oilcloth under a floorboard, waiting for the eye of the storm to give them one clean window into the cave.

And now this.

*You always knew it would come,* she told herself. *You just hoped it would come after.*

The skiff bumped the pilings. The constable stepped ashore with the exaggerated care of a man who didn't trust boats, and Paloma saw that he was young — younger than the coat, younger than the job, with the raw knuckled look of someone who'd been handed a duty he hadn't asked for and meant to see it done properly anyway. He carried a leather dispatch case. He did not look around the dock with curiosity. He looked directly at her.

So Bell had described her to him. That was its own kind of message.

"Captain Dunmore." The constable stopped an arm's length away. Up close he had river-town features, broad and plain, and eyes that kept flicking toward Bell as if checking his heading against a fixed mark. "Constable Reyes. Out of Corvane Port Authority." He opened the dispatch case with deliberate slowness, drawing out the moment either from ceremony or from reluctance. "I'm looking for a Paloma Dunmore, master of the vessel Kestrel. I'm told that's you."

"It's me."

"There's a warrant outstanding. Nine years old." He said it flatly, the way you'd report a tide table. "Smuggling charges. An incident at the Corvane customs wharf. You're required to surrender yourself and return with me for processing."

Nine years. She'd have said eight, maybe seven, if someone had asked her to bet. Time ran strange when you spent it outrunning things; the past either stretched like taffy or collapsed to nothing, and there was no trusting your own count. Nine years since Corvane. Nine years since Denny Okafor had smiled at her across a hold full of untaxed tobacco and said, *if it goes wrong, Captain, you know what to say.* And it had gone wrong, and she had known what to say, because Denny had a wife and a bad heart and a daughter who called her Auntie Paloma, and Paloma had no one at all. So she'd said it. She'd stood in front of the customs board and lied with a straight face while Denny slipped out the back of the story and out of her life, and by the time the lie unraveled — and lies about cargo always unravel — she was three rivers away with a new paint job on the Kestrel and a new name painted over the old one.

She'd been running ever since. Not fast. Just never stopping.

"Captain?" Reyes prompted.

"I heard you." She looked past him at Bell.

Bell came forward then, unhurried, his boots ringing on the wet planks. Up close his smile had the quality of weather glass — smooth, sealed, telling you nothing except what was coming. "Constable," he said warmly. "Efficient of you. The mail skiff is a public service; I do hope Landfall's hospitality hasn't suffered."

"The hospitality's been fine, sir."

"I imagine it has." Bell turned to Paloma, and lowered his voice as though the dock weren't empty, as though Theo and Marisol weren't watching through the port, as though the whole town's ears weren't pressed to the rain-heavy air. "You know, Captain, I wondered how long it would take. People think paper drowns. It doesn't. Paper floats. It floats all the way upriver and all the way back down, and sooner or later it comes ashore exactly where a man wants it." He glanced at the Kestrel, at the patched seam visible below her waterline where she lay against the fenders. "Lovely old boat. Be a shame if anything happened to her while her master was... detained."

There it was. Not even disguised anymore. The warrant wasn't the point; the warrant was the lever, and the fulcrum was the Kestrel, and the weight he intended to move was a bundle of oilcloth-wrapped manifests naming every family Aurelio Voss had robbed.

"You want the ledgers," Paloma said quietly.

Bell's smile widened by a degree. "I want what's best for Landfall. A town recovering from tragedy needs stability, not agitation. Those documents would stir up claims, lawsuits, griefs better left in the water. I've said so from the beginning." He spread his hands, a reasonable man among unreasonable seas. "But I'm flexible. A captain surrenders herself, cooperates fully, assists the constable here with his inquiries — and her vessel remains safe at my moorings. Protected. Insured, even. Accidents happen so easily in monsoon season, don't they? Boats drag anchors. Boats take water. Boats burn, sometimes, though heaven forbid."

Heaven forbid. Paloma held his gaze and felt something in her chest go very still and very cold, the way it went before a crossing she wasn't sure she'd survive. Every stop had cost more than she could pay — that had been the truth of her whole life, the arithmetic she'd never once beaten. But this was different. This wasn't a mooring fee or a doubled repair quote. This was Bell offering to trade her nine years of running for everything Theo believed in, everything Marisol had bled for, everything the drowned families of Landfall had waited a generation to hear spoken aloud.

If she gave him the ledgers, the warrant disappeared. If she refused, the Kestrel would be at the bottom of the harbor within a week, and probably with Theo aboard.

Unless she took the choice off the table entirely.

"Constable Reyes." She turned to the young man, and her voice came out level, almost gentle. "What happens to my vessel if I come with you?"

Reyes blinked. "Nothing, ma'am. A warrant's against a person, not property. She stays where she's moored unless there's separate cause."

"And who decides whether there's separate cause?"

"Well — the harbormaster has jurisdiction over moorage and safety in his district, but—"

"But he can't arrest my boat. He can't seize her without a court order. Is that right?"

"That's right, ma'am."

"Then write it down," Paloma said. "In your report. Witnessed. The vessel Kestrel remains at her moorings, unmolested, subject only to ordinary harbor regulation, until such time as I'm released or the matter's adjudicated. You'll sign it. Bell will sign it. I'd like the storyteller from the market as a second witness — the blind one, Odile. She's got a memory like a ledger and half this town listens to her."

Bell's smile thinned. "Captain, that seems unnecessarily—"

"Formal?" She met his eyes. "I've been on the wrong end of informal arrangements before, harbormaster. They float away. Paper doesn't — isn't that what you said?"

For a moment the dock was silent except for the water slapping the pilings and the far-off grumble of the storm deciding whether to arrive early. Then Bell laughed, short and unamused, and gestured grandly. "By all means. Let's have our paperwork. I'm a servant of process, Captain. Always have been."

*You're a servant of nothing but yourself,* she thought, *and process is just the rope you climb.* But she let Reyes fetch his book, and she let Odile be sent for — the old woman arriving with her white cane tapping the planks and her face tilted toward the voices like a flower toward light — and she stood in the strengthening wind while the words were read aloud and signed, three signatures, one thumbprint, the transaction recorded in a constable's careful hand.

It changed nothing, and she knew it. Paper could be lost. Witnesses could be discouraged. But it bought time, and time was the only currency she had left, and she intended to spend every coin of it.

* * *

They let her go down into the cabin first. Ten minutes, Reyes said, to gather her things. Bell watched from the dock, making no pretense otherwise, and Paloma closed the cabin door behind her and stood in the dim lamplight with her heart going hard against her ribs.

Theo was on his feet before the door shut. "Paloma. What's happening? What did he—" His eyes went to the window, to the grey coat visible through the salt-hazed glass. "Is that a constable?"

"It is."

"Why is there a—"

"Because nine years ago I took the blame for a man I loved like a brother, and the paper finally caught up." She said it plainly, because lying to Theo had become a thing she found she could no longer do, and because he deserved the truth before the town gave him a worse version. "Smuggling. Corvane. There's a warrant. I'm going with him."

"No." Theo's voice cracked on it. "No, we can fix this, we can — Marisol knows people, we can put you on the Kestrel and run the mangrove channels tonight, we've done it before—"

"And leave you here with Bell holding every grudge he's got and no one to sail you out?" She put her hand on the side of his face, rough palm against the boy's jaw, the way she'd done a hundred times without ever once letting herself think of it as anything but practical. "Listen to me. Listen. The ledgers stay hidden. The dive happens when the eye opens, exactly as planned, and you don't wait for me and you don't come looking for me. Marisol leads. You follow. You get those documents into a judge's hands if it's the last thing either of you does."

"We can get them and get you."

"Theo." She waited until his eyes steadied on hers. "Bell wants the ledgers. That's the only reason I'm worth anything to him. As long as he thinks I might still hand them over — as long as I'm a card he hasn't played — I'm safe. Jailed, but safe. The moment you trade them for me, I'm worthless AND we've lost. Do you understand? Me staying locked up IS the plan. I'm the decoy. I've spent my whole life being bait for other people's escapes; I might as well be good at it."

His jaw worked. His eyes were bright and furious and fifteen years old and trying so hard not to be. "You always said never stop moving. You said the day you stop is the day they catch you."

"I know what I said." She smiled, and was surprised to find it was real. "Turns out I was wrong about which way to run. Running's just stopping slowly, somewhere else. This—" she nodded toward the dock, toward the grey coat, toward the cell she could already feel around her wrists like cold water, "—this is moving. Toward something. First time for everything."

Marisol rose from the corner where she'd sat silent through all of it, arms crossed, and for a moment the two women regarded each other across the little cabin — the granddaughter of a thief and the captain of a leaking boat, neither of whom trusted anyone, both of whom had somehow ended up trusting each other.

"I'll get her out," Marisol said. No drama in it. A statement of hydrology, almost. "Whatever it takes. Whatever wall they put between us. Water finds a way, Paloma. So do I."

"I believe you," Paloma said, and found that she did, completely, which frightened her more than the warrant did. "That's why I can do this. Get the ledgers. Don't waste my sacrifice on a bad trade."

She took one bag. In it: a change of clothes, her mother's knife, and — folded flat, wrapped in waxed cloth, where no search would think to look for anything but a woman's keepsakes — a single page torn from the manifest index, listing fourteen names and the tonnage of the lives attached to them. Insurance, she told herself. Leverage. But she knew it for what it was: proof of life. When the ledgers surfaced, this page would surface too, and Bell would know that taking her had gained him nothing and losing her had cost him everything.

At the cabin door she paused. The Kestrel creaked around her, the old hull speaking its endless language of complaint and endurance, and Paloma laid her palm flat against the bulkhead the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you might not see again.

"Hold together, old girl," she murmured. "Hold together for him."

Then she climbed out into the wind and walked down the dock with her spine straight and her bag over her shoulder, past Bell's satisfied face, past Odile's sightless eyes turned faithfully toward the sound of her boots, past young Constable Reyes who wouldn't quite meet her gaze. At the skiff she stopped once and looked back. Theo stood on the Kestrel's deck with his fists at his sides, not waving, refusing to wave, and Marisol's hand rested on his shoulder like an anchor rode.

The rain began as the skiff pushed off — the true rain, the monsoon's opening argument, vast and grey and total, erasing the shoreline stroke by stroke. Paloma sat in the stern with her hands cuffed loosely in her lap and watched Landfall dissolve behind her, and she did not feel afraid.

She felt paid up. Nine years of debt, settled at last, and the strange liberty of it nearly made her laugh. All her life she'd thought surrender would feel like drowning.

Instead it felt like dropping anchor.

Behind her, lost in the rain, the Kestrel rocked at her moorings — and ahead, somewhere under the churning black water of the bay, the cave waited with its dead men's names, and the storm's eye was coming, and Marisol Voss had promised her that water finds a way.

Paloma Dunmore closed her eyes and started counting tides.
