## 13. Holding Cell, Rising Water

Theo had never seen a jail before. He'd imagined them from stories — iron bars, torchlight, men with hard faces — but Bell's jail in Landfall was just the back half of the harbormaster's office, a room with a barred window facing the harbor and a cell built of salt-eaten brick that someone had painted yellow once, long ago, in an optimistic decade. Paloma sat on the cot inside it with her hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting for a ferry, as if she'd chosen this seat herself and might change her mind at any moment.

That was the worst part. Not the bars. The calm.

"You should go," she'd said when they brought her in, before the constable from the mail skiff had even finished his paperwork. "Take Marisol, take the Kestrel, get upriver past the second bend. Bell won't chase you once he has me."

Theo had stood at the bars and said nothing, because anything he said would have been a lie or a promise he couldn't keep, and Paloma Sandoval could smell both through brick.

Now he stood outside in the rain.

The monsoon had arrived three days early and come down on Landfall like something with a grudge. It wasn't rain so much as the sky deciding to relocate to sea level all at once. The market canals had climbed their stone banks by noon; by mid-afternoon the lower walkways were gone, awnings submerged to their poles, vendors hauling crates of dried fish and candle wax up to the second-floor balconies of buildings whose ground floors had been surrendered to the water generations ago. The town smelled of wet rope, woodsmoke drowning, and the green rot smell of the mangroves coming inland.

And still the tide rose.

Theo found the storyteller where he always was — under the awning of the chandlery, which today meant on the chandlery's porch, two steps above brown water, wrapped in an oilcloth poncho with his blind white eyes turned toward the sound of footsteps.

"That's the boy," the old man said before Theo spoke. "The one who photographs things. I hear your little machine clicking in my dreams. Click-click-click. Like a beetle."

"Paloma's in jail." Theo's voice came out smaller than he wanted. "Bell put her in. She didn't even fight."

"Of course she didn't fight." The storyteller turned his face toward the rain, listening to it hammer the awning. "A woman who fights a jail is fighting a jail. A woman who doesn't fight is fighting everything the jail stands for. Which one do you think scares Harbormaster Bell more?"

"I don't know what to do. Marisol's watching the Kestrel. The seam's holding but barely, and if the water keeps rising—" His throat closed. He made himself say it. "If the water keeps rising, the jail floods too. It's on the harbor side. The lowest street in Landfall."

The old man was quiet for a long moment. Rain drummed. Somewhere out in the drowned market, a dog barked at nothing, or at everything.

"When I was a boy," the storyteller said, "there was a flood year. Worse than this. The water came into the upper square and the harbormaster then — not Bell, his father, may the sea sort him out — locked the granary doors because people hadn't paid their grain debts. Locked the doors while the water rose. And you know what happened?"

Theo shook his head, then remembered the man couldn't see it. "No."

"Nothing happened. That's what happened. We stood in the square and we looked at the doors and we muttered, and we went home to our roofs, and the granary stayed shut, and old Tomas Ferro drowned in sight of a thousand sacks of rice." The storyteller's voice didn't rise. It dropped instead, low and even, the way he told the Voss story, the way he told every story that mattered. "For forty years I have told the tale of Aurelio Voss and the cave and the stolen crews, and for forty years people have said, lovely story, pass the rum. And now there are ledgers in that cave with names in them, real names, names of grandmothers, and a woman sits in Bell's jail because she wouldn't hand those names over to a thief. So."

He reached out and caught Theo's wrist. His grip was stronger than it had any right to be.

"So what?" Theo whispered.

"So this time, when the doors are locked, somebody knocks."

* * *

It took him until dark to understand what knocking meant, and longer to find the courage to do it.

He started with the market folk because they were closest and because he knew their faces. The woman who sold him guavas most mornings — Señora Ixchel, though everyone called her the Guava General — was bailing rainwater off her balcony with a cooking pot when he came splashing up the flooded lane.

"They've taken Paloma," he said. "To Bell's jail. And the water's rising."

She stopped bailing. She looked at him for a long time, and Theo watched something move behind her eyes — the same calculation he'd seen on every adult face since the constable's skiff docked, the arithmetic of risk, of what a person could afford to lose. He knew that arithmetic intimately. It was the arithmetic Paloma did every day of her life, the reason she never stayed anywhere: every stop costs more than you can pay.

But the Guava General surprised him.

"Bell fined me double last month because my cousin argued with his clerk," she said. She set down the pot. "Get the fishwives. They're angrier than anyone and they can't drown — they swim like seals."

By full dark, in the intervals between the worst sheets of rain, word moved through Landfall the way water moves through a hull: silently, finding every crack. Theo went door to door along the balconies and boardwalks, soaked through, hoarse, telling the story over and over until it stopped being his story and became theirs. The captain who ran aground saving a boy. The captain who refused Bell's night cargo. The captain who dove a cave with a stranger and came back with proof that the great Aurelio Voss — whose name was on the customs house, whose statue stood in the dry fountain, whose fortune had built half the waterfront — had built it on the backs of crews he never paid, families he erased, a whole drowned quarter of the town whose descendants still lived in its shadow without knowing why they were poor.

"And now she's sitting in a cell," Theo told them, "and the water's at the third step of the harbor street, and Bell's posted one deputy outside and gone home to his dry house on the hill."

He didn't have to say the rest. Everyone in Landfall could read water. Everyone knew what a rising tide did to the lowest street in town.

They gathered in the upper square around midnight, maybe sixty of them, maybe eighty — Theo couldn't count in the dark and the rain, but he could see lanterns, dozens of them, bobbing together like a fleet. Fishermen in oilskins. Market women with children strapped to their backs. The chandlery's two sons carrying the storyteller himself on a chair, because the old man had insisted, absolutely insisted, and no one dared argue with him.

"Boy," he said from his chair, gripping Theo's arm as they started down toward the harbor street. "When we get there, you stay behind me. Bell's men know faces, and yours is on a photograph pinned to his office wall, I'd wager."

"What are we going to do?"

"We are going to stand in the rain," the storyteller said, "in front of a jail, and refuse to leave. It doesn't sound like much. Most true things don't."

* * *

The water was ankle-deep on the harbor street when they arrived, and rising in slow visible pulses, each wave from the harbor shoving a finger of foam further up the cobbles. The jail's barred window glowed dimly with lamplight inside. Paloma's silhouette crossed it once — pacing, unhurried — and Theo's chest clenched so hard he nearly cried out.

One deputy stood under the eave of the harbormaster's office, a young man Theo recognized from the docks, Renny or Renzo, holding a lantern and looking deeply unhappy about his entire life.

The crowd stopped ten paces away. Rain fell on sixty lanterns and eighty oilskins and nobody spoke, and the silence was somehow louder than the storm.

Then the storyteller raised his voice — that voice, the voice that had held generations of children spellbound under the chandlery awning, the voice that had kept the Voss legend alive for forty years against every wind of forgetting — and he began to speak.

"Hear me, Landfall!" he called. "Hear me, Deputy! Hear me, Harbormaster Bell, if you're listening from your dry windows on the hill! Forty years I have told you the story of the cave below the cliffs, and you called it a tale for children. Well, the children grew up. And the cave gave up its dead. And the names of the stolen are written in a ledger in this town tonight, and there is a woman in this jail who bled for those names, and the sea is climbing the street to take her!"

The deputy's lantern wavered. "You can't— you'll all have to disperse, there's a curfew—"

"There's a flood," said the Guava General, stepping forward, and her voice was iron. "There's a flood, Deputy, and you're standing in it, and so is she." She pointed at the glowing window. "My mother worked the Voss docks. My grandmother worked them before her. You think we don't know whose names are in that ledger? You think half this town isn't descended from crews that were never paid?"

More voices joined. Theo lost track of them — a netmender shouting about his grandfather, the baker's widow, boys from the boatyard, old women he'd bought candles from. The names of the stolen, spoken aloud, one after another, into the rain. Voss had tried to erase them from every record. Tonight the town wrote them back into the air.

And through the barred window, Theo saw Paloma stop pacing. Saw her come to the bars. Saw her hands close around them, knuckles whitening, and even at this distance he could read her face: disbelief. She had spent her whole life believing that towns punished outsiders, that every port eventually showed its teeth, that the only safe distance was distance itself. She was watching a town choose her, and she plainly did not know what to do with it.

The water crept up. Ankle-deep became shin-deep. The deputy looked at the flood, then at the crowd, then at the window, and Theo watched the exact moment the arithmetic in the young man's face resolved differently than Bell would have liked.

"I'm going to open the door," the deputy announced, to no one and everyone. "Not because you told me to. Because the cell's flooding and I'm not writing the report that says a prisoner drowned in Landfall custody while a hundred witnesses begged me not to let it happen." He fumbled the keys out. "Bell can have my badge. I never liked the fit anyway."

The lock clanked. The door swung.

Paloma stepped out into the flooded street, into the rain, into the lantern light, and the crowd did not cheer — Theo had expected cheering, had braced for it — instead they did something that hit far harder. They went quiet, and then the Guava General took off her hat, and then everyone did, sixty heads bare in the monsoon, and the storyteller said, from his chair, gently:

"Welcome ashore, Captain."

Paloma stood in the shin-deep water with rain streaming down her face, and for a moment she looked younger than Theo had ever seen her, and more lost, and more found. Her eyes found his across the crowd.

"Theo," she said. "The Kestrel—"

"Marisol's got her. Double lines, storm anchors. Go see for yourself."

She nodded once, and then she did something else he'd never seen her do: she let the crowd close around her, let hands grip her shoulders, let herself be carried forward through the flood rather than walking ahead of it alone.

* * *

They got her to the Kestrel through the back channels, poling a borrowed skiff up what had been a lane that afternoon. Marisol met them at the mangrove edge with a lantern and a face like a storm front of her own.

"You're free," she said flatly. "Good. Now be free quietly, because we have a problem bigger than Bell." She jerked her chin toward the black harbor, where the rain had thinned enough to show shapes: two patrol boats riding at anchor off the customs pier, lamps burning, men moving on their decks. "Bell commandeered the coastal patrol at sundown. Word is he's declared the cave a navigation hazard. Closed 'for public safety.' He's waiting out the storm, and the moment there's a window — the eye, the slack, whatever the sea gives us — he's putting divers in that chamber."

Paloma pulled herself aboard the Kestrel, dripping, and stood a moment in the cockpit with her hand flat on the wheel she'd gripped through twenty years of running. The boat groaned beneath them, the patched seam holding, barely, the bilge pump coughing out its faithful mouthfuls.

"He'll take the ledgers," she said. "Burn them, bury them, sell them back to the families piece by piece. And the warrant's still out on me. Free isn't free tonight, Theo. It's just a different kind of cornered."

Theo looked at the patrol boats, then at the crowd's lanterns still flickering distantly through the rain across the drowned town, then at Marisol, already checking her dive gear by lamplight with the grim economy of a woman who had decided something hours ago.

"The storyteller says the eye comes tomorrow," Theo said. "Midday, maybe. One window."

Paloma's hand tightened on the wheel. Outside, the monsoon screamed over the mangroves, and somewhere in the dark, Bell's boats rode at anchor between them and the cave, and the tide — the impossible, generational, once-in-a-lifetime tide — was still going out.

"Then we'd better be first through that window," Paloma said. "All of us. Even if the whole coast is hunting us when we come back up."
