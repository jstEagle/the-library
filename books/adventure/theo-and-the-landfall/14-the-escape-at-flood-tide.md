## 14. The Escape at Flood Tide

The water came for her in the night, the way it always did.

Paloma heard it before she saw it — a soft, insistent lapping at the cell door's lower edge, then a colder kiss against her bare feet where she sat on the cot with her knees drawn up. The jail had been built a century ago on what the town's founders had assumed was high ground, and like everything else in Landfall, the sea had since renegotiated the terms. By the time the storm's full voice arrived — a long, grinding roar out of the southeast that made the walls hum like struck wire — the water was ankle-deep and rising, carrying with it a smell of salt and diesel and drowned market stalls.

She had been in the cell two days. Two days of watching rain sheet past the barred window, of listening to Bell's boots on the boards above, unhurried, satisfied. He hadn't come down to gloat. He didn't need to. The warrant was real — she'd felt its weight when the constable from upriver laid it on the dock like a dead fish, all the way from Port Meridian, six years old and patient as a hook. *Paloma Dunmore. Aiding and abetting the smuggling vessel Corvina.* Her name signed to a crime that had belonged to Denny Oakes, who had been clever enough to vanish and generous enough, in his cowardice, to leave her holding the paper.

She'd gone quietly because going loudly would have put Bell's men on the Kestrel, and Theo was aboard, and Marisol, and the ledgers were hidden somewhere only Marisol knew. That arithmetic had been simple. What she hadn't counted on was the flood reaching the jail itself, or the way it changed the geometry of everything — because a flooded jail meant a seaward wall under water, and a seaward wall under water meant a door that no key could lock.

She'd noticed the grate on her first morning, back when the tide was merely rude rather than apocalyptic. A rusted iron panel set low in the seaward foundation, half-hidden by a century of barnacle and paint, the kind of maintenance access dock builders cut and then forgot. She'd filed the knowledge away the way she filed away every channel soundings and every debt — not because she intended to use it, but because a captain who didn't know her exits was already drowning.

Now the water was at her shins, and climbing.

* * *

The constable — a young man named Ferro with kind eyes and a bad cough — brought her supper sloshing through the corridor in a tin basin and stood in the doorway wringing rain from his cap.

"Harbormaster says the surge peaks near midnight," he said. "Says you'll be safer here than most."

"Does he." Paloma kept her voice flat. "And after midnight?"

Ferro looked at the water crawling across his boots and said nothing, which was answer enough. Bell wanted her broken slowly, or better, wanted her simply to stop existing as a problem — a prisoner whose trial kept getting postponed by weather until everyone forgot she existed. Out here, in the drowned edge of the world, men did disappear into postponements. She'd known it done to others. She'd run hard enough for six years never to let it be done to her.

"Ferro," she said. "The Kestrel. Is she still at the mangrove moorings?"

"I couldn't say, ma'am."

"Could you say if anyone's asked about her?"

He hesitated. Through the window behind him the lightning showed the harbor road transformed into a river, and something else — figures moving along the raised walkway by the chandlery, lanterns swinging, a whole line of them. Market folk. The storyteller's people.

"They've been out there two nights," Ferro said quietly, as if confessing. "Asking for your release. Old Serafim told them the tide opens things once a generation, and now they're saying the jail ought to open too. Harbormaster's furious. Says it's incitement."

"Is it?"

"It's Landfall," Ferro said, and coughed, and left the basin, and went away up the stairs with his lamp guttering.

Paloma ate standing in ankle-deep water, listening to the storm work the town apart joint by joint, and let herself feel — carefully, the way you test ice — the thing she had refused to feel since the constable's skiff touched the dock: that she was not alone in here. That outside those bars, in the rain, strangers were standing in a flood demanding her name be spoken free.

She had spent thirty years believing towns were tollbooths. It was going to be very inconvenient if this one insisted on being something else.

* * *

Midnight came as a change in pressure more than a sound — the barometer falling so far the air itself seemed to thin, and the roar of the wind dropping into something deeper and steadier, the voice of the sea pushing the whole delta inland. The water climbed her calves. Then her knees. The cot floated free of its bolts and bumped against the wall, and Paloma hauled herself onto the shelf above the sink and watched the black water breathe in the cell, rising and falling with a slow tidal rhythm, patient as anything that has all the time in the world.

That was when she heard the knock.

Three taps, low on the seaward wall, below the waterline. She went still. Three taps again — deliberate, spaced, a rhythm she knew. It was the signal she and Theo had invented their first season together, for man-overboard drills: *I'm here. Where are you?*

She knelt on the sink shelf and rapped the wall three times back.

Then she waited, heart hammering, while the storm filled the silence between knocks. Minutes passed. The water reached her thighs. And then, faintly, through the stone, she heard it — a scraping, rhythmic, methodical. Someone working at the grate from the outside.

*Marisol.*

Of course. Marisol Voss, who dove caves that ate divers for practice, who had swum the flooded squeeze beneath the cliffs with a knife in her teeth and ledgers strapped to her back. A seawall grate in a storm surge was, for her, a Tuesday.

The scraping stopped. Started. Stopped. Paloma imagined her out there in the black churn — no light, because light would bring Bell's watchman running; just hands and current and the memory of where the bolts were. The woman had spent one evening studying the jail's foundations from the water side, back when they'd planned the ledgers' hiding place, and Paloma had thought nothing of it at the time. Marisol studied everything. It was how she'd found her grandmother's name carved in stone a hundred feet inside a mountain of sea.

A bolt gave way with a clang she felt through her palms. Another. The third stuck, and there was a long terrible pause in which Paloma could hear nothing but the storm, and she pressed her forehead against the cold stone and thought, absurdly, *breathe, breathe, she knows how to breathe,* and then the third bolt surrendered with a shriek of rust and the grate swung inward on hinges that hadn't moved in fifty years.

Cold water shoved through the gap like a living thing. Paloma gasped — it was the temperature of deep water, of places the sun had never audited — and then a hand came through the opening, gloved, gripping her wrist with a strength that brooked no argument.

"Can you fit?" Marisol's voice, muffled, torn by wind, coming from everywhere and nowhere.

"I've been fitting through tight spots my whole life."

"The passage runs ten feet to the seaward scupper. It floods completely at peak surge — which is now, so we go *now*, and we don't stop. You'll be underwater twice. First time, four seconds. Second time, longer. Don't fight it. Exhale and move."

"And if I panic?"

"You won't," Marisol said. "You held the Kestrel off the rocks in a seam-burst. This is smaller water."

It wasn't, quite, a compliment. It was better: it was a fact, offered by someone who dealt only in facts. Paloma stripped off her boots — they'd drown her — and wedged her shoulders into the opening. The stone took her like a fist takes a coin. Barnacles scored her arms. The current pulled at her legs, trying to fold her backward, and she hooked her fingers into the grate frame and hauled, inch by inch, until the passage swallowed her to the waist and the world became rock, cold, and the sound of her own blood.

* * *

The passage was exactly as wide as her grief and slightly narrower than her hope. She moved through it the way she moved through everything — by refusing to calculate the odds. Ten feet. The first scupper. Marisol's hand found her ankle and squeezed once: *ready*. Paloma exhaled, and the water took her.

Four seconds underwater was nothing. Four seconds was the length of a wave's slap against a hull. She came up in a vertical shaft of surging black, gasping, and Marisol surfaced beside her, mask pushed up, hair plastered to her skull, grinning like sin.

"One more," Marisol shouted over the wind. "The outflow culvert. It spits us into the harbor basin behind the chandlery. Ready?"

"No," Paloma said honestly.

"Good. Honest divers live longer." Marisol pulled her mask down, gripped Paloma's collar. "Exhale on my mark. Whatever you feel — the pull, the dark, the thinking-you're-dying — it lasts eight seconds. I counted it twice tonight."

"When did you—"

"Before I knocked. Somebody had to make sure the door opened both ways." And she pulled them both under.

Eight seconds. Paloma had been underwater longer plenty of times — swimming for lines, clearing fouled props — but never blind, never in a pipe, never with a stranger's fist knotted in her collar as the only proof she wasn't already dead. The current seized them and flung them forward through absolute blackness, and she counted the way Marisol had taught her without teaching: one, two, three, exhale, don't fight it, five, six — and then light, actual gray storm-light, and they burst up into the flooded basin behind the chandlery with the rain hammering the surface to froth around them.

Marisol hauled her along a guide line — the woman had strung a guide line, of course she had — to the base of the chandlery stairs, where the raised walkway began. They came up out of the water dripping and half-crawling, and Paloma lay on the wet planks laughing, actually laughing, a sound she didn't recognize coming out of her own chest, wild and salt-burned and alive.

"Law failed me," she said to the sky. "Six years of running, and it failed me anyway. But you—" she rolled her head toward Marisol, "—you and a rusted grate."

"People didn't fail you either," Marisol said. "Look up."

Paloma looked.

The walkway above the chandlery was lined with lanterns. Dozens of them, sheltered under oilcloth, held by hands she knew — the storyteller Serafim, sightless eyes turned toward the sound of the surf, his great weathered face tilted up into the rain like a man listening to music; the netmender's daughters; the fish-paste woman whose stall Theo haunted; old Ferro the constable, off-duty, off-uniform, holding a lantern with the sheepish defiance of a man who has finally decided which side of a thing he stands on. They had come out in the peak of the monsoon to stand between the jail and the harbor, a human chain of witnesses, so that no boat could approach the seaward wall unseen. So that whatever happened tonight happened in front of the whole town.

Serafim turned his face toward the sound of her breathing and smiled.

"The tide opens things once a generation," he called out, over the wind, to everyone and no one. "I told you children. I *told* you."

Paloma got to her feet on the swaying walkway, soaked to the bone, scraped raw, wanted by the law of Port Meridian and the petty empire of Harbormaster Bell — and freer than she had felt in thirty years of running. Because running was freedom the way drifting was sailing: motion without direction, escape without arrival. This was different. This was a town that had chosen her, unasked, in a storm, at cost.

Theo appeared out of the crowd — soaked, terrified, furious, thirteen-going-on-fifty — and hit her with an embrace that nearly put them both back in the water.

"You *idiot*," he said into her shoulder, which was, she reflected, the correct order of operations: relief first, recrimination second. "You just let them take you. You just—"

"I know."

"Bell's got two patrol boats fueling at the north dock. He's going to seize the cave 'for safety' the moment the eye passes. Marisol says the eye comes tomorrow mid-morning, and it's the last window before the dry season seals the entrance for good. We have to hide the Kestrel in the mangrove channels tonight and dive the inner chamber during the calm, and you—" he pulled back, gripping her arms, eyes blazing, "—you are *not allowed* to surrender to anybody ever again."

Paloma looked at him — this boy she'd fished out of a river town three seasons ago, who wanted roots the way she'd wanted distance, who had learned knots and soundings and now, apparently, insurrection — and felt something settle in her chest that had been loose and rattling since Port Meridian.

"No promises about the future," she said. "But tonight, nobody's taking me anywhere I don't steer."

Marisol was already moving down the walkway, counting heads, checking the lantern line. "We have maybe two hours of dark left and Bell's watchman will find that empty cell by dawn. Mangrove channels, now. And Captain—" she paused, and in the lantern light her face was grave and young and absolutely certain, "—tomorrow we go get the rest of the truth. All of it. My grandfather's confession is still down there, and so is the list of every family he robbed. Tomorrow, under a still sky, we finish it."

Thunder walked across the delta like a closing door. Paloma Dunmore, fugitive, took one last look at the drowned jail behind her — at the little barred window, at the water sliding out through the open grate like the sea itself changing its mind — and turned her back on it for good.

"To the Kestrel," she said. "And mind the channels. In this surge, even the mangroves are lying about where the deep water is."

They moved out into the storm together, a lantern-line of townsfolk lighting the way, and somewhere behind them the flood tide kept rising, erasing the jail, the warrant, the old life — washing the slate clean for whatever they dared write on it next.
