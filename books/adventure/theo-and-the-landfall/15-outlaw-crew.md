## 15. Outlaw Crew

The mangroves swallowed the Kestrel whole.

Theo had never seen the boat disappear before. She was forty feet of weathered teak and stubbornness, the only home he'd known for three years, and now she sat in a green tunnel of roots and brackish shadow with her mast lowered and her hull painted over — Marisol's idea, a slurry of mud and tannin brushed across the white by lantern light until she looked like something the river had grown rather than built.

"Nobody finds what they aren't looking for," Marisol had said, wringing out her brush. "And nobody looks for a fugitive riverboat in a place like this."

She'd been right so far. Two days hidden in the channels south of Landfall, and no patrol boats had come nosing past their screen of roots. Only herons, and the constant dripping hush of the monsoon working through the canopy above them, and the slow brown water sliding seaward carrying leaves and broken branches and, once, half a market stall torn loose from some flooded dock upstream.

Theo stood at the bow in the gray-green light, watching that debris drift past, and tried to feel like an outlaw. It wasn't working. Outlaws, he figured, probably didn't spend their mornings bailing rainwater and arguing about rice rations. Outlaws probably felt dangerous. He mostly felt damp.

"You're brooding," Paloma said from the cockpit. She had a chart spread across her knees under an oilcloth, weighted at the corners with four brass fittings she'd unscrewed from somewhere Theo couldn't identify. "Brooding burns calories you haven't earned."

"I'm thinking."

"That's what brooding calls itself when it wants to sound useful." She didn't look up from the chart. Her hair was tied back with a strip of sailcloth, and there was a smudge of bilge grease along one jaw she either hadn't noticed or had stopped caring about days ago. "Come look at this. Tell me what you see."

He crouched beside her. The chart was old — older than the storm season, older maybe than Landfall itself — and someone had drawn the delta channels in faded ink that the humidity had begun to bloom into soft gray ghosts. The mangrove labyrinth south of town sprawled across the lower third like spilled ink of its own making: hundreds of interlocking channels, oxbows, dead ends, and tidal creeks with names in three different hands and none of them reliable.

"I see us lost," Theo said.

"You see us *hidden*. Lost is just hidden with bad posture." She tapped a point deep in the maze where two channels crossed. "But here's what I actually want you to see. This." Her finger moved downriver, through the channels, past the last of the mangroves, to the open water of the bay — and to the cliffs on its northern shore, where a small penciled X sat below a notation in her own handwriting: *Voss cave. Depth at mouth, 4 fathoms at mean low.*

"The eye," Theo said.

"The eye." Paloma leaned back against the wheel housing and looked up through the canopy, where rain came down in silver threads that never quite reached them. "Storm's got another day of teeth left, maybe two. Then it does what they all do — it spins itself hungry and opens its eye. Six hours, maybe eight, of wind gone flat and sky gone strange. And during those hours, the surge pulls back off the bay like the sea changed its mind."

"And the cave mouth opens."

"All the way open. Deeper than we've ever seen it. After the eye closes, the back side of the storm hits, and after the storm—" she shrugged, "—the dry season comes early this year, the harbormaster's charts say the spring tides won't drop enough to expose the entrance again until next year. Maybe not even then; the earthquake shifted the bar. This is the window. One dive. Everything we came for is sitting in that inner chamber waiting for somebody brave and stupid enough to go get it."

"Somebody wanted," Theo added. "Don't forget wanted. That's the part that makes us outlaws instead of divers."

Paloma smiled, but it was a thin thing, stretched over something heavier. "I've been wanted before. It doesn't change how you hold your breath."

* * *

They spent the middle of the day preparing, because preparation was the only prayer any of them knew how to say properly.

Marisol laid out the diving gear on the aft deck beneath a tarp and went through it piece by piece with the patience of a surgeon counting instruments. Two full sets of tanks — hers and one rented in Landfall in another life, two weeks and a hundred years ago. A third set cobbled together from the Kestrel's emergency locker, older, its regulator hissing slightly at the second stage. She'd assigned that one to herself without discussion, and neither Theo nor Paloma had been quick enough to argue.

"The manifests are in oilcloth," she said, wrapping each ledger again anyway, checking seals that were already sealed. "Aurelio Voss was a careful thief. He kept his confession dry even while he let the crews drown. We bring them up the same way. Dry, intact, readable. If water touches that paper, sixty years of names turn to porridge, and every family in Landfall loses their proof forever."

"How many names?" Theo asked. He'd asked before, in the outer chambers, running his lantern light over the carved walls — but he asked again, because asking was how he kept it real.

Marisol's hands paused on the oilcloth. "Four hundred and eleven," she said. "That's just the first manifest. There are three more ledgers behind it." She looked at him, and in the filtered green light her face seemed carved from the same material as the cave walls. "My grandmother is the eleventh name on the first list. Aurelio wrote her down himself. Contract number, ship assignment, debt owed — everything except an apology."

Theo nodded and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't be smaller than the silence.

Paloma appeared at the companionway with the tide tables and a pencil stub. "Eye forms tomorrow morning, if the barometer keeps falling the way it is. Slack water inside the bay around ten. That gives us a two-hour transit down the channels, a dive window of maybe ninety minutes before the eye wall starts building, and a run home against the returning surge." She drew the timeline in the air with the pencil, stabbing each point. "It's tight. If anything goes wrong — engine trouble, a patrol boat, a snagged line — we don't get a second try. And if we're caught in the open bay when the eye wall arrives—"

"We won't be," Marisol said.

"You don't know that."

"No," Marisol agreed. "But I've dived in worse than this with less than this, and I'm still here. At some point, Captain, you have to decide whether fear gets a vote." She held Paloma's gaze, and Theo watched the two women do the thing they did — the silent negotiation, the weighing, the grudging arithmetic of trust that had been adding up column by column since the night they'd met at the cave mouth.

"Fear gets a vote," Paloma said finally. "It just doesn't get to drive."

* * *

Evening came down through the mangroves like a lid closing.

They ate cold rice and smoked fish Paloma had traded for in a stilt village two channels north, paying with a coil of rope and a story about being surveyors, which was almost true now. Theo took first watch, as he always did, perched on the cabin roof with his back against the stub of the lowered mast, listening to the rain and the frogs and the far-off mutter of thunder rolling up from the southeast like a throat clearing.

His camera sat in its oilskin pouch against his chest. He'd taken it out earlier and checked it, though there was nothing left to photograph in the green dark — nothing but Marisol mending a strap, Paloma redrawing the channel notes by lamplight, the Kestrel wearing her coat of mud like a disguise. But he'd photographed the cave mouth the first morning the tide went out too far, and that photograph had started all of this, half the town crowding the shore by dawn with lanterns and long faces and hungry eyes. He understood now, in a way he hadn't then, that a picture could be a key. He wondered what the ones he'd take tomorrow would unlock.

Footsteps on the deck ladder. Paloma climbed up and settled beside him without asking, the way she'd started doing sometime in the last week, and for a while they just watched the dark water slide past the roots.

"When I was your age," she said, "I ran away from a port town called Calloway with nothing but a stolen dinghy and a grudge. I thought freedom meant nobody knowing where you were. I spent thirty years proving it to myself." She turned her head. In the gloom her eyes caught what little light remained. "You know what I learned?"

"That nobody knowing where you are is lonely?"

"That too. But mostly I learned that running isn't free. Every port you flee costs you the one you might have stayed in. You pay in places, Theo. And I'm nearly broke." She said it lightly, but her hand found his shoulder and squeezed once, hard, which was how she said things that weren't light at all. "Tomorrow we go get those ledgers. And after that, whatever happens — I'm done paying."

Theo's throat tightened. "You could still run. After. Take the Kestrel, take the ledgers to a judge upriver yourself, skip the trial, skip Bell, skip all of it."

"And leave you?" She snorted. "Leave the boat? Leave the first town that ever chose me back?" She shook her head. "No. Running was survival, kid. Staying's going to have to be something better. I just have to survive long enough to find out what."

Thunder rolled closer. Somewhere down the channels, the monsoon exhaled, and the rain thickened from threads to ropes.

"Get some sleep," Paloma said, rising. "Outlaws need their rest. Tomorrow we work."

* * *

The eye came at dawn, exactly as promised.

Theo felt it before he saw it — the pressure changing in his ears, the rain suddenly holding its breath — and then the light changed, and he scrambled up onto the cabin roof into a world gone utterly, impossibly still. The clouds had torn open overhead into a vast circular well of pale gold sky, ringed all around by the towering black wall of the storm, and the mangrove channels lay mirror-flat, every root and leaf reflected upside down in water that had forgotten how to move.

"It's time," Marisol said, already hauling tanks forward.

They cast off in silence. Paloma took the helm, easing the Kestrel out of her green tunnel with the engine at a whisper, threading channels she'd memorized from a chart drawn by dead men, and Theo worked the bow pole, fending off roots, calling depths. The boat slid seaward through the drowned world, and the eye wall rose ahead of them like the rim of a cup, and inside that rim the air was warm and golden and wrong in a way that made the skin crawl.

Halfway down the last channel, Marisol touched Theo's arm and pointed.

On the southern horizon, small with distance, two shapes were moving through the open water beyond the mangroves. Patrol boats. Bell's colors. Running not toward the caves but parallel to the shore — searching the channels, sweeping methodically, coming their way.

Paloma saw them too. Theo watched her hands tighten on the wheel, watched her jaw set, watched thirty years of running rise up in her face like a tide.

Then she looked at him, and at Marisol, and at the mud-painted hull of the only home she'd ever almost stayed in — and she turned the wheel straight toward the open bay.

"Hold on," she said. "We've got a window. Let's not waste it standing still."

The Kestrel surged forward into the golden stillness of the eye, three outlaws and four hundred and eleven names, running — for once in her long life — *toward*.
