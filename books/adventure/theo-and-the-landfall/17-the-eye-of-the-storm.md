## 17. The Eye of the Storm

**Paloma**

The wind died at 4:17 in the morning, and Paloma Dunmore woke because the silence was louder than the storm.

She had spent eleven days in the mangrove channels learning the grammar of hiding — which cuts ran shallow enough to foil a patrol boat's draft, which overhangs broke up the Kestrel's silhouette against the moon, how to run her engine at idle so the exhaust bubbled out soft as a fisherman's burp. Eleven days of rain hammering the canopy like a fist on a door that would not open. And now, in the black before dawn, the fist simply stopped.

She climbed out of the wheelhouse into a world gone strange. The clouds still churned overhead, but they'd been hollowed from beneath, scooped out like the inside of a coconut, and through that ragged window she could see stars — actual stars, indifferent and cold, the first she'd seen since the monsoon arrived early and swallowed the sky whole. The water lay flat as poured pewter. Somewhere far off, at the rim of the world, thunder grumbled like a dog denied its supper, patient, certain it would be fed.

"The eye," Theo said from behind her, voice thick with sleep. He'd slept on the deck plates under a tarp for so long now that he moved like an old man before his coffee. "Marisol said it might come. She's been watching the barometer since midnight."

"Then Marisol can watch it a while longer." Paloma looked east, where the cliffs of Landfall stood gray against the gray horizon, five miles of open water between them and the cave mouth that had started all of this. "An eye is a promise with a knife behind it. It'll close again. When it does, we want to be done."

Theo was already moving, waking Marisol, hauling the dive bags from under the tarp where they'd stayed dry by miracle or stubbornness. Paloma went to the helm and brought the Kestrel around, slow and gentle, the old boat answering her hands the way she answered no one else. Five miles. In the eye's dead calm, the riverboat could make it in forty minutes if the sea kept its unnatural manners.

She did not let herself think about what waited inside the cave. She thought instead about the feel of the wheel, about the particular way the Kestrel's bow should sit in flat water, about nothing at all. That was the trick of fear. You didn't beat it. You just gave it less room to stand.

The cliffs grew as they came on, black basalt streaked white with bird lime, and below them the sea had drawn back from the rocks in a way that made her skin crawl even now, even knowing why. The cave mouth yawned at the base of the cliff like a held breath. Marisol stood at the bow in her wetsuit, hood down, hair plastered to her skull, and when she turned to look back at Paloma there was something in her face Paloma hadn't seen there before. Not hope exactly. Something older than hope. The look of a woman walking toward a grave she'd been told all her life didn't exist.

"Slack tide in twenty minutes," Marisol said. "The inner chamber floods and drains with the surge. We have maybe ninety minutes of stillness before the eye wall comes ashore, and after that the chamber fills and doesn't empty until dry season." She pulled her hood up. "If we don't get the ledgers today, they stay down there until next year. And Bell will have a year to make sure nobody ever dives it again."

Paloma killed the engine a hundred yards out and let the anchor line sing down into the dark. "Ninety minutes," she said. "I'll hold the boat. If the wind picks up — even a breath — I'm calling you out. Don't argue with me on the radio. Just come up."

"You've never called anyone out of anything in your life," Marisol said, but there was no bite in it. She swung her legs over the rail. "Ninety minutes. And Captain —" she paused, half in the water already, "— whatever's down there, my grandmother's name is going to be in those manifests. I've known that since I was nine years old. Knowing it and reading it are different animals."

"I know," Paloma said. "Go find her."

They dropped beneath the surface together, two lanterns sinking into green glass, and Paloma stood alone at the wheel of the Kestrel in the middle of a sea gone silent, listening to the thunder circle the world like a wolf circling a fire, and waited.

* * *

**Theo**

The outer chambers Theo knew. He'd walked them once at slack tide with a lantern held high, marveling at the carved walls, the names cut into stone in rows neat as ledgers — because they were ledgers, that was the thing, the whole cave was a ledger, Aurelio Voss had written his crime in granite where he thought no one would ever read it. But the inner chamber lay beyond a passage that only opened at slack low water, a throat of rock that breathed with the tide, and Theo had felt it breathing last time, the cold air pushing out against his face like something exhaling, and he had turned back.

Now he swam through it.

The passage was narrow enough that his shoulders brushed both walls, wide enough that Marisol's dive light swept ahead of him in pale blades. The water rose to their chins, then their waists, then their ankles, and then they were climbing out onto stone that had never once, in all the years of the drowned town, been touched by anything but salt water and time.

The light found the ceiling first, and Theo forgot how to breathe.

It was vaulted, cathedral-high, and every inch of it was carved. Not the crude manifest lines of the outer chambers — these were figures, hundreds of them, men and women and children rendered in flowing relief, climbing rigging, hauling nets, standing at rails, faces lifted toward a carved sun at the apex. A fleet frozen in stone. A town that had existed before it drowned, remembered here by someone who had needed it remembered.

"It's the crews," Marisol whispered. Her light trembled. "All of them. Every ship he took. He put them on the ceiling." Her voice cracked down the middle. "He wanted them witnessed. He just couldn't do it while anyone was alive to see."

Against the far wall stood iron chests, seven of them, crusted with rust and coral, their seals unbroken — wax stamped with the Voss crest, a gull over crossed anchors. And beside the chests, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, a single bundle the size of a hymnal.

Marisol knelt in front of the smallest chest and didn't touch it for a long moment. Then she broke the seal.

Inside, wrapped in waxed linen, were books. Ledgers, dozens of them, their pages stuck together with age but sound underneath, the ink barely faded. Marisol opened one with hands that shook and read aloud, her voice echoing off the carved ceiling, off the hundred stone faces:

"*Manifest of the brigantine Cordelia, out of Landfall, master's indenture rolls attached. Crew: forty-one souls.*" She stopped. Swallowed. "*Names follow.*"

Theo crouched beside her and watched the pages turn. Names. Columns of names, each with a wage owed, a debt recorded, a date of disappearance. Not treasure. Evidence. Forty-one souls on one ship alone, and seven chests, and somewhere above them a ceiling full of faces.

"There," Marisol said suddenly, and her finger stopped on a line halfway down a page. She read it silently first. Theo watched her lips move. When she spoke, her voice came out small and ancient. "*Voss, Elena. Cook's station. Wage owed: full.*"

Her grandmother. The woman whose name had been scraped out of the family Bible, whose photograph had been burned, whose existence Aurelio Voss had denied so thoroughly that his own granddaughter had grown up thinking grief was just the shape of their house.

"She was on the Cordelia," Marisol said. "She wasn't lost at sea. He wrote her down. He knew exactly what he'd done, and he paid himself for it, and he sealed it in a cave." She laughed, one broken note that bounced around the stone. "He confessed. The letter — the bundle — that has to be it."

Theo unwrapped the oilcloth bundle with the care of a man defusing something. Inside was a letter, folded twice, sealed with the same gull-and-anchor crest. The paper had gone the color of weak tea but the words stood sharp and black:

*I, Aurelio Voss, being of sound mind and unsound soul, set down here what no court will ever ask me...*

Marisol read it aloud, all of it, every word, her voice growing steadier as she went, as though the confession itself were holding her upright. It named ships. It named dates. It named the arrangement with the harbormaster's office that had emptied the crews' wage accounts and marked the men and women lost so no families would come asking. It named Elena Voss, who had discovered the theft and threatened to go to the port authority, and who had boarded the Cordelia on its final voyage and never been listed among the survivors because she had never been meant to survive.

"He built the memorial himself," Marisol said when it was done. She looked up at the ceiling, at the carved sun. "After. He couldn't live with it quietly, so he hid it loudly, in stone, where his conscience could scream at him in private." She wiped her face with the back of her glove. "We take everything. All of it. The ledgers, the letter, the manifests. Every chest."

They worked fast then, sealing the documents into waterproof bags, loading the packs, Theo carrying two and Marisol three, the weight of seventy years of stolen lives riding on their shoulders through the throat of the passage. At its mouth Theo paused, one hand on wet stone, and looked back at the ceiling of faces one last time.

"We'll come back for you," he said. "With everyone. You'll be seen."

The water was rising around their ankles as they swam out.

* * *

**Paloma**

Paloma saw the wall before she heard it.

It stood on the southern horizon like a mountain range deciding to relocate — a blue-black rampart of cloud reaching from the sea to the top of the sky, and even at that distance she could see it moving, rolling toward them with the patience of geology. The eye was closing. Whatever clock the sky had granted them, the sky was taking it back.

She keyed the radio. "Surface. Now. Both of you. The wall's coming ashore and I am not losing either of you to a squall line because you wanted one more chest."

Static, then Theo, breathless: "Coming up — we've got it, Paloma, we've got all of it —"

They broke the surface thirty seconds later, and she hauled them aboard by their tank straps while the first outrider gusts came across the water like scouts ahead of an army, flattening the swells, shivering the rain off in sheets. Marisol collapsed on the deck with the packs clutched to her chest, laughing and crying in the same breath, and Theo tore his mask off and held up the oilcloth bundle like a lantern.

"The confession," he shouted over the wind. "His own hand. It names everything — the harbormaster's office, the ships, all of it —"

"Then it's worth more than both our lives, which is exactly what it's about to cost." Paloma was already at the wheel, already bringing the anchor up, because the wind had swung a quarter turn in the space of a minute and that was the sea telling her the eye wall was closer than it looked. "Get below. Strap those packs down like they're made of glass. We're running for the mangroves and we're running now."

The Kestrel came about into building seas, and that was when Paloma saw them.

Two boats, emerging from the rain curtain to the north, running dark except for the hard white stare of searchlights. Patrol hulls, Bell's patrol hulls, cutting across the eye's dying calm straight for the cliff anchorage — straight for the Kestrel. They must have been waiting at the edge of the storm all along, riding out the monsoon in the lee of some island, watching the barometers same as Marisol, betting on the eye. Betting that the fugitives couldn't resist one last dive.

Bell hadn't sent his armada to catch them leaving. He'd sent it to catch them arriving.

"They're a mile out," Theo called from the companionway. "Maybe less. They're faster than us in this chop."

"Nothing's faster than anyone in what's coming." Paloma watched the wall of cloud swallow the last of the stars, felt the pressure drop through her teeth, and did the arithmetic she'd been doing her whole life — wind against current against distance against the draft of two patrol boats that had never learned these waters. "Hold on to something. Both of you."

The first rank of the eye wall hit them like a door slammed by God.

The Kestrel heeled, righted, heeled again; rain came horizontal, hard as gravel; the searchlights behind them whipped and swung in the chaos, sweeping the water where the riverboat had been a moment before and wasn't anymore. Paloma drove her blind, by memory and by the feel of the hull, south into the rising sea, toward the mangrove channels that had hidden them once and would have to hide them again — with the ledgers strapped below, with a dead man's confession riding in a waterproof bag against Theo's heart, with two patrol boats clawing through the maelstrom behind them and gaining.

Marisol fought her way up beside her at the wheel, shouting against the roar: "The dock at Landfall! The reckoning — we said we'd read it publicly — the storm breaks by morning —"

"Morning's a long way off," Paloma said, "and Bell's between us and everything."

Lightning split the dark ahead, and in its white flash she saw the mangroves rise up out of the sea like a drowning forest reaching for help — and behind them, faint through the rain, the searchlights turning to follow.
