## 18. Cave-In

The eye of the storm was a lie of light.

Marisol had told them so before they ever went down — had said it plainly, standing in the flooded mangroves with the ledgers' hiding place still a rumor in her mouth: *the sky will open like a wound, and we will have minutes, not hours.* Paloma had nodded the way she nodded at everything, as if the world were a bar she'd been negotiating with all her life. Theo had simply started counting.

Now, in the inner chamber, with the sealed ledgers of Aurelio Voss wrapped in oilcloth and lashed to her chest, Marisol felt the lie begin to close.

The chamber was a cathedral that the sea had built and then abandoned mid-thought. Vaulted rock overhead, ribbed with white mineral veins that caught their lantern light and threw it back in slow, wet shimmer. The tide pools at their feet were black glass, so still they held their reflections like promises. For eleven minutes the storm had been a distant rumor — a heartbeat felt through stone rather than heard — and they had spent those minutes the way you spend blood: carefully, and all of it.

The ledgers were real. That was the thing she kept returning to, the fact her hands kept confirming through the oilcloth, pressing the corners of the books as if they might dissolve. Forty years of them. Aurelio's own hand in the margins, and the confession letter folded into the last volume like a knife left in a wound. Her grandmother's name was in the third ledger, in a column of names, in a column of *prices*. Marisol had read it by lantern light and had not cried, because the water in this place was already doing enough of that.

"We're past time," Paloma said. Her voice did the thing it always did — flat, certain, the voice of a woman reading a river's mood. "The eye wall's coming back. I can feel it in the floor."

"You can't feel a storm through stone," Theo said, but he was already moving, already shouldering his share of the oilcloth bundles, and Marisol saw that he believed her anyway. That was the education Paloma gave without meaning to: you learned to trust a captain's skin before you trusted your own ears.

"I can feel *that* storm," Paloma said. "The one that raised me."

They went for the squeeze.

It was the only way out now. The main entrance — the great yawning mouth that the impossible low tide had exposed, the one half of Landfall had watched Theo photograph like a pilgrim at a shrine — was already drowning. Marisol had seen it on their way through: the sea coming back into the bay not as a wave but as a decision, a slow black rising that filled the cave's throat from the bottom up. In an hour it would be a submerged tunnel again, the cave a lung holding its breath until the next generation's freak tide. In twenty minutes it would be impassable. In ten, the surge proper would arrive, and the pressure differential would turn the entrance into something worse than a wall.

The squeeze passage was a fissure in the chamber's rear wall, barely wide enough for a slim body, rising at an angle toward a blowhole vent somewhere in the cliff face above. Marisol had found it on their first descent, on instinct — the instinct of a woman who had spent eleven years diving wrecks and caves and the spaces between her family's silences. She'd marked it with a chalk arrow. She'd said, lightly, "If it all goes wrong, we go out through the mountain's ear."

Now it all went wrong, and they went out through the mountain's ear.

Marisol went last. That was not chivalry; it was arithmetic. The ledgers rode against her chest in a harness she'd rigged from dive webbing, and the bundles were rigid, and the passage's narrowest point — the pinch, Theo had called it, with the cheerful horror of the young — was exactly as wide as a body and no wider. The ledgers would not bend. She would have to push them through ahead of her, one at a time, and then follow.

"Paloma first," Marisol said. "Then Theo. I'll feed the ledgers through after you."

Paloma looked at her for one second too long. Marisol knew what she was seeing: not a stranger anymore, not exactly. Three days of bilgewater and storm and shared purpose had done what three days should not be able to do. "You come through behind them," Paloma said. "That's not a request."

"Nothing about me is a request, Captain."

Something moved at the corner of Paloma's mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where smiles were kept. Then she turned and went into the rock, and the mountain swallowed her, lantern light shrinking to a coin, a spark, a memory.

Theo went next. He paused at the fissure's mouth and looked back at Marisol, and his face in the lantern light was sixteen years old and a hundred years old at once. "You're right behind us," he said. It wasn't a question, but she answered it anyway, because he deserved the lie or the truth, whichever it turned out to be.

"Right behind you."

He went in. The light went with him. And Marisol stood alone in the drowning cathedral with forty years of stolen names strapped to her chest and began, with the calm she had practiced in a hundred bad places, to feed her grandfather's crimes through a crack in the world.

* * *

The first ledger went through at shoulder height, where the fissure was widest. She heard it scrape, heard Theo's grunt on the other side, heard Paloma's voice, muffled: *got it, next.* The second. The third — the one with her grandmother's name — she held a moment longer than she needed to, her palm flat against the oilcloth, as if the book could feel her.

*Elena Voss. Taken at fourteen. Debited for her own passage.*

She pushed it through and watched it vanish into the rock like a body into a grave, and hated that the image came, and hated more that it was true, and kept working.

The fourth ledger stuck.

It wedged at the pinch, corner caught on a lip of stone, and when Marisol pulled it back to re-angle it, the oilcloth tore against the rock with a sound like a small scream. She worked it free, turned it, fed it edge-first, and felt the moment it caught again — felt the resistance travel down her arms into her chest, into the harness, into her ribs, as if the mountain had decided to hold her family's sins a while longer.

And then the sea arrived.

She felt it before she heard it — a pressure change, a softening in her ears, the tide pools at her feet shivering though nothing touched them. Then the sound came: a vast, wet exhalation from the direction of the entrance, the sound of the bay pouring itself back into the cave's throat. The water in the pools began to rise. Not gradually. *Decisively.* Ankle-deep. Then knee-deep, and the cold of it went through her like a verdict.

"Marisol!" Theo's voice, distorted by rock. "The water — what's happening—"

"Keep moving!" she shouted at the fissure. "Do not come back for me! Push the ledgers to the vent!"

The fifth ledger went through. The sixth. The water was at her thighs now, and the chamber's acoustics had changed — she could hear the entrance flooding behind her, a deep gargling roar, the sea finding its old throat and clearing it. The pressure in her ears climbed. The lantern, set on a shelf of rock, threw jumping shadows that made the mineral veins look like the ribs of something breathing.

She got the confession letter through last, folded small, and heard Paloma's voice on the other side say, "That's all of it," and Marisol thought: *no.* Not all of it. There was still the matter of the woman on the wrong side of the pinch with the sea coming in like a debt collector.

* * *

She went into the fissure headfirst, the way she'd have entered a wreck, arms extended, and the rock took her the way rock takes everything — without malice, without mercy, without any interest at all in her plans.

For a while it was almost working. The passage was a chimney of stone, angled upward, and she moved through it the way she moved through everything underwater: exhale, compress, pull, rest, exhale. The rock pressed her from both sides, held her like a hand, and she had been held by worse things and gotten free of them. Behind and below her, she could hear the chamber filling — the pitch of the water sound rising, the roar gaining body. Above her, faint and impossible, a gray thread of light: the vent. The mountain's ear. The sky.

Then the surge hit the entrance.

She didn't hear it so much as *become* aware of it — a change in the water below her, a sudden heavy push of air through the fissure, compressed, screaming through the narrow stone like breath through a flute. The pressure wave shoved her upward half a meter, slammed her chest against a ledge of rock, and then the mountain moved.

Not much. A meter of the passage wall, maybe less. But a meter in a passage that had no meters to spare is a catastrophe, and Marisol felt the rock settle against her left shoulder and hip with the finality of a door closing.

Pinned.

The word arrived with a strange calm, the way diagnoses arrive. She had seen divers pinned before. She had once helped recover one. She knew the mathematics: a person can hold their breath four minutes, maybe five, if they're lucky and cold and calm. The chamber below her was filling. The water in the fissure was rising to meet her. And the rock was not going to move again, because rock does not do second acts.

Above her, gray light. Below her, black water, climbing. Around her, her own breath, loud and small.

"Marisol." Paloma's voice, from beyond the pinch, very controlled, which meant it was anything but. "Talk to me."

"I'm pinned at the shoulder," Marisol said, and made her voice come out level, and was almost proud of it. "Left side. The wall shifted when the surge hit."

"Can you back out?"

"Back out into a flooding chamber. No."

Silence, and in it she could hear the water rising below her, a sound like the mountain swallowing.

"Then I'm coming back through," Theo said.

"Theo." She closed her eyes. "If you come back through that pinch, there won't be room to get either of us out, and the ledgers drown with all of us. Paloma. Don't let him."

"Marisol—" His voice cracked on her name, sixteen years old again, and she loved him for it, and refused him anyway.

"Listen to me." She made her voice into the voice she used on frightened dive students, the voice that is calm because it has decided to be, the voice that is a rope thrown across black water. "The ledgers are out. The confession is out. Whatever happens on my side of this rock, the names are safe, and the names were the whole point. Do you understand? We were never diving for gold. We were diving for *evidence*, and the evidence is in your hands, and your job now is to be a witness. Both of you. That's your job. Be witnesses."

"Marisol." Paloma again, and now the control was gone, and what was underneath it was worse and better: a woman who had spent a lifetime leaving places, discovering she did not want to leave this one. "There's always a third way. I've spent fifty years finding third ways."

"Then find one fast," Marisol said, "because I'm about to be underwater."

* * *

The water reached her chin at what she judged to be the two-minute mark, though time in a squeeze is a dishonest currency.

She had air. That was the miracle of the vent — the fissure's upper reaches trapped a pocket of it, a lens of breath pressed against the rock above her, and as long as she kept her face turned up into that gray light, she could breathe. The water below her kept rising, but the pocket rose with it, shrinking, a country losing territory in a war. She had, she estimated, the height of the pocket. Maybe a meter of air. Maybe less.

Her left shoulder screamed where the rock held it. She tested the pin again — exhaled fully, collapsed her chest, twisted — and felt the stone grind against bone and give nothing. Her hip was worse, wedged behind a spur, and she understood with a diver's cold clarity that she could not go up and could not go back, and that the only direction left was the one nobody chooses: *through*.

Dislocation. That was the third way. Her shoulder had been dislocated once before, off Puerto Viejo, a wreck dive gone wrong, and she remembered the anatomy of it the way you remember a betrayal: intimately. The joint was already half out of its socket where the rock levered against it. If she could finish the job the mountain had started — if she could tear herself loose the way a animal tears loose from a trap, leaving the caught part behind — the shoulder would pass where the shoulder had not.

The water took her mouth. She turned her face into the last of the air pocket, cheek against cold stone, and the gray light above her was the size of a coin now, and she thought, with a clarity that felt like a gift: *my grandmother was fourteen, and she went into the hold, and she did not get to choose anything. I get to choose this.*

"Marisol!" Theo, above her, his voice arriving thin through stone. "The pocket's shrinking — we can see the water in the vent—"

"I know," she said, and the water took half the words.

She got her right arm above her head, into the gap, fingers finding the lip of rock where the light came through. She locked her hand there. She planted her boots against the wall behind her. And she said, out loud, to no one, to the mountain, to the woman in the ledger: "No."

She pulled.

The pain was total and white and it had a sound, and the sound was her own name in her own mouth. The joint tore loose with a wet, grinding wrongness that she felt in her teeth, and her body convulsed, and for one long second she was nothing but nerve and water and will — and then her shoulder came free of the rock the way a key comes free of a lock, and she was through.

She came out of the vent like something born.

The blowhole mouth was a jagged oval in the cliff face, half-drowned, storm-smashed, and she dragged herself out of it onto a shelf of wet black rock with one arm dead at her side and the sea exploding against the cliffs ten meters below and the rain coming down in ropes. The sky was no longer the eye's pale lie; it was the eye wall, green-black and boiling, and the wind hit her like a wall of hands.

Below her, at the base of the cliff, two of Bell's patrol boats pitched in the surge, searchlights sweeping the water, the rocks, the cave mouth that no longer existed. She saw the light find her — saw it flinch, hold. She heard shouting, thin and torn away. Bell's men, watching the entrance flood, watching the mountain close, able to do nothing. She raised her good arm, not in triumph, not in defiance, but so they could see she was alive — because whatever else Bell's men were, they were witnesses now, and witnesses were the whole point.

The ledgers. Where were—

There. Twenty meters along the cliff top, at the vent's upper exit where the fissure broke the surface of the world: Paloma, on her knees in the rain, hauling the oilcloth bundles out of the rock with her one good arm and her teeth if she had to, and Theo beside her, and the gray oilcloth was dark with water but *sealed*, she'd sealed it, and the names were dry.

Marisol got her feet under her. Her left arm hung like something belonging to another woman. The storm leaned on her. She leaned back.

And down the cliff path, splashing through floodwater with lanterns and rifles and faces gone gray with the sight of what they'd seen — a woman tearing herself out of a drowning mountain — came Bell's men, and behind them, unmistakable even at this distance, even through the rain, the broad, wet, furious silhouette of the harbormaster himself.

Marisol looked at Paloma. Paloma looked at her. And Paloma Dunmore, who had spent a lifetime running, stood up on the cliff with forty years of stolen names in her arms and did not take one single step away.

"Get behind me," Paloma said, to both of them, as Bell came up the path. "And whatever happens — *witness.*"
