## 19. The Reckoning at the Dock

The rain had been falling for so long that Paloma had stopped hearing it as weather and started hearing it as a condition of the world, the way she'd stopped noticing her own heartbeat years ago. It came down in gray sheets off the cliffs of Landfall, drummed on the Kestrel's patched deck, filled the gutters of the flooded market until they ran like small rivers toward a bay that had swallowed its own shoreline. And through all of it, she walked.

She walked because running was over. She understood that now with the strange clarity of a woman who had spent thirty years outrunning things and had finally, somewhere between a flooded jail cell and a maintenance grate at flood tide, run out of reasons to keep moving. Marisol's hand was still cold from the seawall water where it had gripped hers and hauled her up into the storm. Theo was somewhere behind her, shouting her name over the wind. Bell's men were ahead, lanterns bobbing like drowned stars along the dock road, closing the last gap between her and the open channel where the Kestrel waited — patched, battered, and for one more hour, free.

*You could still make it,* said the old voice in her chest, the one that had talked her out of ports and into rivers her whole life. *The mangroves. The dark. You've done it before.*

But the ledgers were wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs, and Aurelio Voss's confession was folded inside her shirt, dry against her skin, and every step she took away from Landfall would be a step taken by a woman who deserved to be chased.

So she didn't run. She turned and faced the lanterns coming down the dock road, and she climbed onto the roof of the fish-packing shed at the head of the pier, boots finding the old crates stacked against its wall, and she stood up in the rain where everyone could see her.

* * *

The town came out of its houses the way water comes out of a cracked dam — slowly at first, then all at once. She saw lamplight bloom in windows along the flooded street. She saw faces under oilskins and umbrellas, under overturned market baskets, under nothing at all. The blind storyteller from the market stood nearest the shed, rain streaming down his ancient face, his white eyes turned up toward her voice as if he could see better than any of them. Old Sefa Voss-Adun, whose grandmother's name Paloma had read carved in salt-black stone two nights ago. The ferry families. The net-menders. Children perched on their fathers' shoulders because the water in the streets was chest-deep on anyone smaller.

And at the front of them all, wading through his own drowned town with two patrol boats' worth of men fanning out behind him, came Harbormaster Bell.

"Paloma Dunmore." His voice carried flat and hard across the water. "You're a fugitive. Step down from there."

"You're right," she called back, and her voice surprised her — steady, river-deep, the voice she used in bad channels when panic would kill everyone aboard. "I am. I broke a man out of your jail tonight. I've been running from the law since before some of you were born. So listen to me while I'm standing still, because this is the only time you'll ever catch me doing it."

Bell raised a hand and his men slowed, uncertain. Rain hammered the tin roof beneath her boots. Somewhere out in the black bay, thunder rolled along the cliffs like a door being closed.

"I have in my hands," Paloma said, "the private ledgers of Aurelio Voss."

The name went through the crowd like a current through still water. She felt it move — a shiver of hats and hoods, a child's sharp intake of breath.

"Forty-one years ago, Aurelio Voss owned nine ships. He did not buy nine ships. He did not build nine ships. He took them — hulls, cargo, and crews — from indentured families who signed seven-year contracts to work their way to free land, and he never let a single one of them off. He worked them until they drowned, or until they ran, and then he wrote in his own hand that they were lost to weather." She pulled the confession from her shirt, unfolded it, held it high. The rain tried to take it; she cupped her body around the paper like a woman sheltering a flame. "This is his letter. Written the year before he died. He knew what he'd done. He sealed it in a cave below these cliffs with the manifests of every crew he stole, and he left it there for a tide low enough to shame him, and no tide has been low enough until this week."

"Those documents are seized evidence," Bell shouted. "Recovered illegally! Whatever's in them—"

"Read it yourself!" Paloma's voice cracked out over his like a whip. "Or are you afraid of what forty years of harbor fees bought you?"

A murmur. Bell's jaw worked. Behind him, one of his own deputies — the young one, Okafor, who had once waved Theo across the mooring dock without checking papers — shifted his weight and did not step forward when Bell gestured.

Paloma looked down at the paper in her hands. Her hands were shaking now. Not from cold. From the strange, terrifying lightness of a woman about to stop carrying something she'd carried so long she'd forgotten its weight.

"My name is Paloma Dunmore," she said. "I have a warrant from Corvane Port for smuggling I didn't commit, for a crime my partner committed and let me hang for, and I ran from it for eleven years. I know what it is to be named guilty by a piece of paper. So believe me when I tell you what this paper says, because I have spent my whole life learning the difference between the law and the truth."

She began to read.

* * *

*"I, Aurelio Emmanuele Voss, being of sound mind and unsound soul, set down here what no ledger in my office will ever show."*

Her voice carried strangely in the rain — not loud, but clear, the way sound carries over open water at dawn. The crowd had gone utterly silent except for the rain itself, which fell on ten thousand surfaces and made a kind of held breath.

*"On the fourteenth of March, forty-one years past, I took possession of the brig Andalusa and her full complement of twenty-six souls under indenture. I did not honor the contracts. I burned the contracts. I told the port authority they were lost with the ship Ferria, which I scuttled myself in deep water for the insurance. The twenty-six were not lost. They were mine. I worked them on the Andalusa, then the Meridian Star, then the Salt Road, until death or desertion thinned them, and I replaced them the same way, three more times, with three more crews whose names I list herein."*

Paloma's throat tightened. She kept reading.

*"I tell myself they signed freely. I tell myself the sea takes what it takes. But I know the arithmetic of what I did: I grew rich on labor I never paid for, and I buried the proof in stone because I could not bury it in myself. If any hand finds this, let it be known that the fortune of Aurelio Voss was built by the stolen, and belongs to the stolen, and every coin of it is owed."*

She looked up. The rain was thinning — the storm breaking apart overhead the way storms do when they've finally spent themselves, ragged gaps opening in the cloud where moonlight fell through in long silver columns onto the flooded street.

"The manifests," she said. "Ninety-one names. Ninety-one people whose grandchildren are standing in this rain right now." She began to read them. "Marisol Voss-Adun, granddaughter of Ines Voss-Adun, Andalusa crew. Sefa Voss-Adun, son of the same. The Okonkwo family, descendants of Daniel Okonkwo, Meridian Star. The Reyes family, of Lucia Reyes, Salt Road. The Amari family. The Duval family. The Ferreira family of the drowned village of Puerto Cielo—"

At that name, an old woman in the crowd made a sound Paloma had never heard before and would never forget — half sob, half song — and the crowd around her closed like hands around a coal.

*Puerto Cielo.* The drowned town. The one the storyteller sang about, the one whose families had scattered into Landfall's margins and become ferrymen and net-menders and nobody, for forty years, nobody.

Paloma read until her voice went raw. She read ninety-one names into the night, each one a person the sea had supposedly taken, each one a person a rich man had taken instead. And with every name, she watched something happen to the town below her that she had never once seen happen anywhere she'd ever fled through: she watched people stop being ashamed. Shoulders straightened under wet oilskin. The old woman from Puerto Cielo lifted her chin. A boy — no older than Theo — repeated his great-grandfather's name aloud to his mother like a prayer he'd just been given permission to say.

* * *

"That's enough." Bell's voice, but wrong — thinner than it had been an hour ago, thinner than it had ever been in her hearing. He pushed through his own men toward the shed. "Dunmore, those papers are worthless. Forged. A con — she's a smuggler, she'll sell you anything—"

"Harbormaster." Deputy Okafor's voice. Quiet. Terrible. "My grandfather was Tomás Okafor. Salt Road crew." He stepped out of the line of lanterns, and Paloma saw that his hand rested on his baton but pointed at the ground. "Third name on the second manifest. My family always said we came from nowhere. We thought we were nobody's." He looked up at Paloma on the roof, rain and moonlight on his face. "Ma'am. Read us the rest."

Bell grabbed his arm. "Okafor. You're under my command—"

"No, sir." Okafor removed Bell's hand from his sleeve, gently, the way you'd remove something dead. "I don't think I am."

It moved through the deputies like the tide itself. The big woman with the scarred knuckles — Vashti, who had guarded Paloma's cell — turned her lantern around and shone it up at the confession in Paloma's hands instead of at Paloma. Another deputy lowered his baton. Another simply stepped aside, opening a lane in the line of law, and through that lane came the whole town, wading forward, hundreds of them, until the flooded street below the fish-packing shed was a sea of upturned faces and Bell stood alone in the middle of it, a man discovering that authority is only a story people agree to believe, and that his had just ended.

"You're finished," someone said, and it wasn't Paloma. It was the blind storyteller, his sightless eyes fixed on Bell with terrible accuracy. "We know about the survey boats. We know about the fees. We know who you sold our history to, Harbormaster. The judge from upriver arrives on tomorrow's mail skiff. I suggest you practice your explanation."

Bell looked around at his town — at the faces, at the water, at the sky tearing open above him — and Paloma watched him understand, in real time, the thing she had spent eleven years failing to understand: that you cannot outrun a reckoning, because a reckoning does not chase you. It waits. It stands on a dock in the rain and reads names until the truth gets too heavy for anyone to carry anymore, and then it sets the truth down, and everyone picks it up together, and it cannot be dropped again.

He turned and walked away into the dark, and not one lantern followed him.

* * *

Paloma stayed on the roof a long time after that. Long enough for the rain to stop entirely, for the clouds to pull apart like bandages, for the moon to stand full and astonished over a bay that glittered like spilled coins. Below her, the town did not disperse. They lit lamps on their porches and in their windows, dozens of them, dozens more, until Landfall blazed against the cliffs like a signal fire answering another signal fire, and people brought food out into the ruined streets and ate it standing in ankle-deep water, laughing, crying, saying names to each other across the flood.

Theo found her there. Climbed the crates and sat beside her on the wet tin, close enough that their shoulders touched.

"You didn't run," he said.

"No."

"You always run."

"I know." She looked out at the lights. At the Kestrel riding her mooring beyond the pier, patched and ugly and alive. At Marisol standing on the dock below, wringing seawater from her hair, looking up at them both with an expression Paloma was beginning to recognize as family. "Turns out I was waiting for a reason to stop."

Theo followed her gaze down to the crowd, where old Sefa Voss-Adun was telling the children something, gesturing at the cave cliffs, and the children were listening the way children listen to legends that have suddenly, impossibly, come true.

"There's going to be a trial," Theo said. "For Bell. And for you — the warrant. Tomorrow, when the judge comes."

"I know."

"Aren't you scared?"

Paloma considered it honestly, the way she considered weather. "Terrified," she said. "First time in eleven years I've stood still long enough to feel it." She smiled, and found that it fit her face like an old coat, rumpled but warm. "But here's the thing about standing still, Theo. When you stop running, you finally find out who's willing to stand with you."

Below them, the whole town was still there. Every lamp still burning. Nobody leaving.

Tomorrow the mail skiff would bring a judge, and questions, and whatever justice looked like when it finally arrived late and soaked to the skin. Tomorrow the ledgers would need to go into careful hands, and the warrant would need answering, and Bell's lawyers would come like gulls after a catch. But tonight the rain had stopped, and ninety-one names hung in the salt air over Landfall like stars that had finally been allowed to fall home, and Paloma Dunmore sat on a rooftop in the first town she'd ever chosen, and waited for morning without wanting to outrun a single minute of it.
