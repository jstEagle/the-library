## 20. Dry Season

The rain stopped the way a long argument ends — not all at once, but in smaller and smaller concessions, until one morning Theo woke to silence so complete it felt like a held breath.

He lay in his bunk aboard the *Kestrel* and listened to it. No drumming on the deck above. No water gurgling through the scuppers. Just the creak of the hull against her mooring lines and, somewhere across the harbor, the first gull of the dry season screaming its idiot joy at a sky that had finally remembered how to be blue.

He climbed out into the cockpit and stood blinking like something newly hatched.

Landfall had drowned for six weeks. Now the town was surfacing. Rooftops he'd half forgotten emerged from the receding floodwater along the low streets, dark and dripping, steaming in the early sun. The market stalls were being unbolted and dragged back out onto the boardwalk by people moving with the brisk, wordless efficiency of a crew that had done this drill before. A boy was bailing out a canoe full of chickens. The whole delta smelled of wet wood and river mud and, faintly, impossibly, of coffee.

Theo leaned on the rail and let it wash over him.

Six weeks ago he'd been a deckhand with a leaky boat and no plans past the next port. Now there were ledgers wrapped in oilcloth stashed under his bunk — the ledgers, the ones that had cost Marisol a cave-in and Paloma a night in a flooding jail and all three of them whatever innocence they'd had left about how the world worked. Six weeks ago he hadn't known what it felt like to belong anywhere.

He was still working out whether he liked it. Belonging, it turned out, came with weight.

"You're up early." Paloma's voice came from below, rough with sleep. She emerged a minute later with two chipped mugs, handed him one without asking, and settled against the rail beside him. She looked thinner than she had before the storm season. The gray in her hair seemed to have won some territory while nobody was watching. But her eyes, when she looked out over the recovering town, were steadier than he'd ever seen them.

"First dry morning," Theo said. "Felt rude to sleep through it."

"Mm." She drank. "Marisol's already gone up to the courthouse. Judge Oyelaran's boat came in on the dawn tide."

"I know. I helped carry her trunk up the dock stairs." He grinned. "She tipped me. An actual coin. Like I was a porter."

"Take the coin," Paloma said. "From what Marisol says, that woman doesn't part with anything easily. If she gave you money, you've already made an impression."

* * *

The courthouse of Landfall had never, in living memory, hosted anything more dramatic than a dispute over crab-trap boundaries and one memorable case involving a stolen accordion. It was a squat building of coral stone at the top of the hill, its steps furred green with damp, and by nine o'clock on that first dry morning every step was occupied.

They had come from everywhere. Fishermen who'd lost fathers to Voss ships that sailed heavy and returned light. Granddaughters of indentured crews, wearing their best clothes even though the hems were still salt-stiff from the flood. The blind storyteller, Old Sef, sat at the very top of the steps with his face turned toward the doors as if he could see straight through them, and when Theo squeezed past him the old man caught his sleeve.

"They're going to read the names," Sef said. His voice was soft, meant only for him. "All morning, they're going to read the names. You understand what that is?"

"A trial," Theo said.

"No." Sef shook his head slowly. "A resurrection. Trials are for the living. This is for everyone else too."

Inside, the courtroom was hot and close despite the windows being thrown open to the drying air. Judge Oyelaran was a tall woman from upriver with a voice like a slow-moving current and a manner that suggested she had heard every lie ever told and found most of them boring. She had been brought down on a chartered packet boat specifically because nobody in the delta could be trusted to judge this fairly — not after Harbormaster Bell had spent years collecting fees, favors, and fear from every family in the district.

Bell himself sat in the dock, flanked by two constables brought upriver with the judge. He had shaved. Someone had found him a clean shirt. He sat with his hands folded and his chin up, wearing the expression of a man who believed, genuinely, that the machinery would protect him — that men like him were what the machinery was *for*.

Theo watched him from the gallery and felt something complicated move in his chest. Not hatred, exactly. Hatred required thinking someone mattered. What he felt was closer to watching a rotting piling finally get pulled out of the water: satisfaction, and underneath it, a small cold wonder at how much had grown around it.

The ledgers went onto the judge's table in their oilcloth, and Marisol unwrapped them with hands that did not quite stay steady.

Theo had seen them before, of course. In the cave, by lantern light, with the tide groaning somewhere behind them. But seeing them here — under electric lights, in front of a judge, in front of the whole town — changed them somehow. They stopped being treasure. They became what they had always been: paper. Names. Dates. Payments made and payments withheld. Aurelio Voss's own handwriting, in the confession letter, saying plainly what he had done — bought crews with debts they could never work off, kept wages in company scrip worthless outside his own stores, insured ships he knew were unseaworthy because dead men couldn't file claims.

Judge Oyelaran read for two hours without stopping. The courtroom stayed absolutely silent except for the turning of pages and, once, a woman in the third row who began quietly weeping and was not asked to stop.

Then the reading of the manifests began, and Theo understood what Old Sef had meant.

Name by name, clerk by clerk, the stolen were called back into the world. Men and women who had signed articles of indenture and vanished into Voss's fleet — some drowned, some worked to debt-death, some simply erased, their shares of pay rolled forward into nothing. Their descendants sat in the gallery hearing grandmothers' names spoken aloud in a courtroom for the first time in sixty years. Each time a name landed, Theo saw it strike someone. A stiffening of shoulders. A hand pressed to a mouth. An old man near the window who mouthed each name silently as it was read, like a prayer he'd been keeping ready for decades.

Paloma sat beside him, arms crossed, jaw set. But when the clerk read the name *Adunni Ferreira*, Theo saw Paloma's eyes close briefly, and he remembered — she'd never said why, but he knew — the name of the first boat she'd ever crewed on, the one her father had died aboard, a Voss ship sailing under-insured into a storm just like the one they'd survived.

"It's real," she murmured, mostly to herself. "It's actually happening. Paper beat money."

"Not yet," Theo said, because he'd learned caution from her and it felt right to give it back.

But it kept happening anyway.

* * *

Bell's defense lasted three days and collapsed like a sandbar at spring tide.

He had lawyers — good ones, flown in at expense that made the whole town mutter about where exactly a harbormaster's salary had gone. They argued that the ledgers were forgeries. Judge Oyelaran had them tested, ink and paper both, and the results read aloud on the second afternoon drew a sound from the gallery that Theo had never heard in a courtroom before: not a gasp, but a long collective exhale, forty people breathing out at once, six weeks of held breath finally released.

They argued that Marisol Voss was a disgraced heir with a grudge. Marisol took the stand in her diving gear's shore clothes — canvas trousers, a salt-bleached shirt, hair still short from the cave — and answered every question in a flat, unhurried voice that gave the lawyers nothing to grip. When one of them sneered about her estrangement from her grandfather, she said only:

"He disowned me for asking questions. I'm asking better ones now."

And they argued — this was Bell's own contribution, delivered in that reasonable baritone that had charmed the town for years — that everything in those books was legal at the time. Contracts were contracts. Debts were debts. The law of that era had permitted what the ledgers recorded.

Judge Oyelaran looked at him over her spectacles for a long moment.

"Yes," she said. "That is precisely the problem, Mr. Bell. And it is why you are not being tried for what was legal sixty years ago. You are being tried for what you did last month. Obstruction. Extortion. The attempted seizure of these documents by armed patrol boats during a declared storm emergency. The falsification of safety inspections on vessels whose owners refused your arrangements." She turned a page. "Shall I continue? I have four more days of it written down."

Bell's chin finally came down.

The arrest itself was almost quiet. The constables read the charges; Bell stood; his lawyer whispered something urgent that Bell waved off. As they walked him down the aisle between the benches, past the fishermen and the granddaughters and Old Sef sitting serene at the end of the row, nobody shouted. Nobody threw anything. The town just watched him go with the level, unhurried gaze of people watching a floodwater mark get scrubbed off a wall.

Except for one moment. At the door, Bell paused and looked back — not at the judge, not at Marisol. At Paloma.

Whatever he meant to say, he didn't say it. Paloma met his eyes and didn't blink, and after a moment Bell looked away first, and the door closed behind him, and that was that.

Outside, the whole town had waited without waiting — clustered in the square, pretending to shop, pretending to gossip. When the news came out, there was cheering, yes, and someone produced a fiddle, and by evening the square smelled of fried plantain and rum. But Theo noticed something else underneath the celebration. People kept touching each other. Hands on shoulders, hands clasped, an old woman holding Marisol's face in both her palms and saying something too quiet for anyone else to hear. Sixty years of a theft nobody could prove, and then six weeks of a fight nobody expected to win — and now the touching, all that touching, like people confirming each other were solid.

Paloma stood at the edge of it with a cup of rum she wasn't drinking.

"You could go in," Theo said. "They'd want you to."

"They're celebrating Marisol today. Rightly." Paloma shrugged. "I'm not good at being celebrated. I'm barely adequate at being thanked."

"You're getting better at staying, though."

Something moved across her face — surprise, maybe, that he'd say it out loud. She looked at him for a long moment, and then, instead of deflecting, she knocked her shoulder gently against his. From Paloma Dunmore, that was practically an embrace.

* * *

The warrant came up on the fourth day, folded inside a dispatch case carried by the same mail skiff that had once delivered it as a threat.

Constable Reyes — the young one, the one who'd stood awkwardly on the dock the day Paloma surrendered rather than let Bell use her arrest as leverage against the *Kestrel* — came up the gangplank holding the papers like they might bite him. Paloma was re-caulking a seam amidships and didn't stop working when he cleared his throat.

"Captain Dunmore. There's, ah. News. From Corvane." Corvane — the port town upriver, the one she'd been running from for eleven years. Theo put down his tools. Paloma kept scraping old oakum from the seam, steady as a metronome, but Theo saw her knuckles whiten around the iron.

"The warrant," Reyes said. "It's dismissed. Withdrawn entirely. Your partner — Dario Vex, the smuggler — he confessed. Full confession, sworn statement, filed three weeks ago. Said he alone ran the cargo, said you took the blame because he had a family and you didn't, said—" the constable glanced down at the dispatch, uncomfortable, "—said you'd spent eleven years paying for his crime and he was done letting you."

The caulking iron stopped.

For a moment the only sound was the water lapping at the hull and, far off, the fiddle still going in the square. Then Paloma set the iron down very carefully, the way you set down something fragile, and held out her hand for the papers.

She read them standing in the midday sun. Theo watched her read them twice. Her face did almost nothing — which is how he knew it was doing everything.

"Eleven years," she said finally. Her voice came out strange, sanded smooth. "Eleven years I charted my whole life around that piece of paper. Every route, every port, every town I wouldn't stop in. I built a map of the world with holes in it." She laughed — one short, disbelieving note. "And he just... confessed. Three weeks ago. While I was hiding in mangroves like a criminal."

"What are you feeling?" Theo asked, because he was twenty-one and had not yet learned which questions not to ask.

Paloma considered it honestly, which was also new.

"Like someone cut a rope I forgot I was tied to," she said. "And now I don't know which way is down." She folded the papers once, twice, and tucked them into her coat — not thrown overboard, Theo noticed. Kept. Proof, maybe. Evidence that the law could arrive late and still arrive true. "Go find Marisol. Tell her we're due at the courthouse tomorrow — the families' claims hearings start, and she'll want us there. And Theo—"

He stopped at the gangplank.

"When this is all finished," Paloma said, looking out at the harbor, at the *Kestrel*, at the town climbing green and dripping out of its six-week drowning, "there's a conversation you and I need to have. About the boat. About what comes next. I've been putting it off because—" she exhaled, "—because I'm out of practice at futures. But the dry season's here, and the tides are settling, and I think it's time."

She picked the caulking iron back up. Conversation over, apparently. But as Theo headed down the ramp toward the market, he caught her reflection in the harbor water — standing alone on her deck, taking the folded dismissal papers back out of her coat just to look at them again, like a woman checking that a door she'd assumed was locked forever had really, truly swung open.

Tomorrow the claims hearings would begin, and the fortune of Aurelio Voss would start finding its way home to the families it was stolen from. And somewhere in the middle of all that, Paloma wanted to talk about the future — *their* future, hers and his, and what the *Kestrel* would carry when the dry season opened the river lanes again.

Theo walked faster, grinning like an idiot, already rehearsing what he'd say.
