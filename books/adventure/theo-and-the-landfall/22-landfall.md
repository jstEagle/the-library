## 22. Landfall

The Kestrel smelled different now.

Theo noticed it the moment he stepped aboard in the blue half-light before dawn, his boots finding the deck by memory rather than sight. For years the boat had carried the scent of diesel and mildew and the particular sourness of a hull that was always, somewhere, taking on water. Now there was fresh paint — Paloma had insisted on a proper coat of white for the cabin house, the first she'd ever paid for without haggling — and beneath it, cedar shavings from the new bilge stringers Marisol had helped him fit during the dry season. And under all of it, faint but unmistakable, salt air that had stopped being an enemy and become simply the smell of home.

He ran his hand along the wheel. It was his wheel now. That still hadn't quite settled into his bones, even after three months of papers signed in salt air, even after Paloma had pressed the brass key into his palm with a grip that said *this is real, don't argue*. Some mornings he woke expecting to be a deckhand again, expecting her voice from the pilothouse — *Theo, watch your line* — and some mornings he woke and remembered that the voice giving orders now was his own.

Today was the first scheduled run of the Landfall Ferry Line. Not a smuggler's schedule, not a fleeing schedule, not a tide-and-prayer schedule. A printed one. Tacked up at the market, painted on a board outside the harbormaster's office — the new harbormaster's office, because Bell's old sign had come down the same week Bell himself had gone upriver in irons. The board read: **LANDFALL–CAVE MEMORIAL RUN, DAILY, WEATHER PERMITTING.** Theo had painted the letters himself, standing on a ladder while half the market shouted advice about his kerning.

The weather was permitting. The dry season had settled over the delta like a hand smoothing a wrinkled sheet — high pale skies, a wind out of the east so gentle it barely troubled the flags, and the river running low and clear between banks of drying reed. Six months ago this same water had tried to drown them all. Today it lay flat as hammered tin, waiting to carry passengers.

Theo lit the lanterns, checked the fuel lines twice, and stood at the rail watching Landfall wake up.

The town had changed too, though more slowly than the boat. The flooded market street had been drained and planked over properly now, the stalls rebuilt on stilts that no longer leaned like drunkards. The blind storyteller — Old Sefa, though everyone just called him the Storyteller — had a new bench near the ferry dock, and every evening a crowd gathered to hear the tale that had become the town's founding story: the cave, the manifests, the names of the stolen read aloud into a breaking storm. He always ended the same way, his milky eyes turned toward the sound of the sea: *"Aurelio Voss built his fortune on borrowed lives, and the tide kept the receipts."*

The memorial site opened next month. Marisol had fought for two seasons to make it happen — not a treasure exhibit, not a museum of gold, but a hall of names. Every indentured crew member carved into those cave walls would have their name cut again into stone above ground, where families could touch them. Descendants had been arriving for weeks, off the mail skiffs and coastal steamers, people who'd never known why their great-grandparents vanished from the shipping ledgers. Theo had watched a woman in her seventies stand before the provisional list pinned to the customs-house wall, find her grandmother's name, and sit down right there on the wet boards and weep until someone brought her tea.

That was what they'd pulled out of the dark. Not gold. Names. Though there had been gold too, in the end — enough of Aurelio's recovered estate, once the ledgers proved the theft, to fund the memorial and restitution payments that would stretch out for years. Enough, Marisol said, to pay a debt that could never fully be paid, which was the only honest kind of payment.

Theo heard footsteps on the dock behind him and didn't turn around. He knew the rhythm. Quick, uneven, favoring the left knee — the knee that had never forgiven the flood-tide escape through the maintenance grate.

"You're early," Paloma said.

"Captain's supposed to be early."

"Captains are supposed to be *on time*. Early is a nervous habit." She came to stand beside him at the rail, a thermos of coffee in one hand, and poured two cups without asking. She'd stopped asking months ago. "Wind's east-southeast. You'll get a little chop past the point."

"I saw."

"Mm." She sipped. For a while neither of them spoke, and the silence was comfortable in a way Theo still found miraculous. There had been a time when silence between them meant distance — her distance, the careful walls of a woman who'd spent thirty years making sure no port ever got attached to her. Now silence just meant morning.

She looked different too. Not softer, exactly — Paloma Dunmore would never be soft; the word wasn't in her inventory — but settled. Like a boat that had finally been tied off to a proper mooring after years of dragging anchor. Her hair was going gray at the temples and she'd stopped fighting it. She wore the same canvas jacket, patched at the elbows, but there was a new pin on the collar: the ferry line's insignia, a stylized wave and keel, which she pretended not to care about and had personally approved the design of.

"Nervous?" she asked.

"No," Theo said, and then, because lying to her had never worked: "Yes. A little."

"Good. Captains who aren't nervous sink boats." She set down her cup. "Passenger manifest?"

"Fourteen booked for the morning run. The Vasquez family going out to see the name wall — old Mr. Vasquez's father was on the *Meridian*, third crew. Two surveyors from upriver. A journalist." Theo made a face. "And a woman who asked if we serve refreshments."

"Do we?"

"There's a cooler with plantains and ginger beer."

"Then yes, we do." Paloma smiled — the rare, crooked smile that used to appear maybe once a season and now showed up most weeks, usually at his expense. "You'll do fine."

"I know."

"You will," she said again, quieter, and looked out at the water. "I watched you hold this boat off the rocks in a monsoon with your arms elbow-deep in bilgewater. A scheduled run with fourteen passengers is a Tuesday."

"It's my first scheduled run."

"And I captained smuggler runs through three blockades before I was twenty. Everyone has a first scheduled run." She bumped his shoulder with hers. "Mine ended with me being chased by a customs cutter for six miles. Yours ends with plantains. You've already outperformed me."

Theo laughed, and the nervousness loosened something in his chest. This was the other thing that had changed: Paloma gave away her experience now instead of hoarding it. For years her knowledge had been armor — every trick of current and cargo and human nature locked behind her teeth, shared only when survival demanded it. Now she handed it out freely, mornings on the dock, evenings over cards, like a woman paying forward everything the river had taught her.

Because she was staying. That was the whole shape of it. She was staying, and staying meant investing, and investing meant teaching the boy she'd signed her life's work over to how to keep it alive.

* * *

The passengers started coming down the dock at seven.

Theo greeted each one at the gangway, checking names against his manifest with a formality he'd practiced in the mirror and immediately abandoned. Mr. Vasquez was a small, upright man in a pressed linen suit, holding his hat against his chest as he boarded, as if the boat were a church. His grandchildren ran straight for the rail. The journalist asked Theo seventeen questions about the cave rescue and wrote down only three answers, which Theo suspected was the correct journalistic method. The refreshments woman took one look at the cooler and declared the ginger beer acceptable.

Marisol arrived last, diving bag over her shoulder, though there was nothing to dive today except the shallows around the memorial jetty, where she was cataloguing artifacts for the opening. She climbed aboard with the easy grace of someone for whom ladders and gunwales were interchangeable.

"Captain," she said, saluting with two fingers.

"Deckhand," Theo returned.

"*Consulting marine archaeologist,*" she corrected, "and don't you forget it, I have a certificate now. Laminated."

Paloma, sitting on the crate by the wheel where she'd apparently decided to ride along — *just this once, for the maiden run, don't make it a thing* — snorted into her coffee. "Laminated. The delta's gone respectable."

"Says the woman with a pension from the ferry cooperative," Marisol shot back, dropping onto the rail beside her. The two of them bickered the way sisters bicker, or old crew, or whatever they had become over a winter of patching hulls and arguing about the memorial's architecture. Theo had stopped trying to define it. They trusted each other. That was the whole of it, and trust didn't need a name.

He cast off at seven-forty, exactly on schedule, and felt the Kestrel answer the wheel like she knew this mattered.

The run out was everything the brochures — well, the single hand-painted brochure — promised. The delta unrolled in green and silver, egrets lifting off the reed beds in white flurries, fishing canoes dipping across the channels. Theo worked the throttle with a light hand, reading the water the way Paloma had taught him: the darker patches where the channel deepened, the telltale ripple of a sandbar's mood, the way birds sat on the water when fish weren't running. Three years ago he'd been a dock kid with nowhere to go. Now the river made sense to him, top to bottom, and the knowing of it felt like a language he'd been born speaking without realizing.

Mr. Vasquez stood at the bow the whole way, hat in hand, wind in his face. When they rounded the point and the cliffs came into view — the low black cliffs below which the cave mouth waited, now marked by a wooden jetty and a flag — the old man's shoulders began to shake.

"That's where he worked," he told his grandchildren, pointing at the water. "My father. The *Meridian*. He was sixteen." His voice caught. "Sixteen years old, and they listed him as cargo."

Theo brought the Kestrel alongside the jetty with a single clean reverse of the screw, and Paloma, watching from the rail, gave him a nod so small that anyone else would have missed it. Theo didn't miss it. He'd learned to read her nods the way he'd learned to read the water.

They walked the name wall together, all of them. Four hundred and eleven names cut into pale stone, arranged not by value or rank but alphabetically, because Marisol had insisted that death and theft flatten everyone equal. Mr. Vasquez found *MERIDIAN CREW, VASQUEZ, E.* and pressed his palm flat against the letters for a long time. His grandchildren went quiet, which children almost never did, understanding something without words.

On the way back, the old man stopped at the gangway and gripped Theo's arm.

"My mother used to say the sea takes and never gives back," he said. "You tell whoever needs telling — the sea gave back today."

Theo looked over the man's shoulder at Paloma, standing at the wheelhouse door with her arms crossed, watching. She heard it. Theo saw her hear it — saw her jaw tighten the way it did when something landed too close to the truth of her. The sea had taken plenty from Paloma Dunmore. And she had spent her whole life believing it would never give anything back, because believing otherwise meant putting down roots, and roots meant loss, and loss was the one cargo she'd sworn never to carry again.

But she was carrying it now. All of it. And she was still here.

* * *

They made the return run by noon, and Theo tied off at the dock to find a small crowd waiting — not for him, he realized, but for the afternoon run, bookings having doubled since the Vasquez family's visit became market gossip. The Storyteller sat on his new bench, and as Theo secured the lines, the old man's voice carried across the boards, mid-legend:

*"—and the girl dove where no diver dared, and the captain held the wheel against the wrath of the sky, and the boy—"*

"Don't let him get to the part about the boy," Marisol murmured, shouldering her bag. "It's insufferable."

"The part about the boy is accurate," Theo said.

"The part about the boy makes you walk differently for a week."

It was true. It did.

Paloma lingered while the afternoon passengers boarded under Theo's direction. She stood at the head of the gangway, ostensibly supervising, actually just watching — watching him call out weight distribution, watching him check the manifest, watching him be, without any ceremony or announcement, the captain of his own boat.

"You don't have to hover," Theo told her, grinning. "I've got it."

"I know you've got it." She didn't move. "Hovering is a privilege of retirement."

"Is that what this is? Retirement?"

"It's what I'm calling it so nobody asks me to run anything." She glanced down the dock, toward the row of buildings that had become familiar — the chandlery where she now had an account, the customs house where she'd testified for two days while the whole town listened, the little rented room above the net-mender's shop where she'd lived longer than she'd lived anywhere in thirty years. "Cooperative wants me on the board, you know. Voted last week. Unanimous."

"What did you say?"

"I said I'd think about it." A pause. "I'm thinking about saying yes."

Theo stared at her. Paloma Dunmore, considering a board seat. Paloma Dunmore, whose entire adult life had been structured around the principle that commitments were traps and towns were temporary. He thought about the warrant, the cell, the flood tide, the grate in the seawall — thought about the woman who had surrendered quietly to protect a boat and a boy, and the woman who had chosen not to run when running was the oldest reflex she owned.

"You're going to say yes," he said.

"I'm going to *think* about saying yes." But the crooked smile was back, and it gave her away completely.

The afternoon run went out and came back golden, the low sun turning the delta to hammered copper, and when Theo tied off for the final time that day his hands were steady and his logbook was full and every passenger had disembarked smiling. He entered the day's numbers in the ledger — *fourteen out, nineteen back, one seasick child recovered fully after plantains* — and closed the book with a satisfaction he suspected he'd never stop feeling.

Evening came down soft. The market lamps flickered on along the planked street. Somewhere someone was frying fish, and the smell drifted out over the water, and the Storyteller's bench filled up for the evening telling.

Paloma was still on the dock.

She'd claimed a spot at the far end, legs dangling over the water the way she never would have permitted a crew member to dangle, a cup of ginger beer in hand. Theo walked down and sat beside her. Below them the river breathed against the pilings, patient and endless, and beyond the point the sea muttered its old dark secrets to a shore that had finally, after four hundred years, started listening.

"First scheduled run," Paloma said. "Complete. No customs cutters, no monsoons, no gunfire. Frankly I'm suspicious."

"Give it time."

"Oh, I intend to." She knocked her cup gently against his. "That's the whole idea."

They sat while the light failed. Theo thought about the night, three years ago, when the Kestrel had ground onto the sandbar outside this town and Paloma had read the water like an old enemy and gotten them off, and how he'd stood at the rail thinking they'd stop here a week, maybe two, just long enough to patch the hull. He thought about how many times since then the whole thing could have ended — the warrant, the storm, the cave-in, any of a dozen moments when the delta could have swallowed them and scattered them like every other port Paloma had fled.

Instead they were here. Sitting on a dock. Watching the lamps come on.

"Can I ask you something?" Theo said.

"You can ask. Whether I answer depends on the question."

"Why here? You ran from everywhere. What made you stay *here*?"

Paloma was quiet for a long time. A night heron crossed the water, low and silent. When she finally spoke, her voice was rough at the edges, the way it got when she was saying something true.

"I used to think belonging was something you found," she said. "Like a harbor. You sail around until you stumble into the right one, and if you're lucky the charts agree. Took me thirty years and half a ocean to figure out that's backwards." She turned the cup in her hands. "Belonging isn't found. It's chosen. You choose a place, and then you choose it again the next morning, and the morning after that, even when it floods, even when it jails you, even when it costs you everything you thought you were. Landfall didn't earn me, Theo. I earned it. One stubborn day at a time." She looked at him, and her eyes in the lamplight were clear and unafraid. "Same way you did. Same way Marisol did. Same way this whole drowned town is doing, right now, name by name."

Theo nodded, and found he couldn't speak for a moment, and decided that was fine, because she'd understand that too.

Down the dock, the Storyteller's voice rose and fell, telling the tale to a new crowd, and the tale had grown new endings over the months — endings about the ferry line, about the name wall, about the captain who stayed. Theo listened to his own life being turned into legend and found, to his surprise, that he didn't mind. Legends were just true stories that a town decided to keep.

Paloma finished her ginger beer and stood, brushing off her trousers, and for a moment she stood there looking down the long lit length of the dock, at the market, at the cliffs, at the whole improbable town that had refused to let her go.

"Well," she said. "Board meeting's tomorrow night. If I'm going to say yes, I suppose I ought to say it while I still remember how."

She started up the dock toward the lights, unhurried, favoring the left knee, and Theo stayed where he was a moment longer, alone with the water and the lamps and the vast quiet certainty that this — all of it, the boat, the town, the woman walking away without leaving — was not an ending.

It was a landfall. The first one either of them had ever meant.

Then he heard her call back down the dock, loud enough for the whole market to hear, in the voice of a woman who had finally stopped running:

"Theo! Leave the lanterns lit. We're home."
