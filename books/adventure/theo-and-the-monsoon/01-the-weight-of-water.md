## 1. The Weight of Water

The delta gave up its dead slowly, and only if you asked nicely.

Theo Vasquez had learned to ask nicely. Six years of it—kneeling in the gray silt of the lowlands with a trowel worn down to a sliver, brushing mud from things that had waited centuries for someone patient enough to say hello. The river buried everything eventually: boats, bones, whole villages that had misjudged the monsoon by a single season. It kept them. It did not give them back. Not unless you knew where to kneel, and how long, and how to read the water's handwriting when it scrawled its secrets across a sandbank at low tide.

This morning the river was being generous.

The site sat two hours' poling from the nearest market town, in a drowned orchard where mango trees stood hip-deep like drowned men reaching for shore. Theo had found it three weeks ago—a midden mound rising out of the floodplain, ringed with potsherds the color of old teeth. Illegal ground. No permit, no charter, no university seal stamped on anything. Just Theo, a canvas tent going green with mold, and forty-one days of careful, quiet work that no one would ever know about except the river.

That was the point. That was always the point.

Theo knelt in the trench now, sleeves rolled past the elbow, forearms caked to the wrist in clay the texture of cold porridge. The monsoon was eleven days out—the barometer in their pack had been dropping all week, and the air had that bruised, heavy feel of a sky holding its breath—but there was time yet. There was always just enough time. It was the one promise the delta never broke entirely.

The trowel clicked against something hard.

Not stone. Stone rang dull. This had a higher note, ceramic-bright, and Theo's pulse picked up the way it always did—not excitement exactly, but something adjacent to it, something that lived in the same neighborhood. They cleared the matrix around the find with a bamboo splint, working outward in slow spirals, because the fastest way to destroy a piece was to want it too much. Patience first. Wanting later. That was the discipline, and it was the only inheritance Theo would admit to having.

The shard emerged into the light like a thing surfacing from deep water.

It was a rim fragment, glazed, the glaze crackled into a web of fine lines. Trade ware, maybe four hundred years old, from kilns far upriver or farther still across the sea. Beautiful. Valuable enough. But none of that mattered, because Theo had stopped breathing.

There was a mark on the base. Inked under the glaze line, small and precise: a stylized delta forked like a bird's foot, with three numerals beneath it in a hand Theo would have known anywhere—in any century, in any darkness, with their eyes burned out.

*V-114.*

Vasquez. Site 114. Catalogued, photographed, published in a monograph with a gold-stamped spine that sat on shelves in seventeen countries. Their father's mark. Their father's numbering system, the one he'd invented himself and taught to every graduate student who passed through his lab, the one that appeared in textbooks now, the one students doodled in margins without knowing they were writing a man's signature.

Except this shard had been in the ground for four hundred years, and the mark was fresh.

Which meant the shard wasn't four hundred years old. Which meant it was planted—which meant someone had salted this site, seeded it with castoffs from a famous excavation, probably fragments rejected as too damaged to keep, dumped decades ago and forgotten. Someone had built a fake antiquity out of his father's garbage.

Theo sat back on their heels in the flooded trench and laughed once, a short ugly sound that startled a heron off the dead mango tree overhead.

Of course. Of course the river's one gift of the season came wrapped in him. You could dig a hole in the farthest silt-flat on earth, you could go where the law didn't reach and the charts ran out, and the ground itself would hand you his name. Elias Vasquez got everywhere. He'd gotten into the soil. He'd gotten into the language of the discipline, into the way sites were numbered, into the shape of Theo's own hands holding a trowel exactly the way he held his.

He'd gotten into the way Theo worked outward in slow spirals, because *patience first, wanting later*, said in that warm lecture-hall voice, twenty years ago, to a child sitting cross-legged on a tarped excavation floor while the other kids were home watching television.

Theo lifted the shard out of the water. It dripped. The little forked delta gleamed, smug as a signature on a check.

They should catalog it. That was procedure—*their* procedure, self-imposed, the private liturgy that kept the work honest even when the work was illegal. Record position. Photograph. Bag and tag. The shard was evidence of site contamination, which meant the whole midden might be compromised, which meant six weeks of kneeling in gray water might have been spent excavating a lie. That thought arrived cold and stayed cold, settling somewhere behind the sternum.

But mostly Theo looked at the mark, and what rose in them was not scholarly concern.

It was the smell of his office. Old paper and pipe smoke and that particular dust that lives in university buildings, the dust of other people's careers. It was the sound of his voice on the phone, telling some journalist, *oh, my child dabbles,* the word *dabbles* landing like a coin dropped down a well. It was the last conversation they'd had, four years ago, standing in a parking lot outside a conference hotel, Theo saying *I found a site, a real one, undisturbed*—and Elias nodding the way he nodded at junior colleagues, pleasant, distracted, already looking over their shoulder for someone who mattered.

Six weeks of work, and the ground had answered with his initials.

"Fine," Theo said aloud, to the heron, to the water, to nobody. "Fine."

They climbed out of the trench. The storm-light was thickening early; the mango trees stood in their flooded rows like witnesses. Theo walked to the fire ring at the edge of camp, where the morning's cooking coals still held a dull orange heart, and stood looking at the shard for a long moment.

Cataloguing it would mean keeping it. Keeping it would mean seeing it every day, packed in tissue among the real finds, wearing its little forged signature like a joke told at their expense. Recording it would mean writing the letter *V* in their own field notes, in their own hand, which was also his hand, and Theo found—they tested the thought carefully, the way you test ice—that they could not do it. Could not put that letter in their notebook. Could not let him have even the ink.

So instead they held the shard over the coals.

Glazed ceramic doesn't burn. Theo knew that better than anyone alive. Fire wouldn't erase the mark, wouldn't unmake the forgery, wouldn't take back anything. It was a gesture with no practical value whatsoever, which was presumably why it felt so necessary. They held the fragment down into the embers until the heat climbed through the clay and bit their fingers, and they held it there longer than that, until the glaze began to craze and flake and the old trade-wear darkened with soot, and when they finally dropped it into the ash it made a small sound, almost apologetic.

"There," Theo said quietly. "Now you're nothing again."

The lie in that was enormous and they both knew it. Nothing was not an option for objects like this. Buried, burned, thrown in the river—it would surface someday, everything surfaced someday, the delta saw to that. Somewhere downstream a fisherman would net it, or a flood would spit it onto a bank, and some future stranger would kneel in the mud and read the mark and think of Elias Vasquez, and Theo's small act of arson would be revealed as exactly what it was: not erasure, just grief with nowhere to put itself.

Theo washed their hands in the orchard floodwater until the ash was gone, then went back to the trench and worked until the light failed, and did not find another marked piece, and told themselves that was the end of it.

*

That night the storm arrived ahead of schedule.

It came in off the sea like something with intent, flattening the reeds, driving rain horizontal through the mango trees. Theo sat inside the tent with a hurricane lamp and a tin plate of rice, listening to the canvas strain, and tried very hard to think about drainage profiles.

Instead they thought about the shard.

Because here was the thing they hadn't let themselves finish thinking, out there in the daylight with the heron watching: the salted fragments had to come from somewhere. Forgers didn't mint their own Vasquez shards. They scavenged—from old digs, from discarded lots, from collections broken up and sold off when an expedition folded. And Theo knew, in the way you know a face across a crowded room, that the V-114 lot had been dispersed after a specific expedition, thirty-two years ago, the one that made their father's name. The Kessarin Valley survey. The find of the century, according to the monograph. According to everyone.

According to everyone except the expedition's own records, which had never been released. Except the names of the field team, which appeared nowhere in the acknowledgments. Except Amara Osei, whom Theo had heard mentioned exactly once, drunk, at a faculty dinner they'd attended at nineteen, hopeful and proud in a borrowed blazer—one of the senior men saying *whatever happened to that woman Amara, she was on the Kessarin team, wasn't she?* and their father laughing easily and saying *she left the profession, I believe,* in the tone of a man closing a drawer.

Theo had been nineteen and had believed him. Had wanted to believe him. That was the part that sat in the chest like swallowed stone: not that Elias lied, but that Theo had helped him do it, by loving him, by taking his version of the world and keeping it safe.

The lamp guttered. Rain hammered the roof like a crowd demanding entry.

Tomorrow, Theo decided, they would close the trench properly, bag the true finds, and pole downriver before the channels flooded. The season was over. The site was compromised anyway—or worse, it wasn't, and the shard was a message rather than a forgery, and Theo didn't have the stomach to find out which.

They were blowing out the lamp when they heard the sound.

A voice, thin and cracked, cutting through the rain from the direction of the water. Calling for help, or calling for anyone, or possibly just calling. Theo grabbed the lamp and pushed out into the storm, boots sinking in the flooded orchard mud, and at the edge of the black water where the boat was tied, a figure lay half in and half out of a leaking skiff—a woman, old, soaked through, one hand clawed into the mud and the other pressed flat against her chest as though holding something in.

She turned her head at the light. Her eyes found Theo's face, and she smiled with cracked lips, and she said, in a voice like paper burning:

"You'll do. You look like someone who owes the river a favor."
