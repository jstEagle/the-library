## 2. A Stranger's Last Bargain

The storm came in from the west like something with a grudge, and Theo watched it build all afternoon from the mouth of the dig trench, mud to the elbows, deciding whether to keep working or admit defeat.

Admitting defeat was a habit they'd never cultivated. Their father had taught them that much, at least—the useful part of him. *A site doesn't care about your comfort, Theo. The earth gives up its dead on its own schedule.* He'd said it standing over a burial jar in some sun-blasted valley when Theo was nine, and Theo had hated him for it then and had built a whole life on it since, which was the cruelest joke of inheritance: you became the thing you resented, down to the sentences.

So Theo kept working while the sky went the color of a bruise and the first fat drops hit the tarp they'd strung over the trench. The shard from yesterday was still wrapped in cloth at the bottom of their pack, charred where they'd held it too long over the flame—no, not this one; this one was clean, a rim fragment with a painted band, nothing marked, nothing that would make them think of anyone but the potter who'd died four hundred years before anyone named Vasquez mattered. This one they could keep.

The rain arrived properly at dusk, a wall of it, drumming the tarp so hard conversation became impossible even with yourself. Theo crawled into the lean-to they'd built against a fallen kapok, ate cold rice and smoked fish, and listened to the delta breathe. Water everywhere now, filling the channels, climbing the banks. In three weeks, maybe four, this whole lowland would be a brown sea and every site they knew would be under two meters of floodwater until October. The monsoon didn't destroy things. It just hid them, patiently, the way certain people hid themselves behind other people's names.

They were banking the fire when they heard the boat.

Wrong sound, wrong hour. A pole knocking hull in that arrhythmic way that meant no one steering, or someone too weak to steer properly. Theo went still, hand finding the machete by the pack—not out of fear exactly, but because out here caution was just another word for self-respect—and waited.

The boat came out of the rain like a piece of driftwood with opinions. It bumped against the bank snag, swung, bumped again. In the stern sat a figure slumped against the gunwale, hooded, one hand still gripping the pole as if letting go would be an admission of something final.

"Ahoy," Theo called, and got no answer but the rain.

They waded out, hauled the bow line, and dragged the boat close enough to see the passenger clearly: an old woman, dark-skinned, weathered past guessing at age, her lips gray and cracked, her eyes open and fixed on Theo with an intensity that had outlived everything else in her body. Her clothes were expedition-grade once—canvas jacket, salt-stained boots—but worn down to the memory of quality. One hand lay in her lap, curled around a metal cylinder the size of a forearm, sealed at both ends with wax and bound in oilcloth.

"You're the digger," she said. Her voice was gravel poured slow. "The one working the low channels alone. They talk about you at the trading posts. The ghost with the trowel."

"They talk about everyone," Theo said. "Come inside before you die on my doorstep. Corpses are bad luck in a camp."

Something moved across her face that might have been amusement. "Superstitious."

"Practical. Dead bodies attract questions, and questions attract officials." Theo took her arm—light as a bird's bone under the wet canvas—and walked her up the bank to the lean-to, then went back for the cylinder, which she surrendered only after her fingers had to be pried loose one by one.

She collapsed onto the ground cloth Theo spread for her and drank the water they gave her in long, unembarrassed gulps, then ate half the remaining fish without speaking. Theo crouched opposite, watching the fever-bright eyes, the tremor in the hands, the way she favored her left side when she shifted. Bad heart, maybe. Bad everything. She'd been dying for a while and had simply declined to inform her body.

"Amara," she said finally, tapping her own chest.

"Theo." No surname. Never the surname.

"Theo." Amara tasted it, nodded once. "Good river name. Short. Hard to drown." She looked around the lean-to—at the neat tool roll, the labeled sample bags, the survey notes weighted under stones—and Theo saw her read the camp the way one professional reads another. "You work clean. Alone?"

"I work alone."

"Mm." She said it the way people say *liar* politely. Then she set down the cup and put both trembling hands on the cylinder between them, like a woman laying hands on an altar. "I'm going to make you an offer, digger. And I need you to listen to all of it before you decide, because I don't have the strength to say it twice."

Outside, the storm hammered on. Lightning turned the delta white for a heartbeat at a time, and in those flashes Amara's face looked carved, ancient, certain.

"There's a temple," she said. "Submerged. Not flooded recently—submerged centuries ago, when the old river cut a new channel and abandoned it. It sits under four meters of water in a valley north of here, past the Kessarin gorge, and it has never been excavated. Never been published. Never been touched by any institution with a charter and a budget and a name." Her eyes came up and held Theo's. "Until mine. Thirty-one years ago I found it. I mapped it. I dove it with two men who are dead now, and I documented every chamber, and then—" her breath caught, and she rode through it with visible fury at her own body, "—and then it was taken from me. All of it. My maps, my notes, my discovery. Published under another name. A man's name. A famous man's name."

Theo felt the world tilt slightly, the way a boat tilts when something shifts below decks. They kept their voice flat. "That's a common story. Everyone in this delta claims someone stole their find."

"Yes. And most of them are liars." Amara unwound the oilcloth with fingers that shook but did not hurry. Underneath, the metal cylinder. She broke the wax seal with her thumbnail. "But I can prove mine. There's a journal inside this tube—my original field journal, the one with the dive logs and the survey bearings, dated, signed, witnessed. The copy that exists in the published record is a forgery of my work, and the dates don't lie. Dates never lie. People do." She drew out a rolled bundle wrapped in waxed silk, and beneath it, a second object: a map, waterproofed, drawn on treated canvas in ink that had survived decades of damp. She unrolled it halfway across her knees.

Even by firelight, Theo's trained eye did what it could not help doing. Contour lines in a precise hand. Soundings marked in fathoms. Datum points referenced to a fixed benchmark notation—a system, a specific idiosyncratic shorthand—

Theo's stomach dropped.

They knew that shorthand. They'd grown up watching it appear in margins, in letters, in the margins of books in a house that had felt more like an archive than a home. It was a survey notation their father had invented himself, refined over years, used by no one else in the profession because no one else had ever needed it.

*Impossible,* Theo thought. *She's lying. Or coincidence. Or—*

Or thirty-one years ago, a young woman with a map had met a young man with ambition, and one of them had walked away with everything.

"Where did you get this notation?" Theo heard themselves ask, and hated how carefully casual their voice sounded.

Amara smiled. It was not a kind smile. It was the smile of someone who had spent three decades sharpening a single blade. "You recognize it. Good. That saves time." She rolled the map closed and pressed it back into the cylinder with the journal, resealed the wax with a match from Theo's kit, and held the tube across the space between them. "Here is my offer, digger. Take this to my daughter. Her name is Suriya. She lives in Kessarin village, past the gorge, on the high bank above the floodline. You have—" she paused, calculating, "—perhaps twelve days before the monsoon seals the valley. After that, no boat goes in or out until October, and I will be dead, and this will sit in your camp gathering mold, and the truth will stay drowned along with everything else."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you refuse, and I'll die anyway, and it changes nothing except my disappointment." She shrugged, and the shrug cost her a coughing fit that bent her double. When she straightened, there was blood on her lip, pink in the firelight, and she wiped it away without looking at it. "But you won't refuse."

"No?"

"No. Because you looked at that notation the way a man looks at a scar he thought was his alone." Her eyes were terrible and gentle at once. "You know whose it is. And you want to know if I'm telling the truth. And wanting to know, digger—that's already half the journey."

Theo sat back on their heels. The rain roared. Somewhere upriver, thunder rolled along the valley like furniture being dragged across a floor. They thought about the burned shard from yesterday, the ash of it still probably in the fire pit, the mark they'd destroyed rather than think about. They thought about a childhood of footnotes and other people's celebrations. They thought about how much easier it would be to say no, to let the old woman die dry and warm and disappointed, to go back to digging alone in places where no name meant anything.

Twelve days. Kessarin was six days' hard poling upstream in good weather. Nothing about this weather was good.

"I don't do passengers," Theo said. "I don't do favors. And I don't do other people's vendettas."

"I'm not asking for a favor. I'm asking for a delivery." Amara laid the cylinder in Theo's lap, and it was heavier than it looked, the way promises always are. "One promise. Carry it to Suriya before the rains seal the valley. That's all. What she does with it after—that's hers. I'm not asking you to fight anyone. I'm asking you to finish my last mile."

"Why me? You could hire a boatman. Pay a courier."

"Because couriers can be bought and boatmen can be bribed, and the men who took my work still have money and still have reach, and if this falls into the wrong hands it burns tonight in some trading post stove." She leaned closer, and her breath smelled of blood and river water. "But you—you hate him. Don't you. Whoever he is to you. I saw it when you recognized the notation. Hatred keeps a package safe better than payment does."

Theo wanted to deny it. Found they couldn't produce the words.

"I'll pay you," they said instead, roughly. "My fee. Half now, half on delivery. I'm not doing this for free, and I'm not doing it for you. I'm doing it because it's six days' work and I'm paid to work."

Amara laughed—a real laugh, rusty, delighted, costing her another cough. "Of course you are. Name your price."

Theo named it. Too high. She matched it without blinking, which told Theo more about her than the whole story had.

"One condition," she added, as Theo tucked the cylinder into the driest corner of the pack, next to the space where the burned shard had been. "You tell her everything. Not the summary. Everything. How you found me, what I said, what I asked. She gets the whole truth of her mother's last days, not a courier's version. Promise me that too."

"That's two promises."

"Life is full of extras." She settled back against the kapok trunk, exhausted now, the firelight making her look translucent, already half-gone. "Sleep, digger. You leave at first light, and you'll need a boatman who knows the gorge. Ask at Nakhon Bend for one who asks no questions. Every bend has one. The quiet ones are the ones who've survived something."

She was asleep within minutes, breathing shallow but steady, one hand resting on the ground cloth as if still holding a pole in moving water. Theo sat awake a long time, the pack beside them, the cylinder's weight pressing through the canvas into their hip like a second pulse.

Twelve days. Six upriver. A daughter they'd never met. A truth they weren't sure they wanted confirmed, wrapped in wax and oilcloth, riding against their spine.

When Theo finally slept, they dreamed of contour lines in a familiar hand, and woke before dawn with the taste of ash in their mouth.

At first light, Amara was still alive, barely. She made Theo repeat the promise twice, word for word, then pressed a small pouch of coins into their hand—half the fee, plus extra, "for the boatman, and for the mistakes you haven't made yet." She refused to be taken anywhere. "I die on land I chose," she said. "Not in a boat. Not in a bed. Here is fine."

Theo struck camp in the gray drizzle, buried their trench markers, and stood at the bank with the pack on their back, looking downriver toward Nakhon Bend and the kind of boatman who asks nothing.

Behind them, the old woman raised one hand—not waving, pointing, the way navigators point at distant weather.

"Go," Amara said. "The water isn't waiting, and neither am I."

Theo went.
