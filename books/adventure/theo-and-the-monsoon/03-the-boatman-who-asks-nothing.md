## 3. The Boatman Who Asks Nothing

The boat was called *Sampaguita*, which was a joke, because nothing about her was sweet or white or blooming. She was thirty feet of river-rot and stubbornness, patched with tarred canvas at the bow, patched with sheet tin amidships, patched with what looked like an old road sign near the stern. Jun had found the sign on a sandbar two seasons back—HALF KILOMETER TO TOLL BRIDGE—and hammered it over the worst of the leak. The river had opinions about that. The river always did.

He was mending a net when the stranger came down the mudbank.

Jun heard them before he saw them: boots in the wet clay, careful, deliberate, the walk of someone who'd learned to move quiet around other people's territory. He didn't look up. Looking up invited conversation, and conversation invited questions, and questions were the one cargo he never carried.

"You're Jun." Not a question either. Word traveled along the waterfront faster than floodwater, and it carried less silt.

"Depends who's asking."

"Theo." A pause, as if the name cost something to say aloud. "I need to go upriver. Kessarin. Before the rains."

Jun kept his eyes on the net, working a torn mesh closed with waxed twine. "Kessarin's four days' run against the current if the weather holds."

"It won't hold."

"No," Jun agreed. "It won't."

That was the whole shape of it, laid out between them like a fish gutted open on the boards. Everyone on the waterfront knew what the pre-monsoon meant: the sky going the color of a bruise by mid-afternoon, the wind turning, the channels that were friendly in April becoming knives by June. Boats went upriver this time of year for two reasons only—desperation or greed—and both of them sank at the same rate.

The stranger crouched at the edge of the planking, close enough that Jun could see the details now. Lean, sun-cracked, hands scarred in the particular way of someone who dug for a living rather than fished—a trowel callus where a boatman would have rope callus, dirt worked so deep into the knuckle creases it looked like tattooing. Eyes that did the thing Jun hated most: they catalogued him. Hull, patchwork, net, face. Weighing him like a find.

"I'll pay double the going rate," Theo said.

"Triple."

"That's robbery."

"That's monsoon rates." Jun finally looked up, let the stranger have his full attention, flat and gray as river stone. "You're not paying for the trip. You're paying for me betting my boat against a storm front. If she sinks up there, I don't just lose a hull. I lose the only thing I own outright, and I go back to—" He stopped. Redirected. "Triple. Half now."

Something moved behind those cataloguing eyes. Recognition, maybe—not of Jun, but of the flinch. The way you could tell another person had a locked room inside them, because you had one too, and you knew the sound a body made when it brushed up against its own door.

"Half now," Theo said. "And I want terms."

"Everyone wants terms."

"No questions. You don't ask why I'm going, what I'm carrying, who I'm seeing. You take me to Kessarin, you put me on the bank, and that's the end of it. Whatever happens after is mine."

Jun set down the twine. This was the part he liked—the arithmetic of distrust, two strangers counting out exactly how much of themselves they could afford to spend. Most people haggled over money. The smart ones haggled over silence.

"Same goes for me," he said. "You don't ask why I'm going, what I'm running from, who put me off this river. No names beyond the ones we've already wasted. You sleep aboard, you eat what I cook, you haul line when I tell you to haul line. And when we get to Kessarin—" he held up a finger, "—you're gone within the hour. I don't dock there. I don't wait there. I don't know anyone there."

Theo's jaw tightened, almost imperceptibly. "You said no questions."

"I'm not asking one. I'm telling you how it has to be." Jun stood, joints cracking, and gestured at the *Sampaguita* with her tin-and-tarpaulin skin. "Kessarin and I have history. History is heavier than cargo. It capsizes boats."

For a long moment the stranger studied him, and Jun felt the strange sensation of being read like water—depths sounded, currents measured, hazards marked. Then Theo pulled out a oilcloth roll and counted bills into his palm without touching skin to skin. Careful. Practiced. Someone who didn't like being touched either.

"When do we leave?"

"Before dawn. The river's lowest at first light; we'll make the narrows before the afternoon wind turns." Jun jerked his chin toward the stern. "There's a tarp under the tiller bench. Don't snore."

* * *

They left in the blue-black hour when the mosquitoes gave up and the herons hadn't started, and the *Sampaguita* slid out of the stilt-town like a rumor leaving a mouth.

Jun ran the boat alone for the first hour, partly to test the stranger and partly because the first hour was his—his ritual, his prayer, the engine's diesel stutter settling into its work rhythm while the banks unspooled dark and dripping. Theo sat in the bow with knees drawn up, watching the water instead of him, and asked nothing. Good. That was the contract holding.

By full light they reached the first confluence, where the brown water of the main channel braided with something greener coming off the hills, and Jun cut the engine to drift and listen. You could hear a snag before you hit it, if you knew what listening for. The stranger listened too—head cocked, breath held—and when Jun threaded the channel through a drowned grove, steering by the ripple-lines over submerged fence posts, he caught Theo watching his hands on the wheel with something that wasn't quite admiration. Closer to recognition. Two people fluent in the same dead language.

"Your boat leaks," Theo said, somewhere past midmorning. Not accusatory. Diagnostic.

"She sweats. There's a difference."

"There's a bilge pump?"

"There's a bucket. There's also a tin patch kit under the thwart, and if she starts drinking faster than I bail, you'll be bailing and not talking about it."

Theo almost smiled. It was a small thing, quickly folded away, but Jun had spent enough years alone to notice the rare ones. "Fair terms."

They ran all day. The country changed as they climbed—the delta's flat sprawl giving way to terraced hills, then to forest so old and heavy it seemed to press the river narrower, the air going green-smelling and electric. Twice they passed other boats heading downriver, loaded deep, families and furniture and goats, everyone getting out ahead of the rain. The passengers stared at the lone boat fighting upstream. Nobody went the wrong way this time of year unless they had to.

Toward dusk the sky began doing what the sky did: stacking itself into towers the color of iron, silent for now, saving its voice. Jun tied up at a bend where a rock shelf made natural shelter and cooked rice and dried fish on a charcoal burner, and they ate ten feet apart, which was the correct distance.

"My family used to run this stretch," Theo said suddenly, then stopped, as if surprised at having spoken.

Jun said nothing. The contract cut both ways, and he was better at keeping it.

But the stranger kept going anyway, quietly, to the fire more than to him. "My father surveyed these rivers. Before the dam proposals, before any of it. He had marks on maps for every channel." A pause. "I hate that I remember that."

The charcoal ticked. Somewhere across the water, a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"You said no names," Jun said at last. "Yours counts double. Theo is already one too many."

Theo looked up sharply—and then, slowly, nodded, something easing and tightening in their face at once. "Understood."

But Jun lay awake that night under the tarp, listening to the rain start far off in the hills like an audience shuffling in its seats, and turned the words over. *My father surveyed these rivers.* Everyone on the waterfront knew whose survey boats had ruled this water twenty years ago. Everyone knew the great man's name, though nobody said it to Jun's face anymore, not since the university men had come through last season flashing charters and clean boots, asking after a boatman who used to ferry for the old expeditions. Asking after him by name.

He'd told them nothing. They'd paid him anyway, insultingly, to forget he existed, and he'd taken the money because spite was also a currency and he was rich in it.

Now here was a stranger with surveyor's hands and a famous man's shadow wrapped around them like wet cloth, headed for Kessarin in monsoon season, paying triple and buying silence. Jun had ferried smugglers, fugitives, one government surveyor, and a monk who turned out to be neither. He knew cargo when he saw it, even cargo that walked and talked and claimed to carry nothing.

The rain thickened. In the bow, Theo's breathing evened into sleep, and the *Sampaguita* rocked on the rising water, sweating through her patches, faithful and dying by inches the way all river things did.

Jun watched the dark and did the math he'd been avoiding since the mudbank: four days to Kessarin, three days of storm behind them, and somewhere ahead, at the end of all that water, a village he had sworn never to see again—where his sister still lived, probably, and his mother, and the grave of a boy whose drowning Jun had refused to lie about, and been cast out for it. Where the word for him was still *the one who told.*

No questions, no names. It was a good contract. It was the only kind two people like them could sign.

He just hadn't counted on the third clause, the one nobody negotiated, the one the river wrote itself: that sooner or later, the water makes everyone tell.

Far upstream, thunder cleared its throat, and the rain came on like a debt being collected.
