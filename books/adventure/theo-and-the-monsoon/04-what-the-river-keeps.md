## 4. What the River Keeps

The river had eaten the road two days ago.

Theo watched it go from the bow of Jun's patched boat, the last visible trace of the highway dissolving under brown water the way sugar dissolves in tea—quietly, completely, without ceremony. Somewhere beneath that flood lay asphalt, a fuel station, a shrine with a cracked Buddha face that Theo had once photographed on a trip with their father. They tried not to think about the trip. They were getting worse at not thinking about him.

"Channel's wrong," Jun said from the stern. He said it the way he said everything—flat, certain, already moving the tiller before Theo finished processing the words. The boat swung left, away from the broad silver throat of the main channel and into a corridor of drowned forest. Water slid between the trunks of kapok trees, so still it looked solid, so deep the roots vanished into rumor.

"The map says main channel."

"The map is a month old and the river doesn't read." Jun's eyes never stopped moving—water, sky, water again. "Rain in the hills. Everything's running high. We go where the water goes deep, not where the water looks easy."

Theo had hired him for exactly this: a man who read the delta the way other men read newspapers, fluently, without sentiment. Three days out from the trading post, and Jun had asked nothing. Not about the waterproofed map Theo kept rolled inside a section of bamboo, not about the temple, not about why a person with a surveyor's hands and a scholar's posture was running upriver ahead of the monsoon with a stranger's dying promise in their pocket. No questions, no names. It was the only contract between them, and Theo clung to it like a rail.

The drowned forest closed overhead in places, branches knitting into a green ceiling. Light came down in coins. Somewhere a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling, and Theo's hands, steady on the map case, remembered being nine years old and hearing that same bird in a different delta, a different lifetime, their father's voice saying, *Listen. The river keeps everything. Our job is just to ask it politely.*

They had burned a shard two weeks ago rather than hear that voice in their head one more time.

* * *

They made camp that night on the roof of a half-submerged ferry office, the water lapping at the eaves, mosquitoes rising off the flood in silver spirals. Jun cooked rice over a spirit stove without being asked and ate without speaking, which Theo was beginning to understand was his version of hospitality.

Theo unrolled the map.

Amara's map. Waterproofed in waxed silk, the ink still sharp after however many years she'd carried it, the creases worn soft as cloth from handling. It showed the valley in cross-section: the river's old course drawn in one ink, the new course in another, and the temple—a small, precise symbol like an eye—marked beneath the floodline of the eastern basin. Around it, dense as scripture, ran her annotations. Depth soundings. Current readings. Seasonal water tables. A whole drowned architecture of knowledge.

Theo had been studying it for three days, and for three days something had been itching at the back of their skull like a word on the tip of the tongue. Tonight, with the spirit stove hissing and the flood breathing against the eaves, they finally saw it.

The soundings.

Amara had taken her depth measurements at intervals that weren't intervals at all—closer together where the bottom changed, sparse where it ran flat, a density of data clustered exactly where a buried structure would throw anomalies. Theo knew that pattern. Had grown up inside that pattern. Their father called it *weighted sampling*, and he'd invented it, refined it across thirty years and eleven celebrated expeditions, and it had his fingerprints all over it the way a signature has ink.

Except this map predated his first published use of the technique by four years.

Theo sat very still. The stove hissed. Water breathed against the building like something enormous asleep.

"Problem," Jun said. Not a question. He'd noticed Theo's stillness the way he noticed everything—without appearing to look.

"Survey method," Theo said. "It's—unusual. Very specific. I've only seen it one other place."

Jun waited. He was good at waiting. It was, Theo suspected, how he'd survived being cast out of a village: he waited until the world forgot it was angry with him.

"The man who invented it is famous for it," Theo said. "Famous enough that it's in every textbook. Famous enough that it has his name on it."

"And?"

"And this map was never published. The site was never published. Amara's expedition was never—" Theo stopped. Started again, quieter. "No one was supposed to know this technique existed here. Not on this river. Not in this decade."

Jun turned a piece of firewood over in the stove, considering it the way he considered the river. "So either she learned it from him," he said, "or he learned it from her."

The sentence landed in the cabin of the ferry office and sat there, heavy as ballast.

Theo had spent twenty years building a wall between themselves and that man, brick by brick: the estranged surname, the illegal solo digs, the burned shard, the refusal—absolute, principled—to think about him. The wall had a door in it, of course. It always had. Theo had just never opened it from this side.

*He learned it from her.* Four words, and the whole architecture of Theo's childhood shifted on its foundations. Every dinner-table story about the breakthrough year, every lecture hall anecdote, the famous paper, the prize, the temple that had made the family name—*the family name*, Theo thought, tasting the irony like river water. If the technique was hers first, then what else was hers? What else had flowed downhill from her expedition into his career, quiet as silt, uncredited as water?

"I need to look at the rest of it," Theo said, and heard their own voice come out wrong—too fast, too hungry.

* * *

The obsession took hold the way floodwater takes a levee: not all at once, but in places, finding the weak seams and widening them.

The next day Theo barely watched the river. Jun handled the channels alone, poling through drowned orchards where fruit floated ripe and unreachable on the surface, and Theo sat cross-legged in the bow with the map open across their knees, reading Amara's hand the way a forensic examiner reads a confession.

The handwriting changed. That was the first thing. The early annotations were cramped, hurried, written by a woman working against weather and money and probably skepticism—*the university rejected the proposal again*, someone had scrawled in a margin, in English, in a different pen. But the survey data itself was immaculate. Weighted sampling, executed flawlessly, by someone who had either been taught by its inventor or invented it.

Then, near the temple symbol, Theo found the second thing.

A small notation in the margin, dated, in Amara's hand: *V. confirms method viable. V. agrees—joint publication, spring season.* V. The initial sat there like a fishhook under Theo's skin. Vasquez. Their father's name, their own abandoned name, sitting in the margin of a dead woman's map like a signature on a warrant.

*Joint publication, spring season.*

There was no joint publication. There was no spring season. Theo knew the published record the way they knew their own scars—every paper, every date, every co-author carefully excluded. The spring season Amara had written about was the season their father had gone into the eastern basin alone, or nearly alone, and come out with a discovery that redefined a career and erased another one entirely.

"Jun." Theo's voice cracked on the second syllable. They cleared their throat. "How far to the temple?"

Jun looked at the sky first, then the water, then, last and briefly, at Theo. "Two days. Three if the storm front holds where it is." A pause. "The front won't hold."

"Then two days."

"You want to tell me what you found on that map?"

The contract hung between them—*no questions, no names*—and Theo felt the full weight of how one-sided it had always been. Jun had given away a piece of himself on the rapids, the story of the drowning and the lie and the village that wouldn't have him back, and Theo had given nothing, had hoarded their silence like a miser. Now Jun was offering to break his own rule, and Theo found they couldn't do the same. Not yet. The thing in the margin was too raw, too new, too much like a wound that hadn't decided whether it was an injury or an inheritance.

"Not yet," Theo said.

Jun held their eyes for a long moment. Whatever he saw there, he nodded once and turned back to the tiller, and Theo understood that the debt between them had just grown, and that Jun knew it too, and that he was letting it stand. That was its own kind of generosity, and it made Theo feel smaller than any accusation would have.

That night, moored in the drowned ruins of a temple—*a small one, provincial*, Theo noted automatically, the training thinking for them—Theo copied Amara's map. Every line, every sounding, every marginal note, onto waterproof paper, in their own hand. It was what their father would have done. The realization arrived like nausea. They kept copying anyway, because the map was evidence now, and evidence needed a twin in case the original drowned, and because some hungers are older than the people who feed them.

By the light of the spirit stove they read the notation again. *V. confirms method viable.* Their father, thirty years younger, standing on a riverbank with a woman the textbooks never mentioned, agreeing to share credit. Theo tried to picture his face and found they couldn't—found that grief and fury had eroded the memory down to a voice and a pair of hands, and even the hands were suspect now.

The rain started an hour before dawn. Soft at first, then serious, the sound of the monsoon clearing its throat. Jun appeared at the bow already soaked, already working the mooring line.

"Front's moving," he said. "We leave now or we swim."

Theo rolled the maps—Amara's and their own copy—into the bamboo tube and lashed it inside their jacket, against the heartbeat, where a person keeps the things they can't afford to lose and can't afford to keep.

As they pulled out into the rising current, Theo looked back once at the drowned provincial temple sinking by inches into the flood, and thought: *somewhere ahead, the real one is waiting, and it has her name all over it, and his fingerprints, and I am sailing straight into the middle of my own family's crime.*

Ahead, through the rain, the channel narrowed toward white water, and Jun shouted something about rapids, and Theo gripped the gunwale and did not answer, because the answer would have been a name, and they weren't ready to say it out loud—not even to the river, which keeps everything.
