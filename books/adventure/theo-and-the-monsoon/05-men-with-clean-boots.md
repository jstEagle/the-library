## 5. Men With Clean Boots

The trading post at Ban Sop Lek announced itself by smell before sight—diesel, dried fish, and the sweet rot of fruit nobody would buy before the rains took it anyway. Jun brought the boat in against the current with the engine coughing, because the engine had been coughing since the second day and he'd stopped pretending it was anything but a negotiation between him and the machine that he was slowly losing.

"You could let me look at it," Theo said.

"It's fine."

"It's not fine."

Jun cut the throttle and let the hull drift the last few meters, bumping the pilings soft as a kiss. "It's fine until Kessarin."

Theo didn't answer. They'd developed a language of silences over four days on the water, and Jun had gotten fluent in them faster than he liked. This one said: *you're lying to yourself about that engine the way you're lying to yourself about everything else.* He pretended not to speak it.

Ban Sop Lek existed for one reason: it sat at the choke point where the river narrowed and every boat going north into the valley had to pass within shouting distance of its docks. Which meant fuel, which meant supplies, which meant people. Jun counted the moored boats out of habit—a grain barge riding low, two long-tails with tarpaulins over their cargo, a government patrol skiff with bored men smoking on its rail. Nothing that made his shoulders tighten.

Then he saw the white boat.

It was tied at the far end of the dock, and it was wrong in the way a heron standing too still is wrong. Too clean. The hull gleamed with fresh paint, the canvas canopy was factory-new, and there was a generator humming on its deck like a contented animal. Nobody ran a boat that clean upriver in pre-monsoon season unless they were being paid to.

"Fuel," Jun said. "Food if it's not spoiled. Twenty minutes."

Theo was already looking at the white boat too. Jun watched their face do something small and complicated—the kind of arithmetic he'd seen on gamblers' faces when the odds turn.

"Theo."

"I heard you." They pulled their pack off the bench seat and held it a moment, weighing it, then swung it onto their shoulder instead of leaving it aboard. "Twenty minutes."

They walked up the dock without looking back, and Jun told himself the prickle at the back of his neck was just the heat.

* * *

He found the leak while they were gone, because of course there was a leak. It lived behind the starboard storage panel where the hull had taken a scrape on the rapids two days back—a weeping seam, slow enough to bail, fast enough to matter if the monsoon arrived early. He had his head and shoulders inside the compartment, lamp between his teeth, pressing his thumb along the seam to feel how deep the rot went, when he heard the boots on his dock.

Not sandals. Not bare feet. Boots—hard-soled, city-made, the kind of footwear that had never once been soaked in river mud—and they came down the planks in step, three pairs, unhurried.

Jun slid out of the compartment and stood up as the first of them reached the boat.

There were three. The one in front wore field trousers that had never seen a field, pressed into creases, and a shirt with the sleeves rolled to exactly the right height. Behind him, a woman with a tablet in a waterproof case and the flat patience of someone who catalogued things for a living. Third man hung back, big through the shoulders, doing nothing in particular with his hands where everyone could see them.

The front one smiled like a man arriving at a hotel desk. "Afternoon. That's a long run you're making, this time of year."

"Is it." Jun wiped his hands on his rag, slow, giving himself time to look past them. Up the dock, at the mouth of the trading post's covered walkway, Theo had stopped dead. From here they were just a figure with a pack, but Jun had spent four days learning the set of those shoulders, and right now they were set like a wall.

"We're with the Meridian Institute," the man said, and produced a folded document, holding it out. "Charter survey. We're recovering property removed from an authorized site. You may have been offered items—documents, artifacts—by persons passing through the delta. If so, there's compensation. Considerable compensation."

Jun didn't take the paper. "I'm a boatman."

"And business is business." The smile stayed fixed; only the eyes moved, ticking across the boat with an inventory-keeper's efficiency. The patched hull. The bail bucket. The false panel Jun had left open because he'd had his arm inside it ninety seconds ago. "Nice boat. Old design. Double-skinned?"

"Single-skinned. Old."

"Mm." The woman stepped forward without being invited and crouched at the gunwale, tilting her tablet to photograph the deck. Jun moved—not fast, not dramatic, just shifted his weight so his body blocked her line on the open compartment. She looked up at him. He looked back. Somewhere in that exchange they agreed to disagree about who was going to blink.

"Name?" she asked.

"No questions," Jun said. "That was the arrangement."

"With whom?"

"With my fare."

The big man laughed once, short, like a bark. The smiling one spread his hands. "Look, friend. Whatever you're carrying north, it isn't yours. The person who gave it to you stole it from an institution that has spent forty years building something worth stealing from. We're not police. We're not going to make trouble for a boatman. But that boat goes no further until we've looked through it, and you can make that easy or you can make it expensive."

Up the dock, Theo hadn't moved. And Jun understood, suddenly and completely, what was in the pack on their shoulder. He'd known there was something—of course he'd known; nobody paid double rate and forbade names over nothing—but knowing a thing as a shape and knowing it as a document that made three men in clean boots cross a province were different knowings, and the second one sat in his chest like swallowed stone.

Because he knew what institutions did. He'd grown up inside the shadow of one—the fisheries bureau, his father's whole life, the ministry seal on everything including the lie about the boy who drowned. Institutions didn't recover property. They recovered *versions*. They made sure the story that got written down was the story that kept the building standing.

"Search it," Jun said.

All three of them looked at him.

"Go ahead," he said. "You have a charter? Show me the clause that says you can board a private vessel. Show me the police officer who signed it. Because I see three people with clean boots and a rented boat, and I know what the harbor master at Sop Lek charges for looking the other way, and I know you paid it, and I know that means whatever you're doing has to stay quiet." He picked up his bail bucket, tipped the gray water over the side, unhurried. "So search it. Loudly. In front of all these witnesses. And when you find nothing, I'll be happy to describe your faces to every boatman between here and the valley, and none of you will buy cooperation on this river again."

The silence stretched. The woman's thumb hovered over her tablet. The big man glanced at the smiling one, waiting for a signal.

And Jun watched the exact moment the arithmetic failed them—watched it happen in the smiling man's eyes, the recalculation, the discovery that a disowned boatman with a leaking boat and nothing to lose was a worse bet than a fare with a hidden document. Men with clean boots needed the river to stay quiet more than the river needed anything from them.

"Another time," the smiling man said pleasantly, and turned.

They walked back up the dock in step. At the top, the smiling man paused beside Theo—said something Jun couldn't hear, brief, almost courteous—and Theo answered with a single word. Then the three of them continued on toward the white boat, and the dock belonged to the heat and the flies again.

* * *

Theo came down the planks carrying fuel cans in both hands and set them down harder than necessary.

"How much did you hear," they said.

"All of it. The part at the top too, probably." Jun nodded up the walkway. "Your friend with the smile knows your face."

"He knows my *name*." Theo crouched, busying themselves with the fuel can caps, and Jun heard what they weren't saying fill the space like water filling a hull. "Vasquez. He said it like a handshake. Like an invitation. *Your father's institute sends its regards.*"

Jun went still. Of all the silences they'd built between them, this was the biggest thing yet to fall into one.

"They're not surveying," Theo went on, voice flat, screwed down tight. "They were sent. Someone told them what moves north on this river this week, and when, and on whose boat. Which means either Amara talked before she died—" they shook their head immediately, answering their own doubt— "no. No, she didn't. Which means someone else knew about the map. Someone close to it. Close to *him*."

"Does it matter which?" Jun said. "They'll follow us now. That boat draws less water than mine and burns twice the fuel. They'll be behind us by dark."

"Then we don't stop again." Theo straightened, and something had changed in their face—Jun had watched fear live there for four days, banked and managed, and now it had burned down to something else, something with edges. "Not for fuel, not for weather, not for anything. Straight to Kessarin."

"That's suicide. The storm front—"

"I know what's coming." Theo slung the pack off their shoulder and knelt at the open storage compartment, the one with the weeping seam, and began working the false panel loose with their fingernails. "Which is why I'm not keeping this where anyone would look. Not on my body, not in my pack."

Jun crouched beside them despite himself. Inside the compartment, past the bilge stain and the rot, Theo pressed the map—wrapped in oilcloth, flat as a knife—into the gap behind the inner skin, into the space Jun had sealed with tar and fiberglass cloth two seasons ago to keep exactly this hull from dying. Their fingers found the seam's edge and paused there.

"This is your repair work," Theo said. "If it leaks—"

"It won't leak. It's the only part of this boat that won't leak." Jun heard his own voice come out lower than he intended. "Why are you telling me where it is?"

Theo looked at him then, really looked, the first time in four days their eyes had stayed anywhere longer than politeness required. Rain-smell was already moving down the valley behind the trading post, and the light had gone the color of old tin.

"Because those men are going to search us again somewhere with fewer witnesses," Theo said. "And next time I might not get to choose what I lose. So somebody else should know where the truth is sleeping."

Jun held their gaze a moment, then reached past them and pressed the panel home until it seated with a soft, watertight click.

"Get aboard," he said, and turned the engine over. It caught on the third try, coughing, stubborn, alive. "We're running the night water. Pray your father's people sleep worse than we do."

Behind them, at the far dock, the white boat's generator cut out—and in the sudden quiet, Jun heard the sound he'd been dreading: an engine turning over, patient and clean, falling in behind them as the first raindrops struck the river like fingers testing a drum.
