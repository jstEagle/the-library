## 6. Leaking Toward Kessarin

*Jun*

The storm front came down the valley like a debt collector, black and patient, and Jun read it the way he read everything—by what it would cost.

He'd been running the Serei's channels since he was nine years old, first poling his uncle's flat-bottomed skiff through the reed shallows, later at the tiller of boats that belonged to men who never asked where he slept. He knew the river's moods the way other men knew their mothers' faces. And right now the river was frightened. The birds had gone quiet an hour ago. The water in the back-channels had stopped moving downstream and started moving sideways, which meant the flood was already pushing up from the delta mouth, filling the low country like water filling a lung.

"We should have taken the eastern fork," Theo said from the bow.

"The eastern fork is a lake by tomorrow."

"Then we should have left two days ago."

"Yes." Jun didn't look up from the tiller. "We should have."

Theo had that quality Jun both resented and quietly relied on—the certainty of someone who had done everything alone for so long they'd forgotten that some things couldn't be done alone. They'd argued about the route at dawn, argued about the cargo weight at noon, and now, with the sky going the color of a bruise, they were committed to the last stretch of open channel before the Kessarin narrows: six kilometers of rapids where the river dropped through broken limestone, and where, in another season, Jun wouldn't have hesitated.

In this season, with the water already high and rising, the rapids weren't rapids anymore. They were a chute. And chutes didn't care how good your hands were.

The boat was called *Mali's Daughter*, though Jun had never known who Mali was; he'd bought her off a widow in Poi Sang for less than she was worth because her hull was scarred and her engine coughed like an old man on stairs. He'd patched her seams twice himself, tarred and stitched, and he knew every weak place in her body the way you know the weak places in your own. There was one along the port strake below the waterline—a soft spot the size of a spread hand—and one near the transom where the wood had gone punky from years of sitting in bilgewater.

He told Theo none of this. No questions, no names—that was the bargain—but the bargain ran both ways, and Jun kept his own counsel out of habit older than their agreement.

"Hold onto something," he said instead, and opened the throttle.

* * *

*Theo*

The river hit them before the rocks did.

One moment they were running fast water, spray coming over the gunwale in cold sheets, and the next the whole world tilted—Theo saw the far bank swing up, saw a granite tooth the color of bone slide past close enough to touch, saw Jun's shoulders drop and shift at the tiller like a man dancing with something enormous. The boat answered him. Not obediently—it bucked, it screamed in its timbers—but it answered, and Theo understood in one white-water instant that everything Jun had said about himself in three days of silence was true without ever being spoken.

Then the transom struck.

It wasn't a sound so much as a fact. A deep, structural crunch that Theo felt in their teeth, and the boat slewed sideways, and suddenly they were shipping water—real water, gallons of it, pouring over the stern in a brown rope while Jun hauled them toward an eddy behind a limestone shelf with the calm of a man parking a cart.

They grounded on a gravel bar in the lee of the rock, and the storm arrived thirty seconds later, as if it had been waiting for them to stop.

Rain came down in ropes. Theo scrambled to bail while Jun stripped off his shirt and went over the side into chest-deep water, feeling along the hull. When he surfaced, his face told the story before his mouth did.

"How bad?" Theo shouted over the rain.

"She'll float." He said it the way you say *the patient will live*, which is not the same as *the patient is well*. "Transom seam's opened. I can stitch it, but not here, not in this. There's a fishing camp past the narrows—we beach there, I fix her proper."

"And if we don't?"

Jun looked at them across the rain, water streaming off his jaw, and for a moment something moved behind his eyes—not anger exactly. Something more tired.

"Then we swim the rest of the way," he said, "and your map drowns with us. Bail."

* * *

*Jun*

They ran the narrows at dusk with the boat riding low and wrong, and Jun did it by feel and memory and the particular stubbornness of a man who has nothing left to prove to anyone. Twice the current took them sideways. Once the keel kissed stone hard enough to lift Theo off their feet. But the *Mali's Daughter* held—held the way patched things hold, grudgingly, out of spite—and then the channel widened, the water smoothed, and the fishing camp appeared through the rain as a scatter of orange lamplight under a lean-to, smoke bleeding sideways in the wind.

There were four men there, drying nets, and they knew Jun.

That was the strange part. Theo watched it happen: the oldest fisherman stood up slowly when Jun stepped onto the planks, and his face did something complicated—recognition, then grief, then a careful blankness, like a door being closed from the inside.

"Jun Sombath," the old man said. "Thought you were dead."

"Wishful thinking, Uncle Rithy."

"You're headed to Kessarin." It wasn't a question. The old man's eyes flicked to Theo, to the wrapped shape of their pack, and back. "In this season. With that boat."

"I'm headed wherever the fare goes."

Rithy looked at him for a long moment, and whatever he saw made him shake his head slowly, the way men do at funerals. Then he jerked his chin toward the largest hut. "Dry space. Tar and stitching's in the third chest. Food's whatever you catch." He paused. "Your mother still lights the lamp in the front window, you know. Every night. Eleven years."

Jun's back was already turned. "I'll pay for the tar," he said, and walked into the rain.

* * *

*Theo*

Theo found him an hour later in the boathouse, working by lamplight with his sleeves rolled, driving waxed thread through the transom seam with a curved needle, his hands steady despite the cold. Theo sat down on an overturned crate and watched for a while. The rain hammered the tin roof like applause from a hostile crowd.

"That old man knew you," Theo said finally.

"Mm."

"Said you were dead."

"People say a lot of things." Jun pulled the thread tight. "Hold this. Here." Theo braced the plank while he worked, and for a few minutes there was only the needle's whisper and the storm.

Then, without looking up, Jun said: "Kessarin's my village. Where we're going. You should know that much, since you're paying me to take you there."

Theo kept their hands still on the plank. "You didn't mention that."

"No questions, no names. Your rule as much as mine." He tied off a stitch, bit the thread. "But a man should know if he's floating into somebody else's grave."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, the river climbed audibly against the bank, a long wet grinding sound, like the valley swallowing.

"My sister drowned when I was fourteen," Jun said. "Sophea. She was eleven. Everyone said it was an accident—she slipped at the ferry landing, high water, bad luck. That's what my father told the village. That's what he told the monks, so they'd say the right prayers. That's what he told himself." Another stitch. His voice never changed register; it stayed flat and workmanlike, like he was describing hull damage. "But I was there. I saw our cousin Naren push her. Saw it clear as I'm seeing you. Naren was sixteen and my father's favorite, and my father decided—before I even finished telling him what happened—he decided what I'd seen. He took my arm at the funeral and he said, *You will say it was the water.*"

Theo's breath had gone very quiet in their chest.

"And I said no," Jun said. "Once. Just once, standing there in front of everyone in my good shirt. And my father looked at me like I'd already drowned too. By the end of the month I was sleeping in the boathouse. By the end of the season I was gone." He set down the needle and finally looked up, and his eyes in the lamplight were dry and absolutely level. "Eleven years I've run this river. Never once gone back. And now the monsoon's about to drown the whole valley, and the fare is taking me home anyway."

The words sat between them in the smoky air, enormous and unarmored, and Theo understood that Jun hadn't asked for sympathy. He'd paid a debt. He'd given a wound to match the journey, because that was the terms of travel between people like them—you showed the scar so the other person knew yours wasn't hidden under the floorboards.

And now it was Theo's turn.

Theo felt it rise in them—the whole architecture of it. The father whose name opened doors Theo refused to walk through. The dig sites worked alone, illegally, deliberately beneath the notice of the institutions that had made *Elias Vasquez* a legend. The shard in the fire two weeks ago, curling and blackening, the excavation mark burning away. There was a story there that matched Jun's wound for wound, and it pressed against the backs of Theo's teeth like a confession trying to be born.

"It's late," Theo said. "You should sleep. I'll take first watch on the water level."

Something flickered across Jun's face—there and gone, quick as a fish turning. Not surprise. Recognition. He nodded slowly, gathered his needle and tar, and stood.

"Yeah," he said. "First watch."

At the doorway he stopped, half in the rain, and didn't turn around. "You know what the funny thing is? I told you all that, and I don't even know your name. Still don't want to." A pause. "But I know you've got one. And I know it costs you something every day you don't say it."

Then he was gone into the dark, and Theo sat alone with the lamp and the rising river, holding a silence that had just become heavier than any artifact they'd ever lifted from the mud.

Outside, the water kept climbing. And far off through the rain, down the black throat of the valley, Theo could see the first faint glow of Kessarin's lamps—and, closer, on the flooded channel behind them, a lantern swinging. A boat, running dark except for that light, moving fast, following their wake upstream.

Someone was coming.
