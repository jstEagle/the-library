## 7. The Temple Under the Floodline

The river told them where to turn before the map did.

Theo had been reading the water for three days now, and it had a grammar all its own: the way the current braided around a drowned sandbar, the brown seam where two channels married, the sudden glassy calm that meant depth below. Jun steered by instinct and silence, and Theo sat in the bow with Amara's map spread across their knees under an oilcloth, watching the valley close around them like a fist.

The hills had gone green-black with rain. Not rain yet—the monsoon was still a bruise on the horizon, a wall of cloud stacked so high it looked geological—but the air had the wet weight of held breath. Every hour the river climbed a finger's width up the banks. Theo could measure it against the stumps of old fence posts they passed, black teeth in the current.

"There." They pointed left, where a tributary slid out of the hills through a throat of karst. "The notch in the ridge. The map shows a shrine marker on the bluff above it."

Jun didn't answer. He never did, not at first. He simply leaned on the sweep oar and the boat came about, slow as a thought changing its mind.

They poled up the tributary through water that should have been dry ground. That was the thing that kept catching in Theo's chest: none of this was supposed to be underwater yet. The map's margins were full of Amara's cramped annotations—*dry season crossing, hip-deep; temple terrace above floodline even in worst years*—and every one of them was already a lie. The valley was drowning ahead of schedule. Whatever window she'd imagined when she drew these lines, someone had slammed it shut early.

"Water's over the lower steps," Theo said, when the temple appeared.

It rose out of the flooded treeline like something surfacing from sleep. A pyramidal massif of dressed stone, terraced, choked with strangler fig, its summit shrine still clear of the water but its flanks already swallowed to the second terrace. Carvings ran along the visible cornice—processions of figures, hands linked, marching toward a central doorway that gaped black even at this distance.

Jun brought the boat alongside a half-drowned fig tree and made it fast without being asked. Then he looked at Theo, and his face said what he'd said at the trading post and every day since: *your errand, your risk.*

"I need maybe two hours," Theo said. "If the water comes up faster than that—"

"It will."

"—you take the boat and you go. You don't wait for me. You get to Kessarin."

Jun's jaw worked once. "You'll drown down there and I'll have carried your ghost upriver along with everything else."

"Then don't carry anything else. Just the promise."

Something moved behind his eyes—not agreement, exactly. Acknowledgment. He pulled the oilcloth tighter over the supplies and turned away, and Theo took that for the yes it was.

* * *

Diving a flooded antechamber is nothing like diving open water, and Theo had learned the difference the hard way, years ago, in a sinkhole cave that had nearly kept them. You go in on a line. You count your kicks. You keep one hand on the guide rope and you never, ever let curiosity spend your air budget for you.

They geared up in the shallows at the base of the third terrace: mask, fins, the battered dive tank Jun had bartered for at the last trading post, a waterproof torch lashed to their wrist. The map showed the antechamber's entrance below the current waterline—a processional stair descending into what had been, in Amara's time, a dry vault. She'd written *chamber intact, floor silt, contents undisturbed*, and beneath it, in different ink, faded: *V. has seen photographs. He understands what this means.*

V.

Theo had stared at that letter for a full day before letting themselves think it. Vasquez. Their father's initial, sitting in a dead woman's handwriting like a knife left point-up in a drawer.

They'd told themselves it was coincidence. Initials were cheap. Half the surveyors in the profession used them. And then, on the fourth night, unable to sleep in the rocking boat, they'd taken out the map and held it under lamplight and seen it—the grid notation in the corner of the site plan, the little hooked symbol for magnetic north, the exact idiosyncratic shorthand Theo had grown up watching chalked across their father's field boards. Nobody taught that shorthand. It wasn't in any textbook. It was a private language, and Amara spoke it fluently, which meant either she'd learned it from him—

—or he'd learned it from her. Or they'd learned it together, side by side, in this valley, decades ago, in a season of Theo's father's life that appeared in no published excavation report, no biography, no carefully curated legend.

Theo had rolled the map up with hands that wouldn't quite steady, and hadn't spoken of it to Jun, and had slept badly, and dreamed of chalk dust.

Now they stood at the water's edge with the entrance somewhere below, and breathed until the shaking stopped.

"In on a line," they muttered. "Count your kicks."

They went under.

The cold hit first, then the dark, total and immediate—the torch beam turning the flood into a brown fog where visibility was three meters and shrinking. Theo found the doorway by feel: carved stone under their gloves, a lintel thick as a man's waist, and beyond it the antechamber opening like a swallowed cathedral. They tied off the guide line to the ironwood stake they'd hammered into a crack beside the door, paid it out, and kicked inside.

One. Two. Three.

The chamber was enormous. The beam kept finding things and losing them again—column bases rising out of the silt like buried knees, a fallen lintel furred white with some pale fungus, walls of bas-relief scrolling past at the edge of light. Processions again. Linked hands. Figures carrying something on a litter between them, faces tilted upward.

Seven. Eight. Nine.

The floor shelved downward toward the inner sanctum, and there—half-buried where the silt had drifted against a stone plinth—Theo saw the shape that stopped their heart.

A sealed ceramic tube. Expedition-grade. The kind survey teams used to carry site plans and journals through wet country. It lay wedged under the plinth's lip as if someone had tucked it there deliberately, sheltering it, and around it the silt was scoured in a fan pattern, as though water had once rushed past and spared it.

Theo's air gauge read just under half. Enough. Barely.

They dug. The silt came away in cold clouds, blinding the beam, and they worked blind for a minute by touch alone—fingers walking the tube's length, finding the wax seal cracked but holding, finding the cap—and then they had it, hugged to their chest, and they turned back along the line counting kicks in reverse, and the whole way up their lungs burned and their mind burned hotter, screaming at them to hurry, hurry, *hurry*, because whatever was in the tube had waited thirty years under the floodline and it could not wait another hour, not now, not when Theo's own name might be written inside it in their father's hand.

They broke the surface in the flooded stairwell, gasping, tore off the mask, and hauled themselves onto the second terrace with the tube strapped across their back like a spine.

* * *

There was a dry pocket under the summit shrine—an overhang where the roof stone had collapsed inward and left a wedge of shadow above the waterline. Theo dragged themselves into it, shivering, and laid the tube across their knees.

For a long moment they didn't open it.

Because they understood, suddenly, with terrible clarity, what they were about to do. If they broke this seal, there was no version of the world afterward in which their father remained the man on the lecture hall posters, the voice on the documentary narrations, the legend whose shadow Theo had spent a decade trying to dig out of. Either the seal held nothing—which would be its own kind of grief—or it held proof, and proof was a fire that did not care who it burned.

Their hands opened it anyway. Hands always knew before hearts did.

Inside, wrapped in waxed canvas gone stiff with age, was a journal. Leather-bound, water-stained at the edges but dry at the heart, the cover tooled with a small stamped device: a coiled serpent above crossed survey stakes. Theo knew that stamp. Every archaeologist of a certain generation knew it. It was the mark of the Meridian Survey, the joint expedition of 1989, the one official history recorded as having ended in failure and dissolution with no significant finds.

Official history was wrong.

Theo opened the journal, and Amara's handwriting flowed up at them like water finding its level—tight, slanted, impatient, alive.

*Day 41. We have cleared the second terrace. The antechamber reliefs are intact beyond anything we hoped. R. says the processional frieze dates the final construction phase later than the Kessarin school believed—he is brilliant and insufferable and I have told him so twice today.*

R.

Theo's mouth went dry. They turned pages with fingers that felt borrowed.

*Day 58. V. insists we delay publication until the full stratigraphy is confirmed. I understand caution but I do not understand him. This is the find of the century and he wants to sit on it. He says: "Not yet. Let me make sure it cannot be taken from us." Taken from us. As if it were ours. As if it were his.*

*Day 63. Found him copying my survey grids onto his own field boards. He laughed when I caught him. Said great discoveries belong to whoever writes them down properly. I asked him what that made me. He didn't answer.*

*Day 71. The funding collapsed. V. has accepted a position—I won't write the institution's name here, I am too angry to spell it correctly. He leaves tomorrow. He has asked me one last time to sign over my site notes. "For the archive," he says. I told him the archive could drown with me.*

*Day 72. I have hidden the journal and the master plans in the sanctum. If I am ever proven wrong about him, let the river judge us both. If I am right, then someday someone who is not afraid of his name will come down here and read this.*

Theo closed the journal.

The chamber under the shrine was very quiet. Below, the river climbed the terraces with the patience of something that had all the time in the world, and somewhere far off, thunder rolled along the ridge like furniture being dragged across heaven.

Vindication came first. It came hot and total, flooding Theo's chest the way the water flooded the valley—*I knew it, I always knew there was something rotten under the legend, I knew the way you know a house has termites by the sound it makes at night.* Thirty years of their father's fame, the prizes, the documentaries, the monographs built on the Meridian Survey's "failure"—and here it was, the whole architecture of his career resting on a stolen foundation, on a woman he'd abandoned in this valley with her notes copied and her name erased.

Then the horror arrived, right on schedule, the way it always does behind vindication, wearing the same face.

Because Theo knew how it would go. They'd seen it their whole life, the machinery of reputation, how it digested scandal and excreted legacy. Exposing him would not erase him. It would make him more famous. The fall of a giant draws bigger crowds than the rise of an honest woman. And Amara—Amara would get a footnote. *Uncredited collaborator, Meridian Survey, 1989.* A paragraph in someone else's redemption arc. Her daughter would inherit a truth that changed nothing except how much it hurt.

And Theo would be the one who lit the fuse. The Vasquez child, burning down the Vasquez name, and everyone would say it was ambition, or bitterness, or daddy issues, and no one would ever ask whether it was justice, because justice was the one motive nobody believed in anymore.

The journal lay in their lap, heavier than the dive tank, heavier than stone.

*Someone who is not afraid of his name,* Amara had written.

Theo looked up, and the sky through the broken shrine roof had gone the color of a bruise going bad, and the first fat drops of the true monsoon were striking the flooded terraces below like stones thrown by an enormous patient hand.

And downriver, faint under the thunder, came the sound of engines. More than one. Running hard against the current, coming fast.

Theo wrapped the journal in the waxed canvas, sealed it inside their pack's dry bag, and stood up in the ruined shrine with the storm breaking overhead and rivals closing on the water below—and understood that the choice Amara had actually left them was not whether to tell the truth.

It was who they'd be standing next to when it came out.
