## 8. The Levee Breaks

The rain came the way Jun had always known it would—not as weather but as a verdict.

He'd grown up with monsoons. He knew their moods, their false mercies, the way they could spend three days sulking on the ridgeline and then fall on a valley like a debt collector. This one had no patience in it at all. It arrived overnight, whole, as if it had been waiting offshore for years just for this.

By dawn the river had climbed two meters past its banks and was still climbing, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees sideways like matchsticks. The boat sat tied to the temple's drowned causeway, riding high on water that should have been a hundred meters away, and Jun stood in the downpour with his hands on the mooring line and felt the old arithmetic of Kessarin running behind his eyes: how many boats, how many trips, how many people, how much time.

The answer to the last one was: almost none.

"Theo!" He didn't bother hiding the urgency. That bargain they'd struck—no questions, no names—had died somewhere back in the rapids, and neither of them had mourned it. "Theo, we have to go now."

Theo came out of the antechamber entrance soaked to the bone, hair plastered flat, arms wrapped around something bundled in oilcloth and pressed against their chest like a newborn. Even from ten meters, through the sheeting rain, Jun knew what it was. They hadn't let go of it since they'd surfaced yesterday, gasping, eyes wild with something between triumph and sickness. They slept holding it. They ate with one hand so the other could rest on it.

The journal. Amara's journal. Forty years of proof that a great man had been a thief.

"We can't," Theo shouted back over the rain. "Not yet—the water's still rising through the lower galleries, I need—"

"You don't *need* anything." Jun waded toward them, boots finding the slick stone by memory. "Listen to me. That sound?"

He made them stop. Made them turn their head east, toward the valley, toward Kessarin.

Underneath the rain there was another sound. Lower. Deeper. A groan that came up through the ground itself, through the soles of their feet, long and straining, like some enormous animal bearing weight it couldn't hold.

"That's the levee," Jun said. "I helped build that levee when I was fourteen. My father laid the first stones. When it makes that sound—" He swallowed. "It doesn't make that sound twice."

* * *

They ran for the boat together, and even then Theo wouldn't put the bundle down, wouldn't lash it below decks where it would be safe, just held it tighter and hauled the line with their free hand like a woman refusing to set down her child in a burning house.

Jun got the engine going on the third pull. The boat wallowed out into a river that no longer had banks, just a brown immensity dotted with the tops of submerged fences and the roofs of storage sheds already surrendering. Rain hit the water so hard it seemed to bounce.

"Kessarin's on the high side of the levee," he said, wrestling the tiller. "But the high side is only high because the levee holds. If it goes—"

"How many people?"

"Four hundred. Maybe more, with the market day crowd." He didn't look at Theo. He watched the channel markers, or what was left of them—most were underwater now, and he was navigating by a childhood map in his head, the shape of a valley he'd sworn he'd never think about again. "My sister's there."

Theo went very still beside him. In all the days upriver, through the rapids and the men with clean boots and the long nights of shared watch, Theo had never once asked about the sister. Had taken the silence Jun offered and matched it with their own. Now:

"Sari," Jun said, before they could ask. "Her name is Sari. She runs the ferry landing now. Our father's old post." His mouth twisted. "She kept everything. I kept nothing. We haven't spoken in eleven years."

The boat bucked over a submerged snag. Something tore along the hull—a long, fibrous shriek—and water began coming in faster than the bilge pump liked.

"Hold that seam," Jun snapped, and Theo jammed their shoulder against the patch, oilcloth bundle wedged between chin and chest, and for one absurd moment they looked like what they were: two people bailing out their own history with their bare hands.

Then the world turned white with noise, and the levee broke.

* * *

Jun heard it before he saw it—a crack like the sky splitting, deeper than thunder—and then, half a kilometer downstream, the earth itself moved. Not collapsed. *Moved*, a section of embankment the length of a village sliding loose all at once, and the river poured through the gap the way water pours through a torn throat.

The wave reached them forty seconds later. Jun had just enough time to cut perpendicular, nose into it, throttle wide, and then the boat climbed a hill of moving water and slammed down the far side hard enough to throw Theo off the seam and into the gunwale. The engine coughed, caught, coughed again.

When Jun could see again, Kessarin was drowning.

He'd imagined this a thousand times over eleven years—in guilt, in nightmares, in the bitter private theater where exiles rehearse what they lost. None of it had prepared him for the sight of the real thing: the market stalls going under in minutes, the school roof already an island, people on the levee's surviving spine hauling each other up out of the flood in chains, and above it all the temple bell tower, his grandfather's bell tower, ringing and ringing with no one pulling the rope, just the structure swaying, the clapper swinging loose, tolling the valley's funeral without anyone's permission.

"They'll go to the bell tower," he heard himself say. "And the shrine hill. Sari will take them to the shrine hill, she knows the water, she—" His voice cracked on her name and he hated it, hated the eleven wasted years, hated the father who'd demanded a lie about a drowned boy and hated himself more for taking eleven years to stop paying for refusing.

Theo grabbed his arm. "Jun. The boat's sinking. We can't carry four hundred people. We can carry maybe twenty, in shifts, if we run the whole day—"

"I know the math." Everyone in Kessarin knew the math. Every monsoon was a lesson in the math. "We start with the ones in the water."

"No." Theo's grip tightened. "Jun. Listen to me. The hunters' camp—it was on the low bank, below the trading post. If the wave took it—"

"So did half the valley. What's your point?"

"Their charter boat. It has a radio. And it has room for thirty." Theo's face was pale under the rain, and Jun watched something move across it—watched the exact moment the choice arrived, the moment he'd seen coming for days without letting himself name it. "If we take their boat, we save more people. But someone has to stay here, at the temple, and mark the journal's location before the lower gallery floods completely. Water rises another meter tonight and the antechamber silt seals over and no one ever finds it again. Forty years, Jun. Her whole life, buried twice."

The rain hammered down. Downstream, the bell kept tolling.

"And you're telling me this," Jun said slowly, "because you want me to tell you which one to do."

"I'm telling you because I can't choose." For the first time since the delta, since the burned shard, since the dying woman in the storm, Theo sounded young. Scared. Honest. "That journal is the only true thing I've ever dug up. And those people are—" They stopped. Looked downstream at the roofs going under, at the small figures clinging to the levee spine. "Those people are your family. And you've spent eleven years choosing not to have one. I know what that costs. I've been doing it my whole life."

Jun stared at them. Then he laughed—a short, broken sound with no humor in it at all.

"You know what my father asked me? The night I left? He said, *tell them the boy slipped, tell them it was nobody's fault, and you can stay.*" The rain ran down his face; it was as good as anything for hiding what it carried. "A boy drowned at the ferry landing because our father cut the safety lines to save money. Eleven years old. And everyone in that village chose the lie, because the lie kept the ferries running and the money coming. I was the only one who said no." He pointed at the drowning valley. "*Them.* They chose it too. Every last one of them looked at me like I was the traitor."

"And Sari?"

The question landed like a hook behind his ribs.

"She chose the lie loudest of anyone," Jun said quietly. "She married the harbormaster's son. She runs the landing now. She is, right now, probably standing on that levee saving lives, because she's better at being good inside a bad system than I ever was." He wiped his face with the back of his hand. "So no, Theo. I don't get to stand on principle while she drowns. Principle is cheap when you're dry."

He threw the tiller hard over, toward the hunters' grounded charter, its mast tilted drunkenly against a submerged grove.

"But hear me," he said. "You're not staying at that temple alone. Whatever you decide about the journal—you decide it looking at the water, not hiding from it. That's the deal now. No questions, no names—that bargain's dead. New bargain: we don't leave each other alone with our ghosts."

* * *

The charter boat was abandoned, half-swamped, its crew fled inland—but the radio worked, and the hold was dry enough, and by the time Jun had it limping toward the levee spine the first survivors were already swimming for them, twenty, thirty, faces he knew and faces he didn't, hands slapping the hull.

Sari was among them.

He saw her from fifty meters and knew her instantly, the way you know a scar—hair plastered dark, hauling a child by the collar with one arm, driving swimmers toward the boat with the other, shouting orders in a voice that carried over the flood exactly the way their mother's once had. Their eyes met across the water. Eleven years passed between them in about two seconds, and neither of them looked away.

"Take them up!" she screamed. "Shrine hill's full, bell tower's leaning—we need runs to the ridge road, all day, all night—"

"I know!" Jun screamed back. "I know how it works!"

Something crossed her face—recognition, fury, grief, gratitude, all of it at once, none of it with time to be felt—and then she shoved another swimmer toward the hull and turned back into the water, and Jun turned the wheel, and that was the whole conversation. It was enough. It would have to be enough.

Behind him, on the deck, Theo stood with the oilcloth bundle still in their arms, watching the temple disappear into the rain to the north. Watching the water climb the last of the causeway. Jun saw them look at the bundle, then at the drowning village, then at the bundle again—and saw the moment the scales tipped, the moment Amara's daughter's inheritance became heavier than any proof.

Theo knelt, and began wrapping the journal in the boat's spare tarpaulin, sealing it, weighting it, preparing it to survive underwater.

"What are you doing?" Jun called.

Theo looked up. Rain streamed off their face, and underneath it was something Jun had never seen there before—not anger, not grief, not the flinch of a person erasing themselves. Resolve.

"There's a sealed cistern under the shrine hill," Theo shouted back. "Amara marked it on the map—she used it as a cache site, forty years ago. Stone walls. Watertight. If I sink the journal there, the flood protects it instead of destroying it." They were already moving to the rail, already measuring the distance to the drowning shore with a diver's eye. "I can reach it. I can plant it like evidence at a crime scene. But I have to go *now*, before the current takes the shoreline apart—and then I swim back through that."

They nodded downstream, at the flood, at the wreckage and the bodies and the bell still tolling.

Jun looked at the water. He looked at his sister, somewhere in it. He looked at the mad, stubborn, self-erasing stranger who had walked onto his boat with a dead woman's map and was now about to dive into a drowning valley to bury the truth where the truth's enemies would never think to dig.

"You come back," Jun said. "That's not a request. That's the new terms."

Theo smiled—for the first time since Jun had known them, actually smiled—and stepped off the rail into the flood.
