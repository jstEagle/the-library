## 9. Loyalty Is a Choice You Make Twice

The journal was in my hands when the levee broke.

I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the only thing I have left to offer anyone. I was standing in the flooded antechamber with Amara's expedition journal pressed against my chest inside its oilcloth wrapping, water at my ribs and rising, and I could feel the shape of it through the fabric—every page, every date, every line of her handwriting that proved my father had taken her life's work and signed his own name beneath it. Forty years of erasure in a package small enough to hold against my heart. And I held it. Even as the first crack ran through the eastern levee like a rifle shot, even as Jun's voice came down through the collapsing entrance shaft screaming my name, I stood there in the brown rising dark and thought: *this is mine now. This is the thing that makes me real.*

The second crack was louder.

Water came through it not as a flow but as a decision—the river choosing, all at once, to be somewhere else. The antechamber's ceiling groaned. Silt bloomed around my legs like ink. And somewhere above me, muffled by stone and flood, I heard something that cut through my obsession faster than any argument could have: a child crying.

Not a child. A woman. But in that moment, with the temple dying around me, every frightened voice sounded like the ones I'd heard the night my mother left—voices calling for someone who wasn't coming.

I swam.

* * *

The valley had become a sea with landmarks. Kessarin village sat on its low rise like an island being eaten from the edges inward, rooftops already swimming, livestock gone, and the levee that had protected three generations of farmers was shedding itself into the current in slabs the size of houses. The rain came sideways. The sky was the color of wet slate.

Jun's boat was wedged against the remains of the market stilt-house, and he was hauling people over the gunwale with his bare hands, no system, no order, just his arms and his fury. His sister was on the roof of the shrine, waist-deep in what used to be its floor, holding two children above the waterline while the current tried to peel them off one by one.

I'd never seen her before that day, but I knew her instantly. She had Jun's jaw set at the same stubborn angle, and she was shouting his name across the flood with a voice that cracked exactly where his did.

"Take them!" she screamed when we pulled alongside. "Take the children first!"

"Theo—" Jun started.

"I've got the boat. Go."

He went. Of course he went. Whatever wall he'd built between himself and Kessarin, whatever lie about a drowning had gotten him cast out of this place, none of it survived contact with his sister's voice. He climbed the shrine roof like a man climbing toward something he'd been told he'd lost forever, and I watched him take the children from her arms, and I watched her look at him—not with forgiveness yet, but with recognition—and I understood that some choices you only get to make once, and some you make twice, and the second time is the one that counts.

Then the current hit the boat broadside and I stopped watching anyone and started fighting the river.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about rescue work in a flood: it is mostly arithmetic performed by exhausted people. Nine seats in the boat, twelve people on the roof, four hundred meters to the high ground at the pagoda hill, and the water gaining a hand's width every ten minutes. You don't think about grief or truth or fathers. You count. You reach. You pull. You tell a shaking grandmother to hold onto the man next to her because the man next to her is solid and the water is not. You do it again. You do it again.

The hunters' camp washed away at midday.

I saw it happen from the pagoda slope, where we'd stacked our ninth load of survivors and were going back for a tenth. Their charter banner—the university crest, my father's institution, the clean boots and clean hands of men who filed paperwork for other people's discoveries—came apart in the current like a paper lantern. Their gear raft rolled. Two of them made it to a tree; the third I hauled out of the water myself, coughing river out of his lungs, and when he recognized me he grabbed my wrist and rasped, "The journal. Where's the journal?"

It was still in the temple. Still in the antechamber. By now, if the collapse had gone the way I thought it would, it was under six meters of new river, wrapped in oilcloth, sinking into the silt with everything else Amara had ever touched.

"Gone," I said.

He stared at me. Somewhere behind him, the valley finished drowning.

"You could have saved it," he said. "You had time."

"Yeah," I said. "I know."

And here is the strange part, the part I keep turning over like a shard in my palm: I didn't say it with bitterness. I said it the way you say *the sun rose*. Because somewhere between the antechamber and the shrine roof, between the child-cry and Jun's sister's hands, the arithmetic had changed on me. A journal proves a theft. It doesn't feed a village. It doesn't put children on high ground. It doesn't make a brother climb back to the sister who stopped speaking to him. Truth is heavy, and I had spent my whole life learning how much weight a person can carry, and I finally understood that the answer is: less than you think, if what you're carrying isn't yours to carry alone.

Amara hadn't asked me to avenge her. She'd asked me to deliver a map to her daughter. That was the whole promise. Everything else—the proof, the vindication, the revenge—I had added myself, the way I'd added my father's shadow to every dig site I ever worked. I kept putting him in places he didn't belong.

The map was dry inside my jacket. It had been dry the entire time.

* * *

We found Amara's daughter on the last run.

Her name was Suriya, and she was standing on the pagoda steps directing evacuation lines with the calm of someone who had been expecting the worst since childhood, and when our overloaded boat scraped against the lowest step she looked at me and said, "You're the digger. My mother wrote to me about you. She said if a stranger ever brought me a map, I should trust them, because they'd have earned it."

She said it like a fact. Like weather.

I took the map out of my jacket. It was warm from my body, which felt wrong and right at once. "Your mother traded it to me," I said. "For a promise. Deliver it to her daughter in Kessarin before the monsoon seals the valley." I looked at the drowned fields, the swallowed shrine, the village that would never exist again in the form she'd known it. "I'm late by half a day. I'm sorry."

Suriya took the map with both hands, the way you receive something living. Then she looked up. "There was more, wasn't there. In the temple. There was more than a map."

"There was a journal," I said. "Hers. From the original expedition."

Her breath caught. Around us, refugees pressed close without pretending not to listen.

"It's under the flood now," I said. "I left it. People needed carrying instead." I braced for the anger—for the moment she'd tell me I'd let her mother's proof drown to save strangers, that I'd chosen wrong, that loyalty to the dead meant something different than loyalty to the living.

Instead Suriya closed her eyes and laughed—a short, broken sound that was also, unmistakably, relief.

"She told me once," she said, "that the difference between an archaeologist and a grave robber is what you refuse to take." She opened her eyes. They were Amara's eyes; I could see the prospector in them, the woman who'd died in my camp trading away her last treasure. "You refused."

"The university will bury her anyway," I said. "Without the journal, there's no claim. No proof. Your mother stays erased."

"No," Suriya said, and unfolded the map against her knee, rain hammering the waxed surface, her finger finding the survey gridlines, the depth markings, the annotations in her mother's hand. "Without the journal, there's no evidence. But maps can be redrawn, and claims can be refiled, and I have been reading my mother's letters for thirty years." Her finger stopped on a notation near the temple's western approach—a coordinate cluster I'd never fully deciphered. "She hid copies, Theo. She always hid copies. She taught me that the safest place for the truth is not a vault." She looked up at me, and the rain ran off her chin like the valley running off its old life. "It's a person who keeps their promises."

* * *

That night, on the pagoda hill, with three hundred villagers packed under tarps and the water lapping at the third terrace, Jun sat down beside me without asking and handed me half of a rice brick. We ate in silence for a while. Below us, his sister was teaching children a song to keep them calm, and every so often her eyes found him across the firelight, and neither of them looked away fast enough.

"You went back for the journal," Jun said finally. "Before the break. I saw you come out of the water with your arms empty."

"I went back for it," I agreed.

"And?"

"And then I heard someone crying, and I did the math differently."

He chewed. The fire popped. Down in the black flooded valley, something vast shifted—the temple settling, maybe, or the river rearranging its furniture.

"My sister asked about you," he said. "She asked who the stranger was who kept coming back for more people when the boat was taking water. I told her your name."

I went still. "You told her my name."

"You have one," Jun said. "Might as well use it."

I looked out at the water. Somewhere under all that brown movement lay a journal with my father's signature in its margins, lying beside the truth it had buried, and I found—to my genuine surprise—that I could picture it without my chest closing. It was down there doing no harm. It was down there being patient. And tomorrow, or the day after, a woman with her mother's eyes was going to start digging in letters and coordinates, and the truth was going to come up out of the ground the way it always does: slowly, and by the hands of someone who refuses to stop.

"I need to go see my father," I said. It came out smaller than I meant it to. Smaller, and steadier.

Jun nodded like I'd told him the tide schedule. "After the water drops," he said. "I'll take you downriver. I owe a visit of my own."

The rain thinned toward midnight. On the far bank, barely visible in the last gray light, a figure stood at the edge of the floodline watching our fires—still, deliberate, wearing clothes too clean for a refugee. Watching me.

Suriya followed my gaze, and her hand closed slowly around the map.

"That's the man," she said quietly, "who signed the charter."
