## 1. Miles, Not Years

Three years is a number. Three years is also forty-one thousand kilometers of trail, if you keep count — and Gideon Zhou kept count the way other men kept diaries.

He did not count days. Days were treacherous. Days accumulated into months and months into anniversaries, and anniversaries had a way of arriving with names attached: the date Devi's tent was found empty below the serac field, the date the inquiry board read its verdict in a room that smelled of wet wool and coffee, the date his name was struck from the registry in Kathmandu and then, six weeks later, from the one in Lhasa, as though a man could be unmade by paperwork on two sides of a border. He had learned to think instead in distances. A season was not three months; it was nine hundred kilometers of switchbacks above the Kali Gandaki. A bad year was not grief; it was the Annapurna circuit walked eleven times with strangers who called him by a name that wasn't his.

The name was Tenzin Lama. It fit him the way borrowed boots fit: serviceable, slightly wrong, blistering at the heel if you wore it too long. The trekking agency in Pokhara had never asked for papers beyond what he'd forged himself, and the tourists never asked for anything at all except photographs and a slower pace and stories about avalanches they could safely be frightened by. He gave them all of it. He was very good at being no one. It was, he had discovered, the one skill the last three years had actually sharpened.

* * *

The morning the letter came, he was measuring out the day in vertical meters — 1,400 up to Thorong La with a group of eight Germans whose idea of hardship was a cold shower — when he saw the boy from the post office waiting outside the teahouse in Muktinath, holding an envelope the way you hold something that might bite.

"Nobody sends letters here," the boy said. "Not real ones."

Gideon took it without looking at the face of it. That was instinct now, three years deep: never let your eyes linger on anything with your old name near it, even a shape, even a guess. He tucked it inside his jacket against the map pocket where he kept the brass compass he no longer trusted and climbed.

He didn't open it until the group was asleep. The teahouse kitchen had gone dark; a single butter lamp burned down to a coin of light on the counter, and outside the wind worked at the prayer flags with the patience of a debt collector. He sat on the stone step with his back against the cold wall and turned the envelope over twice before he broke the seal.

No return address. The paper was good — too good for this altitude, the kind of stock that came out of ministry offices in Kathmandu, heavy enough to hold a crease like a spine. The handwriting was small and exact, every letter standing at attention.

*Mr. Lama,* it began, and he almost smiled at that, at the courtesy extended to a fiction.

*I will not insult you by pretending I don't know who you are. I know what you carried on the Manaslu approach in '19. I know the route notation system you used, because I have spent four months studying maps that use it — maps drawn after your name disappeared from every register in this country.*

*A government survey team went missing eleven days ago on the Larkya Glacier. Four people. Their gear was found intact. There were no tracks. The official search has been suspended pending weather. The unofficial search has been suspended because there isn't one.*

*I am asking you to find them. Off the books. Cash in advance, more than you make in a season, and I suspect money is not the reason you'll say yes or no. I suspect the reason is that whoever is walking up there right now is writing in a language only you can read.*

*Come to Besisahar. Ask for Mirela Osei at the survey office. Come within ten days or don't come at all.*

*— M.O.*

Gideon read it three times. On the third reading his hands were not entirely steady, and he noted that with the detached interest of a guide assessing a client's pulse: elevated, but not yet dangerous.

Route notation. His notation. The cairn codes and bearing shorthand he'd built over fifteen years because standard survey marks told you where a path went but nothing about how it felt underfoot — nothing about the ice bridge that would hold in April and kill you in May, nothing about which scree slope sang before it slid. He had invented a grammar for mountains and taught it to exactly one person, and that person was dead, and now someone was writing sentences in it on a glacier where four people had vanished without leaving footprints.

He folded the letter along its original creases, precisely, and put it away, and did not sleep.

* * *

In the morning he led the Germans over Thorong La in wind that tried to peel them off the ridge like labels off bottles. This was the work now, and he did it well, and he noticed — as he always noticed, the observation arriving with the dull regularity of a dripping tap — that he was happy in the only way available to him. Not glad. Not content. But there was a purity in it: the mountain asked nothing about his past, only about his footing. Every day the trail said the same merciful thing: *here is the next hundred meters. Deal with them.* A man could live inside a hundred meters indefinitely. A man could live there forever, if he never looked up.

At the pass he let the Germans take their photographs and stood apart, facing north toward Manaslu, a white shoulder of it visible through the cloud gap like something watching. Eleven days ago — while he had been eating dal bhat in a lodge in Chame, while he had been telling a story about a fictional avalanche to a woman from Hamburg — four people had walked onto the Larkya Glacier and stopped existing. And someone up there was scratching his private alphabet into the ice.

*Devi,* he thought, and shut the thought the way you close a door on a cold room.

* * *

He refused the job four times in the following week, which was to say he composed refusals and destroyed them.

The first refusal was spoken aloud to the empty kitchen of a lodge in Lower Pisang, at two in the morning, in the flat voice he used for turning clients back: "No." It echoed off the tin roof and sounded, annoyingly, like a question.

The second refusal was practical. He wrote out the reasons on the back of a permit form: he could not enter restricted zones; his face was known to two border services; any official attention, however oblique, would end the fragile arrangement that let Tenzin Lama exist. He read the list twice and found it convincing and then noticed that his hand had already begun sketching, in the margin, the opening sequence of his own code — the double-notch cairn meaning *safe passage, tested today* — and he burned the list in the stove rather than finish the thought.

The third refusal was moral, and it was the one he trusted least. He owed the mountains nothing, he told himself. He had paid. He had paid with a career, a country, a name; whatever ledger had once held his account was closed, balanced, stamped. If a glacier wanted to swallow a survey team, that was between the glacier and the government that sent them. He repeated this to himself across three days of trail, in rhythm with his boots, like a mantra, and the mantra worked exactly as well as mantras ever work for men lying to themselves.

The fourth refusal was the letter itself. He took it out on the evening of the ninth day — one day left of Mirela Osei's ten — intending to burn it and be done, to reduce the whole impossible proposition to ash and heat a kettle over its dying.

He built the fire carefully, the way he built everything now: deliberately, without flourish, a man practicing being ordinary. He fed the stove splinters of pine until the flame stood up straight. He held the envelope over the mouth of it.

The paper began to brown at the corner. Heat crawled up his fingers. And in the warp of the warming paper he could see the ink beginning to shift, the small exact handwriting going soft at its edges, and he thought: *she studied the maps for four months.* Four months of someone sitting under a lamp, tracing lines that should not exist, lines written by a dead man's hand or a dead man's student, trying to decode a language whose last living speaker was guiding Germans over a pass for table scraps and forged papers.

The corner caught. Flame ran up the edge like a lit fuse.

He pulled it back out of the fire and beat it against his knee, and stood there in the smoky kitchen holding a half-burned letter, feeling like a man who has stepped off a trail he's walked a thousand times because something in the snowpack looked wrong.

That was the thing about reading mountains. You could unlearn a language. You could not unlearn the reflex. Somewhere above him, right now, the snowpack was wrong, and some part of him that predated the exile, predated the disgrace, predated even Devi — some part that was simply a man who read danger for a living — had already laced its boots.

He smoothed what remained of the letter on the counter. The fire had taken the bottom third; Mirela Osei's signature was gone, and the words *cash in advance*, and most of the polite machinery of the offer. What survived was the middle. The coordinates of the abandoned high camp. The phrase *no tracks*. The sentence he had been circling for nine days like a bird over a corpse:

*Whoever is walking up there right now is writing in a language only you can read.*

He looked at it for a long time. Outside, the wind changed direction, coming down off the glacier fields, carrying the smell of ice — that clean mineral scent that had been his first language before either Nepali or Mandarin, before English, before names.

"All right," he said aloud, to the stove, to the letter, to no one. It was not agreement. It was surrender timed to look like a decision.

He would go. Not for her, not for the money, not even — he made himself honest for exactly one breath — for the missing four. He would go because there was a sentence written on a glacier in his own dead grammar, and a man does not leave a sentence like that unfinished. A man goes to find out who is speaking.

And underneath that, deeper than he was willing to dig, in the place where he kept the thing he measured everything else against: if the hand writing those codes belonged to the person he feared and hoped it did, then Devi Thapa had been alive for three years while Gideon Zhou erased himself in penance for a death that hadn't happened.

He packed that night. Not the big pack — the small one, the one that said *day trip* to anyone who saw it, though it held everything he actually owned that mattered: the forged papers, the spare glasses, a coil of cord, the brass compass with the cracked crystal that had pointed true north on the day everything ended and had never quite seemed trustworthy since.

Before dawn he left an envelope with the lodge owner — payment for the season he was abandoning, plus a lie about a sick mother in Pokhara — and walked out of Muktinath while the stars were still hard overhead. Behind him the trail ran south, soft and familiar, nine hundred kilometers of it, a whole life measured in miles.

Ahead, north, past the pass, past the border of everything he was allowed, the Larkya Glacier lay under its ice like a page under a hand, waiting to be read.

He turned north.

It cost him nothing, he told himself, going down the switchbacks into the cold blue morning. It cost him nothing at all.

The lie lasted until the sun cleared the ridge, and then it stopped mattering, because ahead of him — twenty days' walk, if the weather held, less if it didn't — someone was writing in his language, and the mountain was already answering.
