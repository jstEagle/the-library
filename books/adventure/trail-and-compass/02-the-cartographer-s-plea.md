## 2. The Cartographer's Plea

The woman was waiting for him at the trailhead when he came down off the mountain with six Dutch trekkers and a porter who sang off-key to keep the yaks moving.

Gideon saw her from two hundred meters and knew she wasn't a tourist. Tourists fidgeted at trailheads, checked their phones, complained about the dust before they'd walked a single step of it. This woman stood the way surveyors stand — weight even on both feet, eyes doing slow arithmetic on the ridgelines, hands still. She wore a government-issue windbreaker with the insignia picked off, which fooled no one, least of all a man who had spent three years learning to read people the way he once read snow.

He considered walking past her. It would have been easy. The Dutch were busy arguing about whether the teahouse in the next village served real coffee or the instant kind, and the porter was laughing at them, and nobody would have noticed one more man in a line of men flowing down out of the Khumbu like meltwater.

But she had already seen him. He could tell by the way her stillness changed quality — not relaxed anymore but coiled, the stillness of someone who has rehearsed this moment and is terrified it will slip away.

"Gideon Zhou," she said.

The name landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well. He heard it fall a long way before it hit bottom.

"You have the wrong person," he said, in Nepali, and kept walking.

"My name is Mirela Osei." She fell into step beside him, unbothered, matching his pace with the ease of someone accustomed to difficult terrain. "I'm a cartographer with the Survey Department. And you are exactly the right person, because I've spent eleven weeks looking for you, and I don't give up on maps or on men."

The porter glanced back. Gideon gave him a small hand signal — *continue* — and the line of trekkers flowed on toward the village without them, leaving the two of them alone on the trail with the evening coming down gold and cold off the icefall.

"I guide trekkers," Gideon said. "Under permit. Under my registered name. Whoever you're looking for—"

"Registered under the name Tomas Ferreira," she said, "a Portuguese national whose passport photo shows a man with both ears intact. Yours—" she nodded at his left ear, where the frostbite had taken the top curve of cartilage years ago, leaving a smooth blunt edge that no hat fully hid, "—is missing something."

He stopped walking. Below them the valley dropped away in terraced steps, and somewhere down there the Dutch were discovering the coffee situation for themselves. Above them the peaks had gone the color of old bone.

"What do you want?" he asked.

"To show you something." She pulled a folded sheet of paper from inside her jacket — not printed, he noticed, but hand-copied, the ink slightly smeared as if it had been read many times in bad weather. "And then I want to hire you. Off the books. Cash. For a job no agency will touch and no insurance company will cover."

"Then it's a job that kills people."

"It's a job that's already killed four people." Her voice didn't waver, but something behind it did — a hairline fracture running through the professional calm. "Or lost them. We don't know which. That's rather the point."

She held out the paper. He didn't take it.

"Mr. Zhou." She used the name deliberately this time, watching what it did to his face. "I wrote you a letter. You burned it — I assume, since you never answered. So now I've walked nine days to say to your face what I couldn't put in an envelope." She took a breath, and for a moment the cartographer fell away and there was only a tired woman in her forties who had been awake too long. "Four of my people went into the Larkya valley seven weeks ago. They were mapping glacial retreat along the standard survey corridor. On the ninth day they transmitted their position one final time, and then nothing. No distress call. No debris field. Search teams went in twice and came back with nothing. The government is preparing to declare them dead and close the file." Her jaw tightened. "I am not prepared to let them do that."

"The Larkya is restricted," Gideon said. "Post-monsoon access was suspended after—"

"After the avalanche. Three years ago. Yes." She looked at him steadily. "I know exactly what happened there three years ago. I've read the report. Both versions of it."

There was only one version. There had only ever been one version. He kept his face still and said nothing, and watched her understand that he understood, and file that away.

She unfolded the paper anyway and held it up between them, turning it so the last of the light caught the ink.

"Before the transmission cut out, our team sent coordinates. Standard practice — grid references, elevation, bearing. Except these weren't grid references." She tapped the column of figures. "I've been a cartographer for nineteen years. I know every coordinate system used in this range, every datum, every military variant going back to the British surveys. These numbers don't resolve to any of them. But they're not random. Random numbers don't repeat in patterns. Random numbers don't *progress*."

The wind came up the valley, carrying the smell of dung smoke from the village below and the older, cleaner smell of ice above. Gideon looked at the paper.

He should not have looked at the paper.

The first figure was a 7. The second was a 7 preceded by a dash. The third was a dash followed by 7 followed by 3. And something in his chest — some muscle he hadn't used in three years, some instrument he'd assumed was rusted shut beyond repair — turned over and began, helplessly, to read.

Dash-seven meant *hazard, do not ascend*. Seven-three meant *third cairn past the waterline*. The sequence progressed the way a route progresses, each figure a sentence in a grammar of movement, and the grammar was his. He had built it himself, twenty-two years old and arrogant, sitting in a Kathmandu tea house with a pencil and a grudge against every existing notation system, because none of them could say in a single character *this route is safe today but will not be tomorrow*. He had refined it over a decade of guiding. He had taught it to exactly one other person, in a tent at five thousand meters, drawing characters in the condensation on the flysheet while the storm tried to peel the mountain apart.

Devi.

The recognition arrived not as thought but as weather — a pressure drop, a darkening, the animal certainty that something enormous was standing just behind him. His hands, he realized distantly, had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with altitude.

"Where did you get this," he said. His voice came out flat and strange.

Mirela Osei lowered the paper slowly, watching him the way she might watch a seismograph needle begin to move. "So you can read it."

"Where. Did you get this."

"It was the team's final transmission. Logged and timestamped, 04:17 local time, day nine of the survey." She refolded the paper with precise, deliberate movements, as if handling something that might detonate. "Our radio operator flagged it as corrupted data. Garbled telemetry. It sat in a file for five weeks until I pulled it myself because I couldn't sleep." She met his eyes. "It isn't garbled, is it. It's a language. Someone on my team — or near my team — was transmitting in a code that exactly one man in the world ever invented."

The valley below had gone blue with dusk. Somewhere a yak bell clanked its idiot, peaceful rhythm. Gideon stood very still and felt the three careful years of his exile — the borrowed name, the measured distances, the small honest work of walking foreigners up and down mountains that wanted nothing from him — tilt beneath his feet like a shelf of snow beginning to move.

"There was a second man," he said finally. "I taught it to a second man."

"Devi Lama," Mirela said quietly. "Your rope partner. Listed as deceased in the Larkya avalanche, three years ago, body never recovered." She paused. "I did my research, Mr. Zhou. I'm a cartographer. Research is what I do."

"He's dead."

"The report says so."

"The report says a lot of things."

"Yes," she said. "It does."

They stood facing each other on the trail, and the silence between them filled up with everything neither of them was saying: the falsified findings, the erased names, the report that had ended a career and buried a man who might not be buried at all. Gideon became aware that his heart was beating fast and hard, a climber's heart, the heart that used to carry him through serac zones and across crevasse fields, waking up after three years of sedation.

"Why me?" he asked. "You have search teams. Helicopters. Government resources."

"I had search teams. Two of them. They walked the standard corridor, found nothing, and came home, because they were reading the valley with official maps." She tapped her temple. "I need someone who reads it the way whoever sent that transmission reads it. If those coordinates mark a location — a camp, a cache, a refuge — then it's a location that exists nowhere on any map I have access to. Which means it exists in *your* map. In your head." Her voice dropped. "Four people, Mr. Zhou. Sunita Rai, thirty-one, mother of one. Emil Brandt, forty-six. Kencho Dorje, twenty-eight. Priya Nair, thirty-five. They had names and families and they walked into a valley and vanished, and the state is about to write *presumed deceased* over all four of them because writing anything else would be inconvenient."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I'll keep looking. I'll find someone else, eventually, or I won't, and four more names go into the ground." She shrugged, a small economical motion. "But I don't think you'll refuse."

"No?"

"No. Because when you read those numbers, your face did something." She studied him with the frank, unsentimental attention of a woman who drew coastlines for a living and knew how much could hide behind a smooth-looking edge. "People look at unfamiliar data with curiosity. You looked at it like a man hearing his own language spoken in a country that's supposed to be empty." She tucked the paper back inside her jacket. "Someone out there is speaking your dead language, Mr. Zhou. And I don't think you can walk away from that any more than I can."

* * *

That night he sat in the loft of the teahouse above the common room, listening to the Dutch argue happily about altitude sickness and card games, and turned the decision over the way he'd turn a stone in his hand, feeling its weight, its temperature, its flaws.

He owed the mountains nothing. That was the lie he'd built the exile on, and it had held well enough: *the mountains owe you nothing, you owe them nothing, walk your distances, keep your head down, be no one.* Three years of being Tomas Ferreira, a competent, forgettable guide with clean paperwork and no history. Three years of measuring life in kilometers covered instead of days endured, because distances were honest — a kilometer covered was a kilometer covered, no matter who you were when you crossed it.

But the numbers on that page hadn't been distance. They'd been time. Dash-seven, seven-three, the progression of a route laid down by someone alive, someone thinking, someone *planning* — and there was only one other mind on earth that knew that grammar, and the reports said that mind had stopped three years ago under ten thousand tons of ice.

If Devi was alive, then everything Gideon had signed was a lie he'd told to protect a ghost.

If Devi was dead, then someone else had learned the codes — and there was no innocent reason for a dead man's language to come out of a restricted valley where four people had vanished without footprints, without wreckage, without a sound.

Either way, the exile was over. He understood that with the same calm dread he felt reading a slope before crossing it: the release of the first crack, distant and small, announcing that the whole face was already moving.

Below him, the common room emptied. The lamp guttered. He took out the letter — he'd kept it, of course; he'd burned nothing but the envelope — and read it again by the last light. *Cash. Off-book. Discretion guaranteed in both directions.* A government cartographer offering discretion was a contradiction, and contradictions were how avalanches started: two loads of snow that couldn't both hold.

He thought about Sunita Rai, mother of one. He thought about a boy of twenty-two drawing symbols in tea house condensation, certain he was inventing something that would save lives. He thought about a tent at five thousand meters, and a voice saying *say it back to me*, and the storm outside like the end of the world.

In the morning, Mirela Osei was packing her bag in the courtyard when he came down the stairs and set his own pack beside hers.

"Larkya Glacier," he said. "Off-book. My terms: we move fast, we tell no one, and when it's finished, you burn every record that connects me to it."

"And if I find something worth recording?"

"Then you'll record it under someone else's name." He cinched the strap tight. "Tomas Ferreira doesn't exist. Make sure it stays that way."

She looked at him for a long moment — the look of a woman adding a feature to a map that the official charts insisted wasn't there — and then shouldered her pack.

"One condition of mine," she said. "When we find them — when, Mr. Zhou, not if — you'll tell me what really happened in that valley three years ago. All of it."

Above the eastern ridge, the first light hit the high peaks, and far to the north, invisible from here, a glacier waited with whatever it was holding.

"That depends," Gideon said, "on what we find in yours."

They started walking.
