## 3. Gear Without Tracks

The high camp sat in a bowl of snow like a held breath.

Gideon saw it first from the ridge, three hundred meters below and across the wind-scoured saddle — four dome tents in a neat arc, guy-lines taut, the kind of geometry that meant discipline or fear. Behind them, the Larkya Glacier shouldered up out of its valley like something surfacing from deep water, blue-black in its crevasses, white where the sun had scoured it. The icefall at its snout was quiet this morning. He noted that and did not trust it.

"You're sure that's it?" Mirela said beside him. She was breathing hard; she'd kept his pace for two days without complaint, which he hadn't expected from a woman who spent her life bent over drafting tables. Her lips were cracked from altitude and her eyes had the fixed brightness of someone running on grief instead of sleep.

"It's the coordinates from your last transmission." He didn't say *and it's pitched exactly the way I would have pitched it*. Not yet.

They came down into the bowl with crampons biting, and Gideon made himself do what he always did: read the ground before he read the camp. Snow tells you everything if you let it. It holds wind direction in its ripples, temperature history in its crust, traffic in its scars. This snow was five days old at least — he could date it by the sastrugi, by the way the wind had planed the surface into long gray combs running northeast, by the thin glaze of refreeze that clicked under his boot edges like broken china.

Five days of snow. And not one mark on it except their own.

He stopped ten meters out and stood very still.

"Gideon?" Mirela had come up beside him. "What—"

"Don't step past me."

She looked at him, then at the camp, then back. "There's nothing there."

"I know," he said. "That's what's wrong."

* * *

He circled the camp the long way, wide, reading every approach. From the south: unbroken. From the glacier side, where the ice rose in a low rampart of old moraine: unbroken, though the wind off the ice had drifted spindrift against the rocks in a way that would have filled any tracks within hours. He allowed for that. He allowed for a lot. That was the job — to be generous with explanations until the mountain itself refused them.

The mountain refused them.

Four tents, each staked with dead-perfect tension, doors facing away from the prevailing wind. A cook shelter strung between two trekking poles, fly angled to shed. The food cache — he found it under a cairn of flat stones behind the largest tent, wrapped in a groundsheet — held sealed ration packs, a full gas canister, chocolate gone white with bloom but sound. A water bottle stood upright beside the nearest tent door, half full, its cap on.

Nobody caps a bottle and walks away from it.

"Radios," Mirela said. She'd gone to the second tent without waiting for permission, and he didn't stop her because her hands were already shaking and stopping her would have been cruelty. She pulled open the flap. Inside, sleeping bags lay rolled — rolled, not dragged out in panic — and on top of one sat two handheld radios, antennas intact, and a satellite messenger with its screen dark.

Gideon crouched at the tent threshold and pressed the messenger's power key. It woke instantly. Battery at sixty-one percent. No messages sent since the last transmission Mirela had shown him — the one with coordinates written in a language only two people alive had ever spoken, and one of those people was supposed to be dead.

"They left," Mirela said. Her voice had gone thin. "They got a call, or they saw weather coming, and they moved down fast and—"

"In what?" Gideon asked. He gestured at the snow around the camp, a slow sweep that took in all of it, every compass point. "Downhill is that way. Two kilometers of open slope before the moraine gives you cover. You don't cross that without leaving a trench. Four people moving fast in soft new snow leaves a scar you could see from the ridge." He looked at her. "There's no scar. There's no track. There isn't even a single boot print going to take a piss behind a rock."

"Wind could have—"

"Not in five days. Not this wind." He picked up a handful of the surface snow and let it fall through his fingers. Fine, dry, unconsolidated — the kind that records everything and forgives nothing. "This isn't powder that fills in overnight. This is a page. And nobody wrote on it."

Mirela stared at the camp as if seeing it for the first time. Then, stubbornly, the cartographer in her fought back: "Maybe they went up. Onto the glacier. Ice doesn't hold prints."

"Glacier ice holds prints better than snow does. And they'd still need to cross thirty meters of this to reach it." He stood. His knees ached in the cold, an old injury speaking its litany. "Your team didn't walk out of here, Mirela. They didn't run out. They didn't get taken out — whatever took them left no drag marks, no blood, no scattered gear." He turned slowly, taking in the perfect tents, the capped bottle, the rolled bags. "This camp wasn't abandoned. It was *closed*. Like a stage set after the play's done. Everything squared away so it would look right to whoever found it."

"To us," Mirela whispered.

"To whoever came looking." He met her eyes. "Which means they knew someone would."

* * *

They searched the camp properly after that, and the searching took two hours because Gideon refused to hurry. Hurry was how people died in places like this, and also how they missed things. He worked the perimeter in expanding rings while Mirela inventoried the tents, calling out items in a voice that grew flatter with each call: expedition logbook — present, last entry mundane, a barometer reading and a note about wind. Personal effects — present. Boots — all eight pairs accounted for, lined up inside tent vestibules.

Eight pairs of boots, dry and waiting, for four people who were nowhere.

It was on the third ring, forty meters out on the glacier side, that Gideon found the thing that turned his cold fear into something hotter and more focused. A cairn. Small, knee-high, built of flat stones stacked with a precision no natural process and no casual hand achieves. He stood over it for a long moment, heart tapping against his ribs like a fist on a door.

Three stones. One offset to the left, tilted. Two level beneath.

*Offset left, tilt, two true.* Route code. His route code. The shorthand he'd invented at twenty-three on the trails above Langtang because official markers got buried or stolen and he'd needed a grammar the mountain couldn't erase. Left-tilted stone meant *hazard*, and the count beneath it meant *how far*. Two hundred meters. Hazard in two hundred meters, bearing determined by the cairn's placement relative to the line of travel.

Someone had built his language into this valley.

"Gideon!" Mirela's voice, sharp. He jogged back. She was standing at the edge of the bowl, pointing down-glacier toward a shallow depression where the wind had stripped the snow thin over older ice. "There. Is that—"

A track. One. A single boot print pressed into the refrozen crust beneath the thin snow, days old, weathered at the edges but legible. Size large. Vibram lug pattern he recognized because he'd owned the same boots for nine years.

One print, heading *away* from the camp, toward the glacier's flank. As if someone had walked out of the closed stage set in a single straight line and then simply ceased to exist.

"That's impossible too," Mirela said quietly. "One print?"

"One print," Gideon agreed. And he heard his own voice come out level, almost gentle, which was how he knew he was most afraid. "You know who leaves one print, Mirela? Someone who wants you to find exactly one. Someone who knows that a blank page frightens people less than a page with a single word on it, because a single word sends you walking in one direction while they go another." He crouched, didn't touch the print, just hovered his palm above it. "Or someone so good they only ever make mistakes on purpose."

"You keep saying *someone* like you know them."

He didn't answer. Because the truth was worse than knowing. The truth was that he knew the *kind* — he'd trained beside that kind, slept in tents with that kind, taught that kind a private alphabet of stones. Devi had been many things: careless with money, generous with everything else, brilliant on ice, incapable of leaving a camp untidy. Devi, who died three years ago in an avalanche above Manaslu, whose death had ended Gideon's career and erased his name from two countries' registries.

Devi, who had learned these codes from Gideon's own mouth, stone by stone, on a trail much like this one.

But Devi was dead. Gideon had signed the report himself. He had written, in careful bureaucratic prose, *guide Devi Thapa, deceased*, and the words had cost him everything and bought a dead man's reputation a clean grave.

And now someone was writing Devi's sentences in stones above the Larkya Glacier.

* * *

They made camp at the edge of the bowl, well clear of the abandoned tents — Gideon wouldn't sleep among gear arranged like props in a play he hadn't auditioned for. He melted snow, heated water, forced Mirela to eat. She ate mechanically, staring at the four tents across the snow, silhouetted now against a sky turning the color of a bruise.

"The survey data," she said suddenly. "Their instruments. Where are the instruments?"

Gideon had been asking himself the same question. A government survey team carried thirty kilos of equipment between them — levels, GPS units, theodolite parts, notebooks, cores. The tents held clothing and personal effects and radios. No instruments. No notebooks beyond the logbook with its bland final entry.

"So either they carried everything out," he said, "leaving no tracks, wearing no boots—"

"—or someone took it," Mirela finished. "And tidied up after."

"And wanted the camp found." He poked the coals of the stove. "Think about it from the other end. If you wanted four people to vanish cleanly, you'd sink them in a crevasse and scatter their gear. Nobody finds anything, everybody assumes avalanche, case closes as tragedy. Instead, someone builds a perfect camp. Leaves food, radios, boots. Leaves a single footprint pointing at the glacier. That's not concealment. That's *invitation*. That's a hand reaching out of the story saying: come further in."

"Then we're being led."

"We've been led since the letter." He said it plainly and watched her absorb it. "Whoever laid those codes knew I'd be the one reading them eventually. Your transmission used my notation. This cairn uses my notation. Either your survey team contained a ghost, or someone has been studying me for a long time and decided the Larkya was the place to make contact."

"Why you?"

"Because I'm the only person alive who can follow it." He looked up at the glacier, where the last light was dying along the seracs, turning the ice wall the color of an old wound. "That's not flattery, Mirela. That's a trap description. Somebody built a door that only opens for me, and then made sure four people disappeared behind it so I'd have no choice but to walk through."

Mirela was silent for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had changed — steadier, colder, the voice of a woman who had spent three weeks refusing to say aloud what she'd suspected since the first night. "My team. Anil, Petra, Dorje, and young Sunita. Are they even alive?"

Gideon thought about the capped water bottle. The rolled sleeping bags. The tidy, patient, deliberate order of it all. Chaos kills people; order keeps them. Whatever had happened in this bowl, it had happened without struggle.

"I think they might be," he said. "I think that's the point."

Above the glacier, the wind shifted, carrying down from the icefall a sound he felt in his sternum before he heard it with his ears — a deep, tectonic groan, the glacier flexing its shoulders, talking in its sleep. Or waking up.

Gideon banked the stove and checked his headlamp battery twice, an old habit, and found his eyes going again and again to the knee-high cairn at the edge of the bowl. Three stones. Offset, tilted, two true. *Hazard, two hundred meters.*

Except — and the realization arrived with the slow, sick weight of a stone settling into a wall — the bearing was wrong. He'd read it quickly before, distracted, and the reading had pointed harmlessly out onto the flats. But cairns were placed relative to the line of travel of the person who built them, and if the builder had come *up*-glacier rather than down, if the code had been laid from the other direction—

Hazard. Two hundred meters. Bearing: directly into the heart of the Larkya Glacier, into the blue-black maze of crevasses below the seracs, into territory no survey team had any reason to enter and every reason to avoid.

Someone wasn't just leaving him a message. Someone was telling him where to go next.

Across the fire, Mirela caught him staring and read his face the way she read contour lines. "What is it? What did you find?"

Gideon watched the last light die on the ice wall, and answered carefully, because some doors, once named, could not be unnamed.

"These codes weren't written by your team," he said. "And they weren't written for me to find the camp." He fed another stick to the flames. "They were written to lead me onto the glacier. Tomorrow, we follow them — and we do it exactly right, because whoever laid this trail knows the grammar better than anyone except the man who invented it." He paused, and the name rose in his throat like something swallowed years ago finally coming back up.

"And the man who invented it is supposed to be dead."
