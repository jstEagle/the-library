## 4. Asha

I had been watching the moraine for two days before I let them see me.

That was the discipline of it — the patience my brother taught me when we were children herding yaks below Sama Gaun: *the mountain gives you one look, Devi. Make it count.* He'd been talking about ibex then, but the principle held for men too. Gideon Zhou and the cartographer woman moved up the lateral moraine like two people who had not yet decided they were a team — him in front, always in front, his weight on the uphill pole the way he never did on commercial routes; her behind, checking a GPS unit she clearly didn't trust.

I sat on my rock above the ice-marginal stream with my notebook open to a page of invented calving measurements and waited for the wind to turn. When it did, carrying the smell of their cook fire down-valley, I closed the notebook and began to descend.

Not quickly. Never quickly. A glaciologist does not hurry on a moraine; she reads it. I made myself read it — the toppled slabs, the meltwater channels cutting fresh through last season's till, the small blue flowers of ice exposed where the sun had eaten the debris cover. I read it because reading was true even if everything else I was about to say was not.

He saw me before I reached the trail. Of course he did. Three years of hiding had not dulled him; it had honed him into something that registered every moving thing within a kilometer. I watched him go still, watched his hand drift — not to a weapon, he didn't carry one, but to the strap of his pack, the gesture of a man bracing to run.

*Don't run,* I thought. *You've spent three years running. It hasn't worked.*

"Namaste," I called, when the distance between us had shrunk to something civil. "You're above the seasonal line without permits. So am I. Perhaps we can be criminals together."

The cartographer laughed — a short, startled sound, the laugh of someone who hadn't slept properly in weeks. Gideon did not laugh. He studied me the way he must have once studied seracs, looking for the fracture lines.

"Asha Karki," I said, and gave him the name slowly, so he could watch me give it. "Glaciologist. Independent. I'm mapping calving behavior on the lower Larkya." I held out the notebook as evidence, its pages dense with figures I had fabricated at four thousand meters over two sleepless nights. Fabricated carefully, though. I had made myself learn the real numbers first, so the lies would sit on a foundation of truth. That was the other lesson from my brother: if you must lie, lie like a surveyor.

"Mirela Osei," the cartographer said, shaking my hand with the desperate grip of a drowning woman who has just spotted what might be a branch. "Government survey office. We're — we're looking for our team."

"I know," I said. "Four people. Last transmission eleven days ago, near the glacier's confluence with the western tributary." I paused, letting the timing land. "There has been nothing since."

Mirela's face did something complicated — relief and grief fighting for the same ground. "How do you—"

"The valley talks," I said. "Hermits hear it before officials do." I turned to Gideon then, deliberately, the way you turn a key in a lock. "And you must be the guide Mirela hired off the books. The one whose name isn't on any registry."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not fear exactly. Recognition of the shape of the trap, even if he couldn't yet see its bars.

"They call you something else now," I said. "Down in the trekking towns. But that isn't your name."

"No?" His voice was flat, careful. Low-pitched, gravelled by altitude and disuse. "What is it?"

"Gideon Zhou," I said.

I watched the name hit him. It was not surprise — he had expected this, or something like it, from the moment I opened my mouth. It was older than surprise. It was the flinch of a man hearing a language he had sworn never to speak again, spoken fluently, by a stranger, on a glacier where no stranger had any business knowing it.

"That's a dead man's name," he said quietly.

"Dead men make the best company," I said. "They don't interrupt."

* * *

We walked up-valley together because there was nowhere else for any of us to go, and because Mirela wanted my data and I wanted — I wanted what I had wanted for eleven months, through three false starts and one aborted approach from the south side that cost me two toes' worth of frostbite and most of my savings. I wanted to see whether the man walking ten meters ahead of me was worth the truth I was carrying.

He walked like Devi.

That was the thing nobody had warned me about — not the fixer in Kathmandu who found me the job, not the half-drunk bureaucrat who'd shown me the blacklisted file with the photograph of two young guides standing shoulder to shoulder beneath a banner I couldn't read in the blur of that afternoon's drinking. Everyone told me what Gideon Zhou looked like. Nobody told me he *moved* like my brother. The same economy. The same habit of testing each step before committing weight, as if the mountain owed him nothing and he intended to collect even less.

Except Devi committed. That was the difference. Devi stepped onto rotten bridges and said yes to expeditions he shouldn't have and loved people loudly and carelessly and completely. This man ahead of me tested every step twice and loved nothing visibly at all.

At the midday halt, while Mirela picked at her food and asked me questions about calving seasons that I answered correctly — I had done my homework, months of it, until I could discuss ablation rates with actual authority — Gideon sat apart on a boulder and cleaned his ice axe with a cloth he kept folded in his chest pocket. Precise strokes. The same cloth, I would have bet anything, every day, the same number of strokes.

"You were in Kathmandu," I said to him, not loudly. Just loud enough.

His hands stopped. Resumed. "Everyone's been in Kathmandu."

"Three years ago. October. The Hotel Tibet, or somewhere like it — the kind of place with a courtyard and a dog that barks at nothing." I kept my eyes on the glacier, giving him the mercy of not being watched while I did this. "You had a fever. You'd come down from something high and it had gone into your chest. You couldn't have been more than a week off the mountain."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"You sat in the courtyard for two days because you couldn't climb the stairs," I said. "The owner's daughter brought you tea with ginger in it and you told her it reminded you of someone. You wouldn't say who." My throat tightened around the next part, and I let it, because the tightness was real and real things survive scrutiny better than performed ones. "On the second night you talked in your sleep. Route numbers. Elevations. And a name."

The cloth had stopped moving entirely.

"You said the name like an apology," I went on. "Over and over. Like you were trying to pay a debt to someone who wasn't in the room."

"You're lying," he said, but his voice had lost its floor. "I've never—" He stopped himself, which was itself an answer. A man with nothing to hide says *I've never been to Kathmandu.* A man with something buried says *I've never* and then swallows the rest.

"Then it's a strange coincidence," I said, "that you flinched just now exactly the way he flinched in his sleep."

I finally turned to look at him, and what I saw frightened me more than the whole expedition had. Not anger. Not suspicion, though both were there, banked and glowing. What frightened me was hunger — the specific, terrible hunger of a man who has been alone so long that even a fabricated memory of himself looks like company.

"Who are you?" he asked.

"A glaciologist," I said, and smiled, and hated myself for how easily it came. "Studying calving seasons. The Larkya calves early some years. This will be one of them. You'll want to be clear of the ice wall before it opens."

"That's backwards," Mirela said from across the camp, sharp with professional reflex. "Calving means collapse. You want to be clear *during*, not before."

"Not on this glacier," I said, and gave them the explanation I had prepared, the one built on real mechanics — the annual cycle of the terminal wall, the way the valley behind it drained and firmed and briefly became passable in the window between major calving events, the route that appeared on no map because no government surveyor had ever bothered to stay long enough to see it. Every word true. Every word pointing, if you knew how to read it, toward the place where I believed my brother had been living for three years.

Gideon listened the way he did everything — completely, silently, storing it. But when I finished he said, "Why tell us? If the route's secret, why hand it to strangers?"

"Because you're going up there anyway," I said. "And dead guides help no one's research."

It was the right answer because it was a cold answer, and cold answers are believable. What I could not give him was the warm one — *because my brother is up there, because you are the only person alive who might recognize the way he thinks, because I have spent eleven months learning to be a glaciologist so that I could stand on this moraine and lie to your face with accurate data* — and watching him accept the cold version, I understood for the first time the full weight of what Devi had described to me once, drunk, laughing, the night before he left for what should have been a routine commercial crossing:

*Yara, that man trusts nothing but the mountain. If you ever need to reach him, become the mountain.*

* * *

That evening the weather turned, as I had known it would — the pressure dropping all afternoon, the clouds stacking up over the peaks in the particular bruised architecture that meant the monsoon was arriving weeks early. I sat outside my tent after the others had turned in and watched the light die on the icefall, and I took out the thing I had carried for eleven months and almost never allowed myself to touch: a photograph, soft-cornered from handling, of two guides grinning under a banner. Devi on the left, arm slung around a narrower, younger man whose smile was already halfway to being a defense.

In the photograph, Gideon Zhou's eyes were on my brother, not the camera. Even then. Even before whatever happened on the glacier, before the report, before the blacklist, before the erasure — even then, he had looked at Devi the way people look at fixed points they navigate by.

I had hated him for that file. Let me be honest, here in the dark where honesty is cheap: when the fixer first showed me the name, I had hated him. *The guide who lied,* the file said. *Signed the report that cleared conditions and buried responsibility.* My brother was dead because of this man's pen — that was what I had believed, climbing toward the Larkya with frostbitten toes and a forged research permit, and hate had kept me warm through more than one bad night.

But hate requires a story, and stories require evidence, and somewhere on the approach, watching this man test every step, I had begun to suspect that the story in the file was written by the same people who wrote all the other files — the ones that made disasters disappear and inconvenient men disappear with them. The survey team missing above us. The gear left intact. The footprints that weren't there. I had heard Mirela describe it on the walk, in fragments, when she thought I wasn't listening, and every fragment had sounded wrong in a way I recognized. Institutional wrong. The wrongness of a story edited by committee.

So now I sat with the photograph in my lap and the storm coming down the valley, and I admitted the thing I had been refusing to admit since the moraine:

I didn't know which of them had lied. I didn't know if either had. I only knew that one of them was dead to the world and the other was dead to himself, and that I had walked into the middle of it carrying a name I wasn't sure I had the right to use.

Asha Karki. It was my mother's name. I had taken it the way you take a coat from a lost-and-found — because it fit well enough, because no one would question it, because the woman who owned it had been gone thirty years and would not have minded. But wearing a dead woman's name had rules, and the first rule was the one Devi taught me: *if you must lie, lie like a surveyor.* Establish your benchmarks. Fix your position. Because sooner or later someone walks up to your fiction with instruments, and the only thing that saves you is having built it on ground that's actually there.

Gideon was going to walk up to my fiction with instruments. I could see it already, in the way he'd watched me all afternoon — not suspiciously, worse, *carefully*, filing away my gestures, my vocabulary, the altitude I breathed at without effort. He was a reader of terrain and I had made myself terrain, and terrain gets read.

The wind rose. Somewhere up-valley, deep in the ice, something boomed — a single tectonic note, the glacier clearing its throat, the sound I had crossed a country to hear and now heard with dread instead of triumph. Calving season, opening early. The window coming. Whatever was hidden back there in the valley behind the ice wall — my brother, or his grave, or the truth about which of these two men had signed what and why — the mountain was about to open the door.

I put the photograph away and checked my boat cache coordinates one more time, though I knew them the way I knew my own heartbeat, and I lay down in the dark and listened to the ice talk.

Tomorrow, Gideon would start asking his quiet questions. Tomorrow, Mirela would decode something she shouldn't. Tomorrow, the river would rise.

Tonight, I let myself remember my brother's face in the photograph, and I made myself a promise in his name, the borrowed one, the dead woman's son's name:

*I will get one of you out of this valley. Whichever one the mountain leaves.*
