## 5. Route Codes

The first cairn appeared where no cairn should be.

Gideon stopped so abruptly that Mirela walked three steps past him before she noticed and turned back, her breath ragged in the thin air above four thousand meters. The valley narrowed here into a corridor of grey scree between two shoulders of dark rock, and the morning light came down it at a slant, gilding nothing, warming nothing.

"What is it?" she asked.

He didn't answer right away. He was looking at the pile of stones — seven of them, stacked with the flat ones outward, the largest stone seated on top like a capstone — and feeling something move through his chest that he hadn't felt in three years. Not fear exactly. Something older than fear. Recognition.

"That's not a trail marker," he said finally. "Local herders build their cairns differently. Taller. Sloppier. They stack for luck, not information." He crouched beside it without touching it. "This one's built to be read."

"Read how?"

He didn't answer that either. He stood, scanned the slope ahead, and found what he knew he would find: another cairn, forty meters up and to the left, five stones this time, the topmost one tilted toward the rock wall on the valley's north side. And beyond it, faint but certain, a third.

A line of them, climbing the valley like stitches closing a wound.

* * *

They had left the abandoned high camp at first light, moving fast over ground that still made Gideon's skin crawl. The tents pitched with military precision. The food cached under stones against scavengers. The radios charged and functional, sitting in their cases as if their owners had stepped out to relieve themselves and simply never come back. And the snow — days old, wind-scoured, unbroken. No footprints going in. None coming out. Four trained surveyors did not evaporate.

Mirela had barely slept. He could see it in the bruised skin under her eyes, in the way her hands shook when she handled her maps — hands that were otherwise careful, a cartographer's hands, precise even when exhausted. She had hired him off the books, paid half up front in cash wrapped in plastic, and asked no questions about why a guide with his skills was working under a name that didn't belong to him. He'd decided that either meant she was desperate or she already knew. With government people, he'd learned, it was usually both.

Now she watched him study the second cairn, and he saw the question building behind her eyes before she spoke it.

"You know these," she said. It wasn't quite an accusation. "You recognized the first one before I even saw it."

"I recognize the style."

"Whose style?"

He straightened up. Above them the valley bent westward, following the glacial meltwater toward the Larkya's lower icefall, and the cairns went with it — patient, deliberate, laid by someone who had all the time in the world and wanted to be followed by exactly one kind of person.

"Mine," he said.

* * *

He told her as they walked, because walking made it easier. Some truths you can only say with your body in motion, your eyes fixed on the next step, so the words can slip out sideways instead of head-on.

"Three years ago I worked the Manaslu region. High-altitude guiding, commercial expeditions, some survey support. My partner — my rope partner, my business partner, my friend since we were teenagers carrying loads for other people's dreams — his name was Devi Rai." Saying the name aloud cost him something. He heard it in his own voice, a slight catch on the second syllable, and hated himself for it. "Devi was the best mountain man I ever met. Better than me. Faster, stronger, more patient with clients, more willing to sit through a storm without complaining. But he couldn't read terrain the way I could. So I invented a system."

"A system."

"For marking routes. Cairns, scratches, stone arrangements — a code. How many stones tells you what kind of information. Which way the top stone tilts tells you direction or hazard. Flat stones facing out means safe passage. A single stone set apart from the stack means turn around." He nodded at the cairn they were approaching. "That one has six stones with the cap tilted east. In my system, that means: route continues, but water crossing ahead, unstable footing."

Mirela stopped walking. "You're telling me you can read instructions off a pile of rocks."

"I'm telling you I spent eleven years teaching that system to exactly one person, because it was never meant for anyone else. It was ours. A private language for two men who spent too much of their lives above the treeline." He looked up the valley, at the line of cairns stitching away into the distance, each one placed with the economy of long practice. "And now somebody is writing sentences in it."

"Devi," Mirela said quietly. "Your partner. The one who died."

"The one who died," Gideon repeated. The words tasted like snowmelt and ash.

* * *

They climbed. The valley floor gave way to lateral moraine, and the moraine to a ramp of frozen debris along the glacier's edge, and always the cairns led them — sometimes close together, almost conversational, sometimes separated by hours of hard travel, as if the hand that built them understood exactly how far a tired man could walk before he needed to hear a voice again.

By early afternoon they had gained eight hundred meters, and the Larkya Glacier opened below them like a river deciding to become a sea. It was vast and white and blue-veined, cracked into seracs and crevasses, running down from the high col between peaks whose names Gideon refused to say aloud. The air had gone thin and clean and merciless. Every breath was a small transaction.

They stopped in the lee of a boulder the size of a house, and Mirela dug out tea and flatbread, and Gideon sat with his back against stone and stared down at the ice and felt the past settle onto his shoulders like a loaded pack.

"Tell me about the avalanche," Mirela said. She said it gently, but she said it. She was a cartographer; she drew lines between points, and she had drawn one between the cairns and the disaster and the man sitting in front of her with a borrowed name.

"There's not much to tell that isn't in the report."

"I read the report. It says conditions deteriorated unexpectedly, a cornice released above the standard route, and two guides and five clients were caught in the slide. One guide survived." She looked at him. "It also says the surviving guide's account contained inconsistencies, and that his certification was subsequently revoked across two jurisdictions. That's the part that ended your life, isn't it? Not the avalanche. The paperwork after it."

Gideon laughed — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it. "You've done your homework."

"It's what I do. Maps are just homework about the earth." She poured tea into his cup without asking. "So the inconsistencies. Were they true?"

For a long moment he watched the light move on the glacier. Somewhere deep in the ice, a serac groaned — a sound like a whale dying, like a door opening in a house where no one should be home.

"The inconsistencies were mine," he said. "I put them there on purpose."

* * *

He told it in order, because disorder would have been mercy, and he wasn't giving himself any.

"We had a commercial group. Seven clients, moderate experience, booked for the standard circuit. Weather window looked good for four days. Devi and I ran the route check together, and I flagged the upper section — the traverse below the cornice field. Warm spell that week. I told Devi we should take the low variant, add a day, lose the view. He argued." Gideon's hands closed around the tin cup until the metal bit his palms. "Devi loved that traverse. He'd run it twenty times. He said I was being conservative, that the cornice had held every season for a decade, that clients paid for the view and we owed it to them. And I let him talk me into it. That's the first sin. Not the route. The yes."

"And then?"

"Then day three, we were on the traverse, and I saw it. The cornice had grown a lip overnight — new snow loading on the windward side, a fracture line you could see if you knew where to look. I called a halt. Told everyone to hold at the anchor point while Devi and I assessed. And Devi—" His voice did the thing again, the catch, and this time he let it happen. "Devi looked at that cornice and told me I was seeing ghosts. Said the lip was shadow, not snow. Said turning back with clients watching would end us commercially. And I believed him. God help me, I wanted to believe him, because he was Devi, and Devi was never wrong about ice."

"But he was wrong."

"He was wrong." The words came out flat as slate. "Cornice released at 11:40 in the morning. Took the traverse with it. Five clients. Two porters. Devi went into the debris field and I—" He stopped. Started again. "I dug for nine hours. Found three bodies. Never found him. They declared him dead when the search called it off on day two, and I kept digging alone for another day because I couldn't make my hands understand that it was over."

Mirela said nothing. She was good at that — a listener who let silence do its work.

"When the inquiry came, they interviewed me for six hours. And I realized, somewhere in hour four, that if I told the truth — that Devi overrode my halt, that he dismissed the hazard, that he led the group forward against my explicit call — then everything those families believed about the man they'd trusted with their husbands and wives and sons would rot. He'd go from beloved guide killed by the mountain to reckless guide who killed seven people. His mother would live the rest of her life knowing her son died a murderer." He turned the cup in his hands. "So I fudged it. I described the cornice as having shown no visible warning signs. I said the decision to proceed had been joint and unanimous. I built the report so the mountain took the blame and Devi's name stayed clean."

"And the inconsistencies they flagged?"

"They flagged that I'd reported no warning signs, but my own field notes — which they seized — recorded the fracture line, the loading, the halt call. Timestamped. In my handwriting." He smiled without warmth. "I was too honest in my notes and too loyal in my testimony, and the gap between them destroyed me. They revoked my certification. Then the second country matched the first, because governments talk to each other faster than they talk to anyone else. And that was that. Three years of walking trekkers up hills under other men's names, measuring my life in kilometers because years were unbearable."

The wind moved down the glacier and carried the smell of ice — that mineral coldness, older than any grief.

"So you protected a dead man's reputation," Mirela said slowly, "with a lie. And the lie cost you everything."

"Yes."

"And now his route codes are leading us up a valley where four people vanished without leaving footprints."

"Yes."

She set down her cup. "Gideon. What are the odds that whoever built those cairns learned the system from someone other than your partner?"

He had been doing that arithmetic since dawn, and the answer kept coming out the same, and he kept refusing to carry it.

"Zero," he said. "The system existed in two heads. One of them is buried under the Larkya debris field." He stood, brushing frost and grit from his trousers, and pointed up-valley, where the afternoon light was failing and the next cairn waited on a shelf of rock — seven stones, flat faces outward, the capstone level and true. Safe passage. Come on. "Which means either I'm wrong about what happened three years ago—"

He didn't finish it. He didn't have to. They both stood looking at the sentence he'd left hanging, at the shape of the only alternative: that the dead man's second head was still attached to a living body, and that body had spent three years in this valley laying stones for a follower it must have known, on some level, would eventually come.

"Or," Mirela said softly, "your partner never needed you to dig him out of the avalanche. Because he was never in it."

Gideon picked up his pack. The straps settled onto his shoulders with the familiar weight, and beneath it, beneath everything, the old guilt shifted and reconfigured itself into something worse — because if Devi had walked away from that debris field alive, then every hour Gideon had spent digging had been a fool's labor, and every year of exile had been payment on a debt that was never owed, and the friend he had ruined himself to protect had let him do it. Had watched him do it. From somewhere.

"Let's move," he said. "We're losing light, and I want to read the next marker before dark."

They climbed. And at the shelf of rock, as he reached the cairn and knelt beside it, Gideon saw what he had missed from below — a small stone, the size of a fist, set apart from the stack by half a meter. A single stone, alone.

In his system, in the private language of two men, it had only ever meant one thing.

*I'm sorry.*

He knelt there in the failing light with his hand hovering over the stone, not touching it, and behind him Mirela asked what it meant, and he found that for the first time in three years, he didn't trust himself to speak.
