## 6. Monsoon Breaks

The storm did not come the way the forecasts promised. It came the way storms come in my family's stories — early, angry, and personal.

I felt it before I saw it: a pressure change in my ears, a taste of iron on the wind, the yaks two valleys over going silent all at once. Then the light turned the color of old bruises and the first drops hit the scree like thrown gravel, fat and warm, smelling of the Terai, of rivers a hundred kilometers away that had already begun to die their way downhill toward us.

"Off the moraine," Gideon said. Not shouted — he never shouted. His voice simply arrived at a volume that made argument impossible, the way it must have sounded to a hundred clients on a hundred ridgelines when the sky turned against them. "Now. Both of you."

Mirela was already moving, clutching her map case to her chest like an infant. I let them get ten meters ahead before I followed, because I needed those ten meters of distance to look at his back without him knowing.

Three days on the trail together, and I had learned the geography of him: the slight hitch in his left stride where an old injury had healed crooked; the way he checked the sky every ninety seconds without seeming to; the habit of touching the brass compass in his chest pocket before any decision, as if asking permission of something dead. In Kathmandu — in the night I remembered and he did not — he had done all of these things. The fever had burned through him for six hours while I sat on the edge of a rented bed with a wet cloth and a job I hadn't yet decided whether to finish. He had talked in that fever. That was how I knew about the compass. That was how I knew about Devi.

That was how I knew he had promised me something he could not remember promising.

The rain found its rhythm then, the true monsoon rhythm, not rain but a standing wall of water with weather inside it. We dropped off the moraine into the valley floor, and the sound reached us before the sight did: a low, continuous roar, like a train that never passes.

The river had eaten the trail.

Where yesterday there had been a braided, shallow thing you crossed ankle-deep on gray stones, there was now a single brown muscle of water thirty meters wide, hauling whole trees sideways, tearing at the cut bank with the patience of something that had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

Mirela stood at the lip of the destroyed crossing and said, very quietly, "No."

"No," Gideon agreed. He crouched, watched the current take another tree, timed its speed against a boulder. I watched him do the arithmetic behind his eyes. "Six, maybe seven meters per second. You'd be at the next gorge mouth in four minutes. Underwater for most of it." He stood. "We go up-valley, find higher ground, wait it out. Twelve hours, maybe eighteen if it's a big system."

"It's a big system," I said.

They both looked at me.

"I've watched these rivers for eleven years," I said. It was even true. "This one is fed by three tributaries and a glacier face that's calving early. It won't drop in twelve hours. It won't drop in two days. And everything above the high-water line here gets flooded too when the valley narrows at the bend — see the silt line on the birches? Four meters up. This whole shelf goes under."

Gideon studied the birches. Then me. I held still under the look, the way you hold still when a searchlight crosses your position. He was doing what he always did with new information: not doubting it, exactly, but weighing who was handing it to him and why.

"You know this valley," he said.

"I know rivers," I said. "Rivers are honest. They go where they have to go. People are the ones who lie about direction."

Something moved through his face — not quite a flinch, but the shadow of one. Mirela, hunched under her poncho, glanced between us with the expression of a woman cataloguing evidence she didn't yet understand.

"So we can't cross," Mirela said. "And we can't stay. And up-valley is—" She stopped. Up-valley was the glacier. Up-valley was the codes, the cairns, the thing none of us had named aloud since the cave. "Up-valley is worse."

"Downstream, then," Gideon said slowly. "Past the gorge. There's a lateral valley at the confluence—"

"The confluence is restricted territory," I said.

He went very still. Rain ran off the brim of his hood and hung there, dripping.

"You've seen the maps," I said. "You knew it before I said it. Everything below the gorge, both banks, fenced off since the survey contract started. Your name isn't just blacklisted, Gideon. Your *face* is. A checkpoint officer with a laminated sheet would know you from across a room."

"And you know this how?" Mirela asked. Her voice wasn't hostile. It was worse than hostile — it was precise. She was a cartographer; she collected coordinates of suspicion the way other people collected stamps.

"Because I read permits the way you read contour lines," I said. "It's my job to know which ground is closed and why."

It was a good answer. It was even mostly true. I watched it land, watched Gideon accept it the way you accept a rope from someone on a cliff — not because you trust them, but because the alternative is falling, and falling has a schedule of its own.

* * *

We walked downstream along the high bank for two hours, the river growing beside us like a rumor becoming a fact. The rain eased into a steady drumming, and under its cover Gideon and I fell into step together, Mirela trailing twenty meters back with her head down, guarding her privacy or ours.

"There's a boat," I said.

He didn't break stride. "There's no boat. We're four days' walk from any settlement with a hull bigger than a butter tub."

"There's a boat," I repeated, "cached at the alder stand past the bend. Fiberglass, seven meters, patched twice. It leaks. Slowly, if you bail; fast, if you panic."

Now he stopped. He turned and looked at me fully, and for the first time since the moraine I saw not the guide, not the erased man, but whatever was underneath both — something tired and sharp and older than his years.

"Asha." My name in his mouth sounded like a question he'd been avoiding. "Who cached a boat in a restricted valley?"

"A glaciologist studying calving seasons," I said. "Who needed to move equipment during flood season, when the trails drown and the river becomes the only road. Who filed the paperwork, such as it was, and accepted that some paperwork lives in gray places." I shrugged. "The same kind of person who invents a route code language, maybe. People who work in gaps."

"That boat is yours."

"That boat is mine."

"And it runs downriver. Into territory I cannot legally enter. Where a checkpoint sits at the lower bridge with my description pinned to a board." He laughed — one dry note, no humor in it, the laugh of a man reading his own obituary in fine print. "You're proposing I solve the problem of being trapped by making myself easier to find."

"I'm proposing," I said, "that the mountain has closed every door except one, and the one door happens to open onto the place you're most afraid of. I don't think that's an accident. I think this valley has been steering us for three days, and I think you know it too, because you've been following cairns laid by a man who's supposed to be dead, and dead men don't stack stones."

The rain filled the silence between us. Downriver, the gorge roared.

"If we take the boat," he said at last, "and we're caught—"

"*When*," Mirela called from behind us. She'd come close enough to hear. Her hood was back now, rain flattening her hair, and her jaw was set in a way I recognized: the look of a woman who had lost four people and refused to lose the argument too. "Let's say *when*. Let's stop pretending there's a version of this where we drift politely past a military checkpoint and everyone waves. If we're caught, I'm a government cartographer with a missing team and a legitimate search mandate — I'll be questioned and released. Asha has papers, apparently, papers that shouldn't exist, which raises questions I'll be asking later." Her eyes flicked to me, then back to him. "And you're a man who officially does not exist, caught illegally inside a restricted zone connected to the disappearance of a government survey team. What happens to you?"

"Nothing good," Gideon said. "But brief."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't a joke. It's a risk assessment." He rubbed rain off his face with the heel of his hand, and I saw the exhaustion underneath, three years of it, the specific fatigue of a man who has been careful so long that carelessness feels like a foreign country. "Every choice costs something. That's the only rule I've trusted since—" He stopped himself. Since the avalanche. Since the report. Since the name he'd buried.

"Then cost it," I said quietly. "You told me once — you may not remember telling me — that the worst decisions are the ones you make to stay invisible. That invisibility feels like safety but it's just danger with the volume turned down."

His head came around sharply. "What did you say?"

"You heard me."

"Where did you hear that?"

"Kathmandu," I said, and let the word sit there between us, heavy as the rain. "A night you don't remember. I've stopped pretending otherwise, and so should you. You talk when you burn. You said a lot of things. Most of them were about a man named Devi. One of them was about who you used to be before you decided to be no one." I held his stare. "I'm not your enemy, Gideon Zhou. I've had three days and a hundred chances to prove otherwise, and I haven't taken one. Take the boat. Bail the water. Trust me the way you trust the river — not because it's kind, but because it's the only thing moving in the right direction."

* * *

The alder stand appeared out of the rain like a held breath — a dark green thicket in the lee of the bend, and beneath it, under a tarp I'd tied down myself fourteen months ago, the boat.

Gideon circled it once without touching it, the way he'd circle a suspect snow slope. He peeled back the tarp, ran his thumb along the fiberglass patch on the port side, tested the bail bucket, the spare paddle lashed under the thwart. He inspected my work the way he inspected everything: thoroughly, silently, with the assumption that anything could kill you if you loved it carelessly enough.

"It leaks," he said.

"From two seams. I've crossed in worse."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be accurate." I pulled the tarp free and folded it. "Accuracy is the only comfort I deal in."

Mirela lowered herself over the gunwale and settled among the gear bags with her map case in her lap, and I understood suddenly that she had already decided — had probably decided back at the drowned crossing, or maybe the day she'd hired a ghost to find her people. Desperation looks, from the inside, like clarity.

Gideon stood at the bow with one foot on the bank and one in the boat, a man straddling two countries of his own life. The river pulled at the hull, impatient. Somewhere upstream, the glacier groaned — a long tectonic complaint, ice letting go of ice, the sound I had come all this way to hear and had heard too often lately, each calving closer together than the last.

"For the record," he said, "if they catch me, I'll tell them you forced me aboard."

"For the record," I said, pushing off, "they won't believe you. Everyone knows guides make their own choices."

The current took us the way current takes everything — instantly, totally, without negotiation. The banks began to slide. The bend swallowed us, and beyond the bend the fog was already rising off the warmer water, thickening, laying itself across the river like a sheet drawn over furniture in a house where no one is supposed to be home.

Ahead, invisible but known to all three of us, the checkpoint waited at the lower bridge.

Gideon took the stern paddle without being asked and began, quietly, to read the water.
