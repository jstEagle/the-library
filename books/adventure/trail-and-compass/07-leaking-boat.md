## 7. Leaking Boat

The river took us at dusk and did not introduce itself politely.

One moment the boat was sliding through brown water the width of a road; the next, the banks peeled away and we were on something the width of a cathedral, carrying the monsoon's first week down from the high valleys in a single brown muscle. Asha sat in the bow like she'd been born there, one hand on the tiller line, the other bailing with a dented aluminum pot. Mirela sat amidships with the survey data wrapped in plastic against her chest, and I sat where the leak was.

Because there was a leak. Of course there was a leak. Asha had called it *seeping* when she'd unearthed the boat from its cache of juniper branches and river stones, and I had been in these mountains long enough to know that *seeping* was what liars called leaks.

"Stitch above the second plank, port side," she'd said, reading my face. "It holds. It held three seasons."

"Three seasons of what?"

"Waiting." She'd smiled, and it had been a good smile, the kind that makes you forget to ask the next question, and I'd hated myself for almost forgetting.

Now the water came in faster than the pot could chase it, and I bailed by the light of a candle stub wedged in a jam jar, and I watched Asha's back and thought about Kathmandu.

* * *

She had told the story on the moraine, the first night, when she'd said my name — my old name, the one that had died in an avalanche report — and I had stood very still and let her talk because a man who has made himself invisible learns that the most dangerous thing in the world is someone who claims to have seen you.

"October," she'd said. "The year of the bad fuel shortages. There was a teahouse in Thamel with a broken neon sign, the one shaped like a Buddha's head, half of it dark so the Buddha looked like he was winking. You were there with a man who laughed too loudly. You drank tea, not beer, even though everyone else drank beer. You kept your back to the wall and your boots on. And at some point in the night you got up and straightened the picture frames on the wall. All of them. You thought no one noticed."

I hadn't remembered any of it. That was the first problem. The second problem was that every detail was true to the shape of me — the tea, the wall, the boots, the frames — true the way a forged passport is true, with the right weight and the right creases.

Tonight, on the water, with the leak under my hand and the candle guttering, I decided to test the forgery.

"The teahouse," I said, not looking up from the bailing. "What did the owner look like?"

Asha's rhythm with the pot didn't break. "Old man, one eye clouded. He kept a radio on the counter tuned to Indian film music and he'd slap it when the reception wandered."

"Wrong," I said. "There was no owner like that. I never went to a teahouse in Thamel with a broken neon Buddha."

The river filled the silence for a moment. Mirela's head came up from her plastic-wrapped bundle like a bird dog's.

"You said you didn't remember that night," Mirela said carefully.

"I don't." I wrung out the pot. "But I know I wasn't there, because I know where I was that October. I was in Manang. Whole month. There was a British woman with a broken ankle and no insurance, and I carried her down to the road on a litter made of trekking poles and pack straps, and it took nine days, and I remember every one of them because my shoulders still do." I looked up at Asha. "So either you're remembering a different night, or you're remembering a story someone told you about me. Which is it?"

Asha bailed. The pot went down into the black water and came up silver in the candlelight, and she poured it over the side with the unhurried economy of someone who had done ten thousand things ten thousand times.

"Maybe I'm wrong about the year," she said. "Monsoons all look alike after a while."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

Mirela waited until Asha went forward to check the tiller line before she moved aft, close enough that the candle threw our two shadows together into one hunched shape on the wet planks.

"Gideon." She said my borrowed name — *Karma*, the name on the forged trekking permit sewn into my jacket — and then corrected herself, quietly, the way she'd started doing two days ago, as if the real name were something fragile she'd been handed and didn't want to drop. "Gideon. I need to say something, and you're going to want to argue, and I need you to not argue until I'm finished."

I bailed.

"Think about it the way I've had to think about it," she said. "A survey team of four vanishes on the Larkya. No footprints. Gear intact. Radios functional. Then, three days later, a woman appears on the moraine — alone, unannounced, in a season when no glaciologist in her right mind is up there — and she knows things about you that you don't know about yourself. She has a boat cached downstream of a valley that is supposed to be closed. She has papers — I've seen them, Gideon, I've *held* them — and those papers are good. Government good. The kind of good you don't get from a printer in Besisahar." Her voice dropped. "She talked us through the checkpoint fog like she'd written the regulations. Because maybe she did."

"Say it," I said.

"She's surveillance. Or she's the reason your survey team is missing. Or both. Someone sent her to find out what you'd do if someone started speaking your dead language. Someone is running you, Gideon, the way you'd run a line down a glacier — and she's the rope."

I set the pot down. The water crept toward my boot.

"And the alternative?" I said. "That she's a glaciologist with a leaky boat and a remarkable memory?"

"The alternative is that everything about her is true and nothing about her adds up, and in my experience—" Mirela stopped. In the candlelight her face looked ten years older than it had in Kathmandu, when she'd come to find me with a folded transmission and a desperation she hadn't tried to hide. "In my experience, when nothing adds up, it's because someone is subtracting."

I looked forward. Asha was a silhouette against the last grey of the sky, hands on the tiller, head tipped slightly as if listening to the river the way you'd listen to a room for footsteps.

"Here's what I know," I said. "She bailed this boat for six hours without being asked. She took the bow seat, which is the seat that hits the waves first. She knew the stitch in the plank, the cache under the juniper, the fog window at the checkpoint. And she knew about the picture frames." I picked the pot back up. "You don't learn a man's habits from a file, Mirela. Files tell you where he's been. They don't tell you that he can't stand a crooked picture. Somebody *watched* him. For a long time. Close enough to love him or close enough to bury him, and I genuinely cannot tell you which, and that is the part that keeps my hands cold."

Mirela was quiet for a while. The river talked to itself in the dark, a low continuous argument.

"So what do we do?" she asked.

"We keep her close, and we keep counting," I said. "And we don't sleep at the same time."

* * *

The night divided itself into watches the way the river divided itself around stones. Asha took the tiller until the moon rose — a thin, scoured moon, no help at all — then Mirela, then me. I didn't sleep during my off-watch. I sat in the bilge with my hand on the wet planks, feeling the boat's pulse through the wood: the knock of the current, the tick of the stitch working loose, the small patient voice of the river saying *I have time, I have all the time.*

Somewhere past midnight I tested her again.

"The man who laughed too loudly," I said, into the dark. "At the teahouse that never existed. What was his name?"

Silence from the bow. Then, evenly: "You tell me."

"That's not how remembering works."

"Isn't it?" She turned her head, and even in the dark I could feel her looking at me. "You've spent three years not remembering. Maybe it works however you need it to work."

I let that sit. The pot went down, came up, poured. Down, up, poured. The leak was winning by a teaspoon at a time, the way leaks do.

"Devi," I said finally. "You mean Devi."

"I didn't say a name."

"You were going to."

"Was I?" She let the tiller line run through her fingers, a soft hiss of wet rope. "Tell me about him, then. Since we're remembering. What was he like, this man who laughed too loudly?"

And that was the trap, and I walked into it with my eyes open, because I wanted to talk about Devi the way a wound wants air. "He was the best climber I ever roped to," I said. "Faster than me, stronger in thin air, funnier at altitude, which is a rare and valuable thing because at altitude nobody is funny. He read terrain like other people read weather. He taught—" I stopped.

"He taught you the codes," Asha said.

"He *learned* the codes. I invented them. There's a difference." The correction came out sharper than I intended, and I heard it land in the dark between us like a stone dropped down a crevasse, falling and falling and never quite hitting bottom. "Everyone gets that wrong. Everyone always got that wrong. They assumed the student was the source, because the student was louder."

"Is that what happened?" Asha asked. "Is that why the report reads the way it reads?"

I stopped bailing. The candle flame stood perfectly still in the windless dark of the boat, and I understood, in that moment, that she knew about the report. Not the public one — the one that had erased me, the one any journalist or bureaucrat could pull from a registry. She knew the *shape* of it, the way you know the shape of a thing you've held.

"Who told you about the report?" I asked.

The river answered first. Then, after a long moment, Asha did, and her voice had changed — quieter, stripped of the moraine's easy confidence, down to something rawer.

"You did," she said. "That October. The teahouse with the winking Buddha. You'd been drinking — not much, you never drank much, but you'd been drinking, and you were feverish, and you told me everything. The avalanche. Devi. The signature. You told me you'd signed your own name into a grave so a dead man could stay a hero." Her hands stilled on the tiller line. "And then you made me promise something, and you cried, and I have thought about that promise every single day for three years, Gideon Zhou, and you don't remember a word of it, and I don't know whether that's a mercy or the cruelest thing that's ever been done to me."

The candle guttered and steadied. My hands, in the bilge water, had gone numb, or I had stopped noticing them.

"I told you," I said slowly. "I wasn't in Kathmandu that October. I was in Manang. There was a woman with a broken ankle."

"I know," Asha said. "I've heard you say it twice now. And I've stopped trying to work out which of us is lying, because I've watched you, and you don't lie about small things, and I don't lie about big ones. So either one of us has lost a month—" she turned back to the river ahead, "—or someone took it."

* * *

I didn't sleep after that. There didn't seem to be a point.

I sat in the bilge and bailed, and I turned the night over and over like a stone with something fossilized inside it. *Someone took it.* Fever does strange things; I'd seen men lose days to it, whole valleys of time. But I'd been in Manang. I could feel the litter poles in my shoulders. Could a man be in two places in one October? Could a man be given a month and then have it handed back with the furniture rearranged?

Ahead, the river bent, and around the bend the water changed its voice — deeper, faster, the note of a channel narrowing. Asha's head came up. Mirela woke without being touched, the way you learn to when the mountains are your office.

"What is it?" Mirela asked.

"Rapids," Asha said. "Grade three, maybe four in this water. And past them—" she pointed with her chin at a darkness ahead that was darker than the rest, a wall of black where the valley pinched shut, "—past them is the restricted zone. The checkpoint is behind us. There is no other checkpoint. From that bend on, every meter of this river is a crime for one of us."

"For me," I said.

"For you," she agreed. She looked back at me then, and the candle caught the side of her face, and I saw something I hadn't seen on the moraine or in the fog at the checkpoint: she was afraid. Not of the rapids. "There's a landing on the left, fifty meters before the narrows. You can put us ashore and walk back and none of this ever happened. Or you can stay in the boat."

The water under us quickened, the way water does when it scents the drop. The leak ticked. Somewhere upstream, thunder — or ice, at this altitude it was impossible to say — rolled down the valley and the river answered it.

"Which way does the boat go if I'm not in it?" I asked.

Asha's silence was its own kind of coordinates. Then she said, so quietly that Mirela had to lean in to hear it over the water:

"Toward the thing your codes are marking. Toward whatever your dead friend didn't want anyone to find."

The bow swung toward the narrows, and the candle finally went out.
