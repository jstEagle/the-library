## 8. Restricted Water

The fog came off the river like something alive, the way fog does in the hills before a storm — not drifting but *arriving*, shouldering through the gorge, swallowing the far bank whole. Asha stood at the bow of the leaking boat with her hand on the painter rope and watched the checkpoint lantern resolve out of the white: one orange smear, then two, then the dark rectangle of the guard hut beneath them.

*Papers that shouldn't exist,* she thought, and touched the oilskin wallet inside her jacket for the hundredth time that day, the way a gambler touches a card he hasn't looked at yet.

Behind her, Gideon bailed water with a dented tin pot, methodical as a clock. He had been bailing since midnight and had said perhaps forty words in all of them. Mirela sat amidships with the survey maps wrapped in plastic against her chest, holding them the way other people held children.

"You're sure about this," Mirela whispered. It wasn't a question. She'd asked it six times since the river forked, and each time Asha had answered with a version of the same truth: *I'm sure enough.*

"I'm sure," Asha said now, because it was the seventh time and even lies get tired.

The current carried them. That was the thing about rivers in monsoon — you didn't steer so much as negotiate. The boat was a hired wreck she'd cached below the confluence two seasons ago, back when this job was supposed to be a matter of weeks: go up the valley, confirm whether the guide who lied was still breathing, come home, file a paragraph, collect. The hull had wept from the first hour. Now it wept steadily, a sound like someone whispering behind her, and Gideon's tin pot kept time against it — *scrape, slosh, scrape* — the most honest conversation on board.

The lantern grew. A rope stretched across the water at chest height, its end knotted to an iron ring on the near bank, and beyond it the guard hut, and beyond the hut the first pylons of the restricted zone's boundary fence running up into cloud. Somewhere above all of it, invisible, lay the Larkya Glacier and whatever was left of Mirela's team, and — though she had only begun to admit this to herself in the last two days — possibly something else entirely. Someone else.

She had seen the second set of codes in Gideon's face before he ever decoded them aloud. She had watched him read those cairns the way a man reads handwriting he knows better than his own.

*Devi,* she thought, and made herself stop thinking it, because hope was a weight and the boat was already low in the water.

"Rope," Gideon said quietly. First words in hours. His eyes were on the barrier line, calculating — she could see him doing it, measuring the current against the rope's sag, deciding whether they could slip under or would have to hail the hut, whether hailing meant questions and questions meant names and names meant the end of everything he'd spent three years building out of nothing.

"We hail them," Asha said.

His head came around. In the fog-light his face was all planes and shadow, older than three years should have made anyone. "We could pass under."

"The rope has a bell line. You can't see it, but it's there — a tripwire strung a hand's width below the main cable. I've watched them reset it." She heard her own voice go flat and official, the voice she used when the truth needed to sound like paperwork. "If we touch it, they don't ask questions. They shoot first and fill out forms after."

Mirela made a small sound. Gideon looked at Asha a moment longer — that long, weighing look she was beginning to know, the one that took inventory of her the way he took inventory of a slope — and then he shipped the pot and picked up the painter.

"All right," he said. "Talk us through."

* * *

The guard who came down to the water was nineteen if he was a day, too big for his uniform, rifle slung backward out of boredom rather than readiness. His lantern threw a circle of yellow that stopped at the gunwale as if the light itself required permits.

"Papers."

Asha had rehearsed this. Not the words — the words changed with every border, every decade, every regime's preferred flavor of stamp — but the *posture*. The slight impatience. The tone of a woman whose time is being wasted by a boy playing soldier. She produced the oilskin wallet and let it fall open the way documents fall open when they have fallen open a thousand times.

Inside: a transit authorization from a ministry office that had been dissolved eighteen months ago, reissued under a directorate created nine months ago, bearing a seal that existed only in the overlap between the two — a gap in the administrative calendar exactly wide enough for a woman with a steady hand, access to a dead clerk's letterhead stock, and no conscience about either. Below it, a cargo manifest listing scientific equipment consigned to a hydrological survey upstream. Below that, three photographs. Herself. Mirela. And a man listed as *K. Tamang, guide*, whose photograph was four years old and whose real name was known to precisely no one in the ministry still drawing breath.

The guard held the lantern close. Water dripped from the boat's hull into the river. Somewhere upstream, thunder muttered like an old man disagreeing with the weather.

"This is a restricted channel," the guard said. "No civilian traffic."

"The hydrological survey is government business." She tapped the manifest without touching it. "Section four. You'll want to log our entry time and note the seal number; the directorate audits these crossings monthly, and if your log shows a delay they'll want to know why the delay isn't in your own handwriting."

It was beautiful, really, how authority worked. Not force — never force, not at checkpoints, not in fog. Authority was the confident assumption that the other man's job was harder than yours and that you knew his regulations better than he did. The guard's eyes flicked to the seal, to the dates, to the photograph of K. Tamang — and here Asha felt the true hinge of the moment swing, silent, load-bearing — and past the photograph to the man himself, sitting motionless in the stern with the tin pot upside down on his knees like a man who had never bailed anything in his life.

Gideon did not look away. That was the trick of him, she'd learned it watching him across three years of distance and eleven days of proximity: he could be looked at directly and remain unseen, because what people saw when they looked at Gideon Zhou was a tired guide, weathered, unremarkable, a face you'd forget before the lantern swung away. The name on the paper did the rest. *K. Tamang.* A stranger wearing his bones.

The guard wrote in his ledger. The pen scratched. Thunder again, closer.

"Glacier road's washed out above the second bend," the guard said, not looking up. "You'll want the portage trail on the east bank. And there's a curfew order effective tonight — storm protocol. Anyone on the water after dark gets detained pending verification."

"Then it's fortunate we're passing before dark," Asha said, "and that you're noting our entry time now."

He lifted the rope. It was nothing, that gesture — a boy raising a cable — and it was the whole border folding open like a door. The boat slid through. Asha kept her posture until the fog closed over the lantern, until the yellow circle shrank to a coin, to a spark, to nothing, and only then did she let her shoulders drop half an inch and discover that her hands had gone cold inside her gloves.

Mirela exhaled like a diver surfacing. "Those papers—"

"Are valid," Asha said. "That's all anyone needs to know about them."

"They're *impossible*."

"Everything impossible is just paperwork nobody's checked yet." She turned forward, to the fog, to the river hauling them north into territory where her forged seal would protect them right up until it didn't. Behind her the tin pot resumed its rhythm. *Scrape, slosh.* Gideon bailed and said nothing, which was its own kind of signature. He knew. Of course he knew — a man erased from two countries' registries develops an eye for documents that shouldn't exist. He had looked at her papers the way she had once looked at his route codes: recognizing a language, wondering who taught it to her, and what it cost.

He hadn't asked. That frightened her more than the checkpoint had.

* * *

They made the east bank at dusk and dragged the boat into the willows, and by full dark the storm arrived in earnest — rain like gravel, wind flattening the willows, the river rising audibly, a black muscle flexing in the dark ten meters below their tarp. They rigged shelter in the lee of a bank cut, and Mirela slept the sleep of the truly exhausted, curled around her maps, and Gideon took first watch without being asked, sitting at the tarp's edge with his back to them, a shape darker than the dark.

Asha lay awake and let the Kathmandu night come, because it always came, because she had never once managed to keep it out.

Three years ago. A guesthouse near the stupa, the monsoon pressing against the shutters the way it pressed tonight against the tarp. She had found him through a chain of guides-of-guides, a name passed hand to hand like contraband: *the man who signed the report, the man who lied, the man who buried my brother.* She had gone to kill him. She wanted to write that plainly, even now, even alone in her own skull — she had traveled four hundred kilometers with a knife and a plan and a grief so large it had its own weather system, and she had found instead a man burning with fever in a room that smelled of damp and kerosene, delirious, alone, abandoned by whoever he'd been traveling with, too sick to stand.

You cannot knife a man who is asking the ceiling for water.

She had stayed one night. That was the story she told herself: one night, to see him die or recover, either outcome acceptable. She had sponged his forehead. She had forced broth into him. And somewhere past midnight the fever had broken open whatever he kept locked in daylight, and Gideon Zhou — the liar, the coward, the man who had signed her brother's death into a footnote about *adverse glacial conditions* — had begun to talk.

Not confessions. She'd waited for confessions and they never came. What came was worse. He talked about the mountain the way other men talked about God. He talked about route codes — *you mark hazard with stacked stones, three high means turn back, five means the valley itself is lying to you* — reciting them feverishly, correcting himself, teaching the empty air. He talked about Devi. About a rope team, about a decision made at a snow bridge at dawn, about a sound like the world clearing its throat. And then, near morning, sweating and lucid by turns, he had gripped her wrist with a strength that surprised them both and said:

"If he's alive — if there's any chance — I'll find him. I swear it on the report. On every lie in it. Even if it takes me into the ice myself."

And she, holding the wrist of the man she had come to kill, had felt the knife-thought die in her chest — not forgiven, not resolved, simply *outmatched*. Because you cannot hate a promise like that. You can only carry it.

He remembered none of it. She had tested him gently, twice, on the boat and before it — mentioned Kathmandu, mentioned fever — and watched his face do nothing, register nothing, a door opening onto an empty room. Three years she had carried his promise for him, like a package left at a house where no one answers the door. She had taken the surveillance contract afterward partly for money and partly because it put her in the valley where a survivor might survive, and she had stayed after the contract ended for reasons she had not yet said aloud to anyone, reasons that had a name and frost-scarred hands and had last been seen by her at a bus station in Jumla eight years ago.

Rain hammered the tarp. Gideon's watch-shape didn't move.

Here was the loneliness of it, she thought, the particular loneliness no one warns you about: to hold another person's forgotten word is to love a ghost of your own manufacture. He owed a debt he didn't know he'd incurred. She had collected on it silently, year after year, letting it justify her — the forged papers, the cached boat, the eleven days of lies braided with true ones — and the debt had never once been acknowledged because the debtor slept through the signing. If she told him, he might disown it. Fever-talk, he'd say. Words mean nothing at forty degrees. And he'd be right, and the package she'd carried would become hers alone forever, addressed to no one, contents unknown.

Or — and this was the thought that finally pulled her down toward sleep, the thought she both dreaded and hoarded — or the promise hadn't been fever-talk at all. Or it was the truest thing Gideon Zhou had ever said, truer than any report he'd signed, and the proof was walking ahead of them up the valley in the form of stone cairns stacked three high and five high, laid by hands that were supposed to be three years dead.

The codes were his language. Someone was speaking it.

She slept, and dreamed of a rope team crossing a snow bridge at dawn, and woke once in the black hours to hear Gideon still awake, still watching, and to realize he was humming — very softly, barely breath — some mountain song in a language she almost recognized, and that he was singing it, she was somehow certain, in harmony with a voice only he could hear.

Above them, upriver, up through the rain and the dark, the Larkya Glacier waited with its secret, and the storm that would open the calving season was already gathering over the pass.
