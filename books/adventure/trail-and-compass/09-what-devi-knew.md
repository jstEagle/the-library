## 9. What Devi Knew

The storm found them an hour after they left the second cairn, and by then the only shelter in reach was a cave Mirela would never have found on her own — a black seam in the valley wall, half-hidden behind a curtain of icefall debris, marked on Gideon's mental map with a code he had invented himself: *D-4, wind-safe, water at back wall, fits four, cold floor.*

He had mapped it eleven years ago. He had slept in it with Devi twice. The knowledge sat in him like a stone swallowed whole.

They got the stove going while the wind outside rose to a scream that made the rock hum. Asha took first watch at the entrance, her silhouette cut sharp against the white chaos beyond, and Mirela spread the survey maps across a groundsheet and pretended to study them. Gideon knew pretending when he saw it. She kept glancing at him over the top of the laminated sheets, the way people look at a locked door they've decided to open whether or not anyone gives them the key.

The cave was small enough that silence couldn't hide anywhere. It filled every corner, pressed against them all, waited.

"You said the codes were yours," Mirela said finally. Not a question. An opening.

"I said that."

"And you taught them to one person."

"One person." Gideon fed another pinch of fuel to the stove. The flame went blue and steady. "Devi Rai. My partner for six years. We ran commercial expeditions together — Manaslu, Cho Oyu, two seasons on Himlung. He was the better climber. I was the better mapmaker. Between us we could read any mountain in that range like a sentence." He paused. "We wrote our own grammar for it."

"Tell me about the avalanche."

Gideon looked at her. She didn't flinch under it. Her hands were steady on the maps, but he'd noticed earlier that she wore her wedding ring on a cord around her neck instead of her finger — a detail that meant something, though he hadn't asked what — and there was nothing performative in her attention. She wasn't asking as an official. She was asking as a woman who had lost four colleagues into a white silence and needed the silence explained.

"The report says conditions," he said. "Unseasonal warming, loaded slopes, a natural release. That's what's written down. That's why I lost my certifications in two countries — because I signed my name to a document that contradicted everything I'd spent fifteen years learning to read."

"You're saying the report is wrong."

"I'm saying I wrote it wrong. On purpose." He heard his own voice come out flat, stripped of anything, and understood distantly that this was how he'd survive saying it: as if describing someone else's crime. "Devi led them onto the glacier."

* * *

It came out of him slowly at first, like meltwater through ice, and then all at once, the way avalanches actually move — not gradually at all, but held, held, held, and then gone.

"It was October. Three years ago. A private group, eight clients, strong ones — bankers, surgeons, one retired military man who thought altitude was a personality trait. They'd paid for the full traverse. Devi had promised them the Larkya crossing in a season when the Larkya crossing should have been refused." Gideon turned his hands toward the stove. They were scarred hands, rope-burned, frost-nicked, and they shook slightly, and he watched them shake as if they belonged to a stranger. "I told him no. I'd been up the approach three days earlier checking route conditions, and the snowpack was wrong. You learn to feel it through your boots after enough years — the layers not bonding, the slope holding its breath. I told him we turn the group around or we take the low route and lose the summit day. He said the clients had paid. He said he'd run it in worse. He said—" 

Gideon stopped. The wind outside climbed a register, and somewhere above them the mountain shed something enormous; they all heard it, felt it through the floor, a deep concussion like the earth clearing its throat.

"He said," Gideon continued, quieter, "that if I wouldn't lead it, he'd take them up himself. And I believed he was bluffing. Or I told myself I believed it. There's a difference, and the difference is where I've lived for three years."

"So you went."

"So I went. Because the alternative was letting him take eight civilians up alone, and because some part of me still thought I could manage him once we were on the ice. I called it supervision. The mountain called it something else." He drew a breath that hurt. "We crossed onto the glacier at nine in the morning. By ten-thirty the sun had done what I'd warned him the sun would do — warmed the loading on the upper slopes until the bond failed. I heard it start. That sound — you never forget it. A deep crack, like the mountain splitting a knuckle. I yelled the anchor command. Devi was forty meters ahead with the first rope team, moving fast, too fast, pushing the pace he'd promised the clients. He looked back. I will see him look back for the rest of my life. And then the slope came down."

Mirela had stopped breathing. Asha, at the cave mouth, had turned around. Gideon hadn't known she was listening. He found he didn't care.

"Four of the eight clients died. Two guides from the other rope team. Nine total. Devi was at the point of the slide path. They recovered six bodies over four days. Devi's was never found. The glacier took him and kept him." His voice did something strange on the last word, bent without breaking. "And then the inquiries started, and the families wanted someone to blame, and the insurance companies wanted someone to blame, and the government board that certifies guides wanted someone to blame. And everyone assumed it would be me — the senior guide who signed off on the route. Except I hadn't signed off on it. Devi had overridden me. There were witnesses. There was a radio log. I could have put his name on it — posthumously, safely, a dead man can't contest a finding — and kept my career, my certifications, my name in both registries."

"But you didn't."

"I wrote that the release was natural. Unforecastable conditions. I signed it, filed it, and testified to it." He finally looked up from his hands. "The investigators didn't believe me. They pulled my route logs, found inconsistencies, found that I'd flagged the hazard privately and then publicly denied it existed. They decided I was either incompetent or lying, and lying was the more comfortable conclusion, because incompetence in a senior guide means the system failed, and systems don't like failing. So they erased me. Both countries. Quietly. No trial, no appeal process worth the paper. One day I was Gideon Zhou, senior guide, and the next I was a name that didn't come up in searches anymore."

Asha spoke from the entrance, her voice careful. "You protected him. After he killed nine people."

"After he *died* killing nine people." Gideon's jaw tightened. "Yes. I protected him. Six years of partnership. He taught me to read ice. I taught him to read weather. We saved each other's lives more times than I can count — real saves, rope-taut, breath-held saves. And his family — his mother was alive then, his sister—" he gestured vaguely backward, toward the storm, toward the world, unaware of how close the gesture came to Asha herself, "—they'd already lost him. Was I going to hand them a corpse and a verdict? Let them spend the rest of their lives visiting a grave that said *your son caused this*?"

"Yes," Asha said softly. "If it was true."

The words landed harder than the storm outside. Gideon stared at her. She held his gaze without apology, wind-reddened, unblinking, and for a moment he had the vertiginous sense again — the one that had haunted him since the moraine — that she knew him, knew this story, knew exactly which wound she was pressing.

"That's the arithmetic you do at three in the morning," he said. "Out here, in daylight, with the bodies still warm — you don't get to be righteous. You get to choose. And I chose the lie."

"And it cost you everything anyway."

"It cost me everything *because* it was a lie. That's the part nobody tells you about protecting the dead. The dead don't thank you. The living punish you. And the lie sits in your chest like a swallowed stone and grows." He laughed, short and bitter, a sound with no humor in it. "I used to think exile would be the hard part. Losing the registries, the permits, the name. It wasn't. The hard part is that I got exactly what I deserved and none of what I needed. I needed the truth out. I needed someone to say *you did the right thing, telling it*. Nobody ever did. Because it wasn't the right thing. It was just the thing I could bear."

Mirela moved then. She gathered the maps, squared their edges with unnecessary precision — grief organizing itself however it can — and when she spoke, her voice was low and even.

"The survey team. My team. Their last transmission plotted coordinates in your codes. Your grammar, Gideon. On my glacier." She let that sit between them. "Three years ago you buried the truth to protect a dead man. Now a dead man's handwriting is walking up a glacier ahead of us, and four of my people are inside it." Her eyes were bright and hard. "So tell me what I'm supposed to do with that."

Gideon had no answer. He looked at the stove flame, at the blue heart of it, and felt the old stone shift in his chest — not lighter for having been spoken aloud, the confession hadn't lightened anything, but *positioned*, somehow, closer to the surface, closer to being coughed up or passed.

What he said was: "Devi is dead. Whatever's writing those codes, it isn't him."

"You believe that?"

"I have to believe that. Because the alternative is that he's been alive for three years while I carried his sin and my ruin, and never once—" The sentence collapsed. He rebuilt it. "Never once sent word."

Silence took the cave again, but it was a different silence now, emptied out, post-confession, the particular quiet that follows a man handing over the worst thing he owns.

It was Asha who broke it. She had come fully inside at some point without him noticing, and she crouched by the stove, feeding it fuel with the easy economy of someone who had done it ten thousand times, and she said, without looking up:

"The codes on the glacier. Show me the sequence Mirela transcribed."

Mirela hesitated, then read it out — the cairn intervals, the marker stacking patterns, the directional logic. Gideon listened with his eyes closed, translating automatically, the grammar running itself the way a native speaker hears a language. And that was when the cold arrived — not the storm's cold, but the other kind, the kind that starts in the spine.

Because the sequence was correct. Perfectly correct. Not an imitation, not a guess, not a pattern copied from an old journal. It carried the revisions — the third-season refinements, the corrections he and Devi had worked out on Himlung in a tent during a five-day whiteout, changes they had never written down, never published, never taught. Changes that existed in exactly two living memories.

One of them was his.

The other memory had walked into an avalanche three years ago and never come out.

Gideon opened his eyes. Asha was watching him, and her face had gone carefully, deliberately blank — the face of a woman seeing exactly what she expected to see.

"You recognize it," he said. Not a question.

"I recognize that you recognize it," she said.

Outside, the storm screamed. And far up the valley, beneath a kilometer of moving ice, something that knew his language was waiting.
