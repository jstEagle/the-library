## 10. The Second Set of Codes

Mirela Osei had spent eleven years turning terrain into paper, and she trusted paper the way other people trusted God. A map was a promise: that the world had been seen, measured, agreed upon. That if you stood where the contour lines said you stood, the ground would honor its own description.

The Larkya Glacier had stopped honoring anything three days ago, when her survey team's last transmission cut off mid-sentence, and every promise on her maps had begun to look like a lie told by someone who'd never actually been here.

She sat in the mouth of the tent with the field notebook open across her knees, the one she'd carried out of Kathmandu in a waterproof sleeve against her chest, and she copied the cairn sequence again by headlamp because copying was how she thought. Gideon Zhou had pointed at the first stone stack two mornings back and read it aloud like a man translating a language he'd sworn he'd forgotten — *offset left, buried anchor, do not trust the ice below* — and she had written it down without understanding why her hands were shaking.

Now, in the blue hour before dawn, with the glacier ticking and settling somewhere below the moraine like a vast animal turning over in sleep, she laid the sequence out in a column and did what cartographers do when a landscape refuses to make sense.

She drew it.

* * *

The codes came in pairs. She'd noticed that on the second day, watching Gideon move from cairn to cairn — he never read a single stack alone; his eyes always went past it, up-slope or down-valley, to a second marker that confirmed or contradicted the first. Two stones meant one thing. Three stones offset from a fourth meant another. It wasn't a trail language, exactly. Trails said *come this way.* This said something more conditional. More like weather reports. Like warnings.

She plotted them.

Each cairn had a position — she'd been logging coordinates obsessively since the first morning, partly because that was her job and partly because logging things kept the fear at a workable distance. Four people had walked into this valley and not come out. Their tents had been found pitched and empty. Their radios worked. There were no footprints. Mirela had learned to survive grief by converting it into data, and so she converted, and the data now lay in front of her in neat columns of latitude, longitude, bearing, and code.

She connected the points in sequence.

And then, because the line looked wrong — too straight for a route, too regular, running laterally across the valley instead of up it — she connected them in the other order. Reverse. The way you'd read a message backward to check for cipher.

The line didn't change. That was the first wrong thing. A route has a direction; a route reads differently coming and going. This sequence read identically both ways, which meant it wasn't a path at all.

It was a boundary.

Mirela sat very still, listening to the glacier tick.

She took a fresh page and drew the valley freehand from memory — she was good enough at that to embarrass the official sheets, which was precisely why she no longer worked for the mapping division proper but for the survey contract that fed it. Then she overlaid the cairn positions. The boundary ran along the true-left margin of the glacier, dipped inward at a tributary icefall, climbed the lateral moraine, and closed across the valley floor in a long shallow arc, like something drawn around a wound.

Inside the arc, her official map showed nothing remarkable. Contour lines, evenly spaced, confident. A dotted line marking the standard survey approach. The words *stable ice, seasonal accumulation* in the margin notes from the 2019 assessment — an assessment she suddenly realized she'd never actually seen, only cited. It had come to her team as a summary. Summaries were normal. Summaries were how bureaucracies digested landscapes. She had built four people's expedition plan on a paragraph she'd never traced back to its source.

Outside the arc, the map was equally unremarkable.

But the cairns said otherwise. Every marker on that boundary carried the same terminal element in its code — Gideon had translated it without emphasis, the way you translate a word so common it stops meaning anything: *do not enter below this line.* He'd said it about the icefall. About the moraine section. About the flat white sweep of the lower valley where their own camp now sat.

He'd said it five times, in five places, and she had written it down five times as five separate local warnings, because that was what a sane person would assume. Local hazards. Scattered dangers.

They weren't scattered. They were a fence.

* * *

Dawn came gray and slow, the light arriving like something reluctant to be seen. Mirela crawled out of the tent with the notebook pressed to her chest and found Gideon already awake, crouched at the edge of the moraine with a tin cup of snowmelt going cold in his hands, watching the upper valley the way a man watches a door he expects someone to come through.

"I need you to read these again," she said. "All of them. In order."

He looked up. Three years of erasure had taught his face to give away nothing, but she was learning its grammar anyway — the slight narrowing when a question landed somewhere old, the stillness that wasn't calm but load-bearing.

"You've got them written down," he said.

"Written down isn't read. I want your voice on them." She knelt and opened the notebook on the stone between them, pages fluttering in the wind coming down-glacier, cold and mineral-smelling. "Start here. First cairn we passed."

He read. His voice was low and even, a guide's voice, the voice that keeps clients functional on bad days. *Offset left. Buried anchor. Do not trust the ice below.*

"And the second?"

*Double stack, gap of three fingers. Ice under tension. Turn back if the temperature rises above freezing after dark.*

"The third."

He paused over that one longer. *Single stone on a boulder, painted face up. The wall calves early. Nothing below the line is safe in calving season.*

"There," Mirela said. "That phrase. *Below the line.* You said it at the icefall too. And at the moraine. I marked each one as its own warning." She turned the notebook toward him, showing the valley sketch, the arc she'd drawn through the five points. "But they're not separate. Look at the geometry. These aren't warnings about spots. They're a perimeter. Someone built a fence around the middle of this valley and wrote the fence in your language."

Gideon set down his cup. He looked at the drawing for a long time, and Mirela watched him arrive at it the way she had arrived at it in the night — not as a discovery but as a confirmation of something he'd been refusing to see since the first cairn.

"That's not a survey route," he said quietly.

"No."

"A survey team doesn't mark a hazard zone. Hazard zones get marked by—" He stopped.

"By whoever wants people to stay out," Mirela finished. "Or by whoever wants whatever's inside to stay in." She pulled out the folded copy of the official sheet, the one her team had carried, and smoothed it on the stone beside her sketch. "Now look at what the government says is here."

He looked. He was quiet for so long she thought he hadn't understood, and then she saw that he'd understood completely, and that understanding had gone through him like cold water.

"There's nothing on it," he said.

"Nothing. No hazard zone. No boundary. No note in the margins. According to this sheet, the entire interior of that arc is walkable, stable, surveyed ice. My team would have planned their route straight through the middle of it." Her voice held, mostly. "I sent them through the middle of it, Gideon. I gave them this map. I briefed them on this map."

"You briefed them on what you were given."

"I'm a cartographer." The word came out harder than she intended. "My whole life is the difference between what's drawn and what's true. If I can't tell the difference, nobody can." She pressed her palm flat on the official sheet, over the blank confidence of those even contour lines. "This isn't an error. Errors are messy. Errors leave edges. This is clean. Someone decided what this valley should say and made it say it."

Gideon rose slowly and walked a few steps up the moraine, looking into the valley — into the zone, she corrected herself, the way you start using a new word for a place once you know what it costs. The morning light was reaching the upper icefall now, gilding the seracs, making the whole thing look benign and enormous.

"When I guided commercially," he said, without turning around, "we stayed west of the lateral moraine. Everyone did. There was an understanding among the guides — nothing written, nothing explained. You just didn't take clients into the middle valley. Devi used to joke that the mountain had a room nobody was allowed to see." His shoulders moved, not quite a shrug. "I thought it was superstition. Old accident memory. Mountains collect those."

"And now?"

"Now I think somebody collected it instead. Filed it. Erased it." He turned back, and his eyes went to the notebook, to the arc. "Those cairns weren't built this year. Some of that stone's been weathered ten, fifteen years. But some of it's fresh. Restacked. Maintained." He said the word *maintained* the way other men said *haunted.*

"Maintained by whom?"

"I don't know." A pause, and then, lower: "I know who taught me the codes. And I know I watched him die. So either I'm wrong about one of those things, or there's a third hand on these stones, and I don't like any of the versions."

* * *

Asha appeared near midmorning, coming up the moraine with that unhurried stride of hers, as if she were walking through a market square instead of a valley that had swallowed four people. Mirela still didn't know what to make of her — the woman knew Gideon's name from a life he claimed not to remember, knew his habits, his tells, the way he took his tea, and offered explanations the way glaciers offered footing: confidently, and never quite where you expected.

Today Mirela almost didn't care. Today she had something more useful than suspicion: a question with a shape to it.

"Your glaciology," she said, when Asha had settled onto the stone beside them and accepted tea she didn't drink. "Calving seasons. You said you study when the walls let go."

"I study when they let go," Asha agreed.

"Then you'd know this valley. What's in the middle of it?" Mirela turned the sketch toward her. "Because according to my maps, nothing. According to these cairns, everything."

Something crossed Asha's face — quick, controlled, gone. But Mirela had spent eleven years reading faces in remote base camps, faces at the moment they received news, and she knew the difference between surprise and recognition being suppressed. This was the second kind.

"You drew that?" Asha asked.

"Mirela did," Gideon said. "From my readings."

Asha studied the arc for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed register — flatter, more careful, a person stepping from one ice bridge to the next. "There's a reason the old guides wouldn't go into the middle valley. Not superstition. Geometry. The tributary icefall feeds the main trunk at a confluence, and the confluence sits over a bedrock step. Stress concentrates there. The whole interior is under compression — the ice doesn't flow, it *stores*. And stored ice doesn't fail gradually. It fails all at once." She touched the arc lightly, not quite touching the paper. "Whoever built this fence understood that. This isn't a route around danger. It's a record of it. Someone measured the failure zone and marked its exact boundary, and then someone else made sure no official map ever showed it."

"Why?" Gideon asked. "Who benefits from a blank space?"

Asha looked at him then, and Mirela saw something pass between them that had nothing to do with the map — an old ledger briefly open, then shut.

"Blank spaces aren't empty," Asha said. "They're where people put the things they've decided not to know. A glacier that stores stress for decades and releases it in one event — that's not a hazard, officially. That's a liability. A liability with villages downstream. Bridges. A hydropower intake. If the government admitted this valley could fail catastrophically, they'd have to evacuate, compensate, redesign. Or they'd have to admit they knew and did nothing." She folded her hands. "So the valley stays blank. The surveys stay summarized. And anyone who measures too carefully gets reassigned."

Mirela felt the sentence land in her chest like a dropped stone. *Gets reassigned.* She thought of her team — Tomas with his borehole sensors, Priya with her ground-penetrating radar, Lukas and Anselm, the twins of the tripod, always finishing each other's measurements. She thought of the last transmission, cut mid-sentence, and the intact tents, and the snow with no story written on it.

"Gideon," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "Read the sequence again. All of it. But this time — don't read it as a trail."

He looked at her.

"Read it as a border," she said. "If the cairns mark a zone, then the sequence has an orientation. A fence has an outside and an inside. Which side were we on when we found the tents?"

Gideon went still. She watched him reconstruct the high camp in his memory — the perfect tents, the cached food, the working radios, the unbroken snow — and place it against the arc on her sketch. She watched him get there a half-second before she did.

"Inside," he said. "The camp is inside the zone."

"They didn't cross the boundary," Mirela whispered. The wind came down the valley and turned the pages of her notebook, and she pinned them flat with both hands. "They were *put* there. Inside the fence. Where the map says nothing can happen, and the stones say nothing will survive." She looked up at the middle valley, at its beautiful, lying whiteness. "This wasn't an accident. My team didn't wander. Someone brought them in, left everything intact so it would look like a disappearance, and counted on the blank map to make sure no one ever looked in the right place."

Nobody spoke. Below them, the glacier ticked and settled, patient as a held breath.

It was Asha who broke the silence, quietly, without looking at either of them. "There's one more thing your fence tells you," she said. "A boundary marks what's kept out. But a maintained boundary — restacked, freshened, tended for years — marks something else too."

"What?" Gideon asked.

"Someone's been going in and out," Asha said. "For a very long time. And they know the fence well enough to live behind it."

High up the valley, at the lip of the icefall, sunlight caught on something small and bright — a flash, there and gone, like glass, like a lens, like a signal. Mirela's heart slammed once against her ribs.

Gideon was already on his feet, binoculars rising, and Mirela saw his hands go rigid around them.

"That's not a reflection," he said.
