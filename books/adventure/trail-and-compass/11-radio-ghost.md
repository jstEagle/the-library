## 11. Radio Ghost

The radio woke him at 3:14 in the morning.

Not with a voice — not yet — but with the sound a radio makes when it's trying to die: a wet crackle, like ice shifting under load, then a long hiss that rose and fell in a rhythm too regular to be weather. Gideon had been sleeping badly anyway, curled against his pack inside the cave shelter with Mirela's stove ticking down beside her and Asha somewhere near the entrance, awake or pretending to be. He'd learned to sleep through avalanches. He could not sleep through that sound.

He crawled out of his bag and put his hand on the set before he was fully conscious of doing it. Mirela's government-issue unit, its antenna lashed to a rock outside with wire she'd scavenged from the survey camp's abandoned mast. The dial glowed faint green. The needle was trembling.

"Nobody should be transmitting," Mirela said from the dark. She was already sitting up. He hadn't heard her wake either. "The relay station on the south ridge went down with the storm. I checked twice."

"Then someone's close."

"Or it's bounce. Atmospheric skip off the storm front. You get ghosts." She said it flatly, the way you say things you want to be true. In the dim light her face was all angles and shadow, and Gideon thought: she has been carrying four missing people for eleven days, and every strange thing this mountain offers her, she has to decide whether to believe.

The hiss swelled. Then, threaded through it, thin as wire pulled taut:

*—seven two mark four—*

Gideon stopped breathing.

He knew the cadence before he knew the words. Route codes were never spoken the way ordinary coordinates were spoken; they had a grammar, a lilt, numbers grouped in threes with a pause where a pause shouldn't be, because Gideon had built them that way fifteen years ago so that a tired guide shouting into wind couldn't mishear a bearing as an elevation. *Seven-two mark four.* Pause. *Nine-one mark one.* Pause. *Zero-zero mark nine.*

He had invented that pause. He had drilled it into exactly one other person, on a rope team above the Dhaulagiri icefall, laughing, both of them half-frozen, Devi repeating it back wrong three times on purpose just to make him swear.

The transmission looped. Same numbers. Same pauses. Perfectly identical, which meant it wasn't a man speaking now — it was a recording, played over and over into a dead frequency, by someone who wanted it found or didn't care who found it.

"Gideon." Mirela's hand was on his arm. "You know this protocol."

"It's not a protocol," he said. His voice came out sanded down to almost nothing. "It's mine."

* * *

They didn't sleep again. Mirela copied the sequence onto the back of her map sheet, three times, checking herself, while Gideon sat with the radio between his knees like something injured. Asha watched from the cave mouth, silhouetted against the last grey suggestion of night, saying nothing — which from Asha was itself a kind of speech.

When the light came up enough to travel, they didn't travel. Gideon spread the map across his thighs and plotted the coordinates by hand, and his hands were steady, which surprised him, because nothing else was.

The first point sat on the shoulder below the Larkya serac field. The second was a rock island mid-glacier. The third—

The third was inside the hazard zone. Inside the blank space the cairns had been steering them away from, the valley within the valley that no official map admitted existed.

"He's marking a route," Mirela said slowly. "Into the zone. These aren't distress coordinates — there's no priority flag, no emergency suffix. It's a guiding sequence. Point to point to point."

"I know what it is," Gideon said. "I wrote the format."

"Then who is broadcasting your format?"

And there it was, the question he'd been circling all night like a bird over carrion. He made himself look at it straight on.

"Devi," he said.

Saying the name aloud in that cave did something to the air. Mirela went still. Even Asha turned fully around from the entrance, and her face in the early light was unreadable in a way that Gideon, after two weeks of reading her, understood perfectly well how to read: she was not surprised. Not even slightly.

"You told me Devi died," Mirela said carefully. "Three years ago. The avalanche. That's why—" She stopped. That's why everything, she might have said. Why the report, why the blacklist, why a man who once signed his name to mountains now measured his life in miles under a borrowed name.

"The body was never recovered," Gideon said. "I told you that part too. They closed the file anyway. A glacier is a convenient coffin — nobody argues with it." He looked down at the map, at the three points strung like beads. "But a body doesn't transmit route codes. And a recording doesn't build itself."

"Someone else knows the system. You said you taught—"

"One person. One person, ever." He heard his own voice harden and didn't soften it. "I've spent three years telling myself a story about that avalanche, Mirela. I've told it so many times it's worn smooth, like river stone. Devi led a commercial group onto the ice against my warnings. The slope failed. Four clients died. I signed the report blaming conditions instead of him, because he was dead and dead men don't need defending, only remembering." He folded the map along its old creases. "Every word of that story depends on him being dead. Take that away and I don't know what's left standing."

Asha spoke from the entrance, quiet. "Maybe you should find out before you decide what it means."

Gideon looked at her. "You're not asking what I think. You're telling me to go."

"I'm telling you that a man who measures his life in distances covered is afraid of one particular distance." Her eyes held his, dark and steady. "That's all."

* * *

They moved by mid-morning, up the glacier's medial moraine toward the first coordinate, and the day was beautiful in the way high places are beautiful when they are about to hurt you — hard blue sky, wind-scoured ice, the serac field ahead glowing like something lit from within. Gideon walked point because walking point was the only place he could think clearly, and he needed to think, because hope had gotten into him overnight the way meltwater gets into a crevasse: invisible, pervasive, and structurally catastrophic.

Because here was the thing he could not stop calculating, the arithmetic that ran behind his eyes with every step:

If Devi lived — if he'd clawed out of that burial, walked away from the disaster that ended Gideon's life, survived three years alone in an ice valley — then he had known, the whole time, what Gideon had paid. He had known Gideon was investigated, blacklisted, erased from two registries, reduced to guiding trekkers under another man's name. He had known and he had never come. Never sent word. Never left a single coded cairn pointing toward forgiveness, or explanation, or even blame.

Hope said: *he's alive, he's alive, he's alive.*

Something colder said: *he's alive, and he chose this.*

Which was worse? Gideon genuinely didn't know. Grief for a dead friend was a wound that scabbed over if you left it alone. But a living friend who let you bleed for three years — that was a wound that stayed open, that you carried water in, that you drank from because it was all you had.

At the first coordinate they found the transmitter.

It was wedged into a cleft in the rock island, a small solar-charged unit, professional grade, its antenna wired to a blade of stone. Someone had shielded it from wind with a curved slab of ice — cut, not broken, cut with a saw. Next to it, weighted under a stone, lay a laminated card with the broadcast schedule inked on it in a neat, patient hand: transmit six hours, rest eighteen, repeat. Written in English and Nepali both.

The handwriting was Devi's. Gideon would have known it anywhere. Devi had written supply manifests for eleven years in that same upright, unhurried script, the script of a man who believed lists were a moral document.

Mirela photographed everything. Asha stood apart, arms crossed, looking not at the transmitter but up-valley, toward the hazard zone, with an expression Gideon catalogued and filed and hated himself for cataloguing: she looked like someone checking whether a door she already knew was open, was open.

"There's a power cell here for months of operation," Mirela said. "This isn't a beacon dropped in panic. This is infrastructure." She turned to him. "Gideon. If your friend is alive, why call us with a ghost signal instead of showing himself? Why route codes? Why not simply—"

"Because he can't walk out," Gideon said. The answer arrived whole, the way answers do when you've been refusing to look at them. "Half-blind men don't cross serac fields alone. Frostbite, three winters, no surgery — he can see shapes and light and maybe not much else. He's been laying codes for anyone competent enough to read them, because he can't come to the trailhead. He's been calling for a guide." He paused. "He's been calling for me. He just doesn't know if I'm still me."

"And if he does know?" Asha asked softly. "If he knows exactly who you are, and what it cost you?"

Gideon didn't answer. He broke down the transmitter, coiled the antenna, and put the laminated card in his breast pocket, against his heartbeat, like a letter he hadn't decided whether to answer.

* * *

They reached the second coordinate at dusk — the rock island mid-glacier — and found the cache.

It was Devi's cache; there was no mistaking it. Food stored the way Devi stored food, tins rotated oldest-outward, a repair kit organized by failure mode rather than tool type. A primus with a spare jet. And beneath everything, wrapped in oilcloth, a notebook.

Gideon sat with it between his knees while the others pitched shelter, and opened it, and the first page undid him more thoroughly than any avalanche ever had.

It was a log. Three years of log entries, in that upright hand, dated with obsessive precision. Weather. Ice movement. Calving events, recorded season by season — and Gideon felt the hair rise on his neck, because the calving data matched, entry for entry, the pattern Mirela had decoded from the cairns. Devi hadn't just survived in this valley. He had studied it. He had become, alone and half-blind, the most complete record of this glacier's collapse that any human being possessed.

And on the inside cover, in fresher ink, a single line:

*If Zhou reads this: the fourth coordinate is not on the broadcast. Come to the ice wall at first light. Bring the woman with the maps. Trust the sister.*

Gideon read it four times.

*Trust the sister.*

He looked up. Asha was twenty meters away, driving tent stakes with efficient, economical blows, and she was watching him watch her, and she did not look away, and she did not explain, and the last light went off the ice and left them all in blue shadow.

"Sister," Gideon said aloud, to no one, to everyone.

The notebook trembled slightly in his hands, and he could no longer tell whether it was the wind or him. Somewhere up-valley, past the darkening seracs, a man he had mourned and resented and defended at the cost of his own name was waiting at an ice wall, blind, patient, certain that the one person whose life he had quietly ruined would come anyway.

And the worst of it — the thing Gideon carried back into the shelter and lay awake with while the glacier groaned its long tectonic syllables — was that the answer was yes. It had always been going to be yes. Hope didn't need to be true to move him; it only needed to wear Devi's handwriting.

He just needed to know, before he reached that ice wall, what Asha had been to all of this from the very beginning.

"Asha," he said into the dark. "We need to talk about Kathmandu. All of it. Tonight."

In the blackness, he heard her set down whatever she was holding.
