## 12. Asha's Ledger

The fire had burned down to a red eye in its ring of stones when Asha finally made her move.

Gideon slept the way he did everything — efficiently, completely, as if unconsciousness were a route he'd memorized and could enter at will. Mirela was deeper still, curled against her pack with the survey data drive zipped into her jacket even now, even asleep. The boat lay pulled up on the shingle behind them, half-bailed, its seams weeping a slow dark line into the river sand. Above them the valley walls rose black and featureless, and somewhere up there, past the swollen water and the fog, the coded cairns continued their patient arithmetic.

Asha unzipped Gideon's pack with two fingers, the way she'd been taught to open anything without waking what slept inside it.

His route notes were bound in oilcloth — of course they were; he'd always kept his field books that way, back when he'd had a name worth writing on the cover. She eased the bundle out, spread it across her knees, and began to copy by the light of the coals: the bearing sets, the cairn intervals, the little marginal hieroglyphs that meant *solid ice* or *hollow underneath* or *turn back here, no exceptions*. His handwriting had changed since Kathmandu. Tighter. Angrier at the page. But the codes themselves were unchanged, and that was the thing that had kept her awake for three nights running — that a man could lose everything else, his country, his registries, his own name, and keep one private language intact, like a coin sewn into a coat lining.

She was halfway through the third page when the coal-light shifted.

She didn't look up. There was no point. He moved quietly for a big man, but not that quietly, and besides, she'd felt him surface from sleep the way you feel weather change — a pressure, a listening.

"You hold the pencil wrong," Gideon said.

Asha kept writing. "It works."

"That's not how I taught it."

"Maybe someone else taught me."

He came around the fire and stood over her, and she let him see the ledger open on her knees, let him see exactly how much she'd taken. It seemed important, suddenly, that he see all of it. That there be nothing left to discover later, in some worse place, in some worse way.

"Page by page," he said. "You've been at this since the cave."

"Since the boat," she said. "The cave I spent bailing."

Mirela stirred, sat up with the abruptness of a woman who had learned to wake fast, and took in the scene — Asha cross-legged with another man's field book in her lap, Gideon standing over her with an expression Asha couldn't read because it wasn't anger. It was something older than anger. Something almost like grief arriving late.

"I told you," Mirela said quietly. "I told you both, days ago. Nobody finds us by accident. Nobody follows these codes by accident." She looked at Asha the way cartographers look at terrain they don't trust. "Who are you working for?"

The river filled the silence for a while. It had risen again through the evening; they'd have to move before dawn or lose the shingle entirely.

Asha closed the ledger. Set it down between them, squared to the fire, an offering or a surrender — she hadn't decided which, and maybe it didn't matter anymore.

"My name isn't Asha Rai," she said. "That part is true, actually. It's the only part I gave you that's true."

* * *

She had rehearsed this confession for eleven days, through fog and checkpoints and the long grey hours of bailing, and every version of it had collapsed under rehearsal except the plainest one, so she gave them the plain one.

"A year ago I was contacted in Pokhara. A fixer I'd done work for — land surveys, boundary disputes, nothing interesting — asked if I'd take a verification job. Not surveillance. Verification. One question, one answer: is a certain guide still alive, and if so, where does he walk?" She turned her hands palm-up toward the fire. "They described him. They had photographs, old ones, expedition portraits. And they had a phrase they used about him, in the brief, twice, like it was official language. *The guide who lied.*"

Gideon didn't move. The firelight found the scar along his jaw and left the rest of him in shadow, and Asha understood suddenly that he had heard that phrase before — that it had followed him the way weather follows a ridge, arriving before him, announcing him.

"There was a file," she went on, because stopping now would be worse than any of it. "Thin. An avalanche report from three years ago, a commercial group, four dead including the lead guide. A second guide whose signature was on the report and then off every registry in two countries within the month. The people who hired me didn't tell me why they wanted him found. They paid too well for me to ask, at first. That's the honest shape of it. I took money to find a ghost, and I told myself it was just work."

"And you found him," Mirela said.

"I found where he *had been*. Manaslu circuit, then west, always west, always under other names. He guided trekkers on routes so easy they insulted the mountain, and he never stayed anywhere longer than a season." She glanced at Gideon. "You measure your life in distances. I know that about you. I knew it before I ever saw you on the moraine."

"How," Gideon said. It wasn't a question so much as a door held open.

"Because the file said so. Because the people who hired me had studied you the way I study ice — season by season, calving by calving. They knew you kept your field books in oilcloth. They knew you counted paces under your breath when you were nervous. They knew—" She stopped. This was the part she had never managed to say aloud, even alone, even to the river. "They knew you made promises when you had fever. That you couldn't be trusted to remember them, but should be held to them anyway."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above, ice shifted in the dark valley with a sound like a door closing in an empty house.

"So I verified you," Asha said. "Alive. Walking. Under the name Tenzin Dorje, which fits you badly, by the way — you wear borrowed names the way you wear that jacket, like something you're waiting to give back. I wrote my report. I sent it. The job ended. Payment cleared." She lifted her chin. "And then I didn't stop."

"Why," Mirela said. Not harshly. Almost gently, which from her was more frightening.

"Because the brief was wrong," Asha said. "Because they called you *the guide who lied*, and then I watched you read a glacier for six days, and liars don't read terrain like scripture. Because they wanted you located but they didn't want you approached, and I've worked for enough governments to know what locate-but-don't-approach means. Because—" Her voice caught on something and she pushed through it. "Because the file had a photograph of the dead lead guide, and I've seen that face my whole life in a cracked mirror and across a kitchen table, and nobody told me they were sending me after my brother's partner."

Silence. The kind that has weight and temperature.

Gideon sat down slowly, across the fire from her, and Asha watched him assemble the sentence he needed, watch it fail, watch him try again.

"Devi," he said. "Devi had a sister."

"Half-sister. Different fathers, same stubbornness." She smiled and found there was nothing in it. "Our mother called us her two compasses — always pointing opposite directions and somehow ending up in the same place. He went into the mountains. I went into the paperwork of mountains. Maps, permits, surveys. Someone had to stay level and count things." She looked at her hands. "When the avalanche report came through, I read it forty times. I knew it was wrong the way you know a map is wrong — the contours didn't match the grief. Four dead, one guide erased, and a report so clean it squeaked. Clean reports are written by people cleaning something. I've spent three years trying to get anyone to look at it. Three years, Gideon. Committees. Ombudsmen. A lawyer in Kathmandu who took my money and my hope and produced nothing but receipts."

"And then the survey team vanished," Mirela said slowly, "and someone decided to check whether the erased man was still—"

"Whether the loose end was still breathing. Yes." Asha met Gideon's eyes across the fire. "I think they wanted to know if you'd come back. If the man who signed that report might someday talk. Or might someday be blamed for something new. I don't know which. I only know the question wasn't friendly."

"But you kept going after the job ended," Gideon said. "You crossed restricted water with papers that shouldn't exist. You talked us through a checkpoint in fog. You've been copying my routes all night." He leaned forward. "Why? If the job ended, why are you still here?"

And there it was — the question she had carried up the whole valley like ballast, the one with no clean answer, the one she'd rehearsed eleven days of versions for and none of them survived the firelight.

"Because I needed to know what kind of man signed that report," she said. "That was mine. Not theirs. Mine. My brother died on that glacier — that's what the world says died — and the last man who saw him alive signed a paper and vanished, and I built three years of hatred on top of that signature. I needed to stand next to you on the ice and find out if the hatred was load-bearing." She swallowed. "It isn't. That's the worst discovery of my life. You're not what they said. Which means either the report lied, or you did, and I've watched you too closely now to believe you did. So the report lied. And if the report lied, then somebody cleaned something — the same somebody, I'd bet my boat, who suppressed whatever the survey team found up this valley. And I want the name of that somebody more than I want air."

Mirela had gone very still. "The falsification style," she said. "In the cache. When we find it. You think it'll match."

"I think this valley has one janitor," Asha said, "and he's been sweeping for years."

Gideon was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was low and stripped, the voice of a man reading hard ground.

"The night in Kathmandu," he said. "You remembered it. I don't. Fever, you said. What did I promise?"

Asha held his gaze and did not look away, though everything in her wanted to.

"You promised Devi's family the truth," she said. "Whatever it cost. You were burning hot and half-delirious and you gripped my wrist hard enough to leave marks, and you said: *if I ever come back from wherever I go, tell them what really happened. Tell them I'll trade anything for it.* And then you disappeared for three years." She finally let her eyes drop to the ledger between them. "So I'm holding you to a word you don't remember giving. That's the loneliest job I've ever had. Lonelier than the boat."

* * *

Mirela walked to the waterline after that, needing distance, needing to look at anything that wasn't a human face. Asha heard her boots on the shingle and let her go. Some truths need to be stood apart from.

Gideon stayed. He picked up his ledger, weighed it in his hand, and then — deliberately, watching her do it — tore out the pages she had copied and handed them to her.

"Keep them," he said. "If you're going to betray me, do it with accurate notes."

"That's a strange kindness."

"It's not kindness. It's triage." He fed a branch to the fire. "Three people on a leaking boat heading into a valley that eats survey teams. We can't afford secrets we haven't inventoried. Now yours are. Mine aren't." Something moved behind his eyes, some door opening onto a room he'd kept locked even from himself. "There's a confession I owe Mirela. About the avalanche. About what I signed and why. I've been carrying it since the cave and failing to say it."

"Say it to me first," Asha said. "Practice on someone who already hates you a little. It builds tolerance."

The corner of his mouth moved — not quite a smile, but the memory of one, archaeological. "You don't hate me."

"No," she agreed. "That's the problem. I spent three years building a weapon and then the target turned out to be the only person who talks to glaciers politely." She hugged her knees. "My brother used to say the mountains keep two ledgers. One for what happened, one for what people say happened. And that the second one always sells better." She stared into the coals. "I want the first ledger, Gideon. Even if it ruins everyone. Even if it ruins me. Especially if it ruins the people who hired me."

"Even if it ruins Devi's memory?"

The question landed precisely, the way his questions always did, on the one contour she hadn't mapped. She thought of Devi at fourteen, teaching himself knots on the balcony rail. Devi at nineteen, waving from a bus to a base camp she wasn't allowed to visit. Devi in the photograph in the thin file, grinning under a load of rope, already halfway to being a story instead of a person.

"Especially then," she said. "Because right now his memory belongs to the people who wrote the second ledger. And I am done letting liars keep my family's accounts."

Gideon nodded once, slow, like a man accepting terms he'd been dreading and hoping for in equal measure. Then he straightened, and his head came up, and Asha saw the change move through him the way a tremor moves through ice — small at the source, enormous in consequence.

"What," she said.

He was looking up-valley, into the black. "The radio," he said. "It's transmitting."

"We turned it off."

"I know." He was already moving toward the pack, toward the broken set they'd salvaged from the high camp, its casing cracked, its battery a dying amber tooth. Static breathed out of it, faint, rhythmic — and threaded through the static, thin as wire stretched across a valley, a voice.

Asha knew the voice before Gideon's face told her he did. She had known it her whole life, across kitchens and bus windows and one final phone call that cut off mid-word.

It was reciting coordinates.

"Gideon," she whispered, and her practiced composure, her eleven days of controlled confession, went all at once to pieces in her hands. "That's Devi. That's my brother's voice."
