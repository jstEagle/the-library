## 13. Calving Season Opens

The mountain told us before it told the sky.

I woke in the grey hour before dawn to a sound I hadn't heard in three years and had never once stopped hearing in my sleep — a deep, tectonic groan rolling up from the glacier's throat, the sound of ice the size of a cathedral deciding to move. I was out of the tent before my boots were laced, breath hanging in the cold, and I stood on the moraine with my hand pressed flat against a boulder as if I could take the mountain's pulse through stone.

Below us, the ice wall at the head of the valley was breathing.

That's the word the old guides used, back when I was young enough to think they were being poetic. Breathing. The glacier expands in the summer heat, the meltwater lubricates its base, and the whole frozen river begins to slide — millimeters at first, then centimeters, then the front face starts shedding itself into the valley in pieces that fall like buildings. Calving. Most years it was a slow, seasonal thing, a background percussion you stopped noticing. But this year the monsoon had come early and hot, and the glacier had been drinking for weeks, and now it was awake ahead of schedule.

Behind me, the tent flap rustled. Mirela emerged with her hair frozen flat on one side, squinting at the ice wall, then at my face.

"That sound," she said. "That's not normal, is it."

"No."

"Is it dangerous?"

"It's an opening," I said. "For about forty hours. Then it closes."

She looked at me the way she'd been looking at me since the second cairn — like a woman reading a map that kept redrawing itself. "Explain."

I pointed to the valley's throat, where the glacier poured down between two rock shoulders in a wall of blue-grey ice maybe two hundred meters high. "When the ice calves, the front face retreats. The seracs that block the hidden route — the one your codes mark — they shear off and fall. For a day and a half, maybe two, there's a corridor through the icefall that doesn't exist any other time of year. After that, the wall rebuilds itself. New ice, new seracs, sealed for another year." I paused. "If your survey team went in through there, they went in during last year's window. If they're still in there, they can only come out the same way. And the window opens now."

Mirela was silent for a long moment. The glacier groaned again, and somewhere high on the wall a chunk the size of a truck broke free and fell in a slow white bloom of powder. The sound arrived three seconds later, a flat crack like a rifle.

"Forty hours," she said.

"Give or take. I'll know better by midday."

"And if we miss it?"

I didn't answer that, and she didn't ask again. We both understood what a sealed icefall meant for anyone trapped on the far side of it. It meant a year. It meant, for four people who had already survived eleven months on a glacier that shouldn't have let them live, possibly nothing at all.

* * *

We broke camp fast. Asha was already up — she was always already up, a fact about her that had stopped surprising me and started simply being part of the landscape, like the moraine and the wind — and she had the stove packed before Mirela had finished rolling her mat. She moved like someone who had done this a hundred times, and she watched the ice wall with an expression I couldn't parse: not fear, not wonder. Something closer to recognition, the way you look at a house you lived in as a child.

"You've seen this before," I said, coming up beside her.

"Calving seasons?" She shrugged. "It's what I told you I study."

"It's not what I asked."

For a moment I thought she wouldn't answer. Then she said, quietly, "I've seen this glacier calve before. Three years ago. From the other side." And she turned away to kick dirt over the stove ashes, ending the conversation as efficiently as she ended all the ones that mattered.

The other side. The far side of the ice wall. The side where the codes led, where the survey team had vanished, where — if the radio ghost was real, if that broken transmission had been what my traitor heart wanted it to be — a dead man might still be walking.

I shouldered my pack and didn't let myself finish the thought. Not yet. There would be time for hope later, or there wouldn't, and either way the mountain didn't care.

* * *

We made the tongue of the glacier by mid-morning, and that was where I stopped being a man with questions and became a guide again, fully, the way you fall into old water.

The approach to the hidden route was a maze of lateral moraine and melt channels, and in the summer flood it was worse than treacherous — it was unreadable, the terrain reshaping itself daily as the ice beneath it melted and slumped. A year ago, this approach would have taken me a full day of cautious probing. But the codes had anticipated it. Of course they had. I had designed the code system for exactly this kind of terrain, and whoever had laid the cairns up this valley had known the ground the way I knew it — which meant, whatever else was true, they had walked it recently, and walked it well.

The cairn at the tongue was a double-stack with a flat stone canted east: *stable line, left of the channel, move before noon.* The meltwater was already rising around its base. We crossed on a bridge of ice the cairn didn't mention but the logic of the line implied, and I went first with the rope, testing each step with the pole, and Mirela crossed with the tight competence of someone who had spent her career in mountains she only half understood, and Asha crossed like water finding its level — no hesitation, no wasted motion, her boots finding the same holds mine did as if she'd watched me walk this kind of ground for years.

Which, she claimed, she had.

By noon we stood below the icefall itself, and I made myself look at it the way I hadn't let myself look at it in three years.

It was beautiful. That was always the obscenity of it — that the thing that killed Devi, that ended me, that had eaten four surveyors and was now offering us a forty-hour door, was beautiful. The wall rose in tiers of fractured seracs, blue in the shadows and white-hot where the sun struck, and along its base the fresh calving had already opened a scar of raw ice a hundred meters wide. Meltwater poured from the wound in braided silver threads. And through the scar, visible only in fragments between the surviving seracs, a corridor — a slot of shadowed ice climbing steeply into the heart of the glacier, exactly where the codes said it would be.

"It's open," Mirela breathed. "It's actually open."

"It's open *now*." I pulled out my watch, then made myself put it away; the glacier didn't run on watch time. "Listen to it. Count the falls."

She listened. We all did. In ten minutes, the wall calved four times — two small, one medium, and one that sent a powder cloud two hundred meters into the air and a shockwave across the moraine that I felt through my boots.

"Four in ten minutes is fast," Mirela said. "Isn't it?"

"For this glacier, this early in the season? It's very fast." I crouched and pressed my palm to the ice, feeling the vibration, the deep peristaltic shudder of a glacier in a hurry. "It's accelerating. Which cuts both ways. The route opens faster, but it closes faster too." I stood, wiped my hand on my trousers, and did the arithmetic I'd been carrying since dawn. "The falls are getting bigger. When the big ones start coming every few minutes instead of every ten, the corridor becomes a shooting gallery. I'd give it forty hours. Thirty-six if the heat holds. Less if the cloud comes down."

"Then we go now," Asha said.

"We go now," I agreed, and then I turned and looked at both of them, because there was a thing that needed saying and I had learned, at terrible cost, that the things that need saying are the ones you say before the rope goes on. "Understand what we're doing. Once we're in that corridor, there is no retreat to this side. The wall will close behind us the same way it opens ahead. Whatever we find in there — your team, the truth about the codes, whatever Asha isn't telling us — we find it on the far side, and we live with it on the far side, and we come out next year if we miss this window. Or we don't come out at all." I let that sit. "So if anyone has anything left to say, say it now."

Mirela's jaw tightened. I watched her think about the four names in her notebook, the transmission she'd played for me in a Kathmandu tea shop a lifetime ago, the coordinates plotted in a dead language only two people had ever spoken. "Say it now," she echoed, and shook her head. "I've been saying it for a year to people who wouldn't listen. I'm done talking. I want the ice."

I looked at Asha.

She met my eyes, and for once she didn't deflect, didn't arm herself with that half-smile she used like a tool. "I have things to say," she said. "None of them will change whether we go. So I'll carry them up the mountain, and you can carry yours, and maybe at the top we'll put them down." A pause. "That's the best I can give you, Gideon."

Gideon. Not the borrowed name, not the alias I'd worn for three years like a coat that almost fit. She had never once used the alias. I'd stopped correcting her in the first week, and I had never decided whether that was because I trusted her or because I'd given up.

"That'll do," I said.

* * *

Roping up took twenty minutes. I put myself on point — no argument was going to move me from that position, and nobody tried — with Mirela in the middle and Asha on the anchor, thirty meters of rope between each of us, coils shortened for the icefall. I taught Mirela the fall signals one more time, made her repeat them back, made Asha confirm the anchor drill. Then I looked up at the corridor one last time, reading it the way I'd been trained to read ice before I'd ever invented a single code: not as a route but as an argument. The mountain was making a case for why we shouldn't come in. My job was to find the flaws in its reasoning.

The corridor climbed in three tiers. The first was a ramp of debris-covered ice, steep but honest, and we took it in forty minutes with the glacier grumbling around us like a sleeping animal that kept half-waking. The second tier was the teeth of it: a traverse under two hanging seracs the size of houses, crossing a slope of blue ice at forty degrees where a single fall would pendulum all three of us into the crevasse field below. I waited at its base for the light to angle off the seracs — you cross under ice in the coldest hour you can get, when it's least likely to shed — and in the waiting, with nothing to do but breathe and watch the wall, the questions I'd been outrunning all morning finally caught up.

Devi's voice on the radio. Coordinates recited in the cadence I knew better than my own heartbeat. Either someone had a recording and was using it — to lure us, to warn us, to mock us — or the man I had mourned for three years, the man whose name I had protected with my career and my country and my own good name, was alive on the far side of this wall and had never once tried to reach me.

And Asha, who knew my habits like a wife, who carried papers that shouldn't exist, who had confessed to being hired to verify whether *the guide who lied* still lived and then refused to say why she'd kept working after the job ended. Whose memory of a night in Kathmandu I did not share. Who had seen this glacier calve three years ago *from the other side*.

Every unresolved thing in my life was on the far side of that wall, and the wall was going to close in thirty-six hours, and once it closed, the questions would be sealed in with the ice for a year. I felt it not as dread but as something worse: relief. A deadline is a mercy. It means you stop choosing and start moving.

"The light's right," Asha said beside me. She had read my waiting the way I read it — by the shadows on the seracs, not the watch. Another entry in the ledger of things she shouldn't know.

"We cross on my signal. Fast feet, no pauses. If I shout *fall*, you throw yourselves flat and you don't look up." I clipped into the line. "Mirela — when we're across, don't celebrate. The third tier is the one that decides whether we make it."

"What's the third tier?"

I looked up at the slot where the corridor narrowed to a single body-width between walls of ice that the morning sun was already warming. "The one the codes mark with the sign for *no stopping*."

* * *

We crossed the traverse clean. It took eleven minutes and cost me ten years, and when my boots hit the safe ice on the far side I stood there shaking with the specific exhaustion of fear spent well, and I watched Asha bring Mirela across with the rope running smooth as silk through her gloved hands, and I thought: *whoever trained you trained you the way I was trained.*

The third tier was worse than I'd remembered, because the last time I'd stood at its base I'd been twenty-six and immortal and the person beside me had been Devi, laughing, saying the code for *no stopping* was my whole philosophy in one glyph. Now the slot was narrower — the glacier had squeezed itself — and the ice on both walls was weeping meltwater in ropes, and somewhere above us, out of sight, the calving went on like artillery over a ridge.

We climbed it in the shadow hour, moving exactly as fast as safety allowed and not one step faster, and the code cairn at the top — a single stone balanced on edge, the starkest mark in my system, *no stopping, no camping, nothing alive stays here* — stood exactly where the sequence said it would, and next to it, half-melted out of a year of ice, was something that stopped me with my hand still on the stone.

A tin box, government olive, the kind survey teams used for data caches, stenciled with a serial number. Wedged under the cairn. Placed there by someone who had wanted it found by whoever read the codes — and by no one else.

Mirela reached it first. Her breath went out of her in a sound I'll remember the rest of my life. "This is ours," she said. "This is our team's cache. This is—" She had the lid off with numb fingers, and she looked inside, and everything in her face changed at once. Not joy. Something colder and harder, rising through the relief like a bone through skin.

"Mirela," I said. "What is it?"

She lifted out a bundle wrapped in plastic — data drives, a field notebook, and a folded sheet of paper with a government letterhead. She held the paper out to me without a word.

I read it. It was a directive, dated eleven months ago, signed three ranks above anyone who should have known this survey existed. It ordered the team to *suspend transmission of hazard assessments from the Larkya sector pending review.* Pending review. The same phrase, I would have bet my life on it, that had appeared in the file that erased me three years ago.

The glacier calved above us, enormous and close, and the whole slot shuddered and shed a rind of ice that shattered around our boots. Asha had her hand on my shoulder, pulling me toward the shelter of the upper wall, and her face when I turned was white — not with fear of the ice.

"You know what this means," she said.

"It means the codes weren't leading us to your team," I said, and the deadline that had been pressing on me all day suddenly reorganized itself into something with a shape and a name. "They were leading us away from something. And we've got thirty-five hours to find out what."

Mirela was already wrapping the drives back in the plastic, her hands steady now, her voice flat with a fury I recognized because I had lived inside it for three years. "Then we don't stop," she said. "No stopping. Right?"

I looked up the corridor, into the blue dark where the icefall opened onto the hidden valley, where a dead man's voice had recited coordinates and four surveyors had walked into a silence that someone very powerful wanted kept.

"Right," I said, and led us into the ice.
