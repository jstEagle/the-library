## 14. The Hazard Zone

The cairns stopped lying to us at the mouth of the valley.

Mirela had spent eleven years turning terrain into paper — contour lines, spot elevations, the patient arithmetic of a landscape reduced to something a minister could hold in one hand. She trusted maps the way other people trusted scripture. But standing at the threshold of the zone Gideon's dead partner had marked, she understood for the first time that a map could be a weapon, and that she had been carrying one all along.

The official survey sheet of this quadrangle showed the upper valley as benign: a gentle ice field, gradient 8 degrees, crevasse density low, "suitable for sustained research operations." She had signed that assessment herself, three years ago, in her capacity as senior cartographer attached to the High Himalaya Glacial Monitoring Program. Her signature sat in the lower right corner like a small, obedient animal.

Now she stood on ground that sheet described as safe, and every hair on her arms was standing.

"Stop here." Gideon's voice came from ahead, low and flat. He had gone still in the way he did — not frozen but *braced*, weight settled into his boots, head tilted as if listening through the soles of his feet. "Look at the last two cairns."

She looked. The stone stacks climbed the lateral moraine in a sequence she could now partially read — Devi's grammar, built on Gideon's alphabet. The final pair broke the pattern. One was doubled, twin stacks side by side. The next was built from red-veined rock, unmistakable against the grey scree, and it faced *inward*, toward the valley floor, rather than marking the route along the flank.

"It's a gate," Mirela said slowly. "A warning and a gate."

"A boundary marker." Gideon crouched beside the red cairn. His gloved fingers hovered over the stones without touching them, the way a man hovers over a wound he is afraid to reopen. "Devi used double-stacks for hazards you could cross with care. Red stone meant *do not cross without reason*. And the orientation—" He glanced up at her, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before, something almost like awe curdled into grief. "He turned it inward. This wasn't a warning to travelers passing through. It was a warning to anyone coming *here*."

"To anyone coming to the glacier," Asha said quietly, behind them.

Mirela turned. Asha stood a few paces back, pack straps creaking, her weathered face unreadable in the flat white light. Three days on the river and two days of climbing had stripped whatever pretense of casual glaciologist she'd arrived with; what remained was harder and more watchful than Mirela liked. But she had talked them through the checkpoint with papers that should not exist, and she had bailed water all night without complaint, and Mirela had decided — provisionally, resentfully — to keep trusting her until trust became stupid.

"The survey team," Mirela said. "Four people. Osei, Lindqvist, Baral, and the seismologist, Kwon. Their last transmission plotted here." She pulled the printout from its waterproof sleeve for perhaps the hundredth time. The coordinates were written in route code — Gideon's code, Devi's hand — and they fell squarely inside the red-stone boundary, maybe nine hundred meters up-valley, beneath the great serac wall where the main glacier bent around the shoulder of the peak.

"And their gear was left at high camp like an offering," Gideon said. "Tents pitched. Food cached. Radios charged." He didn't finish the thought. He never finished that thought. But Mirela had watched him circle the abandoned camp twice in silence, reading snow that held no footprints, and she had seen the exact moment his professional calm cracked into something older and more personal. Whatever kind of person leaves no tracks, Gideon knew him. Or feared him. Or both.

"So we go in," Asha said.

"We go in *carefully*," Gideon corrected. "Double-stack means care. Red stone means reason. We have a reason." He looked at Mirela. "Your team. Your data. If they cached anything, it'll be under stone, off the ice, above flood line. That's how I'd do it. That's how *he'd* do it."

They crossed the boundary at noon.

* * *

Inside the zone, the valley changed character entirely.

Outside, the Larkya Glacier had been loud — meltwater chuckling underfoot, rockfall ticking down the moraines, the ordinary conversation of a living ice mass. Inside, the sound dropped away as if someone had closed a door. The air grew colder by degrees Mirela felt in her teeth. The ice underfoot changed too: cleaner, bluer, shot through with bubbles like trapped breath, and cut by crevasses that ran in long, unnervingly parallel lines, like ruled paper.

Gideon led them along the medial moraine, following a logic he explained in clipped fragments — stay on the debris band, cross the blue ice only at the shadowed seams, never stop in the open between the two rock islands. Mirela mapped as they went, sketching in her notebook because her GPS unit had been behaving strangely since the river, its readings drifting by hundreds of meters and then snapping back, as though the satellites themselves couldn't quite agree on where this place was.

It was Asha who noticed the stakes first.

"Those aren't ours," she said, pointing.

Half-buried in the moraine fifty meters ahead stood a line of aluminum survey stakes, six of them, spaced at precise intervals. They were government issue — Mirela knew the stamp, Program property, serial-numbered — and each carried a small orange flag long since bleached to bone-white. A measurement transect. Someone had laid a scientific instrument grid across the hazard zone and then walked away from it.

"That's a strain-rate array," Mirela breathed. She was moving before she'd decided to, boots crunching over slate. At the nearest stake she knelt and brushed away the gravel. The base plate was intact. The stake itself bore a fine scribed scale, and against that scale—

Her stomach dropped.

The glacier had moved. Of course glaciers move; that was the whole point of measuring them. But the displacement recorded on that stake — the distance the ice had dragged the marker from its original scribed zero — was not a number that belonged on any map she had ever approved. She checked the second stake. Same story, worse. The third stake had been *bent*, bowed down-glacier like a blade of grass in wind, and the ice had flowed over its base and swallowed it to the collar.

"Gideon." Her voice came out strange. "Come look at this."

He came. He read the stakes faster than she did — not the science of them but the story of them — and when he straightened, his face had gone the color of the old snow.

"How fast?" he asked.

"At these displacement rates?" Mirela did the arithmetic in her head, refusing to write it down, refusing to make it real on paper. "This section of the glacier isn't flowing. It's *surging*. Ten, twenty times normal rate. Maybe more." She looked up-valley, toward the bend where the serac wall leaned over the ice like a door about to swing shut. "A surge like this means the whole lower tongue is being compressed against that constriction. Pressure builds. The calving front becomes unstable. And when it goes—"

"When it goes, everything below the bend goes with it," Gideon finished. "Including the high camp. Including the approach valley. Including anyone standing in it."

"Including four surveyors who measured this," Mirela said, "and reported it, and then vanished."

The silence that followed had texture. It pressed on her eardrums.

Asha crouched by the bent stake, touching it gently, the way one touches a grave marker. "You signed the map that called this place safe," she said. Not cruelly. Just factually, the way she said most things.

"I signed the map based on the data I was given." Mirela heard her own voice sharpen and didn't rein it in. "Field teams transmit raw readings to the Program office. The office compiles. The office issues assessments. Cartographers draw what the assessments say. That is how a bureaucracy functions. That is how *science* functions — on trust."

"And if the office lies?"

"Then the whole chain is rotten," Mirela snapped, "and everyone downstream is holding a weapon they don't know they're carrying. Which is why I'm standing in a restricted valley during monsoon season looking for my team, so thank you for the summary."

Asha held her gaze for a moment, then nodded once, and Mirela realized the woman hadn't been attacking her. She'd been *testing* her — checking whether Mirela would defend the institution or follow the evidence. Mirela wasn't sure which answer she'd given. She suspected it was the only honest one: both, and the space between them was where her fury lived now, growing.

"Cache," Gideon said quietly. He was already scanning the slope above the transect. "If your team found numbers like this, they wouldn't radio them. Radio traffic gets logged, logged traffic gets edited. They'd cache the originals. Stone shelter, above flood line, north-facing so the snow doesn't rot the seals." He pointed. "There."

Above the transect, where the moraine shouldered against the valley wall, a tumble of granite slabs formed a low natural alcove. Mirela saw nothing remarkable about it until they climbed to it — and then she saw the cairn. Small, waist-high, built inside the alcove's shadow where it would escape the worst of the weather. Double-stacked. Red-veined capstone.

Devi's signature, guarding someone else's work.

"He helped them," Mirela whispered. "Or they found his markers and understood them. Either way—" She stopped. Her hands were shaking, and she made herself kneel and begin dismantling the little wall stone by stone, carefully, the way you open a letter you already know will burn you.

Behind the stones lay a green ammunition box, military surplus, Program-stenciled. Beside it, a second box, smaller, orange. And wedged beneath them both, a sealed foil data pouch of the kind used for satellite uplink backups.

The green box opened on instruments: a spare theodolite, batteries, a field notebook swollen with damp but legible. The notebook was Baral's — Mirela recognized the cramped handwriting, the little marginal cartoons he drew when bored. The entries began routinely. Stake placements. Temperature logs. Then, eight days after arrival, the handwriting changed. Faster. Angrier.

*Displacement figures 14x baseline. Recomputed twice. Cross-checked against Kwon's seismic — microquake swarms clustering at the constriction, increasing frequency. This is a surge onset. Request immediate reclassification of quadrangle. Request evacuation window before calving season.*

The next entry was dated five days later.

*Response received from Program office: figures "inconsistent with regional models." Resubmit with error analysis. Kwon says our error bars are smaller than the discrepancy by an order of magnitude. We are not wrong. Something else is.*

And the entry after that:

*Lindqvist spoke to a source in the office. There is no error analysis to do. The regional models were adjusted two years ago to fit a narrative. Our raw data contradicts the narrative. The narrative is load-bearing. Do not resubmit. Copy everything. Cache everything. Trust no transmission.*

Mirela read the last line four times. Around her the valley kept its terrible silence, and somewhere far above, deep in the serac wall, the ice ticked and groaned like a house settling — or like a trap considering.

* * *

The orange box was hers.

Not hers personally — but hers institutionally, unmistakably. Inside, wrapped in a plastic map case, lay the master copy of the survey quad she had drawn, annotated in four different hands. And clipped to it, a typed memorandum on Program letterhead, dated fourteen months ago, marked INTERNAL — DO NOT DISTRIBUTE.

She read it standing up, because if she sat down she did not think she would get up again.

The memo was a revision order. It instructed the cartography section to adjust the stability classification of the Larkya quadrangle from Class III (active hazard) to Class II (monitored, acceptable risk), citing "model harmonization across the monitoring network." A handwritten note in the margin, initials she recognized as belonging to the deputy director, added: *"Class III triggers mandatory buffer-zone restrictions. Buffer restrictions void the access easements. The easements are the entire basis of the corridor contract. Harmonize the models."*

Harmonize the models. Make the map lie, because the truth was expensive.

Below the memo, someone — Baral, by the handwriting — had taped a photocopy of an older document. Mirela peeled the tape away with numb fingers and unfolded it, and the world narrowed to a single page.

It was an incident report. Three years old. An avalanche on a commercial route in the eastern ranges. Four clients dead. Two guides: one deceased, one surviving. And in the findings section, in the same bureaucratic register, the same tidy institutional voice she had just read in the revision memo: *"Investigation attributes the event to anomalous loading conditions beyond guide control. Surviving guide's route decisions reviewed and found consistent with standard practice. However, given public confidence considerations, the surviving guide's certification is suspended pending review."*

Pending review. For three years. A review that had never convened, because the suspension was never meant to end — it was meant to be the sentence. The same style. The same fingerprints. Adjust the record, protect the contract, bury the person who inconveniently knew the truth. The pattern didn't just have a name now. It had a *signature*, and Mirela had seen it twice, fourteen months apart, written in the same hand.

"Gideon." She held the report out to him without looking away from it. "Is this yours?"

He took it. She heard him read it — heard the paper tremble slightly in his grip, the only sound he made. When he finally spoke, his voice was scraped hollow.

"This is mine."

"Read the attribution line."

There was a pause. "It cites the regional office review board."

"It cites the *deputy director's* review board. The same office that ordered this glacier's hazard class falsified. The same office your partner has been marking in secret for God knows how long." She turned at last and looked at him, and the fury in her chest had finally found its shape, and it was enormous and cold and clarifying, like the ice around them. "Gideon. You weren't blacklisted for an accident. You were blacklisted for *bookkeeping*. You were a line item. They erased you the way they erased this valley — because the truth was load-bearing, and you were standing under it."

Gideon stared at the report. Something moved behind his eyes — three years of exile rearranging itself, mile by mile, into a different shape. Not an accident of weather. A decision, made in an office, about the value of a man's name.

"They didn't even need me to be guilty," he said softly. "They just needed me to be *gone*."

"And Devi knew." Asha's voice, quiet, from the alcove entrance. She was watching the valley, not them, her hand shading her eyes against the glare. "That's why the codes. That's why the hidden route. He wasn't hiding from the mountain." She turned her head, and her eyes when they met Mirela's were bright and hard. "He was hiding the evidence from the people who falsified the map. He's been running a counter-survey for three years. Alone. In the ice."

Mirela looked down at the cache — Baral's furious notebook, the revision memo, the incident report, the foil data pouch with its satellite backup of raw readings that contradicted everything the ministry believed, or claimed to believe, about this glacier. Enough. It was finally, *finally* enough. Evidence of the suppressed hazard. Evidence of the falsification. Evidence of the pattern, named twice.

"We take all of it," she said. "Every page. And when we're out—"

The mountain interrupted her.

It began as a sound she felt before she heard it — a deep, subterranean exhalation rising through her boot soles, through her knees, through the cage of her ribs. Gideon's head snapped up. Asha took one step out of the alcove. And then the serac wall, half a kilometer up-valley, shed its first argument: a block the size of a house calving off the ice cliff and detonating against the glacier tongue, the boom rolling down the valley and slapping back off the walls, ice dust blooming white against the blue.

Gideon was already counting under his breath. Mirela realized he was timing the interval between events.

"That's early," he said. "Calving season is weeks out. Unless—" He stopped. Looked at the bent stake. Looked at the surging ice. Looked at her, and his face told her the conclusion before his mouth did.

"Unless the surge is accelerating," Mirela whispered. "The compression is feeding it. The instability is feeding *itself*."

Another crack, deeper, longer. Somewhere in the wall, a fracture was propagating with a sound like a giant tearing linen.

Gideon shouldered the green box. "We leave. Now. With everything." He paused at the alcove's edge, and Mirela saw him glance up-valley — toward the bend, toward the country beyond the constriction, toward whatever and whoever waited at the end of a trail of codes laid by a man who was supposed to be dead.

Because that was the thing the cache had confirmed and none of them had said aloud: Baral's notebook ended eight days ago. The transmissions had been recent. Someone was still alive up there, past the hazard zone, past the calving wall — someone keeping Devi's secrets, or sharing them.

And the ice, which had kept those secrets for three years, had just begun, at last, to let them go.
