## 15. Devi's Tent

The cairns stopped arguing with us an hour before we found him.

That was the thing about Devi's codes — they never lied, but they sometimes went quiet, the way a man goes quiet when he hears footsteps on the stairs and needs to decide whether to answer the door. The last marker stood at the lip of a blue-ice hollow where the glacier had folded in on itself like a fist closing around something it meant to keep. Beyond it, the valley narrowed to a throat of seracs, and the codes simply ended. Not faded. Ended. A full stop written in stone.

"He stopped," Mirela said. She had her notebook out, the decoded sequence transcribed in her small, exact hand. "Here. The route just terminates."

"Routes don't terminate." I crouched beside the final cairn, touching the stones. They were fitted, not stacked — each one chosen for shape, seated against its neighbor so wind couldn't pull them apart. Three years of weather and they still held their line. "They get picked up again somewhere else. He didn't stop building. He stopped *showing*."

Asha stood a little apart, her hood back despite the cold, watching the ice wall at the valley's head with an expression I couldn't read. She'd been like that since dawn — present and elsewhere at once, a woman keeping two ledgers in her head.

"You think he knew someone would come," she said.

"I think he built a road for no one for three years, and then one day decided the road was finished." I straightened. My knee cracked, loud as a rifle shot in the still air. "Which means either he gave up—"

"—or he got what he needed," Mirela finished.

Or he heard us coming, I didn't say. Or he'd been listening to our boots on his moraine for two days, reading our pace the way you read a stranger's handwriting, deciding whether the people following your secret road were rescue or reckoning.

We moved into the narrows single file. The ice rose on both sides, old compression ridges standing like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down here to die and hadn't finished. The light came down blue and grainy, sifted through cloud, and every sound arrived twice — once from the source, once from the walls, so that our own breathing seemed to travel with us, a third and fourth member of the party.

It was Asha who saw the smoke.

Not smoke, exactly. The ghost of it — a thread of gray so thin it might have been cloud-shadow, rising from behind a serac wall maybe four hundred meters on. Woodsmoke. Which meant fuel, which meant supply, which meant a fire tended daily by hands that gathered wood in a valley where nothing grew.

Mirela saw it too. Her breath went out of her all at once, and she grabbed my sleeve. "Gideon. Gideon, is that—"

"Don't say it yet." I don't know why I said that. Some animal part of me wanted the camp to be anything else — a survey team's cache, a herder's shelter, a trick of light. Because if there was a living man behind that ice wall, then everything I had carried for three years changed weight. Not less. Different. A load you've learned to walk with, suddenly repacked.

We approached the way I'd have approached a wounded bear — wide, slow, downwind of nothing because there was no downwind in a place like this, visible from every angle and pretending not to be. And that was when I understood how good he was. The camp didn't reveal itself gradually. It revealed itself all at once, the way a face comes out of shadow when the lamp turns: a tent pitched in a hollow scooped from the lee of the serac, guyed with cordage spun from something synthetic and salvaged, storm-flaps lashed flat. A solar panel angled on a rock cairn, glass crazed but functional. A cache wall of fitted slabs, the kind of masonry you see in thousand-year-old villages. And everywhere, everywhere, the codes — scratched into the ice, painted in raddle on stone, laid out in pebbles in patterns I could read the way you read your own name in frost on a window.

He had been writing to himself. Or writing to the mountain. Or writing to me, across three years of silence, in the only language we had ever truly shared.

The tent flap moved.

I want to tell you I felt triumph. That some part of me shouted *I knew it, I knew you were alive.* What I actually felt was colder and more complicated: the specific vertigo of a man who has built his whole exile on a foundation and now watches the foundation open its eyes.

The flap lifted, and Devi Thapa came out of his tent into the blue light, and he was alive.

He was also half-blind. I saw that immediately — the frost scarring across both cheeks, the lids swollen and rimmed white, the way his head turned past me and then back, hunting, focusing with effort, like a man reading a page by matchlight. His beard was iron-gray and untrimmed. His hands, when they came up to shield eyes that no longer worked right, were scarred to the wrist, the nails thick and yellowed. He wore layers of patched expedition gear — gear I recognized, gear I had helped carry, gear that should have been buried under the ice below the Larkya col three years ago along with the man wearing it.

He looked at me for a long time. The wind moved the thin smoke above his stove pipe. Somewhere deep in the glacier, with a sound like a distant door, the ice settled.

"Gideon," he said.

My name. Not the borrowed one. Not the name on the forged permits or the trekking agency's roster. My name, spoken in a voice roughened by three years of kerosene fumes and glacier wind, and it went through me like a pick through crust.

"You're supposed to be dead," I said. It came out flat, almost conversational, which surprised me. There was a whole ocean of feeling under it and somehow only this one sentence made it to the surface.

"Yes." He said it without drama. He said it the way you'd confirm a delivery address. "You're supposed to be in Chile. You always said Chile, if it ever went wrong. You said nobody looks south."

Behind me, Mirela made a sound — small, strangled. Asha did not make any sound at all, and when I glanced back at her, her face was doing something I had no category for: grief and vindication holding each other up, two drunks leaning on the same lamppost.

*Chile.* He remembered. Three years in the ice, half-blind, alone, and he remembered a joke I'd made over cardamom tea in a Kathmandu teahouse, and I felt something crack sideways in my chest, because that was the cruelty of Devi — he always remembered everything, and he had always known exactly which memories to spend.

"How long have you known I was coming?" I asked.

"Since the first cairn moved." He turned his ruined eyes toward the valley mouth. "Nobody moves my stones. I check them. Every week, even in winter, especially in winter. Week before last, the third cairn on the moraine — one stone rotated. Wind doesn't rotate stones. So I started leaving the road open instead of closing it." He smiled, and the smile was the worst thing yet, because it was Devi's smile, warm and crooked, unchanged, the smile of the man who had taught me to read avalanche slopes, worn now on the face of a man who had let the world bury him. "And then I watched you climb the headwall in bad light, taking the left line, because you always take the left line even when the right line is better, and I thought — either I'm dreaming, or my brother has finally come to arrest me."

"Arrest you." Mirela stepped forward. Her voice shook, but her spine didn't; whatever else the government had made of her, it had made her stubborn. "Devi Thapa. Do you understand what your death did? Do you know what it cost?" She pulled the folded report from her jacket — the falsified one, the one that had ended me — and held it up between them like a warrant. "This report. This lie. It put the blame on conditions, and the inquiry needed someone to blame for the *blame*, and they took Gideon apart. Two countries. Both registries. Erased. He signed his name to protect you and they fed him into the machine for it."

"I know what it cost." Still no drama. That was the horror of him — three years alone had burned the performance out of him entirely, and what was left spoke in the level voice of a man reporting instrument readings. "I've had three years to do the arithmetic. I did it every night. I'm very good at arithmetic now. There isn't much else to do."

"Then why?" The question tore out of me before I could shape it, before I could decide whether I wanted the answer. "Why let me carry it? One letter, Devi. One message down-valley, one word passed to any herder, any pilgrim, anyone — *tell the Zhou man I'm alive.* I would have walked here. I would have walked here barefoot. You let them take my name instead."

The wind gusted, flattening the smoke, and in the sudden clarity of the air I watched him weigh the truth the way he used to weigh snowpack — pressing, testing, listening for the layer that would fail.

"Because I ran," he said.

"From the avalanche?"

"From the *report*." He lowered himself onto a supply crate with the care of a much older man, and I realized his legs were bad too, the frost having taken its toll below the knees, the way it takes payment from everyone who stays too long above the lines. "Sit. All of you. This takes longer than the forty hours you think you have — yes, I know about the calving window, I've watched eleven of them, I know exactly when the wall opens and closes, and I'll give you the timing later, and you're going to hate my timing, so sit."

We sat. Even Asha sat, folding down onto a stone at the edge of the camp with her hands wrapped around her knees, and I noticed she had positioned herself where she could see both Devi's face and mine, and I filed that away with all the other things about Asha I was filing away.

"The night of the avalanche," Devi began, "you were right. About everything. The slope was loaded. The temperature curve was wrong. I told the clients the route was safe because I wanted the summit fee, because my mother's roof needed replacing, because I was thirty-one years old and immortal the way all guides are immortal until the mountain files its paperwork." His ruined eyes found me, or near enough. "When it came down, I wasn't buried. I was thrown clear into the debris fan on the far side. Broken ribs, broken wrist, one eye already burning from the ice blast. And I lay there in the dark and I heard the searchers, and I heard them calling both our names, and I thought — they will ask us what happened. They will ask us, and I will have to say *I overruled you*, and my mother will hear it, and the clients' families will hear it, and everything I am will end on that slope. Not from the ice. From the *sentence*."

"So you crawled away." My voice sounded like someone else's.

"I crawled away. Down the far drainage, the one that isn't mapped, the one I found because I'd spent six years learning every unmapped thing in these ranges while other men learned to file reports." He gestured at the valley, the seracs, the fitted stones, the whole impossible architecture of his survival. "And then the report came out — your report — and I heard it repeated in the valleys, that the guide Zhou had blamed conditions to cover his own routing error, and I understood what you'd done, and I understood that if I ever surfaced, your lie would die with me exposed. Your sacrifice only works if I stay dead. You built me a coffin out of your own career, Gideon. The decent thing — the *only* thing — was to lie in it."

"That wasn't the bargain," I said. "The bargain was you'd be dead. Dead men don't need coffins maintained. Dead men don't—" My voice failed and restarted. "Three years, Devi. I measured my life in miles because years were unbearable. Do you know what that is? Do you know what you took?"

"I know exactly what I took." For the first time, his level voice bent. Just slightly. A hairline fracture in the instrument reading. "I took your name because you gave it, and I took your future because you handed it to me, and I have sat in this ice hole for three years telling myself that was mercy — yours and mine both — and every winter I have believed it a little less." He reached into the tent and brought out something wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it with fingers that trembled only at the very end of the motion. A radio. Old, military-surplus, hand-cranked. Beside it, a battered notebook whose cover had been rewritten so many times it was more tape than paper.

"What's this?" Mirela asked, though her hands were already reaching.

"The reason you really came," Devi said. "Don't insult each other. The survey team didn't wander. They were looking for this valley, because someone in your ministry sold them the idea that there was data here worth suppressing — and there is. There has been for three years. I've been recording it." He tapped the notebook. "Calving cycles. Meltwater pulses. Basal readings taken with instruments I repaired myself. The glacier is dying faster than any model says, and when it goes, it goes into the river systems that feed four districts. I wrote it all down in the only code I trusted — ours — because I learned three years ago what happens to reports written plainly in this country. They get edited."

The valley was silent except for the ice, ticking and settling, counting down to something.

"So the missing team," Mirela said slowly, "the intact gear, the no footprints—"

"They found my outer caches. I watched them take the data copies I'd staged — I *gave* them the staging points, in code, because I wanted the information to leave this valley in official hands. Then their radio went quiet, and I don't know why, and that frightens me more than the calving does." He turned toward me, and the blind-man's focus of him was terrible, precise, aimed. "But that's the next problem. Here is the one in front of us." He closed his scarred hand over the notebook. "Everything I just told you — the running, the report, the three years — it stays in this valley unless you choose otherwise. You protected me once with a lie. I'm asking you to do it again. One more season. Get the data out first, testify after, control the story before it controls us. Because if the truth comes out tangled, it won't free you, Gideon. It will bury us both, and this time the mountain won't get the credit."

I looked at the notebook. At the radio. At the face of the dead man who had lived three years on the interest of my sacrifice and was now asking for the principal.

Behind him, at the edge of the camp, Asha had gone very still, and she was looking at Devi the way you look at water in a desert — and Devi, blind as he was, turned his head toward her anyway, as if the quality of her silence had a direction.

"And you," he said softly. "You I know. Even with the eyes gone, I'd know that breathing anywhere." 

The glacier groaned, long and low, and high on the ice wall, with a crack like the sky splitting, the calving season opened early.

* * *

The first block fell while we were still staring at each other.

It went with a sound I will never manage to describe honestly — part thunder, part gunshot, part the tearing of something too large to tear — and the ice wall at the valley's head shrugged off a slab the size of a village house and sent it cartwheeling onto the lower glacier in a bloom of powder that rolled toward us as a white wind, stinging, tasting of stone.

"Forty hours," I said automatically, the guide's arithmetic running whether I wanted it or not.

"Thirty-six," Devi corrected. "Maybe less. It's opening from the top down this year. That's wrong. That's new." He was already moving, blind and limping and faster than he had any right to be, hauling the notebook and the radio into a salvage bag with hands that knew every object in that camp by touch. "Pack nothing you love. We may be running lighter than we planned."

Mirela had the report clutched to her chest. Asha hadn't moved from her stone. And I stood in the falling powder of the first calving of the season, three years of exile and one hour of truth behind me, understanding that whatever I chose next — the lie again, or the name, or the man — I was going to have to choose it at a run.
