## 16. Loyalty vs. Truth

The tent smelled of kerosene and three years of solitude.

Gideon sat on a crate of salvaged survey equipment, his hands still shaking from the crossing of the icefall, and watched Devi move through the small space like a man who had memorized every inch of it — because he had. Devi's movements were economical, blind-man economical: fingers trailing the guyline, hip finding the crate's corner without looking, the kettle lifted from its hook by touch alone. Half his face was a map of frost-scarred tissue, shiny and bloodless, and his left eye had gone the pale gray of glacier ice. The right one still worked, and it was fixed on Gideon with an intensity that made the small tent feel smaller.

"You look old," Devi said.

"You look dead."

"I did my best." Devi poured water into the kettle with the unerring precision of long practice and set it on the stove. "Three years of doing my best at being dead. It's harder work than guiding, I'll tell you that for nothing."

Outside, Mirela and Asha were digging the data cache out of the ice shelf where Devi had hidden it — or where the survey team had hidden it before they vanished, Gideon still wasn't sure which story was true, and neither, he suspected, was Devi anymore. The wind had dropped after the calving began, as if the mountain were holding its breath between performances. In the silence, the glacier spoke in long, deep groans, the sound of something enormous shifting its weight.

"Say it," Devi said quietly. "Whatever you came here to say. Say it now, while the kettle boils, because after that we'll both be too polite."

Gideon looked at him. Three years he had carried this man's name like a stone sewn into his coat. Three years of signing reports that said *conditions*, saying *conditions* to inquiry boards, watching the word *conditions* get translated into *Zhou's negligence* somewhere above his pay grade, until two countries' registries spat him out like a pit. He had done it because Devi was dead and the dead cannot defend themselves, and because a lie told for a dead friend felt, at the time, like the only honest thing left to do.

"You led them onto the Larkya," Gideon said. "Against my warnings. Against the forecast. And I signed your name out of the report and put mine in its place."

Devi didn't flinch. That was the worst part. He'd expected — he'd needed — some flinch, some crack in the scarred face, and there was none. Devi just nodded slowly, the way he used to nod when a client asked a stupid question about altitude, patient and faintly sad.

"Yes," he said. "And then you spent three years paying for it, and I spent three years watching you pay for it, and neither of us has ever once said thank you, so let me say it now." His voice caught, or the wind through the tent fabric made it seem so. "Thank you, Gideon. It was the maddest, kindest, stupidest thing anyone has ever done for me."

"It wasn't kindness." The words came out flat and cold, colder than he intended, and he let them stay that way. "It was cowardice. I couldn't stand for your mother to read what you did. I couldn't stand for the village to say your name and spit. So I gave them mine instead. That's not kindness, Devi. That's just a different way of running away."

The kettle began to sing. Devi took it off the flame without hurrying, poured two cups, handed one over. Their fingers touched on the tin. Devi's hand was a lattice of old burns and newer cuts, the hand of a man who had survived alone on a glacier for three years, and Gideon found himself unable to let go of the cup.

"So don't forgive me," Devi said. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you for something much smaller and much harder." He sat down across from Gideon, knees almost touching in the cramped space, and the working eye held him steady. "I'm asking you to do it again. One more time. Keep the secret."

* * *

"No," Gideon said.

"You haven't heard—"

"I know what you're going to ask. You're going to ask me to bury all of it. The suppression, the falsified report, the survey data, you being alive. All of it, back under the snow, one more winter, one more year, forever." He set the cup down untouched. "No."

"Gideon." Devi leaned forward. "My mother is alive. You know she is. She lights a butter lamp for me every morning. Do you understand what it does to her? Nothing. Nothing, because her son died clean. Died a hero, even — the guide who was killed by the mountain, poor Devi, such bad luck, such conditions." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, half his face unable to join the other half. "If I walk out of this valley, what is she lighting lamps for? A liar. A man who got four people lost because he wanted one more season's fee. Every prayer she says becomes a prayer for a fraud. You wouldn't be giving her back a son. You'd be taking away the only version of me she can live with."

"And the survey team?" Gideon's voice rose, and he heard Mirela's voice under his own, furious and precise. "Four people walked into this valley following your cairns, Devi. Your codes. They're missing. Maybe dead. And their cartographer crossed a country to find them, and what she found instead is a hazard zone the government has known about for years and buried — buried the same way my report was buried, same style, same signatures, maybe the same office. This isn't your secret anymore. It stopped being yours the moment other people started disappearing inside it."

"They're not dead."

Both of them turned. Asha stood in the tent doorway, snow in her hair, her face unreadable in the flat light. Behind her, Mirela was hauling the last of the cache up the slope, a waterproof case nearly as long as she was tall, moving with the grim efficiency of a woman who had decided grief could wait until the paperwork was done.

"The survivor," Asha said. "I've been tracking the signal since the river. It's weak, but it's moving. Slowly. Toward the lower moraine." She looked at Devi, and something passed between them that Gideon couldn't read — something older than the Kathmandu night, older than the lies. "You knew that, didn't you? That's why you kept laying codes. Not to hide. To leave a trail someone could follow out."

Devi said nothing. But he didn't deny it, and in Devi's silence Gideon heard the shape of the truth: three years of cairns, laid by a blind half-dead ghost, marking not a hiding place but an escape route — for whoever might someday need one. The same instinct that had made him a great guide. The same instinct that had killed him, or should have.

"Even if they live," Devi said finally, "it changes nothing. The report stays buried. I stay dead. You go back to being no one, and in a year none of this will have happened to anyone but us."

"And the glacier?" Gideon pointed west, toward the calving wall, where a plume of spray stood up against the gray sky like smoke from a distant war. "That valley below the ice is a summer pasture, Devi. Herders use it. There are two villages downstream of the meltwater. If the government's own data says the wall is destabilizing — and Mirela says it does — then every season you keep that secret is a season those villages don't know they're living under a gun. How many butter lamps is that worth?"

* * *

Mirela came in with the case and set it down between them like a defendant on a table. She was breathing hard from the climb, her lips cracked with altitude, and she looked at Devi the way Gideon imagined she looked at bad maps: measuring, unsentimental, unwilling to be lied to twice.

"This is the survey team's cache," she said. "Found exactly where your cairns said it would be — which means either you hid it, or you found it first, and either way you've known what's in it." She knelt and opened the latches. Inside, wrapped in plastic, were hard drives, paper notebooks, and a sealed envelope with a government stamp. "Do you want to tell him, or shall I?"

"He knows," Devi said.

"I want to hear him say it," Mirela said. "Because I've spent eleven days thinking my team was taken by weather. Weather I can grieve. What I'm looking at is a decision. Someone decided this data shouldn't exist, and someone else decided my team shouldn't have collected it, and I would very much like to know which someone you are."

Devi looked at Gideon. And Gideon saw it happen — saw the old fluency come into his friend's face, the gift that had made clients trust him on rotten ice, the ability to make any listener feel that the dangerous thing was reasonable. He watched Devi build the argument, sentence by sentence, and recognized every joint in its construction because he had built the same argument himself, three years ago, alone, at a desk, with a pen.

"The ministry contracted our survey," Devi said. "Two seasons ago. They wanted the Larkya basin mapped properly because they suspected what the data confirmed: the calving wall is retreating faster than any model predicted. Within five years, maybe less, the meltwater will threaten the lower valley. That's not a report you publish. That's a relocation order. Compensation claims. International attention. Ministers who approved the original surveys going to prison." He spread his burned hands. "So they buried it. And when the survey team started asking why their findings weren't being acknowledged, they pulled the contract, cut the radio authorization, and left them to walk out on their own. My codes were already on the mountain. They followed them in. They couldn't follow them out — the calving opened late that year. I found two of them alive. I've been keeping them alive since. The other two—" His voice failed for the first time. "The ice took the other two before I reached them."

Gideon closed his eyes. Four people. Two alive because a dead man had been stubborn enough to keep living. Two dead because a government had preferred a secret to a warning.

"The falsified report," Mirela said. "Three years ago. The Zhou report. Was that the same office?"

"What difference does it make?"

"Because if it's the same office, then it isn't a coincidence, it's a method. And methods have names, and names can be prosecuted." She turned to Gideon. "He's asking you to keep the secret. I'm telling you what the secret costs. Not you. Them." She nodded toward the west, toward the villages, toward the world. "You've paid enough for other people's secrets, Mr. Zhou. I watched you cross that icefall today carrying a man who let you carry his disgrace for three years. I'm not asking you to hate him. I'm asking you to count."

* * *

They left him alone with Devi. Or perhaps Devi sent them out; afterward Gideon could never remember which. The stove ticked as it cooled. Outside, the calving went on — great percussive cracks, each one rolling down the valley like a door slamming in a house with a thousand doors.

Devi refilled the tea neither of them had drunk and sat holding his cup in both hands, warming them, though the tent was not cold.

"When we were nineteen," Devi said, "you told me you'd never lie to me. On the Annapurna approach, that night it hailed and we sheltered under the truck. You said a guide's whole job is seeing clearly and saying what he sees. You said everything else — the charm, the stories, the confidence — everything else is decoration on top of that one thing." He looked up, and the ice-colored eye caught the lantern light. "Was that true? Or was that decoration too?"

"It was true."

"Then look at me and see clearly, and tell me what you see."

Gideon looked. He saw the boy under the truck, hail drumming on the tarpaulin, both of them laughing too loudly to cover how frightened they were. He saw the man who had led a commercial group onto a rotten glacier for a fee, because pride and poverty make the same arguments. He saw a ghost who had spent three years turning his grave into a lighthouse. He saw his best friend begging him, with his whole ruined face, to keep being the kind of man who lies beautifully for the people he loves.

And he saw, superimposed over all of it, his own name — his real name, Gideon Zhou, the one he hadn't written down in three years — sitting in a registry file marked *revoked*, next to a report he had signed with a lie, next to a truth he had traded away like it was his to trade.

"If I keep it," Gideon said slowly, "I'm not protecting your mother. I'm protecting myself. There's a version of me that keeps this secret because the truth is expensive and I've already paid so much, and I don't want to pay again. That version gets to call it loyalty." He turned the cup in his hands. "But loyalty to what? To you? You're alive. Loyalty doesn't require your death, Devi. Only your lie does."

"They'll take my restitution," Devi whispered. "Everything. And yours — you'll never guide legally again, not anywhere, not ever, if this comes out tied to your name. You'll be the guide who lied twice."

"I was never going to guide legally again anyway. That's the thing you've never understood." Gideon heard his own voice go quiet and steady, the voice he used on clients when the route turned serious. "They erased me whether or not I kept your secret. Erasure doesn't check its arithmetic. I've been no one for three years, and no one has no more name to lose." He stood. His legs ached; the icefall had taken something out of them that would not be returned. "But the villages downstream have names. Mirela's team has names. The two people you kept alive have names. And I am done spending other people's names to protect mine."

Devi bowed his head. For a long time the only sound was the glacier, counting its doors.

"She'll stop lighting the lamps," he said at last, barely audible. "That's what I can't survive, Gideon. Not the prison. Not the shame. Waking up in her house and watching her understand that every morning for three years she talked to a liar."

"Then don't wake up in her house." Gideon crouched, took his friend's scarred face in both hands, and made him look up. "Wake up in a courtroom. Tell it there, first, to strangers, where it counts. Let the truth come out of your mouth before it comes off a page. That's the only version of this she might someday forgive — the one where her son walked out of the ice and confessed, instead of the one where two men dug him up and displayed him." He released him. "You taught me the mountains don't care whose fault it is. They only care whether you read them honestly. So read this honestly, Devi. One last route. The hardest one."

Devi's breath shuddered out of him. When he raised his head, the working eye was wet, and the dead one gleamed like a coin at the bottom of a well.

"There's something else," he said. "Something you need to know before you decide anything. About the night in Kathmandu. About Asha. About what you promised me when the fever had you." He reached beneath his sleeping mat and drew out a folded paper, soft as cloth with handling, and held it out. "You made a promise, Gideon. You just don't remember making it. And I've been hiding behind your forgetting ever since."

Gideon took the paper. Outside, the calving wall thundered — closer now, much closer — and the light in the tent flickered as the ground shivered beneath them.

From the slope above, Asha's voice came down sharp and urgent: "The exit route. It's opening early. We have hours, not days — and the survivor's signal just changed direction. It's heading for the ice wall."
