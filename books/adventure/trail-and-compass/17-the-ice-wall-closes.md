## 17. The Ice Wall Closes

The mountain spoke first.

Not with thunder — thunder was too human a word for it. What came down the valley at dawn was a sound like the earth clearing its throat, a deep tectonic groan that Asha felt in her sternum before she heard it with her ears. She was standing outside the cave shelter with a cup of tea gone cold in her hands when the far wall of the glacier calved, and a block of ice the size of a village church slid into the meltwater lake below and vanished without so much as a splash reaching them.

Gideon was beside her in an instant. He didn't ask what it was. He just stood there, listening the way other men listened to conversations, his head tilted, his breath making small ghosts in the freezing air.

"That's early," he said.

"The season's early." Asha set down the cup. "Or the glacier is."

Behind them, inside the cave, Mirela was spreading the cache documents across a flat stone, photographing each page with the methodical patience of a woman building an indictment one frame at a time. And deeper in the shadow, wrapped in every layer they could spare, Devi sat propped against the rock with his ruined eyes turned toward the light he could no longer properly see, listening to everything, saying nothing.

Asha had known this moment would come. She had rehearsed it on the boat, on the moraine, in a hundred sleepless hours of bailing water and watching Gideon sleep. She had composed speeches. Elegant ones. Ones that explained everything in order — the contract, the verification job, the months after it ended when she kept going anyway, the ledger of small observations that had become something else entirely.

None of those speeches survived the sound the glacier made.

Because now there was no time left for elegance. The ice wall was closing, and whatever she said in the next hour would be the last version of herself these people ever knew.

* * *

They gathered at the mouth of the cave while Gideon laid out the route on Mirela's map, weighting the corners with stones. His finger traced the line up the valley: the hidden ramp behind the serac field, the traverse under the hanging glacier, the notch in the rock wall that opened onto the pass. Forty hours, he'd said last night. Now, hearing the calving accelerate, he revised it aloud.

"Thirty. Maybe less. When it starts going like this, it doesn't stop politely."

"And if we're still inside when it stops?" Mirela asked, though Asha could see from her face that she already knew.

"Then the valley seals," Gideon said. "For a year. Maybe longer. The exit route comes apart in sections — I watched it happen once, three years ago, from the wrong side." He paused. "Devi watched it with me."

Asha saw Devi's head turn toward the sound of his own name, the way a man turns toward a voice calling from another room.

"We go today," Gideon said. "All of us. We move fast, we don't stop, and nobody argues with me about the route."

Mirela began rolling the map. Asha didn't move.

"Gideon."

He looked up. Something in her voice must have reached him, because he sent Mirela ahead to pack, and then it was just the two of them at the cave mouth, the glacier groaning below like a ship breaking up in the dark.

"You have something to say," he said. "Say it walking."

"Not this." She pulled the folded paper from the inner pocket of her jacket — the one thing she'd carried through the river, the checkpoint, the cold nights, closer than her own pulse. She'd told him part of the truth on the boat, when he caught her copying his route notes. Hired to verify whether *the guide who lied* was still alive. She'd let him believe that was all of it. It wasn't even half.

She held out the paper. He took it, unfolded it, and she watched his eyes move across the lines — not the route codes, not coordinates, but a family record written in careful Devanagari, a birth certificate older than either of them, softened at the folds from years of being carried and read and refolded.

His face did something complicated. "This is—"

"Read the names."

He read them. She watched him arrive at the second one, the way you watch a man step off a ledge.

"Asha Devi Thapa," he said slowly. "That's—"

"My name. My real one. I stopped using it because everyone who used it asked me where my brother was, and I got tired of having no answer that wasn't a lie." Her voice stayed level. She'd practiced that too. "Devi Bahadur Thapa. Born in Salleri, in the monsoon, two years before me. My mother said the midwife had to break the door open because the storm had sealed the house. He came into the world through a gap in a wall." She almost smiled. "It should have been a warning."

Gideon looked at the certificate. Then at her. Then back at the certificate, as though the paper might resolve into something less impossible.

"You said you were hired."

"I was hired. That part was true. People tied to the survey contract wanted to know if the guide who signed the false report was alive, and where, and whether he talked. I took the money because it pointed me at the last place anyone had seen my brother's trail partner." She swallowed. "I found you instead. And then the job ended, and I kept going, and I told myself it was because I needed your codes, your habits, your memory of the avalanche. But that was never it, Gideon. I was looking for Devi. I've been looking for Devi for three years. You were just the only door that led anywhere."

Below them, the glacier calved again — twice in quick succession, the reports rolling up the valley like artillery. Ice dust rose off the serac field in a pale curtain.

"He's alive," Gideon said. Not a question. He'd seen the tent; he'd carried the man's weight in his arms since. But he said it anyway, and Asha understood that he was saying it for her — handing her the fact like something fragile, testing whether she could hold it.

"I know he's alive. I've known since the radio transmission. His voice. I'd know it through a wall of static, through a wall of stone." Her composure cracked there, just slightly, a hairline fracture running through the level tone. "Do you know what it's like? To hear a dead man reading coordinates? To spend three years grieving someone and then find out grief was the wrong word for what you were doing?"

"No," Gideon said quietly. "But I spent three years carrying guilt for a man who wasn't dead. So maybe we're even."

"Nothing about this is even." She took the certificate back from him, folded it along its worn creases. "I'm not telling you this so we can compare wounds. I'm telling you because of what happens next."

"Which is?"

"We leave together. All four of us. Or I don't leave."

* * *

He stared at her, and she watched him do the arithmetic she'd already done — the arithmetic of the route, the pace, the weight of a half-blind frost-scarred man against thirty shrinking hours. She watched him arrive at the number that didn't work.

"Asha—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't give me the calculation. I've done it. I know what he costs us. I know every kilo of him is an hour off our margin, and I know the ice doesn't care that he's my brother." She stepped closer. "But hear me clearly, Gideon Zhou, because I will only say this once. I hired myself onto this search. I forged papers that shouldn't exist. I floated a leaking boat down a river into restricted territory. I lied to a government cartographer and stole notes from a blacklisted guide, and I would do all of it again, worse, if it put me in this valley today. I did not cross two borders and three years of silence to walk out of here with an empty pack and a story."

The wind came down off the ice wall and pushed between them, carrying the smell of crushed ice and old stone.

"He may not be able to walk fast," Gideon said. "He may not be able to walk at all by the traverse."

"Then I'll carry him."

"On that terrain? Under the seracs? With the wall coming down?" He shook his head, but it wasn't refusal — she was learning to read him, and what she saw was a man fighting the same stubbornness in himself that he was looking at in her. "You'll die. Both of you. Possibly all four of us."

"Then we die knowing," she said. "Which is more than I had yesterday."

Something moved behind his eyes — that flicker she'd learned meant he was remembering, though she couldn't guess what. The avalanche. Kathmandu. Some promise made in fever that she remembered and he didn't. She thought about telling him that too — *you swore, that night, that if Devi were ever alive you'd bring him home, you made me swear I'd hold you to it* — but there was no time, and some debts were better collected than explained.

"Mirela needs to know," he said finally.

"Mirela already suspects. She watches me the way she watches contour lines — for what doesn't fit." Asha glanced back into the cave, where Devi sat listening to their voices with the terrible attentiveness of the blind. "And Devi knows. He's known since the boat. He heard my voice say his childhood nickname and pretended he hadn't, because he's a coward about exactly one thing in this world, and it's me."

Gideon almost smiled. "Family."

"Family," she agreed. "Love as stubbornness. That's the only definition I've ever trusted."

* * *

They told Mirela first, quickly, without ceremony — Asha laying out the facts the way she'd lay out instruments, flat and functional, refusing to dress them up. Mirela listened with her jaw tight, glancing between Asha and the cache documents spread on the stone, and Asha could see the cartographer recalculating everything: the papers at the checkpoint, the precise knowledge, the months of unexplained persistence.

"You compromised this expedition from the start," Mirela said.

"I completed it. Your team is found. Your data is recovered. The report that buried Gideon is sitting on that stone in photographs." Asha met her eyes. "Judge me after we're off the ice."

Mirela held the stare for a long moment. Then she began packing the documents with brisk, angry efficiency, which Asha chose to interpret as agreement.

Devi was harder.

She knelt in front of him in the cave's dimness, close enough that he could find her hands with his, and she felt the frost-scarred fingers close around hers — stronger than they looked, stubborn to the last cell.

"I heard," he said. "Most of it. The walls are thin and you shout when you're scared."

"I wasn't shouting."

"You were absolutely shouting." His ruined eyes crinkled. It was the first time she'd seen anything like humor in his face, and it undid her more thoroughly than grief would have. "Asha. Little sister. Go with them."

"No."

"The route won't hold my weight. Gideon knows it. You know it. Three people move fast enough. Four people die beautifully, and beautiful deaths are just stupid deaths with scenery."

"Then we'll be stupid together, the way we were children. Remember the river behind the house? Mother said stay out, and you went in, and I went in after you, and we both nearly drowned, and do you know what she said afterward?" Asha gripped his hands harder. "She said, *at least you went in after him.* That was the whole lecture. That was the entire moral. At least you went in after him."

"This isn't a river."

"No. This is a glacier, and my brother is sitting in it waiting to be left behind, and I am telling you for the last time in whatever language remains to us: I am not leaving without you. Tie me to a rock if you want. I'll chew through the rope. I've been chewing through things for three years."

Devi was silent for a long moment. Below them the ice boomed, a long tearing roar, and dust sifted down from the cave ceiling like flour.

"You always were the stubborn one," he said at last. "Mother blamed the midwife."

"She was right to."

He exhaled — surrender or laughter, with him the two had always sounded alike. "Then we go together. But Asha—" His grip tightened until it hurt. "If it comes to choosing on the ice, you choose yourself. Promise me. Promise me the way I never promised anyone anything true."

"No," Asha said gently. "I don't make promises I intend to break. You taught me that too — by example, three years of silence."

* * *

They broke camp in twenty minutes. Gideon moved through the preparations like a man conducting an orchestra he'd memorized — rope coiled, loads balanced, the route recited one final time in his low, certain voice: ramp, traverse, notch, pass. Mirela sealed the photographs in waterproof sleeves against her chest. Devi stood, swaying, and accepted the rope harness without complaint, which from him was its own kind of eloquence.

At the cave mouth, Asha paused and looked back down the valley — at the shattered white plain, the pale curtain of dust still hanging over the serac field, the dark seam of the hazard zone where the truth had been buried in a steel box for three years.

The ice wall at the valley's head was calving in earnest now, great slabs leaning out over emptiness like doors deciding whether to close.

"Thirty hours," Gideon said beside her. "Maybe."

"Then we'd better be brilliant," Asha said, and stepped out onto the ice.

They had covered perhaps two kilometers, moving in Gideon's rhythm — fast, efficient, the route codes unfolding beneath their feet like sentences in a language only he was fluent in — when the mountain changed its mind.

The crack came from above and to the left, high on the hanging glacier, a sound like the sky splitting along a seam. Asha looked up in time to see the serac field shudder, a whole city block of blue ice lean forward, and begin, with horrible unhurried grace, to fall.

"MOVE!" Gideon's voice tore across the ice. "OFF THE RAMP — NOW — RUN!"

They ran. The world behind them became noise — a white avalanche of sound and powder chasing them down the slope, swallowing the ramp they'd just climbed, erasing the route behind them section by section like a sentence being deleted. Asha shoved Devi ahead of her with both hands, screaming words she couldn't hear over the roar, and the four of them burst out onto the lower traverse just as the ice settled into silence and dust.

When the air cleared, Gideon stood staring back the way they'd come.

The ramp was gone. The route behind them — the only way back to the cave, the supplies, the shelter — was gone. Ahead, the traverse ran narrow and white toward the notch, and above it the ice wall leaned, groaning, shedding smaller pieces like a man tearing up a letter as he walks.

"There's no retreat," Mirela said quietly.

"There's only forward," Gideon said. And then his voice dropped, and Asha saw him look at the wall, and at Devi, and at her, and make some private decision he didn't share. "Everyone stays roped. Nobody stops. We reach the notch before nightfall or we learn what the inside of this valley looks like for a year."

Asha tightened Devi's harness with hands that did not shake, because she had decided they wouldn't.

"Forward," she said. "Together. All four."

Above them, the ice wall answered with a long, grinding groan — and somewhere in that sound, Asha thought she heard the mountain asking the same question she'd been carrying for three years:

How much are you willing to lose to bring him home?

She stepped onto the traverse, and did not look back again.
