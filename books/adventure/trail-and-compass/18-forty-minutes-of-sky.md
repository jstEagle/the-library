## 18. Forty Minutes of Sky

The ice wall began to speak at 4:12 in the morning.

I know the time because Mirela had her watch out, timing the intervals between booms the way she timed everything, as if the glacier were a document that could be cross-referenced. The first crack came from deep inside the seracs above the valley's throat — a sound like a giant knuckle popping, then a long groan, then silence, which was worse.

"That's the calving," Asha said. She stood at the mouth of our bivouac cave with her arms crossed against the cold, and in the blue pre-dawn light her face looked like it had been carved from the same ice we were about to cross. "It's early. I told you it would be early."

"You told us a lot of things," Mirela said.

"And you believed just enough of them to be here." Asha turned to me. "How long?"

I had been doing the calculation since the first boom, running it the way I used to run it for clients — not with hope, but with arithmetic. The calving season opened the route through the icefall at the valley's mouth, the hidden corridor the official maps didn't show, the one Devi had been marking with cairns for three years. It stayed open while the glacier shed its winter skin and the ice wall drew back from the lateral moraine. Then the seracs above the throat would finish their collapse, the debris would dam the passage, and the valley would seal itself for another year.

Forty hours, I had told them yesterday. Listening now — to the rhythm of the falls, the spacing tightening like a pulse climbing toward a sprint — I revised it.

"Less," I said. "The wall's accelerating. We cross now, or we don't cross at all."

Devi was already packing.

That was the thing about my brother-in-ropes that three years of ice had not changed: he moved when I moved, packed when I packed, fell into the old choreography as if the avalanche had been a long weekend and not a funeral. He had heard the number. He knew what it meant. He also knew what waited on the far side — inquiries, testimony, the slow machinery of truth — and he packed anyway, and I chose not to look too closely at his face while he did it, because some kindnesses are just postponements.

Mirela checked the data cache one last time — the drive with the suppressed hazard report, wrapped in three plastic bags and strapped against her spine like a second heart. Asha counted rope. I stood at the cave mouth and read the mountain.

That was the phrase Devi had used, years ago, teaching a younger version of himself the route codes. *The mountain is always talking, Gideon. The codes are just a way of writing down what it says.* He had meant it as a navigation trick — cairns stacked two-high versus three-high, stones angled left or right, a vocabulary of forty words that two men could read at a glance and no one else could parse. But standing there, listening to the seracs count down, I understood that we had always used it backward. The codes had been a way of hiding from the mountain — of moving through it on our own terms, fast and clever, outsmarting the ice. That was the arrogance that killed four clients and nearly killed the two of us. Devi had led that commercial group onto the glacier against every warning I gave him, because he believed his codes made him exempt. And I had signed the report that blamed the conditions, because I believed my silence made me loyal.

Now there was only one way across, and it was the honest way: to read what the ice was actually saying, in its own language, with no shortcuts.

"Rope order," I said. "Me, Mirela, Asha, Devi. Forty-meter spacing on the moraine, tighten to fifteen in the icefall. Nobody moves until the person ahead is past the marked line. We don't talk on the ice except in codes." I looked at each of them. "And when I say run, you run. Not toward me. Toward the far moraine. Whatever you hear behind you, you don't turn around."

"Gideon," Devi started.

"That's the price of crossing. All of it, or none of us goes."

He held my eyes for a long moment. Three years of frost had gotten into him somewhere deeper than the skin — his left eye drifted now, slightly, when he was tired, so that looking at him was like looking at two versions of the same man, one present and one a half-second behind. He nodded.

The light came up gray and sourceless, monsoon cloud pressing down on the valley like a lid. We moved.

* * *

The moraine was the easy part — a kilometer of loose stone and old ice, the river running high and brown on our left, carrying the glacier's melt toward the lowlands where it would become someone's drinking water, someone's flood. I kept us moving at a pace I would never have asked of clients, and they kept it, and I registered distantly that these were the strongest three people I had ever roped together: a cartographer who had crossed a country on a forged permit to find her team, a woman who had lied her way into our lives for reasons she still wouldn't fully name, and a dead man walking.

The icefall announced itself as a change in the air — colder, wetter, the smell of old pressure. Then the wall was around us.

I had crossed this throat twice in my life, both times with Devi, both times in the old way: fast, quiet, trusting the cairns. Now the cairns were there — Devi's three-year record of this passage, his secret map written in stone — and I read them the way I should have read the mountain three years ago. Not as permission. As testimony.

Two-high cairn, stones angled left: *stable shelf, but undercut — keep to the right side.* A three-high with a flat capstone: *crevasse bridge, tested once, weight limit two.* A cairn with its top stone split by frost: *this route dies in thaw — do not trust after June.* Devi had been honest in his stones. He had written the mountain down exactly as it was, without flattering it. Whatever else the last three years had made of him, the codes were true.

That thought carried me onto the first ice shelf with something that was almost calm.

The seracs above us were a city of blue towers, a hundred meters high, leaning over the throat of the valley like corrupt architecture. Every few minutes one of them spoke — that knuckle-crack, that groan — and small avalanches of powder hissed down faces we couldn't see. Between the towers, the route wound through a corridor of packed debris: old falls, old years, ice that had already given up and lay where it landed. The new calving would add to it. The question was whether it would add to it while we were underneath.

"Interval's down to ninety seconds," Mirela said at the first rest stop, watch up. "It was six minutes at dawn."

"Then we stop talking about it." I pointed to the next cairn — a two-high, angled left. "Shelf's undercut. Right side only. Single file, fifteen meters. Go."

She went. Asha followed, moving better than she had any right to — she had told us she'd grown up in these mountains, and whatever else was false in her ledger, that wasn't. Devi came last, and I watched him cross the undercut shelf with his bad eye fixed on the crevasse below, and I thought: *I signed my name to a lie to protect this man, and the lie took my name away, and now I'm leading him out of the valley he hid in, and none of it — none of it — was worth what it cost.* The thought wasn't angry. That was the strange part. It was just clear, the way the air is clear at altitude, too thin to hold anything up.

We were two-thirds through the corridor when the mountain stopped counting in minutes.

The boom came from directly above — not the deep knuckle-pop of the morning but a flat, percussive *crack*, the sound of a tower deciding. I didn't hear the rest of it fall. I heard Devi shout a code — the old one, *keta-keta-dun*, *split, scatter, down* — and then the world turned white.

Powder first, then the pressure wave, then the deep grinding roar of a hundred thousand tons of ice finding a new opinion about where it wanted to be. I was face-down behind a debris block with my arms over my head, and the fall hit the corridor fifty meters back the way we'd come, and when the white cleared I turned and looked at where the route had been.

It wasn't there. A serac the size of a cathedral lay across the passage, and behind it — cut off, on the wrong side, in the shadow of the remaining towers — Devi.

I could see him through the settling dust: on his feet, roped to nothing, because we'd gone to short spacing and the fall had parted us where the corridor narrowed. He was looking at me. He raised one hand. Not waving. Pointing — up, and to the left, and I followed the line of his arm and saw it.

The second tower was leaning. Not falling yet. Leaning — its base eaten out by meltwater, its mass committed to the same argument the first one had just lost. When it went, it would take the rest of the corridor, and it would go soon, because the mountain had stopped being patient.

"Devi!" My voice came out raw. "The spur — your cairns — is there a way around?"

He didn't answer right away, and I watched him do the thing I was doing: reading the ice, honestly, for the first time in both our lives. His good eye tracked the debris field, the spur of bedrock on the left wall, the gap between the fallen tower and the leaning one.

"There's a line," he called back. "Under the spur. Forty meters of exposure. Maybe less if the tower's slow."

"And if it's fast?"

"Then it doesn't matter what I say next." He looked at me across the fifty meters of fresh rubble, and his drifted eye and his true eye both found me at once, aligned for the first time all morning. "Gideon. Take them out. I'll take the spur. If I make it, I make it."

The old arithmetic said he was right. The old arithmetic — the one I'd lived by for three years, miles not years, keep moving, don't look back, the mountain takes what it takes — said take the two living women to the far moraine, get the data out, get the testimony out, and let the ice have the man it should have had three years ago. Mirela was already shouting it from ahead: *Gideon, the interval, we have to move —* Asha was silent, and when I glanced at her she was looking at her brother with an expression I will see for the rest of my life, whatever length that turns out to be.

But the old arithmetic was the lie. It was the same lie as the report I signed, the same lie as the codes we used to outsmart what couldn't be outsmarted. Loyalty to a dead friend's reputation had cost me my name; loyalty to a living man's comfort had cost him three years in a hole in the ice. Every version of loyalty I had practiced was loyalty to something false — to the story, not the truth. And the truth, standing there in the falling powder, was very simple: the mountain was talking, and what it said was that there was a line under the spur and forty meters of exposure and a man on the wrong side of it, and the only honest reading of that sentence was *go*.

"Keep moving," I told Mirela and Asha. "Far moraine. Don't wait at the edge — get clear of the runout. If the wall seals, you carry the cache out and you tell it all."

Asha's face broke open. "Gideon—"

"You have a brother to argue with. Go make sure he has the chance."

They went. I watched them until the dust took them — Mirela with the drive strapped to her spine, Asha moving like the word *brother* had put engines in her legs — and then I turned back to the rubble field and started up.

The ground was still settling under me, blocks the size of trucks groaning into their new positions, powder snow filling every footprint the moment I made it. The spur was bedrock, black and old, jutting from the valley wall like the rib of something prehistoric, and the line Devi had read ran along its base: a shelf of ice maybe two meters wide, with the fallen tower on one side and the leaning one on the other, forty meters of exposure to a drop that ended in blue darkness.

I reached the start of the shelf and looked up at the tower.

It was leaning the way a drunk leans against a doorframe — committed, but not yet fallen, swaying by millimeters in some rhythm I couldn't see. Meltwater ran from its base in a bright thread. Somewhere inside it, the deep cracks were talking to each other, getting acquainted.

Devi was thirty meters along the shelf, moving badly. His frost-scarred hands were slow on the rock, and his left leg — I saw it now, the hitch I'd been too far away to read before — dragged on every second step. He was not going to make forty meters before the tower finished its argument.

"Devi! Sit down. Hold position. I'm coming to you."

"You're what?" He turned, and for a moment the old Devi looked out of the ruined face — the man who had taught me the codes, who had laughed at altitude, who had once carried me for six hours with a broken ankle and never once mentioned the weight. "You go back, we both die. That's the mountain, Gideon. You taught me to read it."

"You taught *me*," I said, and started onto the shelf.

The ice under my boots was old and hard and honest — it held. I moved the way the codes were meant to be read: no hurry that wasn't real, no caution that wasn't real, every step a sentence in a language the mountain and I finally spoke in common. Ten meters. The tower groaned, a long descending note, and a sheet of powder sloughed off its flank and hissed past me into the dark. Fifteen. Twenty. Devi was sitting now, back against the spur, watching me come with both eyes.

"You signed the report," he said, when I was close enough to hear him without shouting. "I heard, three years ago. A shepherd told me. I thought — I thought if I stayed dead, the lie would hold. Protect everyone. Protect you."

"It didn't protect anyone. It just made the truth expensive."

"I know that now." He was crying, or the meltwater on his face was, and it didn't matter which. "I know it now, Gideon."

"Then we'll say the rest on the far side." I got his arm over my shoulders. His body was light in the terrible way of bodies that have been eating themselves for three years, and I took the first step back along the shelf with the whole weight of him and the whole weight of the last three years, and the tower above us made its decision.

The crack was enormous. The lean became a fall. And the shelf under our boots — the honest ice, the ice that had held — began, very softly, to tilt.
