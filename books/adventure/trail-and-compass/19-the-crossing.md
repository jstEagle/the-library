## 19. The Crossing

The serac had come down between us like a verdict.

One moment the party was strung out across the glacier's shoulder — Mirela ahead with the rope, Asha behind her, Devi and I at the rear where I'd put him so I could watch him fail. The next, the sky turned white and the world made a sound I felt in my teeth before my ears, and when the powder cleared there was a wall of blue ice the height of a cathedral where Devi had been standing.

"Devi!" Asha's voice cracked across the ice. "Devi!"

I was already moving. Not thinking — moving, the way you move toward a scream in the dark before your mind can argue you out of it. I clipped into the fixed line Mirela had anchored at the last cairn and went up the rubble slope on hands and knees, breath tearing, the mountain groaning around me in long tectonic syllables. Somewhere above, more ice was deciding whether to let go.

I found him in a pocket behind a leaning block the size of a bus. Alive. Sitting with his back against the ice, one leg folded under him at an angle legs don't fold, his half-blind eyes wide and streaming from the dust.

"I heard it," he said. His voice was small. Three years of smallness in it. "I heard it coming and I couldn't—"

"Can you stand?"

He tried. He got halfway and made a sound I will not describe, because describing it would mean admitting what it did to me, and I didn't have time to feel it.

"The route's closing," I said. "Forty minutes of sky, maybe less now. Can you stand on the good leg if I take the weight?"

"Gideon." He caught my wrist. His grip was nothing — frost years had eaten the muscle off him, and what remained was bird-boned and shaking. "Leave me. You know the codes. You can get them out."

I looked at him. Really looked, for the first time since the tent — at the man I had spent three years protecting with a lie, who had spent three years hiding inside that lie while I carried it. My friend. My ghost. The reason I signed my name to a false report and lost both countries for it.

"No," I said.

* * *

Getting him upright was the hardest physical work of my life, and I have carried loads that broke men twice my size. We made a seat-harness out of the spare sling and my shoulder, his arm across my neck, his good leg doing a third of the work and lying about it. He weighed less than he should have. That was the worst part — how light three years of guilt had left him.

The route ran along the base of the calving face, a shelf of old ice maybe four meters wide with the drop to the meltwater channel on our left and the living wall on our right. Above us the glacier spoke constantly now — cracks like rifle shots, deep booms, the hiss of powder avalanches running off the seracs. Every few minutes a chunk the size of a truck went into the channel and threw spray twenty meters high.

Mirela stood at the far anchor, waving us on. Asha had refused to go ahead; she'd planted herself at the near anchor and would not cross until we did, which meant she stood directly under the most active section of the wall, arms out, guiding us by voice whenever the dust swallowed the markers.

"Left! Stay left — the shelf is rotten on the right!"

"I know," I gasped. "I wrote the route."

"You wrote it three years ago," she shouted back. "Glaciers don't wait."

She was right, of course. The codes were a language, but the mountain was the speaker, and it was saying things my old map hadn't predicted. A crevasse I'd have sworn was solid yawned where none should be. I steered us wide, feeling Devi's breath hot and panicked against my neck, counting steps, counting seconds, counting the distance to the anchor as if distance were a currency I could spend fast enough.

Halfway across, the wall calved again.

Not a chunk. A corner of the serac field, whole, the size of a house, and it came down thirty meters to our right and the shockwave hit us like weather. Ice shrapnel sang past. The shelf jumped under my feet. Devi screamed — not from fear but from his leg grinding in the harness — and I felt something tear in my back, a hot wire pulled tight from hip to shoulder.

We went down together. Slid. My glove caught a ridge of old ice and held, and for a moment we hung there, two men on a shelf over black water, the drop humming beneath us.

"Gideon." Devi's face was against my ear. "Gideon, I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I'm sorry—"

"Not now."

"It was me. All of it. The commercial group, the warnings I ignored — I led them up because the fee was double and I told myself the ice would hold. And when it didn't—" His voice broke apart. "When they started asking questions, I ran. I let you sign it. I watched you carry it and I hid in the ice like a rat and I told myself you were better off—"

"Devi." I got my feet under me. Got his weight redistributed. Started moving again, one step, another. "You can tell me on the other side. You can tell everyone, on the other side. Move."

And he moved. Whatever broke open in him on that shelf, it unlocked his body the way grief sometimes does — he stopped being cargo and became a man walking, dragging his ruined leg, matching me step for step, sobbing and climbing at once.

The last hundred meters took eleven minutes. I know because I counted, because counting kept the wire in my back from becoming a thought.

Then Asha's hands were on us, hauling us over the lip at the anchor, and Mirela was cutting the fixed line behind us, and the entire calving face let go at once — a roar like the end of something, a white wall of powder chasing us up the moraine as we crawled, dragged, fell the last fifty meters to safe ground.

Behind us, where the shelf had been, there was water and churning brash ice. The valley sealed itself for another year with a sound like a door closing.

* * *

We lay on the moraine gravel for a long time. Four people breathing. The sun came through the thinning storm and lay on us like a hand.

Devi was crying. Not loudly. The way men cry when they've run out of strength to do it any other way — flat, mechanical, his ruined leg stretched out and his scarred hands open on the stones.

Asha knelt beside him. She didn't touch him at first. Then she took one of those hands and held it, and said nothing, because some things you cannot say to a brother you thought was dead until he's ready to hear them.

"He'll testify?" Mirela asked quietly, beside me. She had her notebook out. Of course she did. Even now, especially now.

I looked at Devi. He felt the look and lifted his head, and his clouded eyes found me with an accuracy that had nothing to do with sight.

"I'll testify," he said. "All of it. The report, the suppression, the hazard data they buried — mine too, whatever I know. Write it down." He laughed, wet and terrible. "Three years I survived out here keeping secrets. Turns out the ice wasn't keeping me alive. It was keeping me from saying anything."

"Then say it," Mirela said, and wrote.

I sat with my back against a boulder and let the wire in my spine hum and watched the glacier finish its argument with itself. Exhaustion had gone past pain into something else — a clarity, almost holy, the way the air is clear after a storm has wrung itself out. I had carried a man across a dying route. I had carried a lie for three years. Only one of those weights was still on me, and I could feel it sliding loose.

"Zhou." Devi said my name — my real name, the first time in three years anyone had spoken it aloud on this side of the border. "Why did you come back for me? After everything?"

I thought about it honestly, because he deserved honesty even if he'd never given it to me.

"Because the truth needed carrying too," I said. "And I was the only one who knew the route."

He nodded slowly. Then he reached into his jacket — that ragged, patched, three-winters jacket — and pulled out something wrapped in plastic and electrical tape, worn soft at the edges from years of handling.

"My copy of the original survey notes," he said. "From the avalanche season. Dates, conditions, the fee ledger, everything. I kept them because I couldn't decide whether they were evidence or a suicide note." He held them out. "They're evidence. Take them to whoever's writing the true report."

I took them. They were lighter than they should have been, too. Everything Devi had become was lighter than it should have been.

Down-valley, the clouds were breaking apart, and through the gap I could see the trail below — and on it, small with distance but unmistakable, figures moving toward us. Uniformed figures. A patrol, or worse, coming up the valley we'd just paid everything to leave.

Mirela saw them a heartbeat after I did. She closed her notebook.

"Well," she said. "Let's make sure they're the ones who get the truth first."
