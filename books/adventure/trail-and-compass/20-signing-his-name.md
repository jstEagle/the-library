## 20. Signing His Name

The generator at base camp coughed twice and settled into its low grind, and Mirela Osei sat down to write the report that would end her career or save it, depending on which version of the truth the ministry preferred.

She had drafted reports for eleven years. She knew their anatomy the way she knew contour lines: the executive summary that said nothing, the methodology section that laundered uncertainty into authority, the conclusions calibrated to whatever the funding ministry wanted to hear. She had written beautiful reports. Reports with clean margins and careful hedging. Reports that had helped bury a mountain guide named Gideon Zhou so thoroughly that two countries' registries showed no trace of him, as if a man could be contoured out of existence like a ridge deemed too inconvenient to map.

She would not write one of those today.

The tent flap was staked open to let in what light the overcast allowed. Outside, base camp had come back to life around them — the four missing surveyors, frostbitten but ambulatory, eating rehydrated rice with the slow astonishment of people who had stopped expecting to eat; the radio operator from the relief column shouting coordinates to a helicopter pilot who kept saying *weather window, weather window* like a mantra against the monsoon; and at the edge of it all, Devi Rai, wrapped in three blankets, sitting on a supply crate with his ruined eyes turned toward the glacier as if he could still read it.

Mirela spread her materials across the folding table: the data cache recovered from the hazard zone, printed transmittal logs, her own field notebooks, photographs of the cairns. The cache was the heart of it. Ice-penetrating radar surveys from eighteen months ago showing basal meltwater channels where the official assessment showed stable ice. Strain gauges reading past critical thresholds, their data flagged and then quietly excluded from the published summary. A draft report — someone's honest draft — with margin notes in a director's hand: *too alarming, soften, we have contracts riding on the access road.* And beneath all of it, the falsification memo she had been looking for since Kathmandu, the template itself: language almost identical to the language used to destroy Gideon Zhou three years ago. *Attribution of causation to guide error is recommended as the least administratively disruptive finding.*

*Least administratively disruptive.* She had underlined it twice, in red, until the pen tore the paper.

Her hands were not steady. Three weeks on the glacier, a swollen river crossed in a leaking boat, an ice wall calving behind them close enough to feel the concussion in her teeth — none of that had shaken her the way this table did. Because this was the part she understood. Mountains killed you quickly. Ministries killed you slowly, and they used your own signature to do it.

She began to type.

---

The first section wrote itself, because it was only arithmetic. Four surveyors missing near the Larkya Glacier, located alive at 5,140 meters in a valley absent from every published map. Cause of disappearance: deliberate misdirection by contracted personnel operating under instructions to avoid documenting the hazard zone. She named names. She had spent a sleepless night confirming them — the project director, the deputy minister's office, the access-road consortium whose letterhead appeared on the funding memoranda. Her fingers hesitated over the director's name for exactly as long as it took to remember Gideon's face in the boat, bailing water through the night, telling her in his flat, exhausted voice what he had signed three years ago and why. Then she typed it.

The second section was the suppressed data. Here she was careful, because carelessness was how they would kill the report. She reproduced every radar transect with its original timestamps. She quoted the strain-gauge exclusions verbatim, with file references. She attached the director's marginalia photographically, so no one could claim forgery. Let them come after the evidence if they dared. Evidence photographed in situ, witnessed by four rescued surveyors and a government cartographer, was harder to disappear than a man.

The third section was harder. It was about Devi.

She looked up from the screen. Devi hadn't moved. The blankets made him small, smaller than a man who had survived three years alone in an ice valley had any right to be. Asha sat beside him saying nothing, which Mirela had learned was how Asha loved people — by staying within arm's reach and asking nothing.

Devi alive. That was the sentence that would detonate everything. Because Devi alive meant the avalanche report was wrong at its foundation. It meant the commercial party's deaths had been attributed to conditions that were real but survivable, and the blame had been steered — first toward Devi, then, when the ministry needed a living scapegoat more than a dead one, toward the man who signed the report protecting him. Gideon Zhou had taken the fall for a lie he believed was kind. The ministry had taken the fall he offered and built a career on it.

She typed: *The undersigned has located Devi Rai, previously listed as deceased in the incident of [date]. Mr. Rai's survival, and his testimony regarding the events preceding the avalanche, materially contradict the findings of the original investigation.*

Then she stopped, cursor blinking, because there was a question underneath that question, and it belonged to someone else.

---

She found Gideon at the camp's edge, doing what he had done every hour since they came down: nothing visible. He stood facing the glacier with his hands in his pockets, watching the ice wall the way other men watched a fire — respectfully, from a distance that admitted it could still reach him. The storm had scrubbed the sky to a hard pale grey. Somewhere below, the river they'd descended ran brown and triumphant.

"The helicopter takes the surveyors tomorrow," Mirela said. "Weather window at dawn."

He nodded without turning.

"I'm drafting the true report. All of it. The cache, the suppression, the falsification template." A pause. "Devi."

Now he turned. His face did what it always did — closed by a degree, then opened despite itself. Three years of being no one had taught him to make his face a locked door, and three weeks with her had worn the hinges loose.

"You understand what happens when I file it," she said. "Inquiries. Both countries. Your name comes up. Not as a suspect this time. As a witness. Maybe as a victim. But your name, Gideon. In print. In registries. Permanent."

"I know."

"And Devi testifies, or he doesn't. If he doesn't, the report still stands on the data alone. But it's weaker, and they'll use the weakness to come for everyone who signed it. Me. You. Asha." She hugged her notebook to her chest, suddenly aware she was rehearsing an argument she'd already lost. "I need one thing from you before I send it."

"Ask."

"The rescue log. Officially, the survey team was located by an unidentified local guide assisting the cartographer. That's how these things get written — the anonymous helper, the good Samaritan, no name required." She took out the log sheet, the government form with its rigid little boxes, and held it between them. "I can file it that way. It would be safer for you. You'd stay invisible, off every registry, untouchable. Or—"

"Or you list me."

"Or I list you. Real name. Real license number, if you still remember it. And once it's in the system, it's in the system. They can contest it, they can investigate it, but they cannot un-write it. You'd exist again. In both countries. With everything that comes with existing — the inquiry, the questions, the people from the old expedition who will want to know why you signed what you signed." She swallowed. "I've watched what the world does to your name once already. I won't choose for you twice."

Gideon took the form from her hands. He read it the way he read terrain — top to bottom, then the fine print, then the spaces between the lines where the danger lived. The wind moved his hair, grey coming in early at the temples, and Mirela thought: *he's thirty-six and he has the face of a man deciding whether to be born.*

"Asha asked me something, on the river," he said finally. "Whether I stayed invisible because it was safe, or because I deserved it." He turned the form over, as if the back might contain different words. "I told her I hadn't decided yet."

"And now?"

"Now I keep thinking about the codes." He looked past her, up-valley, toward the place where the cairns climbed out of sight. "I built those codes so a route could tell the truth to anyone who learned to read it. No maps needed. No permission. Just stones, placed honestly. Devi used them to hide. I used them, for three years, to stay lost." A breath. "But they were never meant for either. They were meant so that anyone who came after could follow — and know exactly where they stood."

He clicked the pen she'd forgotten she was holding out to him. He wrote slowly, each character placed like a stone on a cairn. Mirela read upside down as the letters formed:

**Guide of record: GIDEON ZHOU. License NPL-4471-C.**

His hand paused above the signature line. One second. Two. Long enough that she saw the whole war of it cross his face — the borrowed name dying, the buried one rising, the three years of miles stacking up against a single line of ink.

Then he signed it. *Gideon Zhou*, in a hand steadier than hers had been all morning.

"There," he said quietly. He handed the form back as though returning something borrowed. "Write your report, Mirela. Write all of it. The soft parts and the ugly parts. Put my name wherever it belongs — including the part where I lied to protect a dead man who wasn't dead." He almost smiled. "If I get to come back, I come back whole. Not the version that's convenient."

Mirela folded the log sheet with exaggerated care, because if her hands shook now he would see it, and he had given her enough.

"It'll take months," she warned. "The inquiries. They'll freeze the contract, subpoena the cache, probably try to discredit me first — that's always step one."

"I know how it works."

"They may take the license number and suspend it pending review. You could end up legally nonexistent again, briefly, while they argue."

"Then I'll wait," he said, "in a country that knows I exist." He glanced up-valley one last time, at the ice wall holding its grey light. "That's more than I've had in three years."

Behind them, from the direction of the supply crates, a blanket rustled. Devi's voice, hoarse from disuse and cold air, carried across the camp with terrible clarity:

"Gideon." A pause. "There's something I haven't told you. About the night of the avalanche. About what I actually saw on the ice before it broke."

Asha's head came up sharply. Gideon went very still.

Devi turned his scarred face toward them, toward the sound of them, and said: "You need to hear it before you sign anything else."

* * *

Mirela looked at the report glowing unfinished on her screen, at the signature drying on the log sheet, and understood that the truth, like the glacier, had layers — and they had only cut through the first of them.
