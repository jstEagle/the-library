## 22. Compass North

The morning of the first legal expedition, Gideon Zhou woke before his alarm and lay in the dark listening to a sound he had almost forgotten: rain on a roof that belonged to him.

Not a tent. Not the tarp strung between two boulders on some borrowed slope. A roof — corrugated tin over a rented room above a trekking office in Lukla, with a stove he was allowed to light and a ledger downstairs with his actual name written across the top of its first page, in ink, where anyone could read it.

*Gideon Zhou. Licensed guide.*

He had looked at that line eleven times since the clerk had entered it. He would probably look at it eleven more before the season ended. Some habits of disbelief took longer to break than others.

He got up, dressed by feel, and went down to the office while the village was still blue with pre-dawn. The expedition gear was stacked and weighed and tagged — he'd done it twice last night and once more at three in the morning, which Mirela would have called compulsive and Devi would have called familiar. Four clients this time. A retired teacher from Osaka, two brothers from Melbourne, a quiet young woman from Nairobi who had saved for six years to be here. All of them vetted, insured, documented. All of them walking into mountains with a man whose paperwork finally matched his face.

He checked the manifest one last time anyway.

The brass compass sat on the desk beside the new one — a liquid-damped surveyor's model Mirela had sent from Kathmandu, its case engraved with coordinates he recognized as the mouth of the Larkya valley. He picked up the old one first. Its needle still swung a half-degree lazy to the east, the way it had for twenty years, the way it had the night Asha pressed it into his hand on the far side of the ice wall and said, *It lies a little. So did we all. Learn its error and you can trust it completely.*

He'd thought about that sentence for most of a season. He suspected she'd meant it to apply to more than instruments.

* * *

The clients met him at the airstrip at seven, stamping against the cold, breath rising in white flags. The teacher from Osaka introduced herself twice because she was nervous; the brothers argued cheerfully about who had packed more socks; the woman from Nairobi — Wanjiru — said nothing at all until they were on the trail, and then asked the question every client asks eventually, in one form or another.

"Is it true what they say about you? That you were dead for three years?"

Gideon considered several answers. The official one — the one from the inquiry reports, the contested bans, the slow machinery of two countries deciding whether a man could exist again — was long and full of committees.

"Not dead," he said. "Unlisted. There's a difference."

"What's the difference?"

"A dead man stops walking." He nodded up-trail, where the path bent through rhododendron toward the first pass. "I kept walking. I just stopped signing my name to anything."

She turned that over for half a kilometer. "And now?"

"Now I sign everything." He smiled despite himself. "You'll get tired of watching me do it."

They climbed. The trail was honest work — stone steps worn by generations of boots, porters passing with loads strapped high, a mule train's bells somewhere below in the mist. Gideon set a pace he could have held blindfolded and let the mountain introduce itself at its own speed. This was the part of guiding no registry ever measured: the reading. Where the morning fog would sit and how long it would hold. Which client's breathing would go ragged by noon. Where the water ran under the trail after heavy rain, hollowing the ground you couldn't see.

Three years ago he had measured his life in miles. Kilometers covered, passes crossed, distance put between himself and the man he used to be. Miles were clean. Miles didn't ask questions. You walked enough of them and the horizon stayed ahead of you forever, and nothing behind you ever caught up.

Now he measured in years. It had happened without his deciding it — sometime between the ice wall closing and the inquiry board's third session, sometime between Devi's testimony and the letter from the ministry that began *We are pleased to inform you* and that he had read four times before believing. Years were harder units. They contained winters. They contained people. They contained the whole slow weight of what you'd done and what you'd refused to do, and they didn't scroll past behind you like mileage; they accumulated.

But they also contained mornings like this one.

* * *

They made the first camp by mid-afternoon, a shelf above a river running loud with snowmelt. Gideon pitched the mess tent first, then walked the perimeter out of habit — checking anchors, drainage, the angle of the slope above them. Old reflexes. Good ones, it turned out. The ones that had kept him alive when he was no one were the same ones that made him worth hiring now that he was someone again.

Wanjiru found him crouched at the treeline, studying a scatter of stones near the path.

"Something wrong?"

"No." He straightened. "Someone built a cairn here. Recently."

"Other trekkers?"

"Maybe." He turned one stone over with his boot toe. Underneath, scratched into the surface with something sharp, was a mark: two short strokes and a dot.

His own code. Or close enough to make his chest tighten — the variant Devi had used, left-handed, the strokes cut deep the way Devi always cut them, impatient, certain the mountain would remember even if no one else did.

Devi was in Kathmandu. Gideon had put him on the plane himself, watched him go through security with his frost-scarred hands and his half-blind eyes and his testimony folded in a folder against his chest. Devi was testifying before a commission this month, facing consequences and restitution in equal measure, learning to live in a world that knew he was alive. He was not on this mountain.

Which meant either someone else had learned the codes — a student, an imitator, a guide who'd studied the leaked rescue logs the way Mirela once had — or the marks were older than they looked, weathered soft, and he was seeing ghosts in granite.

"You know what it means," Wanjiru said. Not a question.

"I know what it meant." He set the stone back exactly as it had lain. "Once, it meant *safe passage, water nearby, trust the route.*"

"And now?"

He looked up-valley, where the afternoon light was going gold on the high snowfields. Somewhere beyond those ridges lay the Larkya, the calving glacier, the hazard zone that was no longer a secret — fenced with warning markers now, mapped, monitored, its suppressed data published in a report that had cost three officials their careers and might yet cost more. The valley where he had found his dead friend alive. The valley where he had signed his real name to a rescue log and felt the pen move through him like a key turning.

"Now," he said, "it means someone was here before us, and wanted the next person to know it. That's all a code ever is. A promise between strangers."

* * *

That evening, after the clients were fed and zipped into their bags, Gideon sat outside the mess tent with both compasses on his knee and let the dark come down.

The stars over the Khumbu were the same stars that had hung over his exile — over the borrowed-name seasons in Peru and Georgia, over the leaky boat, over the cave where he'd confessed to Mirela that he had protected a lie because the truth belonged to a man who couldn't defend himself. He had thought, back then, that the sky didn't care who was looking at it. He'd been right, and he'd taken a strange comfort in it: the indifference of the heavens as a kind of shelter. No one up there keeping lists.

The satellite phone buzzed against his hip. Two messages.

The first was from Mirela: *Commission vote tomorrow. Devi's testimony is holding. Thought you'd want to know before you're above the cloud line. Also your replacement compass is better than the brass one and you know it. — M*

He huffed a laugh into the cold. She was right, and he'd never say so.

The second message had no sender attached — routed through a relay, the kind of thing Asha would use, the kind of thing she'd promised to stop using and then used anyway, because Asha's promises were like her papers: real, but not entirely. He opened it.

A single photograph. A moraine he recognized, shot from an angle he recognized — the approach to the Larkya, a year's meltwater later, the warning markers standing small and bright along the old route. And in the foreground, freshly built, a cairn. Two short strokes and a dot, cut deep into the capstone.

Below the photo, one line:

*Someone's laying your codes again. Come see before the season closes. Bring the brass one — it still knows the way. — A*

Gideon sat with it for a long time. The fire ticked. Above the mess tent, the brothers snored in counterpoint; Wanjiru's tent was silent; the river kept up its cold argument with the rocks.

He should feel dread. A year ago, a message like this would have landed in his chest like a dropped stone — the past reaching up out of its grave, the codes, the ghost-language, the whole machinery of erased names and buried nights grinding back to life. He waited for the dread to arrive.

What arrived instead was something quieter and harder to name. Recognition, maybe. The feeling of a route opening — that brief seasonal window when the ice pulls back and the passage exists, and you either commit or you wait another year knowing you'll wonder.

He thought about Devi, testifying in a concrete room in Kathmandu, choosing truth at whatever cost. About Mirela, redrawing maps that had lied for decades. About Asha, who had held a stranger's forgotten promise through a monsoon and never once put it down. About the teacher from Osaka asleep ten feet away, who had crossed an ocean to stand on a mountain with a guide whose name was finally true.

Every one of them had chosen. Eyes open, direction chosen, error known and accounted for. That was all a compass was, in the end — not a guarantee, just a decision you made fresh each morning and kept making.

Gideon typed his reply slowly, thumbs clumsy in the cold.

*Forty days. When the season closes, I'll come. And Asha — next time, sign your name.*

He sent it, then sat a while longer, the brass compass warm in his palm, its needle resting easy in its known half-degree of error. Trustworthy, now that he understood its lie. Like everything else he intended to carry forward.

Above him the stars wheeled, indifferent and enormous, and below him the river ran north, and Gideon Zhou — listed, licensed, named — went to sleep measuring his life in years, and looking forward to spending them.
