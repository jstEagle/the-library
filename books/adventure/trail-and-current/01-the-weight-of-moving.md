## 1. The Weight of Moving

The border town was called Ferren's Rest, which was a joke, because nobody there was resting. Zephyrine Oyelaran had been in it four days, and four days was already two too many. He counted his safety the way other men counted money — not in what he had but in how much distance lay behind him. Two hundred kilometers since the last checkpoint. Three rivers crossed. One name abandoned at a ferry landing like a coat that no longer fit.

He sat in the back of a tea house with his hood up and his hands around a glass he wasn't drinking, watching the door the way he always watched doors. Old habit. The habit had kept him alive through six countries and two blacklists, and it would keep him alive through a seventh if the permit man didn't ruin everything first.

The permit man arrived at dusk. He was small and damp-looking, with the eyes of a man who enjoyed other people's desperation the way some men enjoyed sport. He slid into the seat across from Zephyrine without being asked.

"You're the river man," he said.

"I'm nobody."

"Everybody here is nobody." The man smiled. "That's why the permits cost so much." He set a folded paper on the table between them. Zephyrine didn't touch it. He'd learned that lesson in a port town whose name he'd burned out of his memory: never let them see you want it. Wanting was leverage, and leverage was the only currency that mattered to men like this.

"The crossing stamp is real," the permit man said. "The signature is mine. You'll pass the northern checkpoint without a question asked."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you were never here, and I was never paid, and we both go on being nobodies." He tapped the paper once with a blunt finger. "Four hundred."

Zephyrine had one hundred and sixty, a knife, a coil of rope gone soft with age, and a boat he'd left moored two towns back under someone else's registration. Four hundred might as well have been four thousand.

"I'll need time," he said.

"Time costs more than money." The permit man stood, smoothing his coat. "Three days. After that the price goes up, and after that I sell your description to the checkpoint instead." He said it pleasantly, the way a man describes weather. Then he was gone into the evening crowd, and Zephyrine sat alone with the arithmetic of his life, which never balanced.

* * *

He found work before midnight, because he always found work; that was the one talent exile had sharpened instead of dulled. A freight yard near the water bridge needed cargo run upriver before the rains closed the passes — grain, tarpaulin, drums of lamp oil, nothing interesting. The foreman looked at Zephyrine's hands, then his face, then decided not to look at either again.

"Two hundred for the run," the foreman said. "Three hundred if you take the night stretch through the narrows. Most men won't."

"I'll take the narrows."

Of course he would. That was the disease of him. Safety measured in miles traveled, and miles only accumulated when you moved toward the thing you feared. He'd told himself once, years ago, standing over a grave that wasn't marked because there was no body to mark, that he would spend the rest of his life running from water. Instead he'd made water his trade, because a man runs fastest in the direction of his wound.

* * *

The rescue had been eleven years ago, though some mornings it felt like eleven hours. The Karst Ridge expedition, eight climbers on the high face, the storm that came down early and sideways. Zephyrine had been the guide. Twenty-six years old, already famous in the small fierce world of mountain men, already certain — that was the word, *certain* — that no weather could beat him off a route.

Dami had come along as camp cook and general hand, because Dami followed Zephyrine everywhere, the way a shadow follows a body, cheerfully, asking nothing. Their mother used to say Zeph had the ambition and Dami had the sense, and between them they made one whole man.

The storm broke the party in half at the col. Four climbers above, four below, and Zephyrine chose to go up. Everyone agreed afterward — the inquiry, the newspapers, the tribunal with its gray-faced officials reading from their gray-faced reports — that going up was defensible. The climbers above were in worse shape. It was the right call.

It was the right call, and it killed his brother.

Because while Zephyrine hauled strangers up the face through whiteout, Dami stayed at the col with the radio, and the col flooded, and the meltwater took him. They found his pack three valleys away. They never found Dami. And somewhere in the second day of the storm, with four lives in his hands and his brother's voice crackling dead on the radio, Zephyrine had made a routing decision that saved the climbers and abandoned the descent line — the inquiry called it *negligent judgment under pressure*, and the mountain board revoked his certification, and the country struck his name from its rolls, and the neighboring country did the same within the year, because countries talk to each other about disgraced men the way neighbors talk over fences.

*Blacklisted.* Such a clean word for such a dirty thing. It meant he could not guide anywhere with a license, could not sign a register, could not stand at a trailhead in daylight and say *I am the one who will bring you home*. So he ran rivers instead, unlicensed, unnamed, moving cargo and occasionally people across borders that preferred not to ask questions, and every job ended, and every ending meant moving again, and the moving became the whole architecture of his life until he couldn't remember what stillness felt like except as a kind of drowning.

* * *

The narrows at night were exactly as advertised. The river came down from the high country brown and swollen with early rain, and Zephyrine ran it alone in a borrowed flatboat with a bad engine and a lantern he kept unlit so the light wouldn't give him away to whatever patrols worked the far bank. He steered by sound — the change in pitch where the channel deepened, the hiss of water over the gravel bar that meant swing left, hard left, now.

It should have been terrifying. It was, a little. But mostly it was quiet, the particular quiet of a man doing the one thing he was built for, even if the world refused to certify it. His hands knew the tiller. His ears knew the river. Somewhere under all the exile and the forged names, there was still a guide, and guides got people and things safely from one bank to the other, and that was all any of it had ever been supposed to mean.

He thought about Dami the way he always thought about Dami on the water — not as a wound but as a weight, ballast sitting low in the hull of him. *You'd laugh at this,* he told his brother silently, sliding past a rock the size of a chapel. *Running rapids blind to pay off a forged stamp. You always said I'd die of my own cleverness.*

Dami had said worse things, funnier things. Dami had said that Zephyrine loved mountains because they stood still, and that someday he'd learn to love something that moved. Eleven years downstream, Zephyrine still didn't know what that something was supposed to be. He only knew that everything he loved moved away from him or got taken by water, and so he'd stopped loving things and started outrunning them.

The narrows opened into the long slow reach below the freight yard. He tied off at dawn, collected three hundred from a foreman who wouldn't meet his eye, and walked back into Ferren's Rest with the money flat against his ribs and the taste of river mud in his teeth.

* * *

The permit man counted the bills twice, slowly, enjoying himself. Then he pushed the stamped paper across the table.

"Pleasure," he said, in the tone of a man who had never had one.

Zephyrine took the permit and stood, and that was when the woman came through the tea house door.

He knew her before he saw her face — knew her by the way she moved, quick and economical, like water finding the lowest line. Ines Kovač. Eleven years since he'd seen her last, on the worst day of both their lives, and she hadn't changed except around the eyes, where the certainty he remembered had been replaced by something harder and hungrier.

She scanned the room, found him instantly — of course she did; she'd always been able to find him — and crossed the floor without hurrying, because Ines Kovač never hurried for anyone.

"Zephyrine Oyelaran," she said. "Still hiding in border towns. Still buying yourself with other people's cargo."

"Ines." His voice came out level, which cost him. "Whatever you're selling, I'm not buying."

She sat down across from him uninvited, laid a folded contract on the table, and smiled — and it was the smile that frightened him, because he recognized it. It was the smile of a woman who had done her research and knew precisely which name to say next.

"How's the season looking upstream?" she said. "In the Serein Gorge?"
