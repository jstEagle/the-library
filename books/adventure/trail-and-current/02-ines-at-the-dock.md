## 2. Ines at the Dock

The permit cost Zephyrine four hundred and the forger's silence another hundred, and by the time he'd paid both he was down to coins he could count through his pocket without looking. He stood on the dock at Porte-Grise counting them anyway, because counting was a thing hands could do while the mind went elsewhere, and elsewhere was where his mind had lived since the border town behind him and would live until the next one ahead.

Porte-Grise was a river town that existed for exactly two reasons: cargo going up, and men going nowhere. The docks smelled of diesel and wet rope and the sweet rot of fruit nobody had claimed. Rain came in short, insulting bursts — the monsoon still three weeks out but already sending scouts. Zephyrine watched a barge crew wrestle a tarp over pallets of cement and thought: *that's how you do it, corner to corner first, don't fight the wind, wait it out.* Twelve years since anyone had needed him to know that. Eleven since knowing it had stopped mattering.

He'd taken the job because the forged permit had to be paid for before the harbormaster's office opened Monday, and because the cargo was simple — machine parts, sealed drums, no questions — and because the run was short. Forty kilometers upriver to a mining camp, back by dark. Not the gorge. He'd checked the chart twice, then a third time, tracing the route with a finger gone rough at the knuckle. The Serein branched off above the camp. The contract didn't touch it.

He told himself that twice more while he loaded the *Vela* alone, which was how he preferred it. A boat you loaded yourself was a boat whose balance you knew in your spine. He stowed the drums low and forward, lashed the crate of parts amidships under the tarp, and was cinching the last knot when he heard heels on the planking.

Not boots. Heels. Nobody wore heels on a working dock.

"I heard you were running freight again." The voice came from behind his left shoulder, pitched low, unhurried, the voice of someone who had never once hurried for a man's comfort. "I told myself it couldn't be true. Zephyrine Oyelaran, back on the water. Then I saw the boat listing to port and knew it was you. You always did load like a man packing a suitcase."

He didn't turn around right away. He finished the knot. There was a discipline to that — finish the work in front of you, then face what's behind you — and he clung to disciplines the way drowning men cling to anything with texture.

"Ines."

She came around into his sightline the way weather comes around a headland: gradually, then all at once. Ines Kovač looked older and not softer. The same close-cropped hair going iron at the temples, the same jaw set at an angle that suggested she was listening to an argument she intended to win. She wore a rain shell worth more than his boat and carried nothing, because Ines never carried anything; other people carried things for her.

"Your knots have improved," she said, eyeing the tarp line. "That's the tragedy of you, Zeph. All this competence, and nowhere legal to spend it."

"What do you want?"

"Is that any way to greet—"

"We weren't friends. We were competitors who occasionally shared a rope." He stepped back from the gunwale, putting the boat between them, an instinct so old it predated thought. "Whatever this is, I'm not buying."

She smiled, and it was the smile he remembered from the competition circuit, from the rescue trials, from every briefing room where they'd been handed the same map and told to find the best line through. Ines smiling meant Ines had already done the arithmetic.

"You're running forty kilometers of easy water for a mining outfit that pays late," she said. "I know, because I know everyone on this river, and I know what you owe the forger on Front Street, because I know that too. Don't make that face. I didn't buy your permit. I just know its price, the way I know the draft of every hull on this dock and the debts of most of their captains." She let that sit. "I'm offering real money. Three months' worth of real money. One run."

"No."

"You haven't asked where."

"I don't need to." He turned back to his knots, though they were all tied. His hands wanted occupation the way his chest wanted air. "You're here, and you're offering me triple rate, and there's only one stretch of water that combination makes sense for. So no."

"The monsoon peaks in nineteen days," Ines said, as if he hadn't spoken. "After that, the upper Serein closes until October. Whatever goes up has to go now, or not at all."

"Then it can go with someone else. There are pilots who'll take it."

"There are pilots who'll *try* it." She moved along the dock, reading his boat the way she read everything — critically, possessively, as if the *Vela* were a vessel she'd once considered purchasing. "Half of them will beach in the narrows. The other half will turn back at the first rapid and refund their client's deposit with apologies. You're the only man on this river who's run the gorge eleven times and knows what it does when the water's high. You built half those lines yourself."

"And I unmade one of them." The words came out flatter than he intended, which was the only way he could say them. "You know that. Everyone on this river knows that. It's why I'm cheap and available."

Ines stopped walking. For a moment the dock noise filled the space between them — the slap of water against pilings, a winch groaning somewhere, gulls conducting their eternal petty arguments. When she spoke again her voice had changed register, dropped its performance, and that was worse. Performance he could discount. Sincerity from Ines Kovač was a tool with an edge ground on both sides.

"Dami would be thirty-four," she said.

Zephyrine's hands went still on the rail.

"He'd have hated the mining camps. Too much dust. He'd have been running whitewater tours out of the lower valley, charging tourists double because he could, telling them stories about the gorge that were half true and half legend, and the legend half would've gotten bigger every year." She said it gently, which was the cruelest way she could have said it. "I think about that sometimes. What the river took versus what it left. It left you, and you won't even look at it."

"Don't." His voice came out low and cracked down the middle, and he hated the crack, hated that after twelve years it was still there, a fault line running through him that every earthquake found. "Don't say his name on this dock. Don't say it to buy yourself a pilot."

"I'm not buying anything." She stepped closer, and he caught the smell of her — cold coffee and some expensive soap, the scent of boardrooms and briefing rooms, of a life that had continued upward while his continued sideways. "I'm asking. That's different. I need the best, Zeph, and the best is standing in front of me loading a boat wrong."

"It's not wrong—"

"It is, and you know it, because you know everything about boats except how to stop punishing yourself through them." She reached into her jacket — he flinched before he could stop himself, an old reflex from a country where sudden movements near officials ended badly — and produced not a weapon but a folded chart. She laid it on the crate between them and weighted it with a coin.

The Serein Gorge, top to bottom. Every rapid named in her neat hand. And beside each name, a number: depth readings, flow estimates, dates. Recent dates. She'd surveyed it. She'd spent money surveying it.

"You've already got the data," he said. "Why do you need me?"

"Data tells you what the river is doing. It doesn't tell you what the river is going to do." She tapped the narrows, the black pencil mark where the walls pinched together and the water compressed into something with opinions. "Nineteen days, Zeph. Rising water, falling window. I have supplies that must reach the head of the gorge before the peak, and I have a client who does not accept refunds. I need a man who reads water the way priests read scripture. There's exactly one, and he's pretending to be a freight runner."

Zephyrine looked at the chart and made himself see it — really see it, the whole length of it, the way you'd look at a scar if you finally decided to stop avoiding mirrors. The put-in below the falls. The narrows. Widowmaker, where the current bent around a submerged shelf and ate boats that cut the corner fat. The long flat pool after, where the water went quiet and green and deep, deep enough to hold things forever.

Deep enough to hold Dami.

He'd sworn an oath on that pool. Not aloud, not with witnesses — oaths like that don't need witnesses, they need only the person swearing and the water watching. *Never again.* Never again this river, this gorge, this country's paperwork, this whole watershed of grief. And he'd kept it. Eleven years of keeping it, hopping borders, running easy water, measuring safety in miles already traveled. The oath had cost him everything except itself, and he'd told himself that was a fair trade.

"You said one run," he managed. "Up and back?"

"Up. Deliver at the head of the gorge. My people meet you there." Ines watched him with those gray eyes that gave away nothing except, occasionally, everything. "You'd be on the water four days. Five with weather."

Five days. Four nights. The narrows in rising water. Widowmaker with the shelf drowned and invisible. The pool.

And four hundred owed to a forger, and a blacklist in two countries, and a boat that was the last honest thing he owned, and a name he hadn't spoken in eleven years sitting in his mouth like a stone he'd swallowed whole and never digested.

"If I say no," Zephyrine said slowly, "you'll find someone else. Someone good enough. And they'll either die in the narrows or they won't, and either way you'll get your cargo up, and I'll be here. Still here. Still counting coins on a dock."

"Yes," Ines said simply. She didn't dress it up. That was the thing about her that he'd hated and, in some buried chamber of himself, respected: she never lied about the shape of a deal. Only about its purpose. "That's what happens if you say no. Now tell me what happens if you say yes."

What happened if he said yes was that he went back. Back up the water where his brother's lungs had filled, back past the rapids he'd named, back into the country that had taken his license and his reputation and his right to stand at a memorial service without lawyers present. What happened was that he stopped being a man who had survived the Serein and became, again, a man defined by it.

What happened was that the miles he'd traveled stopped counting.

Zephyrine picked up the coin weighting the chart. It was old currency, from a country neither of them could enter legally anymore. He turned it over once, twice, feeling its worn face against his thumb.

"Triple rate," he said. "Half up front. And my crew signs on with me, not you — I pick who comes aboard, and you don't touch them."

"Done."

"And Ines—" He looked up, and let her see whatever was in his face, because hiding it had never once worked on her. "If this is something else. If there's anything under this cargo besides cargo. I'll dump it in the deepest hole in that river and row home laughing."

For just a moment — a flicker, gone before it fully arrived — something moved behind Ines Kovač's eyes. Something that might have been guilt, or might have been respect, or might simply have been the expression of a woman watching a lock begin to turn.

"It's supplies," she said. "Zeph. It's just supplies."

* * *

He signed the contract that evening in the back room of a dockside café that smelled of fried plantain and engine oil, and Ines transferred the advance before his tea went cold. Walking back to the *Vela* through the blue hour, past shuttered stalls and the first fat drops of the evening rain, Zephyrine caught himself doing something he hadn't done in years: reading the river.

It ran high and brown and fast, carrying the monsoon's early tributes — branches, silt, a drowned umbrella turning slowly like a jellyfish. Nineteen days to peak. He'd have to drill whoever he hired until they bled rope-burn. He'd have to relearn lines he'd sworn to forget. He'd have to sleep within earshot of water that knew his brother's name better than he'd allowed himself to say it.

At the gangplank, a woman was waiting. Stranger, thirties maybe, travel-worn, with the particular stillness of someone who'd learned to be overlooked on purpose. She held a folded paper — references, probably, or a plea.

"You Oyelaran?" she said. "They said at the office you're hiring for an upstream run."

Zephyrine looked at her, then past her, at the brown water sliding toward the sea, going wherever rivers go when they finally stop fighting the pull toward the gorge.

"Yeah," he heard himself say. "I'm hiring."
