## 3. Crew of Strangers

The papers said Amara Voss was born in Kettery, a river town so small it appeared on maps only as a smudge of blue where the road gave up. The papers were good work — better than the ones she'd carried two years ago, which had gotten her detained for eleven days in a customs shed that smelled of diesel and old fish. These had watermarks. These had a stamp from an office that had burned down in the spring, which was either a flaw or a stroke of genius depending on how carefully anyone looked.

She'd paid half her savings for them and told herself it was an investment, the way she told herself a lot of things.

The posting had been nailed to the board outside the ferry office in Marrow's Landing, one sheet among dozens, but it was the only one offering hazard pay *and* passage out. *Crew wanted. Upstream run, Serein Gorge. Experience preferred. Discretion required.* She'd read the last word twice. In her experience, discretion required usually meant someone was paying you to not ask questions, and those jobs ended in one of two ways: with money, or with running.

She was already running. Might as well be paid for it.

* * *

The boat was called the *Vela*, and she was ugly in the way that honest boats are ugly — flat-bottomed, scarred along the port side where something with teeth or rocks had taken its interest, patched in three places with metal that didn't quite match. Amara stood on the dock at dawn and counted the crew as they gathered: six, including herself. A wiry deckhand named Tomas who chewed something that wasn't tobacco. A pair of brothers, Ovid and Ren, who loaded crates without speaking to each other or anyone else. An old engine man called Ferro whose hands shook until they touched machinery, and then didn't. And at the gangplank, checking manifests against cargo with a pencil worn down to a stub, the man who'd hired them.

Zephyrine Oyelaran. She knew the name before he said it aloud. Everyone who worked water in these borderlands knew it, though what they knew depended on which bank of which river they'd learned their trade on. On some stretches he was a hero. On others, a cautionary tale. The story always had the same bones: a rescue in the gorge, years ago, that went wrong in a way that drowned more than one man, and a name struck from two countries' registries like a stain scrubbed out of wood.

He didn't look like a stain. He looked like a man built out of angles and patience, tall and spare, with a face that seemed assembled for weather rather than conversation. When he spoke, his voice came out low and even, the voice of someone who had learned that shouting was a luxury.

"Rules," he said, without greeting any of them. "You don't drink on the water. You don't touch the cargo unless I say. You sleep when I tell you to sleep, because there won't be another time. And if I give an order twice, the second time is the last time." He looked up from the manifest and let his gaze travel across all six of them, unhurried, weighing. "Questions?"

Tomas raised a hand. "Pay—"

"On completion. Half if we make the gorge head, the rest when the client signs."

"What's in the crates?" This from Ovid, jerking his chin at the stacked cargo under tarps.

Oyelaran's pencil stopped moving. It was a small thing, the stopping, but Amara watched it the way she'd learned to watch small things — the way a stillness in birds meant something larger moving underneath. "Sealed freight," he said. "Client's business is the client's."

Ren snorted. "Every smuggler says that."

"I'm not a smuggler." There was no heat in it. That was the strange part. Most men would have bristled; this one simply corrected the record, the way you'd correct a wrong bearing. "Smugglers lie about what they carry. I'm telling you exactly what I know, which is nothing, because I haven't asked. Asking is how crews end up knowing things that get them killed." He rolled the manifest and tucked it into his jacket. "Board. We cast off in twenty minutes."

Amara boarded last. As she passed him at the gangplank, he glanced at her papers — she'd expected that — and held them a beat longer than courtesy required. Long enough for her pulse to tick up. Then he handed them back and said, "Kettery," as if tasting the word. "Small place."

"You've been?"

"No." He turned away. "But I've met people who claim to be from there."

She couldn't tell if it was a warning or a joke, and by the time she decided to find out, he was at the helm and the *Vela* was sliding off the dock into water the color of gunmetal.

* * *

By the third day she understood that Zephyrine Oyelaran ran his boat the way other men ran prisons — except that everyone, herself included, kept volunteering to stay.

It started with drills. Not the casual kind, the kind captains run to satisfy insurance men. Real ones. Man overboard at dawn, with Ferro's oldest oilskin stuffed and weighted and thrown off the stern while the crew scrambled through cold spray to hook it. Capsize procedure. Bail-line formation. He timed everything, wrote the times in a waterproof notebook, and made them do it again when the times weren't good enough.

"The gorge doesn't care about your best," he told them, standing hip-shot against the rail while rain needled the deck. "It cares about your worst. So we practice until your worst looks like your best."

"He enjoys this," Ren muttered, wringing out his sleeves.

"He's terrified," Amara said, before she could stop herself.

Ren looked at her. "Of what? The river?"

She didn't answer, because she hadn't worked it out yet herself. But she'd spent two years reading people the way you read water — looking for the undertow beneath the surface — and there was something in the way Oyelaran handled the *Vela* that didn't fit. He was flawless. Every line coiled right, every command landed a half-second before it needed to, every drill ran like clockwork. And yet he touched the wheel the way a man touches a door he's afraid to open. Gently. Like it might open onto something.

That evening, moored against a rock shelf while the current hammered past, she found him alone at the bow, studying the upstream dark where the river narrowed into the first throat of the gorge. The map was open on his knee, but he wasn't looking at it. He was looking past it, at a bend he couldn't see yet, and his expression was the expression of a man attending a funeral he'd tried to skip.

"You've run this route before," she said. Not a question.

"A long time ago."

"And?"

"And nothing." He folded the map with unnecessary precision. "Get some sleep. We hit the shallows at first light."

She went, but she took the long way aft, past the cargo, and paused at the tarps. Sealed freight. Client's business. She pressed one palm flat against the nearest crate and felt the weight of paper inside — not ore, not tools, not grain. Paper, stacked dense as brick. Nobody pays hazard rates to move paper upstream unless the paper is worth more than the boat.

She lifted her hand and walked on, and told herself she hadn't asked anything, and understood suddenly why he'd made asking the first rule.

* * *

On the fifth day the sky over the gorge head went the color of a bruise, and the radio — Ferro's pride, salvaged from somewhere — crackled out a storm advisory in two languages and neither of them comforting. Oyelaran listened with his head tilted, then turned the volume down without turning it off, the way you might quiet a doctor mid-diagnosis.

"We'll make the narrows before it breaks," he said. "After that, weather decides."

Ferro spat over the side. "Weather always decides. Question is whether we argue."

"We argue." Oyelaran's jaw set. "We're delivering on schedule."

Amara watched him from the rigging, where she'd taken to working the winch because it let her see everything. There it was again — that wrongness. A captain with his skill should have turned back at the first advisory. Any sane contract had weather clauses. But he drove the *Vela* forward like a man late for his own reckoning, and she began, slowly, to assemble the picture: it wasn't the journey he feared. Journeys he could handle; journeys were just distance, and distance was the one thing he clearly trusted. It was the destination. Somewhere upstream, at the head of the Serein Gorge, there was a thing waiting for him that he'd sworn off facing, and every drill, every rule, every merciless hour of preparation was just a man building walls between himself and the moment the boat finally arrived.

She'd known runners before. She was one. Runners never feared the road; they feared the place where the road ended and the past caught up.

That night, rain beginning to speck the deck, she took the watch beside him at the wheel rather than going below. He didn't order her off. They stood in a silence that lasted an hour, the river talking to itself in the dark, and just when she decided the silence would hold until morning, he spoke.

"Why does a woman with Kettery papers flinch when I say the name?"

Her hands went still on the rail. "I don't flinch."

"You did. Right here. Day one." His eyes stayed on the channel ahead, on the black water and the white noise of it. "I'm not customs. I'm not going to turn you in. But if your papers are worth less than you paid for them, I need to know now, not in the middle of the gorge when it matters."

The rain thickened. She weighed it — the truth, or a version of it — and heard herself say, "They're good papers. But they're not mine. The name isn't mine. The rest is close enough."

He nodded once, slow, as if she'd confirmed a calculation he'd already finished. "Then you understand the rules better than the others. People who've rebuilt themselves don't ask questions they can't afford answered." A pause. "And they watch. You watch too much for deckhand work."

"Is that a complaint?"

"It's an observation." For the first time since she'd boarded, something moved through his face that might, in better light, have been the ghost of humor. "Keep watching. Just point it at the water."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then the radio behind them clicked alive, and Ferro's voice came up from below, stripped of its usual gravel, tight and fast: "Skipper — you'd better come hear this. Storm's shifted north. It's coming straight down the gorge." A crackle, then, worse: "And there's a flood warning posted for the lower villages. They're saying the Serein hasn't run this high in twenty years."

Amara watched Oyelaran go very still at the wheel, and saw the word *villages* land on him like a stone dropped down a well — falling a long way before it hit something deep.

"Lower villages," he repeated quietly. To himself, or to the river, or to someone who wasn't aboard anymore.

The rain came down harder, and the *Vela* pushed on into the dark toward the gorge, carrying whatever it carried.
