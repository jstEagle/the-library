## 4. The Sworn-Off Water

The gorge announced itself the way grief does — not all at once, but in stages you can't mistake.

First the walls narrowed, grey limestone rising on either side of the Vela like the pages of a book closing. Then the light changed, going from morning gold to a cold green shade that seemed to come from the water itself. And then the sound: the Serein running fast and deep over drowned rock, a voice Zephyrine Oyelaran had spent eleven years trying not to hear.

He stood at the tiller with his hands loose on the wood and let the river talk.

Behind him, the crew worked the cargo lines. He'd drilled them for three days at the dock — knots until their fingers bled, weight distribution until they could shift two hundred kilos of sealed crates in under four minutes, man-overboard drills until Amara could throw a line blindfolded into a ring floating thirty meters downstream. They thought he was a tyrant. Good. Tyrants kept crews alive. What none of them understood was that every drill had been a prayer, a bargain struck with a river that had already taken what it wanted from him once.

"Depth marker says nine meters," called Tomas from the bow, a broad-shouldered ex-dredger who'd signed on in the border town without asking questions. "Channel's holding center-left."

"Center-left," Zephyrine repeated, and eased the tiller. The Vela answered sweetly, her hull biting the current, and for one clean moment there was only the work, and the work was enough.

Then the river bent, and the Old Crossing came into view.

It was nothing now. A pair of rusted stanchions on the eastern bank where a cable ferry had once run, a shelf of flat stone where villagers had waited with their goods and their goats and their children. The rope was long gone. The stone was green with moss. But Zephyrine's body remembered it the way a scar remembers rain — a low ache, an old heat. This was where Dami used to wave him across, arms wide, laughing at how seriously his older brother took everything. *You steer like the river's your enemy, Ze. It's just water. It just wants to go somewhere.*

*It just wants to go somewhere.* And it had gone somewhere, taking Dami with it, on a day when the water was lower than this, calmer than this, when no one — no one — could have predicted the seiche that rolled down from the upper gorge when the old dam company opened a sluice gate without warning anyone downstream.

Zephyrine's hands tightened on the tiller until the grain printed itself into his palms.

"Captain?" Amara had come aft without him noticing. She did that — moved quiet as ballast shifting. She stood beside him, looking at the crossing, and he watched her catalog it: the stanchions, the moss, the way his knuckles had gone white. She missed nothing. That was the trouble with her. "What is this place?"

"A crossing."

"It's abandoned."

"Everything here is abandoned." He heard the edge in his own voice and didn't pull it back. "Eyes forward. Watch for sweepers — deadfall coming off the banks after the first rains."

She didn't move. She had that quality, too — a stillness that wasn't obedience but patience, as if she were willing to wait out any storm, including the ones inside other people. "You've run this route before," she said. Not a question. A door held open.

"I said eyes forward."

The word came out harder than he intended, hard enough that Tomas glanced back from the bow, and Amara finally turned away — but slowly, unhurried, and he caught the look she gave him before she went. Not hurt. Measuring. As if she'd found something in the water and was deciding whether it was worth diving for.

Zephyrine stared upstream and hated her a little for asking, and hated himself more for the answer she'd already read off his face.

* * *

They made camp that night on a gravel bar below the second bend, because running the gorge in darkness was suicide even by his standards. The storm warnings had come through Ines's radio at dusk — a low-pressure system stacking up over the headwaters, monsoon arriving early and angry — but that was tomorrow's problem. Tonight there was firewood, tinned fish, and the particular silence of six people who didn't trust each other yet sharing one fire.

Zephyrine took first watch alone at the boat, checking the bilge, checking the lashings on the crates. The sealed ones. Ines's precious cargo, strapped amidships under oilcloth, heavier than supplies had any right to be. He'd stopped asking her what was in them. She'd smiled the way she used to smile before beating him in the slalom trials at nineteen — kindly, and with total contempt — and said, *You're being paid to float them, not read them.*

He ran his palm along the oilcloth now. Paper underneath. Heavier than paper should be. Files, maybe. Ledgers. Something bound and official, something that mattered enough to move during monsoon season up a river most pilots had sworn off.

Most pilots. Not him. Because Ines Kovač knew exactly which rivers a blacklisted man couldn't afford to swear off.

"You take watch like it's confession," Amara said, dropping onto the gunwale beside him.

"I take watch like it's watch."

"Mm." She pulled her knees up, chin resting on them, and looked out at the river instead of at him, which he appreciated. The Serein moved silver-black under the half moon, patient as a debt. "Tomas says you knew this water as a boy. That there was a village."

"Tomas talks too much."

"He also says you haven't been back in eleven years, and that the last time, people died."

The night went very still around those words. Somewhere upstream, water fell over rock with a sound like slow applause. Zephyrine kept his hands busy with a lashing that didn't need checking, leather creaking through his fingers, because if his hands stopped moving he was afraid of what they'd do.

"There was a rescue," he said finally, because silence would tell her more. "Eleven years ago. Flash flood, unannounced release from the upper dam. Six people swept off the crossing. I got five of them out." His voice sounded like someone else's, flat and far away. "The sixth was my brother. Fourteen years old. I had him by the wrist. I had him." The lashing pulled tight, tight, tight. "The inquiry said I did everything right. The company said the sluice failure was unforeseeable. The licensing board said a guide who loses his own brother shouldn't be trusted with other people's children. Two countries agreed. So here I am."

Amara said nothing for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost its probing edge; it was quieter, and careful, like walking on river ice. "That's why you drill us the way you do."

"That's why I drill you the way I do."

"And why you snapped at me today. At the crossing."

"Because you ask questions like the answers are free." He turned and looked at her straight on, and let her see all of it, the whole ruined country of his face. "Nothing on this river is free, Amara. Not the water, not the truth, not the past. You want to know things about me, earn them. Bailing water side by side through a storm — that earns them. Sitting pretty on a gunwale doesn't."

Something flickered across her expression — not retreat. Recognition. As if he'd shown her a map of somewhere she'd also been lost. "All right," she said simply. "Side by side, then." And she slid off the gunwale and walked back toward the fire, leaving him alone with the river and the word *brother* sitting in his chest like swallowed stone.

* * *

Sleep didn't come, so near dawn he climbed the scree bank above camp to look at the route ahead, and the gorge gave him its memories whether he wanted them or not.

There: the seam of white quartz on the western wall where Dami had dared him to climb, age ten, and Zephyrine had gotten twenty feet up and frozen, and Dami had talked him down, joke by joke, hand by hand. There: the side canyon where they'd smoked stolen cigarettes and sworn oaths about the boats they'd own someday, twin hulls, twin names. There: the bend where the current stacked into a standing wave taller than a man, and Dami had stood on the shore watching the flood take the crossing, watching his brother swim for the drowning, and Zephyrine had heard him shouting — he still heard him, some nights, eleven years of nights — *Ze! Ze, the little one first, get the little one first—*

And he had. He'd saved the child first, because that was correct, because that was procedure, because procedure was all he had when the world came apart. And while his hands held a stranger's daughter, the river reached up and took his brother's wrist out of his grip like a pickpocket, smooth and casual, and carried him under the seam of white quartz and did not give him back for nine days.

Nine days. Zephyrine had walked the banks for nine days. He knew every stone between here and the head of the gorge the way a mourner knows a coffin's grain.

The sky in the east had gone the color of a bruise. The storm system was coming down the valley faster than the radio had promised — he could feel it in the pressure behind his eyes, in the way the birds had gone silent along the banks, in the smell the air took on before heavy water, mineral and electric, like coins in the mouth. Below him, the camp stirred. Amara was already awake, standing at the waterline, looking upstream the same direction he was. She raised a hand when she saw him. He didn't raise his back, but he nodded, and she seemed satisfied with that, and somehow that satisfaction lodged in him like a splinter — small, foreign, impossible to ignore.

Ines emerged from her tent with a cup of coffee and a chart, and even at this distance Zephyrine could see the energy coming off her, the bright hard hunger of a woman who could smell the finish line. She pointed upstream. She pointed at the crates. She pointed at all of them, one by one, a general counting ammunition.

We push through today, her posture said. Storm or no storm. Gorge or no gorge.

Zephyrine descended the scree with gravel singing under his boots and took the chart from her hands, and his eyes went where hers had gone — upstream, past the third bend, past the narrows, to the place where the Serein rose out of the mountain's throat.

To the dam.

He hadn't let himself think it plainly until now, and thinking it felt like opening a crate he'd been carrying for eleven years: Ines hadn't hired him despite the route. She'd hired him *for* it. Everything about this run — the timing, the cargo, the sealed crates heavier than paper — pointed upstream, to the company, to the men who had opened a sluice gate and called it unforeseeable.

"What are we really delivering, Ines?"

She smiled at him over the rim of her cup, kindly, and with total contempt, and the first drops of the monsoon began to fall on the chart between them, blooming like dark flowers.

"Soon," she said.
