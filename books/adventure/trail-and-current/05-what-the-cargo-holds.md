## 5. What the Cargo Holds

Amara learned the river by learning Zephyrine.

Not the way she'd expected — not through conversation, because he offered almost none, and not through instruction, because his teaching was mostly a raised eyebrow and the word *again*. She learned him the way you learn a current: by watching what it did to things in its path. How he stood at the tiller with his weight on his back foot, always ready to give ground. How he touched the gunwale of the *Vela* twice before every rapid, quick and private, like knocking on a door. How he never once looked up at the gorge walls for longer than a breath, though they were the most beautiful thing Amara had ever seen — thousand-meter cliffs of red and grey stone, streaked with old waterlines like the rings of a drowned tree, hung with vines that poured down the rock in green waterfalls.

He looked at the water. Only the water. As if looking up might cost him something he couldn't spare.

Three days upstream from the border town, and the Serein had already changed character. Down low it had been broad and brown and lazy, a working river. Here it narrowed, gathered itself, turned white where it broke over shelves of submerged rock. The monsoon hadn't fully arrived yet — the sky was still holding its breath, fat grey clouds stacked along the gorge rim like cargo waiting to be loaded — but the river knew something they didn't. It ran higher than the marks on the stone. It carried more debris: whole trees sailing past bow-first, their roots clawing at the air.

"Watch the strainer," Zephyrine called back, pointing his chin at a half-submerged tree wedged between two boulders ahead. "Water goes through, boat doesn't. If we hit that, we're kindling."

The crew — six of them now, counting Amara — murmured acknowledgment. There was Teodor, Ines's man, all shoulders and silence, who worked the forward line. The twins, Bex and Noor, who bickered like weather and moved like one person when it mattered. Old Sefu at the engine, who'd been running rivers since before Amara could swim and treated every sound the *Vela* made as a personal insult. And Amara, midships, hands on the cargo lines, learning.

She'd run rivers before. That part of her story was true, at least — the forged papers were for other things. But she'd never run water like this, and she'd never run it under a man who clearly wished he were anywhere else in the world.

*He's more afraid of this place than of the storm coming,* she'd written in her head on the first night, watching him sit awake at the stern long after everyone slept, facing downstream, toward home, like a man watching for something to come up out of the water after him.

Now, on the third day, the crates began to worry her.

It wasn't the weight of them. She'd hauled worse. It was the way they were packed — too carefully and too carelessly at once. Each crate stenciled with a shipping company's name she didn't recognize, but inside, when she'd shifted one and heard the slide of paper against paper, wrong somehow. Supplies went up the gorge to the survey stations, Ines had said. Instruments, provisions, fuel. But instruments didn't rustle. And no one wrapped provisions in oilcloth sealed with wax.

She mentioned it to Teodor once, lightly, the way you test ice with one foot. He looked at her for a long moment and said, "You're paid to tie knots, not ask questions." Then he'd gone forward and said nothing else for four hours.

So she stopped asking. But she kept noticing.

The rapid came at them an hour past noon, unannounced except by sound — a deep, grinding roar around a bend where the gorge pinched to less than half its width. Zephyrine was already moving, reading it, and whatever he read made his jaw tighten.

"Right channel," he called. "Hug the right wall. There's a hole center-left big enough to swallow us. Sefu — full power, don't let her drift. Everyone hold something that loves you."

They went in.

The *Vela* climbed the first standing wave and Amara's stomach dropped away like a trapdoor opening. Water came over the bow in cold sheets. The gorge walls blurred past on both sides, close enough to touch, and the noise was total — not a sound but a place, a country of noise they were traveling through. She got both fists around a cargo line and held on, watching Zephyrine at the tiller work the wheel in small corrections, precise as a surgeon, his face lit white with spray and absolutely calm.

That was the thing about him she couldn't reconcile. Terror underneath, calm on top, and neither one fake.

Then the wave hit.

It came off the left wall — a rebounder, reflected back into the channel, the kind of water you can't read until it's on top of you. It caught the *Vela* broadside and threw her sideways, and Amara felt the whole deck lurch beneath her like a living thing trying to buck its rider. Somewhere aft, metal screamed against stone. The crate nearest her — the heavy one, third from the port rail, the one she'd noticed because it rode too high and lashed too tight — tore free of its lines.

She saw it go. Saw it slide across the flooded deck, saw Teodor dive for it and miss, saw it strike the rail, split along a seam that had been cut and re-glued — *cut and re-glued*, she thought absurdly, even as everything tilted — and burst open like a struck piñata.

Paper everywhere.

Dozens of folders, hundreds of pages, snatched instantly by the wind and water, fanning out across the deck and over the side in a white blizzard. Amara lunged without thinking, caught one folder out of the air, another off the deck, pressed them under her body as the *Vela* wallowed through the rest of the rapid. Pages streamed off the stern like a wake of gulls. Teodor was shouting. Sefu was shouting. Zephyrine's voice cut through all of it, low and hard: *"Hold the line! Let the paper go!"*

But Amara didn't let it go.

* * *

They eddied out below the rapid, in the lee of a gravel bar, engine ticking, crew soaked and shaking. The storm clouds had come down off the rim while they ran the drop; the light had gone the color of wet slate. Zephyrine stood amidships, dripping, staring at the three soggy folders Amara had saved, which lay on the deck planks between them like something dead.

"Give them here," Teodor said, reaching.

"No," Zephyrine said.

It came out quiet, but it stopped Teodor's hand midair. Zephyrine crouched, picked up the topmost folder himself. His hands were steady, Amara noticed. Steadier than they had any right to be.

He opened it.

Amara watched his face do something complicated. She'd spent enough years reading faces across borders — checkpoint officers, smugglers, liars, priests — to know that what crossed his wasn't surprise. Surprise is fast. This was slow. This was recognition arriving in stages, each one worse than the last.

He read in silence. Turned a page. Turned another. The rain started then, fine and cold, spotting the paper, and he didn't seem to notice.

"What is it?" Amara asked.

He didn't answer her. He read aloud instead, barely, as if to himself, in a voice sanded flat: "*Structural assessment, Serein Headwaters Dam, Phase Two. Seepage rates exceeding design tolerance by four hundred percent. Recommendation: emergency drawdown.* Dated eleven months before the flood." He turned another page. "*Correspondence, Meridian Cascade Holdings, internal. Subject: cost containment. 'Drawdown will expose us to penalty clauses. Recommend continued monitoring.' Signed—*" He stopped.

"Signed who?" Amara said.

"A name I know." He closed the folder. Very carefully, the way you close a door on a room where someone has died. "These are the engineering reports. The internal memos. The settlement drafts. Everything the company swore never existed. Everything the inquiry said was lost in the flood."

"The flood," Amara said slowly. "The one from your—" She stopped herself. She'd pried before, on the second day, and gotten a look that had made her feel like she'd reached into a snare. But she'd pieced things together anyway, the way drifters do: the blacklisting, the brother, the gorge he wouldn't name until he had no choice. "This is about the disaster. The dam collapse."

"They didn't build it wrong," Zephyrine said. "They built it cheap, and they knew, and they let it stand. Eleven months they sat on reports saying it would fail. Four hundred percent." He laughed — one note, no humor in it, a sound like a branch cracking. "I spent six years believing I killed my brother because I misread a river. And the whole time there was a filing cabinet somewhere that said the river was set to kill whoever was on it that day."

The rain thickened. Nobody moved. Even the twins had gone still, and Bex's hand found Noor's shoulder without either of them seeming to notice.

"So burn them," Noor said. "Or throw them in the river. Not our war."

"It's exactly our war," Zephyrine said. "We're carrying it."

"Ines hired us to move boxes," Teodor said, and there was something new in his voice now, something careful. "What those boxes contain is above all our pay grades."

"And yet." Zephyrine flipped open the second folder, scanned it, and Amara watched him arrive at the next stage of recognition, the one she could see coming like the rebounder wave — visible only when too late. He went very still.

"What," she said.

He held the page out so she could see it. A ledger. Payments, dated across six years, itemized against names. Not company names. Family names. Names of villages. And there, halfway down, a village name Amara had heard twice in three days — once from Sefu, describing the best anchorage below the gorge head; once from Zephyrine, in his sleep, the night they'd tied up at the bend, spoken like a prayer or an apology.

*Tarik's Landing.*

"Disbursement schedule," Zephyrine said. "Compensation for silence. Land easements signed under pressure. Consulting fees to elders who agreed the failure was 'an act of God.'" His voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing. It got quieter and quieter, the way deep water is quiet. "The village that fed me for a year. The elders who told me the river took Dami, told me to stop blaming myself, told me God's ways are deep." He looked up, and his eyes were dry and terrible. "They knew. They've known since before I did. They took the company's money and let me carry the blame out of the gorge like a pack."

"You don't know that's what it means," Amara said. "Ledgers have two sides. Maybe they were coerced. Maybe—"

"Maybe." He handed her the folder, and his hand, finally, was shaking. "Read it yourself. Tell me what you see."

She read. She was good at documents — forged enough of them to know how they breathed. And what she saw was what he saw: payments structured small and regular, the signature of consent, not extortion. Villages that refused were marked in a different column, and their column was short, and beside some of those entries someone had written notes in a tidy corporate hand. *Litigation ongoing. Access restricted. Well contamination unresolved.*

Two columns. One for the silenced, one for the ruined. Tarik's Landing was in the first.

Amara closed the ledger and felt the moral ground tilt under her the way the deck had tilted under the rebounder wave. Because she understood — faster than she wanted to — what this paper actually was. It was vindication. It was the thing that would take six years of exile off Zephyrine's back and put it where it belonged. Any tribunal on two continents would reopen his case with this in evidence. He'd get his licenses back, his name, his life.

And the price of it would be paid by the people who'd fed him when no one else would. Every elder named in that ledger would hang. The village would be torn apart by inquiry and shame and lawyers, and whether they'd been coerced or complicit or some human mixture of both, they'd end in the second column — the ruined one. The truth didn't distinguish. Truth was a flood; it went through everything, and it didn't check who was sheltering in the cellar.

"That's why she wants it upriver," Amara heard herself say. "Ines. This isn't supplies. She's moving this to someone. A journalist, a tribunal, a buyer — someone at the gorge head. Before the monsoon closes the route."

Zephyrine looked at her, and for a moment something passed between them that had nothing to do with suspicion — the plain recognition of two people who had both survived by knowing when a story didn't add up.

"She said instruments," he said.

"She said a lot of things."

Teodor shifted his weight. "You're going to want to think carefully about your next decision," he said, and his hand had drifted, casually, to rest near the knife at his belt. "Because Ms. Kovač was very clear about the cargo."

"I'll bet she was." Zephyrine rose. He looked downriver, toward Tarik's Landing, invisible beyond the bend. Then upriver, toward the gorge head, where whatever Ines Kovač wanted waited in the rain. The storm clouds had finished coming down off the rim now, and the first real wind of it moved up the gorge, bending the vines, combing white feathers off the current.

"We re-lash the crates," Zephyrine said at last. "All of them. We keep to the contract, we keep to the schedule, and nobody speaks about what's in the paper until I've had words with Ines Kovač. Face to face."

"And if she doesn't want words?" Teodor said.

Zephyrine picked up the last folder, the one still lying open on the deck, and folded it shut against the rain.

"Then the storm's going to be the least of anyone's problems," he said. "Get us moving, Sefu. We've got a gorge to climb and a liar to meet at the top of it."

Amara bent to the lines beside him, tying down the secret they now all carried, and above them the first thunder rolled down the gorge walls like something enormous turning over in its sleep.
