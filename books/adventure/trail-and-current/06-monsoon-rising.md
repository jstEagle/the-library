## 6. Monsoon Rising

The sky over the Serein had been the color of wet slate since dawn, and by noon it had begun to bruise. Zephyrine stood at the wheel of the *Vela* with one hand on the spokes and the other shading his eyes, watching the western horizon where the clouds had stopped drifting and started building — stacking themselves in slow, deliberate towers, like masonry laid by something patient and enormous.

He had seen that sky before. Every river man had. The difference was that most of them had the good sense to be afraid of it.

"Barometer's falling fast," Amara said beside him. She'd taken to standing watch without being asked, her eyes moving across the water the way his did now, reading the current's mood in the small telltales — the way the eddy lines sharpened, the way driftwood rode higher and faster than it should have. "Half an inch since morning."

"Then we have maybe six hours before the front comes down the gorge." He said it flatly, because saying it any other way would mean admitting what he was already calculating: that six hours was not enough to reach the head of the gorge, and more than enough to drown them all if they stayed on the water when it broke.

Behind him, the deck was a clutter of tarped cargo, and somewhere under those tarps, in a crate stenciled with a shipping company's name that meant nothing to anyone honest, sat the truth about Dami. He had not opened it again since the night it broke open. He didn't need to. The words were printed on the inside of his eyelids now — *structural deficiencies known to management*, *downstream settlements compensated for silence*, *Kandé elders, disbursements logged*. The village that had fed him for two winters after the rescue destroyed him. The village whose children he'd taught to swim in the shallows below the falls. Their names were in that crate too, written in an accountant's hand, reduced to line items in a ledger of bought silence.

And Ines wanted it delivered upstream. To whom, she would not say. To what end, she smiled instead of answering.

The river bent around a bluff of red stone, and Zephyrine brought the bow around with the current rather than against it, saving the engine, saving time he did not know how to spend. Upstream lay the delivery, the contract, the money that would buy Amara new papers and pay off the last of his debts. Downstream, four kilometers back, half-hidden in the fold of the gorge where the Serein widened into brown glass, lay Kandé.

He could still see its smoke from here on a clear day. Three cooking fires, rising straight up in the windless hours before a storm.

"You've changed course twice in the last hour," Amara said quietly. Not accusing. Just noting, the way she noted everything, filing it away in whatever ledger she kept behind those careful eyes.

"I'm reading the water."

"You're reading something." She looked at him, then at the sky, then back. "Zephyrine. If that front hits us mid-gorge—"

"I know what it does mid-gorge."

"Do you? Because you haven't said a word about it, and I've run rivers through three countries, and I've never once seen a captain this quiet going into weather like that."

He almost laughed. Almost told her. Instead he said, "Trim the port tank. I want us riding light through the narrows," and watched her jaw tighten as she went to do it, and hated himself a little for the economy of it, the way he spent trust like a man spending coins he'd stolen.

* * *

Ines found him at the stern an hour later, when the first fat drops began spotting the deck planks like dark coins. She stood with her hands in the pockets of her oilskin, unhurried, watching him watch the water.

"The barometer," she said.

"Falling."

"So is your speed. We've lost forty minutes since noon. You're taking the outside of every bend — the long way — and you dropped to half-throttle through the narrows when there wasn't so much as a ripple." She turned to face him fully. Rain spotted her cheeks and she didn't blink. "I chartered this boat, Zephyrine. I read currents too."

"Then you read them better than you read people, because you hired a crew of strangers and put your evidence on a leaking hull."

"The hull held."

"It held *yesterday*."

For a moment neither of them spoke, and the rain thickened around them, drumming on the tarps, hissing where it struck the river. Somewhere ahead, low and long, thunder rolled down the gorge like a barrel tipped over at the top of the world.

"We push through tonight," Ines said. "We make the head of the gorge by dawn, ahead of the worst of it, and we're done. You get paid. I get what I came for. Everyone walks away richer than they arrived."

"And if we don't beat it?"

"Then we tie off and wait it out, same as any boat."

"There's no tying off above the narrows. You know that. There's no bank worth the name, no eddy deep enough, nothing between here and the head but vertical rock and water that'll be running thirty knots by midnight." He stepped closer, and the old rivalry rose in him like heat, familiar and useless. "You've never run this gorge in monsoon. I have. I buried my brother in it."

Something flickered across her face — there and gone, quick as a fish turning. "I know what you buried here."

"Then say what you actually want. Say why the delivery can't wait a day. Say who's waiting at the head of this gorge that matters more than the lives on this deck."

Ines looked past him then, downstream, toward the place where Kandé hid in its fold of the gorge, and Zephyrine saw it — saw her *know* — and understood with a cold, settling certainty that she had known all along. That the route, the timing, the season itself had been chosen not despite the village but because of it. That he had not been hired despite his history with this river. He had been hired *for* it.

"The delivery waits for no one," she said softly. "Not for weather. Not for grief. And certainly not for a village that sold its silence years ago and has been comfortable ever since." Her eyes came back to his. "Full throttle, Captain. We have a contract."

She walked forward along the deck, sure-footed in the rain, and left him standing at the wheel with his hands shaking — not with fear, though there was fear enough in the sky, but with the specific rage of a man who has just realized the trap closed months ago, in a dockside office, with a handshake.

* * *

He called the crew together under the aft canopy at dusk, when the rain had become a steady roar and the lightning out west strobed the gorge walls white, showing the water below them running high and fast and full of debris. Six faces in the lamplight. Amara at the edge of the group, arms crossed, watching him with that unblinking attention. Old Tomas at the engine hatch, oil to his elbows. The twins, Yusuf and Bala, dripping, young, looking to him the way crews always look to their captain in bad weather — as if the answer lived in his mouth.

"Here's where we are," Zephyrine said. "Storm front hits the upper gorge around midnight. Full monsoon cell, the kind that raises this river eight meters in a night. Above the narrows there is no safe harbor. Below us, four kilometers back, is Kandé landing, and above the landing there's a cut in the bank where the *Vela* can ride out anything the sky can throw."

Tomas wiped his hands slowly. "And the cargo?"

"The cargo goes upstream or it doesn't."

"That's not what I asked."

Zephyrine held the old man's gaze. Tomas had run the Serein longer than anyone aboard; Tomas had ferried company men up this same gorge in the years after the disaster, and had come back from those runs thinner each time, and had never once said why. "You know what's in that crate," Zephyrine said quietly. "All of you do, by now."

Silence, except the rain.

"My brother's name is in it," Zephyrine went on. "So are the names of people in Kandé who took company money to keep quiet about the dam failure — the failure that killed eleven people, Dami among them. Deliver it upstream, and maybe my name gets cleared. Maybe I stop being a ghost in two countries. And maybe the elders of Kandé hang for it, and the village that fed me when no one else would feed me burns down around them, because that's what happens when a town's shame goes public. Everything it built on the lie comes apart with it."

Amara spoke without raising her hand, the way she did. "And if we don't deliver it?"

"Then I break my contract, Ines ruins me with the charter house, and I stay exactly what I am." He heard how it sounded. He made himself finish anyway. "A blacklisted guide with a forged permit and a dead brother."

Yusuf shifted. Bala looked at his feet. It was Tomas who broke the silence, in his gravel way, not looking up from the engine hatch.

"When the company men ran this gorge," he said, "they paid me extra to not look in the crates. I took the money. Fed my family on it for three years." He closed the hatch and stood. "I've been paying it back ever since, one honest run at a time. Doesn't balance. Never balances." He met Zephyrine's eyes at last. "But I know which direction the paying goes. It doesn't go upstream."

Zephyrine nodded once, and couldn't speak for a moment, and covered it by turning to the wheel.

"Then we hold here," he said. "We anchor in the lee of the bluff and let the front pass, and tomorrow I tell Ines the river decided."

"And Ines?" Amara asked.

He looked downstream, into the gathering dark, where the lightning was coming faster now, and the river had begun to rise — he could see it, the water climbing the stones of the bank, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees.

"Ines signed a contract with a man who drowned once already," Zephyrine said. "She should have read the fine print."

They anchored in the lee of the bluff as the full weight of the storm came down the gorge — a wall of rain and wind that turned the world to noise, that snapped the tarp lines twice before they got them doubled, that made the *Vela* groan at her anchor like an animal testing a fence. Zephyrine stood the whole first watch himself, refusing relief, watching the black water climb and climb.

It was near midnight, soaked through, hollow-eyed, when Amara climbed to the wheelhouse with two cups of something hot and stood beside him without speaking for a long time.

"She'll try to take the boat up tonight," Amara said finally. "While you're tired. While the crew's split."

"I know."

"How do you know?"

"Because it's what I'd have done, ten years ago." He drank, and the cup shook slightly, and he steadied it against the wheel. "Because I just realized she didn't hire me to run the river at all. She hired me to be the one holding the wheel when the choice gets made — so it's my name on the mistake, not hers."

Lightning split the gorge, and in its white instant Zephyrine saw the water below them — and saw, riding high on the flood, turning slowly in the current, a shape he knew. A crate. Stenciled. One of theirs, torn loose from the deck, tumbling downstream toward Kandé like a letter the river had decided to deliver itself.

"Get Tomas," he said. "Wake everyone. Now."

Amara was already moving.
