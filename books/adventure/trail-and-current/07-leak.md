## 7. Leak

The debris came out of the dark without warning.

Amara heard it before she saw it — a wrongness in the river's voice, a hollow knocking beneath the rain, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. She was on the bow line, soaked to the bone, her hands so cold they'd stopped registering as hers. Ahead, the Serein had gone white with fury, and through the sheeting rain she caught a shape: a whole tree, root-ball up, riding just under the surface like a drowned giant's shoulder.

"Hard to port!" Zephyrine's voice cut through the storm, raw and certain.

Tomas threw his weight against the tiller. The Vela heeled, groaned, began to swing — but the current was faster than any of them. The root-ball slid beneath them with terrible patience, and then the hull struck with a sound Amara would carry for the rest of her life: not a crash but a crack, deep and structural, the sound of a promise breaking.

The boat lurched sideways. Crates tore free of their lashings. Amara went down hard against the gunwale, her hip flaring white with pain, and when she scrambled up, Zephyrine was already at the bilge hatch, his hand plunged into black water that was rising far too fast.

"How bad?" Tomas shouted.

Zephyrine didn't answer for a moment. Lightning lit his face, and what Amara saw there wasn't fear exactly. It was recognition. As if he'd been waiting years for this exact moment to arrive.

"She's split along a seam," he said finally. "We can't patch it underway. We bail, we run her shallow along the north bank, and we pray the gorge gives us a beach before the river takes the choice away."

"And if it doesn't?" Amara asked.

He looked at her across the pitching deck, rain streaming off his jaw. "Then we swim," he said, "and we find out which of us the Serein wants."

* * *

They bailed.

There was nothing else to do, so they did it — two buckets, a dented cooking pot, and later, when those failed, their cupped hands. The storm arrived in full as the night deepened, the monsoon coming down the gorge walls like an army, and the Vela crawled northward along the bank with water sloshing around their ankles, then their shins, while thunder rolled down the canyon in long, grinding avalanches of sound.

Amara bailed until her shoulders burned and her palms went soft and raw. Somewhere past midnight — though midnight was only a guess under that sky — Zephyrine took the bucket from her hands without asking and pushed her down onto a crate.

"You're done for an hour."

"I'm fine."

"You're bleeding." He nodded at her palms. "Sit. That's not a request."

She sat. It was strange how easily obedience came when the body was finished arguing. He crouched beside her, tore a strip from the hem of his shirt, and wrapped her hands with the brisk, impersonal competence of a man who had dressed wounds in worse places than this. His fingers were steady. Hers were not.

"You've done this before," she said. "Bandaged people."

"A long time ago." He tied off the knot. "In another life."

"The one where you knew this river?"

His hands stopped moving. For a moment she thought he wouldn't answer at all — she'd learned that stillness on him by now, the way he closed like a door whenever anyone asked about the Serein, about the brother whose name Ines had spoken at the dock like a knife laid flat on a table.

But tonight the door stayed open. Maybe because the boat was dying anyway. Maybe because there is a kind of honesty that only comes out of exhaustion, the way water comes out of a split hull.

"Dami," he said.

It took her a second to understand. Then she did, and said nothing, and let the rain fill the silence until he was ready.

"My brother. Two years older than me and twice as brave, which is to say half as smart. We grew up on this water. He could read the Serein like a priest reads scripture — every eddy, every lie the current tells. When the dam company started running barges up the gorge, they needed local pilots, and Dami signed on before I could talk him out of it." Zephyrine's voice was level, which somehow made it worse. "There was a flood. Not like this one — worse. A release from the upper dam that nobody warned us about. The company had cut the warning wire months earlier to save money on maintenance. Dami was mid-river with six passengers when the wall came down."

"What happened to him—"

"He got four of them out." Zephyrine looked at the water rising around their boots. "Four out of seven. He held two people above the water until his arms gave out, and I watched it happen from the bank. I was running the rescue line. My rope, my anchor point, my decision on when to pull the team back. I pulled too early. Or too late. Every inquiry we ever sat through disagreed on which, and both countries involved decided the answer was: my fault."

Amara understood, suddenly, why he measured everything in miles. Why he'd drilled them like a man preparing for judgment day. He hadn't been running from the river. He'd been running toward a chance to get it right, and hating himself for wanting to.

"I'm sorry," she said. It was inadequate and they both knew it, but some things you say anyway.

He shrugged, the smallest movement. "Your turn."

"What?"

"Fair trade. You've been on my boat three days with forged papers and no story. Nobody forges papers over nothing." He handed her the bucket again, gently. "Bail and talk."

* * *

So she told him.

Not all of it — never all of it — but more than she'd told anyone in six years. About the coastal town she'd grown up in, the one that appeared on maps as a fishing village and functioned as something else. About her father, who kept the ledgers for men whose names were never written down. About the night she was seventeen and copied a page out of one of those ledgers, thinking she was helping a boy she loved escape a debt, and discovered weeks later that the page had been used to frame a man who then disappeared, permanently, into the kind of silence that doesn't come back.

"They never proved it was me," she said. Her voice sounded strange to herself, thin under the rain. "But my father knew. And the men he worked for knew. So I left. I've been leaving ever since. New names, new borders, new boats. You learn to sleep light. You learn that every place you love is a place you'll have to leave quickly, so you stop loving places." She bailed a full bucket over the side, watched the river swallow it. "You asked once why I joined a crew running a gorge everyone says is cursed. Because cursed water doesn't ask questions. It just tries to kill you, honestly, face to face. There's a relief in that."

Zephyrine was quiet for a long time. The Vela groaned through a bend; lightning showed the gorge walls pressing close, black stone streaming with waterfalls that hadn't existed that morning.

"You know what I figured out," he said at last, "bailing next to you all night? Running doesn't work. I've run for six years. Changed countries, changed rivers, changed my name on documents almost as often as you have. And the thing I was running from was in the boat with me the whole time. It weighs nothing. It fits in a pocket. And it's heavier than the cargo." He glanced at her. "Whatever page you copied — you've been carrying it the same way."

"Yes."

"It gets lighter," he said. "When you set it down in front of someone. Not gone. But lighter."

The rain hammered. They bailed. And something settled between them — not spoken, not yet even nameable, but real as the rope he'd tied around her hands: the quiet knowledge of two people who had seen each other's worst rooms and hadn't left.

Near dawn, the storm shifted. The wind turned, and through the gray murk ahead Amara saw what Zephyrine saw a moment later — a pale smear in the black of the north bank. Shingle. A beach. Maybe two hundred meters upstream, maybe less.

"There," she shouted, pointing.

Zephyrine stared at it, and she watched something move across his face — not hope, exactly. Calculation. Because the head of the gorge lay beyond that beach, and beyond the head of the gorge lay the delivery Ines Kovač had paid for, and between here and there lay a sinking boat and a crew too exhausted to fight the current much longer.

"Tomas!" he roared. "Bring her in!"

And then, over the wind, from the stern, Ines's voice — calm, wet, immovable:

"We're not stopping. Push for the head of the gorge. That cargo reaches its destination tonight."

Amara saw Zephyrine turn, slow, rain pouring off him, and look at Ines Kovač the way a man looks at a rope he has just realized is tied around his own neck.

The Vela drove on toward the beach — and toward whatever waited past it.
