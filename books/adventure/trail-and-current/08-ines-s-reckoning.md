## 8. Ines's Reckoning

The storm arrived the way debts do — slowly, then all at once.

Zephyrine had been bailing since midnight, and his arms no longer belonged to him. They were someone else's arms, borrowed, aching, moving on contract. The Vela groaned under each swell, and the rain came down in sheets so thick that the gorge walls vanished entirely, leaving them adrift in a black corridor of water and noise. Somewhere behind him Amara was bailing too, her rhythm steady as a heartbeat, and somewhere ahead — he could feel it rather than see it — Ines stood at the bow under the canvas, dry, watching the dark like a woman reading a letter addressed only to her.

He hauled another bucketful over the side and let himself hate her for exactly three seconds. Then he went back to work. Hate was a luxury; the river didn't accept payment in anything else.

"Zephyrine." Her voice cut through the rain, pitched to carry. "I need you at the bow."

"I'm needed here," he shouted back, flinging another load of water into the flood. "The boat's needed here."

"The boat will still be leaking when you get up here."

She was right, which made it worse. He wedged the bucket between crates, wiped his face with a forearm that did nothing, and climbed aft along the gunwale with one hand on the line. The bow deck was slick as glass. Ines stood braced against the mast step, hood back, hair plastered flat, utterly unbothered by the water hammering her shoulders. She'd always been like that — weather happened to other people.

"You wanted to see me drown up close?" he said.

"I wanted to talk where the crew can't hear." She turned. Even in the lightning's stutter he could read her face: the set jaw, the eyes doing arithmetic. He'd seen that face across negotiation tables in three countries, usually just before she took something from him. "How much do you know about what we're carrying?"

Enough. More than enough. The broken crate in the shallows below the narrows, documents fanned across the rocks like a hand of cards nobody meant to show — company letterhead, survey reports, dates that matched the week the dam gates failed and the Serein took eleven people including his brother. He'd spent two days pretending he hadn't read every page while repacking them. "Cargo," he said. "That's all I was hired to move."

"Don't insult us both." Lightning lit her from behind, a white outline around a shadow. "You read them. I watched you read them. So you know what they're worth."

"To whom?"

"To whoever holds them." She stepped closer, and the boat rolled beneath them, and neither of them staggered, both of them too stubborn to give the storm the satisfaction. "Zephyrine. Listen to me. For once in your life, listen past your guilt. Meridian Hydro ruined you. They forged the inspection reports, they paid off the regional office, and when the gates failed they needed a name to hang it on, and yours was the one on the permit. You've spent six years being the man who let the Serein take his own brother. Do you know what these papers are? They're the truth, with signatures attached."

"And the village." His voice came out low and flat, the way it did when he was angriest, though only Amara seemed to have learned to hear it. "Kesh-Taru. The elders who signed the compensation agreements. Their names are in those pages too. Take the money, look away, keep the peace. Your truth has their fingerprints all over it."

"They took blood money," Ines said. "They knew."

"They fed me." The words came out before he could stop them, raw as an open wound. "When I crossed the border with nothing, when my name was worth nothing in four provinces, Kesh-Taru fed me. Old Mama Sesay gave me a bed when I hadn't slept indoors in a month. The elders knew who I was — they knew, Ines, and they sheltered me anyway. Whatever they took from Meridian, they took it because the flood gutted their fishing grounds and the company offered them crumbs or ruin. Don't stand there and tell me about knowing."

Ines was quiet for a moment. Rain drummed the canvas. Below, he heard Amara's bailing pause, then resume — slower now. Listening. He couldn't blame her.

"You think I don't know what they did for you?" Ines said at last, softer. "You think I haven't spent six years watching you bleed out over this river? That's why I came to you. That's why it had to be you running this route." She reached into her jacket — he tensed — but she only drew out a folded page, laminated against the wet, and held it where the next flash of lightning could show it to him. A photograph. A boardroom. Men in good suits, and among them, third from the left, a face he knew: Aldous Renner, Meridian's regional director, the man whose signature sat at the bottom of the forged inspection report that had ended Zephyrine's life. "Renner is retiring in the spring. There's a succession fight inside Meridian right now. Whoever controls this evidence doesn't just clear your name, Zeph. They control the company. They control the dams, the leases, the whole watershed. That's not justice. That's a throne."

"Then it's not mine, and it's not yours either."

"It's ours." Her hand closed on his wrist, grip like a shackle. "Don't you understand? We were both destroyed by these people. You lost Dami and your license and your country. I lost my outfit, my boats, my reputation — they buried me in the same report, Zeph, page forty-one, *contractor negligence, Kovač*. I've been climbing out of that page for six years. This is the first time anyone's handed me a ladder. And you're standing here telling me about *crumbs*."

He looked at her hand on his wrist. Then up at her face, and finally — six years late, in the worst possible place, in the teeth of a monsoon — he saw it clearly. Not ambition dressed as opportunity. Opportunity dressed as justice. She hadn't come to him because the evidence deserved daylight. She'd come because daylight was the cheapest way to make the evidence valuable, and leverage was only worth what someone would pay to keep it dark.

"You're not going to give this to investigators," he said slowly. "You're going to sell it. To Renner's rivals. Or back to Renner himself."

Ines didn't flinch. That was the thing about her; she never flinched. "I'm going to trade it. And everyone comes out ahead. Your name is cleared — I'll insist on it, it's part of any deal, it costs me nothing. The elders' names can be redacted, sealed, whatever the lawyers want. Nobody in Kesh-Taru goes to prison. But I walk back into the world with weight again, and you—" her grip tightened— "you get your life back. Your permits. Your name in the registries instead of the blacklists. Isn't that what you've wanted? Six years of border towns and forged papers and running cargo for men who spit when they say your name. One delivery, Zeph. One run up the gorge, and it's all returned to you."

*All returned to you.* As if a life were luggage left at a station, retrievable on presentation of the right ticket. As if the man he'd become in six years of exile were a coat he could simply put back on.

And that was when the rage came — not hot, but cold and total, sharpened by exhaustion until it felt almost like clarity. He thought of Dami in the water, twenty-two years old and waving, certain his brother would reach him. He thought of the inquiry room, the photographs of the drowned laid out like evidence of his character. He thought of Mama Sesay's hands, flour to the wrists, pressing bread into his because he wouldn't take money. Every one of those things Ines had just priced. Every one of them was currency in her ledger.

"You used my brother," he said.

"I used the truth—"

"You used *Dami*." His voice didn't rise. It dropped, and that was worse, and he saw her register it. "You put his name in your mouth at that dock because you knew it would crack me open, and it did, and I've been hauling your throne up this river ever since like a good beast of burden. You didn't hire a guide, Ines. You hired a weapon. Someone with nothing left to lose, pointed at the thing you want. And the village downstream — the people who kept me alive — they're just collateral in your negotiation. Redacted. Sealed. *Whatever the lawyers want.*"

"If the evidence burns bright enough, nobody looks at the edges—"

"That's not how light works!" He tore his wrist free of her grip. "That's not how any of it works. You can't aim a fire and promise it'll only burn what you choose. I learned that on this river six years ago. Eleven people learned it. You want to play games with Meridian's throne, fine — do it with your own hands. But I am done being held."

For a long moment neither of them moved. The storm filled the silence, vast and indifferent. Then Ines smiled, and it was the saddest thing he'd ever seen her do.

"You always were the honest one," she said. "It's why you lose."

"It's why I'm still here."

She looked past him, down the length of the boat, to where Amara stood motionless at the bail line, soaked through, watching them with an expression Zephyrine couldn't read in the dark. When Ines spoke again, her voice carried deliberately, pitched for the whole crew to hear.

"Then you should know the terms of your contract, guide. Full payment on delivery at the head of the gorge. That was the agreement. If we don't deliver, nobody gets paid, and the Vela and everything on her revert to me." She turned back to him, rain streaming down her face, eyes bright and hard as river stones. "So you have a choice to make before we reach the head. And you should make it soon. Because unless I'm mistaken about that sound—"

She paused. He listened. Beneath the rain, beneath the wind, beneath the groan of the hull, there was something new: a deep, rhythmic thunder rolling down the gorge from upstream, getting louder.

"—the flood surge is coming early. And this boat is taking on water faster than your crew can throw it overboard."

Zephyrine turned toward the sound. Upstream, where the gorge narrowed to its throat, the darkness was moving — a wall of it, coming down.

* * *

He was already moving before his mind caught up, six years of rivers compressed into instinct. "Amara! Get the crew on the lines — everything lashed double, cargo cut loose if it comes to it!" She didn't ask questions; that was the thing about her. She was moving before he finished shouting, waking Tomas and the brothers, driving them to the cleats with a voice that cracked like a whip.

"Ines!" He grabbed the bow line. "You want your delivery? Help me keep us alive to make it!"

She hesitated — one heartbeat, two — and he watched the calculation run behind her eyes, the ledger opening, the price of her own survival weighed against the dignity of refusing his help. Then the first edge of the surge hit the Vela and threw them all sideways, and Ines Kovač, rival, betrayer, queen-in-waiting, went to her knees on the wet deck and seized the line beside him like anyone else trying not to die.

The boat drove forward into the dark, into the throat of the gorge, riding a flood that wanted them dead — and ahead, barely visible through the hammering rain, the pale scar of a shoreline where a man might beach a sinking ship, if the river allowed it.

"Hold on!" Zephyrine roared, to the crew, to Ines, to the storm itself. "Hold on!"

The Vela answered with a shriek of timber, and drove on.
