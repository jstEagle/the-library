## 9. The Head of the Gorge

The Vela was dying, and Amara could feel it through the soles of her boots — that slow, shuddering surrender of a hull giving up its argument with the water. Every wave that broke over the bow now stayed. The bilge pumps had gone from rhythmic to desperate to silent an hour ago, and since then they had been bailing by hand, passing dented buckets in a chain that had become less a system than a prayer.

"Headland!" Zephyrine's voice cut through the rain like a blade through wet canvas. He stood at the wheel with his whole body braced against it, as if he could hold the boat together by will alone. "There — past the bend! The gravel shelf!"

Amara looked where he pointed and saw almost nothing: a pale smear in the murk where the gorge walls pinched inward, where the river, furious with monsoon, split itself around a spine of stone. The head of the Serein Gorge. The place on every chart she'd memorized, the place Zephyrine had spoken of only once, in the dark, in a voice she'd pretended not to hear.

"We'll never make it," shouted Tomas, hauling another bucketful over the side. His hands were bleeding; everyone's were.

"We make it or we drown here," Zephyrine said, "and I've drowned here before."

Amara didn't ask what he meant. There was no time for the old griefs. She wedged herself against the cargo rail, against the crate whose broken lid still gaped like a wound, papers inside swollen soft with water — the truth of the gorge disaster dissolving literally before her eyes, ink blooming into grey ghosts. She'd watched Zephyrine look at those documents for two days the way a man looks at a door he both needs and fears.

The river took the next decision out of anyone's hands.

A standing wave rose off the rock spine — green, towering, lit from within by some trick of the storm light — and the Vela climbed it. Amara felt the world tilt past vertical. Someone screamed. The crate tore free of its lashings and slid, and she threw herself on it without thinking, arms locked around sodden cardboard, and then the world dropped out from under them all.

* * *

They hit the gravel shelf sideways.

The impact went through the hull like a bone breaking. Amara was flung across the deck, shoulder-first into the winch housing, and came up gasping in six inches of water — water that should not be on deck, water rising. The Vela groaned, settled, groaned again, and began, unmistakably, to die.

"Off! Everyone off!" Zephyrine was already moving, cutting the last lines, shoving Tomas toward the rail. "High ground — the shelf holds, the bank doesn't. Move!"

Ines Kovač stood amidships, soaked to the skin, hair plastered flat, one arm locked around the crate Amara had shielded with her body. Her face in the lightning-flash was terrible and calm.

"The evidence comes with us," Ines said.

"The evidence weighs forty kilos and it's already pulp," Zephyrine snapped. "Leave it."

"It is the only thing on this river worth anything—"

The river answered for him. A surge swept the deck, waist-high, and tore the crate from between them. Amara lunged — caught it — and then the current had her too, dragging her toward the gap in the rail, toward the white water churning between the shelf and the far wall. She got one hand on the rail stanchion. The crate hammered against her ribs. Below her, the flood ran brown and fast as a stampede.

She heard Zephyrine shout her name — not "Amara" even, just her name, raw, stripped of everything — and then he was there, flat on the deck, one fist closed around her wrist, the other hand catching the stanchion above hers.

"Let it go!" he roared.

"I have it—"

"You'll go under with it! Let it GO!"

And she saw it then, in the half-second the lightning gave her: his face above hers, and behind his face something older — a brother's hand slipping from a grip exactly like this one, years ago, in water exactly like this. She understood why he'd never come back to this river. She understood what it cost him to hold on now instead of letting the river take its tax and moving on, the way he'd moved on from everything else his whole running life.

He did not let go.

"Zeph—" Her voice cracked. The crate twisted in her arms, heavy as a coffin lid, full of paper that would make him innocent and make a village guilty, full of leverage Ines wanted and truth he couldn't carry and save everyone at once.

"Let it go," he said again, quieter, and it wasn't about the crate anymore, and they both knew it.

Amara opened her arms.

The evidence went into the flood and vanished — swallowed whole, forty kilos of vindication spinning away downstream toward the village it would have destroyed, toward the elders who'd taken company money, toward every version of justice that required someone else to burn. She felt it leave like losing a limb. Then Zephyrine hauled, and she came up over the rail and onto the deck, and his arms were around her, and for three seconds neither of them was bailing or steering or deciding anything.

Then the Vela shuddered a final time and began to roll, and there was no more time at all.

* * *

The gravel shelf gave them thirty meters of soaked stone and shingle between the wreck and the cliff base, and the river was coming for even that. Zephyrine counted heads — Tomas, Priya, the twins, Ines, himself, Amara — seven, all seven, and pointed up.

"There's a goat track. Switchbacks, maybe four hundred meters. It tops out above flood line." He was shouting over the storm but his voice had changed; it was steady now, the steadiness of a man who has finally stopped arguing with himself. "Single file. Rope. Nobody stops on the low sections."

"And my contract?" Ines stood at the waterline, staring at the brown torrent where her crate had gone, and when she turned to him her eyes were dry and furious. "You just threw ten years of your life into that river."

"No," Zephyrine said. "I threw yours."

He clipped the rope to the first anchor bolt — old, rusted, drilled by hands he probably knew, maybe his own, maybe Dami's — and sent Tomas up first. The climb was misery: rain like thrown gravel, wind that tried to peel them off the rock, footholds running with water. Amara climbed third, and twice the flood surged high enough to slap her boots, and each time she looked down and found Zephyrine below her, last but one, watching every handhold she chose.

On the third switchback the track crossed a gully, and the gully had become a waterfall.

It happened fast, the way disasters do — no drama, just physics. Priya went across fine. Amara stepped onto the crossing and the water hit her mid-thigh, cold as a verdict, and her foot found not rock but nothing, and she was sliding, backward, down the gully toward the flood plain where the river waited with everything the gorge had ever taken.

She caught a root. It held for one second. It held long enough for her to see Zephyrine coming down the switchback above her at a speed no sane climber would attempt, rope paying out behind him, and to think, absurdly: *he's done this before, he's done exactly this, this is the rescue that ruined him and he's doing it again anyway.*

The root tore free.

His hand closed on her forearm — the same grip, the same iron, higher up the slope this time — and her swing slammed her against the gully wall and knocked the air out of her, and still he held. He braced his boots against the rock, wrapped the rope twice around his forearm, and pulled, screaming something at her she couldn't hear over the water, his face two feet from hers, blood running from somewhere near his hairline into his eyes.

"Climb," she read off his lips, and climbed.

Hand over hand up his arm, up the rope, onto the switchback, onto solid stone. He came up after her slower, favoring his wrenched shoulder, and knelt beside her in the rain, checking her pulse at her throat with two fingers, counting, until she pushed his hand away and nodded that she was whole.

"You let go," he said. Not accusation. Wonder.

"You held on," she said. "That's the harder thing."

Something passed between them then that the storm couldn't touch — brief, wordless, settled. Then he was up, counting heads again, driving them upward, because the flood was still rising and mercy was a luxury the mountain didn't stock.

* * *

They topped out at dusk, if a storm could be said to have a dusk — a bruised grey dimming into black, the gorge below them a single roaring vein of brown. From the ridge they could see downstream, miles of it, and there, huddled lights: the village, Kessou-les-Berges, its lamps burning against the weather, its people who had fed a disgraced guide once and asked nothing, who would wake tomorrow to a flood and never know what had been carried toward them and destroyed above their roofs.

Tomas built a fire that refused to die. They huddled around it, steaming, silent, seven people who owed their lives to a man most of them had hired on with a week ago.

Ines sat apart, at the fire's edge, looking down at the river. When Amara brought her a cup of tea — because exile teaches you that even enemies get tea — Ines took it without looking up.

"He burned ten years," Ines said quietly. "I hope whatever he bought with it was worth the price."

Amara followed her gaze to where Zephyrine stood at the ridge's edge, alone, watching the water run. He wasn't watching it like a man measuring escape routes anymore. He was watching it like a man taking attendance of his dead — and finding, perhaps, that they could rest now, that the account was finally balanced, paid not in truth or vindication but in something heavier and better.

He turned. Found her looking at him. And smiled — small, exhausted, real, the first one she'd seen on him that reached his eyes.

Then his smile faded, and he looked past her, downriver, and went very still.

"Amara." His voice had dropped all its exhaustion at once. "The village lights. Count them."

She counted. Fourteen. She'd counted them yesterday from the water, passing in the dark.

There were fourteen lamps burning in Kessou-les-Berges tonight.

And the village had only ever had thirteen houses.
