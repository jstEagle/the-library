## 1. The Nothing That Tracks Back

The basin taught arithmetic before it taught anything else. Isandro Ishikawa had learned its sums young: one horse drinks four gallons a day, one man needs water for three days past his last known well, one lie costs a season of trust. The desert kept its books clean. It never forgave a debt, never rounded in a debtor's favor, never cared who you had been before the numbers came due.

He crouched at the edge of the dry wash where the thief had turned north, and read the ground the way other men read letters.

A shod horse, left hind throwing slightly—nicked shoe, or a stone bruise the rider hadn't bothered with. The stride was long but the prints were shallow at the toe, which meant the animal was tired, which meant the rider was pushing it, which meant the rider was in a hurry and stupid in equal measure. Stolen horses were like stolen water: the taking was easy, the keeping was where men died.

Fifteen years in the escarpment country had worn Isandro down to the parts that worked. He was forty-one and looked older in the flat light, gray coming into his black hair at the temples like frost on a fence post. He owned a rifle, a bedroll, a mule named Censo who complained constantly and obeyed absolutely, and a camp kit that fit in one pannier. He owned nothing that owned him back. That had been the whole design.

The mare he was tracking belonged to a widow two days' ride south, a woman named Herrera who had paid him in jerky, coffee, and the kind of gratitude that didn't ask questions. Four hundred head of nothing but one gray mare with a white sock, and her dead husband's saddle on its back, and she'd cried when she said *please*. Isandro had taken the job because the alternative was sitting alone in his cabin doing the arithmetic of his own stores, and that ledger always came out the same: enough, barely, always barely.

He followed the wash north through the morning, letting the tracks come to him rather than hurrying after them. The basin rewarded patience and punished appetite. Men who moved fast in this country left sweat and noise behind them like a trail of spilled grain, and everything that lived here—coyote, buzzard, and the two-legged kind worst of all—followed spilled grain.

By noon he could see the thief's camp from a ridge a half mile out: a scrap of canvas strung between creosote, a small fire burned to white ash, the gray mare hobbled at the edge of the shade. The man himself was asleep, or drunk, or both. Isandro lay on the ridge with his hat pulled low and watched for an hour, because an hour of watching was worth a week of guessing.

The man was young. Twenty, maybe. Boots too good for his coat. He'd built his fire against the canvas windbreak, which was wrong twice over—it smoked the shelter and it threw his shadow long across the sand every time he moved, a flag announcing him to anyone with eyes. He'd watered the mare from a skin but not himself, going by the untouched canteen beside his bedroll. A boy running from something that scared him more than thirst.

Isandro worked out the approach in his head, the way he worked out everything: down the far side of the ridge, along the gravel bar where his boots would print nothing, around through the mesquite. Take the mare's hobble off quiet. If the boy woke, show him the rifle and let him choose. Isandro had put iron on men before and he'd buried two of them in unmarked ground, and both burials sat in him like stones in a boot, and he did not intend to add a third for a horse worth forty dollars.

He came down through the mesquite with the sun at his back, moving when the wind moved, still when it stilled. The mare lifted her head at twenty feet and blew once, soft, and Isandro made a low sound in his throat that old horses remembered from their mothers. She stood quiet while he slipped the hobbles and gathered her lead, and the boy slept on, dreaming whatever thin dreams he'd brought into the basin.

Isandro was thirty feet clear when the boy woke, and by then it was over. He sat the gray mare with the rifle across his thighs and waited while the boy stood up too fast, saw him, and went through all the faces—fear, calculation, the beginning of the lie.

"She threw a shoe," Isandro said. "You've been riding her on it for two days. Another day and she'd have gone lame, and then you'd have been walking."

The boy's mouth worked. "I was going to—"

"No, you weren't." Isandro turned the mare's head south. "Water's at the Herrera place, eighteen miles. Tell them Isandro sent you and they'll fill your skin. Tell them anything else and they'll know you're lying, because I don't send liars."

He left the boy standing in the ash of his own bad fire and rode south, and he did not look back, and the not-looking-back was its own kind of arithmetic: one horse returned, one life unruined, one more year in which the desert had asked nothing of him he couldn't pay.

* * *

He made the Herrera place by the second evening, and the widow fed him stew and asked nothing, which was the other reason he took her jobs. On the third morning he turned Censo north toward his own country—the high bench below the escarpment where he kept a cabin no map bothered with—and felt the particular ease that came of a task closed out clean. Debts paid. Books balanced.

He camped that night in the lee of a sandstone rise, a place he'd used a hundred times, and he did what he always did: small fire, deadfall only, burned down to ash and scattered before he slept. Censo picketed on good grass. Rifle by his hand. He slept the way he'd taught himself to sleep over fifteen years—lightly, in layers, the ear still working while the mind stood down.

He woke once in the black hour before dawn, and did not know why.

That was the thing about the basin. It taught you arithmetic, but it also taught you to trust the remainders—the numbers that didn't come out even, the small wrongness that had no name yet. Isandro lay still and listened. Wind in the creosote, the ordinary whisper of it. Censo shifting, the ordinary complaint. Nothing else.

He got up anyway, and banked the fire coals, and walked out a hundred yards to where the ground opened into hardpan, and stood in the cold looking at nothing.

In the gray of first light he went back with the sun low behind him, so that the shadows stood up out of every depression, and he walked the ground around his camp in a slow spiral, the way he'd read a hundred tracks before.

He found them forty feet out, on the side away from the wind.

Boot prints. A man, walking, not riding—someone who had come in on foot in the night, circled his camp once at a careful distance, and stood. The prints at the standing place were deeper than the walking prints, weight settled, patient. Whoever it was had stopped there long enough to watch him sleep.

Isandro crouched and did not touch anything. The stride was medium, the gait even, the right foot landing a fraction heavier than the left. The boots were good ones, worn at the outside edge of the heel. And the circling itself—that was the part that sat wrong in him. A curious stranger blunders. A thief creeps straight to what he wants. This had been a *circuit*, deliberate, the way a man walks the boundary of a place he's measuring, or the way—

The way someone checks a trapline. The way a ranger reads a camp before deciding whether to come in.

The way *he* would do it.

He told himself the country was full of men who moved like that. He told himself it was coincidence, the right-foot heaviness, the patient stand, the angle of approach that had come in exactly along the line of his own blind side—the side a man watches less because he trusts his fire to cover it. He told himself all of it, and the arithmetic didn't come out, and he stood there in the cold morning with the remainder sitting in his hands like a stone.

Because there was one more thing. The prints circled and then departed, and where they departed, the walker had paused a second time—facing his camp, back to the open basin—and beside that second standing place, half-scuffed by wind but not gone, was a small mark pressed into the hardpan with a boot heel. Two short strokes and a longer one.

He hadn't seen that mark in fifteen years. He had made it a thousand times before that, on fence posts and trail cairns and the doorframe of a house that no longer stood, and he had taught it to exactly one other person, on a summer when the wells were already failing and neither of them knew yet what the drought was going to take.

It was a trail sign. Family sign. The Ishikawa mark for *I am here, and I know you are too.*

Isandro straightened slowly. Censo watched him from the picket with the flat patience of animals, and the sun cleared the sandstone rise and put his shadow out long across the prints, his shadow and the stranger's prints joined on the same ground.

He looked north, toward the escarpment, toward the high bench and the cabin and the passes that would close with snow in six weeks, and the old solitude that had fit him like a coat for fifteen years suddenly hung wrong on his shoulders.

Someone was tracking him. Someone who knew the old signs.

Someone, by every measure the basin offered, who was family—and the family he had, he had buried, or buried himself from, a long time ago.
