## 2. A Debt in Dry Country

The mare came back to him on the third day, as most things in the basin did if you were patient enough to let them.

Isandro had tracked her south along the dry wash, reading the country like a ledger of small debts: a scuffed stone where the thief had cut a corner, a thread of mane hair snagged on greasewood, the particular way a stolen horse drinks—too fast, too deep, trusting water she shouldn't trust. The thief himself he'd found at noon, asleep under an overhang with his boots off and his rifle out of reach, which told Isandro everything about him that mattered. He took the man's boots and rifle first, then woke him with the flat of his hand against a cheek already sunburned to leather.

"Walk east," Isandro said. "You'll find water at the old windmill by dark. After that, keep walking."

The man looked at him with the dull arithmetic of a man doing sums he'd already lost. "You're not going to—" He stopped. Recalculated. In this country, men who talked about what they weren't going to do were usually deciding whether they could afford to. This one decided he couldn't.

"You stole from a horse," Isandro said, "that belonged to a woman who uses it to get her kids to school. That's the whole of your crime. It's small. Keep it small."

He watched the man shuffle east until the heat shimmer took him, then saddled the mare and pointed her north, toward the escarpment and the thin line of his own camp smoke, which he could see now even from ten miles out because he knew exactly where to look for it. Fifteen years in this country had taught him one thing above all others: nothing disappears here. It only gets smaller, and waits.

* * *

Sef Almada arrived two days later, riding a gray mule and leading a second one loaded with supplies, which was how Sef traveled everywhere—never light, never fast, always arriving as though the world owed him a road.

Isandro saw him coming from the ridge and did not go down to meet him. That was its own kind of message, and Sef read it perfectly, reining up at the edge of camp and sitting there while Isandro finished salting the last of the venison. The wind moved between them, carrying sage and the mineral smell of rock baking.

"Ishikawa." Sef said the name carefully, the way a man sets down something heavy on uncertain ground.

"That's not my name."

"No." Sef swung down and stood working his gloves through his hands. He'd gone gray at the temples since Isandro had last seen him—five years? six?—but the eyes hadn't changed. They were still the eyes of a boy who'd kept the best ledgers in three counties and never once written down a debt he couldn't collect. "Reyes, then. Or whatever we're calling ourselves these days."

"We're not calling ourselves anything. You're trespassing."

"On open range?" Sef smiled without warmth. "I'm looking for a ranger."

"You're looking for a ranger who doesn't ask questions. Those are different jobs, and I'm the first kind."

Sef untied the pack mule anyway, unhurried, and set a kettle on Isandro's own fire with the ease of a man who had done it a hundred times in another life. Isandro let him. There was no point defending ground you'd already ceded, and the truth was the coffee smelled good, and the truth was also that some part of him—the part he kept locked below the waterline—had been waiting for this visit the way scar tissue waits for weather.

"The Almada post was robbed," Sef said, pouring. "Three weeks ago. Not money. Not stock. A book."

"A book."

"The ledger. The master ledger. Every account, every deed, every water right recorded in this county for forty years. My father's hand, my grandfather's hand, all of it in one binding." Sef handed him a tin cup. "A woman took it. Alone. Rode out before dawn and left no trail anyone at the post could follow."

"And you followed it yourself?"

"For two weeks." Sef drank. "Then I lost her above the tree line, and winter's coming early this year. When the passes close, that book is sealed in the high country until thaw—and I can't wait until thaw."

"Why not?"

"Because paper rots, Ishikawa. Because accounts don't forgive delay. Because there are men in the valley who've been waiting twenty years for me to show a weakness, and a missing ledger is a weakness." He set the cup down. "Triple rate. Double discretion. No questions asked, none answered. Find the thief, recover the book, bring both or neither back—I don't care which. Just bring the ledger down before snow."

Isandro turned the cup slowly in his hands. Above them the escarpment caught the late light and held it, red as a wound.

"Describe the thief."

"Young woman. Dark hair. Good rider—better than good. She went through the pass country like she'd been born to it." Sef paused, and Isandro heard the pause for what it was: a door held slightly open. "There's more. She left something behind at the post. A note, pinned where the clerk would find it. It said—" Sef recited it flatly, without inflection, the way you'd read a bill of sale. "*Tell the Almadas the water remembers.*"

The wind came down off the escarpment and moved through the sage, and for a moment Isandro could not hear anything else at all.

*Water remembers.* Their mother used to say that. Standing at the dry well on the homestead, in the last summer, when the corn had come up brown and curled and their father sat on the porch steps doing sums that always ended the same way. *The water remembers where it used to go, mijos. We're just the ones who forgot.*

He put the cup down very carefully, so that no one watching would know his hands had changed.

"I don't take the job," he said.

"It pays triple."

"I don't take the job."

Sef studied him for a long moment, and Isandro watched the ledger-keeper's mind work behind his eyes—not anger, not surprise, but calculation, the patient arithmetic of a man who has already priced every outcome. "Because of the name," Sef said finally. Not a question.

"Because of the name."

"You erased it fifteen years ago. I watched you do it. You signed the ranger papers as Reyes and walked into this basin and you have not once, in fifteen years, spoken the word *Ishikawa* aloud. I checked. Men who trade posts talk, and I listen." Sef rose, brushing off his knees. "So tell me why a dead name should cost you triple pay."

"Because dead things stay buried. That's the whole point of burying them."

"And if someone digs?" Sef gathered his reins, unhurried. "The offer stands until the snow falls. I'll be at the waystation at Redrock Gap assembling a crew—you'd have company on the climb, if you change your mind. Odile Ferran, if you know the name. A farrier boy named Tomas. And me." He mounted the gray mule. "Think about it, Ishikawa. The water remembers. Maybe it's time somebody asked it what."

He rode south. Isandro did not watch him go. He stood instead looking north, at the escarpment, at the high country going white-capped earlier every evening, and he made himself finish the coffee Sef had poured, because wasting it would have been one more thing lost.

* * *

He told himself he wouldn't follow the trail. He told himself that for four days, and for four days he worked his rounds—fence lines, a spring dispute between sheepmen, a calf bogged in the alkali flats—and each night he came back to camp and each morning the thought was there again, patient as the tracks themselves.

On the fifth day he rode north past the boundary of his own district, telling himself it was patrol business, that the thief had crossed onto land he was sworn to cover, that duty was duty regardless of whose name was written in whose book. Dread dressed itself in duty's clothes and rode with him, and he let it, because the alternative was to admit he was chasing a ghost across country he'd spent fifteen years learning how not to look at.

The trail surfaced at the mouth of Ishikawa Draw.

He almost missed it—that was the thing. Anyone else would have. The sign was old, three weeks at least, half-eaten by wind, but he read it the way he read everything: a hoofprint angled wrong for a horse simply passing through, a place where a rider had dismounted and stood a long time, weight settled on both feet, facing the draw. Facing home.

She had stood there. Whoever she was, she had stood at the mouth of the draw and looked up at the ruins the way pilgrims look at shrines, and then she had ridden in.

Isandro followed.

The homestead came up out of the earth the way it always did in his memory and never quite the way it was: the house fallen to its foundation stones, the barn roof folded like a dropped wing, the corral rails bleached bone-white and standing out of the dust like ribs. Fifteen years of wind had done what drought had started. But the wind had not touched the well, and the wind had not touched the graves, and those were the two places he rode to first, because he was his mother's son and he knew what mattered.

The well was dry, as it had been dry for twenty years. But someone had cleared it. The stones around the lip had been reset—recently, carefully, by hands that understood masonry—and someone had lowered a lantern down the shaft, because the soot marks were fresh on the inner wall. As if looking for something. As if remembering where something was.

His chest went tight. He made himself turn to the graves.

Two markers. His father's, granite, bought with the last of the sale money—a fact that had always tasted like iron—and his mother's, a simple fieldstone he'd carved himself at nineteen, the letters already softening. He had not come back in fifteen years. He had told himself the desert needed him elsewhere, and the desert had agreed, and the lie had held for a decade and a half because lies hold as long as you never test them.

Someone had tested it.

His mother's marker had been moved. Lifted and reset—an inch off true, the soil beneath disturbed, packed down again by careful hands. Not robbed. Nothing taken that he could see. Disturbed the way you disturb a grave when you're saying something to the person in it. Or asking something.

Isandro knelt in the dust beside the stone and put his palm flat against the carved letters—*Rosa Ishikawa*—and felt the cold of the granite come up through his glove, and for a long moment he did not move at all.

Around him the draw held its silence. The corral ribs. The collapsed roof. The dry well that remembered water.

*A woman,* Sef had said. *Dark hair. Rode the pass country like she'd been born to it.*

Marisol had been born to it. Marisol had learned to ride in this draw before she could write her own name, and she had been seventeen the last time anyone saw her, walking east down the county road with a carpetbag and no backward glance, and Isandro had signed the papers that put her name on the same list as their parents': deceased, unclaimed, estate dissolved. He had buried her alive on paper because it was the only mercy the law allowed, and he had never once, in fifteen years, let himself wonder whether she'd done the same to him.

He stood. His knees cracked. Somewhere above the tree line, in country already whitening, a woman who might be his sister was carrying a book that contained the exact price their family had sold itself for—and Sef Almada wanted it back before the snow closed the passes, paying triple for silence.

Isandro looked north, where the escarpment climbed into cloud, and understood with terrible clarity that he had already taken the job. He just hadn't admitted it yet.

He broke camp at dawn and rode for Redrock Gap.
