## 3. Crew of the Sworn-Off

The last waystation before the escarpment was called the Anvil, though no smith had worked there in thirty years, and it sat at the end of the wagon road like a tooth left standing after the rest had rotted out of a jaw. Tomas Vey arrived two hours before dark with his kit on his back and his father's old shoeing hammer wrapped in oilcloth inside it, and he stood in the yard looking up at the black timbered face of the range beyond and felt something in his chest that was not quite fear and not quite joy but lived in the country between them, where most true things lived.

He was nineteen years old and he had never been above the tree line.

Inside, the waystation smelled of pine pitch, cold ash, and coffee that had been made by someone who hated coffee or hated whoever would drink it. A woman sat alone at the long table cleaning a rifle that did not need cleaning. She had gray shot through her black hair like frost coming into a window, and she did not look up when the door opened, which told Tomas she had already looked—through the window, when he crossed the yard—and had already decided he was nothing worth her attention.

"You'd be the farrier," she said anyway.

"Tomas Vey."

"Odile." She ran a patch through the barrel one more time, then set the rifle down with the care other people gave to sleeping children. "Sef said a boy."

"I'm not—" He stopped. Arguing with the first sentence of a thing never went anywhere good. "I shoe horses. That's the whole of what Sef hired."

"That's the whole of what anybody's hired for." She nodded at the stove. "Coffee's on. It's an insult. Drink it anyway; you'll want the bitterness later."

He poured a cup. It was worse than she'd promised, scorched and thin, and he drank it standing because sitting down felt like agreeing to something. Through the one clean pane of the station's window he could see the trail they'd come up—the switchbacks below the timberline, the basin spread out beyond them in the failing light, all of it going gold and then going gray—and somewhere down there, a day behind or less, was Isandro Ishikawa.

Tomas had seen him exactly once, three years ago, at the spring gather outside Corrido Flats. He'd been seventeen and holding horses for men who talked over him, and Ishikawa had come through with a string of strays he'd cut out of the badlands, and everything about him had been quiet in a way that made the loud men louder. He'd checked a rope burn on Tomas's mare without being asked, said four words total, and ridden off before anyone could thank him. Tomas had thought about those four words more times than he could count. *She'll keep. Watch the near hind.* It wasn't wisdom. It was just attention, given freely to a boy nobody gave anything to. But Tomas had built a whole architecture on top of it, the way you can build a house around a single nail if the nail is driven deep enough.

Now he was on a crew with the man, and the man wasn't even here yet, and the man who *was* here—

Sef Almada came in from the corral as the light died, stamping the cold off his boots, and Tomas understood immediately that he had misjudged the size of the room. Sef was not a large man. He was a compact one, silver at the temples, with the kind of handsomeness that had gone leathery rather than soft, and he moved through the waystation like he owned it, which maybe he did, maybe he owned half the county, but it was more than that. He moved like a man who had bought things all his life and expected the world to be for sale too.

"The boy," Sef said, looking Tomas over the way he'd look over a horse—one hand's breadth along the neck, so to speak, checking teeth. "You've shod for my post before."

"Yes, sir. Two seasons."

"And your work held?"

"It held."

Sef smiled as if Tomas had said something charming. "It held. Good. Up there, held is the only word I want to hear." He poured himself coffee without asking, drank it without flinching, which told Tomas something about the man—that he could take an insult straight and make it look like hospitality. "We move at first light. Ishikawa makes camp tonight at the Red Fork, if he's keeping the pace I paid him to keep, and we join him on the trail tomorrow. Nobody waits for anybody above the snow line. Understood?"

Odile didn't answer. She just began putting her rifle away, piece by piece, which was its own kind of answer.

* * *

They ate beans and salt pork and hard bread, and Sef talked, and Tomas learned more from what Sef didn't say than from what he did.

He didn't say what was in the ledger. He called it *the item*, twice, and *the book*, once, and corrected himself each time with a small tight motion of his jaw, like a man swallowing a stone. He didn't say why a trading post ledger was worth triple pay and a clause about discretion written into the contract in Sef's own hand—he showed them the paper across the table, and Tomas read it upside down: *recovery, condition intact, no copies, no talk.* He didn't say who stole it, except once, when Odile asked flat-out, and Sef said, "Someone who knew the post," and then changed the subject to the weather, which was coming in wrong for the season and everyone at the table knew it.

Tomas washed the plates because that was what he did on crews—the youngest man eats last and cleans up, and he'd never minded, because the sink was a place where nobody watched you and you could think.

He was thinking now, and what he was thinking was this: Sef Almada kept looking toward the door. Not nervously. The opposite of nervously. He looked toward the door the way a lender looks at collateral—not hoping it walks in, exactly, but wanting to feel its weight in the room. Twice, when the wind rattled the boards, Sef's head came up sharp, and both times he turned the motion into reaching for the coffee pot, and both times the coffee pot was already empty, and both times he pretended otherwise.

And when Sef spoke of Ishikawa—which he did three times over supper, always casually, always angled—it was never *is he trustworthy*. It was never *will he do the job*. It was *he'll come*, said with certainty, and *he owes me nothing, which is why I pay him*, said with a smile that didn't reach anywhere near his eyes, and once, low, almost to himself, looking into the fire: "Fifteen years is a long time to spend being nobody. Men get tired of it. Men want their names back." And then he'd caught Tomas watching and put the smile back on like a hat.

Tomas dried the last plate and set it down and thought: *that's not how you talk about a hired tracker. That's how you talk about a thing you're holding paper on.*

* * *

Later, unable to sleep in the bunkroom's horse-smelling dark, he went out to check the stock, because that was the other thing he did, the real thing, the thing under the thing. Three mules and Sef's bay gelding stood hipshot in the corral under a sky so full of stars it looked bruised. The gelding had thrown a shoe on the climb and Tomas had reset it that afternoon, and he ran his thumb along the clinches now, feeling for the work, the way you'd touch a scar to be sure it closed.

"You'll ruin your eyes," Odile said, from the fence rail, where she'd been sitting so quietly the mules hadn't bothered telling him she was there.

"They're ruined anyway. Everyone says so. Farrier squints at firelight till he's forty and blind at fifty."

"Then don't waste the years you've got." She passed him a flask without ceremony. It was some kind of spirit that tasted like pine tar and regret. "You were watching Sef at supper."

Tomas nearly dropped the flask. "I was eating."

"You were watching. Good. Keep doing it." She took the flask back and drank like the pine tar was water. "A crew goes wrong in one of two ways. Bad luck, or bad arithmetic. Luck you can't help. Arithmetic you can see coming, if you watch the man doing the sums."

"What sums is he doing?"

Odile was quiet a while. Below them the basin had gone fully dark, a sea of it, and the range above was a blacker wall, and somewhere up in that blackness was a pass that would close with snow within the month, and somewhere behind them on the trail was a man who had spent fifteen years becoming nobody.

"When I was younger I owed Almada money," she said at last, which was so far from anything Tomas expected that he just stared. "Not much. Enough. Enough that for two years every job I took, every track I followed, part of it was his. You know what that does? It doesn't make you dishonest. It makes you *careful*. You start reading every contract twice. You start noticing when a man writes *no copies, no talk* into a recovery clause for a *ledger*." She looked at him sideways. "Ledgers are records, boy. Records are proof. You don't hire three people and pay discretion wages to get back a record unless what's recorded in it is worth more buried than found."

"You think he means to destroy it."

"I think Sef Almada has never in his life paid triple for a thing he wanted *kept*." She handed the flask back. "And I think our tracker knows it, or will know it soon, and I am very interested in what a man like Ishikawa does when he finds out the job isn't the job."

"Why me?" Tomas said. "Why tell me?"

"Because you watch him too. Not Sef. Him." She nodded at the dark south trail, the Red Fork, the invisible campfire. "All through supper, whenever Sef said Ishikawa's name, you went still. Like a dog hearing its owner's step." She swung down off the rail. "That's either going to save this crew or sink it. Get some sleep, farrier. First light."

She went inside. Tomas stayed at the rail a while longer, working the gelding's clinches again without needing to, listening to the wind come down off the escarpment carrying the first smell of the season's snow—clean, mineral, indifferent—and thinking about the difference between a man you work for and a man you'd follow, and how rarely they were the same man, and how he had signed a contract with one and pinned fifteen years of hoping on the other.

First light came gray and early. They saddled in silence, breath steaming, the mules sour about the cold, and Sef stood in the yard consulting a watch he didn't need, and Odile scanned the south trail with a spyglass, and Tomas tightened the last girth and swung up.

They were a mile out, climbing into the pines, when Odile reined in and raised the glass one more time.

"Dust," she said. "One rider. Moving fast."

Sef smiled his collateral smile. "Right on schedule."

But it wasn't Ishikawa's dun, and it wasn't Ishikawa's seat in the saddle, and as the rider came pounding up the switchback into view—small, hooded, riding like the devil held the mortgage—Tomas heard Odile say, very quietly, to no one:

"Well. That's not who we sent for."
