## 4. What the Homestead Remembers

The homestead announced itself the way grief does—not all at once, but in pieces you'd sworn you'd already buried.

Isandro reined up on the last rise above the valley floor and let the mare blow. Below him, the Ishikawa place sat in its bowl of dry grass like a thing that had been waiting. The roof of the main house had gone in fifteen years ago; he remembered the winter of it, the sound of beams giving up under snow weight while he stood in the doorway and did nothing, because there was nothing left to do that would matter. Now only the stone chimney stood whole, black-topped with lichen, throwing a shadow long enough to reach what had been the corral fence.

Behind him, three hundred yards back and downwind, Odile waited with Tomas and Sef's pack string. He'd told them to hold there. He hadn't explained why, and none of them had asked—though he'd felt Sef's eyes on his back the whole way down from the waystation, weighing him like a man appraising a horse with an old injury.

He rode down alone.

The thief's trail had come here straight as a plumb line. He'd read it for two days across the high bench country: a single rider, light mount, no grazing stops longer than an hour, moving with the kind of economy that meant either desperation or certainty. At the river ford the rider had watered without unsaddling. At the burned-out Cooper cabin she—he kept correcting himself, *they*—had cut the lock instead of breaking the door, which took more time and left less damage.

And now the trail ended here, at a ruin every reasonable thief would ride past.

Isandro dismounted at the edge of the dooryard and stood a moment with his hand on the cantle. The wind came off the escarpment smelling of stone and coming cold. Somewhere below the ridge, a hawk turned on the thermal in slow circles, patient as arithmetic.

*Everything out here is arithmetic,* his father used to say. *Water against mouths. Days against feed. You don't get to argue with it.*

His father had argued with it anyway, right up until the drought won.

* * *

He walked the yard the way he'd taught himself to walk any scene: perimeter first, then approach, then detail. Fifteen years of erasing himself into this country had built a discipline that held even now, when his pulse was doing something unprofessional in his throat.

The thief had camped in the lee of the old barn foundation—the same spot Isandro himself would have chosen, because the wind off the escarpment funneled through the gap in the rise and the stones threw back a little heat after sundown. The fire ring was made from three flat rocks pried out of the foundation wall. Small fire. Burned down to white ash, raked and scattered. The ashes had been dampened. Whoever it was had carried water from the spring rather than risk smoke or embers.

That was ranger work. That was *his* work, the habits he'd learned at fourteen from a father who believed a man's tracks should tell no story.

But it wasn't the camp that stopped him. It was the spring.

The spring lay two hundred yards east, behind a shelf of granite, in a fold of ground you could stand ten feet from and never see. Their father had found it the first year, before Isandro was born, and they'd never told a soul—not the neighbors, not the county, not the Almadas when the Almadas came buying water rights with contracts smooth as river stone. A hidden spring on paper-poor land was the difference between surviving a dry year and not, and the Ishikawas had survived four of them on that secret.

The thief had gone to the spring. Not wandered near it—gone to it, directly, the trail cutting off the dooryard path and angling along the granite shelf exactly where a person would angle if they'd walked it a thousand times as a child carrying buckets.

Isandro crouched over the tracks in the dust by the shelf and didn't move for a long time.

Small boot. Narrow heel. A stride shortened on the uphill, lengthened going back down toward camp, weighted left—a habit of favoring one side, maybe an old injury, maybe just the body's own signature. He knew signatures. He'd spent fifteen years reading them across half a million acres, telling one rider from another by the set of a heel and the drag of a toe.

He knew this signature.

He'd known it since she was nine years old and walked everywhere with her weight rolled onto the outside of her left foot because she'd broken that ankle falling out of the cottonwood and refused, on principle, to let it change her stride.

*Marisol.*

He said the name inside his skull where it couldn't escape, and the professional calm he wore like a second skin went tight across his shoulders.

* * *

She was eleven the year the rain stopped coming for good.

He could still see the dust standing over the south pasture like something alive, could still hear their mother reading the sky every evening and saying nothing, which was worse than saying *drought*. The cattle went first—they sold them to Sef's father at prices that were legal and obscene—and then the yearlings, and then the year after that the bank sent a man out in a white shirt who stood in the dooryard with his hat on and talked about options while Marisol watched him from the porch steps with a look Isandro had never seen on a child's face before. It wasn't hate. It was bookkeeping. She was eleven and she was learning the family arithmetic: what things cost, what things were worth, what got sold.

The water rights went the third summer. Their father signed at the Almada trading post with the contract spread on the counter between bolts of cloth, and Isandro remembered standing behind him reading upside down, sixteen years old and sharp enough to catch the figures that didn't sit right—the yield clause, the priority date moved back two seasons, the access easement written so narrow you could drive a wagon through what it didn't say. He'd started to point. His father's hand had come down flat on the page.

*Sign it,* his father said, to himself, to the pen, to God. And then, quieter, to Isandro, without looking at him: *Some ledgers you keep by burning them.*

Their mother died that winter. Fever, the doctor said, but Isandro had watched her stop drinking water so the rest of them wouldn't have to count cups, and he knew what the ledger actually recorded. Their father lasted eighteen months past her. He walked out one morning in April to check the far fence line and didn't come back, and when they found him he was sitting against a juniper with his hat over his face, asleep or done—it was never settled which, and Isandro had spent fifteen years not deciding.

Marisol was seventeen. She stayed through the funeral, through the reading of the will that gave the dead land to the son and the living debt to both, and then one morning her bed was made and her saddle was gone and there was a note on the table in her slanted hand: *Don't look for me. I'll find you when I'm someone.*

He'd looked anyway. For two years, off and on, quietly, in saloons and hiring halls and the backs of churches in towns she'd have passed through. Then he'd stopped, because stopping was the only way to survive the looking, and he'd been good at stopping ever since. He erased the name Ishikawa out of his own mouth. He took work where nobody asked questions and paid extra not to. He became the man Sef Almada wanted to hire: competent, invisible, and empty in the specific shape of a sister.

And now she'd come home ahead of him, and gone to the spring, and made a small clean fire in the exact hollow of the barn wall where their father used to sit after supper.

* * *

The grave was on the low knoll behind the house, under the juniper that had somehow lived.

Their mother's marker was a slab of local granite their father had dressed himself, because a mason's price was one more entry in a ledger already bleeding. He'd carved her name deep, deeper than the stone needed, the way men carve when carving is the only tool left. *ELENA ISHIKAWA.* Below it, the dates. Below that, nothing, because their father had run out of words somewhere around the chisel's third pass.

The slab had been moved.

Not toppled—shifted. Lifted at one end and set back down a hand's width off true, the moss line broken along the north edge, raw stone showing pale as a wound. The earth beneath the downhill corner was disturbed, pressed, recently. Isandro knelt and put his fingers into the soil and read it the way other men read print.

Someone had dug here. Shallow—no more than two feet, a pocket, not a grave. Dug carefully, in the dark probably, and filled it back in and replaced the stone over it, and gotten the stone almost right, almost perfect, except for the moss.

Almost was a word that belonged to strangers. Family got it exactly right or didn't bother.

He sat back on his heels. The wind moved through the juniper with a sound like pages turning.

There was a cache under that stone. Had been since before the drought—their father's iron box, buried the year Isandro turned twelve, holding the papers that mattered: the deed, the birth records, the marriage lines from the mission two counties east, the photographs. The proof of the name. Their father had put it there saying a family's papers should sleep close to the family, and Isandro had forgotten it existed for fifteen years, the way he'd forgotten everything that hurt to carry.

She'd taken it. Or part of it. She'd known where it was because she'd been nine years old sitting on the porch rail watching their father bury it, filing it away in that relentless childhood bookkeeping of hers, and she'd come back across two hundred miles of hard country to dig up the only thing left that said *Ishikawa* meant anything.

And if she'd taken the papers—

Isandro stood. His knees cracked in the cold. Down in the dooryard his mare shifted and blew, and beyond the rise, faint on the wind, he could hear the pack string's bells, Sef's crew waiting for him to come back up the hill and report like a good hired instrument.

Sef thought he was tracking a thief. Sef was paying triple for discretion, which in Sef's arithmetic had always meant paying for silence. And whatever was in that ledger stolen from the Almada post—whatever numbers Sef wanted buried under the first snows—Marisol had gone to the trouble of stealing it and then riding to the one place on earth that would tell her brother exactly who she was, leaving tracks he alone could read, a fire built the way he built fires, a grave marker set back almost perfectly wrong.

She wasn't running from him.

She was leaving him a trail only family could follow, and daring him to take it.

Isandro looked down at the disturbed stone, at his mother's name going pale under the broken moss, and the calm he'd worn for fifteen years finally tore through. He pressed his palm flat against the granite, cold biting his skin, and let the grief come up through his arm like water finding a crack—fast, silent, and impossible to stop. His shoulders shook once. Twice. Then he breathed it down, because there would be time for grieving later, or there wouldn't, and either way the trail was cooling.

He mounted up and turned the mare upslope, toward the bells and the bad coffee and Sef Almada's patient eyes.

Three riders ahead of him on the mountain now: the sister the world had buried, the rival who owned his debt, and whichever truth was waiting at the top of the range. The first snows were five days out, maybe six.

He gave the mare her head and started climbing.
