## 5. Marisol

The wind above the tree line did not howl so much as interrogate. It came off the escarpment in long flat shoves, testing every seam of her coat, every knot on the pack frame, every resolve she had carried up from the basin like a coal cupped in both hands.

Marisol Ishikawa—no. Not that name. Not anymore. Marisol Vey, of nowhere, of the river towns, of whatever alias the next valley required—crouched behind a shelf of granite and let the wind say what it wanted.

Below her, small as spilled salt, the waystation track wound up toward the pass. And on it, three days back now, she had seen them: four riders strung out like beads on a wire, moving with the particular economy of men who were not chasing but *following*. One of them rode ahead of the others, apart, the way a blade rides apart from the hand.

She had known before she raised the glass. Fifteen years, and she had known at a hundred yards, from nothing but the set of his shoulders—the shoulders of a man carrying his own weather.

*Isandro.*

She said the name once, aloud, just to feel it crack against the wind, then pressed her mouth shut. Names were expensive up here. She'd learned that young.

* * *

The drought years had taught her arithmetic before they taught her grief.

She had been eleven when the springs began to fail, twelve when the Almada trading post became the most important building in her world, thirteen when her father came home from it with a document folded into his coat like something dead he was ashamed to carry. Her mother read it by lamplight twice, then a third time, running her thumb along the margin where the water rights were described—their water, the Ishikawa share of the Escalante ditch, sold for a sum that bought one winter's grain and half a winter's dignity.

"It's fair," her father said, in the voice of a man reciting someone else's sentence.

Her mother said nothing. Her mother took the lamp into the back room where the family papers lived in an ammunition box, and Marisol, pretending to sleep, heard her copying figures long past midnight—the dry scratch of a pencil that sounded, even then, like a warning.

They died the following spring, within nine days of each other. Fever, the doctor said, though everyone in the valley knew fever only finished what hunger started. Marisol was fourteen. Isandro was nineteen and already half-erased, already practicing the art of disappearing into the escarpment country the way other boys practiced cards or drinking. He buried their parents side by side, carved the markers himself, and then he did the thing she had never forgiven and never stopped understanding: he walked away from the name. Sold nothing, kept nothing, signed nothing. Went up into the high country and let the world conclude the Ishikawas had ended.

He thought he was burying the last of it. He didn't know about the ammunition box. He didn't know their mother had copied everything—the original sale, the survey numbers, the margin notes in Sef Almada's own hand, dated two weeks *before* the drought made the water worth stealing. Their mother had known. Had suspected the fix and written it down in a hand gone thin with pencil lead, and then died without telling anyone, because who would have believed her? Who would have taken the word of a dying woman over the ledger of the richest trader in three valleys?

Marisol had found the box at sixteen, alone in the ruins, and had spent fifteen years learning how to carry it.

* * *

Exile, it turned out, was a trade like any other. You apprenticed. You got good.

She had worked river landings under names that fit her like borrowed coats. She'd hauled freight over the low passes in seasons when honest women stayed home. She'd learned to read men the way Isandro read ground—not their faces, which lied, but their weight, their habits, the places they guarded and the places they forgot they had. She learned that a woman traveling alone was either invisible or a target, rarely anything between, and that the trick was choosing which, day by day, hour by hour.

And all those years, in the bottom of her pack, wrapped in oilcloth inside a false seam: her mother's copies. Thin as communion wafers. Heavy as ballast.

For fifteen years she told herself she was waiting for the right moment. The truth was meaner and simpler: she was afraid. The papers proved fraud, yes—but proving it meant standing in daylight, saying her name, becoming visible in a country where visibility was how people like the Almadas collected their debts. Her parents had been erased politely, by paperwork. To un-erase them she would have to write herself back into existence, in ink, in front of witnesses, and let whatever came come.

Then, this autumn, word reached her downriver: old Tomás Almada was dead, and the post passed to Sef, and Sef was consolidating—buying debts, burning records, tidying forty years of arithmetic before some territorial assessor arrived to audit the water claims. Among the records scheduled for the stove: the original ledger of the Escalante ditch. The one book that still held, in Sef's own hand, the dates and figures that could hang him.

If it burned, her mother's copies became the testimony of a dead woman. Worthless. Ash arguing with ash.

So Marisol Vey went back to being Marisol Ishikawa for exactly one night, in the dark, through the window Sef's clerk never latched because no one had ever dared the post yard—and stole the ledger out from under the sleeping wealth of the Escalante valley.

She had expected pursuit. Hired men, maybe. What she had not expected, watching that string of riders come up the basin road, was to feel her heart do something stupid and hopeful at the sight of the fourth horse.

* * *

She left the first message at the cold spring below Redtail Rim, because he would stop there—he always stopped there; he used to fill the canteens there when they were children, before the spring failed, and a man like Isandro returned to dead water the way other men returned to graves.

She scratched it into the bark of the last living juniper, the one that clung to the rim like a held breath:

**THE DITCH REMEMBERS. ASK THE MARGIN.**

No name. No signature. Anyone else would read it as grazing graffiti, a herder's mark. But Isandro had helped their mother copy the ditch surveys when he was twelve; he knew what lived in margins. He would stand at that tree and go very still, the way he went still before hard ground, and then he would look up the trail, and he would know.

The second message she left higher, at the cairn where the trail forked—one stone pulled and set apart, angled east. A child's signal. *This way.* He'd taught it to her himself, on the one summer he'd tried to be a brother instead of a ghost, before the silence took him too. She hated using it. It felt like begging. But the storm season was early this year, and if the snows closed the passes with the ledger still unread, everything—her mother's pencil, her father's shame, her fifteen years of borrowed names—would melt into spring with nothing decided.

The third message was the dangerous one. She left it at the burned homestead.

She hadn't meant to go there. The homestead was a detour, a risk, a place where her feet made decisions her head hadn't approved. But she'd stood in the ruin of the kitchen where their mother had copied figures by lamplight, and she'd found herself doing what grieving people do: arranging things. Righting a fallen stone. Pulling weeds from the grave markers with fingers gone numb.

That was her mistake. She'd disturbed her mother's marker—just the edge of it, just enough—and stood there too long, and left too much sign, and then, in some fit she still couldn't explain, had wedged a strip of ledger paper under the stone. On it, in her careful hand:

**I READ IT ALL. HE CHEATED THEM AND YOU LET THE NAME DIE TO SPARE HIM THE TRIAL. I DIDN'T. COME AND SEE OR STAY GONE FOREVER.**

Cruel. She knew it was cruel even as she wrote it. But cruelty was the only language guaranteed to reach him; tenderness would have bounced off that armor of his like rain off shale. And underneath the cruelty, if he looked—if he ever really looked—there was the other thing, the true thing, which was simply: *I'm alive. I've been alive this whole time. Come find me or don't, but know it.*

She wondered, climbing, whether he'd found it yet. Whether he was standing at their mother's grave right now, hat in his hands, wearing that face he wore instead of weeping.

* * *

Above the tree line the world simplified. Rock, snow-patch, sky, wind. Nothing to hide behind but your own decisions.

Marisol made camp in the lee of a granite spine, built her fire in a pit lined with green stone so it would burn fast and leave little, ate cold beans rather than risk the smell of cooking, and took out the ledger.

By firelight it looked like what it was: a book. Leather gone soft at the corners, pages swollen and dried with thirty years of damp and heat. She turned to the Escalante entries and read them again, though she knew them by heart, because reading them was a kind of prayer—the only kind her mother had left her. There: the survey date. There: the assessed flow, generous, pre-drought. There, in the margin, Sef Almada's youthful hand noting the *projected* failure of the upper springs—a projection made in a year of good rain, which meant knowledge, which meant the drought wasn't weather to the Almadas. It was inventory.

They had starved on schedule. Someone had drawn up the schedule.

She closed the book and held it against her chest and felt the old fear rise in her throat, the fear that had kept her invisible for fifteen years: *they will kill you for this, and worse, they will be right to, because you are one woman with a book, and books don't bleed.*

But then she thought of the juniper bark. Of the tilted stone. Of Isandro somewhere below her in the dark, reading her handwriting, wearing that face.

Defiance and dread, she was learning, were not opposites. They were the same muscle, worked from different sides.

She banked the fire, wrapped the ledger in oilcloth, and was shrugging into her bedroll when she saw it: far below, past the black teeth of the timberline, a spark of light where no light should be. Campfire. Then a second, smaller—someone shielding a flame to check a map, or a message.

Three days behind her yesterday. One night, tonight.

The wind shoved again, flat and hard, and carried up to her, faint beneath its interrogation, a sound she had spent half her life listening for and the other half dreading: hoofbeats on scree, patient, unhurried, coming on.

Marisol pulled her pack tight, looked once more at the firelight below, and began climbing toward the pass in the dark—because the snow was coming early this year, and it was coming for all of them.
