## 6. The Pass Narrows

Odile Vasseur had climbed in weather that killed men, and she had learned one thing above all else: the mountain tells you what it intends, if you're willing to listen. Snow in the first week of the thaw month was the mountain saying *turn back*. But Sef Almada had paid for passage, not for listening, and so they climbed.

The flurries came at midmorning, fat and slow, spinning down through the pines like ash from a fire no one had lit. By noon they were sifting through the branches steadily enough to hush the whole slope, to swallow the sound of hooves and creaking leather until Odile felt as though she were riding inside a bell that someone had muted with a cloth. She didn't like it. Silence on a trail was never empty. It was a held breath.

Ahead of her, Isandro Ishikawa rode with his collar up and his hat brim low, a shape more than a man, dissolving into the gray the way he seemed to dissolve into everything. Tomas was behind her, cheerful even now, humming something tuneless through chapped lips, his young horse picking its way over the frozen root-lace of the trail. And Sef brought up the rear, which Odile found telling. A man who trusted his crew rode point or middle. A man who wanted to watch everyone's hands rode last.

She had known Sef Almada for eleven years, and she owed him for nine of them. The debt was simple and it was not: he had bought her brother's note when the banks wouldn't wait, and he had never once mentioned interest, and he had never once let her forget the principal existed. That was Sef's gift. He made generosity feel like a lien.

"Ground's changing," Isandro said, without turning. His voice carried flat through the snow-hush. "Granite under the duff past this ridge. Ice on the north faces by tomorrow."

"You've been here before," Sef said. It wasn't a question, and there was a thread in it, fine as wire, that Odile heard plainly. Testing. Always testing.

"I've been everywhere before," Isandro said, and gave nothing else.

Tomas laughed, easy and bright. "That's the trouble with rangers. You ask them where they're from and they tell you the elevation."

Odile smiled because Tomas expected her to, but she was watching Isandro's shoulders, the set of them, rigid as fence posts. The thief's trail had bent toward this country two days ago—toward the old Ishikawa homestead, if the survey maps in her head were honest—and since then Isandro had gone quiet in a way that wasn't his ordinary quiet. His ordinary quiet was a stone's quiet. This was a door's quiet. Something behind it, held shut.

They made the ridge by late afternoon and dropped into a hanging valley where the wind couldn't follow, and there Isandro swung down and stood very still over a circle of blackened stones.

* * *

The fire ring was perfect. That was the first thing wrong with it.

Travelers' fire rings were sloppy things—rocks grabbed in the dark, ash dumped any which way, half-burned ends of wood scattered like pick-up sticks. This one was built with care, stones fitted in a neat ring, ash banked and buried beneath a flat cap rock so no spark could ride the wind. Someone had cooked here within hours. When Odile crouched and slid two fingers under the cap rock, the warmth came up faint as a pulse in a sleeping wrist.

"Warm," she said. "Hours, not days."

Isandro hadn't moved. He stood looking at the fire ring the way other men looked at graves, and Odile saw his hand rise halfway toward the stones and stop, hover, fall back. As if touching them would confirm something he wasn't ready to know.

"She banks her fires like our mother did," he said quietly. It was almost not spoken aloud at all. "Cap rock. Windward stack."

Sef stepped close, snow melting on the shoulders of his good coat, and Odile watched him read Isandro's face with the patience of a man counting coins. "You know who we're following," Sef said. Not a question either. Sef didn't waste questions; he spent statements and collected answers.

"I know whose fire this is." Isandro turned away from the ring. "It doesn't change the contract."

"It changes everything," Sef said mildly, "and you know it. But we'll climb on, won't we. We'll climb on."

That night the camp split itself without anyone deciding to. Sef took the fire's windward side with Tomas, who talked cheerfully about shoes and nails and the mule's cracked hoof until Sef's silence wore him down to embers. Isandro sat apart with his back to a boulder, hat over his eyes, a man pretending to sleep with the diligence of a man standing guard. Odile took the cold side, as she always did, because the cold side let you watch everything.

And near midnight, Isandro rose without sound, crossed to her, and crouched—a ranger's crouch, weight on the balls of his feet, ready to move in any direction—and spoke so low the words barely cleared the wind.

"How long have you ridden for Almada?"

"Nine years."

"Why?"

"That's my arithmetic," she said. "Not yours."

He nodded slowly, unsurprised, unoffended. Then: "You've done this work long enough to smell a job that isn't a job. So tell me what you smell."

Odile looked across the dead fire at Sef's bedroll, at the shape of him, still as furniture. She thought about her brother's note folded in the oilskin inside her coat, about the word *principal*, about how Sef had never raised his voice in nine years because he'd never needed to.

"I smell a man who sent three people up a mountain after a book," she said, "when one would do. I smell triple pay for discretion, which is what men pay when discretion isn't a courtesy—it's the product. And I smell a ledger that somebody stole for a reason, and a man who wants it back not to read it but to close it." She met Isandro's eyes. "I don't think Sef wants that book retrieved, Ishikawa. I think he wants it buried. There's a difference, and I think you already feel the shape of it."

Isandro was silent a long moment. Snow ticked against his hat brim.

"If you believe that," he said finally, "why ride for him at all?"

"Because I owe him." She said it flatly, the way you name a disease. "And because debts don't care what you believe. Ask your sister, if you find her. I hear debt runs in families."

Something moved through his face then—fast, deep, gone—like a fish turning under ice. He stood, and paused, and said, "If it comes to choosing, choose the side that lets people live," and went back to his boulder, and Odile sat in the dark doing sums she didn't like: what she owed, what she knew, and what either of those things would be worth on a mountain where the truth was starting to snow.

* * *

The pass narrowed the next morning into a corridor of stone, walls rising sheer on both sides, the trail a shelf cut along a slope of talus that ran down two hundred feet to a frozen creek. The flurries had thickened overnight into a steady sift, dry snow that didn't stick so much as accumulate intention, dusting every ledge white, filling every crack. Bad country. Country where a single shout could bring the sky down.

Isandro went ahead on foot, leading his horse, reading the shelf like a page. Odile followed, and behind her came Tomas with the pack string, and Sef last again, always last, always watching.

Halfway along the shelf Isandro stopped and raised a fist, and the column stilled. He crouched. Odile came up beside him and saw what he saw: fresh scuff marks on the rock, deliberate ones, a small pile of talus stacked beside a boulder perched at the top of the slope above them—a stone the size of a bread oven, sitting in a socket of snow that someone had undercut.

"Leverage point," Isandro said softly. "Someone meant to drop this on whoever came through. Days ago. They didn't spring it." He glanced up the wall, reading. "Or they left it armed."

"Maybe the thief has a conscience," Tomas offered from behind, too loud for the corridor.

"Maybe the thief wanted us to see it," Odile said, and saw Isandro's jaw tighten, because they had both arrived at the same thought: a message only one reader would understand. *Turn back, brother.* Or worse—*I'm watching you.*

Sef came up the line, unhurried, snow in his gray stubble. "Can we pass it?"

"We pass wide and quiet. One at a time. No horses on the shelf—the talus won't hold four." Isandro was already moving, hand signals crisp, and for a moment Odile saw the fifteen years settle on him like a tool belt: this was what he was for, this narrow arithmetic of stone and weight. He sent Odile first. Then Sef. Then himself, walking backward, watching the slope above while Tomas coaxed the nervous pack string along below.

Tomas came last, as agreed, leading the mule wide around the undercut stone, and he was smiling—he was always smiling, the boy smiled at weather, at altitude, at fear—when the mountain coughed.

It started as a sound Odile felt in her teeth rather than heard, a deep granular sigh somewhere upslope, and then the whole face of the slide let go. Not the big stone—that held—but a river of it, talus and old snow liquefying into motion, and Tomas had time to turn, time to say her name, and then the white water of the mountain took him sideways off the shelf.

The mule screamed. The world roared. Odile threw herself flat against the wall and the slide passed below her like a train, a hundred tons of mountain going where mountains go, and when the roar guttered into ticking silence she was already moving down the slope, Isandro beside her, both of them plunging calf-deep into the settling debris.

They found him thirty feet down, half-buried, and alive—alive because a slab of granite the size of a door had arched over him like a roof, and broken, because the same slide had driven his left leg against that roof's edge. The boot was filled with blood already stiffening. His face was gray and astonished, and he was still trying to smile.

"Told you," he managed, "the mule's bad luck."

"Hold still." Isandro's hands were already working, calm, exact, cutting the bootlace, padding, binding, and Odile tore her spare shirt into strips without being told. Above them on the shelf, Sef stood at the edge with the snow sifting past him, looking down—not at Tomas, Odile noticed. At the slope. Reading it. The way a man reads a thing he understands too well.

"The trigger stone held," Isandro said later, when Tomas was splinted and lashed into a saddle, gray-lipped, sweating, alive. He stood at the base of the slide with snow melting on his shoulders, and his voice had a strange evenness, the evenness of a man holding something hot. "But the undercut was freshened. New work on old work. Someone came back to arm it. Recently."

"Meaning what?" Sef asked. "That your sister tried to kill us?"

"Meaning someone did." Isandro looked at him. Just looked. And Odile, standing between them with Tomas's blood drying brown on her cuffs, understood that the question underneath the question was not *who pulled the slope* but *who benefits*, and that Isandro had already run that sum, and that the answer had come out smelling of trading-post ink.

Sef met the look without blinking. "Accusations are cheap on a mountain, Ishikawa. We have a man bleeding and a pass closing. Spend your suspicion where it pays."

They camped that night in the lee of the slide debris, too exposed to risk a fire, chewing cold food in a silence that had teeth. Tomas slept in a fever-dream, murmuring about horseshoes. Odile sat awake, watching the two shapes at opposite edges of the camp—Sef with his back straight as a ledger line, Isandro with his hat over his eyes and his hands loose near his knife—and she thought about how trust works in high country. It isn't given. It's load-rated. And somewhere above them, in the narrowing white throat of the pass, someone had tested theirs and found exactly where it would break.

Near dawn, Odile woke to a sound too soft for the others: a whistle, three notes, descending, threading down the wind from the heights. A range signal. Old country code, the kind drovers used before the telegraph lines.

She knew it. Every kid from the basin knew it.

*Come alone. Come now.*

She looked across the dying dark at Isandro, and found him already awake, already looking at her, his eyes reflecting the last stars.

"You heard it too," he said.

Above them, the snow kept falling, patient as paper, waiting to be written on.
