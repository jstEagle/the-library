## 7. Terms of Survival

The storm came down the escarpment like something with intent.

Isandro had watched it build all afternoon—a bruise of cloud stacking itself against the western peaks, swallowing the sun in slow bites—and he had read it the way he read everything: as arithmetic. Snow by nightfall. Wind out of the northwest, hard enough to strip heat from a man faster than cold alone could manage. And somewhere ahead, above him, a woman who had been dead for fifteen years moving through country that was about to stop forgiving anyone.

He found her fire ring at dusk, half a mile below the cave mouth he'd already marked on his climb up. The ashes were warm when he pressed two fingers into them. Not hours old. Minutes.

She wanted him to find her.

That was the thing he kept turning over as he climbed the last switchbacks with the wind beginning to moan off the rimrock. A thief who didn't want to be caught left cold fires and false trails and doubled back through scree where tracks died. Marisol had left her fire burning down to a warmth he could feel. She had camped in the open where his glasses would pick up the smoke. Every sign she'd left since the homestead—the cairn built Ishikawa-style, three stones flat and one on edge; the tin cup set upside down on a boulder like their mother used to set it to drain—had been a message written in a language only two people alive could read.

*Come find me,* the signs said. *Before the mountain does it for you.*

The cave mouth opened in the cliff face like a held breath, twenty feet wide, deep enough that the light failed ten paces in. Isandro stood at the threshold with the storm at his back and let his eyes adjust.

She sat near the back wall, cross-legged, a small fire between her hands and the dark. The ledger lay on the stone beside her, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord. Her hair had gone gray at the temples—gray, when she'd been nineteen and black-haired the last time he'd seen her—and there was a scar running through her left eyebrow that hadn't been there before. But the way she sat, spine straight, chin level, watching him come without flinching—that was their mother's posture, exact as an inheritance.

"You got my messages," Marisol said.

"I got your messages."

"Then sit down, Isandro. It's going to be a long night."

He didn't sit. He came forward until the fire's heat touched his face and lowered himself onto the stone across from her, rifle across his knees, the way you sit with a stranger in bad country. She noticed. Of course she noticed. Something moved behind her eyes—not hurt exactly, more like a door closing quietly on a room she'd already expected to be empty.

"You look older," she said.

"Fifteen years will do that."

"Not to everyone." Her mouth twisted. "Sef Almada looks exactly the same. Fat and satisfied. Some men age like wine and some men just get poured into better bottles."

"Why did you take the ledger?"

"So we're skipping the part where we ask each other how we've been." She laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle like river ice. "Fine. I took the ledger because it's mine. Ours. Because Sef Almada has spent fifteen years profiting off a piece of paper that says our parents sold water they never agreed to sell, and because every lawyer in the territory would laugh me out of the room if I walked in with nothing but a story and a name nobody remembers." She leaned forward. Firelight carved her face into planes and shadow. "I stole proof, Isandro. That's all. Proof."

"Proof of what? That Papá signed?"

"Proof that what he signed wasn't what Almada told him he was signing." Her voice dropped, went flat and hard as the stone under them. "I've spent years finding this out. The original agreement was for grazing rights on the lower flats. Seasonal. Almada's clerk wrote it up as perpetual water rights, appurtenant, transferable—and Papá couldn't read the fine print any better than he could read English. He made his mark. They made their fortune. When the drought came and the wells went dry, the Almadas owned every drop on that side of the basin, legally, forever, because of one mark our father pressed into wax he couldn't read."

The wind screamed past the cave mouth. Somewhere up-slope, rock ticked against rock, the mountain settling its accounts.

"And you waited fifteen years," Isandro said.

"I *survived* fifteen years." The words came out like something thrown. "You don't know what those years were. You were here. You had the escarpment. You had—" She stopped. Breathed. Started again, quieter, which from Marisol was worse than shouting. "Where were you? That's my question. That's the question I carried across two territories and six winters. Our parents died inside eight months of each other and I stood at the graves and looked around for my brother, my brother who could track a ghost across bare granite, and you weren't there. Nobody knew where you'd gone. Some people said you were dead. I let them say it. Do you know why? Because it was easier than knowing you'd chosen to disappear while I was burying Mamá with my own hands."

"I wrote to you."

"You wrote to me *once*. One letter. From nowhere, unsigned, telling me you were alive and not to look for you." Her voice finally broke open. "What kind of letter is that? What kind of brother—"

"The kind who knew Almada would come collecting." Isandro heard his own voice go low and even, the ranger's voice, the voice he used on frightened animals, and hated himself for using it here. "After the sale, after the drought broke them, there were debts. Debts attach to family. I'd watched what Almada's collectors did to the Reyes place down-valley—took the land, took the stock, took the daughter's dowry. I was eighteen and I had nothing, but I had one thing: I could vanish. So I vanished, and I stayed vanished, because a debt collector can't collect from a man who doesn't exist. I erased myself so there'd be nothing left of the Ishikawa name worth taking."

"That's not erasing yourself. That's abandoning us and calling it sacrifice."

"Yes," Isandro said. "It is."

The honesty of it seemed to stagger her more than any defense would have. She stared at him across the fire, and for a long moment neither of them spoke, and the storm filled the silence with everything they couldn't.

* * *

Outside, the wind changed pitch.

Isandro heard it before he understood it—the storm's roar developing a second voice underneath, a rhythmic crunch of boots on fresh snow, more than one set, moving with the unhurried discipline of men who knew where they were going. He rose without a word and drifted to the cave mouth, keeping to the wall, and looked out into the white chaos.

Lantern light. Two points of it, maybe three, bobbing along the switchback below. And behind them, further down, a fourth light that moved wrong—too steady, too deliberate, sweeping the slope in methodical arcs. Someone searching. Someone good.

"They followed me," Marisol said behind him. She'd come up quiet, the ledger clutched against her chest. "I thought the storm would cover me."

"It covered you from me. Nothing covers anyone from Odile Vasseur." He turned. "How many does Sef have left?"

"Three that I've seen. Him, the tracker woman, and a gun hand named Corliss who smiles too much."

"Tomas?"

"The farrier boy?" She frowned. "He was at the waystation. Why?"

Because Tomas had mended Isandro's saddle girth without being asked, and checked his horse's shoes twice, and looked at him the whole time like a man looking at a story he'd been told and couldn't quite believe. Because boys like Tomas always ended up standing somewhere they shouldn't when the shooting started. "Never mind. Listen to me now." He took her shoulder—she flinched, then didn't pull away—and made her meet his eyes. "They're coming up the switchback. Twenty minutes, maybe thirty, if the storm slows them. There's a back channel through the cave, a chimney in the rear wall. I marked it on my way in. We can be a thousand feet higher before they reach this fire."

"No." She stepped back from his grip. "No more running. Running is what I've done for a year and it's what you've done for fifteen and look where it's gotten both of us. They want the ledger. Fine. Let them come and hear what's in it."

"Hear? Marisol, Sef isn't climbing a mountain in a blizzard to *hear* anything. He's climbing it to make sure no one ever reads that book again. If he can't bury it in a vault, he'll bury it deeper than that."

"Then we read it first." She knelt right there, at the cave mouth, snow swirling in past them, and began untying the oilcloth with fingers that shook only a little. "Right now. Before they arrive. You and me, together, the way it should have been done years ago. You read the fine print—I know you learned, don't tell me you didn't, a man doesn't survive out here without learning to read contracts and brands and weather. You read it, and you tell me if I'm wrong. If I'm wrong, I'll give it back to Sef myself and apologize. But if I'm right—"

She stopped. The oilcloth fell open. The ledger beneath was fat and swollen with damp, its leather cover stamped with the Almada post's brand, its pages edged in foxing brown.

"—if I'm right, then our parents were robbed, and the man climbing up here in the dark to take this away from us has known it for fifteen years."

Isandro looked at the ledger. Then at the lantern lights crawling up the switchback, closer now, close enough that he could see the snow swirling through the beams. Then at his sister, kneeling in the storm-light with their whole ruined history open in her hands.

Every instinct he had built over fifteen years said run. Take the book, take the chimney, take the high country, stay a ghost. Ghosts survived. Ghosts didn't bleed.

But ghosts also buried their mothers alone, apparently, while their brothers practiced being dead.

"Give me the pages from the summer of the drought," he said.

Her breath came out shaky. She turned to the back of the ledger, found the year, and passed the book across. Their hands touched on the binding—her fingers cold as the snow coming through the cave mouth, his probably no warmer—and neither of them pulled away fast.

Isandro opened the ledger and began to read by the dying firelight, while below them, patient as arithmetic, the lights kept climbing.

Halfway down the third page, he stopped.

"What," Marisol whispered. "What is it."

He didn't answer. Because there, in the margin beside the entry recording the Ishikawa water rights purchase—beside his father's mark, pressed into wax by a man who couldn't read—was a second notation in Sef Almada's unmistakable hand, dated four days *after* the signing:

*Amended per agreement w/ R. Ishikawa — verbal. Original terms void.*

His father had never amended anything. His father had been dead of the fever four days later, too sick to speak.

And Sef Almada had written himself a lie and lived on it for fifteen years.

"Isandro." Marisol's voice, very small. "What does it say?"

Below them, the first lantern light cleared the lip of the switchback and swung toward the cave mouth.
