## 8. Sef's Arithmetic

Sef Almada had learned long ago that a man could tell everything about himself by what he counted.

His father had counted cattle, and died poor in a good year because the cattle were thin and the grass was thinner. Sef counted water. Gallons per head per day, acre-feet against snowpack, the slow arithmetic of a dry country where every number eventually came due. He'd built the Almada trading post on that arithmetic, and he'd built it well, and for thirty years the numbers had held—except one, one entry made in a bad winter by a frightened man with a dying wife and two children watching from the doorway of a house that smelled of dust and boiled cactus.

He thought about that entry now as he climbed the last mile below the summit approach, his breath smoking, the hired gun Voss walking three paces behind him the way Voss always did, close enough to be useful, far enough to be deniable.

The ledger weighed nothing in his saddlebag. It weighed everything else.

* * *

He had bought the Ishikawa debt honestly. That was the sentence he carried like a stone in his boot, the one he took out and turned over whenever the guilt got loud. Honest paper, honest interest, honest foreclosure when the rains failed a third year running and old Kenji Ishikawa couldn't make the payment on the water rights he'd mortgaged to keep his family fed. Sef had sat across from him at this own kitchen table—the same table Isandro Ishikawa would later refuse to sit at—and offered terms no other man in the basin would have offered at all. Time. Patience. A rate that stung but didn't bleed.

And then the drought had done what droughts do, and Kenji Ishikawa had died with his wife within eighteen months of each other, and the debt had come due against an empty house and a son gone into the escarpment and a daughter nobody could find, and Sef had taken possession of the water rights the way a man takes possession of anything left unclaimed: legally, completely, and without ever quite looking at it straight.

The falsification had not even been his idea. That was the part that shamed him most, that he hadn't had the courage to invent his own sin. His clerk, a soft-spoken man named Ferran who is dead now these ten years, had pointed out that the original sale recorded the flow at four hundred miner's inches when the gauge read three-forty, and that the difference, compounded over decades of downstream leases, was the difference between a comfortable post and an empire. Ferran had already altered the page before Sef decided whether to stop him. And Sef had looked at the altered page, and said nothing, and that silence had paid for the new warehouse, the second wagon line, the stone house in town with the iron stove his wife loved.

One number. One line of ink. Thirty years of interest.

* * *

He found their fire in the lee of the granite shoulder where the trail narrowed before the shrine flats—two bedrolls, a small flame, and the two of them sitting up when he rode into the light of it, which told him Odile had kept her mouth shut longer than he'd expected but not forever. Isandro stood. Marisol stayed seated, the ledger wrapped in oilcloth in her lap like a sleeping animal she meant to defend with her teeth.

"Odile talks too much," Sef said, dismounting.

"She talks exactly enough," Isandro said. "You want the book."

"I want what I paid for." Sef warmed his hands at their fire without asking, an old habit of ownership that had never once been challenged in thirty years until this moment, when he felt both of them watching him do it. "Triple rate. Discretion. I've been honest with you since we were boys, Isandro, so let me be honest now, at the end of it."

"You've never been honest a day in your life," Marisol said.

"I've been honest about my prices." He crouched by the fire. Up here the cold came off the icefield in sheets, and the wind carried that particular high-country silence underneath it, the sound of a world deciding whether to bury you. "You've read it. You know what's in it. So you know what I'm going to say next, and you know why I can say it without flinching."

"The sale was falsified," Marisol said. "Four hundred inches on the page. Three-forty on the gauge. My father signed under duress with a starving family, and you—" her voice cracked and steadied, cracked and steadied, a rope taking load—"you've been collecting the difference my whole life."

"Yes."

Just that. He gave them the word whole, no varnish, because he'd learned that a lie costs more than the truth if you intend to keep trading afterward. "I bought the debt honestly. I profited from the fraud ever since. Both things are true, and neither one undoes the other, and if you burn that book or bury it or carry it down to the territorial office in spring, I am ruined. Not embarrassed. Ruined. The bank calls the notes, the leases lapse, and two hundred people who work for me are standing in the street by midsummer wondering why the water stopped."

"They'll still have the water," Isandro said quietly. "They just won't have you skimming it."

"You think it works like that?" Sef laughed, and heard how ugly the laugh sounded in the thin air. "Water isn't a thing, Isandro. It's an arrangement. Who holds the paper, who keeps the ditches cleared, who has credit at the store when the crop fails. Tear up the arrangement and you don't get justice. You get a season of chaos and then some man worse than me holding the paper."

* * *

Voss shifted at the edge of the firelight, and Sef raised one finger without looking, and the man went still. There it was, then. The gun was the argument underneath all his arguments, and they all knew it, and Sef hated that he'd brought it—but a man who climbed into the high country in the first snows to confront thieves did not come unarmed, whatever story he told himself on the ride up.

"Here's what I actually came to offer," he said. "Not to you, Marisol. To him."

Isandro's face did nothing. Fifteen years in the escarpment and the man had erased even his expressions; he was a rock face Sef had been reading since they were fourteen years old, and now there was nothing to read, and that nothing frightened him more than the gun at his back frightened Marisol.

"You walked out of the basin fifteen years ago and became a ghost," Sef said. "I know why. Your parents die, your sister vanishes, the name goes rotten in every mouth between here and the river, and you decide the only clean thing left to do is disappear. Fine. Noble, even. But you've spent fifteen years being nobody, tracking stolen horses for men who won't look at you twice, sleeping in country that wants you dead, and for what? For silence. For the privilege of never having to be an Ishikawa again."

"Sef."

"Listen to the price." He leaned forward. "Give me the ledger. Burn it, bury it, hand it over—I don't care which, so long as it stops existing. And in exchange: the past unburned. Your name restored. I'll file the papers myself—I'll swear the homestead reverted to heirs, I'll put your sister's survival on record, I'll stand up in the territorial hall and say the words *the Ishikawa family was wronged* with every ear in the basin listening. You come down off this mountain in spring as somebody. A man with land claims and a history and a name people say out loud instead of around. Standing. Absolution. Everything you buried yourself alive to escape wanting."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pass with a sound like a door opening in an empty house.

"And what does it cost her?" Isandro said.

Sef had prepared for that question. He'd prepared for rage, for grief, for the flat professional voice Isandro used on clients. What he hadn't prepared for was how much it cost *him* to answer.

"It costs her the proof," he said. "Same as it costs me. Nobody comes out of this clean. That's the arithmetic of this country, Isandro—it's never punished anyone who deserved it and never once spared anyone who didn't. You can have truth, and it ruins me and ruins the valley and gets your sister's testimony dismissed anyway because a forged page proves nothing after thirty years except that someone forged it. Or you can have peace, real peace, with your name on it, and the past stays past."

Marisol stood up. She was smaller than he remembered, sharper, weathered like something left out in the sun too long, and when she spoke her voice was low and absolutely steady.

"My father sold our water so we wouldn't starve, and you wrote the number wrong so you'd get rich, and you're offering my brother a *name* as if names were what we lost." She lifted the oilcloth bundle. "We lost him. We lost the ranch. We lost twenty years. And you want us to trade the only piece of paper that says it happened for a polite fiction with your signature on it."

"That's exactly what I'm offering," Sef said. "Because polite fictions are the only currency this country honors. Ask your brother. He's been paying in the other kind for fifteen years, and look what it bought him."

He regretted it the instant it left his mouth—not because it was cruel, but because it was true, and true things said in anger have a way of cutting the man who says them. Isandro looked at him across the fire, and for the first time all night there was something readable in his face, and it wasn't hatred. It was pity. It was the look of a man doing arithmetic of his own, and arriving at a total Sef didn't want to see.

Above them, at the top of the switchbacks where the shrine stones showed white against the dark, the snow began in earnest—fat, silent flakes falling straight down through the windless cold, the kind of snow that meant the passes were closing behind them and ahead of them, that meant whatever happened next would happen with no way down for anyone.

Voss stepped forward out of the dark, and his hand was resting easy on his belt, easy the way a man rests his hand when he's been waiting all night for permission.

"Mr. Almada," Voss said. "How long are we letting them think about it?"

Sef looked at the gun, at the ledger, at the brother and sister standing together on the wrong side of his fire, and understood with terrible clarity that he had brought the arithmetic to its final column, and that the sum was about to be taken out of his hands.
