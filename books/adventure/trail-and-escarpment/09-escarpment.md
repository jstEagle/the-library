## 9. Escarpment

The storm found them at the tree line and did not let go.

It came down the escarpment like something with intent, a white wall rolling off the summit ridge, and by the time Isandro had the last of the horses hobbled behind the windbreak of fallen granite, the world had shrunk to the length of his own arms. Snow filled his collar, his cuffs, the seams of his gloves. He could hear Marisol breathing beside him—quick, shallow, the breath of a woman who had run for nine days on dried meat and fear—and he could hear nothing else, which meant he could not hear Sef's crew, and that was the worst arithmetic of all.

The shrine sat two hundred yards above them, a stone cairn older than any name on any ledger, crowned with an iron pole that pilgrims had once used to hang offerings. In clear weather it was a half-hour climb. In this, it might as well have been the moon.

"We go now," Marisol said, "or we don't go."

"We go."

She had the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, bound there with a strap she'd cut from her saddlebag, and when she moved he could see the shape of it under her coat like a second heartbeat. Fifteen years he had carried nothing. She had crossed a mountain range carrying everything their family had ever owned: its grievance, its proof, its name.

He led. That was the one thing left that he knew how to do. He put his face into the wind and read the slope through his boots—the way the snow lay thin over wind-scoured rock, thick in the lee of every boulder, the places where the mountain kept its old arguments buried under new ones. Behind him, Marisol matched his steps exactly, stepping where he stepped, the way she had followed him across the dry washes of their childhood, before the drought taught them both that following anyone was a way of dying.

They were fifty yards from the cairn when the storm opened just enough to show him the figures ahead.

Three of them, at the base of the shrine. Sef Almada, bent into the wind, one hand on the stones. Odile, a little apart, rifle slung, watching the slope below—which meant watching them. And between them, standing straighter than either, Tomas.

The boy had his farrier's hammer in his hand instead of a gun, and even at this distance Isandro could see how young his face looked with the snow crusting his eyebrows. Nineteen years old. Isandro had shod horses beside him for two seasons and never once asked him what he was running from. It occurred to him now, with terrible clarity, that everyone on this mountain was running from something, and the ledger was only the excuse.

Sef saw them. Even through the white chaos, Isandro watched the man's shoulders change—the way a man's shoulders change when the thing he has paid for finally comes into view. Sef raised one arm, not in greeting but in the flat, commanding gesture of a man who believes the world still takes his orders.

And then the fourth figure stood up out of the snow.

Isandro had missed him because the man had missed nothing—he had dug in behind the cairn's western face, out of the wind, patient as a trap, and now he rose with a rifle already at his shoulder and the muzzle tracking not Isandro, not Marisol, but the ledger-shaped weight against Marisol's chest.

Everything after that happened in the time it takes snow to fall a foot.

Isandro shouted—a word without language, pure warning—and threw himself sideways down the slope. The rifle spoke once, a flat crack swallowed instantly by wind. He heard Marisol cry out, heard her boots skid on the ice-glazed granite, and turned in time to see her sliding, arms wheeling, toward the shelf edge where the mountain simply stopped and the clouds began.

She caught the iron offering-pole. Someone had planted it at the shelf's lip a century ago or more, and it held—it groaned, it bent, but it held—with Marisol hanging off it one-handed, the other hand clamped to the oilcloth bundle, her boots kicking over four hundred feet of nothing.

The gunman worked his bolt. Calm. Professional. A man finishing a job.

Tomas hit him from the side.

The boy came off the rocks like something thrown, hammer first, and the two of them went down together in a tangle of limbs and rifle and snow. The gun discharged again into the sky. Isandro was already climbing, climbing hard and stupid, hands raw on the iced stone, because Marisol's grip was failing—he could see it failing, see her fingers whitening and slipping along the frozen iron—and the wind was peeling her off the mountain inch by inch.

"I've got you." He got a fist around the pole above her hands. "Let go of the book if you have to."

"Never," she said, and the word came out of her torn open, fifteen years in a single syllable.

"Marisol—"

"It's *them*, Isandro. It's all of them. It's the only thing that says we ever—" Her boot found a hold. Lost it. Found it again. Below them, on the shelf, the gunman had gotten an elbow into Tomas's throat and was reaching for the rifle.

Odile reached it first.

Isandro saw it happen in fragments through the snow: Odile's gloved hand closing on the barrel, her voice cutting through the storm—sharp, short, the voice of a woman collecting on a debt of her own—and then the rifle spinning away across the ice, skittering, gone over the edge into the same nothing that waited for Marisol.

The gunman rolled free of Tomas and came up on his feet with a knife, and for one suspended moment the five of them hung on the summit shelf in the roaring white—Sef pressed back against the cairn with his arms out, saying something no one could hear; Odile between the gunman and Marisol; Tomas on his knees, blood threading pink down from his split brow; Isandro holding the pole, holding his sister, holding on.

Then the mountain decided.

The ice under the gunman's feet—the same glazed shelf-edge ice that had nearly taken Marisol—let go all at once, the way ice does, without malice and without mercy. He dropped the knife reaching for purchase that wasn't there. He slid. He went over the lip almost gently, almost politely, and the sound he made was very small, and then there was no sound at all.

Sef lunged.

Not away from the edge—toward it. Toward the crumbling lip where the ice was still going, because the gunman had caught something on the way down, some ledge, some root of stone, and Sef—Sef who had counted every coin of every life on this mountain—was diving full-length across the rotten ice to grab the man's wrist.

Isandro understood it later, or told himself he did: that Sef had hired the man, had brought him up this mountain, and that somewhere beneath the arithmetic there was still a ledger Sef kept that no one could burn. But in the moment there was no understanding. There was only the ice cracking under Sef's chest, spreading outward in white veins, and Sef's boots scrabbling for anything, and the gunman's weight dragging at the broken edge like an anchor.

Isandro let go of the pole.

Marisol screamed his name. He felt her hand close on his collar—felt her choose, in that instant, the same thing he was choosing—and then he was crawling across the shelf with the whole surface talking to him in cracks, lowering himself flat, flat as the dead, and taking Sef Almada's wrist in both hands.

Sef looked up at him. Snow in his eyes, in his mouth. Forty years of rivalry and debt and silence in his face, and underneath it, naked, the thing he had never once said aloud: *I did not want it to cost this.*

"Hold," Isandro said.

"I can't—"

"*Hold.*"

He hauled. The ice gave way beneath Sef in pieces, and Isandro took the whole of the man's weight across his shoulders, across the shelf, back from the lip, until they collapsed together on solid stone and the mountain went back to being only weather.

Below the edge, far below, there was nothing to be done for the gunman. Isandro did not look. Some accounts you cannot settle; you can only refuse to add to them.

* * *

Afterward there was the cairn, and the wind, and the five of them alive where six had started the climb.

Tomas sat with his head wound wrapped in Odile's scarf, shaking, staring at his own hands as though they belonged to someone braver than he'd planned to be. Odile stood at the shelf edge with her back to all of them, keeping whatever watch she kept. Sef knelt in the snow with his wrists loose on his knees, breathing in the ragged rhythm of a man whose books had finally failed to balance.

Marisol stood at the shrine itself, the oilcloth bundle in her hands, snow gathering on her shoulders like a shawl. The iron pole leaned where the storm had bent it. Above them, impossibly, the cloud tore open for a moment—a seam of grey-gold light falling down the escarpment, touching the cairn, touching her face—and closed again.

"He came for us," Tomas said suddenly, to no one. "Sef. He tried to save him."

"I know," Isandro said.

"Why?" The boy's voice cracked. "After everything. Why?"

Isandro looked at Sef, and Sef looked back, and neither of them answered, because the answer was the kind that ruins men to say: that even a man made entirely of arithmetic will dive across rotten ice for the one account he never wanted to open.

Marisol unwrapped one corner of the oilcloth. The ledger showed its worn cover to the wind—to all of them—their family's name embossed in faded gilt, the water rights of a dead valley, the fraud that had fed a trading post for fifteen years.

"Now," she said quietly. Not to Sef. Not to the mountain. To him. "You promised we'd decide together."

Isandro rose. His hands were torn from the ice. His brother-in-law's grave was three hundred miles behind him and his parents' graves were two days below, and the storm was closing again over the summit like a door.

He took the ledger from her hands.

Behind them, Sef got slowly to his feet and said, in a voice scraped hollow, "Isandro. Whatever you're about to do—think about what it costs."

Isandro thought about it. He had been thinking about it for fifteen years.

"The shrine wants an offering," he said.
