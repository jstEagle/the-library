## 1. The Map of Closed Lines

The last spike of the Marrowdale branch had been pulled forty-one years ago, and Beatrix Zhou found it in the weeds on the third day of her survey, half-buried in gravel near the old cattle dock, its head furred with rust the color of dried blood.

She didn't dig it out. She marked it on her chart instead—a small cross at chainage 14.2 kilometers, annotation: *ferrous debris, probable rail fastening*—and moved on, because that was the discipline of it. You did not mourn iron. You measured it.

The morning was doing that thing autumn mornings did in the high valleys, burning off the frost in slow silver sheets so that the dead line seemed to steam, as if something enormous were still breathing under the ballast. Beatrix walked the centerline with her hip against the weight of the surveyor's tripod slung over her shoulder, and the rails—where they survived—ran ahead of her in twin dull threads, sometimes vanishing entirely beneath a tide of bracken, sometimes surfacing in short bright segments where some farmer had dragged a gate across them for decades and polished the steel like a relic.

Dead lines had a sound. That was the first thing she'd learned, nine years ago, fresh out of her engineering qualification and hungry for contracts nobody else wanted. Live track hummed—you felt it through your boot soles, a frequency below hearing, the whole continent murmuring to itself. Dead track said nothing. It just lay there, holding the shape of the trains that used to run over it, the way a riverbed held the shape of water.

She liked that about them. She liked the honesty of absence.

By noon she had closed out the final section—the cutting above Marrowdale village where the rock face leaned in close enough that the old engineers had blasted it back by hand, leaving drill scars in neat vertical rows, a hundred and thirty years old and still legible, still *readable*, if you knew how to look. She set up the total station one last time, shot her closing prism, and let the instrument do its patient arithmetic while she stood with her hands in her pockets and looked down the length of the valley, at the line unspooling away toward the junction eleven kilometers east, toward the mainline, toward everywhere else.

Eleven kilometers of nothing, going somewhere. That was the whole trade, when you boiled it down. She mapped the ways people used to leave.

The radio at her belt crackled. "Zhou. You alive down there?"

"Barely," she said. "Closing shots done. I'll have the full package to you by Thursday."

"Thursday." A pause, the sound of someone chewing. Dev Okonkwo, her contract broker, had a way of pausing that meant he was deciding how much trouble to hand her along with the good news. "Actually, about that. Can you come through the office? There's a thing."

"There's always a thing."

"This one pays double."

Beatrix looked at the drill scars in the rock. Neat rows. Someone had stood here with a star drill and a hammer, all day, every day, for months, so that a train could pass through this cutting carrying sheep and milk churns and people escaping their villages for better lives, until one day nobody came at all.

"Double," she repeated.

"And a coastal job," Dev added, which was cheating, and they both knew it. He knew she took coastal work whenever she could get it. Salt air, sea light, the particular melancholy of infrastructure fighting an ocean—it suited something in her that she had never bothered to name. "Fogbound spur, disused since the sixties. Survey and condition assessment, heritage-adjacent, lots of paperwork. Boring as anything. You'll love it."

"Where?"

Another pause. Longer this time. And Beatrix felt it before he said it, the way you feel a train coming through the ground before you hear it—that sub-audible tremor, the hum of live track, except this was the opposite of that, a deadness opening up inside her chest, a riverbed remembering water.

"Greyhaven," Dev said. "Greyhaven Head."

* * *

She told herself, on the drive back through the hills, that the feeling in her hands was just fatigue. Three weeks of solo fieldwork would do that. She flexed her fingers on the wheel and watched the road unspool and named what she was feeling, clinically, the way she'd been taught to name things: *fatigue, low blood sugar, residual adrenaline from the descent into the cutting.* Not dread. Dread was not a professional emotion. She had spent nine years building a self that ran on professional emotions the way a locomotive runs on coal, and the machine worked, and she kept it fed, and it carried her from one dead line to the next without ever once asking her where she thought she was going.

Her grandmother had come from Greyhaven.

That was the whole of it, really. That was the thing Dev knew and she knew he knew, because three years ago, drunk on cheap lager after a conference in Leeds, she had told him—a sentence, no more than a sentence: *my grandmother grew up on that coast, she never once went back.* And Dev, who forgot birthdays and invoice numbers but never forgot leverage, had filed it away in whatever drawer he kept such things, and for three years he had not offered her a single contract within two hundred kilometers of that coast, and now he was offering her one, and calling it boring.

Her grandmother, Wen Zhou, née Zhou before the marriage changed nothing that mattered, had left Greyhaven in 1953 at nineteen years old with one suitcase and never explained why, not to anyone, not in sixty years of living inland. She had been a quiet woman, precise, an amateur painter of seascapes she painted entirely from memory because she refused to go and look at the sea again. She had died four years ago in a bungalow two hundred miles from salt water, and she had left Beatrix a tin box.

Beatrix had not opened the tin box.

It sat in the footwell of her truck right now, actually, because she'd thrown it in with her gear when she packed for Marrowdale—she couldn't have told you why—and it rode there under the spare fuse box and the backup batteries, a dented biscuit tin with roses faded almost to ghosts on the lid, weighing exactly as much as a biscuit tin should weigh and considerably more than that.

She had avoided Greyhaven Head for nine years. Every time a coastal contract came within range of it, she invented a conflict. Once she'd claimed a knee injury. Once she'd taken a worse-paying job in the opposite direction and called it a scheduling matter. She had built the avoidance into her professional life so smoothly that it looked, from the outside, like preference—she simply preferred other coastlines, that was all, everyone had preferences—and she had lived inside that fiction so long she'd nearly stopped noticing it was a fiction.

Nearly.

Dev's office was above a chandlery in a town whose harbor had silted up a generation ago, and he met her at the top of the stairs with the contract already printed, which told her how confident he'd been. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with reading glasses pushed up into gray-flecked hair and the permanent expression of a man listening to a joke only slightly funnier than the one being told.

"You're scowling," he said.

"I'm evaluating."

"You're scowling *professionally*. Sit down." He slid the folder across. "Client is Halloway Salvage and Recovery. You know them?"

"Reputationally."

"They've got backers—investment group, heritage-adjacent money, don't ask me to explain venture capital, I've tried. They want a full condition survey of the Greyhaven Head spur. Four kilometers of track, a tunnel or two, the seawall section. Apparently there's—" he flipped a page, squinting, "—some geological interest. Tide's been behaving oddly. Exposed some rock that isn't on any chart. They want it documented before winter shuts the window."

Beatrix didn't touch the folder. "Why me?"

"Because you're the best dead-line surveyor in the country and I'm not just saying that because you pay my mortgage."

"Why *me*, Dev."

He took his glasses down out of his hair and put them on, which he did when he was choosing to be honest, and looked at her over the rims. "Because you asked me three years ago never to send you within two hundred klicks of that coast, and I've honored that, and I'm breaking it now because the fee is stupid and because—" he hesitated, and she watched him decide, visibly, whether to say the next part. "Because I think you're running out of map."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means I've placed you on nineteen contracts in five years and you've extended fourteen of them past their scope for no billable reason. It means you took the Marrowdale job knowing there was nothing there but gravel and grief. It means—" he gestured at her, at all of her, the road-dusted jacket and the sun-cracked hands and the eyes that catalogued everything and reported nothing, "—you are the most thorough woman I have ever employed and the least *arrived*. And I think maybe the reason you won't go to Greyhaven is the same reason you should."

Beatrix stood up. She picked up the folder. This was, she understood dimly, the moment to say no—to say it cleanly, the way she'd said it for nine years, and walk back down the stairs past the chandlery smelling of rope and diesel, and take some comfortable contract in the flat interior where nothing was buried and nothing waited, and keep moving, keep mapping the ways out.

Instead she heard herself say: "What's the window?"

"Two weeks for signature. Mobilize by the end of the month. The tides—" Dev consulted the page again, and frowned slightly, as if the figures had rearranged themselves since he'd last read them. "The tides are apparently doing something unusual down there. Spring tides running well past predicted levels. The locals are making a fuss about it. Some cave or other."

"Cave," Beatrix said.

"Tourist thing, supposedly. Appears once in a blue moon, they charge visitors to look at it from a boat. Never been entered, far as anyone knows. Bad luck." He shrugged. "Coastal superstition. Probably a seal nursery or a smuggler's hole. Anyway. Halloway wants eyes on it. Your scope includes the cliff base survey."

Beatrix opened the folder. The header page was dense with boilerplate; the fee made her blink; the site plan showed the spur line running out along the headland in a thin black thread, and she recognized the geometry of it instantly, the way you recognize a face in a crowd that you've been trying not to look for—the curve around the cove, the tunnel bore through the head itself, the little station stub at the end where the world stopped.

Somewhere under the spare fuse box, in the truck parked on the street below, a biscuit tin weighed what biscuit tins weigh.

"Standard terms?" she said.

"Standard terms."

"Then I'll take it."

Dev smiled, and it wasn't entirely a smile of triumph; there was something gentler underneath it, something that might have been worry. "You're sure?"

"No," Beatrix said, and signed anyway.

* * *

That night, in the rented room over the Marrowdale pub, with her survey data backed up in three places and her kit half-packed for the drive west, she took the tin box out of her bag and set it on the table under the lamp.

She had carried it through four contracts and two changes of vehicle and one flood in a storage unit that had ruined everything else she owned. She had never opened it. Opening it would make it evidence, and evidence demanded interpretation, and interpretation demanded conclusions, and conclusions about her grandmother—who had pressed a folded paper map of the Greyhaven coastline into young Beatrix's hands at fifteen and said, in her precise inland voice, *learn this, in case you ever need to find your way home,* and then never spoken of the place again—conclusions about her grandmother were the one survey Beatrix had refused to conduct.

The lamp buzzed. Rain started against the window, soft and businesslike.

Beatrix turned the tin over. The bottom was stamped with a maker's mark and a date: 1948. Older than her grandmother's flight from the coast by five years. Old enough to have crossed the water in the suitcase, if the suitcase had room among everything else she was carrying out.

She got as far as prying up one corner of the lid. Underneath, the edge of paper—folded, yellowed, dense. Letters, by the look of the corner. A bundle of letters, tied, and something flat and stiff beneath them that might have been card, or board, or—

She set the lid back down. Pressed it closed. Her heart was going like a live rail hums, that sub-audible tremor, and she stood there in the lamplight with her palms flat on the tin and made herself name the feeling, clinically, professionally:

Not dread. Anticipation, misfiled.

She put the tin in the bottom of her kit bag, under the waterproofs, where she would not see it for the two-day drive west, and lay down on the narrow bed, and listened to the rain, and did not sleep so much as wait for morning the way a rail waits for a train.

Four kilometers of dead track. A tunnel through the headland. A cave that appeared once in a generation and charged admission and was never entered. A client with too much money and too many questions. And at the end of the line, a station stub where the world stopped, in a town that remembered a name she had spent her whole career pretending was just a surname.

*Just another job,* she told the dark ceiling, in the voice she used for progress reports.

The ceiling, being a ceiling, declined to comment. But in the bottom of the kit bag, in the dark, the tin box sat with its one lifted corner, and the letters inside it lay tied in their bundle like sleepers under snow, waiting for the line to open.
