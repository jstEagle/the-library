## 2. Arrival at Low Water

The train that brought Beatrix Zhou to Greyhaven was the last one of the day and, if the timetables were honest, nearly the last of anything. It was a two-car diesel unit with a wheeze in its air brakes and a driver who waved her aboard without checking her ticket, as though passengers had become a rumor he'd stopped believing but still liked to indulge. She rode alone in a carriage that smelled of salt-damp upholstery and old disinfectant, watching the country fold itself down toward the sea through windows filmed with spray.

She had been on closed lines for eleven years now—dead branches, mothballed spurs, freight sidings gone to rust and bramble—and she had learned their grammar. A living railway announced itself: signals, lights, the shine of use on railheads. A dead one kept its own counsel. You read it by what it declined to say. The embankments slumped into the shape of old shoulders. Culverts choked with silt sang differently under a walking stick than culverts still running clear. Ballast went grey where trains no longer ground it clean. Every abandoned line was a sentence someone had stopped writing mid-word, and Beatrix had made a career of finishing them—not restoring them, not resurrecting them, just mapping exactly where they ended and why.

It was good work. It was quiet work. Most importantly, it was work that kept her moving, and Beatrix Zhou had built her whole adult life on the principle that a person in motion could not be caught by anything standing still behind her.

Greyhaven was the assignment she had turned down twice before.

The first time, three years ago, she'd told Halloway's contract office the spur was too short to justify mobilization costs. That had been true, technically. The second time, eighteen months later, she'd claimed a scheduling conflict with a junction survey up north. Also true, technically. Both times she had sat afterward with her grandmother's letters—the ones that survived, the ones that weren't burned—and felt the particular shame of a woman who could survey any line in the country except the one that ran through her own name.

This time there had been no excuse available. This time the contract had come through her agent with a note attached: *Halloway specifically requests Z. They've heard you're the best with coastal geology.* And this time Beatrix had looked at the map—the little hook of rail curling out from Greyhaven station along the cliff shoulder toward Greyhaven Head, marked CLOSED 1961 in the archive's flat bureaucratic ink—and she had signed anyway, telling herself the same lie she always told herself: *it's just another job.*

The lie lasted until the sea came into view.

* * *

Greyhaven station appeared out of the fog the way things appear in dreams: all at once, and already familiar. One platform, canopied in green-painted iron gone scabrous with rust. A signal box with two of its three levers still standing like sentries who hadn't been relieved. A hanging station sign—GREYHAVEN in flaking white letters on blue enamel—and beneath it, chalked on the platform edge in a hand that had been refreshed recently enough to stay legible: *MIND THE GAP. IT MINDS YOU.*

Beatrix stepped down onto the platform with her kit bag over one shoulder and her instrument case in the other hand, and the fog closed behind the departing train as though it had never been.

The silence that followed was not empty. It was layered. Gulls somewhere above the cliff, crying in the flat tones of birds that have given up expecting fish. Water moving in the harbor below—a slap and suck against stone hulls. And underneath it all, so faint she might have imagined it, the sound the town made simply by existing: a door, a dog, a radio playing something with strings, all of it muffled by weather into a single continuous murmur, like a conversation happening in the next room about whether to mention something.

"Zhou."

The voice came from the signal box. An old man stood in its doorway, one hand resting on the frame, looking at her with an expression she couldn't parse—part recognition, part arithmetic, as though he were counting backward from her face to some earlier version of it.

"That's right," she said. "Beatrix Zhou. I'm here for the spur survey."

"I know who you are." He came down the box steps slowly, favoring his left knee, and she revised her estimate of his age upward—he moved like a man carrying sixty years plus a specific injury. He wore a stationmaster's coat too heavy for the season, brass buttons polished at the edges only, and a cap that had been reshaped by decades of a particular head. "Tom Ferris," he said. "Stationmaster. Such as it is."

He took her instrument case before she could object, testing its weight with a professional's grunt. "Heavy."

"Theodolite. Levels. Tripods." She shrugged. "I travel with my office."

"So did she."

Beatrix stopped walking. The fog beaded cold on the back of her neck. "I'm sorry?"

Ferris didn't turn around. He set off along the platform at his slow pace, and after a moment she had no choice but to follow, because he had her theodolite and because the alternative was standing alone in the fog with a question she wasn't ready to ask.

"Your grandmother," he said, when they'd reached the end of the canopy and the platform gave way to a cinder path running down toward the town. "Edith Zhou. Carried her instruments the same way. Said a woman with her own levels never had to take anyone else's word for true." He glanced back at her then, and his eyes were pale grey, the color of the sea on a day like this. "You've got her way of holding your shoulders. Like you're braced for a door to open."

"I think you've mistaken me for someone else," Beatrix said.

It was a bad lie and they both knew it. Ferris considered her for a long moment, and then—to her surprise and something adjacent to relief—he let it go. He shifted the case to his other hand and nodded down the path.

"Harbor pub does rooms. Mrs. Calloway. Tell her Tom sent you and she'll give you the one facing the water instead of the one facing the wall." He paused. "And Miss Zhou—the tide's out further than I've seen it in forty years. If you're walking the spur tonight, walk it before dark, and mind where the rock shows black below the cliff. Charts don't know everything."

He handed back her case at the top of the cinder path, touched his cap brim with two fingers—an old gesture performed with new hesitation, as though he'd caught himself halfway through it—and turned back toward his signal box without another word.

Beatrix stood a while on the path, listening to his footsteps fade into the fog, feeling the way you feel when a current shifts beneath a boat: nothing visible has changed, but everything is now headed somewhere else.

* * *

Mrs. Calloway's pub was called the Anchor and Chain, which Beatrix thought was either very honest or very optimistic. Inside it smelled of woodsmoke, beer, and brine—the smell of every harbor pub she'd ever known, and yet not quite; there was something else under it, something mineral and cold, like the air off a cellar that goes deeper than the building above it.

Her room faced the water, as promised. From its window she could see the harbor wall, the moored boats knocking gently against each other, and beyond them the great grey shoulder of Greyhaven Head, with the spur line's embankment running out along its base like a scar that had healed badly. The fog was thinning from the seaward side, peeling back in rags, and for perhaps four minutes she had a clear view of the cliff—and of the rocks below it, exposed by a tide that should not have been able to expose them.

She got out the charts that evening, after a supper of chowder eaten standing up because sitting felt too much like settling in.

The Admiralty chart for the coast was twenty years old, and even allowing for its age, the numbers were wrong. Low water today had fallen a full meter and a half below the predicted datum. Not a spring-tide anomaly—she checked the lunar tables twice—but something structural, as though the sea itself had drawn breath and was holding it. On the chart, the area below the Head was marked in the conventional stipple of a rocky foreshore, unremarkable. But tonight, through her window, in the last failing light, she could see the rock down there was not the same grey as the headland. It was darker. Almost black. Laid bare in bands and terraces, wet-gleaming, in shapes that did not look accidental.

Shapes that looked, if she was honest with herself, engineered.

Beatrix closed the curtain on it and told herself she'd survey it properly in the morning, with instruments, with method, with the professional detachment that had carried her safely past every difficult thing for eleven years. Then she unpacked her bag, and at the bottom of it, wrapped in oilcloth because it always traveled wrapped in oilcloth even though she almost never opened it, were her grandmother's letters.

There were nine of them. Edith Zhou had written them in the last year of her life, in a spidery hand that deteriorated letter by letter, to a granddaughter she'd met only six times. They said very little directly. They spoke of tides and timetables and the correct way to brew tea on a locomotive footplate. But threaded through them, the way ore threads through quartz, was Greyhaven—mentioned sideways, always, in phrases like *the water remembers longer than people do* and *there are lines on the coast that were never on any map, and that is how they stayed safe.*

Safe for whom, Edith had never said. Safe from whom, either.

Beatrix read the third letter by lamplight, the one that began *You will get this job offer someday, or one like it, and you will want to refuse it, and you must not refuse it,* and she put it down when her hands started shaking, and she went to the window, and pulled the curtain aside.

The fog had lifted entirely. Stars over the water. And below the Head, where the tide lay at its unnatural ebb, the black rock caught the starlight in long straight lines.

Lines. Plural. Parallel. Spaced, even at this distance, the way rails are spaced.

The past, Beatrix thought, pulling the curtain shut again, doesn't wait for you to arrive. It gets there first. It checks into the room next door. It sits down in the pub and orders what you order and waits for you to notice it's been there all along.

She slept badly, and dreamed of water going out and out and out, farther than any chart allowed, exposing something at the bottom that had been waiting a very long time to be seen—and in the dream, everyone in the town stood on the seawall watching it appear, and nobody said a word, and the silence had her grandmother's handwriting all over it.

* * *

Morning came grey and windless, the kind of coastal morning that feels less like weather than like held breath. Beatrix was on the spur by seven, boots on the old ballast, theodolite on her shoulder, working the line the way she always worked a line: from the station end outward, station by measured station, letting the geometry tell her its story.

The spur was dying gracefully, at least. Rails still in place for most of its length, though furred with orange lichen and drifted with sand. Sleepers soft-edged with rot but mostly sound—the coastal air here was more preservative than destructive, she noted, salt acting as a kind of embalming fluid. The embankment along the cliff shoulder showed classic scour damage on its seaward face, repaired at least twice in different eras: dry-stone at the base, Victorian and patient; concrete above it, postwar and brutalist; and a third repair, recent-ish, of gabion baskets now bulging and split like overstuffed mattresses.

Two miles out, the line curved around the shoulder of the Head and the cliff rose sheer on her landward side, forty feet of banded sedimentary rock seamed with quartz veins. Here the rails ended—or rather, here they *stopped*, cleanly, both rails terminating in mid-ballast with no buffer stop, no ramp, no explanation. Lines that simply stop are rare. Lines that are removed leave scars. These rails had been cut, deliberately, and the cut faces were bright even now, protected all these years by a film of grease she could still smear with her thumbnail.

Someone had meant to come back. Or someone had meant no one ever to go forward.

Beyond the cut, the formation continued as a rough track—walkable, clearly used by fishermen or walkers—for another quarter mile to where the cliff base met the foreshore. And it was there, standing at the terminus of a line that officially went nowhere, that Beatrix saw what the low water had uncovered.

The black rock terraces descended from the cliff base in steps, wide and regular, down to the waterline a hundred meters out. At first glance: natural wave-cut platform. Second glance: the steps were too uniform. Third glance—with her binoculars, bracing her elbows on the last sleeper, breathing carefully—the third glance showed her the walls between the terraces, and the openings in the walls, arched, mortared, and dark.

Tunnel mouths. Three of them, at descending levels, following the curve of the shore. Two were half-drowned in the returned shallows. The lowest, the largest, stood fully open to the air, its threshold glistening with retreating water, its interior a darkness that her binoculars could not resolve into anything except depth.

She lowered the glasses. Her heart was doing something unprofessional.

*The water remembers longer than people do.*

Behind her, gravel crunched. She turned, hand instinctively flat on her theodolite case, and found a woman standing on the track ten meters back—youngish, wind-burned, with a leather satchel across her chest and a sketchbook open in one hand, pencil already moving. The woman looked up, entirely unembarrassed at being caught drawing.

"You're the engineer," she said. It wasn't a question. "Zhou. Halloway's people said you'd be out early." She extended the pencil hand briefly, as an afterthought. "Ilse Marchetti. Cartographer. Same backers, different discipline." Her eyes went past Beatrix to the cliff base, to the black terraces, to the open dark of the lowest arch, and something lit in her face that Beatrix recognized and distrusted on sight, because it was the same thing that lit in her own.

"Those aren't on any chart," Ilse said. "I checked three editions. The lowest one—" she tapped her sketchbook, where the arch was already taking shape in confident strokes, "—that opening's maybe four meters high. Which means whatever's behind it is bigger than a cellar. Do you know how long the tide's been falling like this?"

"No," Beatrix said.

"Do you know what's behind it?"

"No," Beatrix said again, and heard, in her own voice, the exact tone her grandmother must have used for fifty years—the tone of a woman answering questions she already knew the answers to.

Ilse Marchetti looked at her for a moment longer than courtesy required. Then she smiled, turned back to her sketchbook, and said, lightly, in the tone of someone setting a hook:

"Well. Tomorrow's low water is lower still."
