## 3. The Cave That Charges Admission

The Anchor & Tide sat where the harbor road gave up and became the quay, its sign hanging by one chain, swinging in a wind that came off the water with the smell of kelp and old rope. Beatrix pushed through a door that stuck at the bottom—swollen wood, salt-swollen—and stepped into a warmth that hit her like a held breath released. Peat smoke, spilled stout gone sticky on the floorboards, wet wool drying in layers over the backs of chairs. A fire burned low in a grate that had been black since before anyone living was born.

Conversation did not stop when she entered. It *dipped*—a half-second lowering of voices, the way water dips around a stone—then resumed. She had been in a hundred pubs like this one, in a hundred towns whose names she'd let fall out of her memory like tickets from a torn roll. She knew the choreography: find the corner seat with sightlines to the door, order something local, say little, listen much.

She ordered the fish stew because it was the only thing on the chalkboard without an asterisk, and took the corner beneath a window fogged with condensation. Through the glass, the harbor lay emptying under the early dark, boats knocking their fenders against the pier, and beyond the breakwater the headland stood up against the last grey light like a sleeping animal's shoulder.

Greyhaven Head. She had avoided this coastline for eleven years. She had told herself, on the train down, that it was coincidence—the contract had come through Halloway's office, contracts came through Halloway's office, she took what paid. But her grandmother's letters were in the bottom of her kit bag, wrapped in oilcloth, and she had carried them through nine surveys and four countries without once reading past the first page. You do not carry a thing that far unless part of you means to open it.

"Stew's good," said a voice beside her. "Stew's always good here. It's the only thing that is."

He was old, sixty at least, with a face like weathered oak and hands that suggested fifty years of rope work. He set down a pint he hadn't been offered and looked at her with the frank appraisal of a man who has watched every stranger who ever came through his town.

"You'll be the surveyor," he said. Not a question.

"I'm doing a line assessment. For the railway."

"There hasn't been a railway assessment done honest on this coast since 1911." He said it mildly, almost cheerfully, and extended a hand. "Tom Ferris. Stationmaster. What's left of one."

"Beatrix Zhou."

His hand paused in hers—a fraction of a second, no more. Something moved behind his eyes like a fish turning under still water, and then it was gone, and he was just an old stationmaster shaking a stranger's hand.

"Zhou," he repeated, tasting it. "That's a name I know."

"It's common enough."

"It isn't, though." He smiled, and did not press, and pulled out the chair opposite her uninvited. She found she didn't mind. "You'll have seen the spur, then. Walked it?"

"This afternoon. It's in worse shape than the file suggests."

"The file." He laughed, a dry sound. "Files. The file says the Greyhaven Head branch closed in '54 for economic reasons. The file doesn't say the last engine went through with its whistle tied down so nobody would come out and wave goodbye. The file doesn't say my father drove it." He drank. "What does your file say about the cave?"

Beatrix kept her spoon moving. "There's no cave in the file."

"No," Tom said, with satisfaction. "There wouldn't be."

* * *

It took him most of a pint to tell it properly, and he told it the way old men tell things on coasts—circling, digressive, letting the story arrive sideways like weather. Around them the pub filled and emptied; a dog slept under the stove; the fire settled into embers the color of the sea at dusk.

"There's a cave in the cliff below the head," he said. "Sea cave, limestone and slate, older than any of us. And it opens maybe once in a generation—when the tide pulls back far enough and the winter storms have scoured the shingle away from the mouth. My grandfather saw it open in 1913. My mother saw it in '38. I saw it myself in '71, when I was a lad, and I'm the only man in this room who has."

"Entered it?" someone called from the bar, and there was laughter—not cruel, but worn smooth, the laughter of a joke told ten thousand times.

"*Saw* it," Tom corrected, raising his pint. "Seen it. From forty yards, at low water, with everyone else on the shingle. And that's all anybody's ever done, girl, and that's how we like it."

"Why?" Beatrix asked. "A cave's just geology."

"A cave's just geology until people put meaning in it." He leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the deep lines around his eyes. "Here's what they charge the tourists for, and mind, we've charged them every summer since the war—two shillings then, five quid now, and they pay it glad enough for a glimpse of a hole in a cliff. But here's what we don't tell them, or didn't used to, before the young ones stopped believing anything." He lowered his voice, though the whole pub was listening anyway. "In 1912, there was a flood on this coast. A storm surge took out the works on the new coastal line while it was being built. Took men with it. Eleven names cut into the wall of our church, and three families never got bodies back."

Beatrix's spoon went still.

"When the tide opened the cave the next spring," Tom went on, quieter, "the men who went down to look—they stood at the mouth, and those close enough swore they could hear water running inside. Running *inward*. Against the tide. Like the cave was drinking. And there were marks on the rock near the entrance—cut marks, tool marks, clean as a mason's chisel. In a cave that was supposed to be nothing but sea and time."

"And?"

"And nobody went in." He spread his hands. "Not then, not in '38, not in '71. Men who'd handle dynamite for a living stood on that shingle and would not cross the mouth of it. There was a feeling—call it superstition if you've a mind, and most do—that the cave had taken enough already. That it was holding something. That whatever the railway buried in 1912, the sea had agreed to keep."

"That's not a legend," Beatrix said slowly. "That's a guilt."

Tom looked at her for a long moment, and again that flicker behind his eyes, that fish under water.

"Some things are both," he said.

* * *

She saw the woman at the far table only when Tom left and the pub thinned—the kind of noticing that arrived late because the woman had arranged herself to be unnoticed, which was a skill Beatrix recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting. Dark hair cropped short, a canvas jacket with map-case wear at the elbows, and before her on the scarred wood a sheet of drafting film weighted with two pint glasses, on which she was drawing the coastline freehand.

Freehand. Beatrix watched the pencil move—quick, confident strokes, hatching for the cliffs, stipple for the shingle bank—and felt the professional itch she always felt watching someone else do her own craft badly or well. This was well. Better than well. The headland took shape in thirty lines as accurately as a week of instrument work.

The woman glanced up and caught her looking, and did not pretend otherwise.

"You're the engineer," she said. Her accent was somewhere continental, flattened by years of moving. "Zhou. Halloway's letter named you. Ilse Marchetti." She didn't offer a hand; her fingers were black with graphite. "Sit, if you want. I'm working out why your tide charts don't match my tide charts."

"They match. Your datum's wrong."

"My datum's fine. Your datum assumes the seabed hasn't changed since 1968." She turned the film toward Beatrix, and there it was: the bathymetric contours sketched in faint blue, and where the charts showed a uniform shelf below the head, Ilse had drawn a deep scour channel running along the cliff base like a moat. "Something's been digging out the cliff foot. Currents don't do that in fifty years. Currents take centuries. Unless—" she tapped the cave's position, marked with a small, precise x, "—there's a void in there large enough to alter the hydraulics. Which would mean the cave isn't a pocket. It's a system."

Beatrix sat down across from her despite every instinct that said corners, distance, exits.

"How long have you worked for Halloway?" she asked.

"Three weeks. You?"

"Six years."

"Then you can tell me what they actually want." Ilse said it lightly, but her eyes didn't. "Because the contract I signed says *coastal geomorphology survey*, and the retainer they paid says something else. Nobody pays that retainer to look at rocks. And nobody"—she tapped the x again—"hires two specialists independently and doesn't introduce them unless they're hoping we won't talk."

"We're talking now."

"Yes," Ilse agreed. "We are. Isn't it interesting how easy that was?"

The fire ticked. Somewhere behind the bar a tap dripped into a bucket with the patience of tide itself. Beatrix looked at the drawn coastline—at the headland rendered in thirty sure lines, at the small x below it—and felt the old instinct, the one that had kept her moving for eleven years, whisper its usual counsel: *don't ask, don't stay, don't know.* Curiosity was how people got rooted to places. Curiosity was how people ended up with names on church walls.

But her grandmother's letters were in her bag, unread, and the name Zhou was in this town's mouth, and the cave below the head had tool marks in it according to men who'd never gone in, and every one of those facts pulled at her like a current she could chart but not resist.

"What did the locals tell you about it?" she asked. "The cave."

"That it opens once in a generation. That they sell tickets to look at it. That entering it is bad luck." Ilse shrugged. "Superstition. Except—" she hesitated, and Beatrix watched her decide to be honest, watched the decision cost her something, "—except the ticket seller this morning, an old woman at the chandlery, told me her father sold tickets his whole life and made her swear on his deathbed never to go past the mouth. She wasn't performing. I've interviewed a hundred sources for maps. I know performance. That was fear."

"Fear of what?"

"That's the wrong question." Ilse began rolling the film with careful, exact turns. "The right question is: what does a Victorian engineering firm have to gain from a sea cave staying sealed? Because somebody cut those tool marks, and somebody built something down there worth guarding for a hundred years, and Halloway Salvage—which, incidentally, was founded by railway money, look it up—did not send two of us to this town by accident."

She stood, tucked the film into her case, and shrugged into her jacket. At the door she paused, backlit by the last grey light off the water.

"Low water tomorrow is 5:40," she said. "The old woman says the mouth will be clear again. I'm going down to look at it. Not enter it. Just look." A pause. "You can stand next to me and not-look too, if you like. Two people not-looking is better company than one."

The door swung shut behind her. Beatrix sat a while in the ember-light, the stew cold in front of her, listening to the sea work at the cliff below the head—patient, rhythmic, like something counting.

Once in a generation, the locals said. Once in a generation the sea gives it back.

And her grandmother had left this coast in disgrace, carrying letters Beatrix had never dared read, in a year she suddenly, terribly, wanted to check against the flood dates in the churchyard.

She left coins on the table and walked out into the wind, and did not let herself look at the headland.

She failed.
