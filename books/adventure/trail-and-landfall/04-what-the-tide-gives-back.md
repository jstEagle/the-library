## 4. What the Tide Gives Back

### Beatrix

She woke before the alarm, the way she always did in new places, her body refusing to trust a ceiling it hadn't slept under long enough. The window of her rented room above the chandlery was fogged from the inside, and when she wiped it with the heel of her hand the harbor appeared in a slice: black water, black boats, the headland a darker mass beyond, holding its secrets the way a shut fist holds a coin.

The tide was out. Farther out than it had any right to be.

She dressed in the dark, out of old habit—surveys rewarded the early, and the early were rewarded with silence—and let herself down the outside stair with the care of someone who had learned, in a dozen rented rooms across a dozen towns, not to wake the people who might ask questions. Her kit went over her shoulder. The theodolite case bumped her hip. The letters stayed in the room, in the bottom of her trunk, where she had put them the night she'd unpacked and told herself she'd only brought them because the trunk needed weight.

That was a lie, and she was a good enough surveyor to know a false reading when she took one.

The harbor road was wet and empty. Gulls argued over something on the slipway. She walked out along the sea wall, boots ringing on stone, and stopped where the charts said the water should be.

The charts were wrong. Or the sea was.

Below the cliff, where the Admiralty survey of 1911 showed nothing but submerged rock and a hazard notation, the tide had withdrawn like a held breath finally let go. A broad apron of black stone lay exposed, ribbed and glistening, running out toward the headland in shelves and terraces. Rock pools caught the last of the starlight. And there, at the base of the cliff where the land folded in on itself, a mouth.

She had read about it in the pub last night, in the way you read a menu—half listening, professional interest only. The Greyhaven cave. Appears once in a generation. The Ferris family had charged sixpence a head to view it from a rope line in her grandmother's day; Tom Ferris's father had kept the ledger. Nobody went in. Nobody, the barman had said with a smile that wasn't entirely a smile, if they knew what was good for them.

Beatrix did not believe in bad luck. She believed in tide tables, in load-bearing stone, in the difference between what a drawing showed and what the ground insisted on. She climbed down the iron ladder bolted into the cliff—older than she'd expected, the rungs worn bright in the middle by a century of hands—and dropped onto the exposed shelf.

The rock under her boots was not beach rock. She knew that within four steps. Beach rock is rounded, patient, the sea's own work. This was cut. Flat bedding planes where no bedding plane should run, edges too true, a channel grooved into the surface that ran level as a spirit line toward the cave mouth—a drainage channel, or a rail bed. Her hand went to it before her mind did, fingers reading the stone the way they'd read a thousand cuttings, and her breath came out slow.

Engineered. This apron was engineered. Somebody had built a working surface here, below the tide line, and then let the sea take it back.

The cave mouth was tall as a goods-shed door, its arch dressed with stone blocks whose mortar had gone soft as old bread. She stood at the threshold with her torch unlit, letting her eyes adjust, and the cold air that breathed out of it smelled of salt and iron and, faintly, impossibly, of creosote—timber preservative, the smell of every rail yard of her childhood, her grandfather's overalls, the sleepers stacked behind the house in Carrickmoor.

She clicked the torch on.

The beam went in perhaps thirty feet and found the wall. And on the wall, low, at the height of a kneeling hand, in chalk that the sea had failed to erase—

*ZHOU*

She did not flinch. She was quite sure she did not flinch. What she did was take a step backward, one boot scraping on dressed stone, and stand very still with the torch beam fixed on five letters as though they were a fault line and she the instrument sent to measure it.

Her grandmother's handwriting. She knew it the way she knew her own pulse—the high crossbar on the H, the Z with the little serif foot, the same hand that had addressed forty years of letters to a granddaughter in inland towns, letters Beatrix carried in a trunk because the trunk needed weight.

The tide had gone out farther than the charts allowed. Her grandmother's name was chalked inside a cave that officially did not exist. And Beatrix Zhou stood at the entrance with a surveyor's torch in her hand and the first honest feeling she'd had in years rising in her throat like water into a dry channel.

She heard boots on the ladder behind her and did not turn around.

### Ilse

Ilse Marchetti had learned long ago that the coast tells its truths at dawn or not at all, so she was on the headland with her drawing board before the light was worth the name, watching the sea perform its vanishing act. She'd been hired to map a coastline for people who talked about salvage in the smooth voices of men who had never gotten salt in a wound, and she had accepted because the money was good and because Greyhaven Head, on the three charts she'd been able to find, was a blank with a warning on it. Blanks were why she'd become a cartographer. A blank was a dare.

The sea was going out like a rumor being denied. She sketched it as it went—the shelf appearing terrace by terrace, the water sliding off black stone in long silver sheets—and by the time the light turned from iron to pewter to the first thin gold, the cave mouth stood open below her, breathing.

Someone was already down there.

She watched the figure for a while through the long lens of habit: a woman, compact, moving with the particular economy of someone who measured things for a living. The engineer. Zhou. They'd been introduced at the pub the night before—Halloway's people, all of them, gathered under one low ceiling like tools in a box—and Ilse had liked her immediately and been slightly alarmed by how much. Zhou had the kind of attention that made you want to be worth it. She'd also had the kind of silence that made you wonder what she was not saying, and Ilse had spent enough years around silences to know they came in two kinds: the kind that meant *I have nothing to add*, and the kind that meant *I am adding it constantly, in a place you can't see*.

She climbed down. The ladder rungs were cold enough to bite, and the shelf below was stranger than it had looked from above—those channels, those too-true edges. She knelt and set her board on a flat block and began to draw, because drawing was how she thought, her pencil finding the geometry before her words could.

"You're up early," Zhou said, coming toward her along the shelf.

"So are you." Ilse tipped her head at the cave. "That's not on the 1911 chart."

"No."

"Or the 1938. Or the current one. There's a hazard symbol and a note that says 'rock, submerged.'" She turned her board so Zhou could see the sketch taking shape: the shelf, the channels, the arch. "Somebody cut all this."

"Somebody did." Zhou crouched beside the drainage channel and put her hand in it the way you'd put your hand in a river, reading the current. "Six-foot gauge, if this is what I think it is."

"A rail bed. Below the tide line."

"Victorian engineers did worse things for worse reasons." Zhou stood, and there it was again, that flicker behind her eyes, a door closing on a lit room. "I should run a proper survey before the tide comes back. Levels, offsets, the mouth dimensions. You want to sketch the exterior?"

"I want to sketch the interior," Ilse said. "You want company?"

Something moved across Zhou's face—quick, gone, like a fish under a boat. "It's not safe. Slack tide window's short."

"Then we'd better be quick." Ilse was already packing her board. "I don't need you to keep me safe, Ms. Zhou. I need you to tell me what I'm looking at."

That earned her a look—assessing, surprised, and for one unguarded second almost warm. "Beatrix," she said. "If we're going to drown together, you may as well."

* * *

### Beatrix

Inside, the cave was a throat, and the throat had been lined by hands.

Her torch found dressed stone above, cut steps below, a passage running back into the headland on a grade so consistent her engineer's blood sang and her engineer's suspicion howled. This was not a sea cave with pretensions. This was a tunnel that had been driven from the landward side, deliberately, into the cliff, and the sea had been allowed to think it owned the entrance. She set up her hand level on the steps and shot the grade twice because she did not want to believe it the first time: one in fifty, falling inland. A drainage gradient. Somebody had planned for water.

Ilse moved beside her with a small sketchbook, drawing fast, her pencil making a sound like rain on canvas. She was good. Beatrix had worked with surveyors who took an hour to record what this woman took in three minutes, and recorded it better.

"How far back does it go?" Ilse asked.

"Farther than our tide window." Beatrix checked her watch against the tide table she'd memorized at breakfast. "We have maybe ninety minutes before the water starts thinking about coming home. I want the first two hundred feet documented and then we turn around, whatever we find. Agreed?"

"Agreed." Ilse's torch swept the wall. "Beatrix. Come look at this."

She knew before she turned. She had known since she'd seen it alone, and some part of her had been hoping the dawn light had lied to her, the way dawn light lies about everything.

The chalk letters stood at the base of the wall, low, where a kneeling person had written them. Not scratched. Written, with care, the way you'd sign a document. *ZHOU.*

Ilse was looking at the letters. Then, slowly, she was looking at Beatrix.

Beatrix made herself do the professional thing. She crouched, photographed it with the little camera from her kit, measured the lettering height—eleven centimeters—recorded the position by chainage from the entrance, and said, in a voice she kept flat as a level table, "Chalk. Marine environment should have taken it decades ago. It's sheltered here. Microclimate."

"Zhou," Ilse said. "That's your name."

"It's a common name."

"It isn't, though." Ilse said it without accusation, which was worse. She had the tone of a woman reading a contour line that didn't match the ground and knew the ground was right and the map was wrong. "I looked you up when Halloway sent the file. Beatrix Zhou, branch-line surveyor, eleven years, fourteen contracts, no fixed address. There's not many Zhoos in the railway records. There's exactly one, and she's standing in a cave with her name on the wall, and when I said it just now you went white around the mouth."

"I did not go white around the mouth."

"You absolutely did. I draw faces. I know what they do." Ilse crouched beside the letters, not touching them. "Who wrote this?"

The tide breathed somewhere behind them, far off, patient. Beatrix looked at the handwriting of a woman thirty years dead, a woman who had left this coast in disgrace and never once, in forty years of letters, said why—letters Beatrix had reread last night by lamplight until one line stood up out of the page and refused to sit back down. *The sea keeps what it is given, and gives back what it owes. One day it will give it back and you will wish it hadn't, or you will thank it, and I won't be there to tell you which.*

She had thought it was an old woman's poetry. Standing here, it felt like instructions.

"I don't know," Beatrix said, and it was the first lie she'd told Ilse Marchetti, and they both heard it, and neither of them said so. "My grandmother came from Greyhaven. She left when she was young. She never talked about it."

Ilse was quiet a moment. Then she opened her sketchbook to a fresh page and began to draw the letters, exactly, at exact scale, with the date and the chainage in her small precise hand. "Then it goes in the record," she said. "Unattributed. For now." She glanced up, and her eyes in the torchlight were steady and not unkind. "For now, Beatrix."

* * *

### Ilse

They made it out with forty minutes of the window to spare, the sea already lapping at the outer shelf like a dog remembering its post. On the headland, in the strengthening light, Ilse sat on the turf with her board and drew the cave mouth from memory while Beatrix stood at the cliff edge with her back to her, doing whatever it was Beatrix did instead of talking—checking figures, probably, or not checking figures.

Ilse had spent her career among people who hid things in plain sight: developers who wanted maps to say what they'd already decided, agencies that redacted coastlines, clients who hired her for her pencil and resented her eyes. She knew a concealed structure when she saw one, and Beatrix Zhou was one—load-bearing, well-maintained, built to code, and concealing something at her center that the whole design existed to keep the sea from reaching.

The name on the wall. The flinch—yes, it had been a flinch, whatever Beatrix claimed; Ilse had watched her whole body recalibrate around those five letters, the way a bridge recalibrates around a crack. And underneath the flinch, when Beatrix had said *my grandmother came from Greyhaven*, something else had moved through her face, something that was not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of a woman reading a letter she'd written to herself and forgotten.

Ilse drew the arch, the dressed blocks, the chalk name small and exact in the corner of the sheet, and thought about the fact that she had liked this woman within a minute of meeting her, and about what that was going to cost.

"Your tide's coming back," she said, not looking up.

"I know." Beatrix didn't turn. "Ilse. What Halloway told you this contract was—what did they tell you, exactly?"

"Coastal survey, pre-salvage assessment. Recoverable artifacts, commercial valuation." Ilse shaded the shadow side of the arch. "Which is a strange phrase to use about a cave that's been sealed for a hundred years. You don't salvage what nobody's lost. Unless somebody knows what's in there."

The silence from the cliff edge went on long enough that Ilse looked up. Beatrix was staring out at the water, and the morning light had found her face, and on it was the expression of someone standing at a junction with the signals against her and a train she couldn't stop coming down the main line.

"Last night," Beatrix said slowly, "going through my grandmother's papers, I found a reference. To the headland. To a line that was built and then struck from the company's surveys." She turned, and her voice had the careful evenness of a woman laying rails across soft ground, testing each one. "The file Halloway sent you. Did it say who surveyed the original coastal extension? Who drew the charts with the blank on them?"

Ilse flipped back through her folder. "The Greyhaven Extension, 1889. Chief engineer of record was—" She found the page and read it, and then read it again, because the name was the same one chalked in the cave, the same one stitched into the surname of the woman standing in front of her.

"Zhou," she said. "Elias Zhou."

The gulls went up off the cliff in a ragged white sheet, all at once, as if something below had startled them. Far out, the sea was turning, the first of the flood tide sliding in over the black engineered stone, covering the rail bed, covering the steps, sliding up toward the cave mouth where a dead woman's chalk handwriting waited in the dark for the water to come and take its turn at reading it.

Beatrix had gone very still. "He was my great-grandfather," she said. "And until last night, I didn't know his name."

Ilse closed her folder. Down below, the tide was already at the threshold, and somewhere behind them, up the coast road, she could hear the engine of a Halloway truck starting its morning—men with contracts and cranes coming to see what the sea had given back, and to take it away again.

"We should get back," Ilse said. "Before they do."

Beatrix nodded once, and turned up the cliff path, and Ilse followed, thinking about chalk, and water, and how the truth of a coast is always written at the lowest tide—and how someone always arrives, sooner or later, to erase it.
