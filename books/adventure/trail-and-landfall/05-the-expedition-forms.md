## 5. The Expedition Forms

They came on a Tuesday, the way trouble always does—on schedule, in daylight, with paperwork.

Beatrix heard the van before she saw it: the wrong engine note for the coast road, too clean, too urban, grinding up from the lowlands where the hedgerows gave way to wire fences and the sea was something people looked at rather than lived against. She was on the cliff path above the spur with her level balanced on her tripod, shooting elevations along the old ballast shoulder, and she stood very still while the sound climbed toward her, the way you stand still when you hear a snake in dry grass.

The van was white. Of course it was white. Halloway Salvage painted everything white—the vans, the hard hats, the invoices—and Beatrix had spent eleven years telling herself that white meant neutrality, professionalism, the clean canvas of honest work. Watching this one crawl the last mile into Greyhaven, throwing dust that hung in the salt air like a held breath, she understood for the first time that white also meant erasure. White was what you painted over things.

* * *

She met them at the harbor lot because there was nowhere else to meet anyone. The van disgorged five people and an amount of equipment that made her survey kit look like a child's pencil case: hard cases stenciled with the Halloway anchor-and-hook, a drone crate the size of a coffin, dive bags, a portable compressor, and a folding table that unfolded into an office with a kind of mechanical smugness.

"Zhou." The man who said it was fifty-ish, silver at the temples, wearing a fleece that cost more than Tom Ferris's annual coal bill. He put out his hand. "Marcus Cray. Legal and compliance, Halloway Salvage Group. You'll be reporting through me now."

"Through me," Beatrix said, not taking the hand immediately, making him hold it out one beat longer than comfortable. "I was told I'd be leading the survey."

"You are leading the survey." Cray smiled the way a contract smiles—all clauses, no warmth. "And the survey reports to compliance. Standard structure. Everyone knows what everyone's doing." He turned to the others. "This is Ms. Zhou. She knows the line."

*She knows the line.* Not *she knows these lines*, plural, her whole career of dead branches and closed spurs. *The line.* As if the coastal spur were the only thing she'd ever surveyed, as if Halloway had hired a person instead of renting a key.

The divers came next: two brothers by the look of them, Okafor by their name tags, broad-shouldered and quiet, checking their regulators with the tenderness other men reserved for instruments or children. They nodded at her without ceremony. She liked them instantly and distrusted herself for liking them; they were here to do a job, the same as her, and the job was the problem, not the hands doing it.

Last out of the van was the drone operator, a young woman with cropped hair and the thousand-yard patience of someone who lived through screens. Her name was Petrel, which Beatrix suspected was chosen rather than given, and her first act on Greyhaven soil was to look up—not at the cliffs, not at the cave mouth still hidden behind its curtain of fog, but at the sky, assessing it, the way Beatrix assessed ballast. Judging whether the world would let her work.

"Wind's marginal," Petrel said, to no one.

"It's always marginal here," Beatrix said. "That's why the charts have gaps."

Cray's head came around at that, quick as a gull on a chip. "Gaps?"

"Gaps." She let him wait. "The Admiralty surveys never resolved the seabed below Greyhaven Head. Too much surge, too much kelp. If your drones can map what eyes can't, Mr. Cray, they'll be earning their freight."

Something moved behind his eyes—satisfaction, she thought, or appetite—and she felt the first cold stone of complicity settle somewhere below her ribs. She had handed him information. She hadn't meant to. That was how it started; she knew that now, knew it the way you know a tide is turning, by the small water moving against everything you assumed.

* * *

They set up in the old net loft above the chandlery, which Cray had leased by phone before any of them arrived, another fact Beatrix filed under *things done around me*. From its salt-bleached windows you could see the whole harbor and, when the fog thinned, the black slot in the cliff where the sea had begun its once-a-generation courtesy.

Cray worked at the folding table like a man at an altar. Every morning at seven—he kept London time, not tide time—he read contracts aloud. Not to anyone. To himself, in a low liturgical murmur, clause by clause, sub-clause by sub-clause, while he drank coffee from a thermos cup and annotated a master copy with a fountain pen that scratched like a rat in a wall. Beatrix timed him the second morning out of morbid curiosity: forty minutes. Schedule 3, indemnities. He read indemnities the way Tom Ferris read weather.

"You could just read them silently," she said on the third day. "Most people do."

"Silent reading lets you skim." Cray didn't look up. "Skimming is how companies die. I read every word my clients sign, aloud, so I hear what they're agreeing to. And every word signed against them." He tapped the master copy. "You're signatory to Schedule 1, Ms. Zhou. Survey authority, site access, data custody. Have you read it aloud?"

"I've read it."

"But not aloud."

"No."

He finally raised his eyes, and there was nothing cruel in them, which was somehow worse. Cruelty you could fight. What Cray had was certainty, poured smooth as grout. "Then you don't yet know what you agreed to. Most people don't. That's not an accusation—it's the entire basis of my profession." He went back to his murmuring. *Force majeure shall include but not be limited to...*

That night Beatrix took her copy of Schedule 1 down to the seawall and read it aloud to the water, quietly, feeling foolish until the fourth page, when she stopped feeling foolish and started feeling cold.

*All materials recovered within the designated claim area, including but not limited to ferrous artifacts, documents, cartographic materials, and items of historical or commercial interest, shall vest absolutely in Halloway Salvage Group upon recovery.*

*Documents.* *Cartographic materials.* *Items of historical interest.* She stood on the seawall with the paper trembling slightly in the wind off the water and did the arithmetic she should have done in London, weeks ago, when the contract first crossed her desk and she had skimmed it—skimmed it, God help her, exactly the way Cray said people died.

A survey didn't need language about recovered documents. A survey produced drawings and reports; it didn't *vest* anything. Vesting language belonged to salvage. Wreck salvage. Treasure salvage. She thought of the cave mouth opening in the cliff like a held breath finally released, and of the locals who sold tickets to glimpse it and would not go in, and of the phrase Ilse had pulled out of the pub gossip like a splinter: *never entered, on pain of bad luck.*

Bad luck. Or bad history.

She read the rest aloud anyway, every word, her voice roughening against the wind, because Cray was right about one thing: you heard different things with your ears than with your eyes. What she heard, on page nine, was the sound of a door closing behind her—slowly, politely, with all the contractual courtesy in the world.

* * *

The drones went up Thursday, when the wind dropped enough to make Petrel stop frowning at the sky. Beatrix watched from the cliff top as the machine lifted off the turf with a sound like an angry wasp the size of a gull, climbed, and hung above the headland, patient and blind-eyed, seeing everything.

On Petrel's screen, the world became contour and color-code. The seabed below the Head resolved for the first time in the history of the charts: reefs like knuckles, kelp forests rendered in false green, and there—a shape that made even Petrel go quiet. A linear anomaly running out from beneath the cliff, straight-edged, regular, buried under sand and stone.

"That's not geology," Petrel said.

"No," Beatrix said. Her mouth had gone dry. "It isn't."

Because she knew that geometry. She had known it since the second night in Greyhaven, sitting up with her grandmother's letters spread across the boarding-house bed, reading words written seventy years ago in faded iron-gall ink: *the cut runs seaward under the Head, further than any chart will admit, and if they ask, we never built it.* She had told herself then it was metaphor, or madness, or the ordinary exaggeration of an old woman's letter. Standing over Petrel's screen, watching the drone trace a dead-straight line beneath the sea itself, she ran out of ways to tell herself anything.

"There's a tunnel under there," she heard herself say, in her professional voice, the voice she used for reports. "Old. Masonry-lined, if I had to guess. It connects to the cave system."

Cray appeared at her shoulder the way he always did, without footsteps, as if he moved on casters. "Connected to the claim area?"

"The claim area is the cave," Beatrix said. "The claim area is whatever's inside the cave."

"Then anything connected to the cave," Cray said pleasantly, "is inside the claim." He wrote something in the margin of his master copy. The pen scratched. Somewhere below them, the tide was going out farther than the charts allowed, laying bare dark rock, keeping its appointment.

* * *

Friday evening the fog came in thick as wool and the whole expedition retreated to the harbor pub, because there was nowhere else, and because Cray had decided—correctly, damn him—that goodwill was an asset worth cultivating. He bought rounds. He asked the fishermen questions about tides and wrecks and wrote the answers down, and the fishermen, flattered and wary in equal measure, gave him more than they meant to.

Beatrix sat at the end of the bar with a pint she wasn't drinking and watched complicity settle into her the way wet gravel settles into a boot tread: grain by grain, uncomfortable, permanent unless you made the effort to shake it out.

Tom Ferris was there, in his usual corner, and he watched Cray the way an old dog watches a new animal in the yard—not hostile yet, just cataloguing. When Cray excused himself to take a phone call on the step, Tom ambled over and settled onto the next stool with the unhurried air of a man who owned all the time in the world.

"Your backers," he said.

"My employers."

"Mm." He turned his glass a quarter-turn. "Halloway. They did the wreck out of Porthmoor, year back. Lifted the brass off her and left the hull for the lobster pots to foul. Made a fine profit." He took a slow drink. "Town here's been asking after you, Miss Zhou. Asking what it is you're surveying so hard."

"And what do you tell them?"

"That you're a Zhou, and Zhous don't come back to Greyhaven for nothing." He set the glass down with a click that sounded, in the pub noise, like a small door latching. "Your grandmother never told you why she left, I expect."

The floor seemed to tilt a degree, the way decks do. "You knew my grandmother."

"Everyone knew your grandmother." Tom's eyes were pale and kind and utterly unyielding. "Everyone knows a Zhou. There's a wall in the mission hall with names on it, girl. Three of them are yours." He stood, joints cracking like ship's timbers, and patted the bar twice, a benediction or a summons. "When your lawyers and your divers are done playing in the fog, you come and see me. Before the tide takes its chance."

He shuffled out into the fog. Beatrix sat with her untouched pint and felt the gravel shift.

* * *

Saturday morning, Cray read contracts at breakfast in the net loft while the divers checked their gear and Petrel charged batteries, and Beatrix stood at the window looking at the cliff, where the fog was thinning in rags, and the black slot beneath it waited with the patience of something that had already outlasted a century.

"Schedule 4," Cray murmured behind her, pen scratching. "*Recovery operations shall commence no later than...*"

She had led surveys for eleven years. She had walked dead lines from one end of the country to the other, and told herself every time that she was mapping the past so it couldn't follow her. Now she stood in a rented loft above a harbor full of people who remembered her grandmother's name, employed by men who measured the past in recoverable value, holding a signature that made her the legal front for whatever came out of that cave—letters, blueprints, confessions, bones, it made no difference, it would all *vest absolutely*—and she understood, with the clarity that arrives only after the moment it could have been useful, that she had not been hired to lead a survey.

She had been hired to be the reason nobody asked questions. A Zhou, walking a Zhou line, with a clipboard. Respectability, contracted by the page.

The fog tore open along the cliff face. For three seconds—no more—the cave mouth stood revealed, black and patient, and Beatrix felt it looking back at her the way it must have looked at her great-grandfather, whoever he had been, whatever he had sealed inside.

Behind her, Cray's pen paused. "Ms. Zhou," he said pleasantly. "About tomorrow's descent. There's a matter of custody I need you to initial."
