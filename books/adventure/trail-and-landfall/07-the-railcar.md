## 7. The Railcar

The collapse was not a collapse.

Beatrix saw it before anyone else did, and she knew what she was seeing the way she knew a forged signature—by the shape of its confidence. The rockfall that sealed the inner chamber lay in a fan across the passage floor, granite and shale tumbled together, and every stone of it had been placed by hands that understood exactly where hands should place them. The big blocks sat at the base like quoins. The smaller ones were packed behind them in courses, tight as masonry. Water had been working at it for a hundred and forty years and had managed to loosen nothing worth naming.

"Rubble," said Denny Okafor, one of Halloway's divers, sweeping his lamp across it. "Dead end."

"It's a wall," Beatrix said.

She knelt and put her palm flat against the lowest block. The stone was cold enough to burn, slick with condensation, but beneath the wet she felt the chisel ghost of dressing marks—fine, parallel, deliberate. Nobody's avalanche dresses its own stones. She thought of the tool marks Ilse had found in the outer passages three days ago, the engineered cuts nobody could explain, and felt the same sensation she'd been carrying since the cave mouth opened: that they were not exploring this place but being shown through it, room by room, by someone long dead who had wanted very much to be understood.

"Cray will want an assessment," said Priya Nair, the other diver, already unclipping her slate.

"Cray can have one." Beatrix ran her light along the top of the fall, where a gap yawned between the rubble and the ceiling—not large, perhaps eighteen inches at its widest, narrowing to nothing four feet in. "Somebody built this on purpose, and somebody else went through it anyway. Look at the scrape marks on the upper blocks. Fresh relative to everything else down here. Iron on stone."

Ilse Marchetti crouched beside her without being asked. She had a way of arriving at Beatrix's shoulder that had stopped feeling like intrusion sometime around the fourth day and had started feeling like something Beatrix did not have a name for and was not looking for one. "Through there?"

"Through there."

"It'll be tight with tanks."

"We leave the tanks." Beatrix looked back down the passage, toward the grey thread of daylight at the cave mouth, toward the water lying flat and patient in the throat of the rock. Slack tide gave them ninety minutes, maybe less; the barometer had been falling all week and the sea had begun to keep shorter hours. "Denny, Priya—you two hold here, run your line, keep the exit honest. If we're not back in forty minutes, come in after us or don't, but don't do anything brave."

"And you?" Priya asked.

"I'm going to see what my family buried."

She hadn't meant to say it aloud. It came out level and professional, the way she said most true things, and for a moment the passage was so quiet she could hear the tide breathing somewhere below them, a vast slow lung filling and emptying under the cliff. Ilse looked at her—just looked, no question in it yet, only attention—and then began unbuckling her tank harness with the brisk economy of someone who had already decided.

* * *

The gap took them on their bellies, lamps held ahead like candles in a wind that didn't exist. Beatrix went first because it was her weight against the stone first, her ribs learning the geometry of the squeeze, and because some part of her had understood, since the morning she'd found the name ZHOU chalked inside the entrance in a hand that trembled, that this was not going to be a survey she could conduct from arm's length. The rock pressed down along her spine and she breathed shallow and counted, the way her grandmother had taught her to count when the storms came inland off the fens—*one for the roof, two for the road, three for the ones who didn't make it home*—a nonsense rhyme that had never been nonsense at all, she realized now, at fifty feet under a headland her family had helped hollow out.

Then the passage opened, and her lamp fell into a space that swallowed the light whole.

She pulled herself out onto standing-height floor and lifted the beam, and for three full seconds she could not speak, could not even form the intention of speaking, because the chamber was not a chamber. It was a station.

Not metaphorically. Not approximately. It was a railway platform, cut from the living rock, maybe sixty feet long, with a dressed edge, a drainage channel running its length, iron mooring rings set into the wall at intervals—and standing against the far wall, on a stub of track that ran out of the darkness and stopped, was a railcar.

It sat there the way things sit when they have been left carefully: square, level, wheels chocked with timber that had gone black with age but had not rotted, because the air down here was cold and salt-dry and indifferent to time. It was a four-wheel brake van, Victorian, wooden-bodied, its paint gone the color of dried blood but its structure sound, and someone had painted the number on its side within the last century of anyone caring about such things: **G.H.R. 7**.

Greyhaven Head Railway. A company that appeared in no ledger Beatrix had ever audited, on no map Ilse had ever drawn, under no act of Parliament either of them could find. A line that officially never existed, terminating in a station under the sea.

"Ilse," she said, and her voice came out strange, flattened by the rock. "Come and see what we've found."

Ilse came up out of the gap, got her feet under her, raised her lamp—and Beatrix watched the light hit her face and watched understanding arrive there in stages, the way dawn comes up over water. Wonder first. Then the cartographer's hunger, the hunger to measure and record. Then, last, something quieter and more careful, aimed sideways at Beatrix herself.

"You knew," Ilse said. Not accusing. Testing the weight of it.

"No." And it was true, mostly, in the way that mattered least. "I suspected. There's a difference, and I'll answer for it later. Right now—" She turned back to the railcar. "Right now I want to know what's inside."

The door wasn't locked. Of course it wasn't locked. Whoever had sealed this place had wanted it found—that was the whole architecture of it, the dressed stones, the scrape marks, the chalked name at the entrance like a signature on an envelope. The handle turned stiffly, shedding flakes of rust, and the door swung open on hinges that shrieked once and then remembered themselves, and Beatrix's lamp swept the interior of G.H.R. 7 and lit up the last hundred and forty years of her family's silence.

Papers. Thousands of them. Bundled in oilcloth and tied with tarred twine, stacked in cedar trays that some joiner had fitted to the car's interior with loving precision. Blueprint tubes stood in a rack along one wall, brass-capped, each labeled in copperplate: *Cutting 4 — Section East. Sea Wall — Profile C. Greyhaven Viaduct — As Built (Corrected)*. That last word—*corrected*—went into Beatrix like a splinter. Blueprints were corrected when the truth diverged from the plan. Somebody had kept both versions.

And on the near tray, alone, weighted down with a lump of lead shot, a single envelope, unsealed, with one word on it in the same trembling hand that had chalked the entrance:

**Zhou.**

"Photograph everything before anything moves," Beatrix heard herself say, and was grateful to hear her professional voice still worked, still lived somewhere in her body independent of the rest of her. "Ilse—scale bar, register numbers, the lot. If this place comes down around us tomorrow, I want the record to survive."

Ilse was already moving, already shooting frames, her movements quick and sure in the way that made Beatrix's chest ache in a manner she filed firmly under *later*. Beatrix lifted the envelope. The paper had gone soft and ivory with age, and it weighed almost nothing, and it weighed more than anything she had ever carried.

Inside was a single sheet, written on both sides, dense, the ink faded to the brown of old tea. At the bottom, a signature she knew—not from life, she had never met the man, he'd been dead seventy years before she was born—but from the framed certificate that had hung in her grandmother's front hall all through her childhood, the one artifact of Greyhaven her grandmother had kept, face turned always to the wall when Beatrix was small, until the year she died, when Beatrix had turned it over and read the name and felt, for the first time, the shape of a door she would spend twenty years refusing to walk through.

**Elias Zhou. Chief Engineer, Greyhaven Head Railway.**

Her great-grandfather.

She read it standing in the dark, in the cold, with the sea breathing underneath her, and she read it twice, because the first time her eyes kept sliding off the words as if the page were wet.

He confessed. That was the thing her mind kept snagging on, returning to, refusing to process: *he confessed*. The document was dated the eleventh of March, in a year Beatrix knew from the flood memorials, and it was addressed—to the company? To the inquiry that never happened? To whoever came after?—and it laid the whole coastal railway bare in a chief engineer's careful, exhausted hand. The surveys had been falsified. Not mistaken—*falsified*, altered in his own office, on his own tracing table, at the direction of the directors, because the true line would have cost another season and another fortune and the money men in London had a date and a dividend and no patience for the sea. He had signed the false sections himself. He had watched them build the sea wall to a profile he knew was a lie, cutting through ground his own borings showed was unstable, and he had put his name to the certificates because refusal meant dismissal and dismissal meant silence, and he had told himself he would fix it after, after the opening, after the season—

The flood had come in November, before any after. Eleven dead. Three of them named Zhou, though not his wife and not his son—cousins, laborers, people whose names had gone into the ground with the town's shame and stayed there. Elias Zhou had spent the rest of his life, this document said, in the attempt to make the record true, and had failed, because the company that employed him owned the record. So he had done the only thing left to an engineer whose words could be burned: he had built a room the company couldn't find, under a collapse the company wouldn't pay to dig, and he had put the truth in it, on rails, where it would wait.

*Let it be found by someone with the standing to speak,* the last line read, *for I have none left.*

Beatrix became aware, gradually, of two things. One was that she was crying, silently, the tears cold on her face in the cave-cold air, and that she could not remember the last time she had cried, possibly ever, possibly not since her grandmother's funeral, which she had attended for eleven minutes before catching the afternoon train and putting three hundred miles between herself and the coast.

The other was Ilse, standing close, lamp dimmed, saying nothing, holding out a hand—not taking the letter, not reaching for it, just open, just there, the way you hold out a hand to someone standing on a ledge.

Beatrix did not take it. But she didn't step back either, and after a while Ilse folded her fingers closed again, patient as the tide, and turned her light to the blueprint rack, and let the silence be a shared thing instead of an empty one.

* * *

They worked until their watch alarms went. Ilse photographed and registered; Beatrix opened bundles with a conservator's caution she'd learned from a woman in Inverness a decade ago and had never expected to use. Pay ledgers, week by week, going back to the first sod—the wages of men whose descendants still drank in the harbor pub, some of them, Tom Ferris among them, though Tom's grandfather had been a platelayer, not a Zhou. Correspondence between the site office and London, including three letters in which the directors used the phrase *acceptable losses* in writing, in ink, about human beings. And drawings—God, the drawings. The spur line Beatrix had walked her first day in Greyhaven, the crumbling branch everyone believed had served a quarry, running in these plans all the way down through the headland to this chamber, to a tidal siding where boats could be loaded at high water from a station under the cliff. The quarry was real. It was also a cover story. The Greyhaven Head Railway had been a smuggling operation, a freight line, and finally a coffin—for eleven men, and for the truth.

Every bundle she opened made the weight worse, and she kept opening them. That was the inheritance, she understood now, sitting on the platform edge with her boots hanging over the drainage channel her great-grandfather had cut so this room would stay dry: not the shame, and not the exoneration either, whatever shape that might eventually take. The inheritance was the work. Elias Zhou had spent his last years doing exactly what Beatrix had spent her whole adult life doing—mapping lines that officially didn't exist, keeping records the powerful wanted lost—except he had done it knowing it would probably kill him, and she had done it running away.

She had told herself for twenty years that she surveyed dead railways because the work suited her: quiet, marginal, unwatched. Standing in his station, she could no longer pretend she hadn't known, on some level below language, that she was walking her family's lines the whole time. Every contract she'd taken west and north had bent, eventually, toward this coast, the way water bends toward the sea. She had avoided Greyhaven Head for six years. The tide had simply waited for her.

"Beatrix." Ilse's voice, low. She was crouched at the far end of the car, lamp angled into the last tray, and something in her tone pulled Beatrix upright. "There's a second document under the confession. Sealed. Different paper."

Beatrix crossed the platform. In the bottom tray, beneath where the confession had lain, lay a smaller envelope, wax-sealed, intact, addressed in a different hand—a clerk's neat italic—in care of a name Beatrix recognized from the ledgers: the company secretary. And beneath the direction, a line that made the cold of the cave move into her bones:

*To be delivered upon the death of E. Zhou. Undelivered.*

"He tried again," she whispered. "At the end. He tried to get it out through channels."

"And it was buried," Ilse said quietly. "With him, effectively."

Beatrix looked down at the two envelopes—one open, one sealed—and felt the full arithmetic of the room settle onto her shoulders: letters, blueprints, ledgers, a railcar, a station, a truth worth more than the salvage rights Cray had read aloud over breakfast, all of it hers by blood and none of it hers by right, all of it scheduled to become Halloway Salvage property the moment it crossed the cave mouth into daylight.

Above them, faint through a hundred feet of rock, the sea changed its breathing. The tide had turned.

"We need to go," Beatrix said. She took the sealed letter, and after a moment's war with herself she took the confession too, folding both inside her jacket, against her ribs, where her heartbeat could reach them. "Everything else stays registered and photographed. We tell Cray tonight."

Ilse looked at her for a long moment in the lamplight. "Tell him everything?"

Beatrix thought of the fine print, of the phrase *recoverable artifacts of commercial value*, of the black water waiting beyond the dressed stones, and heard herself lie the way her great-grandfather must once have lied, badly, to buy a little time:

"Everything that matters."

They went out through the gap belly-down, into the dark, as the sea began to come for the door.
