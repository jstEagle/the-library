## 9. Tom Ferris Remembers

The tide table on my wall has hung there forty-one years, and in all that time it has never once lied to me the way the sea does. The sea lies with its fog and its flat calm and its sudden generosity — a cave mouth opened like a purse, a stretch of black rock given back after sixty years of keeping. But paper is honest. Paper said low water at 6:14, and paper said the swell would build from the southwest by Thursday, and I have learned to trust the things that stay put.

I stayed put too long myself, some say. Fifty years at Greyhaven station, first as a lad sweeping platforms for my father, then as relief man, then as the man himself when the branch was still alive and the trains still came up from Junction with coal and post and summer visitors who paid their shilling to look at the cave from the cliff path and never once asked to go inside. Now the trains are gone and the platform weeds have gone to seed and I keep the station because nobody has told me to stop, and because a station without a keeper is just a ruin with a clock in it.

I knew her name before she stepped off the morning bus. Zhou. It came up the hill ahead of her the way names do in a small town — Mrs. Tregowan at the shop had it off the contract letter, and by the time Beatrix Zhou walked past the war memorial with her survey case and her boots that had walked a hundred dead lines, half of Greyhaven had already said it out loud to see how it tasted.

Zhou. Like saying *rain* in this town. Like saying *the flood*.

I watched her from the ticket window, which is where I watch everything now, and I did what I had decided to do, which was nothing. Not yet. A thing like that wants doing right or not at all.

* * *

She came back to the station on the third evening, when the light was going copper over the headland and the gulls were arguing about something out past the breakwater. I heard her boots on the platform boards before I saw her — a good stride, unhurried, the stride of someone who measures ground for a living. She stopped at the old departures board, the wooden one with the sliding letters, half of them missing so that it read nonsense now: G EY AVEN — T IN S NOT RU .

"Does anyone ever fix it?" she asked, without turning around. She'd known I was in the window. Surveyor's eyes. You don't spend your life reading ground and miss a man standing behind glass.

"No point," I said, coming out. "It would only be true twice a year, when the heritage lot run their little engine down to the quarry gate and back." I wiped my hands on my rag, though they were clean. Old habit. "You'll be wanting the key to the waiting room for your instruments."

"I can work off the platform."

"You can," I agreed. "But the wind comes round the headland after dark and takes papers straight into the harbor. Waiting room's warmer." I got the key off its nail — third hook, where it's hung since 1979 — and held it out, and she took it, and our fingers didn't touch because I made sure of it. Some transactions you keep clean.

She looked at the board again. "T RAINS NOT RUNNING," she read, filling in the gaps herself. "That's a strange kind of honesty."

"It's the only kind I've got left." I said it lightly but she heard the weight in it anyway; she had that kind of ear. "There's tea in the office if you want it. Kettle's honest too."

* * *

She wanted the tea. They always do, eventually, in a town where the wind has teeth eight months of the year. She sat in my office among the framed photographs of engines long scrapped and the tide tables going back to before the war, and she wrapped both hands around the mug like a woman warming herself at something more than heat, and I let the silence sit between us while the kettle ticked itself cool.

It was she who broke it, which surprised me. Most young ones wait to be told.

"The spur," she said. "The one under the headland. It's not on any survey I've ever seen. Not the regional archives, not the company records, not the 1952 valuation maps. A line doesn't just stop existing."

"This one did," I said.

"How?"

I turned my mug a quarter turn on the desk. Outside, the light was failing and the fog was coming in off the water the way it does here, soft as a hand laid over the face.

"There's a wall in the old goods shed," I said. "Painted names. Have you been in the goods shed?"

"No."

"Then you haven't seen the list." I stood up, joints announcing themselves the way they do. "Come on, then. Better shown than told. Everything in this town is better shown than told — we learned that from the sea."

* * *

The goods shed stands at the far end of the platform, doors swollen shut since the last freight went through, but I know the trick of the left-hand leaf — lift and push, lift and push — and it gave with its usual groan, like an old man getting out of a chair. Inside it smelled of creosote and salt and mouse, and the last of the daylight came through the loading hatch in a single slanted bar full of dust.

Beatrix found the light switch before I could tell her where it was. Of course she did. The bulb buzzed, hesitated, and gave us its weak yellow.

The wall is the whole north end of the shed, plastered over sometime in the fifties by men who understood what they were doing and why. Names in black paint, column after column, each one tall and careful, the kind of lettering you get when someone writes slowly because the writing matters more than the speed. Above them, also in black, in the same hand:

THE SEA TOOK THEM THE NIGHT OF THE 14TH OF OCTOBER, 1928
THE LINE FAILED AND THE SEA CAME IN
REMEMBER THEM WHO BUILT IT AND DIED OF IT

Below, smaller, added later by a different hand — mine, if you must know, thirty years ago with a tin of enamel and a steady arm:

AND REMEMBER THE TRUTH, WHICH WAS ALSO LOST THAT NIGHT

Beatrix stood in front of it for a long time. Long enough that the dust settled in the light bar and the gulls outside went quiet and I could hear her breathing change — that small catch, that hitch in the rhythm, that a man learns to hear at funerals.

She read the names the way she'd read a map, top to bottom, left to right, thorough. And then she stopped. I watched her stop. Her eyes went to the second column, three-quarters down, and stayed there.

Three names. Three Zhous. Painted in 1928 by a grieving town in letters six inches high.

*LI WEN ZHOU, PLATELAYER.*
*MEI ZHOU, HIS WIFE, COOK AT THE WORKS CAMP.*
*ELIAS ZHOU, CHIEF ENGINEER.*

Her grandmother's father. Her grandmother's mother. Her great-grandfather. All of them on the wall, all of them in the flood, and none of them — I would have bet the station on it — none of them ever mentioned at whatever inland table her grandmother had carried them to.

"Tom," she said, and her voice had gone somewhere flat and careful, the voice people use when the floor has moved under them and they're testing whether it will hold. "My grandmother's name was Ruth Zhou. She never—" She stopped. Started again. "She told me she came from a railway family. That's all. *A railway family.* Four words in eighty years."

"Families are like lines," I said. "Some of them get closed."

I got the folding chair from behind the door and set it down for her without asking, and she sat in it slowly, still looking at the wall, and I dragged a crate over for myself and sat too, two old fixtures of the shed facing the names together.

* * *

The fog had come all the way up from the water by then. I could hear it working at the eaves, that soft wet pressure, and the lamp in the hatch-bar had gone from gold to grey. Neither of us moved to leave.

"You want to know why she left," I said. It wasn't a question. I'd been carrying the story fifty years, waiting for someone to come collect it, and here at last was the heir.

"She left before I was born," Beatrix said. "She died when I was nine. I have her letters — a box of them — and half of them are about railway gauges and drainage gradients, and the other half might as well be written in code. There's a page where she starts a sentence, *Father always said the surveys were*—and then it's crossed out so hard the pen went through the paper."

"Elias Zhou," I said, nodding at the wall. "Chief engineer of the coastal extension. The finest engineer this coast ever produced, and I'll say that knowing every name on this wall, because most of them would have said the same. He built the viaduct at Carrick Bend that's still standing. He built the sea defenses below the works camp. And in the spring of 1928 he handed in a survey report on the new cutting above Greyhaven Head that said, in his own words — my grandfather heard him say it at the pub, and my grandfather wasn't a man who invented things — *this ground will not hold. Do not cut it. Reroute inland or lose the line.*"

Beatrix had gone very still.

"The company didn't want to hear it," I went on. "Rerouting meant another season, another loan, another set of shareholders to soothe. So the survey was re-done. By a man whose name isn't on this wall, which tells you everything about how this town keeps score. The new survey said the ground was sound. Elias Zhou was told to sign off on it or resign. He refused to sign. He was removed from the works — quietly, no scandal, engineers are expensive to scandalize — and the cutting went ahead on the false figures, and on the night of October the fourteenth the rain came down the headland and the cutting failed and the embankment went with it, and the sea came into the low town behind it, and the works camp was sleeping in the cut's shadow because the company had moved the camp to save walking time."

I let that sit. The fog pressed at the roof.

"Twenty-six dead," I said. "Every one of them on this wall. And the company's inquiry found — you'll admire this, being in the trade — it found *unforeseeable geological conditions.* Act of God. Nobody's fault. The chief engineer who warned them had already been pushed out, so his warning never entered the record, and the man who falsified the survey got a commendation and a transfer, and the line ran for another twenty years on ground that had already killed for it."

"And my grandmother?"

"Ruth was fourteen when it happened. Lost her mother and her father in one night, and worse than that, lost the truth of them — because the town was angry, and grief makes bad arithmetic, and for a while there were people saying the Zhous had built the line badly, that the flood was *their* failure, that the chief engineer's own works had drowned the camp. Lies. Vicious ones. But a fourteen-year-old girl in a town that's turning on her family's name doesn't wait around to watch the truth arrive late. She packed what she could carry and went inland on the first train, and the town let her go, and I've often thought that was the cruelest part — that we let her go. That nobody ran after that train."

I looked at the wall. At LI WEN ZHOU, PLATELAYER, who had laid track true as scripture for thirty years. At MEI ZHOU, who fed half the camp out of one cookhouse and knew every man's birthday. At ELIAS ZHOU, CHIEF ENGINEER, whose last act in this world had been to be right, and to be punished for it, and to die in the flood his warning should have prevented.

"We painted the names in '29," I said. "Whole town turned out. And then, do you know what we did? Nothing. For fifty years, nothing. We kept the wall and we kept our mouths shut, because the company still owned the line and half the town's wages, and because shame is easier than a fight. Your grandmother carried the shame inland. We kept our copy here. Between us we buried the truth so deep that even the sea forgot where we'd put it."

Beatrix hadn't spoken in minutes. In the yellow bulb-light her face had the quality I'd seen on sailors' wives at the telegraph office, waiting on news, braced for either version of it.

"Tom," she said finally, and her voice was rough. "Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because you flinched," I said.

She looked at me sharply.

"At the cave," I said. "Word gets round. Ilse Marchetti says you went white as the chalk when you saw a name chalked inside the entrance. And because you're a Zhou standing in a room with three Zhous on a wall, and you didn't know they'd be here, and you knew exactly who they were the moment you saw them. I've watched faces in this town for fifty years, Miss Zhou. I know the difference between surprise and recognition."

The fog moaned softly against the shed roof. Somewhere out on the headland, I knew, the cave was open in the dark, holding whatever it held, and tomorrow Halloway's people would be down at it with their contracts and their drones, and this quiet room with its painted names would seem very far away indeed.

"Your grandmother ran from this place carrying a lie that wasn't hers," I said. "And you've been running ever since — don't argue, girl, I've seen your file come through with the contract, eleven surveys in nine years, no address held longer than a lease — you've been running the way she ran, fast and quiet and calling it work. And now the sea has opened the one door in this county that leads back to all of it, and the company that broke your family's name has sent salvage crews through it, and you're standing on their payroll with a Zhou's blood in you and a Zhou's silence on your tongue."

I leaned forward, crate creaking, and pointed one knotted finger at the smallest line of paint on the wall, the one I'd added myself thirty years ago with a steady arm and an angry heart.

*I wrote that for this exact evening,* I said. *"Remember the truth, which was also lost that night." I didn't know who'd come to find it. I only knew somebody would, someday, because truth is like the cave — it goes under, but it doesn't drown. It waits for the tide that gives it back.*

Beatrix stood up. She walked to the wall, close enough to touch it, and she raised her hand and laid her fingers — gently, the way you'd touch a sleeper you didn't want to wake — on the name ELIAS ZHOU, CHIEF ENGINEER.

"He tried to stop it," she said. It wasn't a question either, but I answered it anyway.

"He tried to stop it," I said. "And this town owes his blood an apology fifty years overdue, and I'm the last man here old enough to make it. So consider it made."

She stood there a long moment, hand on the wall, and I saw something go out of her shoulders that had been sitting in them since the day she stepped off the bus — not all of it, mind, but a piece, the way a knot loosens before it comes free.

Then she turned around, and her eyes were wet and level, and she said the last thing I expected and the only thing, looking back, that a Zhou could have said.

"Tom. Tell me about the false survey. Every detail. Who re-did it, who signed it, where the original report went." Her jaw set, and I saw her great-grandfather in it, plain as paint. "Because if that report still exists anywhere — if there's a copy in the company archive, or a draft, or a letter — then the truth my grandmother ran from isn't buried. It's *filed.*"

And I felt the old stationmaster's blood move in me, quick as the day the last train pulled out, because I knew — I had known since the fog came in and the cave stood open and the Halloway men arrived with their clipboards — I knew that somewhere under the headland, sealed behind sixty years of engineered collapse, the sea had been keeping a railcar full of paper.

And I said, "Miss Zhou. Before you go hunting archives — there's something you need to hear about what your great-grandfather did with his final survey. And why the old families charge admission to a cave they'd never enter."

Outside, the fog swallowed the headland whole, and the tide began to turn.
