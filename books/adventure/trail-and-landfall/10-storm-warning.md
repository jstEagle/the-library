## 10. Storm Warning

The barometer in the harbor office had begun to fall on a Tuesday, so gently that only Tom Ferris noticed, tapping the glass with one yellowed fingernail the way a doctor taps a chest. By Wednesday the gulls had gone strange—flying inland in ragged, silent skeins, abandoning the water they'd fought over all summer. By Thursday morning the fog that had wrapped Greyhaven Head for a week tore open like a seam, and Beatrix Zhou stood on the cliff path and saw the storm for the first time.

It was not yet a storm. It was a bruise on the horizon, a wall of cloud stacked violet and iron from the sea to the sky, sitting perhaps forty miles offshore and not moving. That was the wrong of it. Storms were supposed to arrive, spend themselves, and go. This one had stopped, the way a fist stops an inch from the door—worse, somehow, than the blow itself, because now everyone in the town had time to watch it and imagine.

She could smell it from the cliff. Petrol and kelp and something older underneath, the mineral breath of deep water that had been stirred for the first time in years. The tide was out so far that the cave mouth below her stood open like a held note, black rock glistening, the survey stakes she and Ilse had planted along the sill catching what little light there was.

"Two weeks," Tom Ferris said behind her. She hadn't heard him climb the path; the man moved like weather himself, everywhere and nowhere until he was on you. He came to stand at the rail, hands folded on his stick. "Maybe less. My grandfather called them sitting storms. They sit on you like a cat on a mouse, and when they finally move, there's nothing left to bury."

"The charts say the spring tides peak in eleven days," Beatrix said. "After that, the cave seals. Possibly for good. Possibly taking half the cliff with it, if the pressure does what I think it'll do to those old workings."

Tom said nothing for a while. The wind came up the cliff face in long slow breaths, warm and wrong, carrying the smell of the storm to them like a letter from someone you'd rather not hear from.

"Your grandmother stood on this rail once," he said at last. "Watching a storm come. Different storm. Same look on your face, though. Like you're doing arithmetic you don't want the answer to."

Beatrix didn't answer. The letter in her breast pocket seemed to warm against her ribs, the way a coal does when the wind finds it. She had taken to carrying it on her body now, day and night, folded into a waterproof sleeve she'd stolen from the dive kit. It was the most honest thing she owned and the most criminal, and she could not decide, hour to hour, which of those facts frightened her more.

* * *

Halloway Salvage had a way of arriving that Beatrix was beginning to recognize as a signature. They never came when you expected them; they came when you were tired. Cray's launch put into the harbor at dusk on Thursday, riding low with new equipment, and by the time the light went out of the sky the harbor pub's back room had been requisitioned, its tables covered in charts, its fire stoked by someone who wasn't paying for the coal.

"Sit down, all of you." Cray stood at the head of the table with his reading glasses on, and Beatrix noticed for the first time how the lamplight made him look almost kind, the way a well-drawn map makes a cliff look climbable. "I'll keep this short, because the weather won't."

He laid out the forecast the way he laid out everything: in clauses. A stationary depression, stalled over the shelf. A spring tide series beginning in ten days, the highest in six years. A storm surge model that put water in Greyhaven's lower streets and put the headland's seaward face under forces the Victorian engineers—whoever they truly were—had never planned for.

"The cave closes," Cray said. "And it may not close cleanly. Our geotech consultant puts a thirty percent chance of a cliff collapse that takes the seawall with it. That's the town's problem, and the council's problem, and I'm told they're aware." He let that sit, cold as a coin on the tongue. "Our problem is the asset. The railcar and its contents are recoverable, catalogued, and insured. But recoverable means *out*. Everything comes off the headland and onto a Halloway barge before the first surge tide. That gives us nine working days. Nine."

He looked around the table. The divers nodded; they were paid to nod. The drone operator was already recalculating flight windows against the wind forecasts. Ilse sat with her hands flat on the table, her maps squared in front of her like a shield, and did not nod at all.

"Nine days," Beatrix said, and heard her own voice come out level, professional, the voice of a woman who had no letter in her pocket. "The inner chamber is behind the collapse. We can widen the breach, but the railcar's on a cradle of old timber that's been underwater for a century. Move it wrong and we lose the car *and* the passage."

"Then we don't move it wrong." Cray took off his glasses and folded them, and the kindness went out of his face with them. "Miss Zhou. You surveyed the spur. You know the workings better than anyone on this coast. Halloway is paying you, at a rate I'd have thought would buy your full attention, to get that asset out intact. I trust that attention is available."

It was not a question. Beatrix held his eyes and thought about the letter against her ribs, about the second document she had found folded inside the confession two nights ago in her rented room, by lamplight, with her heart going like a shunting engine. *He tried to stop it. They buried him for it.* The words were still there, in the dark of her, waiting.

"Available," she said.

* * *

That night she did not sleep. She sat in the window of her room above the chandler's with the lamp turned low and the letter open on her knees, reading it again as though repetition might make it lighter.

Her great-grandfather's handwriting changed halfway down the first page. The opening was the careful copperplate of a man writing for a record—the falsified surveys, the flood, the names. Then, folded inside, a second sheet on different paper, a different hand or the same hand gone ragged: an order countermanded, a warning filed and refused, a letter to the company board that came back unopened. *I built the line true and they made me build the lie on top of it. If this ever surfaces, let it surface whole.*

*Let it surface whole.* She had read the sentence forty times and it had not yet decided what it wanted from her.

Below the window, the harbor lay flat and black under the bruised sky, and the masts of the fishing fleet stood up out of it like a field of dead trees. Somewhere out there, forty miles, the storm sat and waited, and Beatrix understood with a clarity that felt like cold water that the storm was not the thing compressing her time. Cray was. Nine days, and every one of them now a door closing. The letter in her pocket was a lit fuse in a room full of men with ledgers, and the only question was which end of it she was holding.

She thought about giving it back. Slipping it into the catalogue crate with the others, erasing herself from the story the way she'd erased herself from every story for twenty years—no surname spoken, no roots claimed, no line on any map that led back to her. It would be so easy. It was what she was built for.

She folded the letter along its old creases and put it back against her heart.

* * *

Friday the wind arrived ahead of the storm, a long offshore blow that combed the sea flat and pushed the tide out past every low-water mark in the charts. The cave stood open all day, an impossibility, its throat dry at the sill for the first time in anyone's memory. Halloway worked it like a wound. The divers went in at slack water with lifting bags and slings; the drones threaded the passages with LiDAR, building a ghost-model of the chamber that glowed on Cray's laptop screen, blue and perfect and utterly without soul.

Beatrix spent the day on the cliff with her theodolite, running levels on the headland because it was her job and because standing still was the only way she knew to think. The cliff was talking. She could hear it if she listened—a faint internal grumbling, deep down, like a house settling, like bones. The old workings under the headland were taking the sea's changed breathing personally. Every tide that went out farther than the charts allowed came back harder, and the water found the old tunnels the way water finds everything, patiently, and pressed.

Ilse came up the path at midday with two thermoses and the particular walk she used when she'd decided not to say something. She set the thermoses down on the survey case and stood looking at the sea.

"You felt it too," she said. "The cliff."

"Since Thursday. It's the pressure differential. When the tide drops this far, the water table in the workings drops with it, and the old timber props dry and shrink. Then the surge comes back and—" She made a small gesture with her hand, a collapse in miniature.

"Tom says the seawall's never been tested by a surge this high. He says the last time the tide stood this far out was the flood year." Ilse said it carefully, watching Beatrix's face the way she watched contour lines, for the places where the ground fell away. "The year your family—"

"The year the surveys lied," Beatrix said.

Ilse was quiet for a moment. The wind pulled at her hair, at the corner of her map case, at everything that wasn't tied down, and Beatrix felt like one of those things.

"You've been carrying something since the chamber," Ilse said. "I map people as well as coastlines, Beatrix. It's a bad habit. You've had a weight in your pocket for two weeks and a weight on your face for longer, and they're the same shape."

Beatrix looked out at the storm wall and said nothing, and the nothing was so loud that Ilse finally picked up her thermos and went back down the path, and Beatrix stood alone on the headland with her instruments and her secret and the arithmetic she couldn't finish.

* * *

Saturday, the extraction began in earnest, and Beatrix learned what complicity looked like when it put on work clothes.

It looked like a barge at the harbor wall, and crates stenciled Halloway, and the divers' lifting bags rising out of the cave mouth in the gray dawn like the lungs of something surfacing. It looked like Cray's clipboard. It looked like the first crate coming up the cliff on the winch line, and Beatrix signing the manifest against her own better judgment because the manifest was her job and the job was the only shape her life had ever held.

The letters came up first—three crates of them, oilcloth-wrapped, tied in bundles with the string still knotted by hands a century dead. Beatrix watched them go into the crates and thought of the one against her ribs and felt the difference between them like a fever. Every bundle that left the cave was a voice going into a warehouse in some city she'd never see. Every bundle was a name that would be catalogued, valued, and silenced, exactly as it had been silenced before, by exactly the same kind of men.

"Manifest," Cray said, holding out the clipboard, and Beatrix signed, and her signature looked, to her own eye, like someone else's.

That evening the town met in the harbor pub, though no one had called a meeting. They simply arrived, as towns do when the sea changes its mind, and stood around the fire arguing in the low urgent voices of people who have done this arithmetic before. The seawall. The boats. Whether to haul the fleet out early and lose a week's fishing, or gamble. And underneath it, quieter, the other question: the cave, the crates, the strangers with the winch line, taking the headland apart piece by piece before the sea closed the book on all of them.

Tom Ferris found Beatrix at the end of the bar.

"Eleven days," he said. "You'll forgive an old man for counting."

"Ten and a half."

"And the letter you're carrying. How many days has that got?"

Beatrix's hand went to her breast pocket before she could stop it, and Tom watched the gesture with the mild satisfaction of a man who has been stationmaster long enough to read every timetable, including the ones people keep in their chests.

"I don't know what you—"

"Your grandmother had the same habit," Tom said. "Carrying the truth where it shows. It's why she left, in the end. The truth got too heavy for this town and too heavy for her, and she set it down inland and never picked it up again." He turned his glass on the bar, slow circles. "Storm's coming, Miss Zhou. It'll close the cave, and it may take the wall, and Cray's lot will be gone with everything that floats by then. After that, there's no cave, no railcar, and no proof of anything but what's in a Halloway warehouse. Whatever you're carrying—" he tapped the bar twice, gently, "—it doesn't keep. Nothing does. That's the one thing the sea teaches, if you stand on this coast long enough."

He finished his drink and left, and Beatrix sat in the firelight with the storm pressing against the windows and the letter burning its small steady hole against her heart, and did the arithmetic she had been avoiding since the chamber.

Nine days of work. One letter that could undo a century of silence—or end the only life she knew how to live. A town that remembered her name. A woman on the cliff path who had already read her face like a contour map. And out past the harbor wall, forty miles of violet and iron, the storm sat waiting to close every door at once.

She thought: *I could still put it back. I could still be invisible.*

Then she thought of her great-grandfather's ragged last line—*let it surface whole*—and understood that the choice had already been made, weeks ago, in a cave, by a hand that flinched at its own name.

The question left was only this: who she would be when the truth came out, and how much of the town, and of Ilse, and of herself, the storm would leave standing when it did.

Outside, the first spit of rain came against the glass like something thrown.
