## 11. Ilse Asks the Wrong Question

The storm had not yet arrived, but you could smell it coming—salt and iron on the wind, a greenish bruise along the horizon where the sea met a sky the color of old pewter. Ilse Marchetti had learned to read weather the way other people read faces: her father had been an alpine guide, and before he taught her to draw a contour line he taught her that mountains, like coasts, tell you what they intend to do if you pay attention. The headland was paying attention. The gulls had gone inland. The thrift on the clifftop lay flattened in one direction, all of it combed toward the town as though the land itself were leaning away from what was coming.

She climbed the cliff path with her field case under one arm and her hands in her pockets, because the wind up here had teeth. Below her, Greyhaven sat in its shallow bowl of harbor like something the sea had coughed up and hadn't decided whether to swallow: the pub with its crooked sign, the fish sheds, the station with its single platform and its lamp already lit against the early dusk. And below that, at the base of the black rock, the cave mouth stood open—the way it had stood open for eleven days now, breathing fog, waiting.

Ilse had been drawing it every morning. That was her job, after all; Halloway Salvage paid her to make maps, and she made good ones, the kind with the hachures fine as eyelashes and the soundings written in a hand so even it looked printed. But this morning she had not gone down to the tideline. This morning she had walked up instead, because there was a question lodged in her chest like a splinter, and she had learned—again from her father—that splinters work themselves deeper if you leave them.

Beatrix Zhou was ahead of her on the path.

Ilse had seen her leave the station house twenty minutes ago, alone, no instrument case, no notebook, which for Beatrix was itself a kind of confession. Beatrix went nowhere without instruments. She measured everything—doorways, tide tables, the angle of light off water—as if the world were only real once it had been recorded. To walk out empty-handed meant she wasn't working. It meant she was thinking. And Ilse had watched her think for two weeks now, close enough to know the difference between the engineer's stillness and whatever this was: the set of her shoulders as she climbed, hands jammed in her coat, jaw tight, a woman walking toward something or away from it, possibly both.

The path switchbacked twice above the old quarry, then ran level along the shoulder of the headland, a shelf of turf and broken stone with the drop falling away sheer on the left. Ilse caught up with her where the path narrowed between two gorse bushes, their flowers gone over, prickling yellow-green against the grey.

"You walk fast," Ilse said.

"I walk away," Beatrix said, and then seemed to hear herself, and almost smiled. Almost. "Force of habit."

"From what?"

"Everything."

They walked. The wind came off the sea in long shoves, pushing at their backs when they turned east, dragging at them when they turned. Below, the swell was building—long, glassy rollers marching in from the deep water, each one taller than the last, breaking white against the cave's threshold with a sound like slow applause. In another week, maybe less, the storm would shut that mouth for another generation. Everyone knew it. Cray had said it aloud at breakfast that morning, reading from his weather service printout in the flat voice he used for contracts and forecasts alike, as if the sea were merely another clause to be negotiated.

Ilse let forty yards go by. Then fifty. Then she stopped walking.

Beatrix took three more steps before she noticed, and stopped too, and did not turn around. She stood looking out at the water with her back to Ilse, and the wind pulled her dark hair loose from its knot and whipped it across her face, and she didn't bother to fix it, and that more than anything told Ilse how far inside herself she'd gone.

"I've been redrawing the entrance chamber," Ilse said. "Last night. Cross-referencing my survey with the drone data."

"Find something?"

"No. That's the point. Everything matches. My hand-drawn sections, the LiDAR, the photogrammetry—it all agrees down to the centimeter. Which is why I know the anomaly isn't in the data." She paused. "It's in you."

Beatrix's shoulders moved. It might have been a breath. It might have been a laugh with nowhere to go.

"There was a moment," Ilse went on, "on the first descent. Day four, the passage past the second sump, where the tunnel bends north. You stopped. You put your hand on the wall—here—" she touched her own palm to the air at hip height, the gesture precise because she had replayed it a dozen times in her memory, frame by frame, the way she replayed everything she couldn't explain, "—and you went still. Not surveyor-still. Not checking-your-compass still. I've worked with thirty engineers, Beatrix, and I know all their kinds of quiet. This was different. You looked like someone who'd heard their own name called in an empty house."

The waves broke below. A fulmar wheeled past on stiff wings, riding the wind shear off the cliff.

"And then yesterday," Ilse said, "in the entrance chamber, when we found the chalk marks under the algae. Initials. Dates. You saw them before I did—you always see things first—and your light swung away so fast you nearly took my eye out. And there was a name chalked there, half-legible, and you flinched."

"I didn't flinch."

"You flinched. I map movement for a living. I record displacement. You displaced." Ilse heard her own voice rising and made herself lower it. "Zhou. The initials were Z.Z., cut into the flowstone, and beside them, chalked later, the word *Zhou* in a hand that matches the handwriting on the maintenance ledgers we photographed in the railcar. Your surname is chalked inside a cave your family has never officially touched. There is no Zhou in any document Halloway has filed. No Zhou on the crew manifests. No Zhou in the company histories Cray recites like scripture. And yet the geometry of this tunnel matches a spur line that officially never existed, and the man whose initials are carved into the rock signed a confession we are all pretending not to have read, and his name was Zhou, and *you*—"

"*Don't.*"

It came out cracked, and Beatrix finally turned, and Ilse saw her face properly for the first time in days: pale under the windburn, eyes rimmed red, the look of someone who had not been sleeping so much as lying awake in the dark rehearsing sentences she would never say.

"Don't," Beatrix said again, quieter. "You don't know what you're asking me to do."

"I'm asking you to tell me the truth. That's all. That's the whole request." Ilse spread her hands. "I'm not Cray. I'm not Halloway. I'm the person who's been lying next to you in the signal box listening to you breathe for a week, and I am telling you that whatever it is, it's eating you alive, and I would rather carry it with you than watch you carry it alone."

For a moment—one full wave-cycle, Ilse counted it later, the way she counted everything—she thought Beatrix would break. The engineer's face did something complicated, tectonic, plates shifting beneath the surface; her mouth opened; her hand came halfway up as if to reach.

Then it closed. All of it. The face became a wall, and Ilse, who drew cliffs for a living, recognized the construction instantly: dressed stone, load-bearing, built to survive exactly this kind of weather.

"My family's from here," Beatrix said. "That's all it is. Coming back to a place your people came from does things to you. It doesn't mean anything."

"That's not an answer. That's a press release."

"It's the truth."

"It's a truth-shaped object." Ilse could feel her own pulse in her throat now, anger and hurt braided together so tightly she couldn't tell which was which. "You want to know what I do when a datum doesn't fit? When a sounding comes back wrong, when a benchmark won't close? I don't round it off. I don't file it under 'close enough.' Because a map that lies, even a little, even kindly, kills people. Ships hit rocks. Trains run off rails. So I ask you, as the person whose maps everyone on this expedition is trusting with their lives in that tunnel: is there something wrong with the map? Is there something about this cave, this line, this family history, that changes what we're standing on?"

The wind shoved between them. Gorse scratched at Ilse's sleeve.

"No," Beatrix said.

And it was the smoothness of it that did it. Not hesitation—smoothness. The answer came out oiled, pre-machined, the answer of a woman who had clearly spent days preparing it, and Ilse felt something in her chest go cold and hard and final, the way water goes when the temperature drops past some invisible line and the whole surface decides at once to be ice.

"Okay," Ilse said.

"Okay?"

"Okay." She picked up her field case. "Then I'll keep mapping. Accurately. Everything I find goes in the record, Beatrix. Everything. That's my job, and unlike some people, I intend to keep doing it."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means I liked you better when you were tired and honest than I do now that you're rested and lying." The words came out colder than she'd intended, and part of her—a large part—wanted to take them back the instant they left her mouth. She watched them land. She watched Beatrix receive them without moving, without blinking, the way a seawall receives a wave, and understood that the wall was not just for her. The wall was for everyone. It had probably been under construction for thirty years.

"Ilse." Beatrix's voice dropped, and for a second the crack showed through again, the person behind the stone. "If I could tell you—"

"But you can't." Ilse shouldered the case. "Because of duty. Because of the contract. Because of whatever it is you think you're protecting, or whoever. I understand duty, Beatrix. I grew up with a man who died of it—went back onto the glacier in a whiteout because the clients were insured and the schedule was fixed. Duty is very respectable. Duty gets lovely obituaries." She looked out at the sea, at the cave mouth swallowing wave after wave, and when she spoke again her voice had gone quiet enough that Beatrix had to lean in to hear it, and hated herself for making her lean in. "But I've seen what happens to people who let duty do their feeling for them. They end up accurate and alone. Perfectly surveyed. Nobody home."

She turned and walked back along the path, and Beatrix did not follow, and Ilse did not look back, because she knew—if she looked back, she would see the wall cracking, and she would go to it, and she would forgive everything, and nothing would change. Her father had taught her that too, in the end, in the worst possible lesson: that love makes you stupid about danger. That the people you most want to reach are sometimes the people standing on the cornice, and going out after them is how both of you go down.

So she kept her eyes forward, on the path, on the turf and the stone, measuring her own footsteps the way she measured everything, and behind her the sea kept up its slow applause, and ahead of her the lights of Greyhaven were coming on one by one in the failing afternoon, small and warm and impossibly far away.

* * *

She did not go straight back to the station house. She detoured along the harbor road instead, past the fish sheds, to the sea wall, and stood there a while doing nothing, which was unlike her, holding her cold hands under her arms and watching the water climb the stones.

Tom Ferris found her there eventually. He had a way of appearing the way lighthouse beams appear—not arriving so much as sweeping into view. He leaned on the wall beside her, set down the lantern he carried everywhere now that the evenings came early, and said nothing for a long time. That was the thing about Tom. He treated silence as a shared resource rather than an accusation.

"She's on the headland," Ilse said finally. "Your engineer."

"Aye."

"You knew her grandmother, didn't you. You knew the name meant something before she ever got off the train."

Tom looked at the water. The swell was up; spray drifted over the wall and salted their lips. "Knew of her," he said. "Mara Zhou. Left the year I started on the station. Nineteen sixty-one." He was quiet a moment. "There's folk in that pub tonight who'll tell you the Zhoos were thieves and liars. There's folk who'll tell you they were saints the town wasn't good enough for. Both lots are guessing. Only the dead know the whole of it, and the dead keep their records in strange places."

"The cave," Ilse said.

"The cave," Tom agreed, unsurprised. He picked up his lantern. "Tide gives back what it's owed, girl. Always has. Question's never whether the truth comes ashore." He turned to go, then paused, and the lamplight made his face briefly young and briefly ancient in the same instant. "Question's what you do when it washes up at your feet, and whose hands you hold while it does."

He went on up the harbor road, a small stooped shape against the darkening sky, and Ilse stood at the wall until the cold drove her in.

Back at the station house, the lamp in Beatrix's room was burning. Ilse could see it through the gap in the curtains as she crossed the yard—saw the shadow move behind it, stop, move again. Pacing. Or sitting very still. From outside, at this distance, in this light, the two were indistinguishable.

She stood in the yard a long time, letting the wind decide for her, and the wind blew from the sea, and pushed her toward the door, and she resisted it, because she was angry, and anger is a kind of ballast, it keeps you upright, it keeps you from drifting into people who will capsize you.

Inside, she laid her field case on the table and opened it and took out the day's drawings—the entrance chamber, the passage beyond the second sump, the railcar bay with its engineered collapse—and pinned them to the wall in sequence, the whole cave unspooling across the plaster in ink and hachure. Her map. Her honest map, with nothing rounded off, nothing filed under *close enough.*

And then, because she was who she was—because a datum that doesn't fit doesn't get ignored, it gets circled, dated, and returned to—she took out a fresh sheet, clipped it to the board, and began to draw the one section she had not yet committed to paper: the tunnel geometry, overlaid against the ghost alignments of the spur line that officially never existed, the two tracings converging in the dark like hands reaching for each other across a page.

Somewhere behind the thin wall, the shadow in Beatrix's room stopped moving.

And did not start again.

Outside, far out past the headland, the first edge of the storm came grumbling over the horizon like something being dragged, and the lamp in the window guttered, and held.
