## 13. Sabotage, or Safety?

Cray counted things for a living.

Not money—money was the least of what he counted. He counted clauses, contingencies, hours billed against hours promised, and above all he counted people: their tells, their appetites, the exact weight of fear each one carried and where they kept it hidden. It was a habit so old it had stopped feeling like a habit and become simply the way light entered him. Other men saw a room full of colleagues. Cray saw an inventory.

Which was why, on the ninth morning of the Greyhaven expedition, over eggs that tasted of kerosene from the camp stove and toast that tasted of nothing at all, he noticed that the inventory had changed by exactly one item.

The confession file—the one Halloway's people had photographed page by page in the cave, catalogued, sealed in archival plastic, and locked in the steel document case that traveled everywhere strapped to his own wrist like a second pulse—was short a letter.

One letter. Four pages. Dated the fourteenth of March, 1893, signed with a hand that shook toward the end of every line.

He knew because he had read them all twice before breakfast on the first day, the way he read everything, and because last night, unable to sleep in the wind that worried the tarps like a dog at a fence, he had read them a third time. And this morning the folder held eleven letters where yesterday it had held twelve. The blueprints were all there. The pay ledgers were all there. The confession itself, the crown jewel, the thing worth more than the railcar that carried it—all there. Only the letter was gone, slipped out sometime between lamplight and dawn, out of a case that did not leave his side, in a camp of nine people who had all been, by his own accounting, asleep.

He sat very still with his fork halfway to his mouth and let the arithmetic finish itself.

There were only two ways into the case. His key, which never left the chain around his neck. And the twenty minutes on Tuesday evening when he'd left the case in his tent while he walked down to the harbor wall to make a call from the one spot on the headland where the signal didn't die—a call to Halloway's directors, reporting progress, reporting the discovery, reporting that the contents appeared to be, in the language of the claim, *recoverable artifacts of significant commercial value*. Twenty minutes. He had weighed the risk then and judged it acceptable, because he had weighed the people and found them wanting in imagination. Engineers, divers, a cartographer. People who touched things. Not people who understood that paper was the only cargo that mattered.

He had made one error in his weighing. He had forgotten to account for blood.

* * *

He watched her through the morning.

Beatrix Zhou worked the way water worked—finding the low places, filling them without fuss, asking for nothing. She ran her levels along the cliff path with the expedition's total station, calling numbers to the young surveyor in a voice flat as slate, and if she carried any awareness that the storm offshore had grown teeth overnight—if she felt at all the way the whole coast seemed to feel it, gulls riding lower, the sea working itself up into a long gray muscle—she gave no sign. She was, Cray admitted, the best field engineer he had contracted in nineteen years. That was precisely the problem. The best ones always thought they were invisible. They had usually built their lives on it.

He let her work until the light went long and coppery over the water, until the crew drifted back toward the camp and the smell of stew, and then he fell into step beside her on the cliff path, where the wind came off the sea hard enough to make conversation a private thing.

"Miss Zhou."

"Mr. Cray." She didn't stop walking. Her eyes stayed on the line of the old rails ahead, rusted orange against the green, running out toward the headland like a sentence that trailed off.

"I've been doing an inventory," he said.

"Of what?"

"Of everything." He matched her pace. Below them the tide was coming in over the black rocks it had exposed, swallowing the cave mouth inch by inch, and the sound of it came up the cliff like slow applause. "It's what I do. Contracts are just inventories written down. I count what exists, and I notice what doesn't. It's a dull gift, but it's mine."

She said nothing. But he saw it—the fractional tightening at the corner of her jaw, the half-step hitch in her stride that a lesser observer would have missed entirely. She knew. Of course she knew; she had known since dawn, probably since the moment she took it, and she had spent the whole day doing what she did best, which was being nobody, being weather, being part of the landscape. He almost admired it.

"The document case," he said. "Twelve letters when we catalogued them in the chamber. Eleven this morning."

The wind filled the silence for three steps, four. Then she stopped, and turned, and looked at him with those level dark eyes, and he understood suddenly why Tom Ferris and half the town spoke her grandmother's name like a coin rubbed smooth.

"You should check your lock," she said.

"My lock is fine. My lock was fine. Someone waited for my one careless hour in nineteen years and used it." He kept his voice pleasant, almost gentle; that was how he delivered the worst things. "I'm not interested in denial, Miss Zhou. Denial is just interest accruing. I'm interested in the return of one letter, four pages, dated March 1893, and in never speaking of this again. Those are the terms."

"And if I don't accept the terms?"

The question landed between them and lay there like something dropped from a height.

He had prepared for defiance, for tears, for bargaining—he had a speech for each, honed on harder adversaries than a railroad engineer with salt in her hair. What he had not prepared for was how tired she looked saying it. Not frightened. Tired, the way people are tired at the end of a long shift, as though the ultimatum were merely the last load on a day already too heavy.

"Then you're removed from the expedition," he said. "Effective tonight. Your credentials are revoked, your access is ended, and I file a report—accurate, documented, unanswerable—that Beatrix Zhou removed materials from a salvage site under contract and concealed them. There isn't a rail authority in this country that touches an engineer with that on her record. Not the heritage boards, not the freight lines, not the preservation trusts. You've spent—" he consulted the file in his head, because he always had the file, "—fourteen years mapping closed lines. Every one of those contracts came through someone who trusts my judgment. By Friday you won't be able to get a job inspecting a siding in a town with no trains."

He heard it as he said it, how much it sounded like a man describing the inside of his own prison, and he hated her a little for making him hear it.

She looked past him, out at the water. The storm front had come close enough now that its underside showed, a bruised shelf of cloud dragging the horizon down, and the light on the sea had gone the color of old pewter. Somewhere below, muffled by rock, the cave was closing for the night.

"You've read them," she said quietly. "All of them. You told me you read everything twice."

"Three times, actually."

"Then you know what the twelfth letter says." Her voice didn't rise. That was the unnerving thing about her; everything important she said at the volume of a timetable. "You know what he confessed to. Falsified surveys. A flood he warned them about and they buried. Forty-one names, Mr. Cray. Three of them Zhou. And your clients want to put that paper in a glass case in a lobby in London and sell tickets under it."

"My clients want to own it," Cray corrected. "Selling is a later decision. Ownership first. That's not villainy, Miss Zhou, it's just sequence." He pulled his coat tighter as the wind sharpened. "And whether it sits in a lobby or a museum or a drawer changes nothing about the physics of your situation. You took it. I can prove you took it. The only variable left is whether you do it quietly or loudly."

"Is that what you believe?" she asked. "That it changes nothing? Where a truth lives?"

He opened his mouth to give the contractual answer, the correct answer, the answer he had given in a hundred conference rooms with mahogany and water glasses—and found, to his genuine surprise, that he hesitated. Because he had read the letter three times. Because at two in the morning, alone with the lamp and the wind, he had read the chief engineer's shaking hand describe the moment the company's directors had taken his surveys and altered them, line by line, to save six thousand pounds on sea walls, and had described how he had been paid for his silence and how the payment had felt like drowning in dry air. And Cray, who believed in nothing except the binding force of documents, had recognized the purest document he had ever held. Paper that could still hurt the right people, a hundred and thirty years on. Paper with teeth.

It was, professionally speaking, magnificent. It was also, he was beginning to suspect, going to cost him sleep for the rest of his life, whichever side of the glass case it ended up behind.

"What I believe," he said finally, "is irrelevant. I'm not employed to believe. I'm employed to enforce. You have until tomorrow night. Return the letter to the case—I'll be at the harbor wall at eight, making my call, and I will be careless again for exactly twenty minutes. If it's back when I return, none of this happened. If it isn't—" he shrugged, and the gesture was honest, which somehow made it worse, "—then I do my job, and your life as you've built it ends, and you spend the next thirty years learning what the inside of a blacklist looks like. I'm told it looks a great deal like the outside, only quieter."

* * *

He walked back along the spur alone.

The old rails caught the last light and threw it back in long dull ribbons, and the telegraph poles leaned inland like men listening for news that never comes. Cray had walked a hundred dead lines in his career—they sent him to the dying ones, the disputed ones, the ones where ownership was fog—and he had always liked them, in the way he liked most finished things. A closed line was a settled argument. Nothing left to fight over but the scrap value.

Except this one wasn't settled. This one had been arguing under the sea for a century, patient as sediment, and now the tide had pulled back farther than the charts allowed and handed the argument to a woman who turned out to be the argument's own descendant. He was not a superstitious man, but he was a precise one, and precision forced him to admit that the odds against that coincidence were the kind of odds that made a lawyer check the fine print of the universe.

At the bend by the old signal box he passed Ilse Marchetti coming the other way, sketchbook under her arm, hair whipping loose in the wind. She nodded to him—civil, guarded, the way everyone had become guarded since the railcar came up out of the dark—and then her eyes went past him down the path, to where Beatrix Zhou stood motionless against the brightening storm light, small and straight as a survey marker.

Marchetti stopped. Looked at the figure on the path. Looked back at Cray, and something moved across her face—not suspicion exactly; he'd have known suspicion, it was his native weather. Something more like recognition. As if she had just seen the shape of the conversation he'd been having, printed in the space between two people, the way you can read a letter from the outside if the paper is thin enough.

"Mr. Cray," she said, and her voice was perfectly pleasant, and perfectly cold. "Lovely evening for contracts."

He inclined his head and walked on, and did not let himself hurry, because hurrying was a tell, and he had survived nineteen years by having no tells at all.

But behind him he heard Marchetti's boots quicken on the gravel, closing the distance down the path toward the woman who had everything to lose, and Cray did the arithmetic he was made for, the counting that never slept: one letter missing, one cartographer converging, one storm twelve hours out with the power to bury the evidence forever, and one deadline expiring at eight o'clock tomorrow by the harbor wall.

He had offered her safety, and named its price. Standing in the wind on a dead line with the sea rising behind him, Cray understood—with the clarity that always arrived one move too late to be useful—that he had badly miscounted the room.

Some people, faced with the end of everything they'd built, chose survival.

He had just watched the other kind turn to face him.
