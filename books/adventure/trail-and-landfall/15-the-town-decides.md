## 15. The Town Decides

The Anchor had not been full since the herring left, and Tom Ferris could measure the health of Greyhaven by the state of its floorboards. Forty years behind the bar of an evening—first as a boy hauling crates for his father, then as stationmaster by day and unofficial landlord's conscience by night—had taught him that a town spoke most honestly through its feet. Tonight the feet had come home. Every stool taken, every barrel-top occupied, men standing three deep at the windows with their collars still smelling of salt, women on the benches along the wall with their arms folded against the cold that came in each time the door swung open. The fog outside was the thick kind, the kind that ate the harbor light whole, and inside the lamplight sat yellow and close as butter.

Tom kept to his corner by the chimney breast, where he'd kept it since Margaret died, because from there he could see the whole room and be seen by none of it unless he chose. His pint went untouched. He'd learned long ago that a man who wanted to hear truth in a public house did well to keep his hands empty and his mouth shut.

Beatrix Zhou stood at the end of the bar nearest the door, and Tom watched her the way he imagined a man watches weather come over the headland—not because there is anything to be done about it, but because you want to know what you're in for.

She had changed since she'd come off the water. Not in her clothes—she wore the same oilskin jacket, same boots scarred white at the toe—but in the way she held her head. When she'd first walked into his station office six weeks ago, asking after the spur with a voice like someone reading out a shopping list, she had kept her chin down, her eyes on the paperwork, a woman practicing invisibility so long she'd gotten good at it. Zhou. He'd known the name before she said it, known it the way you know a scar in the mirror. He'd said nothing then. There was a time for everything, and some things needed to ripen in the dark like apples in a shed.

Tonight her chin was up. Tonight she looked at people when she spoke to them.

"Thank you for coming," she said, and the room quieted the way rooms do when they've decided to listen rather than merely hush. "I'll be short. I know most of you have boats to check and children to feed and opinions you've already made up." A ripple of laughter, honest enough. "I'm going to tell you what's in that cave. All of it. Then I'm going to tell you what Halloway Salvage intends to do with it, and what they intend to leave this town holding afterward. And then I'm going to sit down, and you can argue among yourselves, because God knows you don't need me for that."

Tom felt the corner of his mouth move. She had the family voice. He'd heard it once, sixty years ago, from a girl standing on this very floorboards' predecessor, arguing the harbor committee down over the seawall stones. Ellen Zhou had a way of making a room feel like it had been waiting for her. Her granddaughter had it too, though she carried it like something heavy rather than something bright.

"The railcar," Beatrix said. "You've all heard the word by now. Let me tell you what it actually holds. Letters—hundreds of them, correspondence between the coastal line's engineers and the company offices inland. Blueprints—the original surveys, including the ones that were falsified. Pay ledgers naming every man who worked the cliff cuttings. And a confession, signed and dated, by the chief engineer of the whole enterprise."

She paused. The lamp above the bar hissed. Somewhere near the window a gull complained and was shushed by its owner.

"The confession says the flood of 1893 was no act of God. It says the surveys were doctored to save the company money—to route the line through ground that should never have been cut—and that the engineer who signed it knew, and was overruled, and was silenced when he tried to stop it. It names names. It names the dead."

Tom closed his eyes briefly. Sixty years of keeping that wall of names polished in the station house, and here was a woman saying aloud what half the town had always whispered and the other half had paid good money to forget: that the sea hadn't killed those eleven men. Men had.

"And Halloway," said Denny Callow, from his barrel by the window, "pays us how much?"

There it was. Tom had counted the room before Beatrix ever opened her mouth, and he'd put Denny's faction at just under half—maybe eighteen souls, the young ones mostly, the ones with mortgages on boats that leaked and nets that cost more than the fish brought in. Denny wasn't a villain. That was the trouble with money arguments; they were almost always made by decent people who were tired.

"Halloway pays nothing," Beatrix said. "That's the part nobody's read closely. Read Cray's claim papers—I have copies, take them, read them tonight. The salvage claim covers recoverable artifacts of commercial value. Everything comes out of that cave goes to Halloway's backers. The town gets a percentage of *proceeds*, which means a percentage of whatever they can sell, minus their costs, minus their legal fees, assessed by their accountants. In forty years of operation, Halloway has never once paid a host community more than four percent of gross. I've checked. Ilse checked. We checked twice."

"Four percent of what?" asked old Mrs. Tregowan from the bench, sharp as a gutting knife.

"Of whatever they say it is," Beatrix said. "And here's what else. Once they strip it, the cave is done. Their extraction plan involves cutting the collapse seal with thermal lances. The structural survey Ilse and I ran says the chamber roof won't survive it. The cliff above the inner passage is the same rock formation as the headland your seawall sits on. They crack that chamber open the wrong way, and you don't get a heritage site or a payout. You get a rockfall, a new tide channel, and a winter storm with a straight run at the harbor front."

Silence now, the real kind. Tom watched faces turn toward the black windows, toward the sound of the sea they all pretended not to hear at night.

"And if we say no?" That was young Ruth Pengelly, sixteen and furious about everything lately, standing up on the bench seat. "They've got lawyers. They've got a claim filed with the county already. What stops them?"

"Nothing," Beatrix said simply. "Unless the claim is voided. And it can be voided—because a salvage claim requires that the recovered material have no living claimants and no heritage designation. The confession changes both. If the contents are documented publicly—if they're registered as heritage before Halloway extracts them—the commercial value collapses and the protection attaches. But registration needs community backing. It needs this town to stand up at the hearing and say these are our dead and our history, not salvage."

"So we choose," said Denny. "Memory or money."

"We choose," Beatrix agreed, "and I won't pretend it's an easy one. I'll only tell you what's true: the money leaves. Money always leaves. It comes off a boat and goes back on one. What stays in a town is what it decides to keep."

She sat down then, on the stool nearest the door, and took the pint someone pressed into her hand, and the room broke apart into argument the way a wave breaks—noisy, chaotic, going everywhere at once.

* * *

Tom let it run for an hour. He listened to Denny work his faction with figures written on beer mats; he listened to Ruth Pengelly argue heritage with the pure ferocity of someone who hadn't yet learned that fury doesn't pay mooring fees; he listened to Mrs. Tregowan tell anyone within range exactly which of the eleven flood names belonged to her family tree, and how many times she'd swept the memorial stones. He listened to the middle—the great undecided mass of the town—shift and murmur like tide over shingle.

Ilse Marchetti stood near the fireplace with her maps rolled under her arm, saying little, watching Beatrix the way Tom had watched lighthouse keepers watch the sea: steadily, with something underneath the steadiness that wasn't professional at all. Tom had spent a lifetime reading people across counters and ticket windows, and he gave their situation another year, maybe less, maybe a spring.

Finally, when the argument had gone around three times and begun eating itself, the landlord caught Tom's eye across the room. Old habit. The Anchor had no mayor, no council worth the name; it had the pub, and the pub had him, and everyone in the room knew why they'd all come tonight instead of settling it in kitchens. Some votes you cast standing up.

Tom set down his untouched pint, took hold of the chimney breast, and stood. His knees announced their objections. He ignored them.

"I'm going to talk about the flood," he said, and the room went quiet faster than Beatrix had ever managed it, because the flood was the one subject Greyhaven handled like live wire. "Because I'm the last one in this room who remembers it, and memory's my job, same as it's been my job to mind the station and the wall of names and the keys to a railway that hasn't run since before most of you were born."

He crossed slowly to the spot beneath the lamp where Beatrix had stood. His legs knew the distance even if his lungs complained.

"In 1893 I wasn't born. But my father was eight years old, and he stood where you're standing, and his father told him the story, and his father had it from a man named Zhou who worked the cliff cuttings and lived because he'd refused to go into the lower gallery that morning. Refused. Said the ground sang wrong. They called him a coward until the sea came through, and then they called him a prophet, and then the company came down from inland with lawyers and money and very quickly everybody stopped calling him anything at all."

He saw Beatrix go still. Good. She should hear it. She should hear it from someone whose only stake was the truth.

"Eleven men went into those workings on a morning the company had been warned about. Eleven didn't come out. Three of them were named Zhou—brothers, cousins of the man who'd sung the warning. My father used to say the company didn't just kill those men. It killed the *telling*. For sixty years we've sold tickets to look at a hole in a cliff and told tourists a ghost story about smugglers, because the true story had lawyers attached to it. We took their coin and we kept our mouths shut, and we called that prudence."

He turned to Denny's faction, and his voice gentled, because these were not enemies.

"Denny, I know what winter does to your books. I know what the herring leaving did to all of us. If I thought Halloway's money would stay in this town one day past the signing, I'd be first in line with my hand out. But I've watched companies come to this coast for two hundred years, and they come in ships and leave in ships, and what they leave behind is holes." He tapped the floorboard with his boot. "We're standing on what they left last time."

Then he turned to the rest, and his voice came up out of wherever old men keep their loudest selves.

"There's a wall in the station house with eleven names on it. I've wiped the salt off it every week for forty years. Nobody visits it. Nobody's visited it in my lifetime, because visiting it would mean asking the question the company paid us not to ask. Well." He drew breath. "I'm eighty-one years old. I won't see another generation's tide bring that cave back. This is the one chance this town gets to answer for its grandfathers' silence. I say we register the contents as heritage. I say we stand at that hearing and speak the eleven names out loud, all of them, Zhoos included. And I say—" and here his voice cracked, and he let it, because some cracks are the proof of the vessel—"I say we stop charging admission to a lie."

He looked at Beatrix. "Your grandmother left this town carrying shame that was never hers. It's time somebody carried the truth back to her grave. You're doing it. The least the rest of us can do is stand behind you while you do."

For a moment nobody moved. Then Ruth Pengelly began to clap, and Mrs. Tregowan rose from the bench like a ship coming off the slips, and the sound built—not a roar, nothing so clean, but a rolling, grudging, stubborn approval, half the room rising to its feet while the other half sat with folded arms and lowered eyes, counting their losses. Denny Callow drained his pint, set the glass down carefully, and said, to no one and everyone, "Four percent," as if filing the grievance somewhere permanent. But he didn't argue further. On this coast, silence after a vote was consent, and everyone knew it.

Tom made his way back to his corner, knees protesting, heart hammering like a signal lamp in a gale. Beatrix found him there before the crowd fully broke.

"My grandmother," she said quietly. "Ellen. You knew her."

"I carried her trunk to the morning train myself," Tom said. "Nineteen hundred and four. She cried the whole way to the platform and thanked me twice for not asking why." He met her eyes. "I've been saving that question for fifty years. Turns out you answered it yourself."

She looked at him for a long moment, and something passed between them that had no need of words—some settling of accounts older than either of them.

It was Ilse who broke it, coming fast from the doorway with the fog swirling in behind her and her face gone pale as chart paper. "Beatrix." Her voice carried across the emptying pub. "The compound lights. Cray's crew—they're loading dive gear onto the trucks. Right now. In this weather."

Tom watched Beatrix set down her pint untasted, watched the decision arrive in her face like weather over the headland.

Outside, the fog swallowed them both, and the sea began to make ready.
