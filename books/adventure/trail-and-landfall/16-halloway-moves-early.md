## 16. Halloway Moves Early

The storm came in like a rumor first—a darkening on the horizon that the gulls read before any of us did—and then it came in like a fact.

I was in the station house at Greyhaven, going over Ilse's contour maps by lamplight, when Tom Ferris knocked twice and let himself in without waiting for an answer. He had a barometer in one hand and his cap in the other, and he held up the barometer as though it were a verdict.

"Thirty below and falling," he said. "Faster than she's got any right to fall. That's not a blow coming, Miss Zhou. That's a siege."

"How long?"

"Two days. Maybe three if we're lucky, and I stopped believing in lucky around 1974." He set the barometer down on my maps, deliberately, so I'd have to look at it. "The tide'll come back for that cave like it never left. And when it does, anything inside goes with it—or under forty fathoms of cliff."

I thanked him. He lingered at the door, and I could feel him deciding whether to say something else. In the end he only said, "You'll do right by them," which wasn't a question, and left.

By them, he meant the dead. By them, he meant the names on his wall. By them, he meant my family, whose shame I had been carrying around in my coat pocket for eleven days now, folded into quarters, warm from my body heat, heavier than lead.

I should have gone straight to Cray. I should have told him the storm timeline had collapsed, that Halloway's careful two-week extraction window had just been cut to a sliver, and that we needed to move—properly, legally, with documentation and witnesses and Ilse's cameras running.

Instead I sat with the lamp and did arithmetic I didn't want to do.

If Cray learned the window had closed, he would move early. Not carefully-early. Salvage-early: night tides, headlamps, crowbars, no mapping, no record. Everything of value out of the chamber before the sea took its toll call, and everything without a price tag left to drown. The letters. The ledgers. The confession. My great-grandfather's voice, sealed behind Victorian masonry for a hundred and thirty years, pried loose and auctioned off in lots.

Or—if Cray didn't know—I might have days yet. Days in which Beatrix Zhou, licensed engineer, could do this the right way.

I looked at the letter in my coat. I thought about how badly I lied, and how well Cray read people, and I understood, with the flat certainty of a level bubble finding center, that there was no version of the next three days in which Cray moved slowly.

He already knew about the storm. He'd have his own barometer. His own arithmetic.

And his arithmetic didn't include the cave surviving. Only the cargo.

* * *

They moved at half past eleven that night, and I only knew because Ilse was still awake.

She had taken to sleeping in the survey hut above the harbor rather than the crew lodging, and when I climbed the path through the rain to find her, she opened the door before I knocked, hair damp, eyes sharp, a hand-rolled cigarette burning down forgotten between her fingers.

"I heard engines," she said. "Down at the slipway. Three trucks, no lights."

We stood together at her window and watched. Below us, in the wash of a single shielded lantern, Cray's divers were loading gear into an inflatable—cutting tools, lifting bags, the aluminum cases they used for documents. Cray himself stood apart from the work, hands in his pockets, collar up against the rain, looking exactly like a man attending a funeral he had personally arranged.

"He's stripping it tonight," Ilse said. "Before the town wakes. Before anyone can file an injunction."

"He knows about the storm."

"Everyone knows about the storm. That's *why* it's tonight." She turned from the window, and in the lamplight her face was pale and set, and I saw that she had already dressed for outdoors. Boots, oilskin, the waterproof satchel where she kept her field notebooks. "Are we going down there or are we going to stand here and watch them rob a grave?"

"It's dangerous, Ilse. The tide's already turning. The inner passages flood first—"

"So does the seawall, if that cliff comes down, and you don't see me moving inland." She shouldered the satchel. "Beatrix. They're taking your grandfather's voice out of that rock tonight. Are you coming?"

Great-grandfather. But I didn't correct her. There was no time, and perhaps there was no difference anymore; the dead of Greyhaven had folded themselves around me until I couldn't tell which grief belonged to whom.

We went down through the rain, past the sleeping boats, along the shingle below the cliff where the old fishermen's path ran—the path tourists were never told about, the one that stayed dry at all but spring tides. The cave mouth was a black absence ahead of us, breathing cold salt air, and inside it, fifty yards deep, lights were already moving.

Cray had posted no sentry on the seaward approach. Why would he? No one local would come out in this weather, and the expedition's own engineers—he assumed—were asleep in their bunks, loyal to their contracts.

He had made the same mistake everyone made about me. He had assumed I was the kind of person who stayed bought.

* * *

Inside, the cave was a cathedral being looted.

Their lamps threw long shadows up the walls, across the tool marks Ilse had charted, the deliberate chisel-cuts of men who had built a railway into the throat of the sea and then buried it. Two divers waded the shallows of the first gallery, hauling equipment toward the collapse face where the railcar waited. A third worked a hydraulic spreader into the engineered fracture line, and the sound it made—metal complaining against stone that had been silent since Queen Victoria's jubilee—went through me like a nail.

"You're doing this without a structural assessment," I said, stepping into the light.

Everything stopped. Four faces turned, lamp-halos swinging. Cray came forward out of the gloom with the unhurried tread of a man who had expected this conversation eventually, just not here, just not now.

"Miss Zhou." Rain still dripped off my oilskin; I must have looked like something the sea had coughed up. "It's nearly midnight."

"And the tide turns at four. You know that. You've known it longer than I have." I walked past him, past the divers, to the collapse face, and put my palm flat against the stone. It was cold and wet and it hummed faintly, the way rock does when it's been disturbed and hasn't decided what to do about it. "This face is saturated. Every pound of pressure you put on that fracture line is transmitted into the crown of the gallery, and the crown is the cliff itself. You're not extracting artifacts, Mr. Cray. You're mining the foundation of Greyhaven."

"We have a claim."

"You have a claim on things that are *in* the cliff. If the cliff comes down, your claim comes down with it. So does the seawall. So does the harbor front." I turned to face him. Behind him, Ilse had appeared at the edge of the lamplight, satchel open, camera out, documenting everything with the calm efficiency of a war correspondent. Cray saw her too. His jaw tightened.

"Your cartographer isn't authorized for night operations."

"Neither am I," I said. "Arrest us."

For a long moment nobody moved except the water, sliding in and out over the shingle with that patient, tidal patience, counting down to four o'clock. Then Cray exhaled through his nose, and I watched him make the calculation—the same one I'd made in the station house, but arriving at the opposite sum, because his ledger only had one column.

"We're going in," he said. "With or without your blessing. You can stand here and watch, or you can get out of the way. But you will not interfere with a lawful salvage operation, Miss Zhou, or I will bury you so deep in this industry you'll be surveying drainage ditches in the Fenlands until you retire."

"The confession won't save you," I said. "It'll sink you. You know what's in that railcar. Your founders' company falsified the surveys. That document doesn't belong in a warehouse—it belongs in a courtroom, and every hour it sits in your possession makes Halloway look more like the railway company that wrote it."

Something flickered behind his eyes—not doubt, exactly. Recalculation. But the divers were already moving again, and the spreader bit deeper into the fracture, and the argument was over because the noise had swallowed it.

So we followed them in.

* * *

Ilse went first, because she knew the passage geometry better than anyone alive—better than the Victorians who'd cut it, probably, since she'd mapped it stroke by stroke while they'd only ever run trains through it blind. She called the hazards over her shoulder: the knee-deep sill at the second bend, the low arch where we'd have to duck-walk, the chamber where the air tasted of iron and old timber.

The railcar sat where we'd found it, upright on its buried rails behind the deliberate collapse, a black iron coffin that had refused, for a century and a third, to be a grave. The divers had their lifting bags rigged. One of them—Kessler, the big quiet one—was fitting a cutting torch to the car's coupling bolts.

"Don't cut those," I said. "The frame's load-bearing. Cut the anchor points aft, port side first—"

"Miss Zhou gives orders now?" Kessler said mildly, not looking up.

"She's right, though." Cray's voice, from behind us. He had followed us in after all, which surprised me, and shouldn't have. A man like Cray never let valuable things out of his sight, and by now he suspected—I was sure he suspected—that I was the most valuable thing in the cave besides the paper. "Port side first. Do it."

The torch lit with a hiss, and orange light danced across the vaulted ceiling, and for one strange moment the cave looked almost warm, almost alive, like the belly of some enormous patient animal.

Then Kessler's lamp swung wide as he shifted position, and the beam caught the crown of the gallery full-on, and the cave woke up.

It started as a sound like sand pouring through an hourglass, somewhere high and close. Ilse heard it before anyone—she grabbed my arm and said "*down*"—and then a crack ran along the ceiling like lightning frozen mid-strike, and the first stones came down.

Not the big ones. Not yet. A scatter of fist-sized rock, bouncing, ringing off the railcar's roof, splashing into the shallows. Everyone froze. The torch went out. In the sudden dark there was only the drip of water and our own breathing and that fine, terrible hiss of settling debris.

"Lamp on the crown," I said quietly. "Slowly."

Kessler raised his light. Above us, where the ceiling had been a smooth sweep of engineered masonry, there was now a seam—a fresh crack, weeping grit, running eight feet from wall to wall. And beyond it, visible through the dust, the older collapse—the one the Victorians had built to seal the chamber—had shifted. A gap had opened at its crown, a dark slot no wider than a hand, exhaling cold air.

The chamber was still open. We could still get the car out, given six hours and luck.

But the crack said otherwise. I've spent my life reading rock the way other people read weather, and that seam was a sentence with the verb already conjugated: *this ceiling has begun to fail, and it will finish the job on its own schedule, and its schedule is shorter than yours.*

"Everybody out," I said. "Now. Slowly. No running—running shakes the crown."

"We're not leaving the cargo," Cray said.

"You're not leaving with the cargo either, because the cargo weighs four tons and the passage floods at three-forty, and if you try to float that car out tonight you'll be doing it underwater." I kept my voice level, engineer-flat, the voice I used on crews who wanted to push weather. "Out. All of us. We reassess at dawn with proper lighting and a structural survey, and if the face holds, we plan a real extraction. If it doesn't hold—" I looked up at the weeping seam. "Then nothing anyone does tonight matters anyway."

Cray stared at me. And then, God help me, he nodded—because whatever else he was, he was a man who could read a losing hand, and the ceiling had shown him his cards.

They went out ahead of us, divers first, then Kessler, then Cray, his lamp bobbing away down the passage toward the gray breath of the entrance. Ilse paused to shoot one last frame of the railcar, flash strobing white against the iron, and I was turning to follow her when the second crack came.

Louder. Deeper. Behind us.

The crown of the *inner* passage—the low arch, the one bottleneck between the chamber and the world—let go.

It wasn't dramatic, at first. It was a groan, a shift, a sigh of dust. Then a slab the size of a kitchen table peeled off the arch and dropped, and the whole gallery answered it, and the air filled with a roar that was less a sound than a pressure, a weight passing through my chest, and I had Ilse's wrist and we were running, actually running, my earlier order forgotten because the building was falling and the rulebook was underwater.

Stones hailed past us. Something struck my shoulder hard enough to spin me. Ilse fell and I hauled her up by both arms and we came out of the dust cloud into the outer gallery just as the last of it settled behind us, and I turned with my lamp and saw—

Saw the end of the world.

The inner passage was gone. Not blocked—*gone*, filled floor to crown with fallen masonry, a plug of rock and shattered Victorian brickwork as tight and final as a cork. Beyond it, somewhere in the dark on the wrong side, the railcar sat with its letters and blueprints and pay ledgers and confession, and with them, cut off from the tide, from the exit, from us:

"Cray," Ilse whispered.

My lamp swept the rubble. Dust hung in the air like fog. From beyond the plug—muffled, distant, but unmistakable—came a sound I will hear for the rest of my life: a man's voice, thin with shock, calling out names.

Calling mine.

I pressed my palm against the fresh rubble. It was warm from the fall. Somewhere behind four feet of stone, Cray was alive—breathing, shouting, trapped in a chamber with a four-ton railcar, a rising water table, and a tide that would turn at four o'clock and begin looking for ways in.

The old maintenance tunnel. Great-grandfather's escape route. Ilse had charted its upper reaches; the lower workings were flooded, unmapped, and would be filling with the turning tide even now.

I looked at Ilse. She was already pulling out her charts.

"How long do we have?" I asked.

Her pen hovered over the map, over the blue veins of flooded tunnel between us and the man on the other side of the mountain, and she said, "Until the tide turns. About three hours."

Three hours. To find a route through drowned workings that hadn't seen light since 1891, before the sea closed the door behind us and kept it closed for another generation.

I took off my coat, left it with the maps, and reached for a lamp.
