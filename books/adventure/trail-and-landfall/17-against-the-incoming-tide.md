## 17. Against the Incoming Tide

The maintenance tunnel was where her great-grandfather had left it, which was to say: hidden behind a hundred and forty years of rust and ivy and the town's polite forgetting. Beatrix found it by lamplight and memory both—by the blueprints she'd studied until they lived behind her eyes, and by the shape of the hillside itself, the way it swelled just so above the old cutting, a bulge in the earth like a held breath.

The door was iron, painted once, and it opened on the third shoulder-shove with a groan that seemed to come up out of the ground itself.

"Zhou's way in," Ilse said beside her, breath fogging in the dark. She had her satchel of maps strapped tight, her hair plastered to her skull by spray. "His own escape route."

"He built himself a back door," Beatrix said. "Then he sealed the front one and let the sea keep his secrets." Her hand rested flat against the cold iron frame. Somewhere beyond it, down flooded workings and drowned galleries, Cray was trapped with a broken something and a lamp guttering, and the tide was already turning. "Let's see if the back door still works."

Behind them, Tom Ferris stood at the mouth of the cutting with three men from the harbor and a coil of rope, holding the storm lantern high. The wind came off the water in long shoves now, carrying salt and the smell of the swell turning. An hour, maybe less, before the cave mouth went from throat to grave.

Beatrix stepped through.

* * *

The tunnel ran level for forty meters, then dipped. That dip was the first problem, because the dip was already drinking.

Her boots found water at the second bend—ankle-deep, then shin-deep, moving with a slow insistent pull toward the inner dark, toward the chamber where the collapse had sealed Cray in. The sea didn't rush; it seeped. It came up through the workings below like truth through a floor of lies, filling every low place the Victorians had cut and abandoned.

"Lamp high," she called back. "Watch the walls for the blue marks."

Ilse had chalked the route on the way in, days ago, during the first survey—little blue arrows at knee height, a cartographer's habit, marking passages against the day the maps would matter. They mattered now. Beatrix counted them as she waded: one, two, three, each arrow a small cold flare of blue in the lamplight, each one a decision already made by a steadier version of herself.

The water rose past her knees at the fourth arrow. Behind her she could hear Ilse breathing, controlled and deliberate, the breathing of someone who had decided to be afraid later.

"How far?" Ilse asked.

"Two hundred meters to the junction. Then down the incline to the chamber." Beatrix lifted her lamp. The light lay flat and yellow on black water that trembled with distant pressure. "The blueprints show an air pocket over the chamber roof. If the collapse didn't breach it, he's breathing. If it did—" She didn't finish it. There was no version of finishing it that helped.

They went on. The tunnel narrowed, and the rock closed around them like a fist, and Beatrix thought—not for the first time, but for the first time without flinching—of her great-grandfather walking this same passage with his confession folded against his chest, knowing what he knew, sealing what he sealed. He had cut this tunnel as a coward's exit or a conscience's, and either way he had cut it true, because it still held. The crown of the arch was dry. The invert was drowning. Between those two facts, a Zhou walked toward her family's name again.

At the junction, the water was waist-high and cold enough to make her gasp, a bright animal shock that locked her joints for two full seconds before training overrode it. She heard Ilse hit it behind her and swear, softly, fluently, in two languages.

"The incline drops eight meters," Beatrix said. "It'll be neck-deep at the bottom if the seal's holding. Deeper if it isn't." She turned, and in the lamplight Ilse's face was pale and set and utterly certain, and Beatrix felt the thing in her chest that had been pulling in two directions her whole life finally swing all the way to one side. Duty said turn back, mark the loss, file the report. Love said go down. For the first time in her life, they were saying the same thing. "Last chance to wait here. Someone should be at the junction with a line."

Ilse tied the rope off around a stanchion older than anyone alive, tested it with her whole weight, and looked up. "I'm not waiting anywhere," she said. "You should know that about me by now."

"I'm learning it," Beatrix said, and went down the incline into the water.

* * *

The incline was a black throat. She descended it by feel, one hand on the rope, one hand trailing the wall, the lamp lashed to her chest so the light swung with her body and made the water below look like it was breathing. At the fifth step the water took her thighs. At the ninth, her waist. At the twelfth, her chest—a cold so total it wasn't cold anymore, just pressure, just fact—and then her feet found the chamber floor and she stood in it, chest-deep, in the place where her family's whole history sat rotting gently in the dark.

The railcar was there. Of course it was there. Cray's crew had gotten it half-free of the collapse before the secondary fall came down, and now it canted against the rubble at an angle like a ship aground, its Victorian paint ghost-pale under the lamplight, its windows filmed with a century and a half of mineral bloom. And beside it, wedged in the V of fallen stone, one arm pinned to no purpose and one leg clearly wrong, sat Cray.

He was gray. Not frightened-gray, though there was some of that too, but the gray of a man whose blood had better places to be. His lamp was still lit, propped on a stone, throwing his shadow huge and wavering across the railcar's flank. When the light reached him he squinted against it, and Beatrix watched him understand who had come, and watch him decide, visibly, not to say anything about it.

"Zhou," he managed. His voice was small in the big wet dark. "You shouldn't be—"

"The tide's coming in," Beatrix said. She was already moving, hauling herself through chest-deep water toward him, reading the collapse as she came—the slab pinning his leg, the wedge of rubble holding his arm, the unstable shoulder of stone above it all that shifted, very slightly, when the sea pushed. "The chamber floods in"—she checked the tide table in her head, the one she'd memorized the way other people memorize prayers—"forty minutes. Maybe thirty-five. Can you move your leg?"

"No," Cray said, simply. A lawyer to the end. No argument, no embellishment, just the finding of fact. Then, quieter: "The railcar. It has to come out. Everything in it. You understand what's at stake if the sea takes it again."

Beatrix reached him. Up close she could see the blood thinning pink in the water around his leg, could see the fine tremor in the hand that wasn't pinned. She put her fingers to his throat—pulse fast, thready, but there—and then turned to the slab.

"You're worried about chain of custody," she said, "while you're bleeding out in a Victorian railway car."

"I'm worried," Cray said, and for a moment the lawyer's armor cracked and she saw the man underneath, sixty years old and scared and stubborn as bedrock, "that if this goes back under, it goes back under for another hundred and fifty years. And I'm sixty-two, Zhou. I won't get another generation."

She understood it then—all of it, the contracts read aloud at breakfast, the salvage claim, the ultimatum, the whole hard apparatus of a man who had spent his life making sure things got *kept*, by whatever means, because he had seen too many things lost. It didn't excuse it. But she understood it, and understanding, tonight, was a tool like any other.

"Then we get you out," she said, "and the railcar comes with us. Both. Nobody gets left, nothing gets lost. That's the deal."

"That's not how leverage works," Cray said weakly.

"It's how tides work," Beatrix said. "Everything moves at once."

* * *

The slab pinning his leg weighed more than both of them together, but it wasn't seated—it was balanced, tipped against the railcar's iron chassis, and iron was a lever's oldest friend. Beatrix got the pry bar out of the kit on her back, worked it into the gap between slab and chassis, and put her whole weight on it. The metal sang a low note. The slab ground a centimeter. Water slopped against her chin.

"Again," Ilse said, arriving at her shoulder, taking the bar with her, and they leaned together, two women and a length of steel against a hundred tons of geology, and the slab moved another centimeter, and another, grudging as a confession.

Cray made a sound when the leg came free that Beatrix would hear for the rest of her life. She made herself look at it—crushed boot, blood clouding, bone probably intact, probably—and made herself say, "You'll walk on it badly for a while," because a diagnosis, even a guess, was a thing to hold onto in black water.

Then the arm, which was only trapped, not crushed—she worked it loose joint by joint while he breathed through his teeth—and then it was just the arithmetic. Thirty minutes of tide. Two hundred meters of flooding tunnel. One injured man who couldn't swim the deep sections unaided, one railcar full of paper that couldn't take a soaking, and two women with one rope and a pry bar.

"We can't carry both," Ilse said quietly. "Him and the car."

"Yes we can," Beatrix said. "We carry him. We float the car."

She'd been looking at the railcar while she talked, the way she'd looked at dead branch lines her whole career—not seeing ruin, seeing possibility. The car was iron, watertight below the window line if the doors were dogged, and it sat on a cradle of rubble that sloped, however slightly, back toward the incline. The Victorians had built these cars to be moved by hand; four people could shift one on level track, and there was still track under the water, she could feel it with her boot, the old rails running slick and true beneath the flood.

"There's rail under us," she said. "It runs to the incline foot. We dog the doors, we load everything portable inside, we slide it on the rails behind us. It floats when the water's deep enough. It's a boat that happens to be a train."

Cray stared at her. Even gray-lipped and shaking, even with his leg ruined, something in his face reorganized itself—the expression of a man watching a contract rewrite itself in his favor. "That's insane," he said.

"It's engineering," Beatrix said. "Same thing, done properly."

* * *

They dogged the doors. They loaded the letters, the ledgers, the blueprints, the confession—wrapped in oilcloth from the dive kits, bundled tight, stacked dry above the waterline inside the iron box. Cray directed the packing from where he sat, hoarse, precise, naming each document and its order, and Beatrix let him, because a man needed a job to stay alive and his job had always been the keeping of things.

Then they moved the car. It took everything. Ilse and Beatrix at the corners, Cray pushing with his good leg and his good arm from inside the doorway, the four of them—or the three of them and a century of dead intent—grinding the car along the drowned rails, meter by meter, while the water climbed their bodies and the sea hammered somewhere overhead like a fist on a door.

The incline nearly beat them. Going up it, chest-deep, the car wanting to slide back, the rope doubled and run through the stanchion above where Ilse had anchored it—they hauled hand over hand, Beatrix counting aloud because counting was a thing her voice could do when everything else was failing. Twenty steps. Fifteen. Ten. The water dropped from her chest to her waist to her thighs, and the railcar came up behind them like a slow iron moon rising out of the sea.

At the junction, Tom Ferris was waiting with the rope and the harbor men, and his face when the lamp found them—when it found Cray, alive, and the oilcloth bundles visible through the car's grimy windows—was the face of a man watching the dead stand up and walk home.

"Take him," Beatrix said. "Then the documents. Then the car comes apart and comes out in pieces if it has to. Go."

They went. And Beatrix stood a moment in the junction with the water at her knees and rising, and listened—to the sea working overhead, to the drip and groan of a hillside deciding what it would keep and what it would give back—and felt, for the first time since she'd signed Halloway's contract, completely unconflicted. Every part of her was pointed the same direction. It felt like standing upright after years of leaning.

Ilse's hand found hers in the dark.

"Together," she said.

"Together," Beatrix said, and they followed their own blue arrows out, toward air, toward the storm, toward the town with its lit windows and its wall of names—three of them Zhou—waiting to hear whether the confession had survived the sea.

Behind them, far down in the dark, the first of the inner galleries gave way with a sound like a held breath finally released. The cave was closing. By morning it would be gone, sealed for another generation, keeping nothing but water.

But the papers were coming up the hill.
