## 21. Landfall

The ink was still tacky when the light came through the station house windows and found it.

Ilse Marchetti had been awake since four, though she would not have called it waking. She had drifted in that grey country between sleep and work where her best lines were always drawn, the ones no instrument could find but the hand knew. The map lay across three trestle tables pushed together under the great south window of Greyhaven's station house, weighted at the corners with brass survey weights and a teacup Beatrix had abandoned weeks ago and never reclaimed. It measured nearly two metres by one and a half, and it had taken her seven months to finish, because a definitive map is not drawn so much as argued into existence—against memory, against water, against the stubborn erasures of the sea.

She stood over it now with her coffee going cold and let herself look at it whole for the first time.

The cave of Greyhaven Head opened on the paper like a lung. She had rendered the flooded lower galleries in a blue so deep it seemed wet, the tidal passages hatched with the careful notation she'd invented during those first terrified descents: *water returns here faster than a man can walk.* The dry upper chambers she'd left the colour of bone. And there, sealed forever behind the collapse of that final night—the collapse that had nearly taken Cray, that had taken the chamber itself beyond any human reach—she had drawn the railcar as it existed now only in memory and blueprint: a small precise rectangle, annotated in her own hand, *contents recovered; vessel remains.*

She had argued with that rectangle for months. There was a version of her that wanted to leave it blank, to admit honestly what could never be verified again. But Tom Ferris had settled it without knowing he'd settled anything, back in February, when she'd shown him the draft and he'd put one blunt finger on the empty space and said: "You don't leave the dead off a map just because you can't see them."

So the railcar stayed. Drawn from memory, from the blueprints, from the confession's own engineering notes in a Victorian hand that Ilse had come to know almost intimately—that of Edmund Zhou, chief engineer, who had tried to stop the flood and been silenced for it. She had traced his linework until she could feel his pressure on the page, the sure heavy strokes of a man who drew like someone building an argument against time. Somewhere in the last months he had stopped being a historical figure and become something closer to family. Not hers by blood. Hers by cartography, which was its own kind of kinship.

Outside, spring had finally committed itself. The gorse on the headland had gone loud with yellow; the harbour water lay flat and bright as hammered tin; and down on the spur line, where for eleven months men had worked with rails and ballast and the particular patience of restoration, the track ran clean and true from the station throat all the way to the headland loop, gleaming faintly in the early light like a sentence newly written.

Today they would run it.

* * *

The wall of names hung in the station house waiting room, and Ilse passed it every morning, and every morning she looked at it, and this morning she stopped.

Tom Ferris had begun it decades ago—a board of painted wood behind glass, listing the dead of the flood of 1893, compiled from parish records and gravestones and the stubborn recollection of old families. For most of Ilse's time in Greyhaven it had held forty-one names. Three had been missing. Everyone had known which three, and no one had said why, and the omission had sat in the room like a chair turned to face the wall.

Now there were forty-four.

*ZHOU, EDMUND — engineer.*
*ZHOU, MARGARET — his wife.*
*ZHOU, THOMAS — their son, aged 19.*

The letters were gold leaf, done by a signwriter from up the coast whom Tom had driven two hours to fetch, refusing to trust the work to anyone who hadn't heard the story first. He had told the signwriter everything, Ilse had gathered—all of it, the falsified surveys, the silencing, the confession sealed in the dark for a hundred and thirty years—and the man had worked slowly and charged almost nothing and shaken Tom's hand at the end like they'd buried someone together.

Below the restored names, mounted on a small brass plate, hung the thing Ilse had carried up the cliff path through the storm eight months ago: a facsimile of Edmund Zhou's confession, the first page of it, in his own handwriting. The original lived in the county archive now, behind glass, catalogued and witnessed—but Tom had insisted the station house have its copy, because this was where the railway had been run from, and this was where the truth should stand when people came to ride the line.

*We were told the sea wall would hold,* the faded ink read. *I surveyed it myself and I knew it would not, and I wrote saying so, and my letter was returned unopened. I record this here so that someone, someday, will know the cost was not paid in ignorance.*

People did come to read it. Not crowds—not yet—but a steady trickle: an elderly woman who stood before the Zhous for twenty minutes and then asked Ilse, quietly, whether Margaret had been musical, because there was a piano in a photograph somewhere; a school party from inland who went silent in a way children rarely do; a man who had worked Halloway Salvage's dock crew and came once, alone, on a Sunday, and left before anyone could speak to him.

And every day, Tom Ferris polished the glass with a chamois cloth, the way he had polished it for fifty years, except that now his cloth lingered on the new names a moment longer, and Ilse had decided long ago not to pretend she didn't see it.

"You're staring at your own work," Tom said from the doorway behind her, and she turned to find him holding two mugs of tea, offering one. He was smaller than he'd been a year ago—or perhaps the winter had simply pressed him down a little—but his eyes were the same, shrewd and kind and entirely unsentimental about sentiment. "It'll still be there tomorrow."

"I know." She took the tea. "I keep checking the gold leaf hasn't flaked. It's irrational."

"It's not irrational." He looked past her at the wall, at the three names that had waited a century in the dark. "My grandmother used to say a name is a kind of track. You lay it, and after that, people can arrive." He sipped his tea. "Speaking of arriving. Your driver's on the platform."

* * *

Beatrix Zhou had never in her life been late for a departure, and Ilse had learned that the reverse was also true: she arrived absurdly early, and then found work to fill the gap, because idleness made her itch like wool. So it was no surprise to find her already on the platform at half past five, dressed in the heritage railway's dark blue uniform with the keeper's badge—*B. ZHOU, LINE KEEPER*—pinned at her collar, running her hand along the boiler of the little tank engine they'd named *Margaret*, after the woman on the wall.

The engine had come up from a preserved line in the midlands, a compact Victorian saddle tank that the restoration society had bought for less than the price of a car, and Beatrix had spent the winter learning her the way she learned everything: completely, obsessively, with grease to the elbows and a notebook filling with the locomotive's particular temper. *Margaret* was, everyone agreed, a fitting name—stubborn, quiet-running, and possessed of a whistle that could be heard clear across the bay.

"You finished it," Beatrix said, seeing Ilse's face, reading her the way she always did—one glance, whole chapters. "The map. Last night. Don't tell me you didn't sleep, because I can see it in your shoulders."

"I slept."

"You're lying beautifully. It's one of the things I love about you." But she said it lightly, and her eyes went soft around it, and Ilse felt the familiar warmth of being known—thoroughly, precisely known, the way she herself knew coastlines. Eight months ago that feeling had frightened her. Now it was simply the ground she stood on.

"Come and see it before we go," Ilse said. "After. When we're back. I want you to look at it properly, not in five minutes."

"After," Beatrix agreed, and then, because she was Beatrix, she added: "Though I've already seen the draft eleven times."

"And you've corrected me twice, and both times you were right, and I've forgiven neither."

They walked the train together while the sky went from pewter to rose. This was their ritual now, and had been since February: the slow walk down the rake of three restored carriages, checking axle boxes and brake rigging, Beatrix calling out items and Ilse ticking them against the list on her clipboard, though Ilse suspected the list was mostly theatre and the real inspection happened in Beatrix's hands, in the way she touched each wheel bearing like a pulse. The town thought of them, Ilse knew, as a settled fact—*the keeper and the cartographer*, the way one might say *the church and the harbour*—and there had been no announcement, no moment, only a gradual accumulation of shared mugs and shared keys and eventually the small house on Harbour Row with two desks in the front window, one covered in maps and one in timetables.

At the rear carriage, Beatrix paused with her palm flat against the cold steel of the frame.

"First test run," she said. "Down a line my great-grandfather surveyed, that my grandmother fled, that I spent fifteen years avoiding." She shook her head slowly. "Do you know what my mother would say? She'd say I've lost my mind. Zhou women don't go back. Zhou women keep moving."

"And yet."

"And yet." Beatrix smiled—not the quick professional smile she gave contractors and county officials, but the other one, the rare one, the one Ilse had a private theory appeared only when Beatrix forgot to guard it. "You know what I figured out, somewhere around November? Running was never the inheritance. That was just the damage talking. The actual inheritance—" she tapped the rail beneath them, twice, like a knock on a door— "was this. Building things that last. Laying track that holds. He tried to do it right, and they broke him for it, and the family spent a hundred years mistaking flight for the family trade." She looked out along the shining line toward the headland, where the sea showed silver between the gorse. "I'm ending the mistake."

The sun cleared the headland then, and the whole spur lit up at once, rail after rail flashing westward, and Ilse thought—as she had thought a hundred times that winter, drafting the cave by lamplight—that light on steel was the most honest thing in the world. It showed exactly what was there.

"Right," Beatrix said, brisk again, climbing into the cab. "Passengers in twenty minutes. Board of trustees, county planning, the heritage grant people, half the town, and Tom Ferris sitting in the front row like it's his funeral or his coronation, possibly both. Are you riding with me?"

"I'm riding with you," Ilse said. "Front of the cab. Someone has to check your blind side on the curve at Ferris Point, and you drive like a woman being chased."

"I drive like a woman who has somewhere to be."

"It's the same thing with you."

* * *

They ran at six minutes past seven, into the full flood of morning.

Ilse stood at the cab window with the wind tearing at her hair and watched Greyhaven fall away behind them—the station house with its wall of names, the harbour with its boats turning on the tide, the crooked roofs catching fire with sunrise—and ahead of them the line unspooled through country she had mapped and remapped until she knew every culvert and cutting by heart. Gorse and blackthorn. A field of newborn lambs scattering like spilled chalk. The old mileposts, repainted, counting down from a distance that had once meant exile and now meant nothing but miles.

*Margaret* ran sweetly, her exhaust beating a steady rhythm, and Beatrix drove her with the unhurried precision of someone entirely at home in her own hands. At Ferris Point the line swung close to the cliff, and below them, far down, the sea lay calm over the place where the cave mouth had been. Ilse looked for it out of habit. There was nothing to see. The storm of last autumn had closed it with a violence that had rearranged the whole lower cliff face, and the sea had sealed it the way it seals all its secrets—completely, indifferently, for another generation or another century. The chamber with the railcar was beyond anyone's reach now. But its contents rode in the county archive and in Ilse's map and in the gold-leafed names on the station wall, and that, she had come to believe, was how the sea kept score: it took the places and gave back the truths.

"There," Beatrix said quietly, nodding ahead.

Where the old maintenance tunnel had emerged above the cliffs—Edmund Zhou's escape route, the passage through which Beatrix had led Cray out against the turning tide—the restoration crew had built a small stone shelter, and inside it, visible from the train, stood a memorial: a simple granite marker with the forty-four names cut deep, facing the sea. They had dedicated it in March, in wind, with the whole town present and Cray standing at the back in a good coat, having driven nine hours to be there, saying almost nothing, which from Cray was a eulogy.

Beatrix sounded the whistle as they passed—one long note that rolled out over the water and came back changed—and Ilse saw her jaw tighten and ease, tighten and ease, and reached over without looking and laid her hand on her arm, and left it there.

The rest of the run passed in a kind of golden ordinariness: the level crossing with its new gates, the halt at Millbrook where two children waved with total commitment, the long gentle climb to the headland loop where the trustees stood applauding on the platform as if a ten-minute test run were the conquest of some mountain range. Which, Ilse supposed, in a way it was. Beatrix brought the engine to rest with a stop so smooth the tea in the cab flask didn't ripple, and the crowd surged forward, and Tom Ferris was the first up the cab steps, gripping Beatrix's hand in both of his and saying, gruffly, "Your grandmother would have—" and then stopping, and starting again, and settling on: "She'd have been proud. And she'd never have said so. So I'm saying it for her."

That evening, the station house emptied, and the light went long and amber through the south windows, and Ilse hung the map.

Tom had built the frame himself—oak, from a beam salvaged out of the old signal box—and they raised it together onto the waiting-room wall, three feet from the glass case of names, so that anyone who came to read the dead could turn and see the shape of the place that had taken them and given them back. Ilse stepped down from the ladder and stood beside Beatrix in the empty room, and they looked at it together: the cave rendered whole, the blue water and the bone-coloured chambers, the little rectangle of the railcar drawn faithfully in the dark where no living person could ever stand again, and in the corner, in small formal lettering, the title she had chosen months ago and never changed:

*Greyhaven Head — Definitive Survey. Drawn from memory, blueprint, and the papers of E. Zhou, Engineer. For the town that remembers.*

"Well," Beatrix said at last, and her voice wasn't quite steady. "That's us, then. On the wall. Permanently."

"That's how walls work."

"I've spent my whole life making sure nothing of mine ended up on one." Beatrix turned to her, and the evening light caught the grey coming in at her temples, and the crease between her brows that only appeared when she was happy and off guard. "You've pinned me to a wall in a station house in the town I ran from for fifteen years. Do you understand what you've done?"

"Irreversible," Ilse said. "Like all good engineering."

Beatrix laughed, and pulled her in, and kissed her there in the empty waiting room with the smell of oak shavings and coal smoke and the tide coming in outside, and Ilse thought: *landfall*. All those years of charts and coastlines, and she had never once understood the word until now—it didn't mean arrival, it meant the moment the running stops, the moment the keel touches ground and holds, and you step off onto solid earth and call it yours.

They were still standing there, forehead to forehead, when the knock came at the station door.

Tom answered it. Ilse heard the murmur of voices, and then his footsteps crossing the waiting room, slower than usual, and when she turned he was holding out an envelope—a thin one, postmarked from far up the coast, addressed in a stranger's careful hand.

"For you," he said, and there was something in his face she couldn't read. "Marked personal. And Miss Marchetti—you'll want to look at the return address."

Ilse took it. Turned it over.

The sender's name meant nothing to her. But the place it came from was a village she knew from her own charts, sixty miles north, on a dead branch line she had once marked, in another life, with a small neat notation she had never forgotten:

*Closed. Sealed tunnel. Reason unknown.*
