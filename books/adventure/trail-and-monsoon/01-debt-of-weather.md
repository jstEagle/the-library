## 1. Debt of Weather

The storm came off the icefields at four in the afternoon, the way it always did, like something with a grudge and a schedule. Ulyana Oyelaran watched it build from the cab of her truck, parked on a gravel shelf above the Kestrel River, and felt the old hunger rise in her chest — that animal pull toward weather, toward the thing that could kill her, because it was also the only thing that had ever paid.

"Come on," she murmured to the sky. "Be worth it."

On her dashboard screen, the drone's feed showed the storm front from two hundred meters up: a wall of slate and green-black cloud dragging its skirt of rain across the valley, lightning stuttering inside it like a bad heart. The contract was simple. Meridian Weather Partners wanted forty minutes of usable footage of a supercell interacting with the glacial outflow — katabatic wind shear, they'd called it, the cold air pouring off the ice meeting the warm river valley and doing violence. They paid by the usable minute. She needed every minute she could get.

Sixty thousand owed. That was the number. It sat in her head the way a stone sits in a boot — not sharp anymore, just always there, reshaping every step. Sixty thousand to the Vantablack Syndicate, who had bought her debt off three other creditors the way you'd buy a used tarp: cheap, cheerful, and with no intention of letting it stay folded.

She launched the second drone from the truck bed, fingers moving through the pre-flight without thinking. Wind check. Gimbal lock. Return-home point set to the truck, because if there was one lesson this country had taught her, it was to always know where home was, even when home was a rust-streaked flatbed with a cracked windshield and 340,000 kilometers on the odometer.

"Ulyana." Dev's voice in her earpiece, thin over the radio static. He was down at the riverbank with the gauge pole, measuring the surge. "Water's coming up fast. That front's going to hit the outflow before it hits you."

"I see it."

"Do you? Because from down here it looks like the river is about to have opinions."

"The river always has opinions. That's why I'm up here." She lifted the drone off the bed and walked it out into the open, watching the feed bloom across her screen. The storm was close enough now that she could feel it in her teeth — that pressure drop, the taste of ozone and rock dust. Somewhere behind the clouds, the Meridian Glacier was shedding its skin, feeding the river that fed the flood that fed the footage that fed the debt.

It was a circle. Everything in her life was a circle lately, and all of them were draining.

The first cell hit at 4:41. She got eleven beautiful minutes — the anvil cloud rotating slow as a door opening, the rain shaft coming down in gray curtains, a bolt of lightning striking the far ridge with a crack she felt through her boots. The drone held steady in gusts that would have folded a lesser machine. She narrated under her breath out of habit, calling timecodes, wind speeds, the color of the light. Forty years ago, someone else had stood on this gravel and called the same numbers for a different reason. The maps used to name this valley. Now the maps just said *unregulated catchment*, which was what the world called places it had decided to stop loving.

Then the wind shifted.

She felt it before she saw it — a hard slap from the northwest, the katabatic surge Dev had warned her about, cold air falling off the glacier like water off a cliff. The drone bucked. On her screen the horizon spun, and she fought it, thumbs working, coaxing the little machine back toward the truck while the wind tried to fold it into the river.

"Hold, hold, hold—"

The return-home point was the truck. The truck was fine. The drone made it to within thirty meters of her, fighting the crosswind, and then a gust came through that was less wind than verdict, and the drone went sideways, down, into the willow scrub at the edge of the shelf. The feed cut to static.

Ulyana stood very still in the rain, listening to the storm hammer the roof of the world.

"That was the good one," Dev said over the radio. Not a question.

"That was the good one."

"How many minutes did you bank?"

"Eleven. Maybe twelve usable." She did the math without wanting to. Twelve minutes at Meridian's rate was nine hundred credits. Nine hundred against sixty thousand. She laughed, once, and it came out wrong.

* * *

They dug the drone out together as the storm's trailing edge soaked them through. It was dead — arm snapped, gimbal shattered, the camera lens ground full of grit. Ulyana held it in both hands and looked at it the way you look at a friend's coffin.

Dev crouched beside her, rain running off his hood, a big patient man built like something the river had failed to move for years. He didn't say anything for a long moment. Then: "We could patch the old one. The 200-series. It flies ugly but it flies."

"It films uglier. Meridian won't take it."

"Meridian can go—" He stopped, because headlights were coming up the valley road.

The vehicle was black and boxy and too clean for this road, and it stopped twenty meters away with the confidence of something that had never been stuck anywhere in its life. Two people got out. The one in front wore a pale gray suit that the rain refused to touch, and he smiled before he was even fully out of the car, a wide, warm, professional smile, the kind that gets printed on brochures for dental clinics.

"Ms. Oyelaran." He crossed the gravel with his hand already extended. "Mr. Fenn. Vantablack. We've spoken."

"We've spoken twice. Both times I liked you less by the end."

His smile didn't move a millimeter. "Consistency is a virtue." Behind him, the second figure stayed by the car, arms folded, wearing the particular stillness of a man paid to be furniture until he wasn't. Fenn surveyed the dead drone in her hands, the storm-wrecked camp, the truck with its peeling paint and hand-painted registration number, and his eyes did the appraisal openly, pleasantly, the way a butcher looks at a hanging carcass. "Rough day?"

"Good footage. Bad landing."

"Mm. Eleven minutes, I'm told. Our friends at Meridian are chatty." He clasped his hands behind his back and rocked slightly on his heels, a man enjoying a stroll. "Ulyana. May I call you Ulyana? Sixty days. That's where we've landed. Sixty days, full settlement, or we take the rig, the trailer, and whatever equity exists in your contract portfolio — which, between us, is more sentimental than financial."

"You drove four hours to tell me something you told me on the phone."

"I drove four hours because I like you, and I wanted you to hear it looking at my face." He beamed. "Also because the scenery is extraordinary. All this meltwater. The glacier's having quite a season. Calving early, they say. Big year for salvage along the outflow — all that flood debris, all that history coming downstream." His gaze lingered on her, mild and knowing, and she understood that Vantablack knew exactly how she made her living between contracts. Salvage. Dredging the river's leavings. It was in her file; everything was in her file.

"Sixty days," she said.

"Sixty days. And Ulyana — I genuinely hope you make it. You're one of the interesting ones. Most debtors are just arithmetic. You're a story." He turned back toward the car, then paused, rain beading on his shoulders like the sky couldn't get a grip on him. "Oh — one more thing. There's work upriver. Post-flood salvage. Some of our clients have interests in what the surge brought down. If you were to find yourself... gainfully employed in that direction, we'd look kindly on prompt payments. Very kindly."

He said it like a gift. She heard it like a hook.

* * *

That night the storm cleared and the cold came down hard enough to freeze the puddles, and Ulyana sat in the truck's cab with the heater rattling and the ledger open on her knee, though she didn't need the ledger. She knew the numbers the way she knew her own pulse.

Nine hundred credits earned today. Drone replacement: four thousand, if she bought refurbished, which meant another week of filming with equipment that embarrassed her. Fuel, food, Dev's share, the mooring fees on the dredge barge she half-owned with a man in Kessel Port who never answered his radio. Sixty days. Sixty thousand.

Dev knocked on the window and climbed in uninvited, carrying two tin cups and a thermos that had seen the fall of better civilizations. He poured, handed her one, and looked at the ledger without reading it.

"Fenn's offer," he said. "The salvage job upriver."

"It's not an offer. It's a leash with a bow on it."

"Maybe. But it points upstream, and upstream is where the money is right now. Whole valley's talking about the surge. Biggest meltwater event in a decade — the river moved boulders, Ulya. Moved *bridges*. Everything the flood took apart between the moraine and the delta is sitting in the gravel bars waiting for someone with a dredge and no fear." He sipped. "And we have a dredge. Technically. We have a dredge the way I have hair."

She almost smiled. Almost. Outside, the moon was coming up over the icefields, and the Meridian Glacier caught it and threw it back silver, a forty-kilometer wall of blue-white silence at the head of the valley. She'd filmed that glacier a hundred times. Sold its face a hundred times. It had never once looked back at her.

Until tonight, somehow, it felt like it was looking.

"One job," she said finally. "Upstream, past the bend at Harrow Bar. We dredge the debris fields, we sell scrap and whatever the river coughed up, we pay Vantablack something real, and we do it on our own schedule, not theirs." She closed the ledger. "I'm not giving them the truck, Dev. The truck's the last thing I own outright. You give them the rig, you give them the winch, and after that you're not a contractor anymore, you're an employee. I've seen how their employees stand. Like the floor might ask them questions."

Dev looked at her for a long moment, steam rising off his cup. "You know what's upstream past Harrow Bar?"

"Gravel bars and drowned junk."

"And the Meridian's outflow cave. The ice route. Nobody salvages up there this late — calving season starts in weeks, and when it does, the whole lower tongue lets go and seals the canyon till spring." He shrugged. "But the flood was big. Bigger than anyone's saying. Whatever the surge pulled loose up there is still coming down. Might be worth seeing what arrives before the ice closes the door."

Ulyana looked out at the glacier gleaming under the moon, vast and patient and keeping whatever it kept, and felt the exhaustion in her bones sharpen itself into something with edges. Defiance, maybe. Or just the particular stubbornness of a woman who had lost a drone, nearly lost a truck, and refused to lose the argument.

"First tide tomorrow," she said. "We go up and see what the river kept for us."

She didn't know yet that the river had kept something with a seal on it. She only knew that sixty days was a countdown, and countdowns were just storms with numbers, and she had never once backed away from a storm.

She slept badly, and dreamed of blue ice, and woke before dawn with her hands already cold and ready.
