## 2. What the River Keeps

The river gave back what it took, but never in the same order.

Ulyana Oyelaran had learned that the hard way across nine flood seasons on the Meridian outflow: a church roof here, a child's bicycle there, a whole barn arriving intact three weeks after the surge that stole it from a valley two hundred kilometers upstream. The water was a slow-handed thief with a bad memory. It dropped things where it pleased and kept no ledger anyone could read.

She stood hip-deep in the shallows below the gravel bar they called the Fingers, watching the morning fog burn off the braided channels, and tried to read yesterday's ledger. The surge had been a monster — a glacial lake burst somewhere above the ice line, sending a brown wall down the outflow at four in the morning. She'd heard it from her bunk in the truck, a sound like a mountain clearing its throat, and had lain there cataloguing everything she couldn't afford to lose: the rig, the dredge, the winch, Dev's collection of broken radios he swore he'd fix someday. The flood had spared all of it. It had also, apparently, rearranged the entire riverbed.

"Bar's gone," Dev said, coming up the bank with his boots slung around his neck, wading barefoot because he claimed the cold made his feet think faster. He was a narrow man, all elbows and patience, with a beard he trimmed by burning it. "Whole eastern channel moved forty meters north. There's a new hole under the third finger you could lose a bus in."

"Then we don't lose a bus in it."

"I'm just saying the river did our prospecting for us." He dropped onto the bank and started pulling his boots on. "Floods scour deep. Anything heavy that was buried shallow is now buried interesting."

Ulyana looked at him. Dev had a genius for saying alarming things in the tone of a man discussing lunch.

"We're not treasure hunters," she said. "We're debris contractors. We pull scrap, we log it, we sell it by weight. That's how we stay boring, and boring is how we stay alive."

"Boring," Dev repeated, tasting the word like a bad vintage. "Right."

They worked the new hole anyway, because the contract from the regional salvage office covered the whole bar and Ulyana had sixty days to make numbers that would keep the Vantablack Syndicate's cheerful enforcers cheerful. The dredge coughed to life at noon, its pump swallowing silt and spitting gray slurry into the settling pond, and for six hours the day was exactly as boring as she'd promised herself it would be. Truck axles. A refrigerator full of something nobody opened. Fence posts. A piano, or what the river had decided a piano should become — a blackened harp of wire and rotting felt that Dev hauled up the bank cradling it like a casualty.

It was near dusk, light going copper on the water, when the claw came up heavy.

Ulyana felt it before she saw it — a shudder through the dredge frame, the engine note dropping like the machine had bitten stone. She killed the throttle and leaned over the intake grate.

"That's not rock," she said.

"Nope," Dev said, already grinning, which was the problem with Dev; he grinned at exactly the wrong moments. "That's got weight to it. That's got *intent*."

They brought it up slow, the winch complaining, and broke the surface at last light: a chest the size of a sea trunk, furred with gray silt, trailing weeds like something drowned rather than buried. It was lead-lined — Ulyana could see the dull metal where the silt had sloughed off one corner — and bound in iron straps gone orange with rust. It weighed enough that the winch arm bowed carrying it, and when they wrestled it onto the gravel it sat there with the settled, patient gravity of a thing that had been waiting.

Dev scraped mud from the lid with his thumbnail, then stopped scraping.

"Ulyana."

"What."

"Come look at this."

In the center of the lid, under a crust of river clay, was a stamp. Two stars, one nested inside the other, struck deep into the lead with an official die — the kind of seal that meant paperwork, authority, a government somewhere at the other end of it.

Ulyana knew the seal. Everyone who worked the Meridian knew it, the way you know a face from old photographs. The double star of Kestria, pressed into border markers and customs plates and the brass buttons of a diplomatic service that had ceased to exist forty years ago, when Kestria stopped being on maps and started being a question people asked quietly.

"That's not possible," she said.

"River doesn't care about possible." Dev crouched, turning the stamp in the low light. "Lead-lined. You know why you line a chest in lead?"

"So the contents don't corrode."

"So the *contents don't read*. Lead stops radiation, signal, scanning — anything." He looked up at her, and the grin was gone now, replaced by something more careful. "Somebody didn't want this found by accident. Or found at all."

The fog was coming back off the water. Somewhere downstream a bird cried once and thought better of it. Ulyana put her hand flat on the lid and felt the cold come up through the lead like the chest had its own small weather.

"We open it tomorrow," she said. "Daylight. On the tarp, logged properly, like debris."

"Sure," Dev said. "Like debris."

Neither of them believed it.

* * *

Dev couldn't sleep, which was normal, so he told himself the chest wasn't the reason, which was not.

He sat in the truck cab with the dome light off and the window cracked, listening to the river work the bar in the dark. The chest sat outside under a groundsheet they'd weighted with stones, and every twenty minutes he found himself checking it, the way you check a door you've already locked.

He'd grown up two valleys over from the Meridian, in a town that existed mostly as a fuel stop, and he'd spent his whole life around people who'd lost things to water. What he'd never been around was something the water had *kept* — held, guarded, carried forty years downstream of wherever it went in. The Kestrian barge. He'd heard the story a hundred times growing up, told badly at fuel-stop bars: the diplomatic vessel that went upriver during the last days and never came down, swallowed by the glacier along with whatever it was carrying. Records, gold, bodies, depending on who was telling it. The version everyone agreed on was simpler: whatever was aboard mattered enough that two governments had spent a decade pretending it hadn't.

And now it was sitting under a groundsheet ten meters from his sleeping crewmate, stamped with a dead nation's star.

At midnight he gave up and went out and lifted the corner of the tarp, telling himself he was checking the stones. The moon was up, thin and white, and the lead lid threw back a dull glow like a fish's flank.

"You're going to wake her," said a voice behind him, and he nearly went into the river.

Ulyana stood at the truck's shadow-line, arms crossed, hair loose, looking less like she'd been asleep than like she'd been standing there deciding whether to let him do this.

"It's going to bug me all night," Dev said.

"It's going to bug me all year." She came forward and crouched beside him. For a while neither of them touched it. Then she said, "Pry bar's in the bed."

"You said daylight."

"The river's had it forty years. I don't want to give it forty more minutes of my permission."

The lock was rusted shut and the pry bar made short, ugly work of it, iron shrieking once into the dark before Ulyana clamped a hand over the hasp like she could take the noise back. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, were bundles. Documents, dozens of them, sealed in wax that had survived better than the paper. And on top, a single folio in a hand too neat to be hurried, addressed in faded ink:

*Manifest of Effects — Diplomatic Barge *Kestria's Word* — Final Passage.*

Dev read the name twice. Below it, in columns, the manifest listed what the barge carried on its last run: sealed testimony, depositions, a registry of assets held in trust, and — his eye snagged on the line and refused to leave it — *records of accession, Government-in-Exile, complete.*

"Complete," he said aloud. "As in, the whole legitimate government of Kestria, on paper, in one box."

"As in worth more than every truck I'll ever own." Ulyana's voice was flat, careful, the voice she used when the ice was thin. She repacked the folio with hands that weren't quite steady. "Worth more to some people than to others. That's the part I don't like."

"Who wouldn't like it? Exiles have been looking for this since before we were born."

"Exactly. And so has everybody who made them exiles." She tied the oilcloth down and looked up at him, and in the moonlight her face had gone hard and young at once. "A chest doesn't float forty years and wash up at my feet by accident, Dev. Somebody lost it, or somebody put it in the water. Either way, whoever wants it back knows roughly where to look, and the moment we log this with the salvage office, we tell them exactly where it is."

The river ran on in the dark, patient, indifferent, keeping its own counsel.

"So we don't log it," Dev said slowly.

"So we think very carefully about what we are now." Ulyana stood, brushing grit from her knees. "Because as of tonight we're not debris contractors anymore. We're either the richest people on this river or the deadest, and the difference is about sixty days of staying quiet."

She reweighted the tarp, and they stood a moment looking at the low mound of it, ordinary as a pile of scrap, holding a country inside.

That was when Dev pointed downstream, toward the black water where the channels braided away toward the coast.

"Headlights," he said. "Two vehicles. Stopped. They've been there since before you came out — I watched them kill their lights when I lifted the tarp."

Ulyana didn't move for a long second. Then, very quietly: "Get the chest in the truck. Don't run. People only chase you if you look like you've got something worth chasing."

"And if they already know?"

"Then," she said, watching the dark where the headlights had been, "we find out how much."
