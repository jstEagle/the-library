## 3. The Optimists Call

The chest sat in the middle of Ulyana's tent like a guest nobody had invited, radiating cold.

She'd wrapped it in a tarp against the damp, but the lead lining drank the heat out of the air around it, and by the third morning she'd stopped sleeping near it. Not because she was afraid of it — she told herself that twice a day, with increasing conviction — but because it hummed. Not audibly. In the way that a loaded question hums in a quiet room. *Kestria*, stamped in double stars on weathered oak. A country that existed now only in the memories of people old enough to have lost one.

Dev crouched over it with a magnifying loupe, cataloguing the manifest's water damage by lamplight. "Third page is gone entirely," he said. "Eaten by forty years of river. But look — the barge name repeats here, and here." He tapped two lines. *MV Concordance of Stars.* "Diplomatic registry prefix. That's not a cargo hauler, Ulya. That's an embassy on a hull."

"An embassy at the bottom of a glacier."

"Embassies end up in worse places." He said it lightly, but his hands weren't light. They were careful, the way they got when he was handling something he thought might be holy or might be a bomb and hadn't decided which.

Ulyana looked out through the tent flap at the Meridian Glacier, eight kilometers upriver, blue-white under a sky the color of dishwater. Somewhere inside all that ice was a sealed chamber of meltwater and compressed time, and somewhere inside *that* was whatever the Concordance had been carrying when the river took it. Proof. The manifest used the word three times before the water ate it: *proof, proof, proof*.

Proof of what, the river hadn't said.

Her satellite phone rang at dusk.

Nobody called her satellite phone except creditors and her mother, and her mother had been dead six years. She let it ring four times, then answered, because ignoring the Vantablack Syndicate had never once made them smaller.

"Ms. Oyelaran." The voice was warm the way a heating pad is warm — applied comfort, no source. "This is Mr. Fenn. I don't believe we've spoken directly. I handle optimistic outcomes for the Syndicate."

"I know what you handle."

"Then we'll skip the pleasantries, though I do enjoy them." A pause with paper sounds behind it. "You're camped at the confluence below the Meridian outflow. You recovered certain materials from the flood debris on the fourteenth. I'd like to talk about those materials."

Ulyana stepped outside, into the wind, where Dev couldn't read her face. Downriver, the water ran high and brown with glacier flour. "I don't discuss inventory over open channels."

"Of course not. Which is why I'm calling to invite you to dinner. Tomorrow evening. There's a survey vessel anchored at Kilometer Nine — the *Generous Hour*. My treat, my chef, my discretion. Bring the chest if you like. Or don't. Either way, you'll leave with better options than you arrived with."

"And if I don't come?"

"Then tomorrow I'll be having a very good meal alone, and the day after that, your sixty days will become fifty-seven, and we'll both be sad about it." The warmth never wavered. That was the thing about Fenn's voice — it didn't rise or fall. It just stayed pleasant, like weather that had decided to kill you eventually and saw no reason to hurry. "Seven o'clock. Dress for the boat. It's always cold on the water."

He hung up before she could refuse, which she supposed was its own kind of answer.

* * *

They came into camp the next morning while she was checking the winch.

Not Syndicate men — she knew their silhouettes now, the cheerful broad-shouldered patience of them. These moved differently. Six figures in matching gray expedition shells, moving up the far bank in a loose file, unhurried, professional, stopping to plant instruments. By noon there were tents. By midafternoon, a hydrofoil skiff ran a survey line across the outflow channel, sonar puck blinking on its transom, and a drone — a good one, military-surplus, the kind Ulyana had lost two of this season — hung above their camp like a patient wasp.

"They're not even hiding it," Dev said, binoculars up. "That's a salvage fleet. Three vessels minimum, support barge, dive team. Somebody paid real money."

"Who?"

He passed her the binoculars. On the lead skiff, a woman stood at the rail talking to two others, and even at this distance Ulyana caught something in how she held herself — spine straight, chin level, the posture of someone standing on ground that wasn't there anymore. When the skiff turned, Ulyana saw the patch on her shoulder. Gray cloth, stitched by hand: two stars.

"The exiles," Ulyana said quietly.

"Kestrian diaspora's got money scattered all over the world. Old families, shipping fortunes, insurance claims that never settled." Dev lowered his voice as if the wind might sell him out. "Forty years they've been looking for what happened to their country. And you're sitting on the receipt."

The trap closed around her so gently she almost admired it. The Syndicate wanted the chest's contents to own them. The exiles wanted them to *be* owned — returned, restored, made public in the way that nations are made public. And she stood between them holding a box of other people's history, worth either enough to erase her debts or enough to get her killed, depending entirely on who reached her first.

"Wanted by everyone," Dev said, reading her face the way he read water. "Owned by no one. You see the problem."

"I see the dinner invitation."

"That's the same problem wearing a nicer shirt."

* * *

The *Generous Hour* was a converted research vessel, white hull, too many antennas, lit from within like an aquarium. A crewman in civilian clothes walked her aboard with exaggerated courtesy, and Ulyana noted the bulge of a shoulder holster under his jacket the way she noted current speed — professionally, without comment.

Mr. Fenn turned out to be a small, neat man in his fifties with silver hair combed like weather and the unlined face of someone who had never once carried anything heavy. He poured her a glass of white wine she didn't touch and seated her across a table set with actual linen, forty kilometers from the nearest road.

"You lost a drone last week," he said. "A Kestrel-9, serial ending 4471. Insured through a syndicate subsidiary, incidentally. Small world." He smiled. "Your contract footage from the storm chase covered eleven percent of your outstanding balance. You're carrying, as of this morning, one hundred and ninety-two thousand. Plus carrying costs. I want to make those numbers go away."

"In exchange for?"

"The contents of the chest. Exclusively. Everything it points to, everything it contains, delivered intact and unexamined-by-anyone-else to my clients. In return: full forgiveness. Clean slate. Your rig stays yours. You walk away owing nothing to anyone." He spread his hands, presenting the offer like a dessert cart. "It's the best deal you will ever be offered in your life, Ms. Oyelaran. I mean that sincerely. Most people never get one this good."

"And the exiles' fleet downriver? Do they get a copy of the menu?"

Fenn's smile didn't move but something behind it did, a shutter adjusting. "The parties who retained us have an interest in certain historical materials remaining... consolidated. Dispersal reduces value. Value reduced means obligations resurface. You understand arithmetic."

"I understand that 'consolidated' is a strange word for evidence."

"Evidence." He tasted the word. "Is that what you think it is? Let me tell you what I think it is. I think it's forty-year-old paper in a wet box, and its only value is scarcity. The moment it exists in ten places, it exists nowhere. The moment it exists in one place, it exists for whoever holds it." He leaned forward, and for the first time the warmth thinned enough to show the machinery underneath. "Here's what I actually admire about you, Ulyana — may I call you Ulyana? You survived the storm season on skill and debt. You took a salvage job nobody else would bid because everyone else read the river charts and saw death. You are, objectively, the best gambler on this water. So gamble. But gamble knowing the house has already seen your cards."

"And whose house is that?" she asked. "Because the exiles hired a fleet, and you're buying exclusive delivery, and neither of you talks like people chasing treasure. Treasure doesn't need exclusivity. *Testimony* does."

For three full seconds, Mr. Fenn stopped smiling. It was the most honest moment of the evening, and she understood she would probably never get another one.

"You're smarter than your credit rating suggests," he said. "Which is why I'll say this once. Whatever is in that glacier, my clients believe it threatens people who are still alive, still powerful, and still licensed by governments you'd recognize from your own passport. If you deliver it to me, you're rich and free. If you deliver it to the exiles, you're a hero to maybe nine thousand stateless people and dead broke. If you try to keep it—" he shrugged pleasantly, "—then you're a woman alone on a river with a box everyone wants, and I genuinely don't recommend the third option. It has poor historical precedent."

She stood. "Keep the wine."

"Seven days," Fenn said to her back, warm again, terrible again. "My offer expires with the week. After that, I'm afraid, the market decides, and markets are so much less kind than I am."

* * *

The walk back along the bank took an hour in the dark, headlamp bouncing off scree, the glacier ahead of her a faint gray presence like a sleeping animal. Dev met her at the treeline with coffee already poured, which meant he'd watched the whole meeting through binoculars and had opinions queued.

"Well?"

"He offered everything. Full forgiveness. One hundred ninety-two thousand, gone, if I hand over the contents exclusively."

"To the Syndicate's clients. Who are?"

"He wouldn't say. Which tells you they can't afford to be said." She wrapped her hands around the cup, letting it burn. "And the exile fleet hasn't made an offer at all. That's what scares me about them. Fenn wants to buy me. Those people want to *trust* me, and I haven't earned it, and they're spending money they can't spare on a fleet to shadow a camp because they can't afford to do anything else."

Dev was quiet a moment. "There's a third option nobody's pricing."

"Don't."

"Somebody broadcasts it. All of it. Everywhere at once. Then it's not scarce, it's not ownable, and every buyer in this river valley is suddenly holding nothing."

"Fenn literally explained why that fails. Scarcity is the value—"

"For *them*." Dev's eyes caught the firelight. "But you're not selling, Ulya. Are you?"

She didn't answer. Because the honest answer was that she didn't know yet, and the more honest answer was that some part of her — the part that had spent three seasons watching storms eat towns while insurance adjusters calculated depreciation — already knew exactly what she wanted to do and was terrified of it.

Across the river, the exile fleet's camp lights burned steady. One figure stood at the waterline, facing her side, unmoving. Watching the chest's location, or watching her. With the double-star people it had always been the same thing.

Behind her, upriver, the Meridian Glacier groaned — a long, deep sound like a door being tested in its frame. Six tides until calving season opened the route. Seven days until Fenn's offer died.

Whichever came first, somebody was going to reach that ice.

She drained the coffee and started packing rope.
