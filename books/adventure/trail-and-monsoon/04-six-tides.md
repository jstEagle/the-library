## 4. Six Tides

Dev Okonkwo had never trusted arithmetic he liked. Numbers that came out in your favor were, in his long experience, the first sign that you'd miscounted somewhere — left out a variable, rounded past a truth, assumed the river would behave like the chart instead of like itself. So when the tide tables finally resolved on the third night at camp and told him something almost generous, he checked them four times before he let himself say it out loud.

He did it with chalk, because chalk was honest. The plywood wall of the mess tent had become his blackboard over two days of arguing with himself, and by lantern light it looked like what it was: a man's whole nervous system laid out in white dust.

The Meridian Glacier's outflow was tidal for eleven kilometers inland. Everyone knew that; the charts said so, and the drowned forest along the banks confirmed it twice a day with its black roots gasping air. What the charts didn't say — what only Ulyana's battered hydrology manuals and Dev's own stubborn surveying had established — was how *high* each surge lifted the water where the river went under the ice.

"Say it again," Marisol said from her bunk, not looking up from the winch cable she was re-splicing. "Slow. I want to hear whether you believe it."

"The mean surge raises the outflow one point nine meters at the ice front," Dev said. "Spring tides raise it three point four. The cave mouth sits two point six above low water." He tapped the chalk line he'd drawn across the plywood, a horizontal scar representing the lip of the ice cave they'd seen through binoculars from the moraine — a dark slot in the glacier's blue flank, breathing mist. "So. Ordinary tides: we don't get in. Spring tides: we get in with eighty centimeters to spare, if we're running a shallow boat, if the weather holds, if the river isn't carrying half a mountain's worth of silt that day."

"And spring tides come when?"

"Every fourteen days. We've had one." He drew six tallies under the line. "Six more before calving season opens the face and everything downstream of the snout turns into artillery. After that, no boat goes near that ice for any money anyone could pay us."

Marisol set down the cable. "Six chances."

"Six chances. And we need one clean run upriver, into the cave, back out, all inside a single surge window — call it five hours of usable water, less if the wind argues." He looked at the tallies until they blurred. "I've been on rivers my whole life, Mari. I have never once been given six tries at anything. The river gives you the day it gives you."

"Then we're rich," she said, and grinned, and he loved her a little for it, and hated the arithmetic for making even love feel like a calculation.

* * *

Ulyana took the news the way she took most things: standing up, arms crossed, staring at the thing as if it might flinch first.

"Six," she repeated.

"Six," Dev said. "Maybe seven if the equinox surge runs high, but I wouldn't plan around maybe."

She turned to the map table, where the lead-lined chest sat open under a tarp weighted with stones — not hidden, exactly. Dev had noticed she'd stopped trying to hide it after the second night, when the drone they couldn't afford to replace caught a boat hull glinting twelve kilometers downstream, riding at anchor with its lights doused. The exiles' fleet. Ilma's people. They knew where the chest was. Hiding it would have been a courtesy to no one.

"So we get one shot at the barge," Ulyana said, "and everyone within a hundred kilometers knows we're going to try."

"That's about the shape of it."

"And Fenn knows too."

Dev said nothing, which was its own answer. Mr. Fenn's last visit had ended with the envoy's cheerful promise ringing in the camp like a bell nobody had asked for: *Deliver the contents exclusively, and every number you owe becomes zero.* Fenn smiled the way weather smiled before it turned. Somewhere out beyond the moraine, Dev was certain, there were men being paid to know exactly which night the spring tide came.

Ulyana pressed both palms flat on the map table and looked at the glacier's penciled outline as though she could hold it still by force. "Then we don't waste a single one of the six. First spring tide, we scout the approach. Second, we go."

"Second," Dev agreed. "Not first?"

"First, I want to see the cave mouth with my own eyes at surge height. If I'm wrong about the depth, I'd rather be wrong in a rubber boat than in the *Marianne*." She glanced up. "You'll pilot?"

"I'll pilot," Dev said. It wasn't a question he'd been given room to answer any other way, and he found, examining himself honestly, that he didn't want one. That frightened him more than the ice did.

* * *

Tomasz left on the fourth morning.

No scene. No argument. Dev woke to find the younger man's bunk stripped, his share of the gear racked neatly by the tent flap, and a note weighted under the coffee pot in handwriting that tried very hard to be steady:

*Copied the manifest. Not stealing — copying. Someone has to sell the truth while you're busy being noble with it. Six tides is five more than a man needs to die for another man's country. Don't follow me. — T.*

Marisol read it twice and handed it back without comment. Ulyana read it once, folded it, and put it in her jacket pocket over her heart, which Dev understood was either sentiment or bookkeeping — with Ulyana it was usually both.

"He took the skiff?" Dev asked.

"Took the skiff, took two days' rations, left the rifle." Marisol's voice was flat in the way she kept it flat when flatness was mercy. "Left the rifle, Dev. You notice what that means? He wanted us to know he could've taken it and didn't. That's an apology. That's Tomasz apologizing the only way he knows how."

Dev walked down to the bank alone. The river ran low and green-gray between its braided channels, patient as a creditor. Downstream, at the limit of sight, the exiles' anchored hull caught the morning sun and threw it back like a coin flipped into the air and never caught.

He thought about Tomasz walking the gravel bar toward the coast road, a copy of a forty-year-old manifest folded against his chest, going to sell a dead nation's paperwork to whoever paid — the Syndicate, the exiles, the licensing ministry, it hardly mattered, the buyer changed nothing about the goods. And Dev found that he could not make himself hate him. That was the worst part. He ran the numbers himself every night; he knew exactly what the numbers said. A crew of four against a glacier, a salvage fleet, and the Vantablack Syndicate, with sixty days of debt compounding behind them like floodwater. Arithmetic said walk. Arithmetic said take the copy and the skiff and live.

Loyalty, Dev thought, was just arithmetic you refused to finish.

He stood there until the cold came up through his boots, then went back and started checking the winch.

* * *

They spent the fifth day preparing the *Marianne*, and Dev spent it listening to the river.

That was the skill nobody hired him for and everybody needed: not reading charts but hearing water, the way a doctor hears a chest. The Meridian outflow had a voice, and this week its voice was wrong. Too much grind in it, a deep subsonic rumble under the surface noise, like a man talking through gritted teeth. Silt load rising. Something shifting up in the accumulation zone, some chamber of meltwater filling faster than it should.

He mentioned it to Ulyana at dusk, while she greased the trailer bearings by lantern light.

"You're saying the glacier's unhappy."

"I'm saying the glacier is *talking*. Whether it's talking sense or talking nonsense, I can't tell you yet. But if the calving season comes early—" He stopped, because the rest of the sentence was a cliff.

"If it comes early, our six tides become fewer." She wiped her hands on a rag and looked up at the mountain, where the icefield caught the last red light and held it like a coal. "How many fewer?"

"I don't know. That's the honest answer. Could be six. Could be four. Could be tomorrow."

Ulyana was quiet a moment. Then she laughed — short, real, exhausted, the laugh of a woman who had already lost her truck once that season to a river and was not going to be surprised by anything water did again.

"Then we go on the first tide after all," she said. "Scout and run in the same breath. If the ice is going to change the schedule on us, we change ours first."

"It'll be dark coming back."

"It's always dark somewhere, Dev. Pick your dark."

* * *

The first spring tide came on a Tuesday, under a sky the color of wet slate, and it came *high* — higher than his tables promised, three point eight meters at the ice front, the river shouldering up into the drowned forest until the black trees stood waist-deep and the current reversed itself and ran uphill, groaning, toward the glacier.

Dev stood at the *Marianne*'s wheel with the throttle at half and watched the water do the impossible thing it did twice a day, and felt the old vertigo of loving a force that did not love him back.

The cave mouth appeared around the final bend like a held breath: a slot of deep blue in the gray-white face, and beneath it, revealed by the surge, a flooded antechamber the charts swore didn't exist. Water poured into it in a slow, muscular swirl. Eighty centimeters of clearance, his arithmetic said. His eyes said less. His eyes said the ice overhead hung close enough to touch, and did touch, once, with a sound like a knuckle rapping a coffin lid.

"One pass," Ulyana said beside him, binoculars up. "We look. We don't enter. Mark the channel, mark the ceiling, mark the time window. Then we're out before the ebb starts pulling."

"We're out before the ebb starts pulling," Dev agreed, and brought her in.

For eleven minutes the *Marianne* floated inside the glacier's throat, and Dev learned things no table could teach him: that the channel bent left thirty meters in, that the ceiling dropped to a terrifying kiss above the mast, that the blue light in there was the color of a memory of water rather than water itself. And at the far end, where the tunnel narrowed to a choke of collapsed ice, he saw it — or saw the suggestion of it, a dark massed shape behind the jam, angular, man-made, patient. Metal. Forty years of patience.

"There," Ulyana whispered, as if the glacier were asleep. "There it is."

The ebb began early. Of course it did. The river gave them their eleven minutes and then took them back with interest, and Dev ran them out on a falling tongue of water with the ice ticking overhead like a clock winding down, and when they cleared the face he discovered his hands ached and realized he'd been strangling the wheel since minute one.

Back at camp, chalk in hand, he amended the board. Under the six tallies he wrote, in letters small enough to be cowardice: *window shorter than planned. choke at 30m. barge confirmed.*

Five tides left. Maybe fewer. One crew member gone with the truth in his pocket. A fleet at anchor downstream, and somewhere beyond it, he was sure, Fenn's people counting the same moon he was.

Marisol came to stand beside him at the board. "Well?" she said. "Tell me the number."

Dev looked at the tallies, and at the river running out dark and fast toward the sea, carrying everything it kept and everything it took.

"Enough," he said. "If we spend them perfectly."

Nobody answered, because nobody believed in perfect spending — least of all him. Out past the moraine, unseen, the first spring tide of the exiles' own count was already falling, and whatever they decided it meant, they would act on it soon.
