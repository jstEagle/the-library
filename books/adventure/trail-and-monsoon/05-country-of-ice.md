## 5. Country of Ice

The glacier did not want us, and it made that plain from the first hour.

We left the river at dawn, where the outflow flattened into braids of gray water and the moraine began — a country of broken stone that some older, crueler god had dumped across the ice and then abandoned. Ulyana Oyelaran's boots found no rhythm in it. Every step was a negotiation: rock rolling underfoot, slate edges like dropped knives, meltwater seeping up between stones in cold veins. Behind her, Dev swore steadily in three languages, a metronome of complaint that meant he was fine.

Ahead, Ilma moved like she'd been born on this ground.

She probably had been. Kestrian highlands, Ulyana remembered from the manifest's marginalia — before there was no such thing as Kestrian highlands, because there was no such thing as Kestria. Forty years of erasure, and here was its daughter, walking a glacier in another nation's shadow, carrying her dead country's coordinates in her head.

"Watch the gray ice," Ilma called back, not turning. "White ice holds. Gray ice is crying."

"Crying," Dev repeated flatly. "Lovely. The glacier has feelings."

"It's melting," Ilma said. "That's what I mean."

They climbed. The Meridian rose ahead of them for most of the morning like a wall built by something with too much time — blue at its base, white above, streaked with the black lines of ancient ash falls, all of it groaning at intervals with sounds Ulyana felt in her sternum rather than heard. She had chased storms across two continents. She knew the sound of large things deciding to move. This was that sound, slowed down by a factor of ten thousand, which somehow made it worse.

Her satellite phone buzzed against her hip at the second rest stop. She checked it out of habit and immediately wished she hadn't. A message from Fenn. No threat in it — Fenn never threatened; he *offered*, cheerfully, the way weather offered hail. Just coordinates and a single line:

*Third tide is your window. After that, the route closes for the season. We're watching your progress with admiration.*

*We.* Ulyana looked back down the valley. Far below, tiny as spilled rice, the rival fleet's camp sat along the riverbank — six vessels, cranes folded like sleeping insects, and among them, she was almost certain, the glint of glass aimed uphill. The exiles' fleet. The people who had hired Ilma. The people who wanted what was in the glacier just as badly as the Syndicate did, and who would be far less polite about it than a smiling man with a debt ledger.

She pocketed the phone without answering.

"You should eat," Ilma said, appearing beside her with the silence of someone raised to move quietly over other people's ruins. She held out a strip of dried fish. "The ice takes more from you than you notice."

Ulyana took it. It tasted of salt and smoke and nothing else, which was honest food. "You've done this crossing before."

"Twice." Ilma crouched, tracing a line in the gravel with one finger. "Once when I was nineteen, with my father. He wanted to see the Meridian before—" She stopped. Redrew the line. "He said glaciers are the only honest borders left. They move, but they tell you they're moving."

"And your father?"

"Gone." The word was level, finished, a door closed thirty years ago. "Like everything else with a Kestrian name on it."

Ulyana chewed the fish and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't a lie or an intrusion. Around them the wind came down off the ice field in long, cold breaths, and somewhere beneath their feet, deep in the blue dark, a diplomatic barge waited with a government folded inside it like a letter never delivered.

The crevasse field opened in front of them just past noon, and it looked, at first, like nothing at all.

That was the trap of it. Snow bridges spanned the cracks so cleanly that the whole slope read as one unbroken white page. Only the shadows gave it away — faint blue-gray seams running diagonally, like veins under skin. Ilma stopped them forty meters short and roped them together herself, checking each knot twice, then a third time, her hands quick and certain.

"We cross in line. My spacing. Nobody improvises." She looked at Ulyana last, and longest. "You're heaviest. You go third, behind me, in my steps exactly. Dev anchors."

"I'm not *heavy*—" Dev began.

"You're carrying the drill kit," Ulyana said. "You're heavy."

The first hundred meters were almost pleasant. The sun was warm on the snow, the rope slack between them, and the glacier's groaning had settled into a low, conversational mutter. Ulyana let herself believe, briefly, that the worst part of this job would be the paperwork afterward.

Then the world opened under her right foot.

There was no crack, no warning shot. One moment the snow was snow; the next it was a lid, and she was through it, and the light went from white to a blue so deep and total it seemed to have weight. She fell maybe four meters before the rope snapped taut and her shoulder nearly left its socket, and then she was hanging — swinging in slow, sick arcs over a shaft that went down farther than her headlamp could argue with. Meltwater ticked somewhere below, patient as arithmetic.

Above her, muffled by the bridge she'd punched through, shouting. Dev's voice. Ilma's voice cutting through his, giving numbers, giving orders. The rope burned across the broken edge of the hole, sawing into old snow that was already starting to give way in small, whispering collapses around the lip.

Ulyana did the only useful thing available to her, which was nothing. She spread her weight, gripped the rope with both hands, and hung in the blue silence while the people above decided whether she lived.

It occurred to her, with strange clarity, that this was the entire shape of her life lately. Hanging. Waiting on other people's knots. The Syndicate holding one end of her rope, the salvage contract holding the other, and underneath her always the same bottomless blue — sixty days, sixty days, sixty days — except now the abyss had a name and a seal on it, a double star stamped in lead, and everyone she owed and everyone who owed her was hauling on the same line.

The rope jerked. Slid a half-meter. Jerked again.

"ANCHOR'S SET!" Dev's roar, cracked with effort. "ILMA, GO!"

And then Ilma was coming down the rope toward her — actually *down*, hand over hand, into the hole, boots braced against the ice wall, descending past her into the cold with the calm of someone walking downstairs. Her face, when she reached Ulyana's level, was composed. Not calm — composed. There was a difference, and Ulyana recognized it, because she wore the same expression every time Fenn smiled at her.

"Harness loop," Ilma said. "Clip to mine. Now."

Ulyana clipped. Their eyes met at a distance of half a meter, suspended over nothing.

"Why?" Ulyana asked. It came out rougher than she intended. "Your fleet wants the chest. I'm the thing standing between you and an exclusive. Letting me drop solves a lot of problems."

Ilma's breath fogged between them. Below, the meltwater ticked. Above, Dev strained against the anchor, and the snow bridge shed its small white whispers.

"Because my father taught me knots," Ilma said, "and the state that erased us taught me everything else. I decide which lessons I keep." She kicked off from the wall. "Climb."

They climbed. It took eleven minutes that Ulyana would remember for years — the rope burning, the ice wall slick as glass, Ilma calling footholds in a low, even voice, Dev hauling above until his arms shook. When Ulyana finally rolled out onto the white surface and lay gasping under the ordinary sun, she laughed once, a short ugly bark of relief, and Ilma stood over her and did not laugh at all.

"The barge," Ilma said.

Ulyana squinted up at her. "What about it?"

"You've been thinking of it as cargo. Salvage. Something with a price." Ilma pulled her gloves off, flexed her fingers, put them back on. "My father was a courier for the government-in-exile. The last legitimate one — elected, seated, recognized by nine nations before the maps changed. When Kestria fell, they didn't just lose territory. They lost the *record*. Treaties, land claims, the names of every official who signed the transfer, the testimony of who brokered what and for whose benefit. Forty years of exile governments arguing over photocopies." She looked north, up the ice, toward the blue tunnel where the barge slept. "The originals went on that barge. Sealed. Escorted. And then the escort reported it lost, and the nation that licensed the escort became very rich in the districts that used to be ours, and nobody asked questions, because asking questions required being a country, and we had stopped being one."

The wind moved across the snowfield. Somewhere below them, faint but distinct, the glacier spoke — a long, grinding syllable, like a giant turning over in sleep.

"So it's not treasure," Ulyana said slowly.

"It's a witness." Ilma turned back to her, and for the first time since the river, Ulyana saw the grief underneath the composure — not worn openly, but structural, load-bearing, the way a keel sits under a boat. "Every person who could testify is dead or scattered. The documents aren't proof of a crime, Oyelaran. They're proof of a *country*. That it existed. That it was stolen properly, with signatures, by people who are still in power." Her jaw tightened. "My fleet's backers want it to win restitution in court. Your creditors want it to stay buried. And you—" she almost smiled, "—you want your truck."

"My truck is a very good truck," Ulyana said, because if she didn't say something small and stupid she was going to say something true, and the truth was that she was lying on a glacier above a drowned nation, holding a rope-burn on each palm, feeling the exact weight of a grief that wasn't hers and understanding it anyway. She had no homeland to lose. But she knew what it was to have something taken and priced by strangers.

Dev limped over, rubbing his anchor arm, and looked from one woman to the other. "I hate to interrupt the moment," he said, "but the tide chart doesn't care about any of this. Third surge hits the outflow at 0400 tomorrow. If we're not at the ice cave mouth by then—"

"Then the season closes and the barge keeps its secrets until the ice gives it up on its own terms," Ilma finished. "In twenty years. Maybe thirty."

"Then we walk," Ulyana said.

She got up. Her shoulder throbbed where the rope had caught it; her palms stung; the abyss was still down there under the snow, patient, unhurried, waiting for the next person who trusted the wrong bridge. She looked up the glacier toward the seam of blue in the white, where the meltwater had carved its tunnel, where a barge full of testimony waited behind forty years of ice.

They walked.

They made good distance through the afternoon, following Ilma's line along the medial moraine, and it was near dusk, with the ice glowing amber and then violet around them, that Dev — ranging ahead with the binoculars — stopped, went very still, and passed the glasses back without a word.

Ulyana raised them. High on the ridge to the east, backlit by the last of the sun, tents had bloomed. Six of them. And beside them, catching the light, the long thin shapes of ice anchors already set, and figures moving with the unhurried confidence of people who believed the mountain belonged to them.

Not the exiles' fleet. These colors were wrong. These were charter colors — clean, corporate, new rope.

Someone had bought their way onto the glacier ahead of them.

And taped to the nearest tent pole, facing downslope, deliberately, so that anyone climbing from the river would see it through glass at a thousand meters, fluttered a single sheet of paper with a familiar cheerful signature at the bottom.

Fenn had sent company.
