## 6. The Fleet at the Moraine

Ilma Vask had learned to read camps the way other people read weather.

From the ridge above the outwash plain, the rival fleet looked almost gentle in the late light — six hulls drawn up along the shingle bar like sleeping animals, cook-smoke standing straight in the windless cold, a string of work lamps coming on one by one as the dark came down off the glacier. She counted cranes. Three. Counted winch trucks. Four. Counted men moving between the fires, and stopped counting at thirty because the number stopped mattering after that. Thirty was enough. Thirty was a verdict.

"They're closer than yesterday," she said.

Ulyana stood beside her with a tin cup of something that pretended to be coffee, steam curling off it into the blue dusk. She didn't answer right away. Ilma had learned that about her in the four days since the crevasse — that Ulyana Oyelaran held silence the way other people held tools, and picked it up only when it was useful.

"They moved up to the second bar," Ulyana said finally. "That's tidal water now. When the surge comes, their anchors will be sitting in a river that thinks it's an ocean."

"Then they know the tides too."

"Everybody knows the tides. Knowing them and respecting them are different trades." She drank, grimaced at the cup as if it had betrayed her personally. "That's the *Meridian Star* at the north end. I'd know that crane anywhere. Halvard Sørholt's rig."

Ilma went still. "You know him."

"I salvaged with him for two seasons, back when I still believed salvage was a trade and not a debt collection. He taught me how to read ice under snow." A pause. "He also taught me that everyone has a price, and his is lower than most people think."

"Which is?"

"He never told me. That was the lesson." She set the cup down on a stone. "Get some sleep, Ilma. Tomorrow the river starts talking, and we'll want to be listening."

Ilma did not sleep. She climbed to the ridge instead, wrapped in the expedition's one good blanket, and watched the fleet's fires burn down to coals, and thought about a country that existed now only in the way she pronounced certain vowels. Forty years since the maps were redrawn. Forty years since the barge *Vesperine* went into the Meridian Glacier with her government's last legitimate records sealed in its hold — and with the names of the men who had sold it, which was the part the world had never been allowed to read.

Her grandmother had taught her the double-star seal by drawing it in spilled tea on a tabletop. *This was ours,* she'd said. *Remember it, because no one else will.*

Ilma remembered. That was the whole of her job now, and the whole of her country: a seal drawn in tea, a manifest in a lead-lined chest, and six tides of melting water standing between her and the truth.

* * *

The sabotage happened on the third night, and Ilma found the first cut anchor line at dawn.

She had gone down to the shingle to check the water gauge — a plank with notches she'd driven into the bar herself, because the exiles' money had bought her a scout's contract but not a scout's equipment. The river was low between surges, braided and shallow, and the fleet's nearmost vessel, a converted trawler called the *Kestrel's Own* — someone aboard had a sense of history or a sense of cruelty, Ilma couldn't decide which — sat heeled over on its anchor lines like a drunk against a fence.

Except one of the lines wasn't holding anything anymore.

It lay in the shallows, and it hadn't frayed. Frayed line told you a story of wear, of chafe against stone, of a slow honest failure. This line had been cut, and cut recently, and cut by someone who knew rope: a single clean pass, the strands still bright where the blade had gone through, no fuzzing, no argument. The severed end drifted in the current like a thing waving goodbye.

She crouched over it for a long time, the cold coming up through her knees.

Behind her, gravel crunched. Dev — the crewmate, the quiet one, the one who did the arithmetic — stood at the edge of the bar with his hands in his pockets, looking at the cut line with an expression she couldn't file anywhere.

"That's the third one," he said.

"You've seen the others."

"I've seen the others." He crouched beside her, not touching the rope. "Starboard line on the *Kestrel's Own*, and the kedge on the trawler behind her. Somebody's been busy at night."

"Your people?"

Dev looked at her then, and his face did something complicated. "Our people are four tired salvagers and a scout who doesn't sleep. You think one of us is swimming the outflow in water that would stop your heart in ninety seconds?" He shook his head. "It's not us. But it's not them either — why would Sørholt cut his own anchors?"

"Because he wouldn't." Ilma stood. Her mind was moving fast now, the way it did when a country's whole history balanced on a rope's end. "But the Syndicate would. Or whoever hired the fleet would rather it fail *before* it reaches the ice than share what it finds. A wrecked expedition can't testify."

Dev was quiet a moment. "You sound like you've seen this before."

"I've read this before. In the records we do have." She looked upriver, toward the pale wall of the Meridian, blue-white in the morning haze. "Kestria didn't die of weather, Dev. It died of men who preferred it drowned. This is just the same river, forty years on."

He absorbed that. Then: "Ulyana needs to know. And then we need to decide whether to warn them."

"Warn them?" Ilma almost laughed. "They came to take what's ours."

"They came to take what somebody's paying for. That's different." He was already walking back toward camp. "And if their ships pile up in the surge with dead men tangled in the lines, that's not justice, that's just weather with a body count. Come on."

* * *

Ulyana heard them out without expression, which Ilma was beginning to understand was her version of shouting.

Then she said, "Show me," and the three of them went down to the bar, and Ulyana handled the cut line the way a doctor handles evidence of a disease she'd hoped never to see again. She turned the severed end in her scarred fingers. She looked at the fleet, at the water, at the sky, doing whatever arithmetic salvagers did instead of praying.

"It's not Sørholt," she said at last. "His people check their gear like priests. If a line was cut, he'd have found it at inspection." She dropped the rope. "Which means someone's cutting them *after* inspection, which means someone's watching this camp closer than we're watching theirs."

"The Syndicate," Ilma said.

"Maybe. Or the people who hired the fleet, cleaning up their own investment. Or a third party we haven't met yet." Ulyana's jaw tightened. "Here's what I know. The next surge is the third tide. It comes up that channel" — she pointed at the braided throat of the river where it narrowed between the bars — "and everything on the second bar takes the full push. If the *Kestrel's Own* is down two lines when it hits, she'll swing broadside, and the river will fold her around that rock like paper."

Dev said, "We could warn them."

"We could." Ulyana looked at the fleet for a long time. The work lamps were coming on again, evening coming early under the glacier's shadow, and smoke rose from the cook fires, and somewhere over there men were laughing at something — Ilma could hear it, faint and human, carried on the cold air. Men who wanted to take her country's bones to the highest bidder. Men who also, presumably, had mothers.

"They'd ask why we knew," Ulyana said. "And we'd have to tell them about the sabotage, and then they'd know someone wants this wreck failed, and they'd start asking *who*, and the answer is somebody with enough money to hire thirty men and cut lines in freezing water at night." She exhaled. "And then it stops being a salvage race and becomes a war, and we are four tired salvagers and a scout."

"So we do nothing," Ilma said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't quite an accusation, but it sat between them like a stone.

Ulyana looked at her. "We do what we can live with. There's a difference, and the river's going to teach it to us tonight."

* * *

The third tide came up the valley like something with intentions.

Ilma stood on the high moraine with the others and watched the outflow rise — not gradually, the way rivers were supposed to, but in a wall of gray-green water carrying its own weather, its own sound, a sound like a mountain clearing its throat. The fleet had seen it coming; she could see figures running along the *Kestrel's Own*'s deck, could see the engines turn over, could see — and her stomach dropped — the ship swing as the surge took its remaining lines and began, patiently, to unwrap them from their anchors.

Two lines gone. One holding. The math was simple and it was murder.

The ship swung broadside. The river hit her amidships.

What happened next took eleven seconds and Ilma would remember all of them for the rest of her life. The last anchor line parted with a crack she felt in her teeth. The *Kestrel's Own* went around, stern-first, toward the rock Ulyana had named, and men were screaming now, not laughing, and one of them — a big man in a yellow foul-weather suit, hauling on a wheel that no longer answered to anything — went over the side as the deck tilted, and the river took him the way it took everything, without malice and without negotiation.

"Ulyana—" Dev started.

She was already moving.

Ilma watched her go down the moraine at a dead sprint, watch her kick the truck into gear and drive it — *drove it*, the madwoman — down onto the shingle bar with the surge chewing at the wheels, and throw the winch cable into the water with a throw-weight that must have taken ten years off her shoulder. The cable paid out. The river pulled. For one long, impossible moment nothing happened, and Ilma understood with total clarity that she was watching a woman spend her rig — her rig, the thing she'd taken a salvage job up a glacier to protect — on the life of a man who had come to rob her.

Then the cable went taut, the truck screamed, and the river gave up its argument.

They pulled him out at the tail of the bar, forty meters downstream of where the *Kestrel's Own* crumpled around the rock with a sound like the end of the world. He was big, gray-faced, coughing the river out of himself in long tearing spasms, and when Ilma reached them he had one fist knotted in Ulyana's jacket and his eyes wide open and fixed on her face.

"Sørholt," he rasped. "Sørholt's not — he's not the one paying. You have to — the license — *check the license*—" And then he folded forward, shivering, done.

Ulyana held him up and looked at Ilma over his shoulder, and her face in the work-lamp light was the face of a woman doing new arithmetic.

"Get him to the truck," she said. "Warm him up. And Ilma — when he can talk again, I want every word."

Ilma pulled the man's arm across her shoulders. Behind them the wreck groaned against the rock, and up the valley, under the first stars, the Meridian Glacier stood blue and silent, keeping what it kept.

The license. She turned the words over as she walked, and felt the shape of something underneath them, cold and waiting.

Whoever had hired the fleet had done it with paper. And paper, unlike rivers, could be read.
