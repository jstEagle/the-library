## 7. Third Tide, Rising

The third tide came up the outflow like a held breath finally released.

Ulyana felt it before she heard it — a tremor through the soles of her boots, a shiver in the meltwater pooling at the tunnel mouth. Then the sound arrived: a deep, arterial pulse rolling down from the coast, the ocean pushing inland into the glacier's throat the way it had pushed for ten thousand years, patient as arithmetic.

"Water's rising," Dev called from the rope line behind her. "Forty minutes of headroom, maybe fifty. After that the lower passage drowns and we swim home or we don't."

"Fifty's plenty," Ulyana said, and did not look back at him, because if she looked back she would see the doubt on his face and she would borrow some of it, and she could not afford to carry anything heavier than her lamp.

They went in.

* * *

The ice tunnel was not a tunnel so much as a vein — a blue corridor the glacier had melted through its own body over centuries, walls polished to a glassy translucence that turned their headlamps into auroras. Light entered the ice and refused to leave. It bent, pooled, lingered, so that walking through it felt like walking inside a memory that hadn't decided yet whether to keep you.

Ilma moved ahead with the certainty of someone who had dreamed this passage before she ever saw it. She'd grown up on stories of the Meridian, Ulyana had learned — exile children raised on maps of a country that no longer existed, taught the shape of glaciers they'd never touch. Kestria lived on in bedtime geography. That was either the saddest thing Ulyana had ever heard or the most stubborn, and she still hadn't decided.

"Pressure ridge ahead," Ilma said. "We go single file, left wall."

The ridge was a fist of ancient ice thrust up through the floor, veined with ash from some eruption decades dead. They climbed it hand over hand, crampons biting, and from its crest Ulyana saw down the length of the vein — and saw, half-buried in the ice two hundred meters on, a shape that stopped her breath.

It was long. It was wooden. It was lying on its side like a whale that had beached itself inside the mountain and been slowly swallowed for it.

"Oh," Dev said softly, beside her. Just that. *Oh.* The way people say it when they've spent their whole lives around wrecks and rust and salvage, and then see something that isn't any of those things. Something preserved.

"The *Vessa Kestri*," Ilma whispered. Her voice cracked on the second word, the old name spoken aloud for maybe the first time in forty years inside the country that had erased it.

They descended toward it in silence.

* * *

The barge had gone into the ice stern-first, driven by whatever surge or avalanche had taken it the night Kestria died. Forty years of glacial flow should have crushed it to matchwood, but the ice here moved slow and gentle, cradling rather than chewing. The hull was deformed — one whole flank stove inward where the pressure had found it — but the superstructure survived, and along its side, beneath a skin of clear ice like cellophane, letters were still legible:

**VESSEL OF THE LEGATION OF KESTRIA**

Diplomatic barge. Floating embassy. The last legitimate government of a drowned nation, carrying its archives to safety, and the ice had caught it mid-flight and kept it, the way amber keeps an insect.

Ulyana put her palm against the hull. Cold bit through her glove instantly, honestly, without malice. "Dev. Cutting gear."

"You're sure we have time?"

"We have fifty minutes and we're going to use forty-nine of them."

He grinned despite himself — that was Dev, fear converted directly into appetite — and unslung the thermal lance while Ilma began mapping the hull with her gloved hands, reading it like braille, finding the seams. "Here," she said. "The archive hold is amidships, below the waterline. Sealed compartment. Lead-lined." She looked up, and in the lamplight her face was stripped of everything except the thing underneath. "My grandmother helped pack those crates. She told me. I thought she was telling me a story."

"Grandmothers mostly are," Ulyana said. "Doesn't mean they're lying."

The lance hissed and spat, melting a doorway through forty years of silence. Meltwater ran down the cut in bright threads. And when the slab of hull fell inward with a groan like a waking animal, the smell that came out was impossibly, heartbreakingly ordinary — paper, ink, canvas, wood. A library's smell. A country's smell.

Their lamps swept across rows of lead-lined crates, strapped and stenciled, every seal intact.

And in the center of the hold, wedged upright between two crates as if placed there by a careful hand, a strongbox the size of a sea chest, banded in steel, stamped with the double-star seal of Kestria.

"That's it," Ilma breathed. "That's the chancellor's dispatch box. That's — everything."

Ulyana hauled it free. It weighed what truth weighs, which is more than gold and less than water. She got it onto her shoulder harness, cinched it down, and was turning to call the retreat when her lamp caught something else — a document case lying open on the deck beside the crates, its contents spilled and frozen mid-scatter, papers fused to the steel deck in a fan, as though someone had been reading them when the end came.

She knelt. She shouldn't have. There was no time. She knelt anyway.

The top sheet was a memorandum, typed, on letterhead she recognized only because she'd seen its modern descendant a hundred times — on the salvage licenses pinned to the fleet office wall, on the court order Fenn had waved at them across a folding table. The Hydrographic Authority. The licensing state. The government whose stamps made salvage legal and illegal, whose registry decided who owned what the river gave up.

The memo was dated eleven days before Kestria fell.

She read it twice, because the first time her mind refused the plain meaning of the words. It was a schedule. A coordination of schedules — naval movements, border closures, the withdrawal of the hydrographic survey vessels that were supposed to be escorting the legation barge downriver. Signed off in the margin in pencil, in a neat bureaucratic hand, with a single word:

*Confirmed.*

"They weren't lost," Ulyana said. Her voice came out flat, strange, underwater. "The escort wasn't late. It was recalled. On purpose. Someone in the licensing ministry told the navy to stand down and let the barge go into the flood alone."

Ilma crossed the hold in three strides and read over her shoulder, and Ulyana heard the sound she made — small, involuntary, the sound of a person being handed the corpse of a story they'd carried since childhood. Not *the storm took them.* Not *the war was chaos, and chaos has no address.* An address. A signature. A pencil note in a tidy hand.

"They knew," Ilma said. "The state that now sells salvage licenses on this river — they sold Kestria first. We've been racing the Syndicate for treasure, and it was never—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "It was never treasure. It's testimony. Every crate in this hold is a witness, and the murder weapon is signed."

Below them, faint but unmistakable, the glacier groaned — a long tectonic complaint, the sound of a mass the size of a mountain range remembering it can move.

"Tide's peaking," Dev said from the cut. "We need to be moving *now*, Ulya. Whatever you found, it keeps. Ice keeps things. That's the whole problem with ice."

But Ulyana was still looking at the fanned papers, and her lamp had found one more — a shipping manifest, commercial, private-sector letterhead, listing cargo received at a river port three days after the fall. Salvage rights, pre-assigned. To a company whose name had changed twice since, but whose registration number she knew, because it was the parent number under which the current salvage fleet — the exiles' rivals, the ones camped on the moraine — held its license.

The same fleet. The same bloodline of ownership, running unbroken from the betrayal to this morning.

"They didn't lose the thread," she said. "They've been holding it for forty years. Waiting for the glacier to give it back."

"Ulyana." Dev's hand on her arm, hard. "Tide's turning. We go or we drown in a library."

She took the memorandum. Carefully, with a salvager's hands, she freed the frozen sheet from the deck, rolled it, sealed it in a waterproof sleeve against her chest, next to her skin where the cold couldn't reach it. Then she stood, adjusted the strongbox harness, and gave the hold one last look — all those intact seals, all those witnesses waiting patiently in the dark for a country that no longer existed to come collect them.

"We'll come back for the rest," she told them. It wasn't a plan. It was a promise, and she had no idea yet to whom she was making it.

They climbed out through the cut as the meltwater came licking up the lower passage, black and quickening. Behind them the blue vein of the tunnel filled with rising light, gorgeous and indifferent, and somewhere far above, transmitted down through a kilometer of ancient ice, came a sound none of them mistook for the tide.

Engines. Aircraft engines, descending toward the moraine.

Fenn had found them.

And now he wasn't offering to buy the chest. Now, Ulyana understood, climbing into the light with a nation's confession strapped to her back, he would be coming to take it — with lawyers, with muscle, with the full weight of a state that had forty years of practice making evidence disappear into cold places.

The tide was going out. The route home was closing.

And the sky over the glacier was filling with the wrong kind of birds.
