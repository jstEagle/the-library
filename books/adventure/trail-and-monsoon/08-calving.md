## 8. Calving

I heard the glacier before I saw it fail.

That's the thing nobody tells you about ice under pressure — it doesn't crack like wood or groan like a ship. It *sings*. A high, thin note that lives in your back teeth, in the fillings of your jaw, and then it drops an octave into something you feel through the soles of your boots before your ears ever catch it. I'd been on the outwash plain for three days running the pump lines to our meltwater sump, and I knew the Meridian's voice the way you know a friend's footsteps on a stairwell. So when the singing started at four in the morning, two octaves above anything I'd heard from her before, I was out of my bunk with my boots half-laced before Ulyana even stirred.

"Dev?" Her voice came muffled through the tent canvas. "What time is it?"

"Early."

"Early bad or early interesting?"

I stood outside in the gray pre-dawn light and looked up at the face of the glacier, a kilometer of blue-white wall holding back the weight of a mountain range, and I watched a seam I'd been pretending not to notice all week open its mouth another handspan wide.

"Interesting," I said. "Interesting-bad."

* * *

By full light the whole camp was on the moraine, and the argument had already gone around twice. The plan had been simple — simple the way plans are when you make them warm and fed: five tidal surges left before calving season opened the ice route properly, and we needed one good surge to run the gear barge up the outflow channel to the cave mouth where Ulyana and Ilma had found the wreck. Third tide had given us the strongbox and the documents. Fourth tide was supposed to be extraction. Clean arithmetic. Six tides, we'd said back at base camp, and Dev will do the sums because Dev is the only one of us who can do sums without crying.

Except the glacier had stopped doing arithmetic with us.

"The meltwater lake behind the eastern lip is rising faster than the channel can drain it," I told them, kneeling in the gravel with a stick, drawing what I'd seen. "That seam I flagged on Tuesday has widened maybe forty centimeters overnight. When that lip lets go, everything upstream comes down the outflow at once. Not a tide. A *flush*."

"So we move extraction up," Ulyana said. She'd been awake for thirty hours and looked like it, salt-cracked lips, eyes red from the ice tunnel's glare, but her voice had that flat steadiness she got when things were worst. "We run the barge on the next surge instead of waiting."

"The next surge is marginal," I said. "Sixty percent of what we need to float her loaded."

"Then we take less weight."

"We take *less time*, is what we take." I drove the stick into the gravel. "Ulyana, if we're mid-channel when that lip goes—"

"I know what happens if we're mid-channel when the lip goes." She looked up at the glacier, and I watched her do the thing she does, the thing that makes her impossible to work for and impossible to leave — she took the fear and folded it into something small enough to carry. "Marginal surge. Light load. We go on the fifth tide whether we're ready or not."

Which was when the airplane came over the ridge.

* * *

It was a white turboprop with no markings except a registration number, and it circled the valley once with the insolent slowness of something that owned the air it breathed, then put down on the frozen shallows of the lower lake with a spray of ice chips. Six people climbed out. Five of them were big and dressed alike, in matching expedition shells, moving with the loose readiness of men paid to be ready. The sixth wore a charcoal coat that had never been near weather until this trip, and he smiled at us across two hundred meters of ice the way Mr. Fenn smiled at everyone — warmly, patiently, like a man greeting old friends at a funeral he'd arranged.

"He brought lawyers' paper and muscle," Ilma said beside me. She'd come down from the scout post at a run when the plane appeared, and her breath was still ragged. "That's not a negotiation party. That's a seizure party."

Fenn stopped at a respectful distance and held up a document wallet like a man showing credentials at a border. "Miss Oyelaran! Mr. Adeyemi! Wonderful to see you both looking so — resilient." His eyes went past us to the gear barge, to the strongbox crated and lashed amidships, and the smile deepened by a degree. "I'll keep this brief, since I know how you value brevity. There's a provisional admiralty order, signed yesterday, placing the wreck of the diplomatic vessel *Kestrian Star* and all contents under state receivership pending litigation. This gentleman—" he indicated one of the five, who produced a tablet with the document glowing on it, "—is a court officer. These other gentlemen are here to ensure the order is respected. You have until the end of today to surrender the recovered materials. In exchange, the Syndicate is prepared to be generous regarding certain outstanding obligations."

"You mean my debt," Ulyana said.

"I mean *your* debt, yes." Fenn's voice stayed pleasant. "All of it. Forgiven. A clean ledger, Miss Oyelaran. How long has it been since you had one?"

I watched her hands. That's how you read Ulyana — not her face, which lies beautifully, but her hands, which hang still when she's calm and go quiet-still when she's furious. They were quiet-still now.

"That wreck isn't salvage," she said. "It's testimony. Forty years of murdered people's paperwork. And you want me to trade it for a receipt."

"I want you to trade it for your *life*, in the financial sense," Fenn said, and for just a moment the warmth dropped away and I saw the machine underneath, the cheerful enforcer machinery of the Vantablack Syndicate, all its teeth showing. "The court order is real. The fleet anchored below your camp is real. The tide schedule is real. Everything is real, Miss Oyelaran, except your options. Surrender the box. Keep the truck. Live."

And that was when the glacier sang again — loud this time, a tearing chord that made every man in Fenn's party flinch and grab for nothing — and the eastern lip of the meltwater lake began, with terrible unhurried grace, to come apart.

* * *

I want to describe it accurately, because later there were arguments about what happened, and accuracy matters more than anyone's version.

The lip didn't explode. It *slumped* — a section of ice the size of a chapel roof tilting forward into the lake with a sound like the world clearing its throat, and the lake stood up. Not a wave exactly; more like the whole surface lifting, gathering itself, and then the outflow channel simply ceased to exist as a channel and became a river in flood, brown water carrying ice chunks the size of trucks, all of it pouring down toward the lower anchorage where the exile fleet lay at anchor.

Their lead vessel — the *Alder's Promise*, a converted trawler with forty years of patched hull and a skipper named Rask who'd hated us politely for two weeks — was directly in the path.

I saw it happen in pieces. The flood front coming down the valley, maybe six meters high, rolling. The fleet's anchor watch scrambling. Rask's engine starting too late, the *Promise* swinging on her chain broadside to the current. And then the water hit, and the trawler was driven sideways onto the shoal at the channel's edge and pinned there, listing hard, her deck awash as the flood kept feeding through, her crew — eleven people, I counted, eleven — clinging to the superstructure while the river tried to peel their ship off the rocks and drown them in the process.

Fenn's men were already moving back toward the plane. Court orders, apparently, don't cover acts of God.

"Dev," Ulyana said. "The winch line. Can we reach them?"

I did the sums. Everyone thinks I'm being slow when I pause, but I'm not being slow, I'm being honest. "Not from here. Current's too strong for a throw line, and if we bring the barge anywhere near that shoal she's pinned too." I looked at the water, at the *Promise* grinding against the rock with each pulse of the flood, and I did the sums again hoping they'd change. They didn't. "There's one way. The high-water shelf on the north bank runs to within thirty meters of her bow. Somebody crosses the shelf, takes a line across on the throw gun, and they walk her crew off along it while the flood lasts. Maybe ninety minutes before the surge peaks. After that the shelf goes under."

"Then somebody better be fast," Ulyana said, and started pulling on her harness.

"No." Ilma's voice. Quiet, the way she always was, which meant everyone listened. She was already dressed for the ice, rope coiled over her shoulder, the throw gun in her hands. "You're the claimant, Ulyana. If you die, the documents die with whatever legal standing you gave them. And you—" she nodded at me, almost smiling, "—are the only one who can run the pumps and keep this camp off the flood line. But I'm the scout. Crossing broken ground fast is the entire job description." She checked the gun's charge with a click. "Also, they're my people. Whatever Rask thinks of me, they're Kestrians, and I'm not watching Kestrians drown from a dry hillside."

"Ilma—" Ulyana started.

"Ninety minutes," Ilma said. "Count them for me, Dev. If I'm not back when the shelf goes under, don't come after me. Come after the truth. That's the whole point of me."

She was moving before Ulyana could answer, dropping down the moraine in that flowing way she had, like water finding its own level, and I keyed the radio and started counting.

* * *

Here is what ninety minutes sounds like from the wrong side of a flood.

It sounds like wind. It sounds like the pump engines I ran at full throttle to keep our sump ahead of the rise, and Ulyana shouting coordinates into the radio, and Fenn's plane lifting off without anyone aboard it looking back. Mostly it sounds like the river — a sound with no bottom to it, the sound of a mountain learning to be a sea.

Ilma crossed the shelf in eleven minutes. We watched her through binoculars, a dark figure against gray water, and when she reached the point nearest the *Promise*'s bow she knelt, braced the throw gun against her shoulder, and fired the line across the churning gap. First shot fell short. Second shot caught the trawler's rail and held.

Then we watched her stand on that drowning shelf and wave eleven strangers across a rope, one at a time, in a flood, in weather going bad, and every single one of them went — including Rask, who paused at the rail with his ship dying under him and then took the rope like a man accepting a hand he'd sworn he'd never need.

Eighty-one minutes after she started, Ilma came back up the moraine with twelve people behind her, soaked to the sternum, frost already forming in her hair, and behind her the flood took the last of the *Alder's Promise*'s freeboard and rolled her slowly, gently, completely onto her beam ends.

Rask stood in front of her, dripping, shaking, and said something to her in Kestrian that I didn't understand but understood anyway.

And that was when I looked up at the glacier and saw that the seam hadn't closed. It had widened. All the way down to the waterline.

"Ulyana," I said. "The fifth tide isn't coming. The whole face is coming."
