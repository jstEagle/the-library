## 9. The Weight of Proof

The sixth tide was coming, and Ulyana could feel it in her teeth.

She sat in the lee of the barge's shattered hull with the strongbox open across her knees, the glacier groaning overhead like a ship changing its mind about sinking. Blue light came down through forty meters of ancient ice and turned everything — the ice, the documents, her own hands — the color of a drowned sky. Dev crouched at the tunnel mouth with a signal mirror, watching for the fleet. Ilma was gone. Ilma had stayed behind on the fifth tide to cut the trapped rivals free, and none of them had heard her voice since.

The documents were dry. That was the miracle of it — lead-lined, wax-sealed, packed by some Kestrian clerk forty years ago who had understood exactly what he was carrying and wanted it to outlast him. Ulyana turned a page with a fingertip gone numb from cold. Signatures. Dates. A letter on Foreign Ministry letterhead from a nation that still existed, authorizing the sale of a nation that didn't.

*The Meridian Accords, private annex.* The words that proved Kestria hadn't fallen. It had been *sold* — its borders traded away in a backroom deal brokered by the very government whose salvage licenses now dotted the river like flies.

"You're reading it wrong," Dev said quietly, without turning from the tunnel mouth. "You're reading it like it's worth money."

"Everything's worth money."

"Not this." He glanced back at her. His face was frost-nipped and exhausted and absolutely certain. "This is worth *true*. There's a difference, and you know it, or you wouldn't be holding it like that."

Like what? Like an ordnance disposal tech, maybe. Like a woman holding the one object in the world that could either erase sixty days of debt or make her corpse very easy to explain.

The mirror flashed twice from the tunnel mouth. Dev's shoulders dropped. "He's coming," he said. "Fenn. Alone, looks like. Walking up the ice like he owns the weather."

"He does own the weather," Ulyana said. "We just rent it."

* * *

Mr. Fenn arrived the way he always did: smiling, unhurried, dressed in a wool coat too clean for the moraine, as if the glacier had agreed not to touch him. He stopped ten meters off and spread his hands, a man presenting a gift.

"Ulyana Oyelaran." His breath fogged. "You look terrible. Glaciers do that to people."

"Fenn."

"I'm going to make this brief, because we're standing inside something that wants to kill us, and because you've had a hard week." He nodded at the strongbox without looking at it, which was its own kind of threat — the confidence of a man who already knew what was in it. "Sixty days ago you owed my employers a sum I won't embarrass either of us by naming. As of this morning, that number has grown teeth. But I'm authorized to make it vanish. All of it. The truck, the drone, the interest, the — let's call them historical inconveniences. Gone. Debt-free, Ulyana. Free."

"And all I have to do."

"The box. Its contents, exclusive and complete. My employers become the sole custodians of the Kestrian record, and you walk out of this valley owing nothing to anyone." He smiled wider. "It's a good deal. It's the best deal you will ever be offered. And here is the part I want you to really hear: it is also the last deal. The tide comes in ninety minutes. After that, the calving season seals this route for eight months, and my employers' patience — which has been, frankly, heroic — expires with it. There is no third option. There is no clever thing. There is yes, and there is the rest of your short life."

Ulyana looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed — a raw, cracked sound that bounced off the ice and came back strange.

"You rehearsed that," she said. "In the helicopter. You practiced it in the mirror."

"I did," Fenn admitted pleasantly. "Did it work?"

"No." She stood, knees protesting, and lifted the strongbox. "Because you made one mistake. You told me there's no third option. Fenn — I'm a storm chaser. Third option is the *only* option. The first two options are what other people stand in."

Something flickered behind his smile — not fear, exactly. Recalculation. "There are six men behind me on the ice," he said gently. "And a court order in my pocket claiming this wreck for a licensed salvage operation. Whatever you're imagining—"

"I'm not imagining anything. I'm doing arithmetic." She patted the satellite uplink case slung across her back — salvaged from the barge's comm room, jury-rigged with Dev's spare battery packs over two sleepless nights. "Forty years ago, some men sold a country and buried the receipt in a glacier. They counted on the ice keeping it. They counted on everyone who cared dying off, getting tired, getting bought. And they almost got away with it, except water is honest. Water brings things up. Water doesn't take sides." She tapped the case. "So here's my counteroffer, Fenn. I don't sell this to your employers. I don't sell it to the exiles. I don't sell it to anyone. I give it away. Right now. To every exile network, every archive, every journalist with a working inbox, all at once, simultaneously, so fast that no buyer can own it and no court can un-ring the bell. You can't buy what's already everywhere."

The smile died. What was underneath it was worse — professional, patient, terribly calm. "You'd be broadcasting your own death warrant."

"I've been carrying one of those for months. At least this one would mean something." Her hands were shaking. She let them. "You think I haven't run this scene a hundred times? You offer me freedom, and I take it, and then I spend the rest of my life knowing I was the last lock on the door. That every Kestrian who kept a language alive in their kitchen for forty years got sold twice — once by the ministry, once by me. No." She shook her head. "Justice doesn't belong to whoever can afford it. Not anymore. Not today."

Fenn exhaled slowly through his nose. "Ilma Vasa is probably dead, you know. The river took three boats yesterday. Your crewmate chose the losing side of a rescue."

"She chose the right side. Those aren't always the same." Ulyana knelt, set the case on the ice, and began unbuckling it. "Dev. Antenna."

Dev was already moving, feeding the cable, his face gray but steady. "Signal's going to be ugly through the ice ceiling," he muttered. "Might take four minutes to handshake."

"We have ninety minutes," Ulyana said. "For once in my life, we have time."

* * *

Fenn didn't order his men forward. That was the thing she never forgot afterward — that he simply stood there, hands in his pockets, watching her work with the expression of a man auditing a loss he'd already written into next quarter's books. Maybe he knew what she knew: that six men couldn't cross forty meters of crevassed blue ice before a handshake completed, and that killing her wouldn't recall a broadcast any more than shooting the rain.

The uplink light blinked amber. Amber. Amber.

"Come on," Dev whispered. "Come on, you ugly beautiful piece of junk."

Amber.

Then green.

Ulyana pressed transmit, and forty years of buried country went up through the ice into the dark above the weather. Manifests. Signatures. The annex. The names of the men who had signed, two of them still alive, still powerful, their faces on currency and courthouse walls. She watched the progress bar crawl and felt something in her chest come loose that had been cinched tight since the day the Syndicate's enforcers first smiled at her across a folding table — not relief, nothing so kind as relief. More like a keel finally touching water.

"It's done," she said.

Fenn studied her for a long moment. Then, incredibly, he inclined his head — a small, genuine bow, the first honest gesture she'd ever seen him make.

"Do you know what happens now?" he asked.

"The Syndicate settles cheap rather than be named in the same document as the men who sold Kestria. You'll be back in sixty days with new numbers. Smaller ones. Politer ones."

"What happens," Fenn said, "is that you've just become the most interesting person on two continents, and my employers are not the only ones who collect debts." He turned, boots crunching, and paused at the edge of the blue light. "For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much — I hope the river keeps being honest to you, Ms. Oyelaran. It rarely is to anyone else."

Then he was gone, up the tunnel, and the glacier swallowed the sound of him.

Dev sank down beside her against the hull. Neither of them spoke. Above them the ice ticked and boomed, the sixth tide building somewhere in the dark throat of the valley, and Ulyana realized she was crying — not elegantly, not meaningfully, just leaking, cold tears freezing at her jaw.

"We're still broke," Dev said at last.

"Brokest people in the world."

"And Ilma might be—"

The mirror at the tunnel mouth flashed. Once. Twice. Three times — the pattern they'd agreed on, the one that meant *alive, coming back, bring rope.*

Dev was on his feet before she was. Ulyana grabbed the empty strongbox, then left it. It had done its work; it weighed nothing now, and she was done carrying things that belonged to dead countries.

They ran for the light together, three tides ahead of the water, toward whatever the river had decided to give back.
