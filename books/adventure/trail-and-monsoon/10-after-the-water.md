## 10. After the Water

The settlement came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Catastrophes should arrive on Fridays, at least — something with weight to the day of it. Instead Mr. Fenn's lawyer called at nine in the morning while Ulyana was elbow-deep in the truck's transmission, and she had to listen to the whole offer with grease to her wrists and a torque wrench balanced on her knee.

"Mr. Fenn is prepared to discharge your outstanding obligations in full," the lawyer said, "in exchange for a mutual non-disparagement agreement and your signature confirming that all materials recovered from the Meridian site were surrendered or destroyed."

"All materials," Ulyana repeated.

"The strongbox, its contents, any copies, derivatives, transcriptions—"

"You're asking me to sign a paper saying I never read what I read."

"I'm asking you to sign a paper saying you won't talk about it."

She looked out through the windshield at the river, low and green with glacial flour, running quiet now that calving season had passed and the Meridian had given up what it was going to give up this year. Forty years ago someone had signed papers in a warm room too, she thought. Papers that made a country stop existing. It was amazing how much history came down to ink applied by comfortable hands.

"Tell Fenn I'll take half," she said. "Half the debt, cleared. And no signature."

The silence on the line lasted long enough that she checked whether the call had dropped.

"Half is not—"

"Half," she said, "is what it costs him when his name shows up in the same broadcast as everyone else's. He knows what I kept back. A copy, timestamps, the works, sitting with three different exile networks on a dead-man switch. You want to litigate my credibility, be my guest. But every day this goes unresolved is another day he has to wonder."

Another silence. When the lawyer spoke again, his voice had changed registers entirely — the smoothness gone, replaced by something almost human, almost tired. "He'll want the non-disparagement clause regardless."

"He can have it. I don't need to disparage him. The documents do that work for free."

The call ended. She sat in the driver's seat with her hands still filthy and laughed once, a short ugly sound, because forty thousand in Syndicate debt had just become twenty thousand over a phone call, and it was the cheapest anyone had ever bought her silence, and the reason it was cheap was that her silence wasn't for sale at any price. They'd settled for the version of her they could afford: the one who'd already given everything away.

Dev found her there an hour later, still under the truck, and slid a mug of coffee along the garage floor until it bumped her boot.

"Well?" he said.

"Twenty grand left. Payable over two years if I keep taking weather contracts." She rolled out on the creeper and looked up at him. His face was doing the thing it did when he was doing arithmetic he didn't like — brow furrowed, mouth pulled sideways, like the numbers were physically heavy.

"That's four more storm seasons," he said.

"It's two, if the seasons are bad enough."

"That's not the flex you think it is, Ulya."

"It's the only flex I've got." She took the coffee. It was terrible, burnt the way Dev always burnt it, and she drank it anyway, because it was hers, made by someone who'd stayed, and there was a category of terrible things that were worth more than good ones for exactly that reason.

* * *

The restitution proceedings opened in a courthouse in a city whose name Ulyana had to look up twice — a gray northern port where the Kestrian exile community had been quietly accumulating for four decades, running schools and bakeries and a Saturday-language program nobody official had ever bothered to shut down. She watched the first session on a lagging stream, sitting between Dev and Tomas in the truck cab with the laptop propped on the dash, because the satellite signal wouldn't reach inside the camp.

On the screen, a woman with white braids stood at a podium reading names. Not the names of officials. The names of the drowned, the disappeared, the ones who'd been on the barge and the ones who'd waited on the shore for a rescue that the state had sold off before it ever launched. Ilma had told Ulyana about them on the descent from the glacier, in the flat voice she used for things too large for inflection: how the manifest listed sixty-one souls, and how the Kestrian government-in-exile had counted one hundred and four, because families don't travel as manifests, they travel as tangles, cousins and in-laws and neighbors' children folded into the record however the record would hold them.

The woman at the podium reached the end of the list and said, in accented but precise English: "We have the documents. We have the seal. We have the testimony of the salvager who recovered them, who chose to give them to us rather than to any buyer. Let the record show that Kestria did not die. Kestria was killed, and we are here to say so, in court, on paper, in the language they tried to bury."

The chat beside the video was moving too fast to read. Somewhere in it, Ulyana knew, were people who'd spent forty years believing they were the last ones who remembered. Now they were thousands, coordinated, organized, holding a legal claim older than most of the lawyers opposing it.

Tomas cleared his throat. "They're going to cite you. In the filings. As the recovering party."

"I know."

"You okay with that?"

Ulyana thought about it honestly, which was more than she usually managed. Her name attached to a nation's ghost — her face, probably, eventually, dragged into hearings, asked to testify about ice and tide and a chest fished out of brown water. It sounded awful. It also sounded like the first time in her life her name would mean something other than *owes*.

"Yeah," she said. "I'm okay with that."

* * *

The first tide of the new season came in on a morning cold enough to make the truck's breath fog silver. Ulyana drove down to the salvage flats alone at first — old habit, checking the waterline, reading the debris field the way other people read weather — and then the others arrived without being summoned, because they always did now. Dev with the thermos. Tomas with the survey drone he'd rebuilt from her wrecked one, cannibalized parts and stubbornness. Priya, who'd come aboard after the glacier, after hearing what happened, after telling Ulyana flatly that any crew crazy enough to flood a nation's secrets to the world was a crew worth shipping with.

They stood in a row above the tideline like people waiting for a train.

The surge came up the river channel the way it always did — not a wave but a swelling, a slow muscular rise, the sea reaching inland to take back what the land had borrowed. Debris turned on the flats. A length of rope. Half a crate with no markings at all, belonging to no nation, carrying nothing but water.

"No chest this year?" Priya asked.

"River gave up its one miracle," Ulyana said. "It'll sulk for a decade now."

Dev poured coffee — five cups, though there were four of them, and set the fifth on the tailgate facing the water, and nobody asked who it was for, because they all knew.

The letter had come six weeks after the calving. Forwarded twice, delayed by customs, written in pencil on paper soft as cloth. Ulyana had read it standing in the same spot she stood now, and she carried a photograph of it on her phone the way other people carried photographs of children.

*Ulyana,* it began, in handwriting that slanted hard to the right, like everything about Ilma leaned into motion.

*You will be told I died freeing that ship's crew, and you should not believe a softer version. I chose it. The rope held me until the others were clear and then the ice decided otherwise, and I want you to know there was no tragedy in it. There was arithmetic — the kind Dev does, the kind I finally learned — and the numbers came out with eight strangers alive on the wrong side of the equation, and I have never in my life gotten a sum so clean.*

*I stayed behind so that I could be the one who stayed. Do you understand? All my life I have been the one who left — left Kestria, left camps, left crews, left lovers, always moving ahead of whatever was coming. On the glacier I finally found the thing worth staying for, and it was not a country. Countries are lines drawn by men in warm rooms. It was the staying itself.*

*You flooded the truth to the world and it cost you everything you'd built your life around owning, and I think you learned the same lesson a different way: that what we keep is worthless and what we give away is the only thing that lasts. My mother used to say the river keeps what it loves. I used to think that meant drowning. Now I think it means this — the water takes the loose things and leaves the bedrock, and the bedrock is whoever stood next to you when the ice started talking.*

*Tell Dev his coffee is an abomination and I drank it gladly. Tell Tomas the drone footage of the crevasse crossing makes me look graceful, and to never correct the record.*

*As for you — you asked me once, on the moraine, why I trusted you before you'd earned it. I never answered properly. Here is the answer: because you looked at the chest and your first expression was fear and your second was wonder, and people whose wonder survives their fear are the only people worth following anywhere.*

*Kestria may come back. The lawyers are good and the documents are better. If it does, there will be maps again, and anthems, and some child born in a port city will learn a homeland she's never seen. That will be beautiful and it will not be mine. Mine is a truck that smells of solder and burnt coffee, a tide I can set my watch by, and four people who stood on a glacier and chose each other over every offer the world made them.*

*Keep the fifth cup filled. I'll know if you don't.*

*— I.*

The surge peaked and began its long withdrawal, the water sliding back toward the sea with the patience of something that had all the time in the world. On the flats, the unmarked crate bobbed once and settled, going nowhere, belonging to no one.

Ulyana picked up the fifth cup, touched it to the tailgate in a small toast, and drank it herself, because Ilma had also written, in a postscript: *and if you pour it out on the ground I will haunt you specifically.*

"To the season," Dev said.

"To the season," they echoed, and the river ran on, keeping what it loved.
