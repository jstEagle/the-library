## 2. The Optimists

Tulsa in late September smelled like hot asphalt and someone else's money. Odile Whitlock noticed that first, stepping off the Greyhound with her duffel over one shoulder and her laptop bag cutting a groove into the other—how a city could smell like wealth without ever showing you any of it. The oil money had gone vertical decades ago, up into glass towers that threw long shadows across the old brick streets, and down here at street level what remained was heat, exhaust, and the particular desperation of people trying to look like they belonged in buildings they'd never enter.

She'd dressed for the meeting the way she dressed for funerals: the one blazer she owned, black, slightly too big since she'd lost weight she couldn't afford to lose, over a shirt she'd ironed in a motel bathroom with a borrowed hair straightener. Her boots were steel-toed and salt-stained from three seasons of chasing weather across half the continent. There was nothing to be done about the boots. Vane would see them and understand exactly what he was looking at—a woman who worked for a living—and maybe that was better than pretending otherwise. Optimists, the Syndicate called themselves. They liked to know what they were betting on.

The address Casimir Vane's assistant had given her was on Boston Avenue, in a building that had been a bank once and then something else and now was something else again, all marble and brass fittings gone soft with age. The elevator smelled of cedar. On the eleventh floor, a receptionist with silver hair and reading glasses on a chain looked up at Odile as if Odile were weather herself—something to be tracked, catalogued, anticipated.

"Ms. Whitlock. Mr. Vane is expecting you. He does so love a punctual debtor."

There it was. Not *client*. Not even *borrower*. *Debtor*, said pleasantly, the way you might say *birdwatcher* or *gardener*—a hobby, an identity, a small fixed point in the universe of the Vane Syndicate's ledger. Odile smiled because there was nothing else useful to do with her face, and followed the hallway to a corner office where the windows ran floor to ceiling and the whole southern sky lay open like a promise nobody intended to keep.

Casimir Vane stood at the glass with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the clouds. He was smaller than she'd imagined—she'd built him tall out of five years of phone calls—and broader through the shoulders, silver at the temples, tanned the uniform tan of a man who golfed at clubs where the membership fee equaled her annual grant funding. When he turned, his smile arrived before the rest of his face did, warm and total, sunshine through a break in the overcast.

"Odile Whitlock." He crossed the room with his hand extended. "Five years I've been talking to your voicemail, and here you are in the flesh. You're shorter than you sound."

"You're older than you sound."

His laugh was genuine, which somehow made it worse. "Honest. Good. Sit, sit." He waved her into a leather chair angled toward the view while he poured coffee from a carafe on the sideboard—real coffee, dark and expensive-smelling—and handed her the cup like a gift. "Do you know what I was looking at when you walked in? Those cumulus towers out past Broken Arrow. Building all morning. By four o'clock there'll be hail in Creek County, mark me. Everyone else sees a pretty sky. I see capital waiting to be deployed."

"I'm not weather, Mr. Vane."

"No?" He settled into the chair opposite her, ankles crossing, entirely comfortable. "You chase storms for a university that pays you in gas money. You've logged more severe weather events than most National Weather Service offices. And for five years you have been, what is the word—*uncollectible*? No. That's not right either." He tapped his chin. "*Latent.* That's the word. Latent energy, Ms. Whitlock. A storm is just potential that hasn't found its excuse yet. So is debt. So are you."

Odile held the coffee and didn't drink it. Five years ago, fresh out of grad school with her mother's medical bills stacked like cordwood and no insurance and no inheritance except a name nobody in the family liked to say out loud, she'd signed papers she hadn't read carefully enough. The Vane Syndicate didn't lend money the way banks did. Banks wanted collateral. The Syndicate wanted *outcomes*. They were optimists, officially—they bet on people the way other firms bet on futures, wagering that a struggling scientist or a failing restaurateur or a drowning novelist would eventually become the thing their potential promised. And when the potential didn't pay out on schedule, well. Optimism, Vane liked to say, was just patience with interest.

"The balance," she said. "I'd like to hear the current number."

"Ninety-one thousand, four hundred and six dollars." He said it the way other men said good morning. "Compounding quarterly. But you didn't come all this way to hear arithmetic. You came because my office called and offered you a ladder, and a drowning woman will climb anything."

The words landed clean, no apology in them, and Odile felt the old familiar vertigo—the specific nausea of being seen completely by someone who owned a piece of you. She set the cup on the armrest so her hands would have something to do besides shake.

"What's the job?"

Vane leaned back and laced his fingers over his knee, and for a moment the salesman's warmth dropped away and she saw the thing underneath, the actuary, the bookmaker, the man who had read ten thousand contracts and forgotten none of them.

"Greywater Point," he said.

The name meant nothing. She let her face say so.

"A village," he went on, "on a stretch of coast your maps have politely stopped acknowledging. Eroded cliffs, a harbor that silted shut in the fifties, a population that dwindled to zero by attrition and then by flood. The sea has been taking it back a yard at a time for eighty years. Last full survey put the old town center under nine feet of water at high tide." His eyes glittered. "Underwater real estate, Ms. Whitlock. Which is worthless—unless something valuable went down with it."

"And something did?"

"That," Vane said, spreading his hands, "is what a salvage contract is *for*. We don't know. That's the beautiful part. We're buying uncertainty at a discount." He stood, moved to his desk, and drew a folder from a drawer—manila, thin, deceptively light. He laid it in her lap like a doctor delivering a diagnosis. "A client of ours holds title claims to the seabed parcels around Greywater Point. Claims going back to before the flood. They want the site surveyed, catalogued, and anything of value recovered and documented. Salvage law is a funny animal—finders, keepers, but only if the finding is done properly, with paperwork, with witnesses, with everything aboveboard and photographed." He smiled again, that total smile. "We need someone meticulous. Someone who documents things for a living. Someone with field experience, sonar literacy, and—" the pause was surgical, "—a strong personal incentive to complete the work."

Odile opened the folder. Inside: a survey map of a coastline she didn't recognize, tide tables, a boilerplate contract dense enough to lose a body in, and a photograph of gray water against gray cliffs, unremarkable as a stranger's vacation snapshot. Something about it snagged her anyway—the quality of the light, maybe, or the way the waves broke in a pattern that looked almost deliberate, almost like writing.

"The terms," Vane said, settling back into his chair, "are simple. You complete the survey and recovery within ninety days. Every artifact recovered becomes Syndicate property, documented and appraised. In exchange, we retire your balance in full. Clean slate. You walk away owing us nothing but a thank-you note." He raised his coffee cup in a small toast. "Potential energy, fully discharged. Everybody loves a happy ending."

"And if I don't find anything?"

"Then you'll have worked very hard for ninety days, and your debt will still be here, growing patiently, like a garden." He said it kindly. That was the trick of him, she was beginning to understand—he delivered threats in the voice of a man discussing rainfall, and kindnesses in the voice of a man discussing threats, until you couldn't tell the weather from the warning. "But you'll find something. I have an excellent feeling about this one. I'm an optimist—it's practically a religious conviction."

Odile turned the contract pages without reading them, buying time, watching him watch her. Ninety-one thousand dollars. Ninety days. A drowned town at the edge of maps. It was too neat, and she knew it was too neat, and she also knew that a woman with her options didn't get to inspect the ladder for rot before climbing it.

"Why me?" she asked. "You've got crews. Licensed salvagers. Why send a storm chaser?"

For the first time, Vane's smile flickered—not away, just sideways, like light moving across water. "Because you're thorough," he said. "Because you document. Because you have never once, in five years, missed a scheduled call, missed a payment arrangement, or tried to disappear on us—which puts you ahead of seventy percent of our portfolio, incidentally. And because Greywater Point is remote, technically demanding, and unglamorous, and the licensed salvagers all want shipwrecks with gold in them." He shrugged, expansive. "Also, frankly, because you're cheap. Your debt does most of your salary for us. Efficient. I admire efficiency the way some men admire horses."

* * *

She read the contract that night in the motel room under the buzzing yellow light, all thirty-one pages, twice, with a highlighter and a legal dictionary app she trusted more than her own exhaustion. Most of it was standard salvage boilerplate. Some of it was not.

Clause 14: *All findings, including documentary materials, correspondence, and personal effects recovered from the site, shall be surrendered to the Syndicate for appraisal and disposition.*

Documentary materials. Personal effects. If there were letters down there, diaries, photographs—eighty years of a drowned town's private paper—none of it belonged to the dead anymore. It belonged to the optimists.

Clause 22: *Contractor shall report all significant discoveries to the Syndicate within 48 hours of discovery, prior to external disclosure of any kind.*

Prior to external disclosure. Before she could tell anyone. Before a museum, a historical society, a newspaper. Whatever was down there, she would have to hand it to Vane before the world heard its name.

And clause 27, near the end, in type only marginally smaller than the rest: *In the event of contractor non-performance, obstruction, or material breach, the outstanding balance shall be subject to reassessment at the Syndicate's discretion.*

At the Syndicate's discretion. She read that sentence four times, letting it mean what it meant. Fail, obstruct, or displease, and the number wasn't fixed anymore. The number was whatever they decided it was.

She thought about calling her sister in Wichita, and didn't. What would she say—that she'd traded a cage with bars for a cage with better lighting? Marisol would tell her not to sign. Marisol was always telling her not to do the things that kept them fed.

Instead Odile sat on the motel bed with the contract on her knees and the lamp buzzing overhead, and she did the thing she always did when a storm was coming and there was nothing left to decide, only data to gather: she made lists. Gear she'd need. Sonar rentals. Tide charts. A boat, and someone local who knew the water, because she didn't, and the sea did not forgive amateurs.

The photograph from the folder lay on the bedspread beside her. Gray water, gray cliffs, waves breaking in that strange deliberate pattern. She picked it up and held it under the light and finally saw what had snagged her the first time: along the base of the cliff, where the rock met the foam, there was a darkness in the stone. A shadow with edges. Almost rectangular. Almost, if you squinted and wanted it badly enough, like a doorway.

She told herself it was erosion. She was good at telling herself things; five years of practice.

In the morning she signed the contract in Vane's office, in ink, while he watched with the contentment of a man watching rain fall on his fields. His handshake was dry and warm and perfectly calibrated.

"Ninety days," he said. "Bring me something wonderful, Ms. Whitlock. I do so hate to be wrong about people."

Driving out of Tulsa that afternoon with the contract folded in her bag and the highway unspooling east toward a coast she'd never seen, Odile passed a church sign that read *BLESSED ARE THE DEBTORS, FOR THEY SHALL INHERIT*—someone's joke, or someone's sermon, the letters sun-bleached to near invisibility. She drove through it and kept driving, and somewhere behind her the first thunderheads of the evening were already standing up over the plains, tall and patient, full of everything they hadn't released yet.

The coast was two days away. The tide tables in her bag said the extreme lows wouldn't come for weeks.

She had no way of knowing, then, that the sea had been holding its breath for eighty years—and that in a few weeks' time, it intended to exhale.
