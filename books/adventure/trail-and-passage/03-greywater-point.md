## 3. Greywater Point

The coast announced itself before Odile saw it—a smell first, salt and rot and something older underneath, like the sea exhaling through the bones of a place it had swallowed. Then the road turned, and there it was: Greywater Point, or what remained of it.

She pulled the borrowed truck onto a shoulder of crushed shell and gravel and sat with her hands on the wheel, looking at the edge of the world. The land simply stopped. Not dramatically—no cliff face plunging into surf—but with the quiet indecency of a page torn out of a book. The coastline here had been retreating for eighty years, eating roads, houses, churchyards, whole streets that appeared on maps her grandfather would have owned. What was left was a stub of headland, grass going gray-green in the wind, and beyond it the water: pewter-colored, patient, enormous.

Somewhere under that pewter lay a village.

Odile got out. The wind hit her like a hand on the chest, smelling of kelp and cold iron. Gulls wheeled over the tideline, screaming their one opinion endlessly. She walked to where the grass gave up and became sand, then shingle, then the wet dark margin where the ocean wrote and erased its signature twice a day.

*Greywater Point.* The name wasn't on any map she'd been able to buy. She'd found it instead in a county survey from 1947, a footnote in a coastal erosion study, and in the memory of a woman at a bait shop forty miles inland who'd said, "My grandmother was born there," and then wouldn't say anything else about it at all.

Vane's contract papers called it "the subject site." Four syllables of bureaucratic nothing for a drowned town. Casimir Vane talked about debt as potential energy; apparently he talked about dead villages as assets too.

Odile crouched and let her fingers trail in the wet sand. Somewhere beneath her feet, beneath the shifting shoals and the silted channels, were foundations, wells, maybe a bell from a church tower lying on its side like a sleeping animal. And somewhere—according to the sonar targets she was being paid to find—something else. Something Vane wanted badly enough to forgive five years of compounding interest.

That was the part that kept her up at night. Not the debt. Debts she understood; debts were just weather you lived inside. It was the *want*. Optimists didn't pay for salvage contracts out of sentiment. They paid because they expected a return.

She stood, brushed the sand from her knees, and went back to the truck to unload.

* * *

The boat was rented from a marina two towns down the coast, a twenty-six-foot workhorse with a cabin the size of a confessional and an engine that started on the third try, every time, as if it were making a point about reliability. The sonar rig was borrowed too—from Dr. Amara Osei at the university, who had extracted a promise of raw data files in exchange and made Odile sign a form confirming the equipment was worth more than the truck carrying it.

"Side-scan and sub-bottom," Amara had said, walking her through the towfish and the topside unit in the parking lot outside the lab. "You'll see structure in the sediment layers. Foundations, wrecks, pipelines. Whatever's down there, it'll show you the shape of it." She'd paused, adjusting her glasses. "This is a salvage job, Odile? For whom?"

"A private client."

"Private clients don't rent boats. They hire crews." Amara's look had been level, unhurried, the look of a woman who read grant applications for a living and could smell a lie the way sharks smell blood. "Be careful what you find. Finding things is easy. It's the after that gets people."

Odile had laughed and said something breezy about shipwrecks and insurance money, and Amara had let her go, which was its own kind of warning.

Now she backed the trailer down the ramp at Greywater Landing—which was not a landing anymore, just a cracked apron of concrete ending abruptly in six feet of air above the water, the ground beneath it long since gone—and launched alone into the gray morning.

The chart plotter showed depth contours that were decades out of date. The sea had redrawn everything since. She motored out past the point, watching the broken water where the current met the shoal, and began, methodically, to work.

The towfish went over the stern on thirty meters of cable. The topside display woke with a soft electronic chirp, painting the seafloor in shades of amber and shadow. Odile ran transect lines north to south, spacing them tight, logging coordinates, keeping the boat steady against a chop that came in long, low swells from the southeast. It was work she knew—the same discipline as storm intercepts, really. Position. Data. Patience. The willingness to sit inside something dangerous and watch it carefully.

For two hours the bottom told her nothing but sand and ripple marks, the ordinary grammar of a shallow shelf. Then, on the seventh line, half a mile off the point, the display changed.

At first she thought it was rock—a reef finger, maybe, or the rib of some old wreck. But the return was too regular. Too rectangular. She slowed the boat, circled, ran the line again from the opposite heading.

There. A grid of hard returns beneath four meters of water and another three of soft sediment: parallel lines crossing at right angles, spaced the way walls are spaced. Streets. Or the ghosts of streets, the foundations of a village lying in its own grave, silted to the eaves.

Odile cut the engine and drifted. The gulls cried. Her heart was doing something she didn't want to name.

"Hello," she said quietly, to no one, to everyone down there.

She spent the rest of the day mapping the grid, extending the survey east toward the point itself, where the chart showed deeper water past the shoal line. And it was there, in the last hour of light, running a line she almost skipped because the fuel gauge was nagging, that the sonar found the second thing.

It wasn't rectangular. It wasn't anything. It was an absence—a void in the sub-bottom profile, a cavity in the bedrock beneath the sediment, roughly oval, maybe ten meters across, sitting under the cliff line where the headland reached out into the sea. The kind of feature that could be a collapsed cistern. A karst pocket. An old well shaft.

Or a cave. A cave that the retreating cliffs had slowly uncovered, year by year, the way a surgeon uncovers bone.

Odile stared at the screen until her eyes burned, then logged the coordinates twice, in two different notebooks, because some habits are older than trust.

* * *

Teodor Marsh found her at the landing the next morning, or perhaps she found him—it was hard to say afterward who had approached whom. He was leaning against a pickup truck the color of rust and patience, drinking coffee from a thermos cap, watching her load gear with the expression of a man grading a student's technique.

"You run your lines too tight together," he said, by way of hello. "Wastes fuel."

Odile straightened. He was sixty-something, maybe older, built like driftwood—all weathered surface and hidden density—with a face that had been sunburned so many times it had given up and become leather. His eyes were pale gray, nearly colorless, and they moved when he looked at things, never resting, the way birds' eyes move.

"And you know how I run my lines how?" she asked.

"Saw you yesterday. From the point." He nodded eastward, toward the headland. "Been watching boats work those waters my whole life. Most of them fish. Some of them dive. You're neither."

"I'm surveying."

"For who?"

"That's between me and my invoice."

Something that might have been approval flickered across his face, quick as a fish turning. He took another sip of coffee. "Marsh," he said. "Teodor Marsh. I pilot. Dive support, cargo runs, taking fools out to places fools shouldn't go. You'll need a local. Those shoals shift with every blow, and your little rental doesn't draw enough water to be safe and not enough to be fast. Worst of both."

"I haven't asked for a pilot."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "But you will."

Odile studied him. There was a whole conversation happening underneath this one, and she couldn't yet hear most of it. "What makes you think I'm coming back?"

He shrugged, a slow geological movement. "Because you logged that void off the point twice. I watched you write it down, put the notebook away, and write it again somewhere else." He drained his coffee and screwed the cap onto the thermos with the care of a man handling ordnance. "People only do that when they've found something they don't want anyone else to know they found."

The wind came off the water and pushed between them, cold and tasting of the deep channel.

"I don't know what you're talking about," Odile said.

"Course not." He opened the truck door. Then, over the roof, without any particular emphasis, as if remarking on the weather: "My grandfather worked a mail boat out of Greywater before it went under. Ran passengers and post to the village till '29. He used to say the point had a hollow in it, somewhere below the high tide line, that the old-timers wouldn't go near. Said the fishermen called it the Quiet Place, because sound didn't act right around it. Shouts went flat. Compasses sulked." He settled behind the wheel and looked at her through the open window, those pale eyes finally still. "I always figured that was grandfather stories. But I know these waters better than any chart, Ms.—?"

"Whitlock."

"Ms. Whitlock. And I ask very few questions. It's my best quality." He started the engine. "I'm at the dock at seven, if you decide you need a pilot. If you decide you don't, mind the shoal off the east point. It's grown a hundred meters since your chart was printed, and it has eaten two boats this year already."

He drove off in a haze of dust and diesel, leaving Odile standing in the wind with her notebooks and her borrowed instruments and the distinct, unsettling feeling of having been read like a sonar display.

* * *

She hired him the next morning, because he was right about the shoal and right about the boat and right, though she hated admitting it, about her.

They fell into a rhythm over the following days that surprised her with how easily it formed. Teodor handled the boat with an intimacy that bordered on telepathy—he'd throttle back a full second before the water changed under them, reading swells and current seams the way she read pressure falls and cloud towers. He asked no questions about the contract, the client, or the void. He brought coffee in a dented percolator that lived on the engine housing and tasted faintly of diesel, and he told stories about the coast without ever quite telling stories: the hurricane of '54 that moved a lighthouse; the winter the harbor froze and men walked out to their boats on foot; the way the cliffs lost "about an arm's length" every year, sometimes more in a bad storm, so that houses his father remembered standing on the point now stood, if they stood at all, under twelve feet of green water.

"The sea gives back more than people think," he said one afternoon, third day out, as they idled over the drowned street grid waiting for the towfish battery to charge. He nodded at the sonar screen, at the amber geometry of foundations. "Takes a town, sure. But it keeps it. Keeps it tidy, even. Sorts everything by weight. Light stuff washes away, heavy stuff settles, sorts itself into the story it wants to tell." He glanced at her. "Question is whether you want to hear what it says."

"It's a job, Teodor."

"Sure." He poured more coffee. "Everything's a job until it isn't."

On the fifth day, the anomaly grew teeth.

They'd been running tight grids over the void off the point, building a picture of it layer by layer, and the picture had stopped making comfortable sense. The cavity wasn't natural—or rather, it wasn't *only* natural. The sub-bottom profiles showed the karst pocket, yes, but also hard returns within it. Angular. Deliberate. Objects resting on the floor of a space that should have been flooded to its ceiling with four thousand tides' worth of sand.

Instead it read dry. Or close to it. Air-filled, at least in part, sealed somehow against the sea that pressed on every side of it.

"That's not possible," Odile said, for perhaps the sixth time that day.

Teodor leaned over the display, coffee forgotten. His face, usually as readable as a blank wall, had gone carefully, deliberately neutral—the neutrality of a man deciding what not to feel yet.

"My grandfather's Quiet Place," he said at last. "Sound goes flat. Compasses sulk." He straightened up and looked east, toward the point, where the afternoon light was turning the water to hammered tin. "You know what the old-timers said lived in the hollow? Nothing. That was the thing. Everything else along this coast had something living in it—crabs, eels, wrecks full of fish. The hollow had nothing. Fish wouldn't swim into it. Birds went around." He picked up his coffee and drank, and his hand was very slightly unsteady, and he pretended it wasn't. "I used to think that was a story to keep children off the rocks."

"And now?"

"Now I think," he said slowly, "that whatever's down there, it's been waiting a long time for somebody with a machine that can see it. And I think I'd like to know, before we go poking at it, whether that's good luck or bad."

Odile looked at the screen. At the angular returns nested in the dark oval of the void, patient as held breath. At the coordinates she'd written twice in two notebooks, because some part of her, some animal part older than training, had already known this find was different—had known it the way you know a storm cell is rotating before the hook echo confirms it, in the spine, in the teeth, in the small voice that says *pay attention.*

The tide was falling. On the horizon, the light was doing something strange, thickening, the way it did before weather. Behind her, somewhere inland, a phone held a message from Tulsa she hadn't listened to yet, because listening to it would make Vane real again, and today, over the drowned streets of Greywater Point, she wanted one more hour of not owing anyone anything.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Spring low's tomorrow evening. Lowest tide of the month." She watched the point, where the retreating cliffs stood in the failing light like the last page of a book someone had torn halfway. "If your grandfather's hollow opens with the tide, tomorrow's the day it opens."

Teodor said nothing for a long moment. Then he turned the boat for home, and the drowned village slid away beneath the keel, its streets and silences folding back into the amber dark.

"Seven o'clock," he said. "And Whitlock? Bring a light that doesn't need batteries."

That night, in the rented room above the closed-up general store, Odile lay awake listening to the sea work the shore, and just before sleep took her, she remembered—unbidden, unwelcome—the exact wording of the clause she'd skimmed past in Vane's contract. Section nine. *All finds of historical or scientific significance to be reported to the client within seventy-two hours of discovery.*

She checked her watch against the wall clock.

She had fifty-one hours left.
