## 6. Contract Terms

The satellite phone sat in the footwell of the rented boat like something venomous.

Odile looked at it for a long time before she picked it up. It was a heavy, ugly thing, the kind of phone that existed to make calls you couldn't take back, and it had been riding with her since Tulsa, sealed in its plastic case, its battery charged and waiting. *Report all significant findings to the Syndicate within forty-eight hours of discovery.* Clause eleven, subsection three. She'd read the contract four times on the drive east and twice more since anchoring off Greywater Point, and every reading had left the same taste in her mouth—the taste of a door closing softly behind her.

The tide had given back the cave three days ago. She had spent those three days telling herself she was being careful, being methodical, being a professional. She had measured the cave mouth, logged the tidal windows, noted the strange dryness of the air inside, the way the salt crust on the crates had been left undisturbed for what might be ninety years. She had not photographed the brass plaque. She had not photographed the crates. She had not photographed the journal—*E. Whitlock, 1931*, the water swelling the pages like a held breath—because some animal part of her had understood, standing in that dry dark with her headlamp trembling on the stone, that the moment she made a picture of it, it stopped being hers.

Clause eleven didn't require photographs. It required *reporting*.

She climbed to the boat's stern where the wind could get at her face and dialed the number she'd been given, and Casimir Vane answered on the second ring, as if he'd been holding the phone against his chest all week.

"Miss Whitlock." His voice came through warm and unhurried, a man with nowhere to be and all the time in the world to be nowhere. "I was hoping to hear from you. How are our waters?"

"Productive," Odile said.

A pause, small and pleased. "Go on."

She had rehearsed this. She had rehearsed it on the walk down from the cottage, on the dock, in the boat, and every rehearsal had collapsed the same way, because the truth was that she didn't know how much to give him. Give him too little and he'd send someone to find out why. Give him too much and she'd never stand inside that cave alone again.

"An extreme low tide on Tuesday exposed a fissure in the cliff face north of the old harbor line," she said. "It opens into a dry chamber. There's cargo inside. Crates, rope, hardware. A brass plaque with an expedition name and a date."

Another pause, longer this time, and she could hear him breathing, could almost see him—the sunlit office in Tulsa, the plants he never let die, the smile he wore like a man wearing his best coat. "And the name," he said, "and the date."

"Meridian. 1931."

The silence that followed had a texture to it. Not surprise. Odile had chased storms long enough to know the difference between the quiet before a cell fires and the quiet of a sky that already knows what it's going to do. This was the second kind. Casimir Vane was not surprised. Casimir Vane was *satisfied*.

"Well," he said, and laughed softly, a sound like coins settling in a pocket. "Well. You've been busy."

"Is the name significant to you?"

"Everything is significant to me, Miss Whitlock. That's what optimism is—believing the world is full of significance, and that it can be yours if you're willing to do the work." Papers rustled on his end, or something like papers. "Tell me about the condition. The cargo. Is it intact? Water damage?"

"Variable. The chamber stays dry, but the sea gets in at high tide. Some of it's rotted through. Some of it looks like it was packed yesterday."

"Documents?"

The question came too fast and too smooth, and Odile felt it slide past her like a hand checking her pockets. She looked out at the water, at the grey-green swell rolling in against the cliffs, at the place where the cave mouth hid itself twice a day beneath six feet of cold Atlantic.

"Some paper," she said. "Waterlogged. I haven't assessed it."

"Haven't assessed it," Vane repeated, gently, the way a man repeats a child's small lie. "Three days in a dry chamber full of 1931 and you haven't assessed it. You're a careful woman, Odile Whitlock. I respect that. Care is a form of diligence and diligence is a form of faith." A breath. "But I'm going to ask you plainly, because I think we've earned plainness with each other. Is there anything in that cave that belongs to you? Personally?"

The wind pushed at her back, as if trying to shove the words out of her before she could stop them. She thought of the journal in her duffel bag under the bunk, wrapped in a towel, drying page by page in the salt air, opening to her like a hand. *E. Whitlock.* Her own name, a generation removed, written in a firm old-fashioned cursive on the inside cover, above a date and a single line she had read forty times:

*If anyone finds this, I was here, and I chose.*

"No," Odile said.

"Good," said Casimir Vane, and she heard in the word everything he'd chosen to believe. "Then here's what happens next, and I want you to hear it as good news, because it is. I'm dispatching a retrieval team. Specialists—conservation, documentation, security. They'll arrive within the week, and they'll handle the extraction end to end, which means you are relieved of all the heavy obligations. Your survey work is complete. Your contract obligations are, I'm happy to say, substantially discharged. You'll be credited, of course. Generously. The discovery fee alone should make a real dent in that number we discussed."

The number. Five years of compounding interest on a debt she'd taken at twenty-six to keep her mother's house from the bank, back when Casimir Vane had sat across from her in a Tulsa diner and explained, with that sunshine smile, that debt was just potential energy—stored effort, waiting for its moment. She'd signed because the alternative had been watching the auction from the curb.

"And me?" she said. "Am I relieved entirely?"

"Oh, I'd never dream of it." Warm as bathwater. "You know those waters now. You know the tides. The team will need a local liaison with your—instincts. Think of it as a promotion." A pause, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped half a register, gone almost tender, which was somehow worse. "Odile. I want to thank you. Sincerely. You've found something important, and important things are my favorite kind. Rest tonight. You've earned it. The team will make contact before they land."

The call ended. Odile stood at the stern with the phone still warm against her ear and watched a gull hang motionless over the water, riding the wind without spending anything, and thought: *that's what he does. That's what the Syndicate does. They find the things already falling and they position themselves underneath.*

* * *

Teodor Marsh was waiting at the dock when she brought the boat in, which was itself a kind of message. In the week she'd known him he had never once been waiting anywhere; he arrived the way weather arrived, when it arrived, and you adjusted. Now he stood at the head of the fuel pier with his hands in the pockets of a canvas coat gone soft as cloth from thirty years of salt, and he watched her tie off without offering to help, which was also a message. On the water you tied your own lines.

"Vane?" he said.

"You could hear that from the dock?"

"I could see it. You've got the look. Everyone gets the look after the first call." He turned and started up the pier, not checking whether she followed, and she followed. "How much did you tell him?"

"The truth. Most of it. Minus the parts that were mine."

Teodor grunted. They climbed the wooden stairs to the road, past the shuttered bait shop and the church whose steeple leaned a degree or two east, the way everything in Greywater Point leaned, the whole village settling slowly toward the sea like a tired man toward sleep. The drowned village wasn't the one with the church. The drowned village was out there under the swell, washed off the maps in the fifties, and the people who lived in this one—the surviving one—had made a quiet profession of not looking at the water too long.

"Other boats been asking about the point," Teodor said, when they reached the top. He said it the way he said everything, flat and forward, a man laying cards face-up. "Three this week. Two said they were fishing."

"Were they?"

"One had gear. One had gear still in the plastic." He looked at her. "Third one didn't come in at all. Anchored out past the shoal line two nights running, lights off, then gone before dawn. My cousin runs the fuel dock. He remembers hulls the way you remember storms. He didn't know that one."

Odile stood with the wind coming off the water and felt the shape of the thing close around her by one more notch. The Syndicate team, coming in days. Unknown hulls, already here. And somewhere behind all of it, clause eleven and clause nineteen and the whole bright smiling architecture of the Vane Syndicate, which bet on people the way she bet on weather, except her storms never bet back.

"Teodor," she said. "If a man anchored off your coast with his lights off for two nights—would you say he was fishing?"

"I'd say he was listening." He shrugged his heavy shoulders. "Coast like this, you learn the difference between people looking at the water and people looking *for* the water. Your cave's been under that cliff for ninety years, girl. It was under there before you came and it would've stayed under there. Things stay hidden because somebody wants them hidden, or because nobody wants them found. You broke the second kind of hiding. First kind's worse."

"What do you mean, worse?"

He took his time answering. Down at the dock a man was coiling rope in slow, economical loops, and the sound of the water against the pilings came up regular as breathing.

"I mean," Teodor said, "that in 1931 there were men on this coast asking questions too. My grandfather was a boy. He remembered them—said they came through with instruments and money and a sponsor nobody would name, and they went out to the point and came back wrong. Quiet. Not frightened-quiet. *Finished*-quiet. Like men who'd read the last page of a book and were just walking around waiting for the cover to close." He looked at her then, and his eyes were the flat grey of the water past the shoals. "Your great-grandfather's name was in the church ledger here. I checked, after I saw yours. E. Whitlock. Signed the guest book at the mission house, September 1931. Never signed out."

The wind moved through the grass at the roadside, a long low hiss, and Odile stood very still inside it.

"You knew," she said. "You knew the name was connected before I ever found the plaque."

"I knew a Whitlock was connected." He started walking, hands back in his pockets. "I didn't know it was *yours*. That's your business, not mine. But I'll tell you my business, since you're paying me to have it: I don't take boats out to the point for people who won't say what they're looking for. You never said. I took you anyway, and I've been asking myself why for a week." He stopped at the corner where the road bent inland toward the cottage she was renting, and he didn't look at her when he said the rest. "I think it's because you look at the water the way my grandfather said those men looked at it. Like it owed you something. So. Whatever the Syndicate team comes to take out of that cliff—you decide fast whether it's yours or theirs. Because the sea gives things back, Miss Whitlock. It's the living that take them away."

* * *

That night she did not sleep. She sat on the cottage floor with the lamp turned low and the journal open on her knees, drying now to a brittle brown, its pages crackling faintly when she turned them like something talking in its sleep.

She had lied to Casimir Vane, and the lie sat in her chest with a weight she hadn't expected. Not guilt, exactly—she owed the man nothing but money, and money was the one thing she'd learned to live without. It was more the weight of a door she'd closed herself, the first one, the one that mattered. She had stood in that cave with her great-grandfather's handwriting under her hands and she had said *no* to the only person with the power to take it from her, and now the lie had to be fed, maintained, kept alive, and lies were expensive. Everything was expensive. That was the whole lesson of her life.

She read by the light of the lamp, and the dead man's voice came up off the page steady and dry, a surveyor's voice, an engineer's, a man who measured things because measuring was how he loved the world.

*September 14. — The sponsor's representatives were with us again today. They ask questions in a circle, so that you answer before you understand the question. Aldous says they are optimists. I said optimists about what? He said, about us. They believe we will find it. They believe we will give it to them. I did not like the way the second belief sat next to the first.*

*September 19. — The chamber is real. The instruments read wrong inside it—not broken, wrong. Barometer steady while the storm walks over us. Compass holding a bearing no compass should hold. Reyes wants to leave. The sponsor's man laughed at him. I did not laugh. I wrote down the bearing.*

*September 23. — Men are forgetting things. Small things. Aldous could not recall his mother's name at supper, then had it back in an hour and was ashamed, and we did not speak of it. The sponsor's man has gone back to the city. He was smiling when he left. He was smiling the whole time, now that I think of it. He never once stopped smiling, and I cannot remember his face, only the smile, as if the smile were the man and the man were something he was wearing.*

Odile closed the journal and pressed her palm flat against its cover, against the cracked leather gone soft as skin, and sat in the dark of the cottage listening to the sea work the cliffs two miles north.

*They believe we will give it to them.*

She thought of Casimir Vane's voice on the phone—*you've found something important, and important things are my favorite kind*—and she thought of the retrieval team already packing crates in some Tulsa office, conservation specialists, documentation, security, and she understood with a cold, clarifying certainty that she had not reported a discovery. She had rung a dinner bell.

Outside, far out past the shoal line, where the water went black under the last of the light, a boat sat at anchor with its lights off.

It had not been there at dusk.
