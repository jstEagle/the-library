## 7. The Rival

The boat appeared on the third morning, riding at anchor a half-mile off the point like something that had always been there and was only now admitting it.

Odile saw it first from the cliff path, a white hull with too much gear on its deck for a fishing boat and not enough rust for an honest one. *Corliss Salvage & Recovery*, the transom read, in lettering so clean it looked stenciled by lawyers. She stood in the wind with her binoculars raised and counted crew: four visible, moving with the unhurried economy of people who were very good at pretending to be idle. One of them was hauling crab pots. The pots came up empty every time. He stacked them anyway, neatly, like props between takes.

"Fishing," Teodor said when she told him that evening. He was mending a net he never used, his big hands working the twine without looking at it. "That's what they'll say if you ask."

"Should I ask?"

"Wouldn't help." He tied off a knot and finally looked up. His face had the weathered patience of driftwood—everything soft long since stripped away, everything left built to take a beating. "Corliss boats have been working this coast twenty years. They find things. They don't always report finding them." A pause, weighted. "They've got Syndicate money behind them. Everyone knows it. Nobody says it."

Odile felt the words settle into her chest like cold water into a boot. Of course they did. Of course Vane hadn't mentioned it—the way he hadn't mentioned the tides, the erosion rates, the reason a drowned village nobody remembered still needed salvaging after forty years. The contract's fine print lived in her duffel bag; she'd read it twice more since reporting the cave, hunting for clauses about exclusivity, about rivals, about what happened if someone else got there first. The document was silent on all of it, which meant the silence was the clause.

"When did they anchor out there?"

"Yesterday. Late. Came around the headland with their running lights off, which is either illegal or confident." Teodor shrugged one shoulder. "Both, probably."

* * *

They came for her two days later, or rather she went to them—or rather, neither of those things exactly, because Ilsa Corliss simply appeared.

Odile was at the Salt Line, the only bar within thirty miles of Greywater Point, a low-ceilinged room that smelled of diesel and fried fish and the particular salt-rot of wood that has been wet for generations. She'd gone to use the payphone—her cell got nothing but static off the headland—and she was feeding coins into it, listening to the ring tone stretch toward nowhere, when she felt someone come to stand beside her. Not close. Just present, the way weather is present.

"You're the Whitlock girl."

The voice was low and amused and carried a faint Scandinavian flattening at the edges, vowels planed smooth. Odile turned.

Ilsa Corliss was perhaps fifty, tall in the way of women who had spent decades bracing against boat decks, gray-blond hair cut short and practical, a face that might once have been called handsome and had since been promoted to something better: interesting. She wore oilskin over a flannel shirt and held two glasses of whiskey, one extended.

"I don't drink with strangers," Odile said.

"Then let's fix that." Ilsa set both glasses on the phone ledge, freed her hand, and offered it. "Ilsa Corliss. Now we're not strangers, and you're holding up my evening."

There was no way to refuse gracefully, so Odile didn't try. The handshake was firm, dry, brief—a businesswoman's handshake wearing a sailor's calluses. Ilsa picked up both glasses again and nodded toward a corner table under a porthole window fogged with salt. It wasn't quite an invitation. It also wasn't anything Odile could decline without making a scene in a town where she was already the stranger.

"I know who you are," Odile said, sitting. "Your boat's been anchored off the point for three days."

"Four." Ilsa smiled. It was a good smile, warm at the corners, entirely unconnected to her eyes, which were pale gray and patient as gulls. "And we're fishing."

"Teodor says your pots come up empty."

"Teodor Marsh says a great many accurate things." Ilsa slid one glass across the scarred wood. "Drink. It's terrible whiskey, but it's local, which means it's honest about being terrible. I respect that in a liquor and a coastline."

Odile looked at the glass. Looked at the woman. Somewhere behind her ribs, the old instinct stirred—the storm chaser's instinct, the one that read pressure drops and green skies and the sudden hush before a wall cloud lowered. This was that hush. Everything in the room had gone quiet in a way that wasn't quiet at all.

She drank. The whiskey was terrible. It burned going down and then bloomed warm, and she hated how much she appreciated it.

"So," Ilsa said, settling back, crossing one ankle over her knee. "You found something."

It wasn't a question, and that was the first surgical cut—not the question itself but the confidence behind it, the total absence of hedging. Odile kept her face still. She'd learned stillness chasing storms, sitting in a truck while hail hammered the roof like the sky was trying to get in, waiting to see what the atmosphere would do next. Stillness was a skill. She deployed it now.

"The contract's with the Syndicate," she said. "Whatever I found belongs to them."

"Mm." Ilsa turned her glass slowly on the table, watching the amber ring it left. "That's what I like about you already. You didn't say *what did I find?* You didn't play innocent. Innocent people waste my time and their own." Her eyes came up. "You said *the contract*. Which tells me you understand contracts. And understanding contracts, you must also understand that the Vane Syndicate holds paper on you, not on the sea. The sea doesn't sign anything."

"The Syndicate's retrieval team arrives next week."

"They arrived yesterday, in a manner of speaking." Ilsa's smile deepened a fraction. "Brannoch's people are staging out of Harborton. I have friends there. Good friends, the kind who tell you things over drinks, which is how I learned that Casimir Vane has dispatched six men, two barges, and a man whose reputation involves a fishing accident that everyone agrees was not a fishing accident." She sipped her own whiskey, delicately. "All for a drowned village. Curious, isn't it? Forty years that coast has been giving up its dead, and nobody cared. Then you file one survey report, and suddenly the optimists are investing."

The word *optimists* landed strangely—Ilsa said it with a small twist of contempt, as though the term were an inside joke she'd heard too many times. Odile filed that away. Rivals with Syndicate ties, yes—but ties could chafe.

"I don't know what you want from me," Odile said.

"Honesty. Refreshing, isn't it, how rarely anyone asks for it directly?" Ilsa set down her glass. Outside the porthole, the light was failing; the harbor water had gone the color of gunmetal with a bruise of orange along its edge. "Here's what I want, Whitlock. Nothing tonight. Nothing tomorrow. I want you to lie awake tonight and consider that you are standing between two organizations that both believe the thing you found is worth more than your life, and only one of them has ever paid out on that belief. Ask yourself which one. Ask yourself what Vane's own founder did to the last crew that worked this coast."

The room seemed to tilt slightly. Odile kept her hand flat on the table and her voice level. "What do you know about 1931?"

For the first time, something moved behind Ilsa Corliss's eyes—something quick and guarded, a curtain twitching shut. It lasted less than a second, but Odile had spent ten years reading faces through windshields while tornadoes organized themselves on horizons, and she caught it. Caught it and understood: Ilsa knew more than she was showing. Knew it specifically, personally, in the way of family stories rather than salvage gossip.

"1931," Ilsa repeated, mild as milk. "A fishing season. A bad storm in November. Boats lost. Old news." She stood, unhurried, and dropped bills on the table—enough for the whiskey and several rounds Odile hadn't ordered. "Enjoy the coast, Miss Whitlock. It's generous, if you know how to ask. But remember—" she paused at the edge of the lamplight, and for a moment the charm fell away entirely, the way a mask doesn't slip so much as get set down deliberately, "—generous things attract crowds. And crowds trample."

Then she was gone, the door swinging shut behind her, letting in a gust of cold salt air and the sound of rigging clinking somewhere out in the dark.

* * *

Odile walked back along the harbor road with her collar up and the whiskey warming a small dishonest furnace in her chest. The Corliss boat rode at anchor offshore, one lantern burning at its masthead, red, steady, the color vessels show when they're constrained in their ability to maneuver.

*Constrained,* she thought. *Or claiming to be.*

At the rented room above the bait shop, she took out the journal—wrapped now in three layers of plastic, dried page by page over two weeks with a fan and a patience that had cost her sleep—and sat with it under the lamp without opening it. She didn't need to open it. She knew what was in the early entries. She knew E. Whitlock's handwriting, cramped and slanting, the same hand she'd seen on letters her grandmother kept in a shoebox. *Sponsors insist on discretion. Half the crew doesn't know what we're surveying for.* Sponsors. Plural. Money behind the money.

Ilsa had asked about 1931 the way you ask about a scar you already know the shape of.

Odile pulled her notebook toward her—the real notebook, the one she'd started keeping after the second night, written in shorthand of her own devising, because paranoia, she was learning, sharpened into clarity if you fed it data. She wrote:

*Corliss anchored four days. Empty pots = theater. Ilsa knows Brannoch's staging schedule (Harborton source). Ilsa flinched at 1931 — personal knowledge. Not hired curiosity. Blood knowledge.*

She stopped. Read it back. Added:

*Everyone bidding on the same secret. Question is not who finds it. Question is who gets to leave with it.*

Outside, the tide was turning. She could hear it, a long exhalation against the cliffs below the point, the sea drawing breath to give something back or take something away—it had never learned the difference, and neither, she was beginning to understand, had any of them.

She looked at the journal, and then at the window, where the red lantern of the Corliss boat burned steady over black water, and she thought about what Ilsa had said: *ask yourself what Vane's own founder did to the last crew that worked this coast.*

She hadn't answered yet. But tomorrow, when the tide dropped, she was going back into the cave, and she was going to read until she had an answer.

Because everyone at this table was bidding on the same secret—and Odile Whitlock was done being the stake.
