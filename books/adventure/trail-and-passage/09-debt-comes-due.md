## 9. Debt Comes Due

They came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were for oil changes and dentist appointments, for the small administrative machinery of ordinary life—not for the arrival of men who smelled of jet fuel and money.

Odile saw the helicopter first, a black insect crawling up out of the southeast against a sky the color of dishwater. She was on the bluff above Greywater Point with her survey notebook open on her knee, pretending to log tide data she had already logged twice, when the sound reached her—a flat, insistent chopping that made the gulls lift off the cliff grass in a single offended motion. Teodor stood beside her, hands in the pockets of his canvas coat, watching it come with the expression of a man reading weather he didn't like.

"That's not a fishing charter," he said.

"No," Odile said. "It isn't."

The helicopter set down in the flattened field behind Marsh's boathouse, rotors beating the tall grass flat in a widening circle, and before the blades had even finished slowing, three figures dropped out into the wind. The first was broad and moved like something engineered rather than born—shoulders too wide for the doorframe of any normal life, jaw like a cinder block, a gray rain shell zipped to the throat despite the mild air. He crossed the field without looking at anything except the path ahead, which told Odile everything: this was a man who had already decided what mattered and would not be distracted by scenery. Behind him came two others, younger, matching, carrying hard-sided cases that clinked with the particular music of expensive equipment packed by people who respected equipment more than people.

"Brannoch," the big man said, when they met at the edge of the gravel lot. He did not offer a hand. He said his own name the way you'd read a serial number off a crate—confirmation, not introduction. "You're Whitlock."

"I'm Whitlock."

"Vane says you found something." His eyes went past her shoulder to the cliffs, to the sea beyond them, gray-green and patient under the low cloud. "He'd like to know where."

*He'd like to know where.* Not *may we see it.* Not *show us what you've got.* The phrasing sat wrong in Odile's ears, and she let it sit there while she studied Brannoch's face. It was a face built for efficiency—no wasted lines, no expression that hadn't been approved by someone. She thought about Casimir Vane in his Tulsa office, sunlight pouring through the blinds, talking about debt as potential energy, as stored power waiting for its moment. This, then, was the moment. This was the discharge.

"The contract says retrieval support arrives after full documentation," she said. "I'm mid-survey. Another week, maybe ten days, and I'll have a complete site map, condition reports, provenance photographs—"

Brannoch raised one hand, just slightly, and Odile stopped talking. It wasn't a threatening gesture. That was the unsettling part. It was the gesture of a man silencing a radio that was playing something he'd already heard.

"Week's gone," he said. "Storm system's building off the shelf water. You've seen the pressure charts." He said it flatly, but his eyes flicked to hers for half a second, and there—there was the test. He knew what she was. Vane would have told him. Storm chaser. Weather junkie. A woman who could read an isobar the way a priest reads scripture. He wanted to see if she'd flinch at being handled by someone who thought he knew her business better than she did.

"I've seen the charts," Odile said evenly. "It's four days out, minimum. Maybe five. And it tracks north of here."

"It tracks where it wants to track." Brannoch turned to the two younger men. "Unload the sonar. The good array." Then back to her. "Show us the cave, Whitlock. Today's low is at 16:40. We ride it in, we assess tonnage, we start staging extraction gear tonight. By the time that storm makes landfall, everything worth carrying is on a boat."

The wind moved through the grass behind the boathouse, a long dry whisper, and Odile became very aware of Teodor standing at her left shoulder, silent, watchful, a fixed point she could steer by.

"There's a problem with your timeline," she said.

Brannoch waited. He was good at waiting. She suspected he'd been hired partly for it.

"The cave doesn't open on schedule. It opens on extreme lows—spring tides, minus deviations. Today's low barely clears the sill. You'd get maybe forty minutes of ankle-deep access through a flooded passage, in poor light, with gear you don't know how to carry through a squeeze that's killed better swimmers than either of us." She kept her voice level, technical, boring. Boredom was armor. "Next real window is in six days. Spring series. Minus two-point-one. That's when you can bring a team in safely."

Something moved behind Brannoch's eyes—calculation, or contempt, or both wearing the same coat. "Six days."

"Six days."

"And until then?"

"Until then I finish my documentation. Per the contract. Section nine, subsection C. Full site record precedes retrieval operations. Your lawyers wrote that clause, not mine—I assume because Vane likes his assets catalogued before they're touched."

For a long moment Brannoch looked at her, and Odile understood that she was being weighed—not as a person, not even really as an adversary, but as a component. A piece of equipment that had developed opinions about its own deployment. She had seen men look at instruments that way, at radar units and probe trucks, deciding whether a malfunction justified repair or replacement. It was the most honest look anyone from the Syndicate had given her yet, and she hated how much she preferred it to Casimir Vane's sunshine.

"Fine," Brannoch said finally. "Document. But you document *with* us. My people shadow every trip to the site. You don't go in alone. You don't photograph anything you don't give us copies of. And you don't—" he stepped closer, close enough that she could smell the rain shell, synthetic and clean, nothing human underneath it, "—talk to anyone else about what's down there. There are other boats working this coast. If Corliss gets a location out of you, that's obstruction, and obstruction has terms attached. You remember the terms, Whitlock? You signed them."

She remembered them. She remembered every line of that contract now, the way you memorize the layout of a room after someone dies in it. Clause fourteen. *In the event Contractor's actions materially diminish Asset value, outstanding obligations shall be adjusted at Syndicate discretion.* Adjusted. Such a gentle word for doubling.

"I remember," she said.

"Good." Brannoch turned away, already done with her, already issuing quiet instructions to the younger men in a voice pitched below the wind. Cases were coming off the helicopter. A generator. Tripods. Something long and padded that Odile guessed was a core sampler, and her stomach tightened at the sight of it—you didn't bring a core sampler to retrieve crates. You brought it to take the ground itself apart.

Teodor walked her back toward the boathouse, and only when they were out of earshot did he speak, low, without turning his head.

"Six days," he said. "That true? About the tides?"

"Half true. There's a workable low tomorrow night. Minus one-four. Tight, cold, short. Enough for two people if they're careful and don't mind getting wet."

"And you told him six because—"

"Because every day I keep them out of that cave is a day I finish reading the journal." She kept her eyes forward. Her heart was going harder than she wanted it to, a trapped-bird rhythm against her ribs. "Teo, there are pages in that thing I haven't gotten to yet. Pages that matter. If Brannoch's crew goes in first, they'll haul out everything that isn't nailed down and Vane's lawyers will own the rest of my family's history before I've finished understanding it."

Teodor was quiet for several steps. The gulls had resettled on the bluff, screaming their grievances at the helicopter, which sat in the field like a dead black beetle, promising more of its kind.

"You're lying to armed men about tide tables," he said at last. It wasn't quite a question and wasn't quite disapproval. It was the tone of a man taking inventory.

"I'm managing information," Odile said. "There's a difference."

"Not as much as you'd hope."

* *

That night she couldn't sleep. The boathouse loft was cold and smelled of diesel and old rope, and through the single salt-hazed window she could see the Syndicate camp glowing down the shore—a cluster of white dome tents and generator light strung along the shingle like a string of infected pearls. Men moved between the tents. Once, near midnight, she heard the generator pitch change and guessed they were charging battery banks for lights, for pumps, for whatever machines Brannoch intended to introduce to a chamber that had kept its silence for ninety years.

She sat up with the journal wrapped in a towel across her knees, drying another spread of pages under a lamp, and tried to read, but the words slid. E. Whitlock's handwriting was a beautiful, disciplined copperplate that deteriorated as the entries deepened, letters leaning like trees in wind, and tonight all Odile could see in the deterioration was time pressing on a man the way debt pressed on her—the same weight, different currency.

*The sponsors ask nothing and expect everything,* her great-grandfather had written, in an entry dated that final September. *This is how the rich pray.*

Odile closed the journal gently, as if the pages might bruise, and lay back, and listened to the sea work the cliffs below. Somewhere out in the dark, the tide was turning. Tomorrow night's low would come at 23:10, minus one-point-four meters, a narrow cold window lit by a moon three days shy of full. Two people could make it in. Two people could reach the inner passage, verify what she needed verified, and be out before the water returned. Two people—and she already knew who the second one had to be, and already knew what it would cost her to ask.

Because the truth she was circling, the truth that kept her awake while the generators hummed down the shore, was simpler and worse than any tide table: she wasn't stalling Brannoch to protect the artifacts. She was stalling him because of what she'd read in the last legible entry before the handwriting collapsed entirely. *We have begun to measure wrong. Or rather—we measure correctly, and the measurements are wrong. Hollis forgot his daughter's name today and laughed about it. Laughed.* And beneath that, pressed so hard the pen had nearly torn the paper: *If you are reading this, do not bring them here. Whatever they promised you, do not bring them.*

Ninety years ago, someone had written a warning addressed, impossibly, specifically, to her. And she had responded by inviting a salvage crew.

She got up in the dark and stood at the window, watching the white tents glow, and made herself say it plainly, inside her own skull, where no one could bill her for it: *You called them. Clause six. You reported the find, and you did it because the debt was eating you alive, and now the collectors are on the beach.* Fear without exit—that was the shape of her life, and always had been. Five years running from the Vane Syndicate across half a continent, chasing storms because storms were the only things that pursued her honestly, and it had all funneled down to this: a cold loft, a drowned village, a dead man's warning, and six days of borrowed time purchased with a lie about lunar cycles.

Below, a tent flap opened. Brannoch stepped out into the generator light alone, cup of something steaming in his hand, and stood looking out at the black water exactly as she was looking at him. For a moment Odile had the vertiginous feeling that he could see her, that the distance between the shore and the loft was nothing, that men like him had instruments for that too. Then he turned, crushed his cup flat one-handed, and went back inside, and the camp settled into its electric murmur.

Tomorrow night, 23:10. She would go in with Teodor, ahead of Brannoch's schedule, ahead of everyone, and she would find out what her great-grandfather had died—or worse than died—to seal. Because if the journal's last warnings meant what she feared they meant, then the most dangerous thing on this coast wasn't the storm building offshore.

It was what happened to people who stayed too long in that chamber. And she had just promised to show the way in.

Down the shore, unseen in the dark, one of Brannoch's men began walking the tideline with a flashlight, sweeping the beam slowly across the water's edge, patient as a lighthouse, thorough as a creditor.
