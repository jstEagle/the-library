## 13. Brannoch's Patience Ends

The morning the Syndicate found the cave, Odile woke to the sound of generators.

She'd been sleeping in the rented room above the bait shop, in a bed that smelled of salt and other people's dreams, and for a moment—half-dreaming, half-drowning—she thought it was thunder. Her body had spent fifteen years translating low frequencies into weather, and the idle growl of a diesel generator rolling up the coast road sat in the same register as a distant mesocyclone. She was reaching for her logbook before she understood what she was hearing.

Then she looked out the window and saw the trucks.

Three of them, white and unmarked, parked along the shingle below the cliff path like a row of pulled teeth. Men in gray coveralls were unloading crates of equipment, floodlights on tripods, a portable winch rig. The tide was going out, and the light was flat and silver, and everything about the scene had the terrible competence of people who had done this many times in places with no names.

Brannoch stood at the base of the cliff with his hands in his pockets, watching the water retreat as if he had ordered it personally.

Odile dressed without turning on the lights. She moved through the small room by memory—the narrow bed, the table where her notes lay under a false bottom of tide tables, the window with its view of the point—and she felt something in her chest tightening with every step, a slow ratcheting pressure, like a storm gauge climbing past the red line.

They had found it. Of course they had found it. She had given Vane the location weeks ago, per contract, and she had told herself the tides would buy time, that the spring lows were erratic this year, that Brannoch's team would need her expertise to time the approach. She had told herself a great many things, and now the generators were running and the truth was standing on the beach in gray coveralls, and none of her stories had survived the night.

* * *

She went down to the shore because not going would have been worse.

The cliff path was slick with overnight rain, and she took it carefully, one hand on the rock, feeling the old stone breathe its cold up through her palm. Below, the surf worked at the tideline with its patient, mindless industry. The cave mouth was already exposed—a black seam in the cliff face, wider than she remembered, as if the sea had been quietly widening it all these weeks just to spite her.

"Miss Whitlock." Brannoch didn't turn around when she came down. He was a large man built like something geological, with a face that seemed assembled from spare parts left over after more expressive faces were made. His voice was quiet in the way that machinery is quiet between cycles. "You're up early."

"I live here," Odile said. "More or less."

"You live in Tulsa." He turned then, and his eyes did the thing they always did—moved over her face like a man reading an instrument panel, checking each gauge against the last reading. "You're under contract. The contract specifies availability."

"The contract specifies consultation on tidal timing." She kept her voice level, kept her hands loose at her sides, kept her breathing slow the way she did inside a storm when the hail started coming down sideways. "Nobody consulted me about today."

"We didn't need to consult you about today." One of the coveralled men carried a crate past them toward the trucks, and Brannoch watched it go with something that might have been satisfaction in a man capable of it. "The tide tables are public information, Miss Whitlock. You'd know that better than most. Your little delay strategy—" he said the word *strategy* the way another man might say *lie*, gently, with no heat at all— "was always going to run out of ocean."

Inside the cave, she could hear the team working. Floodlights had been rigged at the entrance, and their glare bounced off the wet stone in long white streaks. She could hear crates being dragged, rope creaking, the particular scrape of brass on rock that she had come to know too well over the past weeks—that sound was the Meridian Expedition's plaque, or instruments from the deeper chamber, or E. Whitlock's belongings being hauled out into the light by men who thought inheritance was inventory.

"They shouldn't touch the inner chamber," she said.

Brannoch raised an eyebrow, a small economical movement. "Why not?"

Because my great-grandfather sealed it with his own mind. Because the things in there take memories out of you like splinters, and you won't notice until you've lost something you loved. Because I've been losing things myself, small things, Teodor's middle name, the date of the Greensburg storm, and I've been writing everything down like a woman going blind memorizing colors—

"Because the structural integrity is questionable," she said. "The chamber sits under a fault line. Your explosives schedule assumed the outer cave only. If you compromise the vault, you lose everything above it, including your extraction route."

It was a good lie. It was even mostly true. Brannoch studied her for a long moment, and she watched him weigh it, watched the instrument-panel eyes flicker through readings she couldn't see.

"Noted," he said finally. "Stay available."

* * *

They spent the whole day hauling.

Odile stood on the shingle and watched the Meridian Expedition come apart. Crates went up the cliff path on the winch rig, one after another, tagged and logged by a woman with a clipboard who never smiled. Rotted rope. Corroded instruments. Tin trunks with stenciled numbers. A case of glass specimen jars packed in straw gone brown as tobacco. Everything that had lain in the dark for ninety years was being carried into the flat silver daylight and loaded onto white trucks, and Odile stood there with her hands in her jacket pockets and catalogued each loss the way she used to catalogue storm damage—methodically, because methodical was the only way to keep from screaming.

Teodor arrived mid-morning in his boat, ostensibly to check his mooring lines, actually because Odile had texted him a single word at dawn: *they're*. He stood beside her on the shingle, hands jammed in the pockets of his oilskin coat, and watched the trucks fill.

"Ninety years down there," he said quietly, "and they clear it out in a day."

"They've had practice."

"Mm." He shifted his weight. "That big one. Brannoch. He asked me questions yesterday. About you. About how often you'd been out to the point, whether you'd taken anything off the beach." Teodor's jaw worked. "I told him you were thorough but honest. Which is true, near enough."

"Near enough," Odile echoed.

"Odile." He lowered his voice further, under the generator noise. "Whatever you've got hidden—and don't tell me, I don't want to know it—you should know he's not asking anymore. Yesterday he was asking. Today he's counting."

She knew. She had known since dawn, since the generators. But hearing it said aloud made it solid, made it have weight and edges, and she had to look at the sea for a moment to keep her face arranged.

By late afternoon the outer cave was empty. She saw it herself, wading the last few yards of the ebb with the floodlight glare still hanging in the air like fog. The walls where the crates had stacked were pale rectangles in the algae, ghost-outlines of ninety years of darkness. The brass plaque was gone from its rock. The passage leading back toward the inner chamber yawned black and untouched, and two of Brannoch's men stood guard at it with the bored alertness of hired patience.

They hadn't breached the inner chamber yet. That was the one coin she still held, and she held it knowing the exchange rate was falling by the hour.

* * *

Brannoch came to her that evening.

Not to the bait shop—to the boat ramp, where she was cleaning the survey gear she'd have to surrender soon if she had any sense, though sense and she had never been close. He walked down the ramp with the tide slurping at the pilings behind him, and he stopped a few feet away, and he held out one hand, palm up.

"Your notes," he said.

The evening was very still. Far out, a gull made a sound like a hinge.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Miss Whitlock." He said her name with the mildness of a man describing weather to a child. "I've been doing this work for twenty-two years. Do you know what I've learned? Everybody hides something, and everybody hides it the same way—in the place they think nobody else would ever look. And do you know why that always fails?" He didn't wait for an answer. "Because the place nobody else would look is always the first place they themselves will go. You've been out to that cave eleven times that we can document. Eleven. Your boat fuel purchases alone tell that story. And every time, you came back with nothing. No samples. No photographs. No salvage." He tilted his head. "A woman under debt contract to the Vane Syndicate, standing on top of the richest find on this coast, taking nothing? There are two explanations. Either you're a saint, or you've already taken what matters and hidden it somewhere only you would look."

The tide sucked at the pilings. Odile made herself breathe.

"My notes are tide observations," she said. "Contract deliverables."

"Then you won't mind surrendering them for verification." His palm stayed open, patient as erosion. "Along with the sonar unit, which is Syndicate property on loan. Along with your access to the Marsh boat, effective immediately—we'll be chartering our own transport for the remaining work, and Mr. Marsh's discretion has become a liability we can't price." Something moved at the corner of his mouth, not quite a smile. "You can hand them over now, or my team can find them. The second option costs you more. It always costs more."

Every door, she thought. Every door has a creditor behind it.

She gave him the notes. All of them—the tide logs, real ones, and beneath them the false-bottomed folder where she'd kept her transcriptions of the journal's dangerous pages: the coordinates, the mechanism descriptions, the entries about the ledger and what it did to the men who stood too near it. She'd copied them in her own shorthand, the same system she used for storm data, unbreakable to anyone but her. She handed the folder over with steady hands and let him believe he'd taken everything.

He flipped through it on the ramp, unhurried, while the water darkened around them. Then he took her sonar unit, and her sample kit, and finally a folded paper he'd brought for the purpose, which he held out to her.

"Amended terms," he said. "Sign acknowledging receipt."

She read it under the dying light. *Obstruction of contracted recovery operations constitutes default. Default doubles outstanding principal, effective immediately upon determination.* Vane's signature block at the bottom, sunny as ever, the ink of a smiling man.

"He called me this afternoon," Brannoch said, almost conversationally. "Mr. Vane. He wanted me to tell you something himself, but he settles for accuracy." He recited it flatly, without inflection, the way you'd read a barometric pressure: "'Tell Odile I'm her biggest believer. Tell her potential energy only pays out if the mass moves. Tell her the number is now one hundred and forty thousand, and it becomes two hundred and eighty thousand the moment I decide she's obstructing.'" He folded the amendment away. "He also said—I'm quoting exactly—'she'll do the right thing. She always does. That's what I bet on.'"

Odile signed. Her signature came out steady, which surprised her; some part of her had detached and was operating the hand remotely, the way she operated herself inside a tornado's inflow, calm because panic was a luxury the physics wouldn't allow.

Brannoch took the paper, nodded once, and walked back up the ramp. At the top he paused, silhouetted against the last light, and said, without turning:

"For what it's worth, Miss Whitlock—the shorthand in your notes. My cryptographer will have it open by Thursday."

* * *

She sat in the dark of her room above the bait shop and listened to the sea.

They had her notes. They had her gear. They had her boat—or Teodor's boat, which amounted to the same geography. By Thursday, maybe sooner, they would have her shorthand translated, and then they would have the coordinates of the inner chamber, the description of the ledger, everything her great-grandfather had bled into those pages to warn her with. The outer cave was stripped bare. Two guards stood at the passage. The storm was coming—she could feel it building out over the water the way you feel a tooth go bad, a pressure with no face yet, and when it arrived it would seal the cave forever, and everything would be decided in the space between the tide and the tempest.

And the debt stood at one hundred and forty thousand dollars, waiting to double.

She got out the one notebook she had left—the small waterproof one she'd kept zipped inside her jacket, the one Brannoch didn't know existed because it had never left her body—and she wrote by feel in the dark, fast, pressing hard so the pages would hold the impression even if the ink failed.

*Inner chamber intact. Guards rotate at slack water, 40-minute gap. Spring low Thursday 0410. Storm makes landfall Friday.*

*If they reach the ledger first, it's over—not just for me. For everyone whose name is in it. For everyone whose name could be put in it.*

*E. Whitlock chose oblivion over disclosure. I used to think that was cowardice. It wasn't. It was arithmetic.*

Below her window, a truck engine turned over in the dark. Headlights swept the ceiling, crossed the wall, and slid away down the coast road toward Tulsa, carrying the Meridian Expedition home to the optimists.

Thursday, she thought. Forty minutes of slack water, four hours before dawn, and one chance.

Outside, far out past the point, the sea began to speak in the low voice it saved for storms.
