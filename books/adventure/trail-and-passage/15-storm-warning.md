## 15. Storm Warning

The barometer began to fall on a Tuesday, and Odile felt it in her teeth before she read it in the numbers.

She was sitting on the seawall above Greywater Point with a thermos of coffee gone bitter and cold, watching the horizon do something she hadn't seen it do since Kansas—since before Kansas, really, since her father's kitchen radio and the way he'd go quiet mid-sentence when the wind shifted. The air had that taste. Ozone and salt and something older underneath, something mineral, like the sea had turned over a stone it had been keeping for a hundred years.

The pressure reading on her handheld said 991 millibars and dropping fast.

"Teodor," she said, not turning around. He was somewhere behind her, mending a net he never used, because mending things was how Teodor thought. "Come look at this."

He came. He always came, which was one of the three or four things she trusted about him absolutely. He stood beside her, squinting east where the sky met the water in a line so clean it looked drawn on with a straightedge, and he was silent for a long time.

"That's not a squall," he said finally.

"No."

"That's not even a nor'easter."

"No," Odile said again, and heard something in her own voice she didn't like—a hunger, an old hunger, the thing that had put her behind the wheel of a chase truck at nineteen years old with no money and no sense and a heart going like a drumline. "That's a once-in-a-lifetime system. Maybe twice. Look at the fetch on those swells already. That's energy coming from four hundred miles offshore. Something's organizing out there, and when it organizes all the way—"

"When it comes ashore," Teodor said, "it takes the point."

"It takes everything." She scrolled through the model runs she'd pulled that morning off a borrowed satellite uplink, the spaghetti plots of forecast tracks fanning out across the screen like a hand of bad cards. Every single line made landfall within sixty miles of Greywater Point. "Surge of eighteen, twenty feet. Maybe more if it hits at high tide. The cave floods to the ceiling. Permanently. The whole cliff face will come down inside a season after that."

Teodor sat down slowly on the seawall beside her, the net forgotten in his lap. He was a man who moved slowly as a rule, but this was different. This was the slowness of someone doing arithmetic they don't want to finish.

"The ledger," he said.

"The ledger," she agreed. "And every crate left in the outer chamber. And the chamber itself. And—" she almost said *the journal's secrets*, but the journal rode against her ribs now in a waterproof case, day and night, and its secrets were hers alone. "Everything the sea gave back, it's about to take back again. For good this time."

They sat with that while the gulls wheeled overhead, screaming their idiot joy, ignorant of futures.

"You know what I keep thinking?" Teodor said at last. "I keep thinking maybe that's not such a tragedy."

Odile looked at him. His face was weathered down to essentials, all planes and patience, and there was no irony in it. He meant it. Some part of him—the part that asked too few questions, the part she'd originally mistaken for indifference and had come to understand as wisdom—wanted the ocean to close over the whole business and be done with it.

"I know," she said. "Part of me wants that too." She turned the cold coffee in her hands. "But Vane's people are still here. Brannoch is still here. And men like that don't let a storm make their decisions for them. They'll just work faster."

As if summoned by the mention—and perhaps he was; Odile had begun to suspect Brannoch could hear his own name spoken anywhere within ten miles, the way sharks were supposed to hear a heartbeat—a radio crackled at her hip. The Syndicate channel. The voice that came through was flat and clipped and belonged to one of Brannoch's technicians, a woman named Sorel who never said anything she hadn't been told to say.

*"All contract personnel to base camp. Zero-eight-hundred tomorrow. Extraction schedule revised. Acknowledge."*

Odile pressed the transmit key. "Acknowledged," she said, and hated how easily the word came.

* * *

Base camp had grown teeth since the first week.

What had been two tents and a generator was now a small industrial settlement on the bluff above the point: four structures, a crane assembly bolted into the rock, floodlights on poles, and a satellite dish aimed at the sky with the fixed devotion of a sunflower. Brannoch ran it the way a run of bad weather runs—everywhere at once, touching everything, owned by nothing.

At 0800 the next morning, Odile stood among a dozen hired hands in the largest tent while Brannoch unrolled a chart of the coastline on a folding table and weighted its corners with socket wrenches.

"New timeline," he said, without greeting anyone. "The meteorological picture has changed. We have seventy-two hours, maybe eighty-four, before conditions deteriorate past operational limits. After that, this site is gone." He tapped the chart where the cliffs broke. "Everything in that cave comes out before then. Everything. The Syndicate did not charter this operation to watch its assets drown."

A hand went up near the back—one of the Corliss crew, actually, which was interesting; since the truce, Ilsa's people had been folded into the work rotation under protest, and everyone pretended this arrangement was normal. "And if we can't get it all? Priority?"

Brannoch didn't hesitate. "The deep chamber first. The instruments, the sealed records, the primary device. Crates from the outer cave second. Structural debris last—it's worthless anyway." His eyes swept the room and snagged, briefly, on Odile, the way they always did, the way a hook snags on cloth. "Whitlock. You know the inner passage better than anyone living. You'll guide the first teams in."

"I know the tides better than anyone living," Odile said. "Which is different. And which is the problem. The spring low window tomorrow is six hours. After that, the next low won't clear the passage floor at all. You'd be sending people into a drowning pipe."

"Then we use the six hours."

"With how many people? Because crowding that passage isn't just inefficient, it's dangerous. The air exchange is marginal. Twelve people breathing in there drops the usable time by half."

Brannoch regarded her with the flat patience of a man listening to equipment complain. "Then you'll have to be efficient," he said. "You're very efficient, Whitlock. It's one of the things your file says about you." He rolled up the chart. "Zero-dark-thirty. Be there."

The meeting dissolved into motion—ropes coiled, crates inventoried, the crane tested with a groan that carried all the way down the bluff. Odile stayed behind until the tent emptied, studying the chart's ghost impression still pressed into the table's surface, thinking about files, about what else a file might say about her, about who wrote it and when.

"You're going to slow them down," said a voice at her shoulder.

Ilsa Corliss leaned against the table's edge, arms crossed, watching the camp through the tent flap with the expression of a woman auditing a competitor's books. Up close, in daylight, the surgical charm was switched off, and what remained was harder and more tired than Odile expected.

"Am I," Odile said.

"Don't insult us both. You've been sandbagging since Brannoch arrived. Wrong tide numbers, wrong air estimates, wrong everything, all of it just plausible enough to survive a glance." Ilsa's mouth twitched—not quite a smile. "It's good work. I'd have done the same. But he's onto you. He keeps your numbers and checks them against his own divers, and yesterday his numbers started beating yours. So whatever game you're playing, play it faster, because the man doesn't punish obstruction. He replaces it. And replacement, in my experience, involves a long drive and a shallow grave."

"Charming."

"I'm not charming. Charming costs extra." Ilsa pushed off the table. "My crew pulled crate manifests from the outer cave last night. Fourteen boxes of instruments, samples, records. And one item logged separately, in the sponsor's own hand: *Item zero. Do not surface.* You know what that refers to."

Odile held her face still. "Do I."

"You do, or you wouldn't have spent three nights sleeping in a boat instead of your bunk." Ilsa let that sit. Then, quieter: "Whatever you're planning, Whitlock—the storm, the delays, all of it—I want you to know I'm not going to stand in front of it. My people are forgetting things. Orders, names, the route back to their own bunks. Two of them quit this morning and couldn't tell me why. I've decided I don't care what happens to the ledger. I care that my crew sees Friday." She walked toward the flap, then paused. "Seventy-two hours. Whatever you're going to do, do it before the weather does it for you. Weather's honest. Men aren't."

She left. Odile stood alone in the emptying tent with the pressure gauge in her pocket ticking downward like a slow clock, and admitted to herself, in the privacy of her own skull, that Ilsa was right on every count—including the one about being caught.

* * *

That night the storm announced itself properly.

It was still five hundred miles out, but its leading edge reached them first as light—green lightning flickering along the eastern horizon in silent sheets, hour after hour, illuminating the swells as they came ashore bigger than any Odile had seen on this coast. The sound arrived later, a long low rumble like freight cars coupling somewhere under the world. She climbed to the boat where she slept and sat in the cockpit with the journal open on her knees, reading E. Whitlock's handwriting by red lamp, and the entries from late September 1931 spoke to her across ninety-odd years with terrible fluency.

*The barometer is falling the way it fell before the Galveston storm, God rest them. Mr. Vane's telegram says continue work regardless. Continue. As though the weather were a clerk to be instructed.*

*The men ask what we are still doing here. I ask myself. The answer is that we are not permitted to leave. The answer is that some of us no longer remember wanting to.*

*If the great storm comes, perhaps it will settle the question for us. There are worse things than the sea deciding.*

Odile closed the journal and pressed it flat between her palms, feeling the pages breathe.

Her great-grandfather had watched this same kind of sky. Had felt the same drop in pressure, tasted the same ozone. Had stood—or crouched, or knelt—in that same cave while the ocean wound up its fist. And in the end, when the choice came, he had chosen the ledger's bargain: feed it what you can't afford to remember. He had fed it everything, apparently. Himself included.

She was not going to make the same trade. But she was beginning to understand, with the fatal clarity of a forecaster watching a track converge, that some version of the choice was coming for her whether she wanted it or not. Seventy-two hours. The cave would die. The ledger would die—or be hauled out into the light by Brannoch's crane, crated, shipped, delivered to Casimir Vane's smiling hands, and set to work measuring whatever the Syndicate decided the world should forget.

Unless.

The idea had been forming for days, shapeless, the way storms form—scattered cells of intention drifting together, feeding on each other, organizing. She had watched a thousand systems do it. She knew the signs. A spark here, a convergence there, and suddenly what was chaos becomes a structure, becomes a *thing*, becomes inevitable.

Brannoch's team had brought explosives. She'd seen the cases in the supply manifest—shaped charges, meant for cutting the crane foundation free of the rock when the extraction finished. Meant, officially, for demolition of the temporary works. Unofficially, she suspected, for making sure the cave never gave up anything else to anyone else. Vane would want the site sterilized after harvest. Optimists cleaned up their losses.

The same charges, placed differently, placed *early*, placed where the passage narrowed—

The green lightning sheeted again, and in its light Odile saw the whole plan laid out as clearly as a forecast map: the extraction teams in the outer cave, the tide rising, the charges, the collapse, the sea taking back its vault with everything in it except the pages she carried out against her ribs. It would cost her everything the Syndicate thought she owed them. It might cost more than that. The passage was a throat, and throats close.

But the storm was a deadline, yes. It was also a weapon. Her whole life she had chased storms for data, for grants, for the thin pleasure of standing adjacent to power. This once—this once—she would stand inside one and aim it.

She clicked off the lamp and lay back in the cockpit, listening to the swell detonate against the seawall below, counting the seconds between lightning and thunder, doing the math she'd been doing since she was nine years old in her father's kitchen. Five miles. Four. Closing.

At 0300 her radio woke her, Sorel's flat voice tight with something new:

*"All personnel. All personnel. Base camp reports the low-pressure center has tightened—storm arrival estimate revised. We now have forty hours. Forty hours. Brannoch says the schedule moves up again. First insertion team goes into the deep chamber at dawn. Whitlock guides. Acknowledge."*

Odile stared up at the rigging swaying against the green-lit clouds, and acknowledged.

Forty hours. Dawn was coming, and with it the deepest dive yet into the chamber where her family had erased itself—and somewhere behind her, in the dark of the supply tent, waited the charges that could end the argument forever.

She checked the waterproof case against her ribs one last time, and began, quietly, to plan.
