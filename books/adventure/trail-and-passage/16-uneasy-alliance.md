## 16. Uneasy Alliance

The storm had a name by morning. The weather services were calling it *Meridian*, which felt less like coincidence than like the sea making a joke at our expense. I stood in the doorway of Teodor's boathouse with coffee going cold in my hands and watched the sky over Greywater Point do something I'd only ever seen it do over open plains: organize itself. Stacking. Thinking. A storm that plans is worse than a storm that rages.

Behind me, the radio murmured advisories in its flat bureaucratic voice—coastal flood warning, evacuation advisory for low-lying structures, marine forecast deteriorating after midnight. Teodor had his back to me, coiling rope with the deliberate economy of a man who'd decided long ago that panic was a luxury item.

"She'll make landfall two days out," he said without turning. "Maybe three if she stalls on the warm water. But she's coming here."

"I know."

"The cave won't survive her."

I knew that too. I'd done the math in the dark hours before dawn, lying in my bunk with the journal's final pages spread across my chest like a cold compress: surge heights against cliff geometry, wave fetch against the cave mouth's orientation. The Meridian Expedition had sealed their chamber behind stone and secrecy and seventy years of tide, and a single night of historic water would undo all of it. Whatever Vane wanted pulled from those crates, he had a window measured in tides now, not days.

And somewhere out past the point, riding at anchor under fishing flags that fooled no one, the Corliss crew was doing the same arithmetic.

* * *

Ilsa Corliss came ashore at slack water, alone, which was itself a message. Her skiff bumped against Teodor's dock pilings soft as an apology, and she stepped onto the planks with her hands visible and empty—a gesture so carefully theatrical that I almost admired it. She wore the same oilskin jacket from the bar in Harrow's Neck, the one that made her look like she'd been carved out of weather rather than dressed for it.

"Whitlock." She stopped ten feet away, close enough to talk, far enough to signal she understood the geometry of distrust. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other guy."

"I have. He's my second officer, and he spent last night asking me what harbor we were based out of. Our harbor. The one he's berthed in for six years." Her voice was level, but there was something underneath it, a hairline fracture running through the professional calm. "He wasn't drunk. He wasn't hit on the head. He just looked at me across the chart table like I was a stranger who'd wandered onto his boat, and asked very politely where he could find his bunk."

Teodor set down his coil of rope. He didn't say anything, but he didn't leave either, and Ilsa noticed. Good. Let her perform for an audience.

"It started with the small things," she went on. "Olsen forgot a heading he'd run forty times. Vasquez wrote the wrong date in the log—wrote 1931, Odile. In his own handwriting, clean as you please, then crossed it out and couldn't tell me why he'd written it. And now Petersen." She exhaled through her nose. "Three men. In four days. My crew has worked wrecks off every coast between here and the Azores and never once forgotten their own names, and suddenly they're standing in front of open crates full of dead men's instruments and losing pieces of themselves like loose change."

"You've been inside," I said. Not a question.

"My divers brought up two crates before I called them off." Her jaw tightened. "Before I understood what we'd hooked."

"And now you're here."

"Now I'm here." She looked past me, toward the cliffs, gray-green under the bruising sky. "Because I did some reading after your people started hauling gear around like they were robbing a pharaoh's tomb, and because my grandfather ran salvage out of Harrow's Neck his whole life and used to drink with old men who talked about the Meridian like it was a church they'd been thrown out of. They said the expedition didn't get lost. They said the expedition *stopped*." Her eyes came back to mine, sharp and searching. "They said the ones who came back weren't the lucky ones."

The wind moved through the boathouse, rattling the plastic sheeting Teodor had nailed up over the broken window. Somewhere down the coast, thunder spoke in a language I'd spent my whole life learning and still couldn't translate fast enough.

"What do you want, Ilsa?"

"A truce." She said it plainly, no charm laid over it, and somehow that was more unsettling than all her surgical sweetness at the bar. "Vane's fixer has explosives on that cliff and a deadline in his pocket, and he doesn't care what comes out of that cave as long as it comes out *valuable*. You don't care what comes out as long as it comes out *yours*. And I—" she paused, and for a moment the captain's armor slipped and I saw the woman underneath, tired and genuinely frightened, "—I don't want any of it anymore. I want my crew off this water before Meridian arrives, and I want the men I still have to stop forgetting why they're afraid."

"That's not a truce. That's a retreat."

"It's both." She held up one finger. "Here are my terms. Nobody goes into the inner chamber—not you, not Brannoch's team, not me. We pull the outer crates if we must, because Brannoch will pull them with or without us and better he does it supervised than unsupervised. We get every soul off this point before the surge hits. And whatever's deeper in"—her voice dropped, and I watched her choose the words like footing on scree—"whatever your great-grandfather died to seal, stays sealed. Permanently. If the sea wants it back, the sea can have it."

"And after the storm? When Vane's accountants start asking where their investment went?"

"After the storm," Ilsa said, "we decide nothing else yet. That's the whole point of the word *yet*." She extended her hand, palm open, callused from lines and winches. "Truce until landfall. After that, we're strangers again—or something else. But right now, today, we're the only two people on this coast who understand what's in that cave, and I would rather stand next to my enemy than watch her drown alone."

I didn't take her hand. Not yet. I looked at it instead—at the rope scars, the salt-cracked knuckles—and I thought about the journal, E. Whitlock's careful slanted script describing how the crew had begun leaving notes for themselves pinned inside their coats, *my name is, my ship is, my daughter's birthday is*, because the chamber took things and gave nothing back. I thought about Vasquez writing 1931 in a logbook. I thought about my own missing pieces: Teodor's middle name, gone like a coin through a hole in a pocket; the date of the Greensburg storm, which I had known the way other people know their own blood type.

Proximity had a price. Ilsa had paid some of it already, in her own crew's small erasures, and she'd come ashore anyway—which meant she was either brave or desperate, and the best alliances are built on both.

"One condition," I said. "My documentation continues. Everything that comes out of that cave gets photographed, logged, and copied before Brannoch touches it. If the Syndicate tries to bury what happened in 1931 along with the evidence, there'll be a record that exists outside their reach."

"Agreed. One condition of mine." Her hand stayed open between us. "When we pull out, we pull out together. No one gets left on that point—not my men, not Brannoch's, not you. I've read enough of the old stories to know what this coast does to stragglers."

I took her hand. Her grip was dry and strong and colder than the morning air justified.

"Wary solidarity," I said.

"Is that what we're calling it?"

"It's what it is. I'll be watching you the entire time."

"I'd be insulted if you didn't." For the first time since she'd stepped onto the dock, Ilsa smiled, and it was the smile from the bar again—but thinner now, worn down to something honest. "For what it's worth, Whitlock, I'm sorry about the drink. I went into that conversation planning to take everything you knew and give you nothing. It's not often I'm sorry."

"It's not often anyone means it."

* * *

We told Brannoch together, which was a performance in itself.

He received us in the Syndicate's field camp above the tideline—a cluster of canvas and generators and crated explosives that he referred to, without irony, as "the office." He was a big man built like something industrial, with a face that did its feeling in millimeters, and when Ilsa laid out the truce terms he listened with his arms folded and his eyes moving between us like a man counting exits.

"Corliss wants to pull her people out. Fine. That's her funeral budget." His voice was gravel poured slow. "But the extraction schedule doesn't change. Vane wants this site cleared before the surge, and cleared means *cleared*—every crate, every instrument, everything with a serial number or a story attached. The client's paying for completeness."

"The client," I said, "is paying for artifacts he doesn't understand, recovered by crews who are starting to forget their own orders, from a chamber that killed eleven people in 1931. Maybe suggest he revise his expectations."

Brannoch turned his head toward me with the unhurried menace of a crane swinging its load. "Your job was survey, Whitlock. Your job is not editorial."

"My job became complicated when your client's grandfather bankrolled an expedition and left them to die on this coast. Ask him about the Meridian sometime. Watch his face."

Something flickered across the millimeters—there and gone, but I caught it, and I filed it away where I keep the things I intend to use later. Brannoch knew something. Or suspected. Either way, the name had landed.

"The schedule holds," he said finally. "Two dive windows left before the surge makes the approach impossible. Tomorrow's spring low, and the day after, if the swell permits. Corliss can coordinate her withdrawal around our operations. Any interference—" he let the sentence hang, unfinished, the way men like him prefer their threats: load-bearing and unstated.

"No interference," Ilsa said smoothly. "Cooperation. You'll find we're very good at staying out of the way."

"We'll see."

Walking back down to the water, side by side through the camp's churned mud, Ilsa spoke without moving her lips much. "He's scared."

"Brannoch? He doesn't have the equipment."

"He checked the tide tables three times while we were talking. Men who aren't scared don't check twice." She kicked a stone out of her path. "Whatever Vane told him about this site, it wasn't the whole truth, and Brannoch knows it. Scared men with incomplete information are more dangerous than confident ones. Keep that in mind tomorrow."

Tomorrow. The spring low. The last good window before the sea closed the book on Greywater Point for good.

* * *

That evening I sat in the boathouse with the journal open on my knees, reading the entries I'd already read, re-photographing pages I'd already photographed, checking my own notes against themselves like a woman counting her fingers after an accident. The lamp threw my shadow huge against the wall, and I kept glancing at it, half-expecting it to move independently.

*Day 41,* E. Whitlock had written, in a hand gone shakier than the early pages. *Rourke has taken to writing his mother's name on his forearm in ink. He says it fades. He re-inks it every morning and cannot tell us why he started. Alvarez swears the instruments are not broken—they measure truly, he insists, it is we who have become imprecise. I no longer know which of these possibilities frightens me more.*

*I have begun keeping this journal in duplicate. One copy travels in the supply crate marked SPARES. If you are reading this and I am not myself, please return the other to my wife. Tell her the sea did not take me. Tell her I gave myself away a piece at a time, and that I would do it again, because some doors must be shut by someone willing to forget they ever opened them.*

I closed the journal and pressed my palms flat against its cover, feeling the warped leather breathe under my hands like something sleeping.

Teodor appeared in the doorway with two mugs and the particular silence he used for offering comfort without admitting to it. He handed me one and lowered himself onto the workbench across from me, looking out at the water, where the first advance squall line of Meridian was beginning to build on the horizon—a long dark shelf cloud, organized and patient, coming on like a debt.

"You trust her?" he asked.

"No."

"Good. Trust is for churches." He sipped his coffee. "You working with her, though?"

"Yeah. I think I am."

"Then watch her hands, not her mouth. Hands tell you first." He nodded at the journal. "And watch yourself too, Odile. You've been reading that thing for days, and you've been to the chamber twice. What have you lost this week that you haven't noticed yet?"

I opened my mouth to answer—to list the middle name, the storm date, the small documented toll—and found, with a lurch like a deck dropping away beneath my feet, that I couldn't remember my mother's voice. Not the words she used to say, not the sound of it, not whether it was high or low. I knew that she had sung to me. I knew it the way you know a fact in a textbook. The voice itself was simply gone, scooped out clean, and I had no memory of it leaving.

The mug trembled in my hands. Teodor watched me, and whatever he saw in my face made him set down his coffee and put his hand over mine, rough and warm and solid as a mooring cleat.

"Hey," he said quietly. "You're still here. Whatever's missing, you're still here."

Outside, the shelf cloud ate the last of the daylight, and far out on the black water, the lights of two anchored crews blinked at each other across the rising sea—enemies, allies, witnesses—all of us waiting on the tide, and on what the tide was about to take back.
