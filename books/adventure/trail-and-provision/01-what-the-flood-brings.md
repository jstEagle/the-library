## 1. What the Flood Brings

The river woke angry that spring, and Yara Whitlock loved it for exactly that reason.

She stood at the wheel of the *Meridian* with her boots planted wide on the wet deck planking, one hand resting easy on the brass rim of the wheel, the other holding a chipped enamel mug of coffee gone lukewarm an hour ago. The flood had come down from the high country overnight—she'd felt it in the way the water changed its voice against the hull, a deeper note, a hunger in it—and now the whole valley was a brown, muscular animal, dragging whole trees sideways, peeling fences off their posts, carrying somebody's roof past at a stately tilt like a coffin meant for a giant.

"Spring flood," Doss said beside her, spitting over the rail with the satisfaction of a man confirming the world still worked according to his expectations. "River's up nine feet at the gauge stone. Ten if you believe the mark old Tammer painted drunk."

"I believe the river," Yara said. "Tammer's marks are for tourists."

The *Meridian* was forty years old and looked every day of it, which was how Yara preferred her boats and her men: honest about their wear. She was a stern-wheeler of modest length, patched in six places with steel that didn't match her original skin, smelling always faintly of coal smoke and engine oil and the particular green rot of the river itself. Her paddle wheel turned with a sound like slow applause. Yara had inherited her from her father along with his debts, his charts, and his habit of talking to the water as though it were listening.

She talked to it now, low, under her breath, the way she did when the current ran strange.

"Easy, girl. Show me your pockets."

Because that was what a flood was, really—a river turning out its pockets. Every spring the high water brought down what winter and the hills had been hoarding: fence rails, drowned lambs, barrels, once an entire piano that Doss swore he could have sold. Most of it was wreckage. Some of it was salvage. A captain who knew where to look could pay her crew's wages for a season off what the flood shook loose, and Yara Whitlock knew where to look better than any captain working the Longwater between Karst Reach and the sea.

"There," she said, pointing with the mug. "That eddy behind the willow bar. Something's riding the boil."

Doss squinted. "Log."

"That log is turning against the current."

Logs don't argue with rivers. That was the first law of the water, older than charts, older than names. Anything that fought the flow wanted something—or was held by something—and both possibilities interested her.

She brought the *Meridian* around in a long, respectful arc, letting the current do half the work, easing toward the willow bar where the flooded river split and curled back on itself. The eddy there was a slow gray eye in all that rushing brown, and in the middle of it rode a shape that resolved, as they closed, into something boxy and dark, sitting far too deep in the water for driftwood.

"Grappling hook," Yara called aft. "And someone wake up Piet. I want hands on the net winch."

The deck came alive behind her in the particular rhythm of a crew that had done this a hundred times—boots on planks, the creak of the davit, young Piet stumbling up from the engine deck with his shirt buttoned wrong and sleep still in his face. Yara held the boat steady against the push of the main current, feeling it lean on the hull like a drunk leaning on a friend, and watched the shape turn slowly in the eddy.

It was a chest. A real chest, iron-banded, the kind built to survive being thrown off bridges or buried under floorboards, and it was floating upright, which meant either it was packed with cork or the river was being uncharacteristically polite.

"Throw's yours, Piet," she said. "Don't embarrass me."

Piet threw. The weighted net went out wide and came down true, settling over the chest in a bloom of rope, and the winch took the strain with a groan that harmonized nicely with the paddle wheel. The chest rose dripping, streaming brown water, swinging on the line like a hanged man, and swung aboard with a boom that Yara felt through her boot soles.

Heavy. Heavier than it should be.

They got it onto the foredeck and gathered around it the way sailors gather around anything the river gives back, which is to say with greed on their faces and superstition just underneath. The chest was maybe four feet long, oak gone black with age, bound in iron straps furred with rust. Water wept from its seams. And stamped into the lid, pressed deep enough to have survived decades of soaking, was a seal: a circle, and inside the circle a bridge spanning three wavy lines, and above the bridge a star.

Yara went still.

She knew that seal. Not from any book—she'd never been much of a reader—but from songs. Barroom songs, the kind sung late, the kind that stopped when strangers walked in. *Down by the waters of Vessia fair, where the bridge met the star and the king kept his oath*—half the verses lost, half the rest nonsense, but every boatman on the Longwater knew the chorus without knowing why. Vessia. The drowned nation. The country that wasn't on maps anymore, erased a hundred years ago so thoroughly that schoolmasters pretended it had never existed, swallowed by the reservoirs and the treaties and whatever happened in those years the histories skipped like a scratched record.

"That's not a maker's mark," Doss said quietly. All the satisfaction had gone out of his voice. "Captain, that's—"

"I know what it is."

"It's bad luck, is what it is."

"Doss, you told me last month that women passengers were bad luck, and then that widow from Millbrook paid triple fare and knitted you socks."

"These are different socks," Doss said with dignity, and shut up.

Yara crouched by the chest. Up close she could see the workmanship—the joinery was fine beneath the grime, the iron bands hand-forged, the lock a heavy thing with a keyhole shaped like nothing she recognized, some old pattern. The wood had been underwater a long time, but not a hundred years. Not even close. The seal was crisp. Someone had made this chest within living memory and put a dead nation's crest on it, deliberately, and then the river had carried it to her.

Floods bring things, her father used to say. Your job is to decide what you're going to carry forward and what you're going to let the water take back.

Her father had been dead five years this spring. She thought about him less than she used to and missed him more, which she suspected was how grief actually worked, contrary to everything the poets claimed.

"Pry bar," she said.

"Captain." Doss shifted his weight. "Salvage law says we can open it. Nobody's arguing that. But a chest with that seal on it—there's people downstream pays good money for Vessian relics, and other people who get very unfriendly about them. My cousin saw a man knifed in Corren's Landing over a spoon with that star on it."

"A spoon."

"A very nice spoon."

Yara looked at the chest, and the chest sat there being patient, the way heavy things are patient, and the restless curiosity that had been humming in her since she'd spotted the eddy turned over and showed her a colder underside. Doss was right, and she hated that he was right, because she was already going to open it. She'd known she was going to open it from the moment the net settled. A woman who made her living reading water was not going to leave a sealed mystery sitting on her own foredeck.

"Pry bar," she said again, gently, and Doss sighed the sigh of a first mate who had followed this woman into weather no sane man would sail into, and fetched the pry bar.

* * *

The lock gave before the hinges did, which surprised everyone; rust should have welded it shut. Instead the mechanism turned with a grinding reluctance, as though it had been oiled recently and then left to seize, and Piet said, "Somebody's been keeping this thing ready to open," and nobody answered him because everybody was thinking it.

Inside: water, first. It sloshed out over the deck, gray and cold-smelling, and Yara noticed—filed away without knowing yet why she filed it—that the water inside was clear. River water carries silt. Clear water meant the chest hadn't come down through the flood at all. It had been submerged somewhere still and deep, and released only lately.

Under the water lay oilcloth. Layers of it, wrapped tight, tied with waxed cord, and beneath the oilcloth, documents. She could see the corners of them, stiff and pale, and she could see that they were dry. Dry, after however long in the river. Sealed against water by someone who understood water.

"Well," Doss said. "That's not smuggler's work. Smugglers wrap in canvas and pray."

Yara lifted the top bundle out and set it on a coil of rope. The oilcloth was stitched, not just tied—stitched with care, the seams sealed with pitch. Whoever had packed this chest had expected it to swim. Had expected it to be found. Her skin prickled.

There were letters in the bundle, a dozen or more, tied in stacks. There was a ledger with a cracked leather spine. There was a document folded in thirds, heavy paper, and when she touched it she stopped, because the paper was thick as skin and the ink on the visible corner was written in a formal hand with gold leaf catching the light at the initial letter.

And there was a letter, half-burned. Its edges were charred black, curling like autumn leaves, as though someone had thrust it into a fire and then—changed their mind. Snatched it back. The surviving fragment was small enough to fit in her palm.

She read it standing there in the spray and the gray morning light, with her crew crowded around and the flood roaring past the rails, and the words seemed to arrive from very far away:

*—must reach the source or no one. If it falls to Crane's people, everything he did was for nothing, and I cannot—I will not—have bought all this for nothing. Trust the river. It knows the way home better than—*

The rest was ash.

Yara read it twice. Then a third time, because her eyes kept sliding off the handwriting, refusing to settle on it, the way eyes slide off a face you know in a place where that face has no business being.

She knew that handwriting.

She knew it the way she knew the *Meridian*'s engine sounds, the way she knew which of the deck boards groaned in false alarm and which groaned for real. She knew it from freight manifests signed in her cabin. From the notes he'd left on the galley table: *Back Thursday. Feed the cat whether he deserves it or not.* From the single letter he'd written her from the hospital at Corren's Landing, two weeks before the fever took him, the ink already going shaky at the ends of the lines.

Aldous Whitlock's hand. Her father's ink. On a letter hidden in a sealed chest bearing the crest of a nation that had been dead a hundred years, a nation that had never appeared on any chart he'd ever owned, in any of the forty years she'd known him, in any story he'd ever told, and Aldous Whitlock had told stories the way rivers tell them—constantly, and mostly true.

"Captain?" Piet said. "You've gone white as a gull."

"It's nothing." Her voice came out level, which cost her something. "Get this below. Dry berth, my cabin, not the hold. Piet, you and Sarn carry it—don't drop it, don't open anything else. Doss, log it as salvage, description vague. 'Water-damaged goods, contents under assessment.' That'll keep the harbor master happy and curious in equal measure."

"And the papers?"

"Mine," she said, and heard how it sounded, and didn't soften it.

They carried the chest below, and the deck returned to its ordinary noises, and the flood went on roaring past with all its stolen fences and drowned trees, indifferent, carrying its next secrets toward whoever was fool enough to be out on the water today. Yara stayed at the wheel because staying at the wheel was what she did instead of thinking, and steered the *Meridian* down the swollen channel toward Harrow Bend and home dock, and the whole time her thumb moved absently against the wheel's brass rim, worrying it, the way it did when something wouldn't sit right.

*Trust the river. It knows the way home better than—*

Home. The source. Her father had spent his whole life on this river, same as her, running freight between the same eleven towns, and in all that time he had never once—not once, and she would have remembered, the way you remember holes in a familiar wall—never once mentioned Vessia except to sing the damn song, badly, while shaving.

People don't hide nations from their daughters by accident.

* * *

Harrow Bend dock received the *Meridian* the way it always did, with rotting pilings and a greeting committee of gulls and old Marta Kell from the fuel office, who waved her ledger and shouted the mooring price as though Yara might have forgotten it overnight. Yara paid without haggling, which Marta noted with narrowed eyes, because Yara Whitlock haggled the way other people breathed.

By mid-afternoon the chest sat in her cabin on the deck locker, and she had gone through everything twice.

The letters were old and new mixed together—some yellowed to lace, some barely faded. They were in several languages, two of which she couldn't read at all, and the ones she could read were fragments, halves of correspondence, pages cut from larger documents with a blade. Names recurred: a family name, *Vane*—no, not Vane, *Vessa*, or something near it—crossed out and rewritten. The word *heir* appeared more than once. The word *treaty*. The word *Crane*, and each time it appeared it was underlined, hard enough to tear the paper, by a hand she recognized.

Her father's ledger was worse than the letters. It was worse because it was mundane. Columns of dates, runs, payments received, payments owed, in his tidy bookkeeper's script—and among the ordinary entries, in the same ink, the same hand, entries marked with a small star. The star from the seal. She counted nineteen of them across thirty years. Nineteen runs, spread across her entire childhood, marked with the crest of a drowned country, each entry noting only a route and a sum and, in his cramped marginal shorthand, a word she finally decoded around dusk: *carried.*

He had carried things for Vessia. For whatever remained of Vessia. For thirty years, while tucking her in, while teaching her to read water, while singing the song he must have known was not entirely a song.

Yara sat in the failing light with the half-burned letter flat on her knee and felt the restless curiosity of the morning curdle slowly into something with weight and edges. Unease, first. Then the colder thing underneath unease, the thing that lived in the part of her that had survived twenty years on this river: the understanding that a chest does not float free. A chest is released. Someone, somewhere upstream, had put this into the water—deliberately, sealed, addressed by implication to whoever was clever enough and greedy enough to pull it out—and her father's handwriting inside the lid was either a message meant for her or a coincidence, and she had never once in her life been favored by a coincidence.

She lit the lamp. Outside, the dock boards creaked under evening foot traffic, and the flood muttered against the pilings, and somewhere down the waterfront a barroom singer was mangling the old chorus, *down by the waters of Vessia fair.*

That was when she saw the man in the gray coat.

He stood at the far end of the dock, past the fuel office, in the space between the lamp posts where the light gave out. He wasn't doing anything—that was the thing. Dock idlers fidget. They watch boats, they watch girls, they watch the water. This man watched her cabin windows. He had been there, she realized, long enough for the gulls to have stopped complaining about him, and when she shifted to see him more clearly, he didn't startle and he didn't flee. He simply stepped back, unhurried, into the dark between the warehouses, the way a man steps who knows exactly where the dark is.

Yara blew out the lamp and stood a long time in her own black cabin, listening to the river.

Then she reached over and drew the bolt on her door, and sat back down with the half-burned letter, and read it a fourth time, aloud, very quietly, finishing the sentence her father had never finished—as if saying it might make the next chapter introduce itself:

"Trust the river. It knows the way home better than anyone."

The flood knocked softly against the hull, all night, like something asking to be let in.
