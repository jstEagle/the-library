## 2. Her Father's Ink

The Meridian rode low at her moorings that morning, heavy with floodwater in her bilge and the particular tiredness of a boat that had fought a river all night and won nothing but survival. Yara had been awake since before dawn, pumping her out by lamplight while the water around the pilings ran brown and fast with everything the flood had torn loose—fence rails, a drowned hen still tied to its perch, somebody's washboard spinning downstream like a pale coin.

She should have been sleeping. Instead she sat on the deck planks behind the wheelhouse with the chest between her knees, and the dock was quiet enough that she could hear the river chewing at the pilings below, patient as a debt collector.

She'd meant to open it last night. She hadn't. She'd carried it aboard like something pregnant, set it down in the lamplight, looked at the crest pressed into its lead seal—a tower above three wavy lines, the mark of Vessia—and then she'd gone and made coffee she didn't drink and checked lines that didn't need checking, because some part of her had already understood what opening it would cost, the way you understand a storm is coming from the smell of the air.

Now there was nothing left to do but open it.

The lock was bronze, greened with a century of river, but the mechanism turned when she worked it with oil and patience—the kind of lock built to be opened again after long sleeping, which told her someone had expected this chest to be found, eventually, by hands that deserved instructions. The lid came up with a sigh of cold air that smelled of wax, cedar, and old ink.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, lay papers.

Yara told herself, for about four seconds, that she was going to look through them the way a captain looks through salvage: cataloguing, detached, already calculating resale. Then she peeled back the first fold of oilcloth and saw the handwriting, and those four seconds were over.

She knew that hand. She would have known it blindfolded, by the pressure of the nib alone. A slanted, impatient script that leaned forward like a man always halfway into his next sentence. Her father's hand. Aldous Whitlock, who had died five years ago in a rented room two hundred miles downstream with forty dollars to his name and a river pilot's calluses on his fingers, leaving her a boat, a debt, and a lifetime of stories she'd believed because he was the one telling them.

*No,* she thought. *Different hand. Similar.* People's writing rhymed across generations; she'd seen it before. It happened.

It wasn't different.

The first document was a receipt, written in that hand, dated twenty-two years back. *Received of the bearer one sealed packet, contents unknown, for conveyance upriver to Karst Reach. Payment rendered in full. —A.W.*

The second was another receipt, eleven years later. Same hand. Same formula. *Contents unknown.*

The third was not a receipt. It was a letter, or what remained of one—half-burned, the bottom third eaten away to a brown lace of char, as if someone had held it to a candle and then changed their mind. The surviving portion read:

*If you are reading this, then the river has done what I could not, and brought it home. I was paid to carry these things and I carried them, and I told myself for thirty years that a courier is not responsible for what he carries, only that it arrives. That is a lie. I have known it was a lie since the year the valley went under. The chest must reach the source, or no one. Not the consortium. Not the exiles' committees in Corvane who will sell it for a seat at table. The source, or no one. If I am dead, find the woman who—*

There the burn took the rest.

Yara read it twice. Then a third time, moving her lips without knowing it, the way she did with charts in bad light. The dock boards creaked somewhere behind her and she flinched hard enough to knock her elbow against the chest's iron-bound corner, and when she turned there was nobody—just gulls working the flood line, just the water sliding past with its cargo of the drowned and dislodged.

*Find the woman who.* Find the woman who what? Who what?

She sat back on her heels. The sun had come up properly now, laying a hard white stripe along the dock, and she became aware of how exposed she was—sitting in plain view with an open chest full of documents that a dead man had warned, in his own burned handwriting, must reach *the source or no one*. She glanced up and down the waterfront out of pure habit. Barge crews hosing mud off their decks. A customs clerk arguing with a fishmonger. The usual morning machinery of Marrow's Landing doing its usual indifferent work.

And at the far end of the dock, near the rope walk, a man in a gray coat who wasn't doing anything at all.

That was what snagged her, more than anything about him. Everyone on a waterfront was doing something—mending, hauling, loitering with intent toward a wage or a drink. This man stood at the rail where the dock met the shore road, hands folded behind him, facing the moored boats with the settled attention of a man reading. He was middle-aged or older, lean, hatless, with hair the color of wet ash, and his coat was good wool, city-cut, wrong for Marrow's Landing the way a dress shoe is wrong for a gravel bar. He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at the *Meridian*, with the same unhurried thoroughness he might give a document.

Yara closed the chest. Locked it. Did it slowly, so that if he was watching, he'd see nothing but a salvager securing her find—which was, after all, exactly what she appeared to be.

Her heart was going harder than the situation warranted. She knew that too. But the letter had put her father's voice directly into her ear, and once a dead man starts talking inside your head, every shadow on the dock acquires intentions.

* * *

She took the chest below, into the stern cabin, and shut the door, and lit the lamp even though daylight leaked through the port, because she wanted the small circle of yellow, wanted the feeling of a room with edges. Then she went through everything.

Under the receipts and the letter: a second layer, wrapped separately in waxed canvas and tied with cord that had been knotted in a pattern she didn't recognize—deliberate, repeating, almost decorative. Inside that layer were documents older by far. Parchment, some of it, gone soft and translucent at the folds. A map of the upper river drawn in faded sepia, showing reaches she knew and reaches she didn't, and above the last named rapid a valley marked not with a town's symbol but with the same crest as the seal: tower above three wavy lines. Someone had annotated the margins in pencil, tiny and precise—not her father's hand. There were letters with broken seals, official-looking, in a language she half-recognized from church hymnals and barroom songs: Vessian, the tongue of the drowned country. And a slim ledger bound in blue cloth, its spine cracked, which she opened expecting more receipts and instead found columns of dates, payments, initials, and route codes going back decades—her father's courier book, or part of one, though the entries were coded rather than plain, and the initials at the top of each column were not hers to guess at yet.

She didn't open the ledger fully. Something in her balked at it, the way you balk at a door in your own house you've never noticed before. Later. She'd deal with the ledger later.

What she kept coming back to were the receipts. Twenty-two years ago. Eleven years ago. Her father had been carrying things for Vessia—or for whoever used Vessia's crest—for longer than Yara had been alive, or nearly. And all those years he'd been coming home between runs, sitting at their kitchen table with his coffee and his charts, teaching her to read water, telling her the story about how he'd once outrun a customs cutter with nothing but ballast trim and nerve—and never once, not in thirty years of stories, had he said the word *Vessia*.

Except he had. Once. She was nine, maybe ten, and they'd passed a flooded church steeple standing alone out of a reservoir, and she'd asked whose church, and he'd been quiet so long she'd thought he hadn't heard, and then he'd said, "A country's," and wouldn't say more, and she'd filed it away as one of the places where her father kept doors.

She'd spent five years thinking she knew which doors those were. Grief, she was learning this morning, was less like a wound and more like a sunken wreck: you thought you'd marked the spot, and then the flood moved it, and it came up in your nets in pieces you weren't ready for.

*The source, or no one.*

She got up and poured herself the coffee from last night, cold, and drank it anyway, standing at the little aft window where she could see the dock through a slit of curtain. The man in the gray coat had moved. He was closer now—twenty yards, maybe, strolling along the rail with his hands still folded behind him, pausing here and there as if admiring the flood debris. He paused opposite the *Meridian* for the length of ten slow breaths. Then he walked on toward the customs house and out of her sightline, and Yara discovered she'd been holding the curtain edge in a fist tight enough to ache.

* * *

Midmorning, Doss came aboard.

Doss Kettering had been her first mate for six years, a broad, slow-moving man with a beard he trimmed with fish knives and a genius for engines that bordered on the devotional. He came up the gangway carrying a crate of pump leathers, saw her face, and set the crate down without being asked.

"Well," he said. "Either we're sunk or we're rich."

"Neither." She nodded at the cabin door. "I pulled a chest out of the flood last night. Vessia's mark on it."

Doss whistled low, the way men did when the word came up, because everyone knew the songs even if nobody knew the truth. *"Where the towers sleep beneath the stream,"* he hummed, tuneless. "That's salvage, captain. That's yours by right of recovery. Sell it to the antiquarians in Corvane and we refit the old girl proper. New boiler tubes. Maybe that icebox you keep mooning over."

"It belonged to my father."

Doss stopped mid-reach for the crate. He'd known Aldous Whitlock too—everyone who'd run the river in the last forty years had known him, or known *of* him; Aldous Whitlock had been one of those pilots whose name got spoken at bars as a standard other pilots failed against. Doss lowered himself onto the hatch coaming, which was his way of sitting down for a long conversation.

"You want to say that again?"

"He wrote the receipts inside. His hand, Doss. I'd know it in the dark." She heard her own voice come out flat and careful, the voice she used for reporting damage. "He was paid to courier them. More than once. And there's a letter, half burned, saying the contents have to reach 'the source or no one,' and then it burns off right where it's getting interesting."

Doss was quiet for a while. Out on the water a log raft went by, riding high and fast, and the wake of it slapped the hull.

"Aldous," Doss said finally, "used to take private courier work every winter, you know that. Off-season, cash jobs, never talked about them. Everybody figured it was gambling debts or a woman." He rubbed his beard. "You think it was this?"

"I think my father spent thirty years carrying secrets up this river and never told me a single one, and now one of them has floated four hundred miles to land on my deck, and there's a man in a city coat walking my dock like he's auditing it." She turned the cold cup in her hands. "So no, I don't think it's salvage. I think it's a job. One he couldn't finish."

"And the man in the coat?"

"Don't know. Gray wool, ash hair, stands like a schoolmaster. Been looking at the boat since dawn."

Doss grunted. "Could be nothing. Could be an antiquarian's agent who spotted the pull before you did. Flood salvage draws vultures." He didn't sound convinced, and neither did she. "Or could be whoever burned that letter looking for the rest of it."

"That's the part I keep turning over." She looked at the cabin door, behind which the chest sat with its coded ledger and its half-murdered letter. "Somebody tried to destroy that warning and couldn't finish. Somebody else sealed the whole thing to survive a century underwater. Those aren't the same somebody. My father was one of them. Which one, I can't tell yet, and that's—" She stopped, started again. "Five years, Doss. Five years I've been carrying him around like he was finished, like I had the whole of him measured. Turns out I had the hull and none of the cargo."

Doss looked at her with the expression he usually saved for a failing engine: respect mixed with the certainty that worse was coming. "What do you want to do?"

"What I always do. Read the water." She stood, rolled her shoulders until something in her back cracked. "First, this stays between us and the deck plates. Nobody else touches the chest, hears about the chest, or dreams about the chest. Second, I'm going up the dock to see if our friend in gray wants to introduce himself. Third—"

She stopped. Because through the aft window, in the slit of her vision, the man in the gray coat had reappeared—and this time he wasn't looking at the boat. He was standing at the foot of her gangway, patient as high water, and as she watched he removed a pair of wire spectacles, polished them on his sleeve with a scholar's absent care, and looked directly up at the stern window. Directly at her. From twenty feet, across bright morning air, she would have sworn he smiled—not unkindly, which somehow made it worse—and inclined his head a precise half-inch, the way a man greets someone whose name he already knows.

"—third," Yara said, setting down the cup, "he's come to me, so I don't have to go to him. Stay here. Watch the chest."

She went up the steps and out into the light, and behind her, in the dark cabin, her father's handwriting waited on its oilcloth like a voice holding its breath.
