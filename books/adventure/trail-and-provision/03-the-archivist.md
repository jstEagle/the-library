## 3. The Archivist

The stranger in the gray coat came aboard at first light, when the river was still the color of pewter and the mist hung so thick on the Meridian's rails that it beaded and dripped like slow rain.

Yara had been awake since before dawn—she always was, flood season or no—and she heard him before she saw him: a measured tread on the dock planks, unhurried, the walk of a man who had never once needed to run for a boat. Most passengers ran. They came down the gangway with their bags swinging and their hats clutched to their heads, terrified of being left behind, as though the Meridian were a departing train rather than a vessel that would wait all day for coin.

This man did not hurry. He stopped at the foot of the gangway, looked up at the wheelhouse where Yara stood with her coffee going cold in her hands, and waited until she acknowledged him. Only then did he climb.

"You're Captain Whitlock," he said. Not a question. His voice was low and precise, the voice of a man who read aloud for a living—or wished he did.

"Depends who's asking."

"Elias Vane." He produced a card from an inner pocket of that gray coat, holding it out between two fingers as though it were something fragile and old. The card was heavy stock, cream-colored, and bore nothing but his name and a single embossed device: a circle broken by a vertical line, like a sun half-swallowed by the horizon.

Yara didn't take it. "Never heard of you."

"Most people haven't. That's rather the point of my work." He tucked the card away again without offense. Up close, she could see him better than she had from the wheelhouse window the evening before: mid-thirties perhaps, lean, with dark hair cut short and a face that might have been handsome if it ever relaxed. It wasn't relaxed now. There was a tightness around his eyes, a coiled quality to his stillness, like a man standing very carefully at the edge of something deep. His coat was good wool but travel-worn, the cuffs slightly frayed, the hem stained with the particular dried mud of the lower river. He'd been walking. For days, by the look of him.

"You were watching my boat last night," Yara said.

"I was watching your chest."

The words landed between them like a dropped anchor. Yara set her coffee down on the sill of the wheelhouse window, slowly, and let the silence do its work. She'd learned long ago that most men rushed to fill a silence. The ones who didn't were the dangerous ones.

Vane didn't fill it. He stood at the head of the gangway with his hands loose at his sides and met her stare, and after a moment he said, quietly: "May I see it?"

"No."

"Captain—"

"This is my deck, and that is my cargo, and I don't show either to strangers who skulk on docks after dark." She stepped out of the wheelhouse onto the hurricane deck, letting him see all six feet of her, the boathook leaning against the rail within easy reach. Old habit. Flood season brought drifters and worse. "Now. You can tell me what you actually want, or you can walk back down those stairs and I'll cast off in ten minutes and never think about you again."

Something flickered across his face—not fear, exactly. Recalculation. He glanced past her shoulder, toward the open hatch amidships where the chest sat under a tarpaulin lashed down against the morning damp, and Yara watched his eyes track to it with an accuracy that told her he already knew precisely where it was.

"The chest that came down with the flood two nights ago," he said. "Sealed. Bearing the crest of Vessia."

Yara kept her expression flat, which took some doing. "There's no such place."

"There is no such place *on maps*," Vane said. "That's a different thing entirely, and everyone on this river knows it. Every barroom from here to the delta has a song about Vessia. Drowned nation. Sunk beneath the reservoir waters when the consortium closed the High Dam. A hundred years gone." He paused. "Except it isn't a hundred years, Captain. That's part of the lie. But we can come to that later, if you'll hear me out."

"Or you can get off my boat."

"Or I can pay you," he said, "very well."

* * *

She let him into the pilothouse because it was cold on deck and because, though she'd have denied it under torture, she wanted to hear what he had to say. She put him in the corner by the chart table where she could watch his hands, and she poured herself more coffee, and she said, "Talk."

He talked. And the strange thing—the thing that unsettled her more than anything else he said—was how little of it was persuasion. He didn't flatter her, didn't spin tales, didn't perform. He laid facts out on the chart table like a man laying out instruments before an operation.

"I'm an archivist," he began. "Trained at the Vessian exile archive in Correnn, across the border. We keep what remains. Records, genealogies, fragments of the treaty negotiations, testimonies from the drowning. Four generations of people cataloguing the wreckage of a country, waiting for—" He stopped himself. Started again. "Waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

"For the chest you pulled out of the floodwater."

Yara drank her coffee and said nothing.

"You found it," he went on, "because the flood found it first. Two nights ago the upper tributaries crested, and somewhere upstream—in country the consortium doesn't survey, doesn't map, pretends doesn't exist—something gave way. An old cache. A dead man's cache." His eyes came up to hers. "Your father's cache."

"My father's been dead five years."

"Yes."

"And you know this how?"

"Because Aldous Whitlock was our courier," Elias Vane said simply. "For eleven years. He carried documents upriver for us—letters, records, small things that wouldn't survive the official channels. And once, years ago, he carried something larger. Something he sealed and hid rather than deliver, because delivering it would have gotten a great many people killed—including, he believed, the people it was meant to save." He spread his hands. "Two nights ago the river opened his hiding place. And the river brought it to you. To his daughter. On his old route."

The pilothouse felt smaller than it had a moment ago. Yara became aware of her own heartbeat, of the creak of the hull beneath her feet, of the fog pressing against the windows like something listening.

Inside the chest's lid, in her father's handwriting, she had found a line inked neat and sure: *To the source, or to no one.* She hadn't told a soul. She hadn't even said it aloud to herself.

"How do you know about my father?" she asked, and her voice came out lower than she intended.

"Because I've spent four years reading his reports. Because I know his hand better than my own by now. Because—" Vane hesitated, and for the first time since boarding, he looked away from her, out the window at the gray water sliding past the hull. "Because I was supposed to meet him, once. Six years ago, at a crossing above Karst Reach. He never came. We learned later he'd been hurt. That he'd retired from the work. That he died the following spring." He turned back. "I'm sorry. I know that's not worth much, coming from a stranger. But it's true."

Yara looked at him for a long moment. Her father, dead five years, had been a quiet man with ink-stained fingers and a laugh that came rarely and completely, a man who'd taught her to read water before she could read words. A courier. Running errands for a drowned nation's ghosts, all those years he'd told her he was running freight.

*The past refusing to stay sunk,* she thought, and hated how much she wanted it to stay sunk.

"What's in the chest?" she asked.

"That," said Elias Vane, "I can't tell you. Not because I don't want to—but because I don't fully know. I know what we believe it contains. I know what it means if we're right. But Aldous sealed it, and the seal is intact, and there are perhaps three people alive qualified to open it without destroying what's inside." He drew a breath. "What I can tell you is that it must go upstream. To the source valley. And that I will pay for passage—for me, and for the chest—to get there."

"The source valley." Yara laughed, short and humorless. "Mister Vane, there is no source valley. The upper river runs into consortium territory, through locks they control, past towns they garrison, and then it just—ends. Charts stop. Even if there were such a place, you're talking about running a hundred and forty miles of the most controlled water on the continent with consortium property in my hold."

"It isn't consortium property."

"It's *contraband*, then, which is worse."

"It's Vessian property," Vane said, "a hundred years withheld. And yes, Captain, I understand exactly what I'm asking. I understand the locks at Karst Reach, the gunboats on the middle stretches, the price on sympathizers' heads in every town from here to the gorge. I've spent my whole life studying what the consortium does to people who carry Vessian paper." His voice stayed level, but something underneath it had begun to burn; she could hear it now, banked but real. "I'm asking anyway. Because if that chest holds what I think it holds, it's worth more than every dam they've ever built. And because you're the only captain on this river who both knows these waters and has reason to help."

"I have no reason at all."

"You have your father's handwriting inside that lid," Vane said quietly. "Don't tell me you haven't looked."

* * *

She made him wait on deck while she thought, which was partly tactics and partly because she needed to stand at the rail and look at the river and not at his face.

The Meridian lay against the dock with her lines groaning, riding high and light—no cargo booked downstream, no passengers yet, the spring flood still throwing debris past her bow in brown ropes. Downstream was money. Downstream was always money: grain, timber, mail, the safe dull trade she'd built her life on since inheriting the boat. Upstream was tolls and inspections and men with consortium brass on their collars asking questions with guns in their hands.

Upstream was also the only direction the chest cared about.

*To the source, or to no one.*

Her father had written that. Her father, who had never in his life asked her for anything, had left her a sealed box and an instruction, and a stranger was standing on her deck offering to make the instruction possible.

And the stranger knew too much. That was the splinter working under her skin. He knew about the chest, about her father, about the flood, about the route. He knew about the seal. A man that well-informed was either exactly what he claimed—a scholar who'd spent years preparing for this moment—or he was something else wearing scholarship like a coat. The consortium had archives too. She'd heard stories.

But then, she reminded herself, watching him stand at her rail with his travel-stained hem and his careful hands—he'd known about her father's death, the year of it, the injury. If he were consortium, why come alone? Why come poor? Consortium agents arrived in threes, with papers and muscle and the smell of money. This man smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and four hundred miles of walking.

It proved nothing. She knew it proved nothing. Rivers taught you early that the calmest-looking water held the worst rocks.

She went back into the pilothouse.

"Here are my terms," she said. "You pay double passage, in advance, plus a hazard fee, because we both know what we'd be running. You eat crew rations, you stand watches if I ask, and you touch nothing on this boat without my leave—especially not the chest. You don't give orders to my crew. You don't discuss the chest with anyone but me. And the moment I say the run's off—locks too hot, water too high, any reason at all—we turn around, and you keep your mouth shut about it. Are those terms clear?"

"Perfectly."

"And one more thing." She leaned against the chart table, arms crossed. "You said there's no such place on maps. Fine. So draw me one. Right here." She slid a pencil and a scrap of chart backing across the table. "The source valley. Where it is. How you get there. If you can't, this conversation's over."

Something moved behind his eyes—there and gone, quick as a fish turning under the surface. He picked up the pencil. He held it over the paper for a moment longer than a man should need.

Then he drew. A line upstream, past Karst Reach, past the marked end of the charts, into blank paper. A tributary branching north. A narrow gorge. And beyond it, a small shape like a cupped hand, ringed twice with hills.

"I can't give you distances I don't trust," he said, setting the pencil down. "The last accurate survey was made before the drowning. But the way is real. My grandmother walked out of that valley as a girl. She drew it for me from memory, and I've walked half of it myself." He looked up. "The rest, Captain, we find together."

Yara studied the little drawing. Cupped-hand valley, double-ringed hills, a gorge like a throat. It matched nothing on any chart she owned—which meant it matched nothing the consortium owned either, and that, at least, was worth something.

"Passage money," she said. "In advance. Half now, half at Karst Reach, and if you default at the lock, I sell your luggage to pay the toll."

"Agreed." He counted notes from a leather wallet—more than double passage, she noted, though she kept her face still—and set them on the chart table under the coffee pot where they couldn't blow away. Then he extended his hand.

She looked at it. She didn't take it.

"Don't mistake this for trust, Mister Vane," she said. "I'm carrying you because you can pay, and because my father pointed upstream, and not one inch further than that. You step wrong on my boat—even once—and you'll finish this journey swimming. Do we understand each other?"

For the first time since he'd boarded, Elias Vane smiled. It changed his face entirely, cracked it open, showed her the man underneath the archivist—younger, wearier, and more frightened than he wanted anyone to know.

"We understand each other perfectly, Captain Whitlock," he said, and withdrew the unshaken hand without embarrassment. "Better than you think. I grew up on the Correnn ferry. I know exactly what captains are worth, and exactly what happens to passengers who forget it."

"Hm." She gathered the notes, counted them twice, and stowed them in the strongbox under the helm. "Then welcome aboard. We cast off with the tide turn, two hours. There's bunk space below—take the aft cubby, and mind the low beam."

He nodded and went down the stairs, his tread measured even now, and Yara stood alone in the pilothouse with the fog thinning off the water and the river running strong and brown toward the sea.

She looked upstream. Somewhere up there, past the locks and the gunboats and the end of every map she trusted, was a valley shaped like a cupped hand, and a father's secret, and whatever it was that Elias Vane wanted so badly he'd walk four hundred miles and board a stranger's boat to reach it.

*Trust your river,* her father used to say. *Rivers don't lie. People do.*

She put the wheel over and began, methodically, to check her engine room, her lines, her fuel, her flares—preparing the Meridian for the hardest run of her life, and telling herself the tightness in her chest was only the cold morning air.

She almost believed it.

She was halfway down the ladder to the engine room when the dockhand came running along the planks, waving his arms, shouting her name—and behind him, far down the waterfront, a gray-coated figure who was most definitely not Elias Vane was showing a photograph to the harbormaster.
