## 4. Passengers and Predators

The Meridian took the current at first light, when the river wore its gray silk skin and the mist stood waist-deep on the water like a congregation waiting to be dismissed.

Yara Whitlock stood at the wheel with her hands light on the spokes, feeling the boat answer her before she asked it anything—the old girl always did, reading her palms through the wood the way a blind woman reads a face. Behind her, the dock at Lowmarsh shrank into the fog, and with it went the last three days of feverish work: caulking seams that hadn't needed caulking, repainting the name on the stern because she couldn't think of anything else to do with her hands while the chest sat locked in her cabin below, humming with other people's secrets.

She had told herself she wasn't running. She was simply departing on schedule, as any sensible captain would, with two paying passengers aboard and a third riding on credit.

The paying passengers were an odd pair. A merchant named Corvel Hask, soft-bellied and soft-spoken, bound for Tarrow's Landing with what he claimed was a shipment of dye-stuffs—though his trunks clanked like they held something heavier than indigo. And beside him, a woman who had introduced herself only as Sef, paid in mixed coin counted out without haggling, and then said almost nothing at all for two full days of loading and provisioning. She was small and brown-haired and forgettable by design; Yara had watched her move through the crowded dock and noted how the crowd seemed to part around her without knowing why, the way water parts around a stone it has decided not to argue with.

And Elias Vane, the archivist, standing at the bow rail in his gray coat even now, watching the fog peel off the water as though he expected the drowned nation of Vessia to rise up out of it and wave.

Yara didn't trust him. She trusted the river beneath him, which was a different thing entirely, and for the moment it would do.

* * *

By midmorning the sun had burned the mist away and the Meridian was working upstream through the wide green country below Karst Reach, past willow banks and cattle standing belly-deep in the shallows, past fish traps and the occasional leaning shrine where boatmen left coins for whatever saint governed drowning. The flood was still high—two feet over summer mark, brown and muscular—and Yara could feel the extra weight of it in the wheel, the way a rider feels a horse that wants to run.

Sef appeared at her elbow sometime after the second bend.

"Captain." Her voice was low, uninflected. "There's a boat behind us."

"There's always a boat behind us. It's a river."

"Not like this one." Sef didn't point. She kept her eyes forward, on the water ahead, which told Yara something about her. People who pointed were announcing. People who didn't were reporting. "It's been holding our speed since the second lock. Never closer than half a mile. Never farther. A steam launch, black hull, no cargo lines."

Yara let a slow breath out through her nose and turned the wheel a degree, easing the Meridian toward the cut bank where the current ran strongest. "You've got sharp eyes for a passenger."

"I've got nervous eyes," Sef said. "There's a difference."

"Is there?" Yara glanced at her sidelong. Up close, in honest daylight, the woman looked tired in a way that sleep wouldn't fix—a weariness worn down to the bone, like a stair tread polished by decades of feet. "Where'd you learn to read hulls, Sef? You don't strike me as a dyer's clerk."

"I've been a lot of clerks." Something flickered across her face—not quite a smile, not quite a wince. "I read water because I grew up on it. Before the dams."

Before the dams. Yara filed that away. Everyone said *before the dams* about something different—about cheaper grain, or open fishing, or the villages that had gone under the reservoirs when the consortium flooded the upper valleys. But the way Sef said it had a specific gravity, like a coin dropped in still water.

"I'll keep an eye on your launch," Yara said.

"Thank you." Sef hesitated, and for a moment Yara thought she might say something else—something that mattered. Then the moment closed over like water over a sunk thing, and Sef nodded once and walked aft, unhurried, her boots making no sound on the deck boards.

Yara watched her go, then watched the river behind them.

The launch was there. Black hull, low profile, a smudge of smoke hanging over it like a thought someone was trying not to have. Holding their speed exactly. Half a mile back, patient as debt.

She found Doss coiling line near the paddlebox and jerked her chin toward the stern. "Steam launch, astern. Been shadowing us since the second lock. Take a look and tell me what you see."

Doss leaned on the rail, squinted, spat thoughtfully over the side. "Consortium hull," he said. "That's a Bureau boat. Survey class." He straightened, and his weathered face had gone carefully blank, which from Doss was louder than shouting. "Could be charting flood damage."

"Could be."

"Flood season's nearly done."

"Mm."

Doss rubbed the back of his neck. He'd been her first mate eleven years, since he was a gangly deckhand who fell in the Nine Sisters and came up laughing, and she knew every weather his face could make. This one—this flat, careful nothing—was new, and she didn't like it.

"You want I should talk to the passengers?" he offered. "Keep 'em below if things get interesting?"

"No. Keep everything exactly as boring as it is. Boring is armor." She squeezed his shoulder as she passed. "I'm going to have words with our archivist."

* * *

Elias Vane was in the saloon with the chest open in front of him, which was against the single rule Yara had set, and he had the grace to look caught when she came through the door.

"The lid stays shut unless I'm in the room," she said.

"I was only—" He closed it gently, as though apologizing to it. "There's a document inside I've been trying to reconstruct from memory for six years. I wanted to see if I remembered it correctly."

"And did you?"

"Almost." His eyes were bright and unhappy. That was the thing about Elias that kept snagging her: he was too sincere. Liars learned to wear sincerity like a coat, but they took it off when they thought no one was looking, and she had yet to catch Elias without his. Whatever he was hiding, he hid it from himself first. "Captain Whitlock. Is something wrong?"

"A Bureau launch is following us. Consortium survey class, holding station half a mile back since the second lock." She dropped into the chair across from him and put her boots up on the opposite bench, deliberately casual. "Now, you're a man who knows a great deal about chests and very little about rivers, so let me educate you. Bureau launches don't chart in pairs, and they don't chart this stretch in spring, and they especially don't hold station behind a single riverboat for four hours without coming alongside to ask directions. They're not following us, Mr. Vane. They're following *it*." She tapped the chest with one heel. "Which means somewhere downstream, somebody opened their mouth. Which means I want to know every soul who knew you were hiring passage upstream, and when."

Elias had gone pale. Not theatrically—pale the way men go pale when the floor moves under them. "No one," he said. "I told you. I made arrangements through intermediaries. Letters left at dead drops in Vell Hollow and—"

"Dead drops." Yara laughed, short and hard. "You hired a riverboat through dead drops and you're surprised someone noticed? Elias. Think. A stranger turns up in Lowmarsh asking captains to run him two hundred miles upriver into consortium territory, carrying nothing but a gray coat and a lot of money. Every dock rat, every lock keeper, every barmaid between here and Karst Reach knows there's something worth following on my boat. You didn't need to tell anyone. You told everyone just by existing."

He stared at her. "Then why did you take me aboard?"

Because the money was good and the chest was already hers whether she liked it or not. Because her father's handwriting was inside the lid and she couldn't leave it sitting in a Lowmarsh warehouse gathering questions. Because the river was going upstream anyway and so, apparently, was her whole life.

"Because I'm a fool with good instincts," she said, standing. "Stay away from the windows. And Elias—if that launch comes alongside, you let me do the talking. You have the face of a man with something to confess, and it'll get us all hanged."

* * *

They tied up that evening at a coal stage below Tarrow's Landing, a lonely timber platform jutting off the bluff with a rope-ferry landing and nothing else, because Yara wanted no part of the Landing's customs house until she understood what the customs house wanted of her. The crew took on coal by lantern light. Corvel Hask ate two dinners and complained about the wine. Sef stood at the stern rail for hours, watching the dark water, and Yara pretended not to notice that the black launch's smoke had finally faded from the horizon an hour before dusk—or that Sef had watched it go with an expression Yara could only describe as grief.

It was near midnight, Yara alone in her cabin with the chest and a lamp, when she heard the engines.

Not on the river. On the road above the bluff—an engine coughing, gears grinding, the particular laboring sound of a heavy vehicle being driven fast on roads built for ox carts. Then voices, many voices, and lantern light swinging wild through the trees, and the rope-ferry landing creaking under sudden weight.

Yara snuffed the lamp, slid the chest's key into her boot, and went up on deck quiet as smoke.

Below the bluff, at the Landing itself—two miles downstream, but sound carries over water at night—lanterns were blooming along the customs dock like fever blisters. Even at this distance she could hear it: boots on planks, a whistle, a voice amplified by anger or authority or both. And threaded through it, carried clean across the still water in fragments, the question.

"—chest. Sealed chest, iron-banded, Vessian crest. *Anyone.* The captain's name is Whitlock. The boat is called the Meridian—"

Doss materialized beside her at the rail, and behind him the deckhands, and behind them, silent as a held breath, Sef and Elias and Corvel Hask in his nightshirt, all of them listening to their own names read out over the water like a sentence being passed.

"They know the boat," Doss murmured. "They know *you*, Captain."

"They know my father's name," Yara said quietly. "Whitlock. There's more than one of us on this river. Always has been." But even as she said it, she heard how thin it sounded, and she saw in Doss's face that he heard it too.

On the far dock, the lanterns were dividing—search parties forming, spreading out along the shore road, one group starting up the bluff trail toward the coal stage. Toward them.

"Captain." Sef's voice at her shoulder, low and urgent. "The side channel. There's a slough behind the coal stage—old flood chute, runs north through the willow bottoms and rejoins the main stem above the Landing. Your draft can make it tonight if the water's still high enough. It won't be high enough tomorrow."

Yara turned and looked at her properly for the first time. "How does a dye clerk know my draft?"

"How does a dye clerk know the flood chutes of Tarrow's Landing?" Sef held her gaze, steady, unblinking, and in the lantern light Yara saw for the first time the fine scarring on her knuckles, the calluses of someone who had handled ropes or weapons or both. "Because I've run this river before, Captain. In boats like yours. For people like them." She pushed back her sleeve.

Inside her wrist, in faded blue-black ink no larger than a thumbnail, was a mark Yara knew from toll gates and dam postings and the wax seals on consortium freight: the interlocked circles of the Consolidated River Authority, drawn small and precise, a brand worn where it could be hidden by a cuff.

The deck went very quiet. Somewhere below the bluff, the search party's lanterns reached the top of the bluff trail and began to swing toward the coal stage, fifty yards and closing.

"Heard you the first time," Yara said at last, dry as August. "Get below, all of you. Doss—steam's up?"

"Never let it die."

"Then we're leaving through the back door." Yara spun the wheel hard over as the deckhands threw off the mooring lines, and the Meridian swung her stern toward the narrow black mouth of the slough, where the willows leaned close enough to comb the hurricane deck, and the water beyond was unmapped, unlit, and hers—for the next few hours, at least—to lose.

Behind them, a voice on the bluff shouted, and a lantern began to run.
