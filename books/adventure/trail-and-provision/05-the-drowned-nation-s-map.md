## 5. The Drowned Nation's Map

Elias Vane had spent eleven years learning to wait. Waiting was the archivist's first discipline—waiting in reading rooms while clerks decided whether to let him see a folio; waiting in border towns while permits crawled through offices; waiting through the long gray years when the Consortium's censors burned faster than he could copy. He had learned to make waiting look like patience, and patience look like harmlessness, and harmlessness had carried him farther than money ever could.

But tonight, on the deck of a riverboat called the *Meridian*, tied up at a mudbank landing with frogs singing in the reeds, his patience was coming apart at the seams like wet paper.

Yara Whitlock had not opened the chest again.

She'd hauled it aboard two days ago from the flood flotsam, found her dead father's handwriting inside the lid, and gone quiet in a way that Elias recognized and feared. Grief was manageable. Grief he could work around, could offer tea to, could sit beside in silence until it loosened its grip. But suspicion—that was different. Suspicion asked questions. And every question Yara Whitlock asked herself about why her father's ink was inside a sealed chest bearing the crest of a drowned nation brought her one step closer to questions Elias could not answer honestly.

He sat at the rail with a cup of the boat's terrible coffee going cold in his hands and watched her work. She was checking the mooring lines for the third time that evening, though nothing threatened them but the current's slow pull. Her hands moved over the rope with an intimacy that made him envious—not of her, exactly, but of the certainty of it. She knew what she was holding. She knew what held her.

He had never known either.

"You're staring," she said, without turning around.

"I'm admiring your knots."

"My knots are none of your business." But she came back along the rail toward him, wiping her hands on her trousers, and there was something in her face tonight—a loosening, or perhaps just exhaustion wearing the mask of one. "You paid me passage money to go upstream. You've been on my boat three days. You've eaten my food, drunk my coffee, and told me nothing except that you're an archivist and Vessia is real. I've been patient because you paid well and you don't snore. But tomorrow we pass Karst Reach, and after that I stop making stops for curiosity."

"I understand."

"Do you?" She dropped into the chair across from him, elbows on knees, studying him the way she probably studied weather. "Because here's what I understand. My father—who has been dead five years, who I buried, who I grieved—wrote something inside that chest. Something meant to be read by whoever opened it. And whatever it is, it scared him enough to seal it under wax and crest and lock. So either you tell me what's in that chest worth drowning for, or I put you ashore at Karst Reach and keep my questions to myself."

Elias set down the coffee. His heart was beating hard, and he was grateful, suddenly, for all those years of practice—for the stillness he could put on like a coat. What she didn't know was how much he wanted to tell her. How long he had waited for exactly this: someone with a boat, a reason, and a wound the truth might salve.

"Come below," he said. "I'll show you."

* * *

The cargo hold smelled of pitch, lamp oil, and river damp. Yara hung a lantern from its hook and Elias knelt beside the chest, which sat where they'd lashed it, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp. His hands were not quite steady as he worked the locks. He noticed her noticing, and made himself slower, more deliberate. An archivist handled documents with reverence. That much of his performance was no performance at all.

The lid came up. Inside, beneath the false bottom he'd pried loose the first night while the boat slept, lay the packet: oilskin-wrapped, corded, sealed with a stamp he had seen only twice before in his life—in a fragmentary treaty copy in the archive at Corvane, and once, impossibly, in a dream he'd had so often it felt more like memory.

"What am I looking at?" Yara said.

"The Treaty of the Source." He lifted the packet out with both hands, the way one lifts a sleeping child. "Signed in the last spring before the flood. Between the Crown Council of Vessia and the River Compact—the alliance of valley towns upstream of the delta. It grants the Vessian heirs perpetual rights to the headwaters. All of them. Every tributary above the gorge, every spring, every fall."

"And that matters because—"

"Because rights to water are rights to everything downstream." He heard his own voice quicken, the zeal rising in it, and did not check it this time. Let her hear it. Let her hear what conviction sounded like; it was the only currency he had that wasn't counterfeit. "The Consortium's entire empire is built on dams, Yara. The Karst Reach locks. The High Barriers at Corvane. They control the flow, so they control the barges, the mills, the irrigation, the price of grain in every market from here to the coast. Everyone assumes they built that empire. They didn't. They *inherited* it—from a crime."

Yara had gone very still. In the lantern light her face was all planes and shadow, a figurehead carved from something harder than wood. "Say it plainly."

"The Consortium drowned Vessia." He said it the way he had rehearsed saying it for eleven years, in empty rooms, to audiences of chairs. "Not a flood. Not bad luck, not an act of God, not the weak levees the histories blame. The upper sluices at the Serpent's Neck were opened on the night of the spring melt—opened deliberately, by men paid to do it—with the whole of the lower valley asleep behind its dikes. Eleven thousand people. The songs say drowned in their beds, and the songs are wrong; most of them woke. The water took four hours to fill the valley. Four hours." His voice cracked on the number and he hated it and let it stand. "And then the Consortium erased the maps, bought the historians, burned the parish records, and built its dam monopoly on the graves. This treaty—" he touched the oilskin "—is the one document that survived. Signed, witnessed, sealed. If it reaches the heirs, it voids every water claim the Consortium holds above the gorge. All of them. At a stroke."

Silence, except the river talking to itself outside the hull, and somewhere aft a crewman's boots crossing the deck.

Yara looked at the packet for a long time. Then at him.

"Why does my father's handwriting open this chest?"

There it was. The question he had been sailing around for three days, the reef beneath the waterline. He had prepared answers—three of them, ranked by how much truth each contained—and now, kneeling in the lamplight with the treaty between his hands, he found himself reaching past all three toward a fourth he hadn't planned.

"Because he was the courier," Elias said. "The last one. There were others before him—six couriers over ninety years, carrying copies, fragments, testimony, moving the truth upstream a few miles at a time, generation to generation, like a relay. Your father was the seventh. He was given the original and told to get it to the source valley or destroy it. 'The source or no one'—you read that yourself, in the half-burned letter."

"How do you know what I read?"

"Because I wrote the letter."

The lantern swung slightly on its hook. Yara didn't move. Elias watched her assemble it—the stranger in the gray coat at the dock, the too-large purse, the convenient appearance—and saw the moment she arrived at *trap*, and the moment after it, when she looked at his hands on the treaty and reconsidered.

"Go on," she said, in a voice like a drawn blade laid flat on a table. "Slowly."

"I'm the archivist of a society that doesn't officially exist. We call ourselves the Remembrance. Survivors founded it—people who got out of the valley that night, or whose grandparents did. For a century we've kept what we could: names, records, fragments. Most of it lost. Most of us dead or scattered. Ten years ago we found proof the original treaty still existed, hidden by the sixth courier in a house in Corvane. We needed someone to move it—someone unconnected to us, someone the Consortium had never heard of. A river man. Your father had run the upper reaches for thirty years. He knew the gorges. He owed us—" Elias stopped. Edited. "He agreed to help us."

The lie slid in smoothly, worn smooth as river stone from rehearsal. *He owed us.* True, in a way. Just not the way she would hear it.

"And instead of delivering it," Yara said slowly, "he sealed it in a chest and put it in the river."

"He was being hunted. The Consortium learned a courier existed—they didn't know who. He couldn't risk carrying it overland, couldn't trust any road or any lock. So he did the only thing left. He trusted the river itself. Sealed it, weighted it, and set it adrift above the delta during the flood, when the water would carry it into the backchannels where Consortium patrols don't go. Where the only people who'd find it were river folk. People like you." Elias met her eyes. "He didn't know it would be your boat. But I think—I've always thought—he would have chosen it if he could."

Something moved in her face then, brief and deep, like a fish turning under ice. He watched her fight it—grief and anger wrestling in the same expression—and he understood, with a clarity that felt almost cruel, that he had just handed her both a wound and a gift in the same motion. Her father hadn't abandoned her. Her father had been fighting a war she'd never known about, right under her keel, for years.

Or so she would believe. Elias looked down at the treaty and felt the other thing stir in him, the thing he kept below the waterline, the annex pages folded into his coat lining that no one aboard would see unless he chose. *Editing,* some cold inner clerk observed. *As always.*

"So the heirs," Yara said. "There are heirs. Alive."

"The line survived. Scattered into exile, renamed, married into ferry families and farming towns. The Remembrance has traced four branches. One is close—closer than the Consortium knows. Upstream." He paused. "In the source valley."

"There is no source valley. The maps say the gorge closes out. Nothing above it but mountains."

"The maps are Consortium maps." He allowed himself the first smile in days. "Captain Whitlock, you of all people should know what a river thinks of a map."

That earned him something—not quite a laugh, but its ghost. She stood, walked a slow circuit of the hold, touching things as she went: a crate, a coiled line, the ribbed curve of the hull. Thinking with her hands. He'd watched her do it for three days and had already begun, treacherously, to find it endearing.

"If even half of what you say is true," she said finally, "then everyone who helps you is a target. The Consortium doesn't burn historians for exercise."

"No. It burns them for policy."

"And you want me to pilot a hundred-year-old steamboat full of paying passengers straight up the throat of that empire, carrying the one document that can break it."

"Yes."

"You're insane."

"Almost certainly." He rose, knees cracking, and held the treaty out to her. "Hold it. You should know what you're carrying."

She hesitated—then took it. Unwrapped the oilskin with a courier's care she didn't know she had. The parchment inside was heavy, foxed at the edges, the ink browned but iron-gall stubborn, refusing to fade. Two columns of script in old Vessian cursive, and at the foot of each column, signatures: seven of them, each with a wax pendant seal. Elias watched her trace the largest signature with one finger without touching it, hovering, the way people hover over relics.

"It's real," she said. Not a question.

"It's real."

She wrapped it back up slowly, and when she spoke again her voice had changed—flatter, more careful, the voice of a woman recalculating a route. "Karst Reach. Tomorrow. The Consortium runs the locks there. If they're asking about a chest anywhere on this river—"

"They will be. Soon."

"Then we need to be through before they start looking hard." She hung the lantern back on its hook, and in the swinging light her face was unreadable again, the captain restored, the daughter stowed below. "Get some sleep, Archivist. You've got a long river ahead of you, and so do I."

* * *

He did not sleep.

Elias sat on the forward deck with his back against the deckhouse, wrapped in his gray coat, watching the stars wheel over the black ribbon of the current. The treaty lay locked in the hold below, and the annex pages lay folded against his heart, and between those two weights he was stretched thin as a bridge cable.

They would hate him when they learned. Sef, with her watchful eyes and her wrist she kept turned away from people—she would hate him least, maybe, because she of all of them knew what it was to carry a thing that could detonate your life. But Yara. Yara, who had just tonight taken her father's ghost down off the cross she'd built for it and started building him a better monument—Yara would not forgive the edit. Because the edit was about her father. About what the sixth courier had really entrusted to him, and what her father had really been asked to do with it, and why the letter Elias had written—the half-burned one, the one she'd read—had been worded so carefully to omit the name of the person the treaty named.

*The source or no one.* True words. But the source had a face, and Elias had seen it once, years ago, in a Corvane reading room, in a marginal note in a dead woman's hand, and he had spent every day since deciding whether the truth was a gift or a weapon.

The restoration movement had its figurehead—an heir the exiles had anointed, beloved, plausible, safe. If the treaty's named line became public knowledge in the wrong order, the movement would shatter before it ever reached the valley. Elias had convinced himself, in eleven years of convincing, that withholding the name was mercy. Strategy. Kindness with a scholar's justification.

Tonight, watching Yara sleep-walk her grief back into purpose, the justification rang hollow for the first time.

A floorboard creaked aft. He turned his head slightly and saw her—the quiet passenger, Sef—standing at the rail in the dark, looking not at the water but at him. Their eyes met. Neither moved. Somewhere far downstream, faint as a rumor, a lock bell rang out the hour at Karst Reach, and Elias thought: *tomorrow.*

Tomorrow the river narrowed, and the lies would have less room to swim.

Sef turned away first. Elias stayed where he was a long while longer, listening to the Meridian breathe at her lines, and practiced, one more time, the sentence he hoped he'd never have to say aloud.
