## 6. Sandbar Justice

Doss had counted the coins twice before the third man sat down at her table.

The Ferryman's Rest smelled of tar and fried eel and the particular sourness of men who drank to forget what they'd seen on the water. Doss had been coming here for eleven years, back when she was just deckhand under Captain Whitlock senior, and in all that time she had learned one rule above all others: never take a meeting near your own berth. But the flood had closed every road south of Marrow Bend, and the consortium's man had named this place, and coin was coin no matter where it changed hands.

She told herself that last part three times while she waited. It didn't help.

The third man wore a gray coat that had never once been rained on. He sat without asking, set a leather folio on the table between them, and folded his hands like a man settling in for scripture.

"You're Doss Merren," he said. "First mate of the *Meridian*. Eleven years with the Whitlock family." He smiled at her the way an appraiser smiles at a cracked vase—sympathetically, because he already knew what it was worth. "That's loyalty. Rare thing, these days."

"What do you want."

"Your route." He opened the folio. Inside were charts—not the consortium's standard river surveys but something finer, annotated in a hand Doss recognized from a hundred cargo manifests. Yara's hand. Her stomach turned over slowly, like a boat taking water. "We know you're going upstream. We know you're carrying something. What we don't know is which channel you'll take past Karst Reach, or when. That information, delivered honestly, is worth—" he slid a paper across, "—this."

Doss looked at the number and made the mistake of letting her face show it.

"There it is," the man said softly. "I've seen that look on a lot of faces. River folk especially. You spend your whole life being paid by the mile, and then someone offers you ten years of miles all at once, and you find out what your loyalty weighs." He tapped the paper. "It's not even betrayal, Miss Merren. Not really. The captain's mixed up in something old and dead—a drowned country, sealed chests, songs people sing drunk. You'd be pulling her out of a grave. Family should thank you."

"She's not my family," Doss said, and heard how thin it sounded even as she said it.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "But you've been pretending a long time, so I expect one more pretense won't strain you."

She took the paper. She told herself she wouldn't act on it—that she'd take the advance and lose it at cards, or bury it, or hand it back. She told herself a great many things that night, and the river outside the window kept moving the way rivers do, indifferent to the arithmetic of men.

* * *

Three days later, anchored in the willow shade below Cutter's Bluff, Doss passed the message.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the shame of it—the whole rotten business fit inside a routine. She went ashore at the fishing hamlet to buy salt pork, as she did every run, and left a folded chart-corner weighted under a stone at the base of the third mooring post, as she had done once before, at Marrow Bend, telling herself that first time had been a test, a probe, nothing committed. This time there was a date on it. A channel. *Karst Reach side-water, four days hence.* Enough for the consortium to be waiting.

She got back aboard and couldn't look at anyone.

Sef was coiling line on the foredeck, her movements precise and unhurried, the way they always were. The quiet passenger. Doss had watched her for two weeks now and still couldn't read her—couldn't tell if those steady gray eyes missed anything, and if they did, whether they bothered keeping score. The other passengers played cards and complained about the food. Sef watched the banks. Doss had assumed she was afraid of pursuit.

Now, standing at the rail with the empty space under the mooring post behind her, Doss wondered what else on this boat was watching.

* * *

It came apart faster than she expected, and worse.

They raised the hamlet's dock at midmorning two days later to take on water, and Yara went ashore herself—which she almost never did, which meant she'd heard something, which meant everything was already lost before Doss understood the shape of it. Doss stood at the gangway pretending to check cargo lashings and watched the captain stop at the third mooring post, crouch, and come up holding nothing at all, because Doss had retrieved the stone's placement mark that morning in a panic and moved it, and the movement itself was the confession.

Yara stood there a long moment. Then she looked up—not at the post, not at the water, but directly at Doss, across forty feet of morning air, and Doss felt the look go through her like a gig through a sturgeon.

"Everyone on deck," Yara said. Not loudly. She never needed to be loud; the boat had trained her voice the way wind trains a tree. "All hands. Now."

They gathered in the waist of the *Meridian*: Elias Vane with his archivist's pallor and his too-many questions; the two paying passengers blinking and grumbling; Sef, arriving last, positioning herself where she could see both the gangway and the crew; and Doss, whose hands would not stay still, who finally clasped them behind her back like a cadet.

Yara held up the chart-corner. Even folded, Doss knew its edges the way you know your own teeth.

"Somebody left this at the mooring post," Yara said. "Marked with our route and a date. Addressed, I'd wager, to men in gray coats who've been asking about us at every dock since Marrow Bend." She let the silence work. She'd learned that from her father, Doss thought wildly, from the same father whose handwriting was inked inside a chest full of ghosts, and the thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome: *maybe betrayal runs in boats.* "I'm not going to guess. I'm going to ask once. Who did it?"

Nobody spoke. The river slapped the hull. Somewhere upstream a lock bell clanged, faint and stupid and ordinary.

"It was me."

Every head turned. Doss hadn't decided to say it. Her mouth simply went ahead, the way a man steps off a dock he's been staring at too long. "It was me, Captain. And before you ask why—I'll not dress it up. They offered money. Real money. Ten years of wages for a chart-corner and a date." She lifted her chin, because if she was going to hang she'd hang square. "I took it."

The sound that came off the crew was ugly. One of the paying passengers swore. Old Tam, the engineer, looked at Doss the way you look at a hull breach—as though the water were already around his ankles. And Yara—

Yara didn't move. That was the terrible part. Doss had prepared for rage, for shouting, for the crack of a fist or the bark of dismissal. Instead the captain just stood with the folded chart in her hand and looked at her first mate of eleven years with an expression Doss had only ever seen her wear at funerals.

"Eleven years," Yara said quietly. "You ate at my table. You helped me scatter my mother's ashes off Harker Point. When my father died you held the boat together for six months because I couldn't stand up straight." She unfolded the chart-corner, studied it as if it might contain some explanation the words hadn't. "And you sold us for a number."

"The number was fair," Doss said, and hated herself for it, and kept going anyway because stopping now would mean feeling it. "Captain—you're chasing a dead nation up a dead-end river over a box somebody fished out of a flood. Gray coats are burning towns downstream over whatever's in that chest. I've got a bad knee and no pension and a sister in Vell Hollow who needs surgery I can't pay for. You want to call that betrayal fine. I call it arithmetic."

"Arithmetic." Yara nodded slowly. "My father used to say the river doesn't do arithmetic. It does gravity. Everything rolls downhill eventually, and everything lands where it lands." She turned to the crew. "Here's where it lands. We're two days from Karst Reach, and if the consortium knows our route, they know it because of her. Every soul on this boat is in danger because of her. So she doesn't sail with us."

"Yara—" Tam started.

"That's *Captain*," Doss snapped, surprising everyone including herself, "or have we stopped pretending there's a chain of command on this floating funeral?"

Yara looked at her for a long moment. "There was never any pretending," she said. "That was the difference between us."

* * *

They put her ashore on the sandbar below Cutter's Bluff.

It was Yara's decision and she made it without cruelty, which somehow made it harder to bear than cruelty would have been. The bar was a long crescent of pale gravel and bleached driftwood, maybe two hundred yards end to end, high enough to sit above the flood line, with a fisherman's hut at its landward tip—roof intact, door latched, a stack of split cordwood under oilcloth. Yara had clearly scouted it before she'd ever called the meeting. Doss understood that too, and it stung worst of all: the captain had known for days. Had let her make her delivery, let her tie herself to the deed completely, and then chosen the ground.

"You'll have the skiff," Yara said, walking the bar beside her while two deckhands unloaded Doss's sea chest, her tarpaulin, a cask of water, a sack of hardtack and salt pork. "Oars, a lantern, oil. The hamlet's six miles downbank—you can walk the gravel shelf at low water or row the cut when the level drops, probably tomorrow evening. There's coin in the chest. Your full shares through this leg, plus severance. I'm not starving you and I'm not stranding you. I'm just not sailing with you."

"Generous," Doss said.

"I'm trying to be." Yara stopped and faced her. The flood-swollen river ran brown and muscular beyond the bar's edge, hauling branches and fence rails and one entire uprooted orchard tree past them toward the sea. "Doss. Last chance, and I mean it as a friend, whatever else I am now. Whatever they promised you—they'll take the information and they'll still burn your name at every dock from here to the delta, because that's what they do with people who sell things. You'll be worth more to them as an example than as an asset. Come with me instead. Give me what you gave them, and I'll forgive it. I'll even mean it, eventually."

For one moment—one long, swaying moment—Doss nearly did it. She saw it all laid out plain: the apology, the redemption, the slow rebuilding of trust over years of honest miles, the way you'd rebuild a hull plank by plank. She saw her sister's face too, and the surgeon's fee, and the gray coat man's voice saying *ten years of miles.*

"I can't," she said. It came out smaller than she wanted. "The money's half spent already."

Something closed in Yara's face. Not anger. Worse—an ending. "Then I hope it buys what you need," she said, and turned and walked back across the gravel, and did not look back, and Doss stood alone on the sandbar with her life in boxes around her and understood that she had just watched eleven years walk away without looking back either.

* * *

The crew fractured along lines Doss could map from the shore, which was a special kind of torture.

She watched it from the hut's doorway through the afternoon as the *Meridian* lay at anchor a quarter mile off, taking on water. Tam wouldn't speak to the young deckhands who'd sided with Doss's arithmetic—two of them, Pell and young Osk, who'd muttered that night at supper that the captain had gone mad chasing ghosts. The paying passengers had heard everything; one demanded his fare back and was refused. Elias Vane paced the foredeck like a penned animal, radiating the particular frustration of a man whose grand mission kept snagging on ordinary human weakness. And Sef—

Sef did something small, that evening, that Doss watched from two hundred yards and never forgot.

A quarrel broke out on deck. Pell, liquored on contraband gin, got loud about the chest, about what was in it, about how much the consortium would pay for it *whole* rather than for a chart-corner—and Doss saw the idea move through him like current through water, saw him look at the hold hatch, saw Osk grab his arm and the two of them start shoving. Tam waded in. The passengers scattered. And in the middle of it, before Yara could even reach the deck, Sef moved—not fast, not dramatic, just *precisely*, stepping between Pell and the hatch with a boathook taken off its brackets, reversed, held low and easy, and said something none of them could hear at that distance but which stopped Pell cold. Then she set the boathook down, picked up the dropped gin bottle, poured its contents over the side, and went back to coiling line as if nothing had happened.

By midnight Pell and Osk had each had a private word with the captain. By morning they were signed off at the hamlet dock with fair shares and no hard words—or no more than the situation deserved. The crew that remained was smaller, quieter, and harder. Doss watched the *Meridian* raise anchor at dawn, saw Yara at the wheel and Sef beside her on the pilothouse stair—*beside* her, in the place Doss had stood for eleven years—and felt the true cost of her arithmetic arrive all at once, the way a bill arrives after the meal.

She sat in the fisherman's hut with her coins spread on the table and counted them, and counted them again, and the number didn't change, and neither did the hollow feeling underneath it.

* * *

The gray coats came at dusk.

Two of them, poling a flat-bottomed skiff out of the willows with the ease of men who'd done it before, and Doss knew before they touched the bar that the chart-corner had been delivered, that her name was in their ledger now, that Yara had been right about the example. The tall one from the Ferryman's Rest stepped onto the gravel and smiled his appraiser's smile.

"Miss Merren. Efficient work. The date and channel were most helpful." He glanced at the hut, the woodpile, the neat little exile she'd been marooned into. "Our captain has a sense of theater, I see. Sandbar justice." He said it lightly, but his eyes were doing sums. "Tell me everything about the boat's condition. Crew remaining. Where they'll shelter between here and the Reach. Do that, and there's a second payment in it—and passage south afterward, wherever you like."

Doss looked at the money he set on the table. She thought of her sister's knee-deep debt. She thought of Sef pouring gin into the river, and Yara's face closing like a door.

"They're running the flooded side-channel at Karst Reach," she heard herself say. "The one on the old survey charts. Nobody runs it at this water level. She thinks nobody knows it exists."

The gray coat man wrote it down. "And the chest?"

"On board. In the hold, under the forward ballast."

He thanked her. He actually thanked her, courteously, and left a purse heavier than the first, and poled away into the dusk, and Doss sat alone on the sandbar listening to the river eat the light, and understood with perfect clarity that she had just sold the last thing she had left to sell.

Then she noticed the skiff was gone.

Her skiff—the escape Yara had left her, oars and lantern and all—was not tied at the bar's edge where she'd left it. She walked the gravel twice, heart climbing, before she found the answer dragged up on the far spit: fresh boot prints, two sets, leading from the waterline to the spot where the skiff had been, and back. Taken hours ago. Taken while the gray coats watched her count her coins.

Which meant someone had been on this sandbar tonight before the gray coats ever arrived. Someone who had watched her give up the side-channel. Someone who now had the only boat within six miles—and a head start downriver toward Karst Reach, toward the *Meridian*, toward whatever they intended to do with what they knew.

Doss stood in the failing light with a stolen purse in her hand and a cold spreading through her middle that had nothing to do with the river wind, and began, for the first time in eleven years, to be genuinely afraid of what was coming down the water.
