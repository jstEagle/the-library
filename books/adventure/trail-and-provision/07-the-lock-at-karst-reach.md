## 7. The Lock at Karst Reach

The Karst Reach announced itself an hour before they saw it—not by sight but by sound, a low grinding hum that Yara felt through the soles of her boots before she heard it with her ears. The river narrowed there, squeezed between walls of pale limestone riddled with caves and springs, and the consortium had built its locks into the throat of the passage like a fist closed around a windpipe.

She stood at the wheel well before dawn, watching the water change. Upstream of the reach the current ran brown and fat with snowmelt, but here it quickened, whitened, began to talk to itself in the language of stone.

"You've been quiet since the sandbar," Sef said, coming up beside her with two cups of chicory. She handed one over without asking. "Quieter than usual."

"Doss is still back there." Yara took the cup. "Marooned on a pile of wet gravel with a week's provisions and a traitor's conscience."

"He sold us."

"I know what he sold us." She drank. The chicory was bitter and hot and exactly right. "I'm deciding how much of him I mourn."

Sef didn't answer that, which was one of the things Yara was learning to like about her. The quiet woman asked questions like a surgeon made incisions—precisely, only when necessary—and otherwise kept her own counsel. Yara still hadn't decided whether that was wisdom or training. Consortium surveillance officers, if that was what Sef was, would be trained to be easy company.

The Meridian rounded the last bend, and there it was: Karst Reach.

Two lock gates of black iron spanned the channel, set into masonry older than the consortium itself—Vessian work, Elias said, though he said everything was Vessian work. A signal tower rose from the near bank, flying the consortium's gray-and-gold pennant, and beside it a customs house with a chain stretched across the approach channel, its links thick as Yara's wrist. On the upstream side, beyond the locks, the river waited, higher than it should have been, swollen with the spring flood and backing up against the dam works forty miles north.

A man in consortium gray stood on the lock wall with a speaking trumpet.

"Vessel approaching! State your name, cargo, and port of registry!"

Yara cupped her hands. "Meridian! Out of Corlow Landing! Timber goods and general freight!"

"Stand off the chain. Inspection and toll assessment. All cargo holds will be opened."

Elias appeared at the rail, gray coat buttoned to his chin, and Yara watched him take in the signal tower, the customs house, the four armed men visible even at this distance. His face did something complicated and then settled into calm, the way a man smooths a bedsheet over something hidden beneath.

"They'll want the chest," he said quietly.

"They'll want everything." Yara turned the wheel, bringing the Meridian around in a slow arc away from the chain. "That's what tolls are. They just haven't written down yet that this particular toll is you."

* * *

They anchored in a willow-choked cut two miles downstream, close enough to watch the locks through glasses, far enough that the sentries' lanterns were just smudges of orange. Yara spread her father's old charts on the chart table in the stern cabin—the good ones, the ones she'd inherited along with the boat and the debt—and weighted the corners with brass fittings.

"There's no way past," Doss's replacement, a broad-shouldered deckhand named Pell, said flatly. He was new this season, hired out of Corlow, and he looked at the charts the way a man looks at a sermon he doesn't believe. "The reach is a hundred yards wide between the bluffs. The locks are the only gap in the rock. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows what everyone knows," Yara said. "Which is why the consortium built their fist where everyone can see it." Her finger traced the south bluff, where the contour lines crowded together like a crowd pressing toward a door. "But my father didn't learn this river from charts. He learned it from men who poled it before the dams, when the water did what it pleased. And those men left marks."

She found it where she knew it would be—a faint pencil line, faded almost to nothing, running from the main channel up over the south bank and rejoining the river half a mile above the locks. Beside it, in her father's cramped hand: *Old channel. Runs when the river runs high. Watch for the splitter rock.*

"The flood's put six feet of extra water on this stretch," she said. "The old channel's been dry as a sermon for thirty years, except in flood years. It runs behind the bluff through what used to be a side slough, comes back in above the reach. Half a mile of water where the maps say there's trees."

Pell leaned over the chart, squinting. "Through the woods?"

"Through what used to be the woods. When that channel runs, it runs deep—it carved that bluff for ten thousand years before the river changed its mind. But it's narrow, and it turns twice, and there's a rock in the middle of the second turn that shears hulls open like a knife through bread." She tapped the pencil note. "My father called it the splitter. He ran it twice. He told me about it exactly once, the night before he died, and I thought he was telling me stories to keep me from worrying about him."

"And now?" Sef asked.

"Now I think he was leaving me a road." Yara rolled the chart closed. "The chest goes below, wrapped in oilcloth and lashed to the keelson. If we ship water, the chest floats free in the hold rather than going overboard. Elias—you're the lightest, you'll be on the bow with a boathook calling the turns. Sef takes the lead line. Everybody else lash themselves to something solid."

Elias had gone very still. "You're serious. You'd take a ninety-foot steamboat through an unmapped flood channel to avoid paying a toll."

"I'd take her through hell itself to keep consortium hands off that chest." Yara met his eyes. "The question is whether you're brave or just enthusiastic."

Something flickered across his face—there and gone, too fast to name. "I'm both," he said. "Unfortunately."

* * *

They raised anchor at first gray light, when the sentries on the lock tower would be rubbing sleep out of their eyes and the river mist lay thick enough to hide a cathedral. Yara brought the Meridian down the middle of the channel at bare steerageway, engine ticking over at a whisper, until the south bluff slid abeam. Then she rang the engine room bell twice—slow ahead—and put the wheel over.

The willows parted. There it was.

The old channel opened in the bank like a mouth, a lane of moving water threading back into the flooded timber, its surface deceptively calm in the mist. Six feet of floodwater had drowned the underbrush; dead saplings stood to their chins, and the current within the channel ran fast and glassy, sliding between the trunks of trees that had been forest floor a generation ago.

"Sweet mercy," Pell breathed.

"Mercy's got nothing to do with it," Yara said. "Slow ahead. Sef, sound your leads."

Sef stood at the bow rail with the lead line coiled in her hand, and she swung it with a rhythm that was almost musical. "By the mark five!" she called. "By the mark five! And a quarter less five!"

Five fathoms. More water than the Meridian drew by three times over. Yara allowed herself one breath of something that wasn't quite relief.

Then the channel narrowed, and the trees closed in, and it stopped being a survey and started being a fight.

The banks pressed close—close enough that the branches of drowned oaks scraped along the guards with a sound like fingernails on a coffin lid. The mist swallowed everything beyond twenty feet. Yara steered by the current itself, reading the water the way other people read print: the smooth V of faster flow marking the deep channel, the faint dimpling where a submerged snag slowed the stream, the telltale boil over the stump of a tree that had been full-grown when her father was young. The wheel never stopped moving. Small corrections, constant corrections, the kind that looked like nothing and were everything.

"Turn coming!" Elias called from the bow, his voice tight. "I can see the bend—trees end ahead, open water to the right!"

"To the right means the splitter," Yara muttered. "Sef!"

"By the mark four!" A pause. The lead swung again. "Four! Less four!"

Less four fathoms. Still plenty—but the bottom was rising, which meant they were swinging wide of the old thalweg, drifting toward the outside of the coming turn, and the outside of the turn was where the splitter waited.

Yara rang the bell. "Half ahead!"

The engine note climbed. The Meridian surged, and Yara put the wheel hard over to starboard, swinging the bow toward the inside of the turn, toward the shallows she could feel more than see. For three heartbeats they were committed—no taking it back, no stopping, the boat answering her hand with the sluggish obedience of ninety feet of loaded wood and iron.

The turn came all at once, the way turns do.

The channel bent left, hard, and the whole flooded world seemed to pivot around them. Dead trees swept past the windows. The current grabbed the stern and tried to swing it wide, out, around, into the path of the rock, and Yara leaned into the wheel with her whole body, hauling it over, feeling the rudder bite and then stutter as it came into the shadow of the bank.

"Rock!" Elias's voice cracked. "Starboard bow! Rock!"

It rose out of the mist like the back of a sleeping animal—black limestone streaked white with bird lime, no more than a boat-length off the bow, water parting around it in a V of white foam. The splitter. Close enough to touch. Close enough that Yara could see the old scars in it, grooves worn by hulls that hadn't read her father's charts.

"Full astern!" she shouted down the tube, and slammed the telegraph over.

The engine reversed with a shudder that ran the length of the boat. The Meridian's headway died, hung, and began to fall backward—but the current had the stern now, and the stern swung, and for one endless second Yara watched the boat pivot sideways across the channel like a gate swinging shut, beam-on to the rock, beam-on to the current, dead and helpless as driftwood.

No. Not helpless. She'd spent thirty years learning what this river did when nobody was watching.

"Stern line!" she roared. "Pell—stern line to the big oak, port side! Now!"

Pell moved like a man who'd stopped thinking, which was the only way to move. He hooked the line over the bollard and flung the coil at the trunk of a massive drowned oak leaning out of the water on the port quarter. The line caught, held, snapped taut—and the Meridian checked, the swing arrested, the boat hanging in the current with the splitter's foam washing along her starboard guard close enough to spit into.

For a moment nobody breathed.

Then the current, finding the boat held at the stern, did the only thing it could do: it pushed the bow. Pushed it around, down-stream, away from the rock, pivoting the Meridian on her mooring line like a dancer on a partner's hand. Yara watched the bow swing through the turn—through the exact line she'd wanted, the deep channel, the safe water—as if the river itself were putting her where she belonged.

"Cast off!" she shouted, and Pell slashed the line free, and the Meridian came out of the second turn of the old channel heading true, upright, alive, with the splitter sliding past her starboard quarter close enough to count the lichens.

The channel straightened. Widened. The mist thinned, and ahead—half a mile, no more—the old channel spilled back into the main river above the locks, joining it in a smooth braided fan of current that no sentry was watching because no sentry had ever needed to.

Yara became aware that her hands ached. She looked down and saw her knuckles bone-white on the spokes, and made herself loosen them finger by finger.

"Well," she said, and found her voice was rougher than she expected. "Toll paid."

* * *

They came out into the main channel in bright morning sun, the Karst Reach locks already falling astern, and the crew made noise the way crews do—laughter too loud, jokes repeated twice, the giddy release of people who had genuinely believed they might die before breakfast. Even Pell was grinning. Sef coiled her lead line with the unhurried economy of someone filing a tool, but Yara caught her looking back at the gorge walls with an expression she couldn't read, and decided not to ask. Not yet.

Elias came aft to the wheel, and up close Yara could see that his composure had cost him something. His hands weren't quite steady. There was a scratch along his jaw where a willow branch had caught him, and he touched it absently, like a man confirming a wound is real.

"That channel," he said. "Your father showed you."

"My father told me. Once. I chose to remember it."

"Do you understand what that means?" His voice dropped low, urgent, and there was that fervor again, the zealot's fire she'd seen the night he'd shown her the treaty. "A courier for the consortium—who knows every smuggler's path on this river? Who leaves his daughter a map of ways around their locks, their tolls, their eyes?" He shook his head slowly. "He wasn't working for them, Captain. Whatever he told himself. Men don't leave escape routes for the people they serve."

Yara looked at him for a long moment. The sun was warm on the deck. Behind them, the locks of Karst Reach glinted uselessly in the distance, guarding a river that had already gone by.

"Or he was covering both sides so thoroughly that even his escape routes had two doors," she said. "I've learned not to build cathedrals out of my father's handwriting, Vane. It collapses."

But the words sat strangely in her mouth, because he was right about one thing, and she'd known it since the sandbar: her father had known things he shouldn't have. Had drawn lines on charts that only a man trusted by both sides could draw. The chest below her feet, lashed to the keelson in its oilcloth, was proof of that much.

She put it out of her mind and gave the river her attention, because the river demanded it. Ahead the valley widened, the water smoothed, and the Meridian settled into her long stride, eating miles.

It was Sef who spotted it—an hour later, a smudge of smoke rising from the tree line on the north bank where no town should be. Yara raised the glasses and found a landing: a wood-pile dock, a lean-to, a single flat-bottomed skiff pulled up on the mud. And standing on the dock, waving both arms over his head with the desperate energy of a man signaling a passing train, a figure she recognized even at half a mile.

Doss.

Her first mate. Her betrayer. Marooned, soaked, and flagging them down like the prodigal son.

Yara lowered the glasses slowly. Beside her, Sef's hand had drifted, very casually, to the knife at her belt.

"Captain?" Pell called from the deck. "Do we stop?"

Yara looked at the figure on the dock, at the smoke, at the long miles of river ahead, and turned the question over in her mind like a stone she wasn't sure she wanted to lift.

"Bring her alongside," she heard herself say. "Easy. And somebody get a rifle on deck."
