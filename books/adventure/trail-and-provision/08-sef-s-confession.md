## 8. Sef's Confession

The tattoo itched before rain. It had always itched before rain, ever since the ink man in Corvane's lower ward had pressed the needle into the soft skin inside her wrist—a small coiled serpent swallowing its own tail, the mark of the Meridian Compact's survey division. Fifteen years, and her body still kept its own ledger of storms.

Sef stood at the rail of the *Meridian* as the first fat drops came down and watched the water dimple gray on gray, and thought: tonight. Tonight she would tell Yara Whitlock, or she would slip over the side at the next mooring and let the river decide what she was worth.

Behind her, Elias Vane's voice drifted from the wheelhouse, low and animated—he was showing the captain something in the chest again, or pretending to. Doss was below with the engine, cursing a fuel line. The two paying passengers, Bren and his wife, played cards in the saloon and lost cheerfully to each other. An ordinary evening on an ordinary boat, if you didn't count the sealed chest of a drowned nation riding in the hold like a second keel, or the consortium lock-gates waiting downstream at Karst Reach, or the fact that Sef herself was the trap none of them had sprung yet.

She flexed her wrist. The serpent coiled and coiled.

*You were good at this once,* she told herself. *That's the whole sickness of it.*

Three weeks ago she had been sitting in a canal-side office in Corvane, across a desk from a supervisor named Ilex who smelled of clove oil and never blinked enough, receiving her orders: a riverboat called the *Meridian*, out of Lowmarsh, captain named Whitlock, daughter of the old courier. Book passage. Watch the chest. Report at every dock with a wire office. Simple work. She'd done simpler and harder both; surveillance was the one trade the Compact let women do without pretending they were something else, and Sef had been precise at it for eleven years. Precise, promoted, trusted. Her file was clean as new ice.

Then they'd sent her upriver ahead of the boat, to Karst Reach, to await further instruction—and she'd seen what awaited meant.

It was the village of Tarnholt that did it, though Tarnholt wasn't even on her assignment. She'd had two days to fill and a habit of walking, and she'd walked out past the lock-works into the terraced country above the reservoir, where the water behind the consortium dams lay green and still as glass in a dead man's eye. There were stumps there. Whole hillsides of them, drowned trees standing in six feet of clear water, and among the stumps—chimneys. She'd waded out before she understood what she was doing, boots filling, skirt heavy, and put her hand on one. A chimney, standing alone where a house used to be, and under the green water she could see the pale rectangle of a doorstep, and a fence line running off into the deep like a seam of stitching on a body.

Tarnholt hadn't flooded. Tarnholt had been *flooded*. The difference mattered. Rivers rose on their own schedule; men with charts and writs rose on theirs. And when she'd climbed back to the dry bank, wringing out her stockings like any traveler caught by weather, an old man had been watching her from the path—not hostile, just tired—and he'd said, "Surveyor?"

"Passenger," she'd said.

He'd nodded slowly, the way people nod when they've decided not to argue. "They surveyed us too," he said. "In '91. Nice young fellow with a theodolite. Took elevations for a month. We gave him eggs." He looked out at the drowned chimneys. "Two years later we gave up everything else."

Sef had reported to Ilex that evening, on schedule, in full detail, because that was what she was. And then she had sat awake in her rented room above the wire office until dawn, listening to the lock-gates groan open and shut below her window—the sound of the river being told where it was allowed to go—and understood that she had already made her choice somewhere between the doorstep and the old man's voice, and that everything since was only her body catching up.

So now: tonight.

* * *

Yara Whitlock found her at the rail, which Sef suspected was not an accident. The captain moved through her own boat the way water moves through a channel—finding every hollow, filling every space—and she had been finding Sef more often these last two days. Watching. Not unkindly. The way a pilot watches a shoal she hasn't charted yet.

"You're getting wet," Yara said, coming to stand beside her. She handed over a tin cup of something hot and bitter—dock tea, the kind that tasted like boiled rope but kept your hands steady. "Rain's set in for the night. We'll tie up at Willow Bend rather than run dark."

"The passengers won't like losing the hours."

"The passengers can lose the hours. I don't lose boats." Yara drank, looking downriver into the murk. Somewhere ahead, invisible in the rain, the Karst Reach locks waited with their consortium toll-men and their questions. Sef saw the captain's jaw tighten around the thought even though her face stayed easy. "You've been quiet since Karst came up in conversation. Quieter than quiet, and you started quiet."

"I've been thinking about getting off at Willow Bend," Sef said. It was true, in the way that all honest confessions begin true and then have to be made truer.

"Bad idea."

"It's a safe idea."

Yara turned and looked at her then, fully, and Sef felt the look go through her like a sounding line going through dark water—touching bottom, measuring. "Nothing on this river is safe right now," the captain said. "There are men asking after a chest at every dock south of the bend, my first mate sold me to them once already, and the man sleeping in my guest cabin claims to be an archivist of a country that isn't on maps. Safe left this boat at Lowmarsh." She took another drink. "If you want off, I'll put you off, and I'll wish you well doing it. But don't dress it up as safety. Dress it up as what it is."

"And what is it?"

"That's yours to say. You're the one leaving."

The rain thickened. Down in the saloon, Bren laughed at some card he'd been dealt, a big easy sound that seemed to come from a different world. Sef held the tin cup in both hands and felt the heat of it and thought about how many times she had stood in rooms like this—in towpath taverns, in lock-keepers' kitchens, in the cabins of boats she'd been sent to watch—with a cover story fitted to her mouth like a mouthguard, smooth and practiced, and how every one of those times she had been protected by exactly this: that nobody asked a second question, because nobody cared enough about a quiet woman to ask one.

Yara Whitlock was asking a second question. That was the trouble with her. That was why the report Sef should have wired from Karst Reach sat unwritten, and why the wire office at Willow Bend would go unvisited tonight, and why the serpent on her wrist itched like a wound deciding whether to close or open.

"If I tell you something," Sef said, "you're going to want to throw me in the river. I'd like to ask you to hear all of it first. All of it. Before you decide."

Yara set her cup on the rail cap. When she spoke, her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a woman clearing debris out of a channel. "You're consortium."

Not a question. Sexhaled. "Inside my left wrist. Survey division, eleven years. My name in their rolls is Sefa Marren, and three weeks ago I was given this boat and this chest and your father's name, and orders to follow all three and report at every wire office south of the Reach." She made herself keep eye contact. "I haven't filed a report since Karst. I burned my instructions there. And I am telling you this now, in the rain, on your deck, because I saw what they do to the places they drown, and I would rather be thrown off this boat than be what I was."

For a long moment Yara said nothing. The rain filled the silence, drumming on the deck boards, running off the awning in bright threads. Sef watched the captain's face and read it the way she'd been trained to read faces—except that training had never prepared her for this, for wanting desperately to be misread, or rather to be read truly, which was the opposite skill entirely.

"My father," Yara said at last, slowly, "carried things for people who paid him. I learned that much recently, and I'm still learning what it means. So I know something about finding out that someone you've shared a deck with isn't who you thought." Her eyes flicked toward the hold, where the chest slept. "Doss sold our route for coin. You sold your eyes for wages. The difference I'm trying to weigh is that Doss did it for greed and you—" she stopped, jaw working, "—you're standing here telling me yourself, which no agent alive would do unless she'd broken, or unless this is the longest game anyone's ever played against me."

"It's not a game."

"Prove it."

"How?"

"I don't know yet," Yara said, and there was something almost angry in her honesty, something raw. "That's the truth, Sef. I don't know what proof looks like anymore. Every proof I've been handed this month has come with rot underneath. But here's what I do know." She stepped closer, and the rain ran down between them like a curtain neither would part. "You could have gone over the side at Willow Bend like you said. Clean escape, no confession, no risk. Agents don't walk away from a live operation to stand in the rain and hand me their wrist. Whatever you were, whatever they made you—that choice back there wasn't theirs. It was yours."

"They'll send others," Sef said. "Now that I've missed my reports, they'll assume the worst and move faster. Crane doesn't wait. He'll have gunboats at the Reach by tomorrow night, maybe sooner. He may already have bought someone aboard."

"Doss is ashore."

"Doss isn't the only one who's ever needed money." Sef heard her own voice drop. "Captain—I need to show you something else. Not my wrist. Something I took from the Karst office when I burned my instructions."

Yara went very still. "What kind of something?"

"A schedule." Sef reached into her coat, into the oilcloth packet sewn against her ribs where the tattoo hid on the other arm, and drew out a folded sheet of thin paper, damp at the edges despite everything she'd done to protect it. "Crane's dispatch rotation for the upper river. Gunboat assignments, lock garrisons, wire codes. Eleven years, Captain—you don't spend eleven years in that house without learning where the keys hang. If you're going to run the Reach, you should run it knowing exactly what's hunting you. Consider it—" she searched for the word and hated that she had to search, "—consider it the first payment on a very large debt."

Yara took the paper. She read it under the awning light, and Sef watched the captain's face change as the numbers resolved—watched the pilot's mind take hold of the intelligence and start turning it over, testing it against her own knowledge of the river, checking it for sandbars and snags. It was, Sef thought, like watching someone read a chart of a place they'd sailed blind their whole life.

"This puts a patrol steamer at the Reach itself," Yara murmured. "And two more at the narrows above. And this code column—" she looked up sharply. "This is current. This week's rotation."

"I memorized the cipher years ago. It's how they test loyalty—leave papers out, see who reads them. I always read them. I was very loyal." The word came out of her mouth like a stone she'd been carrying and finally set down, and it made no sound at all when it landed, and somehow that was worse than if it had crashed.

Yara folded the paper along its creases, precisely, and put it inside her coat. Then she looked at Sef's left wrist—at the sleeve covering it—and said, "Show me."

Sef pushed the wet fabric back. The serpent gleamed dully in the lamplight, tail in mouth, patient as it had been for fifteen years.

Yara studied it without touching. "Does it mean what I think it means? The Compact eats itself. Everything comes back around to serve the center."

"It means I belonged to them before I belonged to myself. It means I was nineteen and hungry and they were the only ones offering." Sef pulled the sleeve down. "It means I can't ever fully prove what I am, Captain, because the proof is under the skin and it doesn't come off. That's the cost. I knew it when I defected and I know it now. Nobody switches sides cleanly. You just pick a direction and bleed toward it."

The rain eased, the way mountain rain does—all at once, like a decision. Beyond the rail the river ran high and brown and powerful, carrying the spring melt down from country none of them had seen, and somewhere up in that country was a valley, and a source, and whatever future was folded inside a sealed chest in the hold.

Yara picked up her tea, found it cold, and poured it over the rail without ceremony. "Here's what happens now," she said. "You sleep in the forward cabin, not the crew quarters—not punishment, just prudence; my crew's trust is a cracked hull right now and I won't load it heavier. You eat at my table. You don't touch the chest. And when we reach the Reach, you stand next to me on the wheelhouse deck and you read that water with me, because if half of what's in your paper is true, I'm going to need every pair of eyes that knows how the Compact thinks." She paused. "And Sef—if there's a wire office at Willow Bend, you'll walk past it with me, in front of my crew, so everyone sees whose side the spy stands on now. Trust isn't private on a boat. It has to be public or it isn't real."

"Yes, Captain."

"Don't call me that yet. Call me that when we're through the Reach." Yara turned to go, then stopped, half-facing her, and for a moment the captain's composure slipped and Sef saw the younger woman underneath, the daughter, the one still grieving a father who had turned out to be made of contradictions. "Why?" Yara asked quietly. "Eleven years, and then Tarnholt, and then this. What actually tipped you? People don't burn their lives down over drowned chimneys."

Sef thought about the doorstep under the green water. About the old man and his eggs. About the sound the lock-gates made, telling the river where it was allowed to go.

"Because I realized I'd spent my whole career drawing maps of places I refused to look at," she said. "And I was tired, Captain. Tired in a way sleep doesn't fix."

Yara nodded once, slow, as if filing that away in some private ledger of her own. Then she went aft toward the wheelhouse, and Sef stood alone at the rail with the rain stopping and the river talking to itself in the dark, and she became aware that she was crying—silently, uselessly, the way you cry when a long illness breaks—and that for the first time in fifteen years the tears weren't for anything she'd done.

They were for the fact that someone had believed her anyway.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat in the forward cabin with the lamp turned low and listened to the boat breathe—timbers ticking, water slapping the hull, Doss's replacement snoring in the crew quarters—and she wrote, in a small notebook she'd kept for eleven years and never once written the truth in, the full account of what she was and what she'd seen, dated and signed, to be opened if she died. Insurance, of a kind. Or a confession, of a kind. On a boat like this the two were probably the same document.

Near midnight there was a knock, and Elias Vane leaned in without waiting, holding two cups of the dock tea and wearing the expression of a man who has been eavesdropping successfully and wants credit for discretion.

"I heard," he said simply. "Walls on riverboats are paper." He handed her a cup and settled onto the opposite bunk, folding his long frame down, and for a moment the zealot mask slipped and what was underneath looked almost gentle. "You should know that what you did tonight matters more than you think. The movement has been blind for a century. Eyes inside the Compact—" He shook his head. "There are people in the valley who will weep when they hear there was a serpent who turned."

"There are people in the valley who will shoot first when they hear it too."

"Also true." He sipped his tea. "Which is why I'm glad the captain believes you. Though I confess I'm curious how far that belief extends." His eyes went to the notebook in her lap, mild and interested. "Because tomorrow we pass the Reach, and after the Reach, Sef, the river stops being a river and starts being a reckoning. Everyone aboard will have to choose what they carry. Even her." He nodded vaguely aft, toward the wheelhouse, toward Yara. "Especially her, I think. Her father's shadow is longer than she knows."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

Elias smiled, stood, and collected his own cup. "Nothing. Archivist's habit—we see shadows everywhere. Occupational disease." At the door he paused. "Sleep if you can. Tomorrow we find out what you're worth."

He left, and Sef sat with the lamp low and the notebook open on her knees, and outside the rain began again, soft and steady, and inside her left wrist the serpent itched, and itched, and itched—though whether from the coming storm or from something finally healing, she couldn't yet say.

Upstream, unseen in the dark, the lights of the Karst Reach lock-works glimmered faintly on the rain, and among them, a telegraph key began to chatter.
