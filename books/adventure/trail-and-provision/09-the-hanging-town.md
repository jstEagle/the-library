## 9. The Hanging Town

Elias Vane had spent eleven years learning to read a country that no longer existed. He could parse the water-stained marginalia of Vessian shipping manifests, could recite the liturgy of the drowned calendar, could look at a bend in the river and tell you which village had stood there before the consortium's engineers opened the sluices and let the valley drink itself to death. He had thought this made him prepared for anything.

He was not prepared for Brassen's Hollow.

They smelled it before they saw it—coal smoke and tar and something sweeter underneath, like fruit left too long in the sun. The Meridian came around the last bluff at midmorning, Yara at the wheel with her collar up against the spray, and the town unfolded along the near bank in tiers of gray timber: a mill town once, Elias guessed from the ruined flume, now garrisoned. Consortium banners hung from every eave, black-and-white chevrons gone limp in the windless air, and on the flat ground beside the old market square someone had erected three gallows.

Not scaffolds. Gallows—proper ones, with fresh-cut beams still bleeding sap, built by men who expected to use them again.

"Slow us down," Sef said quietly from the rail. She had appeared without sound, as she always did, and her eyes were fixed on the square. "There's a crowd."

Yara eased the throttle back until the Meridian was barely making way against the current, close enough to the bank that Elias could see individual faces in the gathering below: dockhands, mill workers, women with children hoisted onto their shoulders so they wouldn't miss it. A festival atmosphere. That was the worst of it—the paper lanterns strung between the lampposts, the vendors already working the edges of the crowd with roasted chestnuts and cups of warmed wine. Someone had decided an execution was an occasion.

Three figures stood on the platform beneath the beams. Two men and a woman, hands bound, and even at this distance Elias could see what they were wearing: the high-collared coats of the old Vessian style, deliberately archaic, deliberately defiant. Sympathizers. The word the consortium used for anyone who remembered.

A garrison officer moved along the platform reading from a document, his voice carrying across the water in fragments. *"—crimes against the River Compact—sedition—distribution of forbidden materials—"*

"Forbidden materials," Sef repeated softly. "Books. He means books."

"We should pass wide," Yara said. Her hand was steady on the wheel but her jaw was set in a way Elias had learned to read as anger held under pressure. "There's nothing here for us."

"There is everything here," Elias said, and heard his own voice come out strange, too loud, too bright. "That's the point. That's exactly the point. They want us to pass wide. They want the whole river to pass wide and pretend this is weather."

"Elias." Yara didn't turn from the window. "I've got cargo contracts, a cracked hull we patched at Vell Hollow, and two passengers I'm responsible for. We are not stopping."

"I'm not asking you to stop the boat." He was already moving toward the gangway, fumbling with the latch, and he would remember afterward how calm his hands seemed, how reasonable his own voice sounded, the way a man's voice always sounds reasonable to himself right up until the moment the world proves him a fool. "I'm asking you to drop me at the landing. I'll go in alone. I only need an hour. There will be people in that crowd who remember Vessia—who might know the source families, who might have kept correspondence—and if I stand among them, if they see one of their own—"

"See what?" Yara crossed to him in three strides and took his arm, not gently. "See a man in a fine coat shouting Vessian words at a garrison? You'll be the fourth body on that platform before noon."

"They're going to die anyway."

"Yes." She said it flatly, without cruelty, and somehow that was worse than any argument. "Yes, they are. And you dying next to them changes nothing except the number."

"You don't understand what it is to watch this," Elias said, and pulled his arm free, harder than he meant to. "You pilot your boat and you count your miles and you keep your head down, and that's survival, I understand survival, I've done it. But I have spent my whole life in archives, Captain. Paper. Dead people's handwriting. I have read a hundred accounts of days like today, written by people who stood where I'm standing and did nothing, and every single one of them ends with the same sentence: *I should have spoken.* I will not write that sentence. I will not be another man who watched."

The words rang out over the deck, and the crew went quiet. Doss paused halfway up from the engine well. Even the river seemed to hold its breath between gusts.

Yara looked at him for a long moment. When she spoke again her voice was low, almost gentle, which he understood dimly was the most dangerous register she owned.

"When my father died," she said, "I found out he'd been lying to me for years. Everything I loved about him turned out to be a story I'd told myself. And do you know what saved me? Not truth. Not justice. Work. Getting up and piloting the boat, because the boat needed piloting and grief doesn't steer itself." She nodded toward the shore, toward the lanterns and the chestnuts and the waiting beams. "That down there isn't a battle. It's a message, written in bodies, and the message is *stay away*. You want to answer it? Fine. Answer it by getting the treaty to the source. That's the only speech that matters. Everything else is just noise they get to hang."

"And if there is no source?" His voice cracked on it. "If the valley's a story and the treaty's paper and all we're doing is running upstream from one hanging town to the next—then what have we survived *for*, Captain?"

She didn't answer. Behind them, from the square, came a drumroll.

* * *

He watched it happen. That was the thing he could not forgive himself for, later, in all the years he would carry it: he watched, because Yara would not put him ashore and he would not jump, and so he stood at the rail with his knuckles white on the wood while the drums stopped and the officer lowered his paper and the first trapdoor opened.

The sound carried across the water. He had not expected the sound. Archives did not contain the sound.

The woman went second. She did not struggle. She had turned her face toward the river before the end—toward the water, toward the old drowned country, or perhaps just toward the light on it—and Elias found himself mouthing the opening line of the Vessian burial rite, the one that began *We give you back to the current*, and his lips moved and no sound came out and the third trapdoor opened and then it was over and the crowd cheered.

Cheered. Chestnut smoke drifted out over the water. A child laughed somewhere near the front.

Sef stood beside him. He didn't know how long she'd been there. She said nothing, offered nothing, only stood with her arms crossed and her eyes closed partway, like a woman listening to music she hated.

When it was done, Yara brought the Meridian about, slow and deliberate, giving the town its full width of river, and took them upstream past the gallows close enough that Elias could see the officer checking his watch.

* * *

The argument happened that night, in the cabin, with the lamp turned low and the chest sitting between them like a third participant.

"You could have let me off," Elias said.

"No."

"You had no right—"

"I had every right. This is my boat." Yara sat across the chart table from him, a cup of tea untouched at her elbow, going cold. "And more than that—I told you the truth on that rail and you didn't hear it, so I'll say it plainer. If you'd gone ashore, you'd be dead, and the treaty would be in a consortium evidence locker, and everyone those people died believing in would stay dead forever. Your speech wouldn't have saved them. It would have just given the garrison a fourth coat to hang."

"You can't know that."

"No. I can't." She leaned forward. "But I've buried crew, Elias. I know what a body costs and what it buys, and that platform wasn't selling anything worth the price. What I need to know—the only thing I need to know—is whether you understand that. Because if some part of you is planning to martyr yourself at the first opportunity, then you're not a passenger anymore. You're cargo I can't afford."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above, footsteps crossed the deck, Sef taking the middle watch.

"What would you have me do?" Elias asked finally, and hated how small his voice was. "Forget it? Log it in a ledger and make way?"

"No. Remember it. Write it down, if writing's what you do—names, faces, the date, the officer's insignia if you got it. Put it in the book you're going to write about all this." She tapped the table once, hard. "But understand the difference, because it's the difference that keeps people alive. Witnessing is surviving and telling. Surviving is just surviving. And what you wanted to do today wasn't witnessing. It was dying with company."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Nothing about this river is fair. But it's true, and I deal in true things." She rose, took her cold tea, and poured it out the porthole into the dark water. At the door she paused. "Get some sleep, Archivist. Tomorrow we run the narrows above Brassen's Hollow, and I need your eyes on the banks, not on the past."

She left him alone with the lamp and the chest.

* * *

He did not sleep. Instead he sat with the treaty spread open under the lamp, the vellum soft as skin after its century underwater, and he read the annex pages for the hundredth time—or rather, he read the gaps where annex pages should have been, because there were gaps, there had been gaps since before Vell Hollow, and every day the gaps weighed a little more.

Three signatures named the heir. Three seals confirmed the succession. And one clause—a clause he had cut out with a razor in a rented room in Corvess two winters ago, and burned in a tin dish while his hands shook—named the bloodline precisely enough that anyone who read it would know the exiles had been following the wrong branch of the family tree for a generation.

*The succession passes through the daughter of the eastern house,* the missing page had said. *Let no claimant of the western line presume.*

He had told himself it was mercy. He had told himself the restoration movement would shatter, that the elders would tear each other apart, that Crane's agents would use the division to pick off the survivors one household at a time. He had told himself that a kinder lie was a load-bearing lie, and that when they reached the source he would find a way—some way—to let the truth emerge gently, on his terms, at a hour of his choosing.

But he had stood at a rail today and watched a woman die with her face turned toward a country that no longer existed, and the lie had felt very heavy in his pocket, heavier than vellum, heavier than iron.

Above him, boots on the deck. Yara's voice, low, calling Sef down from the watch. Then silence, and the river muttering against the hull, and Elias sat in the lamplight with the treaty open before him and understood, with the clarity that comes only to the sleepless, that the choice he had been deferring since Corvess was not deferred anymore.

Tomorrow, or the day after, someone would ask him a direct question. Sef asked direct questions. Yara asked worse ones.

And far downstream, though he could not have known it, a consortium gunboat was coaling at Brassen's Hollow pier, its captain reading a report that mentioned a riverboat called the *Meridian*, and marking her last known position with a pencil stroke that pointed, like an arrow, upstream.
