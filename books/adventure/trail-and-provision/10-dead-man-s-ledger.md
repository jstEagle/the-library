## 10. Dead Man's Ledger

The rain came at dusk and did not stop, drumming on the Meridian's deck like a thousand small impatient fingers. Yara had tied up in a willow-choked cut three miles above the Hanging Town, a place she'd used before when she wanted to be forgotten by the river traffic—no landing, no lights, just black water sliding past the hull and the willows closing overhead like a curtain drawn by someone who meant to keep it drawn.

She sat alone in the cabin with the chest open before her and a single lamp turned low.

They had run hard since the Hanging Town. Elias hadn't spoken for two days—not truly spoken; he'd answered questions in clipped syllables and spent his hours hunched over the treaty with his jeweler's loupe, reading Vessian script as though it might forgive him. Sef took the night watches without being asked. The crew kept their distance from all three of them, and Yara let them. A boat could smell fear the way dogs smelled lightning, and hers was full of it.

But tonight was not about any of them. Tonight was about the second layer.

She had known it was there since the dock at Corren's Landing, weeks ago now—a false bottom, felt rather than seen, the way her father had taught her to feel for smugglers' compartments when she was nine years old. *Tap with your knuckle, girl. Honest wood sounds hungry. Lying wood sounds full.* She'd been putting it off. There was always a reason: the locks, the mutiny, the rapids, the patching of the hull, the hundred small emergencies that made up a life on the water. But there were no emergencies tonight. Only rain, and the lamp, and the chest, and the sound of her own breathing.

*Tap with your knuckle.*

The lying wood sounded full.

The panel came up on hidden brass pins, no tools required, because her father had built things to be opened by hands like his own—broad, scarred, patient. Inside lay a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, dry as a sermon despite everything the river had done to the chest above it. Whoever had made this compartment had made it to survive drowning.

Her father's name was written on the oilcloth in his own hand. *T. Whitlock. For Yara, if I don't come home.*

She didn't touch it for a long time. The lamp hissed. Rain worked the roof. Somewhere out in the willows a heron gave its one dry note, like a stick snapping underwater.

Then she untied the cord.

* * *

The ledger was a riverman's ledger—cheap paper, canvas boards gone soft as cloth at the corners, the spine held together with tarred twine. Her father's handwriting filled it, page after page, in the same slanting hand she knew from cargo manifests and the margins of her school primers, the hand that had written *proud of you, skipper* on the last letter he ever sent her. That was the cruelest part. It was unmistakably him. There was no forger alive who could have faked the way he crossed his sevens or dotted his letters slightly left of center, as if even his pen were always looking back over its shoulder.

It was not a confession. Confessions had shape, remorse, an arc. This was bookkeeping.

*Received of H. Crane, per agent Mollis, forty crowns — delivery, Karst Reach, sealed tube — contents unknown.*

*Received of the Vessian circle (via the ferryman Osk) twenty crowns — packet of letters, Corren's Landing to points south.*

*Received of H. Crane, sixty crowns — passenger manifest, Meridian, spring run.*

Yara read it three times before the meaning settled into her bones like cold water into wool. He had taken money from both sides. From Halvard Crane—the name sat on the page like a hook in her throat, the same name that would one day own the locks and the dams and half the river's soul—and from the Vessian exiles, the scattered faithful, the people who sang drowned-nation songs in barrooms and passed letters hand to hand like contraband candles. Her father had been their courier too. Their trusted courier. And Crane's.

She turned pages with fingers that didn't feel like hers. Dates went back thirty years. Back before she was born. Back to the year the maps changed, when Vessia stopped being a country and became a song.

There were entries where the two payments fell within days of each other. One week in her eleventh year: *Crane, fifty crowns, list of Vessian contacts on the lower reach* — and then, four days later — *Vessian circle, thirty crowns, warning forwarded ahead of the sweep at Millbrook.* He had sold names and then sold warnings. He had fed the wolf and then run ahead of it shouting, and both sides had paid him, and both sides had trusted him, and for thirty years he had lived on the difference between what he gave each of them.

And she had thought she knew him.

* * *

She remembered being small, sitting on the pilothouse bench with her legs swinging, watching him read the water. He'd taught her every riffle and eddy between Corren's Landing and the gorge mouth, but more than that, he'd taught her how to think about the river. *The current doesn't lie,* he'd say, *but it doesn't tell you everything either. Your job is to know which is which.* She had built her whole life on that sentence. She had built her whole idea of him on it too: a man who dealt straight, who kept his word, who had died five years ago with nothing to his name but the boat and her, because honest men die poor on the river.

Honest men die poor. Consortium couriers retire comfortable. And the ledger showed disbursements—small ones, careful ones—that had gone somewhere. Not drink; her father had been a teetotaler with a temper about it. Not women, not cards. She found the answer in the back pages, in a column headed only *the girl's account*, and her stomach turned over slowly, like a boat taking water.

He had paid for her apprenticeship papers with consortium money. The license in her pocket, the one that let her captain her own vessel, the proudest document of her life—it had been bought, in part, by lists of names handed to Halvard Crane.

Yara set the ledger down. Her hands were shaking, and she watched them shake with a strange detachment, as if they belonged to someone standing on a bank watching a wreck.

* * *

The cabin door opened behind her and she didn't turn. She knew the step—light, deliberate, a woman who had learned to walk so no one heard her coming and then decided, somewhere along the way, to stop hiding it.

"You've been sitting here four hours," Sef said.

"Have I."

"The lamp's nearly dry." Sef came around into her sightline, glanced at the chest, at the ledger, at Yara's face, and whatever she saw there made her pull the second stool over and sit down without asking. "Elias is asleep. Or pretending. The crew drew lots for watches and Brannick has first."

"Sef." Yara's voice came out flat and strange. "Did you know my father?"

Sef went very still. It was the stillness of a woman who had learned that questions were traps and silence was the only ground that never shifted. "I told you what I was," she said finally. "Consortium surveillance, six years. Before that, a records clerk in the Karst office."

"Then you'd have access to courier files."

"I would."

"Was there a file on Tomas Whitlock?"

Sef looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the rain thickened, and the willows hissed. "Yes," she said.

Yara nodded slowly, as if confirming something she'd already known, though she hadn't—hadn't let herself. "What did it say?"

"That he was reliable. That he was expensive. That he was—" Sef paused, choosing, and Yara watched her choose, "—that he was the best double asset Crane ever ran on the river. And that Crane never fully trusted him, because a man who sells both sides can always sell a third."

"There wasn't a third."

"No," Sef said quietly. "There was you. Crane's file noted it as leverage. *One daughter, one boat, no other attachments. Subject motivated by family security.*" She said it without softening it, and Yara understood why—Sef had decided somewhere in the last weeks that lies of comfort were just slow knives, and Yara found she respected it even as it flayed her.

"So everything he did for me—"

"Was real," Sef said. "That's the thing about men like your father. The file called him a mercenary, and the Vessian circle called him a martyr, and both of them were wrong, and both of them were right. He was a man keeping two sets of books and trying to make them balance against each other. Sometimes the balancing act *is* the conscience. It's ugly, but it's a conscience."

"He got people killed." Yara's voice cracked on it. "The list of contacts. The sweep at Millbrook—I remember the sweep. Eleven people taken off a ferry. Two of them hanged in the square at—" She stopped. The Hanging Town. That was why it was called that. She had grown up poling past its gallows-post the way you pole past a snag, not looking, the way river people didn't look at things the river put there.

"Yes," Sef said. "Millbrook was Crane's operation. And the warning that saved the other nine families came through your father. I've read both reports. They sit in the same drawer in the Karst archive, six inches apart. I used to wonder about that drawer." She leaned forward, elbows on knees. "I used to wonder what kind of man could write both documents. Now I'm sitting on his daughter's boat, helping carry his chest upstream, and I still wonder."

"What do you wonder now?"

Sef was quiet long enough that the lamp flame steadied between them. "Whether he spent thirty years trying to undo the first bad year," she said. "Or whether the first bad year was just the price of admission and everything after was performance. And whether it matters. And whether it matters *to whom*." She stood. "You should sleep. We run the narrows at first light if we're going to stay ahead of Crane's patrol boats."

She paused at the door. "Captain. The ledger isn't the whole of what's down there, is it? I saw the way you're sitting. There's something you haven't read yet."

Yara looked down. Beneath the ledger, at the bottom of the compartment, lay a sealed envelope, heavy paper, wax stamped with the Vessian crest—the same crest that rode the chest itself. Her father's hand across the front: *For the source. Read aloud or burn unread, but do not read it alone.*

"Do not read it alone," Yara repeated.

"Then don't," Sef said. "Wake me when you decide."

The door closed behind her. Yara sat with the envelope in both hands, feeling its weight, thinking about drawers six inches apart, about a girl on a pilothouse bench learning that the current doesn't lie but doesn't tell you everything either. About which one her father had been—the current, or the lie.

The rain kept on. The lamp guttered low. And through the cabin wall, faint under the weather, she heard Elias moving in the forward cabin when he should have been asleep—floorboards creaking once, twice, toward the hold where the chest had slept these many weeks, stopping, retreating.

Yara slid the envelope into her coat, against her heart, where the license sat.

First light, then. The narrows. And whatever her father had wanted said aloud—or burned—she would not face it alone, and she would not face it unwatched. She blew out the lamp and sat in the dark listening to the river, which told her nothing, and told her true.
