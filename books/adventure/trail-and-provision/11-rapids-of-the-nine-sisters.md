## 11. Rapids of the Nine Sisters

The gunboat came out of the morning mist like a rumor made flesh.

Yara saw it first as a smudge on the water below Karst Reach—a black shape where no black shape should be, riding the current against all sense, because nothing but a consortium vessel had business coming upstream this far past the locks. By the time she could make out the twin smokestacks and the forward-mounted swivel gun, its canvas tarpaulin already thrown back, she'd counted her options and found them wanting.

"They've been waiting for us," Sef said from the rail, her voice flat in the way it went flat when she was most afraid.

"Then they know these waters better than I hoped." Yara spun the wheel a half-point, gauging the current's pull at the bow. The Meridian groaned beneath her feet, her patched hull still tender from the side-channel run two days back. "Or they don't know them at all, and they're counting on that being enough."

Elias came up from below with the chest strapped to his back like a pilgrim's burden, his face pale above his collar. "Can we outrun her?"

"You can't outrun a gun, archivist. You can only decide where you'd rather be standing when it fires." She looked downstream, past the gunboat, to where the river bent west into country no chart in her cabin bothered to name. The Nine Sisters. Her father had spoken of them exactly once, drunk enough to be honest and sober enough to remember: *nine drops in nine miles, and the river between them mean as a widow.* No pilot ran them. No pilot who valued his boat, his crew, or his skin.

But a gunboat drew six feet even when empty, and the Sisters' upper reaches, if half of what her father said was true, drew less than four.

"Doss used to say there were two kinds of captains," she murmured, more to herself than anyone. "The kind who die running, and the kind who die wondering."

"And which are you?" Elias asked.

"The third kind." She threw the engine telegraph full ahead. "The kind who makes everyone else do the dying first."

* * *

The gunboat's whistle shrieked across the water—two long blasts, the consortium's signal to heave to—and then, when the Meridian gave no answer, a single crack that wasn't a whistle at all. The shot fell short, throwing up a column of brown water off the stern quarter, close enough that spray rattled against the pilothouse glass like gravel.

"They'll hull us," Sef said. "That next one won't miss."

"No," Yara agreed. "It won't."

She put the wheel over hard to starboard and took the Meridian out of the channel—out of the deep, honest, charted water where a steamboat belonged—and drove her toward the western bend, toward the white noise rising beyond the willow stands like distant surf. Behind them the gunboat hesitated. She could almost feel the pilot aboard doing the same arithmetic she'd done: *she can't possibly be going in there.*

"She's turning!" someone shouted from the deck. "She's following!"

Of course she was. Crane didn't send gunboats to negotiate.

The river narrowed. The banks closed in, willow giving way to granite, granite to slick black stone streaked with foam. Yara felt the current take hold of the Meridian's keel the way a hand takes a wrist—not gently, not cruelly, just absolutely—and understood that from here forward, the boat would go where the water said, and her job was only to suggest otherwise at the right moments.

"Sef! Bow watch!" she called through the voice tube. "Call me the water, not the rocks—the rocks I'll see. Tell me where it runs green and where it boils white!"

Sef scrambled forward along the rail, one hand on the lifeline, and planted herself at the stem like a figurehead with opinions. "Green left of the mid-channel bar! White water center-right—there's a hole under the bluff, big one!"

Yara read the river ahead while listening behind, the way her father had taught her before she was old enough to steer: *the water tells you everything, girl, if you stop looking at what you fear.* The surface ahead lay in long unbroken V's pointing upstream—fast, deep, survivable. Where the V's shattered into chaos, something waited underneath. A rock. A drowned tree. A sister.

The first drop came without warning, a seam in the world where the river simply stepped down three feet and kept going. The Meridian's bow pitched, her plates shuddered, and the pilothouse filled with the sound of the whole boat being shaken like a dog shakes a rat.

"Second sister ahead!" Sef's voice, raw with spray. "It splits around an island—left side looks—no, there's a sweeper! Tree in the water, whole pine, right across the left chute!"

Right chute, then. Right chute past the island, where the water stood up in ridges like plowed ground. Yara fed the wheel through her hands by feel, watching the island's downstream point where the currents collided and boiled, and threaded the gap with less than a boat-length to spare on either side. Bark from the sweeper hissed along the starboard guard, close enough to leave a scar.

Behind them, the gunboat's whistle blew again—shorter blasts now, uncertain ones. Its pilot had stopped at the head of the rapids. Good. But gunboats carried launches, and launches carried men, and men with rifles didn't need six feet of draft.

"Third sister!" Sef screamed. "Big one! It doesn't look like water, it looks like—"

"I see it!"

It looked like the end of the world. The river dropped away ahead of them into a trough of churning white, and at its heart stood a standing wave taller than the pilothouse, a mountain of water that did not move downstream but stayed fixed in place, climbing and collapsing and climbing again. Every instinct Yara owned told her no boat could live in that. Every hour of her life on this river told her the same thing it always told her: *the wave is not the danger. The wave is the signpost. Read what it's telling you.*

A standing wave that big meant a ledge below it, a shelf of rock the river poured over. And a ledge meant the safe line was where the water came over smoothest—which was never where it looked smoothest.

"Hold onto something!" she bellowed aft. "Everyone down, now!"

She took the drop straight down the middle, because the middle was where the foam ran flattest, because the middle was where the river had already scoured itself a throat. The Meridian went nose-down into the trough and for one endless second Yara was steering a waterfall, the wheel light as a toy, the propeller clawing at air and water both. Then the hull met the ledge's tail and slammed flat, and the whole boat leapt like a landed fish, and Yara's knees buckled, and somewhere aft a crate broke loose and thundered across the deck.

They were through. They were through, and the fourth sister was already roaring ahead.

* * *

The fourth, fifth, and sixth sisters came in quick succession, each one a different lesson in humility. The fourth tried to set them on a rock shelf; Yara backed water against the current's own pull and slid past with inches to spare. The fifth was narrow as a barn door and demanded a turn mid-drop that no rudder should have been able to make; she made it anyway, tearing the port paddle-wheel float loose with a crack like a rifle shot. The sixth was a lie—a long stretch of seemingly gentle water that hid a whirlpool big enough to swallow a skiff, and they skirted its rim so closely that Yara could look down into its slow-turning eye and see driftwood circling, circling, going nowhere.

Her arms burned. Her shirt clung to her back. Through the voice tube she could hear the crew working the pumps without being told, because the bilge alarm had begun its low insistent moan somewhere after the fifth sister, and moaning bilges on a hundred-year-old boat meant one thing and one thing only.

"We're taking water," Doss's replacement, a silent Kettering boy named Pell, reported at the pilothouse door. His face was gray. "Not fast. But steady."

"How steady?"

"Steady enough that we want to be aground somewhere before dark."

"Then we'll be aground somewhere before dark." She said it like it was a plan instead of a prayer.

The seventh sister announced itself differently than the others. There was no roar ahead—instead the river went strangely quiet, gathering itself between high walls of stone, and the water turned the color of hammered tin. Yara knew that quiet. She'd grown up on that quiet. It was the silence of a held breath.

And it was there, in that false calm, that the gunboat's launch appeared.

It came around the bluff below them—oars out, eight men, a swivel gun mounted at the bow small enough to be rowed and large enough to end them. It had cut the corner while the Meridian danced through the sisters, taking the long calm eddy behind the bluff that Yara now realized must exist, must have existed all along, if a man knew these waters well enough to look for it.

Which meant somebody aboard knew these waters. Somebody who wasn't supposed to.

"Launch off the port bow!" Sef's voice cracked across the deck. "Gun's manned—they're ranging on us!"

There was no room to maneuver. The seventh sister's throat was barely wider than the Meridian herself, the stone walls sheer on either side, and the launch sat squarely in the only line the current offered. Yara could run them down—could try—but the launch's gun needed only one shot, and at fifty yards it wouldn't miss.

"Yara." Elias stood beside her in the pilothouse, chest still strapped to his back, and for once the archivist had nothing clever to say. Just her name, and the question inside it.

"Get below," she said. "Both of you. Now."

The swivel gun spoke—a puff of smoke, a flat crack—and the ball took the Meridian's rail amidships, splintering wood and spinning away over the water. A second later the launch was alongside, grapples flying, and men were coming over the side with pistols drawn and Crane's silver dam-badge glinting on their collars.

What followed lasted ninety seconds and felt like a year. Yara fought at the pilothouse door with a boathook, using the narrowness of the passage the way she'd used the narrows of every rapid since dawn—making the constraint work for her, letting only one man reach her at a time, putting the hook's iron point where it would do the most persuading. Pell swung a capstan bar. Somewhere aft, one of the deckhands screamed. And Sef—

Sef came out of nowhere like weather.

Yara caught only fragments: a flash of the woman's dark hair, the gleam of a knife that had lived hidden in her boot since Karst Reach, the particular terrible economy of someone who had been trained by people who trained killers. Two of Crane's men went over the side in as many breaths. The grapple lines parted. The launch, swinging wide on the collision, kissed the stone wall and stove in its oar-banks.

And then the seventh sister ended the argument for everyone.

The river dropped. All of it, at once, without ceremony—the false calm breaking like a fever, the whole throat of the gorge tilting into a chute of white that no swimmer and no sinking launch could argue with. The grapples tore free. The launch went sideways into the maw, rolled once, and was gone, its men scattered into the foam like seeds.

So was Sef.

Yara saw it happen and did not see it happen—one moment the woman was on the deck beside her, hand locked in the shroud, and the next the river had simply taken her, the way it took everything it wanted, without malice and without negotiation.

"*Sef!*"

The word tore out of Yara's throat before thought. Ahead the eighth sister roared, white and final, and every part of her that was a captain—the part that had buried two crews' worth of mistakes, the part that knew the arithmetic of rivers—screamed that the boat came first, that the boat always came first, that a captain who traded her vessel for one drowning soul would soon have neither.

She steered for Sef anyway.

* * *

Later, she could never fully reconstruct those four minutes. They existed in her memory as sensation without sequence: the wheel jammed hard over, the Meridian pivoting on her own length in water too fast for any sane maneuver; the stern swinging toward the stone wall with the whole river's weight behind it; the scrape along the bluff that would cost them planking and nearly cost them everything; the eddy behind the eighth sister's tail-ledge, a pocket of slack water no bigger than a barn floor, existing for exactly one reason—to catch what the river spat out.

Sef came around the eddy's rim on her back, arms wide, face to the sky, alive by the grace of whatever gods keep books on river pilots and their debts.

Yara got her by the collar of her coat, hauled her over the broken rail, and together they collapsed onto the bloodied deck, gasping, while the ninth sister—the last one, the one her father had called the widow-maker—thundered past them untouched, its fury spent on empty water.

For a long time neither of them moved. The Meridian drifted in the eddy, listing slightly to port, her bilge pump moaning its tired song. Mist rose off the rapids and softened the gorge walls until the world seemed made of gauze and noise.

"You steered for me," Sef said finally. Her voice was shredded. "You had the boat. You had—" She stopped, started again. "Nobody steers for the person in the water."

Yara stared up at the strip of sky between the gorge walls. Her hands wouldn't stop shaking, and she found she lacked the will to make them.

"My father used to say the river keeps a ledger," she said. "Every debt, every mercy, entered fair. I've spent five years paying into it and never once drawing out." She turned her head, looked at this woman she'd distrusted from the first mile, whose wrist bore a mark she'd chosen to ignore, whose knife had just saved them all. "I guess today I drew."

Sef laughed—a broken, waterlogged sound that might have been crying in another register. Then she pressed her forehead against the wet deck planks, and her shoulders shook, and Yara put a hand on her back and let the river have its privacy around them.

* * *

They grounded the Meridian at dusk on a gravel bar below the ninth sister, in a fold of the gorge where cottonwoods leaned over the water and the current ran gentle as a creek. Pell and the two remaining deckhands worked the pumps while Yara stripped to the waist in the cold and went into the flooded bilge with a lantern clenched in her teeth.

She found the wound on the starboard side, forward of the turn of the hull: a seam opened along a foot of planking, sprung where the seventh sister's wall-scrape had stressed timber already weakened by the side-channel run. Not fatal. Not tonight. But the Meridian was bleeding, and gravel bars were not shipyards, and the nearest place a hull could be properly patched was Vell Hollow—three days further upstream, if Elias's map of the hidden villages could be believed, and if the map was true.

She climbed out into the blue evening light, soaked to the ribs, and found Sef waiting with a blanket and, absurdly, a cup of coffee.

"The chest is dry," Sef said. "Elias hasn't let go of it since the launch came alongside. He's sitting on it right now, actually, like a hen."

"He would." Yara took the cup. Her fingers were too numb to feel its heat, but she drank anyway. Below them the ninth sister muttered in the dark, still hungry, cheated of its due. Above them the stars came out in the cold clear band of sky the gorge allowed.

"Pell says we shouldn't go on," Sef said quietly. "He says we should beach her here, walk out east, take our chances overland."

"Pell isn't the captain."

"No," Sef agreed. "You are. So I'm asking you, captain, and I'm asking because I was in the water today and I know exactly what this river thinks of us: how much does she have left?"

Yara looked down the length of her boat—at the splintered rail, the missing paddle float, the list she could feel through the soles of her boots. Five years of her life, and her father's before her, nailed plank by plank into these decks. She thought about the ledger again. About debts and mercies, and about how the only way through the rest of this country was forward, because backward was full of guns.

"She has enough," Yara said. "Barely. Which is more than I can say for the men in that launch." She drained the coffee and handed back the cup, and something in her chest settled—something that had been loose since the flood first brought the chest aboard. "We run for Vell Hollow at first light. And Sef—when we get there, you and I are going to talk about where you learned to use that knife."

Sef held her gaze for a long moment. In the lantern light, for the first time since Karst Reach, she didn't look away.

"Fair," she said. "I think it's time I told you."

Upstream, faint on the night wind, came a sound that froze them both: a whistle. Two long blasts. The consortium's signal to heave to—blown by something moving on the dark water above them, between the Meridian and the hidden valley, where nothing should have been moving at all.
