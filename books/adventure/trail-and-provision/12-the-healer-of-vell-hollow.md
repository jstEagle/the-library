## 12. The Healer of Vell Hollow

The village did not exist.

Sef understood this the way she had once understood her orders at the consortium: completely, and without needing to be told twice. Vell Hollow was a fold in the gorge country, a place where the river bent back on itself so sharply that from any ridge above, a traveler would see only forest and shadow. The Meridian lay beached in a willow-choked inlet two miles downstream, her cracked hull groaning like an old woman climbing stairs, while Yara's carpenter—what remained of him, one man named Pell with sawdust in his eyebrows—measured the wound in her planking and pronounced it survivable, barely, if they could find seasoned oak and someone who knew how to work it.

Which was why they were walking single file up a deer trail that no map acknowledged, following a boy who had appeared out of the ferns that morning as if he'd been waiting for them all his life.

"He knows," Elias murmured beside Sef, for the fourth time since dawn. "He looked at the chest mark on Yara's tarp and he *knew*. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means we're about to find out whether knowing things keeps people alive," Sef said.

Elias smiled at that—the quick, bright smile he deployed like currency—but she had learned to watch what his hands did when he smiled, and his hands were doing something now: pressing flat against his coat pockets, holding something down, or holding himself together. She could not tell which. Five days since the Nine Sisters, since Yara had hauled her half-drowned out of white water by the collar of her shirt, and Sef had spent those five days learning to read Elias Vane the way she'd once read consortium dispatches: not for what they said, but for what they were careful not to say.

The boy stopped at a curtain of hazel branches woven through with dead bracken. It looked, to Sef's trained eye, exactly like nothing—a collapse of brush against a rock face. The boy lifted it aside like a door.

Beyond it, the gorge opened into a bowl of green terraces stitched with water channels, and the village of Vell Hollow sat inside it like a secret kept so long it had grown comfortable. Stone houses with turf roofs. Flax drying on racks. A millrace turning somewhere below, its sound folded under birdsong. And everywhere water—diverted, channeled, coaxed through wooden sluices with an intimacy that made Sef's neck prickle, because she had spent three years cataloguing consortium waterworks and she had never seen anything built like this. This wasn't engineering. This was a conversation between people and a river that had been going on for generations.

"They never found it," Elias breathed. He had stopped walking. His face had gone strange—not joyful, not quite. Stricken and exalted at once, like a man seeing a ghost he'd prayed for. "A hundred years. They burned the maps, they bought the archives, they drowned the lowlands—and it was *here* the whole time."

"Keep your voice down," Sef said, "and your face off."

But it was too late; they had been seen. An old woman came down the middle terrace path with a staff she did not lean on, moving with the unhurried authority of someone who owned every stone she stepped on. Her hair was white and braided close to her skull in the old Vessian fashion Sef had only seen in confiscated photographs. Her eyes went first to Yara, then to the chest strapped to Doss's back—Doss, returned and repentant, carrying it like penance—then to Elias, and stopped there.

"You're the archivist," she said. Not a question.

"I'm—" Elias began.

"I know what you are." She said it without heat, the way one identifies weather. Then, to Yara: "You'll be wanting Maren. Your boat's hull is bad but not hopeless, and Maren's father built river barges before the Hollow closed itself. Come. You can carry your grief indoors; it's damp out."

She turned and walked, expecting to be followed, and they followed.

* * *

The healer's house smelled of smoke and comfrey and something older underneath, a mineral smell like wet stone in a deep cellar. She was called Odile, though she did not offer the name; Sef learned it later from the boy, who attached himself to their party with the silent devotion of children everywhere toward dangerous strangers. Odile set them around her hearth, fed them barley soup with river greens, and asked questions in the manner of a surgeon probing a wound—brief, precise, without tenderness.

Where had they come from. Who had seen them pass. How many days behind them were Crane's men.

"Two days," Sef said, when Yara glanced at her. "Maybe three if the ford at Karst Reach is still flooded. They have a gunboat but it can't follow past the Sisters."

"And you." Odile's eyes settled on Sef and stayed. "You sit like a soldier. You watch the door. You counted my exits when you came in—I watched you do it. Who are you?"

The soup went cold in Sef's mouth. Across the hearth, Yara shifted, and Sef felt the question arrive in the room before anyone spoke it: *why haven't we thrown her off yet?*

"She was consortium surveillance," Yara said quietly. "She defected. I trust her."

"You trust her." Odile turned the words over like stones looking for the soft underside. "In the Hollow we say: the river forgives the drowned, but it does not forget the current that took them."

"I don't need forgiveness," Sef said. "I need to finish this."

Something flickered across the old woman's face—approval, or its close cousin—and she let it go. But she did not stop watching Sef, and Sef understood that she never would. That was fair. That was, in fact, more honest than Yara's trust, which Sef carried sometimes like a hot coal she hadn't asked to hold.

It was the chest that changed everything.

Yara opened it because Odile demanded it—demanded with such flat certainty that refusal would have been its own confession. The lid came up, the oilcloth came back, and there lay the treaty: vellum gone amber with age, seals hanging from it like dried fruit, the Vessian crest pressed deep in wax that had outlived the nation that made it.

Odile went very still.

Sef had seen stillness used as a weapon before—in consortium directors pausing mid-sentence to make a room afraid—but this was different. This was stillness like the moment after lightning, when the air is deciding what to become. The old woman reached out and touched the wax seal with one finger, and her hand, which had not trembled once all evening, trembled now.

"Where," she said, "did you get this."

"My father carried it," Yara said. "For a long time, apparently. He died five years ago. I'm finishing his route."

"Your father." Odile looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious at once. "Your father carried the *Restoration Compact* through occupied water for thirty years, and you didn't know? You piloted that river your whole life and you didn't know what slept in your own hold?"

"I know what it is now," Yara said. "Elias showed me. It grants the heirs of Vessia rights to the source valley. It voids the dam claims."

"The heirs." Odile laughed then, a short terrible sound with no humor in it. She rose, went to a shelf crammed with clay pots and bundled herbs, and stood with her back to them. When she spoke again her voice had dropped into something older, recited, worn smooth by repetition like a stone in a current. "*By right of blood unbroken and covenant sealed, the Compact names the heir of the line of Maravel.*" She turned. "That is the clause your archivist has been so carefully not reading aloud. Am I wrong?"

Silence. Sef watched Elias.

He had gone pale. Not the dramatic pallor of a man performing shock—she had interrogated enough people to know performance when she sat across from it—but the gray, drained stillness of a man watching a wall crack along a line he had hoped would hold. His hands were in his pockets again. Pressing down.

"The line of Maravel," Odile continued, merciless, "was thought ended. Drowned at the Fall, or scattered, or bought. Every exile faction in forty years has crowned some convenient cousin and called it restoration. But the Compact does not name cousins. It names *blood*, and blood leaves traces even when names are buried." She sat back down, slowly, and looked at each of them in turn. "The heir named in that document is alive. I have held her hand. I have dressed a fever on her when she was nine years old and did not know what she was."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the millrace turned and turned.

"Who?" Yara asked.

And Odile, healer of Vell Hollow, keeper of a hundred years of careful silence, looked directly at Sef.

Not at Elias. Not at the exile figureheads whose names Sef knew from consortium briefing files—names like Kestrel and Ambrose, faces on wanted posters and restoration pamphlets alike. At Sef. At the defector. At the spy with the consortium mark scarred inside her wrist.

"No," Sef said.

"You were born Sefia Maravel," Odile said. "Your mother brought you through the gorge in the winter of the purges. We changed your name and gave you to a river family, and the consortium's adoption records did their work better than we ever dreamed—they made you into their own instrument. But blood doesn't read files." She leaned forward. "I knew your mother's face the moment you walked in. You wear it like a debt."

The room tilted. Sef heard herself speaking from very far away, very evenly, the way she'd been trained to speak when everything was burning: "That's not possible. My parents were ferrymen. They died in the Karst flood when I was six. There are records."

"There are records," Odile agreed gently. "We wrote them."

* * *

Later—she could not have said how much later—Sef stood alone on the terrace above the millrace, and the valley breathed around her in the dark, and she tried to assemble herself out of the pieces.

She had built herself twice already in her life. Once at six, when the ferry family's kindness ran out and the consortium orphan intake took her measurements and her name and filed her under *assets*. She had built herself into an instrument: observant, deniable, useful. Then she had built herself again on the deck of the Meridian, out of white water and Yara Whitlock's improbable, infuriating faith, into something that might, given time, become a person.

Now a third blueprint lay in front of her, drawn by a dead mother and a hundred-year-old treaty, and she did not want it. That was the thing no one understood, she thought, gripping the terrace rail until her knuckles ached. Everyone assumed the revelation of a hidden inheritance was a gift. No one asked the person being handed a crown whether she had ever, even once, wanted to be a symbol. Symbols got people killed. She had watched symbols get people killed from the inside; she had written the reports.

Footsteps behind her. She knew the cadence—too light, too deliberate. Elias.

"So," she said, without turning. "You knew."

A pause. Long enough to confirm everything.

"I suspected," he said.

"Suspected." She turned. In the dark his face was mostly eyes, and the eyes were doing that bright, urgent thing she had once mistaken for honesty. "Odile says the clause names the line of Maravel. You've had the treaty open for weeks. You read Vessian script like breathing. You're telling me the name never registered?"

"I'm telling you," Elias said carefully, "that the Compact names a line, not a person. Lines fork. Records burn. I couldn't verify—"

"*You couldn't verify.*" Sef laughed, and it came out ugly. "Or you verified, and it was the wrong answer. Because the movement has its heir already, doesn't it. Someone the exiles love. Someone safe. Someone whose claim doesn't detonate the whole restoration the moment it's spoken." She stepped closer. "What did you burn, Elias? On the boat. What pages went into the stove?"

His silence had texture. She stood inside it and waited, the way she'd waited across tables from men with more power than conscience, and finally he said, very quietly:

"If the truth comes out wrong—if it comes out as a spy's confession instead of a birthright—it won't save Vessia. It will split it. Crane will use it. The elders will tear each other apart over legitimacy while his mercenaries walk in through the front gate. I was trying to protect the future."

"You were trying to protect *your* future," Sef said. "The one where you're the archivist who restored the nation. Where you get to decide what history keeps." She shook her head. "I've read your kind of file before. Zealots make the best liars. They lie for the cause, and the cause always needs just one more lie."

"That isn't fair."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Almost nothing about me has been fair since I was six years old." She turned back to the dark valley, to the small lights of Vell Hollow, to the people who had hidden her blood for twenty-five years and never thought to ask her consent. "You have until we reach the source valley. Whatever you're still hiding—you bring it into the open yourself, or I will. And Elias? I'm consortium-trained. I'm very good at finding what people hide."

He left without another word. His footsteps going away were faster than his footsteps coming.

Sef stayed at the rail a long time. Below her, the millrace turned in the dark, patient as a heart, and she thought about currents—the ones that take you, the ones you choose—and about how the river forgives the drowned but does not forget.

Behind her, up the slope, a door opened. Yara's voice, low: "Sef. You should come see this. Pell found something else in the chest—under the false bottom. Papers with Crane's seal on them."

Sef closed her eyes.

Of course, she thought. Of course there was another layer. There was always another layer.

She pushed off the rail and went inside.
