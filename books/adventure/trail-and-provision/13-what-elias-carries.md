## 13. What Elias Carries

The fire pit behind the healer's longhouse was meant for cooking, but at this hour it held only embers and the smell of pine resin, and that was what Elias Vane had come for. Fire answered to no one's questions. Fire kept its own counsel.

He had waited until the house went quiet—until Sef's breathing slowed in the loft above, until Yara Whitlock stopped pacing the porch with that restless captain's stride, until old Maren the healer finally banked her lamps and muttered her way to bed. Then he had taken the annex from the chest, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, and carried it out into the dark like a man carrying something he had stolen from a church.

In a sense, he had.

The annex was eleven pages of vellum sewn into the treaty's spine, and it was the most dangerous document in Vessia—or the most merciful, depending on which way a man chose to lie to himself. Elias had been choosing his way for six years now, since the night in the Grayhaven archive when he'd first broken the seal on the copied folios and read past the clauses everyone recited, down into the succession provisions written in a chancellor's cramped, dying hand.

*The heir named herein is the child of the second marriage, not the first.*

One sentence. He could still see the ink bleeding where rain had touched the original before the archivists got to it, a small gray bloom like a bruise on the page. One sentence, and everything the exiles had built—the songs, the societies, the little brass pins shaped like the Vessian double-river crest that old men wore in their lapels in port towns from here to the coast—all of it pointed at the wrong name.

He knelt at the fire pit now and unwrapped the oilcloth. The pages caught the ember-light and glowed faintly gold, vellum holding light the way water holds a lantern. He had burned copies before. Three times, in three cities, he had fed reproductions to flames and told himself the originals were safe, that he was only pruning. But this was the treaty itself. This was the thing Yara Whitlock had hauled up out of a flood on a rope and a hook, the thing her father had died carrying, the thing Crane's consortium would burn cities to reclaim—and Elias was about to put pieces of it into the fire because the truth inside it was heavier than the movement could bear.

*A true believer protects the faith,* his teacher had said once, back when Elias was young enough to believe teachers. *Even from itself.*

He heard the floorboard creak behind him and did not turn around. Some part of him had known, all evening, that Sef watched him. She had the eyes of a woman who had spent years being paid to watch people—a flat, patient attention that never blinked when you wanted it to. He had told himself she was asleep up there in the loft. He had told himself a great many things.

"You should be resting," he said. "Tomorrow we walk."

"So should you." Her voice came down soft through the cold air. "You've been out here an hour. I counted."

"Counting is surveillance work."

"It is." She came around the fire pit and stood opposite him, arms crossed against the chill, her breath ghosting silver. In the ember-light her face was all planes and shadow, younger than her years and older than her face. "So is watching a man carry documents out of a locked chest at midnight when he thinks everyone's sleeping. I used to do that too. Before I defected. You remember."

"I remember."

"What are you burning, Elias?"

He looked down at the pages in his hands. There was no good answer, so he gave her the truest one available: "Pages I shouldn't have."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have left." He heard the bitterness in his own voice and hated it. Six years of careful stewardship, six years of being the man who knew and said nothing, and in one week on this woman's boat—in Yara Whitlock's boat, which was its own strange country with its own laws—he had come apart like wet paper.

Sef didn't move. She was very good at not moving; he'd noticed that the first day, the way she could stand at a rail for hours and become part of the boat. Now she became part of the night, part of the firelight, and waited.

Elias fed the first page to the embers.

It caught slowly, curling at the corner, the fire walking up the vellum in a thin orange seam. He watched the succession clause go—*the child of the second marriage*—watched the words blister and blacken and lift as ash into the dark. It felt like cutting off his own hand and watching it burn, and it felt like mercy, and both feelings were honest, and that was the whole sickness of it.

"The annex," Sef said quietly. Not a question anymore.

"Yes."

"What's in it?"

"Provisions. Signatories' oaths. Land schedules." He listed them the way a man lists the rooms of a house he's about to demolish. "And the succession clause. The part that names who the treaty restores."

"Sef Ashkari," she said. "Daughter of the exile line. The elders in Grayhaven have taught children her name for fifty years. There are songs."

"There are songs," Elias agreed. His throat was tight. "There are always songs. Songs are how movements survive without facts."

The second page went onto the coals. He watched the land schedules burn—parcels of the source valley measured in chains and rods, boundaries drawn by dead men, every line a promise the consortium had drowned in water and paperwork. That part hurt worst. Those pages were innocent. Those pages were just geography, and geography had done nothing wrong.

"Elias." Sef crouched across the fire from him, close enough that he could see the ember-light reflected twice in her dark eyes. "I was consortium surveillance for nine years. I know what a man looks like when he's destroying evidence for someone else, and I know what he looks like when he's destroying it for himself. Which are you?"

The question landed like a hook set deep. He almost laughed. "You'd make a fine interrogator."

"I was one."

"Then you'll appreciate honesty." He wiped his hands on his coat, though they weren't dirty. "The heir named in the treaty is not Sef Ashkari. It isn't the exile line at all. The last chancellor married twice. The first marriage produced the daughter whose descendants founded the restoration movement—the ones the exiles follow, the figurehead family, the songs. The second marriage produced a son, born after the drowning, smuggled out in a grain barge. The treaty names *that* child. And that child has descendants living today, and I know where, and if the annex survives to the valley, the elders will read it aloud and discover that everything they've believed—their whole claim, their whole identity—rests on the wrong branch of the tree."

Sef had gone very still. Even the wind seemed to go still around her, the pines above the longhouse holding their needles.

"And you decided," she said at last, each word placed like a stone on a scale, "that you would decide for them. That you'd burn the truth and let them keep the beautiful lie."

"I decided that a movement that learns its founding story is false, on the eve of claiming its homeland, will shatter. Yes." He met her eyes. "Crane's men are two days behind us. Maybe less. When they arrive, the valley will need to be unified or it will be dead. A divided nation cannot survive a siege. I am trying to keep them alive, Sef. That's all. That has always been all."

"You're trying to keep them obedient."

"Obedient and alive are the same thing right now!"

His voice cracked out over the clearing louder than he intended, and somewhere in the trees a bird startled into flight, wings slapping the dark. Elias pressed his palms against his knees and breathed. The third page lay in his lap, half-burned already at one corner where an ember had spat. He smoothed it flat with trembling fingers.

"You think I haven't asked myself?" he said, quieter now. "Every night for six years. Every archive, every safehouse, every night I lay awake listening to other men snore and asked myself: who gave you the right? Who made you the keeper? And the answer is always the same—no one. No one made me anything. I made myself this, because someone had to carry it and everyone else who knew was either dead or bought. So yes. I chose. God forgive me, I chose, and I would choose again, because the alternative is handing Crane a knife and telling him exactly where to cut."

Sef was silent a long moment. The fire ticked and settled. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—not softer, exactly, but closer, the way a voice gets when it's coming from somewhere painful.

"When I worked for the consortium," she said, "we had a doctrine. Information asymmetry. Whoever holds the fact holds the power, so you never let a fact travel unless it buys you something. I told myself I was different from them because I eventually used my facts to help people instead of hurt them. But the shape of the act was the same. Deciding alone. Holding the door shut." She nodded at the burning pages. "You're doing Crane's arithmetic, Elias. You're just rounding in the other direction."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It probably isn't. Fairness is another thing we decide alone." She reached across the fire, and before he could stop her, took the remaining pages out of his hands.

He lunged—too slow, too tired—and she was already standing, the annex held against her chest, four pages of vellum and one hundred years of a nation's secret between her crossed arms.

"Give them back."

"No."

"Sef—"

"You said the second-marriage line has descendants living today." Her face was unreadable in the firelight, but her voice had developed a hairline fracture he could hear. "You said you know where."

"I do."

"How."

"Because I've spent six years finding them. Because keeping the secret required knowing whether the secret could ever be forced out of me. Because—" He stopped. The fire popped. Somewhere far off, the river spoke in its sleep, that low endless murmur under everything, the sound Yara Whitlock probably heard even in dreams.

Sef sat down slowly on the bench beside the fire pit, the pages still clutched to her, and stared into the coals for a long time. When she finally looked up, there was something in her expression he couldn't name—something that wasn't anger and wasn't pity and frightened him more than either.

"Read me the names," she said.

"What?"

"The descendants. Read me the names from the annex. If you're going to be the keeper of this, then I'm going to be the auditor. That's the price of me not waking Yara tonight and telling her what you're doing to her father's chest." She laid the pages on her knee, deliberately within his view, deliberately out of his reach. "You don't get to decide alone anymore, Elias. That ended the moment I saw you kneel down at this fire. Read me the names."

His mouth was dry. The fourth page was the genealogy—he knew it by heart anyway, had transcribed it so many times in so many hidden notebooks that the letters lived behind his eyelids. *Marek, son of the second marriage, b. in the year of the Drowning. Marek's daughter Ilse. Ilse's son—*

He stopped.

The ember-light moved on Sef's face. The river murmured. And Elias Vane, who had spent six years constructing a fortress out of one terrible fact, felt the fortress shift on its foundations, because he had never once let himself look directly at the final name on the genealogy while sitting across from the woman who carried it.

She saw him see it. Of course she did. Nine years of surveillance; she read faces the way he read folios.

"Read them," she whispered.

He couldn't. His voice had left him entirely. So instead he reached out, slowly, and turned the fourth page toward her, angling it to catch the firelight, and watched her eyes travel down the column of dead and living names to the last entry—to the birth date, the place, the single surviving descendant of the second marriage, recorded in a chancellor's hand a century ago and verified by Elias in a Grayhaven registry six years ago.

He watched her read her own name.

The sound she made was very small. Not a gasp—something before a gasp, the sound a rope makes just before it parts. Her hand came up to her wrist, to the old tattoo scar she sometimes rubbed when she thought no one noticed, and pressed there as if checking her own pulse, as if confirming she was still the same person she had been ninety seconds ago.

"That's not possible," she said.

"The registry records were thorough. The consortium kept better files on Vessia than Vessia did." His own voice sounded far away. "They tracked the second line for decades. They lost them in the border provinces twenty years ago. I found them again through a midwife's ledger and a shipping manifest and eleven months of my life I don't like to discuss."

"They're wrong. It's a clerical error. Names repeat—"

"Sef."

"Don't say it."

"If the annex reaches the valley—if Crane reaches the valley—this stops being a choice any of us gets to make. They will find you. They will test your blood if they have to. The only question left is whether you learn it from me, by this fire, tonight—or from Halvard Crane, at gunpoint, in front of everyone you're about to lead." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and made himself hold her gaze. "I burned these pages because I thought the truth would destroy the movement. I was wrong. The truth was never going to stay buried. I just needed it to be you who dug it up. Not them. Never them."

For a long moment neither of them moved. Above the longhouse the stars wheeled slow and indifferent, and the pines talked to each other in the wind, and down in the dark the river ran on toward the source, carrying everything toward everything, the way rivers do.

Then Sef folded the annex pages along their old creases, tucked them inside her coat, against her heart, and stood.

"Yara," she said. "Wake Yara. If this is going to break, it breaks tomorrow, at dawn, with the captain standing there. She pulled this chest out of the river. She gets to hear it first." She paused at the edge of the firelight, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "And Elias—if you lied to me about even one name on that page, there is nowhere on this earth you can run that I won't find you."

She turned toward the longhouse. Through the window, Elias could see lamplight bloom suddenly in the healer's kitchen—Yara Whitlock, awake, already reaching for her boots, already moving toward trouble the way water moves downhill.

Two days ahead of Crane. Two days, and a secret that weighed as much as a nation.

Elias stayed kneeling at the dead fire a moment longer, ashes cooling on his hands, and understood at last that he had never been the keeper of the secret at all. He had only been the one carrying it—upstream, all this way, to the person it had been waiting for.
