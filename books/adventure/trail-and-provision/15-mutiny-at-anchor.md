## 15. Mutiny at Anchor

Doss had been walking back toward the river for three days when he heard the Meridian's whistle.

It came down the valley of the Karst Reach like a voice he'd know blindfolded—two short notes and a long, the signal Yara used to call him home from the taverns when they were both younger and the boat was smaller and the world had fewer teeth. He stood in the middle of the towpath with his pack on one shoulder and his shame in the other, and he listened to that whistle until it faded, and then he started to run.

He found her anchored below the old toll island, where the channel split around a shoal of black stone. She sat low in the water, riding heavy, her paint scabbed from the Nine Sisters and her starboard rail patched with fresh lumber that hadn't yet taken her color. But she was afloat. That alone nearly put him on his knees on the bank.

There were men on her deck who shouldn't have been there. Doss counted six before he made himself stop counting, because counting them meant admitting what they were: Crane's men, in river clothes with river guns, standing at ease among Yara's crew as if they owned the planks beneath their boots. And half her crew—half his family—stood with them. Not fighting. Not even arguing. Just *standing there*, the way men stand when they've already decided something terrible and are waiting for it to be finished.

He saw Yara at the wheelhouse door. Sef beside her, a boathook in her hands held like she knew what a boothook was for. Between them and the mutineers, lashed to the deck amidships under a tarpaulin and two chains, the chest.

Doss set down his pack behind a driftwood snag, took off his coat, folded it, and laid it on top. If this went badly, he didn't want to be wearing anything he'd want back.

* * *

Yara had known it was coming since dawn.

She'd woken to the sound of voices too quiet to catch—the particular hush of men arranging themselves—and she'd lain in her bunk and done the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since Crane's launch had pulled away three days ago with its offer still hanging in the air like smoke. A fortune. Pardons. The Meridian freed of every debt against her hull. Half her crew had families downstream. Half her crew had debts. Half her crew had spent the last week watching gunboats and gallows and a captain who steered toward both, and Yara could not honestly say she'd have followed herself.

So when Pell Grange stepped forward at first light with four others and said, "Captain, we've talked it over," she wasn't surprised. That was the worst part. She'd had all night to not be surprised.

"We're not asking you to give up the boat," Pell said. He was fifty years old and had piloted barges before Yara was born, and he couldn't meet her eyes. "We're asking you to give up the box. Crane lets us all walk away. Every one of us. You too."

"And if I don't?"

Pell's jaw worked. "Then we take it."

The word hung on the deck between them like something dead. Behind Pell, the other four shifted—Hask, the Voss brothers, young Tam with his hands already shaking. Six of eleven. She'd known the numbers too.

"You understand what you're doing," Yara said quietly. "Not to me. To yourselves. Crane doesn't pardon people. He pays them until they're used up, and then he drowns them, the way he drowned a country."

"Vessia's a song," Hask said. "My daughter's a debt."

"That's the truest thing anyone's said on this deck in a week," Yara said, and meant it, and saw it land—that was the cruelty of it. These weren't cowards or traitors in their own minds. They were tired men doing arithmetic with the only things they had left.

Sef moved slightly at her shoulder. "You don't have to do this," she said to the mutineers. "Whatever he promised—"

"You'd know," Tam said bitterly. "Inside your wrist and all."

"Yes," Sef said simply. "I would know. That's why I'm telling you."

And that was when the sixth man came up the gangplank—the one Yara hadn't counted, because he hadn't been hers. A broad man in a gray river coat with a consortium pin hidden under the lapel, who introduced himself as Master Corren and spoke with the soft patience of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice, because other people raised theirs for him.

"Captain Whitlock." He tipped his hat. "Director Crane regrets the necessity. Truly. He thinks highly of you. Your father—"

"Don't," Yara said.

"—spoke of you often," Corren finished anyway, gently, because cruelty dressed in kindness was his trade. "Now. The chest comes ashore by noon, or my associates here assist your crew in taking it, and the incident is noted in ways incidents get noted. Nobody needs to bleed. I've seen to it that everyone profits but you, Captain, and even you—we'll leave you the boat. Consider it mercy."

"The boat's not mine to give," Yara said. "Neither is the chest."

Corren sighed, genuinely regretful, and looked past her at Pell. "Noon," he said again, and checked a watch that probably cost more than the Meridian's engine.

That had been an hour ago. Now the sun stood near its mark, the chest sat chained under its tarpaulin, and the deck had gone quiet in the way water goes quiet above a weir.

Yara stood at the wheelhouse door with Sef beside her and did the last arithmetic left to her. Six against two, plus Corren's hired muscle. She could fight. She might even win, if winning meant a deck slicked with the blood of men whose children she knew by name. Leadership, her father had told her once, drunk and sentimental on the foredeck, means choosing who eats. He hadn't mentioned choosing who leaves.

She was still choosing when the gangplank creaked.

* * *

Doss came aboard the way he'd come aboard a hundred times, hand over hand up the line, and the whole deck turned to watch him do it.

He looked terrible. Three days of hard walking had ground river mud into everything he owned, and his beard had gone from trimmed to feral, and there was a healing cut across his brow that he'd stitched himself, badly. He stood at the foot of the wheelhouse stairs, dripping river water off his boots onto Yara's clean deck, and looked at the mutineers, and at Corren, and at the chest, and finally at Yara.

"I sold your route," he said. No preamble. No excuse. His voice carried the way it always did, big and rough, built for shouting across water. "To Crane's people, at Karst Reach. That's how they knew the side-channel. That's how Sef nearly drowned. It was me."

Nobody moved. Somewhere astern, the river slapped against the hull, patient as ever.

"I marooned myself before anyone could make me do worse," Doss went on. "Walked three days to come say it to your face instead of letting you hear it from theirs." He jerked his chin at Corren. "Because this man buys things, Captain. That's all he does. He bought me cheap and he's buying them cheaper, and when he's done buying, he throws away the wrapper. Ask the source villages. Ask Vessia."

"Master Doss," Corren said pleasantly, "your pardon was contingent on—"

"My pardon died on a sandbar," Doss said. "I'm not here for it. I'm here for her." He looked at Yara, and his eyes were wet, and he let them be. "I'm here to stand where I should've stood the first time."

Corren's patience thinned by a degree no one else could measure. "This is touching. It changes nothing. Noon has come and gone, gentlemen."

"Noon hasn't," said Pell Grange.

Everyone turned. The old pilot had not moved from his place among the mutineers—but he had stopped looking at Corren, and was looking at Doss, and something in his face was being torn slowly down the middle.

"Doss sold us out and walked back to say so," Pell said. "What did you walk back from, Corren? Nothing. Men like you send other men to walk." He spat over the rail. "I signed on to Whitlock's boat because her father pulled my brother out of the Nine Sisters when every other captain ran. I'm not proud of this morning. But I'll be damned if I hand over a dead man's chest to a living leech because I got scared."

"Then you're off the boat too," Hask said sharply. "You said—"

"I said we talked it over. I never said I'd finish it." Pell stepped sideways, out of the mutineers' line, and stood facing them, which was somehow worse for them than facing Yara. "Tam. Boy. Look at me. Your daughter's debt gets paid by honest wages same as dirty ones. Slower. But you get to sleep."

Tam's shaking hands went still. He looked at the chest. At Yara. At the water, as if it might advise him.

It was Corren who ended the arithmetic. "Enough," he said, and snapped his fingers, and his four hired men came off the rail where they'd been lounging, and the morning broke open.

* * *

It lasted ninety seconds, and Doss remembered most of it in pieces forever after.

The Voss brothers rushed the wheelhouse and met Sef's boathook and Yara's winch bar, and learned in the same instant that neither woman had been holding those tools for decoration. Hask went for the chest chains and got Doss's shoulder in his sternum instead, both of them going down across the foredeck in a tangle of fists and curses—old friends, beating each other on behalf of rich men. One of Corren's professionals drew a pistol and Yara's thrown winch handle caught his wrist mid-draw, and the shot went into the sky and came down somewhere in the shoal grass, startling herons.

Tam never threw a punch. Tam stood frozen in the middle of the deck with his face in his hands while his crewmates brawled around him, and when it was over he was still standing there, and that image stayed with Doss longer than any bruise.

It ended because Corren ended it. The moment the tide turned—Pell gripping Hask's collar, Sef's boathook leveled at the professional's throat, Doss kneeling on the Voss brothers' backs—Corren raised one hand and his men disengaged with drilled smoothness, backing to the rail.

"Well," Corren said, straightening his coat. "A pity. Director Crane will be disappointed." He looked at Yara with something that was almost admiration. "You realize he'll stop offering now. What comes next isn't purchased."

"Tell him I know rivers," Yara said, breathing hard, "and he doesn't."

Corren smiled thinly, tipped his hat to the company at large, and went down the gangplank. His men followed. The mutineers remained, five men on a deck that no longer wanted them, in a silence louder than the fight.

* * *

They dealt with it at the rail, in the afternoon light, the way crews deal with everything: plainly, and close enough to touch.

Yara didn't shout. Doss had expected shouting—had earned shouting—and its absence was worse. She stood before the five of them with the river wind pulling at her hair and spoke at the volume of a woman telling her crew the day's run.

"Here's what happens. Nobody hangs, nobody drowns, nobody gets beaten again. You tried to sell the chest because you were afraid, and fear's not treason—it's just expensive." Her eyes went down the line. "But you can't sail with me. Not because I don't forgive you. Because trust on a boat is like a hull—you can patch a crack, but you never forget where it is, and you spend every mile listening for it. I won't do that to you, and I won't do it to the ones who stood."

She nodded to Doss, who brought out the pay chest—his job again, and this time honest work—and counted out fair shares into five canvas bags. Full wages through the season. Passage money home. Even Tam's daughter's debt, covered from Yara's own reserve, though she said nothing about it and Doss only understood later, watching Tam weep into the bag without opening it.

"Pell," Yara said. "You turned inside the hour. When this is over—if there's an over—come find me. There'll be a place."

Pell's chin trembled once. He saluted, clumsy and old-fashioned, the way deckhands did in songs.

One by one they went down the gangplank onto the toll island shingle—Hask stony-faced, the Voss brothers not looking back, Tam looking back so many times it hurt to watch. Five men standing on a gray beach with five canvas bags, watching the boat that had been their whole world prepare to leave without them.

Last of all, Doss stood before Yara at the gangplank head, and the words he'd carried three days upriver finally failed him.

"You came back," Yara said.

"I sold you out first."

"You came back." She gripped his shoulder, hard, the grip of a captain and not a friend, which was the only kind he could bear. "First mate. If you ever betray me again, I'll maroon you somewhere with weather."

"Yes, Captain," Doss said, and his voice cracked clean in half, and he didn't care.

* * *

They cast off at dusk, running light with six hands where there had been twelve, the patched hull groaning its complaints into the darkening water. Doss took the wheel while Yara stood at the rail beside Sef, both of them watching the toll island shrink behind them, five small figures still visible on the shingle until the bend took them.

"He'll come again," Sef said. "Crane. Without offers next time."

"I know."

"And beyond Karst Reach there's no charts, no locks, no witnesses. Just us and whatever he sends."

"I know that too." Yara turned upstream, where the gorge waited somewhere past the last of the lamplight, unmapped and unlit. "But he's never met my river."

Doss, at the wheel, rang the engine bell twice—full ahead—and the Meridian leaned into the current and climbed.
