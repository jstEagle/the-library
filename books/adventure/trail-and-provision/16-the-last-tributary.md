## 16. The Last Tributary

The river stopped being a river an hour past dawn.

Yara felt it before she saw it—the way the current beneath the Meridian's hull changed its mind, the steady push of deep water giving way to something quicker, shallower, threaded with stone. She cut the engine and let the boat drift, listening. Upstream, where the valley should have opened wide enough for morning mist to pool, there was instead a sound like grinding teeth: water forced through a throat of rock.

"Gorge mouth," Sef said from the rail. She had the chest's map spread against her hip, weighted at the corners with river stones. "Past this, nothing. Not consortium charts, not Vessian ones. The old surveyors called it the Last Tributary because they said it was the last water anyone bothered to name."

"Charming name," Doss muttered. He stood at the stern with his arms crossed, favoring the shoulder he'd wrenched during the mutiny, watching the water as if it might apologize. "Sounds like something you say before things go badly."

"Everything sounds that way lately," Yara said.

She brought the Meridian in slow, easing her toward the northern bank where a shelf of gravel ran out into the current like a tongue. The gorge rose ahead of them—two walls of gray limestone leaning together, so close in places that the trees on either rim seemed to be shaking hands overhead. The river entered it without hesitation, folding itself narrow and white and gone.

Her boat could not follow. She had known it since Karst Reach, since the Nine Sisters, since the crack in the hull that Vessian hands in Vell Hollow had patched with resin and prayer. The Meridian was a riverboat, built for broad water and patient channels, forty years of oak and iron held together by her father's stubbornness and then by hers. The gorge would take her apart the way a child takes apart a toy—curiously, completely, without meaning any harm.

She tied off at the gravel shelf and stood a long moment with her hand flat on the wheel, feeling the engine tick as it cooled.

"You could wait here," Elias said behind her. He came up the companionway with the treaty case strapped across his back, looking rumpled and earnest and entirely too hopeful. "With the boat. If Crane's men come upriver, someone should—"

"No one's staying with the boat." She said it more sharply than she meant to. She softened it by not apologizing. "If we leave her, we leave her empty. Anything left aboard is something Crane can burn or steal or hold hostage. And she's not—" *Mine to lose,* she almost said, and didn't. "We all go. We carry what matters."

"The chest weighs as much as a drowned man," Doss observed.

"Then we'll carry it like one," Yara said. "With respect, and taking turns."

* * *

They broke camp on the gravel shelf in the gray light, and it took less time than Yara expected, which was its own small grief. Five years she had lived aboard the Meridian. Everything she owned that mattered fit into two canvas bags and a oilskin roll of charts, and everything else—the galley pots, the spare line, the brass lamp her father had won in a card game the night Yara was born—stayed behind in the dark of the cabin like organs in a body that had decided to keep living anyway.

Sef watched her lash the cabin door. "You could lock it."

"It doesn't lock. Never has." Yara tested the hasp, found it good, and stepped back. "My father said a locked boat tells the river you expect thieves. An open one tells it you trust the water to sort people out." She paused. "He also lost two outboard motors that way, so I don't know how much wisdom there is in it."

They divided the load among six: Yara, Sef, Elias, Doss, and the two crew who had stayed through the mutiny—a lean, silent pair named Corrin and Marette who had signed on for wages and somehow arrived at something closer than wages. The chest went onto a frame of ash poles that Doss lashed together with the efficiency of a man who had done worse jobs for worse reasons. It rode between four shoulders, swaying, and even empty of its documents it carried the weight of everything inside it: the treaty, the annex pages Elias hadn't burned, the ledger Yara still hadn't finished reading, the confession she was trying very hard not to think about.

Crane's papers were in there too. Her father's handwriting, page after page, describing a betrayal decades old—the location of a hidden nation traded for money, and then a lifetime spent hauling restitution up and down the same river like a man bailing out a boat with a hole in the bottom. She had read enough. Not enough to understand. Enough to know that understanding wouldn't come from reading, and might not come at all.

"Captain." Marette nodded upstream. "Smoke."

Yara turned. Far downriver, around the last bend below the gorge mouth, a thread of gray rose against the green—not chimney smoke, too thin and moving wrong. Engine smoke. A boat running hard, running hot, coming up the channel they had just climbed.

"Crane?"

"Could be a trader," Doss said, without conviction.

"Traders don't run their engines at full burn in the shallows." Yara looked at the gorge, then at the smoke, then at her boat sitting patient and helpless on the gravel like an old dog told to stay. "We go now. Quiet, quick, no talking on the move. If they reach the Meridian, let them find her empty and let them wonder."

She took one last look aft—at the wheelhouse glass catching the early sun, at the wake-line of dried mud on the hull marking every flood they'd survived—and made herself turn away before the look became a ceremony.

* * *

The gorge swallowed sound first.

Within fifty paces of the entrance the world narrowed to water-noise and rock, the walls rising sheer on either side until the sky was a bright ribbon far overhead. The trail—if it deserved the word—clung to the eastern wall, a shelf of broken stone no wider in places than the chest was long. Below it the river ran fast and green and blind, churning through slots in the rock with a sound like continuous applause for something terrible.

They moved in single file. Yara led, reading the stone the way she read water: where the footing was honest, where it lied, where a handhold that looked solid was actually a slab waiting to shear loose under weight. Behind her came Corrin, then the chest on its frame, borne by Doss and Marette and Elias rotating through the carrying positions, then Sef at the rear walking backward every hundred steps to watch the way they'd come.

By midday the smoke question answered itself. They heard it before they saw it—engines, but wrong engines, the flat snarl of outboards pushed past their design, and voices carrying strangely off the stone. Yara pressed the column into a fold in the cliff and waited while three boats ran the gorge mouth below them, gunwales crowded with men in consortium gray, one of them standing in the bow with binoculars raised.

The man with the binoculars swept the walls. For ten seconds his lenses passed within a few degrees of the fold where six people and a national treasure crouched in the dark, breathing shallow. Then he lowered them, said something to the helmsman, and the boats ground ashore at the gravel shelf beside the abandoned Meridian.

Yara watched a consortium officer walk her gangplank and step aboard her boat, and something in her chest pulled tight as anchor chain.

"He'll search her," Doss breathed.

"He'll find nothing. There's nothing to find." But her hands had closed into fists without asking permission. "Look at him. He doesn't know what he's standing on. To him she's just a boat."

"To you she isn't," Sef murmured.

No one answered that. Below, the officer emerged from the wheelhouse holding Yara's brass lamp, turning it over in the light, and then—he did something strange. He set it back down on the chart table where he'd found it, carefully, aligning it with the edge, and left it there. Yara couldn't decide whether that was discipline or superstition or some order from Crane about leaving no mark, and she found she hated not knowing.

The boats pushed off twenty minutes later, running deeper into the gorge, chasing the rumor of them upstream. Yara gave it an hour before she moved the column on.

* * *

They made camp that night on a ledge above a side-cascade, a place where the gorge opened just enough to admit a slice of sky full of hard stars. No fire. Cold rations passed hand to hand in the dark. The chest sat in the middle of their little circle like a guest of honor nobody had invited, and Yara found herself sitting with her back against it, feeling through the wood the faint vibration of the waterfall below, as if the documents inside had a pulse.

Elias settled beside her. In the starlight his face looked younger and more exhausted than his voice ever admitted.

"You're doing the arithmetic," he said.

"What arithmetic?"

"How many days of food. How many miles of gorge. How long before Crane's boats find the portage trail on the far bank, if there is one." He pulled his coat tighter. "Archivists do the same thing with manuscripts. How many pages survive, how many burned, how much truth can be reconstructed from the holes. You spend your whole career measuring loss."

"And what do you measure it against?"

He was quiet a moment. "Against what it cost the people who made it," he said finally. "Every gap in a record is a person who died keeping that secret, or a person who failed to. That's why I'm here, Yara. Not for the treaty. For the gaps. My grandmother was born in Vessia the year before the water came. She remembered a city. Nobody else alive does. When she died, the city died again, and I swore—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "I've said too much. I always say too much when I'm tired."

"You've said too little for weeks," Yara said. "But tonight's not the night I press you. Tonight I'm too tired to fight fair."

Something moved through the dark between them—not quite warmth, not quite trust, but the shape of a thing that might become either. Above them the ribbon of sky slid westward, and the stars wheeled, indifferent and beautiful, over a nation that existed only in an old woman's memory and a sealed chest and the stubborn feet of six people climbing toward it.

* * *

On the third day the gorge tried to kill them.

It happened at a place where the trail simply ended—a collapse, ancient or recent, where half the shelf had sheared away into the river and left a gap of perhaps eight meters of blank vertical rock. Below the gap the water ran white through a slot no wider than a coffin. There was no going around. The map showed a crossing marked in faded ink with a symbol Elias identified, after a long silence, as the Vessian sign for *faith*.

"That's not reassuring," Doss said.

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be accurate."

They solved it the way the old surveyors must have: a rope anchored to a flake of granite, traversed one at a time, packs slung tight, boots finding holds that existed only because someone else had once needed them to. Yara went first, testing each placement, and the river roared beneath her close enough to throw cold spray up the wall and into her teeth. Halfway across, the rock under her right foot moved—a plate the size of a dinner plate, loose as a lid—and for one long suspended second she hung on the rope above white water with nothing under her at all, and the gorge waited to see what kind of woman she was.

She swung. Found a crack with her toe. Pulled through.

Getting the chest across was worse. They rigged it on a second line and ferried it hand over hand, four people hauling while two steadied the frame, and when it finally swung over the gap and thudded onto solid stone, Marette sat down hard on the trail and laughed the high, cracked laugh of someone who has been frightened for a very long time and only now permitted herself to stop.

That evening, making camp beyond the collapse, Yara caught Sef watching her with an expression she couldn't parse.

"What?"

"Nothing. Just—you crossed like you'd done it before."

"I haven't. But I've run water my whole life, and water teaches you one lesson over and over." Yara fed a strip of dried fish to the dark, chewing slowly. "The moment you freeze is the moment it takes you. You commit or you drown. Everything else is detail."

"And your father? Did he teach you that?"

The question landed precisely where Sef had aimed it, which was becoming a habit Yara tolerated only because it was useful. Her father, who had committed—to the courier work, to both sides, to the penance, to whatever mix of greed and guilt had kept him rowing up and down this river for thirty years. Her father, who had never once in her memory frozen, and who had betrayed a nation anyway.

"He taught me the river part," Yara said. "The rest he kept to himself. Turns out he kept quite a lot to himself."

Sef nodded slowly. "When we reach the valley, the elders will ask about him. The chest is known. The courier is known. Some of them have waited a century to learn what happened to the man who carried their treaty out." She hesitated. "What will you tell them?"

"The truth," Yara said, and heard how it sounded—like a promise and a threat aimed at the same person. "Whatever the truth turns out to be. They've earned it. So did he, maybe. I'm still deciding."

* * *

On the fifth day the walls began to open.

It came gradually, the way dawn comes—the ribbon of sky widening, the limestone giving way to granite, the roar of the confined river softening into something broader and older. Trees appeared on the rims again, real forest, uncut. And the water itself changed: clearer, colder, tasting of snowmelt and stone, running with the particular confident clarity of water that has never been dammed, diverted, or sold.

Yara knew it the way she knew weather coming. This was source water. They were drinking the top of the river.

They abandoned the trail that afternoon for a tributary canyon branching north, following Elias's map and the growing frequency of marks carved into the rock—small, deliberate, centuries old. A spiral. A closed eye. Once, unmistakably, the crest of Vessia, cut deep into a boulder the size of a chapel, worn smooth by time but still proud.

Doss ran his thumb over it. "People lived out here. Whole lives. Right under Crane's maps."

"Right under everyone's," Elias said quietly. "That was the point."

They made camp that night in the mouth of the tributary, and Yara walked alone down to the water while the others slept. The Meridian was two days' march behind her, sitting empty on a gravel shelf with her cabin unlocked and her lamp on the chart table, and Yara understood suddenly, standing knee-deep in the coldest water of her life, that she had already said goodbye. Somewhere back at the gorge mouth, in the moment she'd turned away before the look became a ceremony, she had buried the woman who measured her life in miles of river and nothing else. Whatever walked into the source valley tomorrow would be someone new, carrying the same scars and none of the certainties, and she found—to her surprise—that she wasn't afraid of that. She was afraid of the opposite. Of arriving. Of the chest opening and the treaty signing and the war of paper and reprisal beginning, and of learning whether her father's ghost weighed more or less than her father's sins.

Behind her, upstream, a sentry's whistle sounded—soft, double-noted, Corrin's signal.

Yara waded out and climbed back to camp, where the others were already on their feet, staring north up the tributary canyon. High on the far wall, barely visible in the last light, lamps were being lit. One, then another, then a string of them descending a switchback trail toward the valley floor—deliberate, unhurried, a message written in fire.

*They see us,* the lamps said. *Come.*

"They know we're here," Sef whispered.

"Someone does," Elias said, and Yara heard it again, that hairline fracture under his voice, the thing he was editing out. "For better or worse—they know."

Yara shouldered her pack and looked north, where the lamps descended through the dark like stars falling in reverse, and somewhere behind them, far downriver, she imagined she could hear engines.
