## 17. The Source Valley

Elias had rehearsed this arrival a thousand times.

In the archive at Corvane—before it burned, before he learned that archives burn—he had built the valley in his mind from fragments: survey notes smuggled out in hymnals, a shepherd's testimony copied twice and then destroyed, the hydrological surveys of the consortium's own engineers, who had spent forty years looking for what they called the Anomaly—a watershed that fed the great river but appeared on no gauge, no map, no ledger. He had assembled Vessia the way a man assembles a face from a description: faithfully, lovingly, and almost certainly wrong.

Now he stood on the last shelf of rock above the gorge with the chest strapped to Sef's back and Yara Whitlock's rope around his waist, and found that he had been wrong about everything except the light.

The valley opened below them like a held breath let go after a hundred years. Terraces climbed the southern wall in green steps—orchards, he realized, apple and quince by the blossom, though the season was early for both—and water moved through them in channels so old they had worn themselves into the architecture, running beside footpaths, under bridges the size of coffins, turning wheels that turned nothing he could name until he saw the mill and understood. The houses were stone at the base and timber above, roofed in split shingle gone silver, and every one of them faced the river. Not toward it. *Facing* it, the way congregations face an altar. On the far bank, cut into the cliff itself, rose a facade of columns and arched windows that had no business existing in a valley the consortium had erased from every map in the world—the drowned nation's last public building, or its first new one, carved rather than built, as if the mountain had been persuaded to remember what a capital looked like.

Smoke rose from chimneys in straight, unhurried lines. Children's voices carried up the gorge, thin and bright, arguing about something in a language Elias knew only from paper—from treaty preambles and land grants and the marginalia of exiled poets. Vessian. Spoken. Alive. He gripped the rope and felt his eyes sting and told himself it was the wind off the snowmelt.

"You're crying," Sef said. She had come up beside him without a sound; she did everything without a sound. The chest was on her back as if it weighed nothing, though he knew it weighed more than any of them could carry for long.

"The wind," he said.

"Of course." She looked down at the valley with an expression he could not read, which frightened him more than Crane's gunboats had. "It's smaller than I remembered."

He turned. "You said you'd never—"

"I said I'd never been here." Her jaw was set in the way it set when she was deciding how much truth a moment could hold. "I didn't say I'd never seen it."

Below them, a bell began to ring—not alarm, not yet, but the flat urgent rhythm of a watch-post signal—and figures emerged onto the terraces, small at this distance, pointing up at the three of them on the shelf of rock where no one was supposed to be able to stand.

"They've seen us," Yara said, coming up the last of the climb with Doss behind her, breathing hard, her hands still shaped to a wheel she had abandoned two days ago at the gorge mouth. She looked at the bell tower, at the converging figures, and Elias watched her do the arithmetic she always did—exits, currents, odds—and arrive, as she always did, at staying anyway. "So much for arriving quietly."

"We were never going to arrive quietly," Elias said. "We were always going to arrive like a verdict."

* * *

They came down to them through the orchards, six of them, moving with the unhurried competence of people who had drilled for this day for generations and half hoped it would never come. They carried long guns of an old pattern, maintained beautifully, and wore no uniform—only a blue thread knotted at the collar, which Elias recognized from a description in a smuggled letter and which undid him more than the columns had. The Blue Thread. The loyalists' mark. He had written a monograph on it. It had been cited eleven times. None of the citations had known it was real.

Their leader was a woman of perhaps sixty, gray-braided, with a face like a riverbed—everything visible, everything worn smooth by what had passed over it. She looked at Yara first, because Yara stood in front, because Yara always stood in front. Then the chest on Sef's back. Then, last and longest, at Elias.

"You're the archivist," she said. Not a question.

"Elias Vane. Of the Corvane collection." He inclined his head, and heard how the title rang hollow here, in the living country his papers had only mourned. "What remains of it."

"Corvane burned nine years ago."

"Yes."

"Then you are what remains of it." Something that was not quite warmth moved through her voice. "I am Elder Maren Ostrev. You will eat, and sleep, and tomorrow you will open that chest before the council of the valley. Not tonight. Nothing that changes everything is opened at night—it breeds mistakes." Her eyes went to Sef again, and stayed. "You carry it well."

"It's not heavy," Sef said, "if you know what it is."

Something passed between the two women then, brief and unreadable, and Elias felt the first cold thread of it—the thing he had been carrying since Vell Hollow, since the annex pages, since the night by the fire when he had fed the truth to the flames page by page and told himself it was mercy. Maren Ostrev looked at Sef the way a woman looks at a door she thought was painted on a wall.

But she said nothing, and led them down.

* * *

The valley fed them better than they had eaten since the Meridian. Bread with a dark crust and a sourness that meant old starter, meaning unbroken tradition, meaning a hundred years of mornings. River fish smoked over apple wood. A cider that made Doss close her eyes and say something profane and reverent. They ate in a long hall whose back wall was the living rock, and on that rock, by lamplight, Elias read names. Thousands of them. Cut in careful ranks, some ancient, some recent enough that the stone dust still showed in the grooves.

"A census?" he asked, when their host—a young man named Pell who spoke trade-tongue with a Vessian accent that made Elias's chest ache—had stepped away.

"A ledger," said an old man across the table, without opening his eyes. "Of the dead. Every Vessian the consortium killed, drowned, starved, or worked to death, so far as we could learn it. We add to it when we learn more. There is room for another century." He opened his eyes. "There will not need to be."

Yara had gone quiet in the way she went quiet—completely, dangerously, like deep water. She was looking at the wall, and Elias saw her lips move once, and knew she had found a name. He did not ask which. Some questions you already know the answer to, and asking them only makes the other person say it aloud.

Later, in the narrow room they were given—two beds, a window slit facing the river, shutters that locked from inside, because even paradise keeps habits bought with survival—Sef sat on the sill with her back to him and said, without turning:

"Tomorrow, when the chest opens, the elders will read the treaty. And the treaty names an heir. And the heir they have been waiting for has a shape, doesn't he? Someone they've already chosen. Someone already here."

Elias kept his voice level. "The succession clause is complex. There are conditions—"

"There's a name, Elias." She turned. In the dark her eyes were the only part of her. "I've been reading faces since I was fourteen years old. It was my profession. Maren Ostrev looked at me today like a woman reading a document she'd been promised. And you have been walking through this valley like a man carrying a broken jar, trying to keep the smell from escaping." She stood. "Whatever you burned out of that annex—I'm going to know it by tomorrow sundown. You can choose whether I learn it from you or from the fallout."

She left. Elias sat on the bed that smelled of cedar and wool and listened to the river, which here ran clear and quick and honest over stones it had never been allowed to drown anyone with, and understood that he had run out of current. A man can steer a lie downstream for a long time. But rivers end. Valleys end. Tomorrow there would be a council, and a chest, and a treaty, and a thousand names on a wall, and every one of them a reason the truth mattered more than the mercy he had told himself he was performing.

He took out the last thing he had saved from the annex—not a page but a rubbing, graphite on tissue, of the seal and signature block of the succession clause, made in Corvane when he was young enough to believe that documents were neutral things. He looked at it for a long time. Then he put it away, because keeping it or burning it would change nothing now, and because his hands were shaking too badly to trust with fire.

* * *

The council convened at midmorning in the columned hall cut into the cliff, and Elias understood, walking into it, why the consortium had needed to erase Vessia rather than merely defeat it. Defeat makes ruins. This was not a ruin. Under the vaulted stone, beneath windows that threw long blades of morning light across the floor, perhaps two hundred people had gathered—elders in the front rows, tradespeople and farmers behind them, children on the edges being hushed by parents who clearly remembered being hushed in the same places. A functioning nation. A claim with a heartbeat. Everything the consortium's lawyers had sworn, in forty years of filings, could not exist.

Maren Ostrev presided from a plain chair—no throne, he noted, no dais; Vessia had apparently decided somewhere in its exile that thrones were how the trouble started—and the chest was brought forward on a table of black oak and set before the assembly, and the hall went silent in a way Elias had never heard silence go. Not empty. *Full*. Two hundred people holding one breath between them.

Yara unlocked it. She had kept the key since the flood, and when Elias had once asked her why, she had said, "Because my father trusted me with it, even if he didn't know it," and he had understood then that whatever else happened, the captain would stand by the chest to the end. She lifted the lid. The smell of oilcloth and old iron rose up, and with it, for Elias, the smell of Corvane, of his whole life, of the boy he had been among the shelves.

Maren Ostrev drew out the treaty herself. Unrolled it on the oak. The assembly leaned forward as one body.

And Elias watched the elder's eyes move down the preamble, the articles, the water rights, the source claims—watched her face do exactly what he had known it would do, article by article, hope hardening into legal fact—and then watched her reach the succession clause.

Watched her stop.

Watched her read it again.

The silence changed quality. He had spent his life in rooms full of readers; he knew the acoustics of comprehension. Maren Ostrev's finger came to rest on a line near the bottom of the parchment, and she did not look up, and her voice, when it came, was very quiet and very steady, which was worse than shouting.

"This names the heir of the blood of Vessia." Her finger did not move. "By name."

"Then it's done," someone called from the benches—an old soldier's voice, hungry with a century of waiting. "Read the name. Let us hear our sovereign's name at last."

Maren Ostrev raised her head. Her eyes went past the treaty, past Yara, past Elias as if he were glass—

—to Sef.

Who stood at the edge of the light with her arms crossed and her face perfectly composed, the face of a surveillance officer holding position, and who met the elder's gaze and did not flinch, and Elias understood that she had known. Had perhaps known since the orchard. Since before. Since the moment she had said *it's smaller than I remembered* and let him believe she meant the valley.

"Read it," Maren Ostrev said, but she was looking at Sef, and everyone in the hall was looking at Sef, and the name on the parchment was suddenly the least important thing in the room, because the room had already read it in the elder's face.

Elias stepped forward. His mouth was dry and his heart was hammering and somewhere behind him Yara said his name in a warning tone, and he ignored it, because he had run out of current, and a man at the end of the river has only one choice left.

"Before anyone reads anything," he said, and his voice cracked on the second word, and he let it, "there is something the council must know. About the annex. About the succession. About why I burned it." He drew a breath that hurt all the way down. "And about who sent me."

At the far end of the hall, by the doors, a guard who had been standing watch since dawn quietly turned the lock behind him—and Elias, seeing it, seeing the man's collar and the set of his shoulders and the way his hand rested near his knife, understood with terrible clarity that the verdict he had been dreading had not been delayed after all.

It had arrived ahead of them.
