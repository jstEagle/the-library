## 19. Crane at the Gates

The horn came at dawn.

Not a boat horn—Yara had spent thirty years learning what horns meant on water—but a land horn, brass and arrogant, three long notes that rolled down the valley walls and died in the terraced fields below the village. She was on the guest-house porch with a cup of barley tea going cold in her hands when the sound reached her, and she knew before anyone spoke what it meant. You didn't bring a brass horn up a gorge unless you wanted people to know you'd arrived.

Sef was already running through the wet grass, barefoot, shouting names Yara had learned over six days of mending and waiting: *Ondrey. Ondrey—the gate.* The valley woke around her like a body remembering pain. Doors opened along the row houses built into the hillside, their turf roofs still silvered with dew. Children were scooped up by grandmothers. Somewhere a bell began to ring—not the meal bell she'd come to know, but a thinner, faster note, an alarm that had not been rung in living memory and yet had never been allowed to rust.

Yara set down her tea and went to find Elias.

She found him at the eastern overlook, where the path from the gorge mouth climbed through birch scrub to a shelf of granite overlooking the valley's single entrance. He had the treaty case strapped across his chest like a bandolier, and he was watching the gorge with the fixed expression of a man reading a document he already knew by heart and hated anyway.

"How many?" Yara asked.

"Hard to say. The mist's still down in the narrows." He handed her the spyglass without looking away. "I count boats at the landing. Six. Maybe eight."

She put the glass to her eye. The gorge mouth was a gray wound in the mountain wall, and out of it, one by one, came Crane's men in flat-bottomed assault skiffs—river iron, shallow draft, each carrying eight soldiers in dark canvas with the consortium's dam-and-chain mark stenciled on their backs. They moved with the ugly efficiency of men who had done this before. Behind them, riding the last skiff like a passenger on a Sunday excursion, stood a figure in a pale coat who could only be Halvard Crane himself, hands clasped behind him, surveying the water as though it were property he'd come to inspect.

"He brought them up the Nine Sisters," Yara said quietly. "In those tubs."

"He bought pilots. Ex-raftsmen who ran the Sisters before the dams closed them." Elias's jaw worked. "There is always someone willing to sell a river."

"There always has been." She lowered the glass. Below, the first skiffs were grounding on the shingle beach where the valley's stream braided out of the gorge. Soldiers poured ashore and began forming ranks with a discipline that made her stomach tighten. This wasn't a raid. It was an occupation arriving on schedule.

"Where are the elders?"

"Calling the valley together at the meeting house. Sef is with them." He finally turned to look at her, and for once there was no archivist's polish in his face, no charm worn like a coat. Just a thin man with tired eyes and a nation's paperwork strapped to his ribs. "Yara. If they take the treaty—"

"They won't."

"You can't promise that."

"I'm not promising anything." She looked back down at the beach, at the pale coat standing ankle-deep in Vessia's water. "I'm telling you what I'm going to do."

* * *

The meeting house smelled of woodsmoke and old wool, and it was full of people who had spent a century perfecting the art of being invisible and now had nowhere left to hide. The elders sat in their crescent of carved chairs. Farmers stood along the walls with scythes they hadn't carried since their grandfathers' funerals. And at the center, beside the head elder—a woman named Maren with a face like weathered rope—stood Sef, wearing the valley's gray traveling cloak, looking like nothing so much as a woman being measured for a coffin.

"The gorge path takes two hours on foot," Maren was saying as Yara slipped in. "We cannot move the whole valley. There are children who cannot walk it, elders who cannot climb. And if we run, they follow, and next time they bring more than eight skiffs."

"Then we don't run," Yara said.

Every face turned to her. She felt the weight of it—the stranger's weight, the outsider's weight—and let it settle on her shoulders the way she'd learned to let a deck load settle: evenly, or not at all.

"Crane wants two things," she said. "The treaty and the heir. He's brought maybe sixty men and enough gunpowder to make this quick. What he hasn't brought—" she held up a hand, ticking fingers, "—is anyone who knows this water."

"This isn't water," said a farmer near the wall. "It's a valley."

"It's a valley full of water." She walked to the great map on the meeting-house wall—the one Elias had shown her on the second night, inked by exile hands, every channel and flume and millrace of the hidden valley rendered in loving detail. Her finger found the gorge mouth, then climbed. "You built these. Your grandparents did. Flumes to carry snowmelt to the terraces. Millraces. A cistern system up under the rim—I saw the intake gates yesterday when I walked the high path. Sluice channels cut into the rock above the gorge road." She turned. "Your whole valley is a machine for moving water downhill. And Crane is about to march sixty men up a narrow road at the bottom of it, in flood season."

Silence. Then Maren said slowly, "The spring gates haven't been opened in forty years. The mechanisms are stone and timber. Some will be rotted through."

"Some will hold." Yara met her eyes. "I've spent my life betting on which parts of a river will hold and which won't. I've been wrong plenty. But I've been wrong alive." She swept her hand across the map. "Show me your sluicemen. Show me whoever remembers how these gates work. Give me until midday, and give me every strong back you can spare, and when Crane's column reaches the Narrows—" her finger stopped on a defile where the gorge road pinched between cliff and water, barely wide enough for four abreast, "—we'll introduce him to the river he thinks he owns."

An old man near the front—bent, white-bearded, with forearms like knotted root—began, very quietly, to laugh.

"I kept those gates," he said. "My father kept them before me. Forty years since anyone asked." He rose, joints cracking like green firewood. "Captain, you'd better come see what we have to work with."

* * *

They worked through the morning, and it was the strangest labor of Yara's life: all her craft, transposed. On the Meridian she read current by the lean of reeds and the color of water over shoal; here she read it in the engineering of dead men. The intake gates under the rim were oak and iron, swollen shut with decades, and it took six men and a windlass and the better part of an hour to crack the first one open—and when it opened, the sound was the sound she knew best in the world, the deep throat-note of water finding its old channel again, a groan and then a rush and then a steady roar filling a flume that had been dry since before she was born.

The old keeper—his name was Tam, and he talked constantly, the way lonely men do—walked her the length of the high system while crews cleared debris from the channels. Three main sluices fed the terraces. Two diversion cuts could dump water straight down the cliff faces into the gorge. And above the Narrows, cut into the rock a hundred feet up, ran the old flood-spillway: a stone trough built to shed avalanche meltwater safely past the village, discharging through a series of chutes directly onto the gorge road.

"It was built for emergencies," Tam said, patting the spillway's mossy lip. "Rockfall blocking the road. That sort of thing. Never for—" he hesitated.

"For drowning soldiers," Yara finished.

He looked at her sharply. "Is that what we're doing?"

She thought about it honestly, because the question deserved honesty. She thought about the hanging town, and the empty skiffs, and Doss on his sandbar, and her father's ledger with its two columns of payments. She thought about Crane's voice saying *your father understood the value of order.*

"No," she said. "We're doing what rivers do. We're not killing anyone who turns back. Post runners on the high rocks with white cloth. Anyone who retreats gets to retreat. Anyone who keeps coming after the water hits—" she shrugged, "—that's the river's business, and theirs."

Tam studied her a moment, then nodded slowly. "Maren was right to trust you."

"Maren doesn't trust me. Maren trusts the treaty." Yara wiped her hands on her trousers and looked down-valley. From here she could see the whole approach: the shingle beach, now crowded with stacked crates and picketed skiffs; the gorge road winding up through the trees; and far below, tiny as toys, the advance column already moving, banners furled, bayonets catching the noon light. "Trust the water instead. It's the only thing down there that's ever been honest."

* * *

Crane sent a herald at midday.

The man came up the road alone under a white rag tied to a pike, and Yara watched him from the overlook with Sef and Elias and half the valley's fighting-age population ranged along the ridgeline above. The herald stopped at the foot of the switchbacks and unrolled a paper and read in a voice trained for boardrooms:

"Halvard Crane, Director of the Consolidated Watershed Consortium, offers terms. Surrender the document known as the Vessian Treaty and the person claiming its inheritance. In exchange: full amnesty for all residents of this valley, guaranteed non-interference in perpetuity, and compensation—" the herald squinted at the page, "—to be negotiated. Refusal will be met with force. You have until the sun touches the western spur."

Nobody answered. The valley had gotten very good, over a hundred years, at not answering.

But Crane hadn't come all this way to be ignored, and twenty minutes later he came himself—up the road on foot, flanked by four guards, stopping well short of the switchbacks, close enough to be seen clearly through Yara's glass. Up close he was smaller than his reputation, a neat gray man with neat gray hair and the patient eyes of someone who had spent decades buying things and could not conceive of a thing that wasn't for sale.

"Captain Whitlock," he called up, and his voice carried off the rock the way voices carry in gorges. "I confess I hoped it wouldn't come to this. Your father and I did business for many years. He was a reasonable man."

Yara stepped to the edge of the overlook, where the sun put her in full view. Beside her, she felt Sef go rigid.

"Was he?" she called down. "Which year?"

Something flickered across Crane's face—the first honest expression she'd seen on him. "He came to understand, eventually, what his early work had cost. As I hope you will. Captain, I'm offering these people peace. Real peace. A century of hiding ends today either way—it ends with a signed accord or it ends with graves. Help me choose the kinder version."

"You drowned a country," Yara said. "And now you're offering to sell the survivors the water."

"I offer them survival. It's the only currency that's ever mattered." He spread his hands, reasonable to the last. "The sun is moving, Captain."

"So is the river." She stepped back from the edge. "You've got your answer."

Crane looked up at her for a long moment, and then he smiled—thin, almost regretful—and turned, and walked back down toward his column, and the valley exhaled around her.

"Well," Elias said softly. "That's that."

"That's that," Yara agreed, and turned to Tam. "Open the upper gates. All of them. Let her fill."

* * *

The sun touched the western spur at seven minutes past five, and Crane's column entered the Narrows at nine past.

Yara watched it happen from the spillway control ledge, a shelf of rock above the chutes where she stood with Tam and two of his grandsons and the windlass levers that would drop the final flash-boards. Below and ahead, the gorge road ran along the cliff base, hemmed between black stone and the brawling white of the spring-fed river. Crane's men came up it in column of fours, sixty strong, bayonets slung, boots crunching on old gravel. Crane rode a mule near the middle, pale coat bright against the gloom of the defile. Above them, invisible on the rim, the diverted sluices had been pouring water into the old reservoir basin behind a timber dam all afternoon—water with nowhere to go but down, held back by boards that Tam's grandfather had shaped by hand.

"Wait," Yara murmured, mostly to herself, reading the column the way she'd read a bend in a river. Wait for the head of the column to pass the first chute. Wait for the tail to clear the last one. Wait for the mule. A trap sprung too early caught only scouts; sprung too late, nothing at all. Timing was everything, on water and off it. Her father had taught her that, hauling freight on schedule through waters that killed the impatient. Whatever else the man had been, he'd taught her that.

The head of the column passed beneath the first chute. The tail cleared the last. The mule walked into the Narrows, and Crane's pale coat disappeared into the shadow of the cliff.

"Now," Yara said.

Tam's grandsons threw the levers. The flash-boards dropped with a boom that echoed off both canyon walls, and the reservoir let go.

It did not fall so much as arrive. A hundred feet of accumulated spring water hit the first chute, sheeted across the stone lip, and came down onto the gorge road as a single white wall—then the second chute released, and the third, and the Narrows filled with roaring, tumbling, thunderous water, the river suddenly everywhere it had always intended to be. Yara heard screaming, and splintering timber, and the peculiar hollow clang of a rifle striking rock, and above it all the voice of the flood itself, which was the oldest voice she knew.

"White cloth!" she shouted, and the runners on the rim leaned out and waved their banners, and downstream of the deluge the soldiers who could still stand saw them—saw the white rags snapping in the spray—and broke. Not all at once, but in a rolling collapse, like a seam giving way, men dropping rifles and stumbling back down the road through knee-deep water, away from the Narrows, away from the valley, away from whatever godless engineering had just fallen on them out of a clear sky.

The mule, sensibly, had bolted. Crane was on his feet in the shallows, pale coat plastered black, hat gone, staring up at the rim with an expression Yara could read even at this distance, because she'd seen it a thousand times on the faces of men whose barges had just gone out from under them. It was not fear. It was recalculation. Somewhere behind those patient eyes, a new number was being computed.

"Let them go," Yara said, as her runners started forward along the rim with ropes and staves to help the stranded ones downstream. "All of them. Even him."

Tam spat over the edge. "After what he—"

"After what he did, exactly. We're not him." She watched the flood water begin to drop, the chutes throttling down as the reservoir emptied, the Narrows returning to something like quiet, littered with abandoned arms and one very dignified, very soaked director standing hip-deep in a river he had spent his life pretending to own. "Besides," she added, "he's got to walk home. That's punishment enough."

But even as she said it, her eye caught something downstream that took the satisfaction out of her mouth. At the shingle beach, where the skiffs lay picketed, figures were moving that shouldn't have been moving—men who had stayed behind when the column advanced, six of them, working fast and low along the waterline. Not retreating. Boarding. And not boarding the skiffs.

Boarding the one boat in the valley big enough to make the lower gorge runnable.

The Meridian's launch—the tender they'd hauled up the last tributary and cached at the beach, patched and provisioned for the journey home.

"No," Yara breathed. Then louder, turning, already running: "Elias! Where's the treaty case? *Elias!*"

But the overlook behind her was empty, and down on the gray water, six of Crane's men were pushing the tender off the shingle, and the last thing she saw before the birch scrub swallowed her view was a seventh figure stepping aboard—tall, thin, coat dark with river water—raising one hand, whether in farewell or apology, toward the valley walls.

Toward her.

* * *

She reached the beach with Sef and a dozen of the valley's young men at her heels, lungs burning, in time to watch the tender swing into the current at the gorge mouth, oars biting, Crane's soaked soldiers hauling at the thwarts. Elias stood at the stern sheets with the treaty case strapped across his chest, and he was not struggling, and he was not shouting, and that—that more than anything—was what told her.

"He went willingly," Sef said. Her voice was flat and terrible. "He planned it. The whole time."

Yara stared at the boat shrinking into the gorge's gray throat, at the man she'd pulled out of the flood, fed at her own table, argued with, half-trusted, and understood—with a clarity that felt like cold water closing over her head—that she had never once actually known him.

Downstream, faint on the wind, came the sound of oarlocks keeping easy time.

Someone who knew the river was rowing that boat.
