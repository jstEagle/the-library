## 20. Her Father's Confession

The rain had stopped by the time they finished counting the dead and the captured, but the valley still dripped with it—water running off the terraced roofs of Vell Hollow's sister village in bright threads, pooling in the wheel-ruts of the road where Crane's mercenaries had come up the gorge and been turned back by the river itself. Yara walked through it all with her boots squelching and her hands still shaking, though not from fear. Fear had burned off hours ago, somewhere between the moment she'd opened the sluice gates and the moment Crane's men had gone into the flood channel screaming. What remained was something quieter and harder to name.

The prisoners were being held in the granary under guard of a dozen Vessian farmers with hunting rifles and faces like closed doors. The captured papers were stacked on a trestle table in the longhouse—the valley's meeting hall, its rafters black with decades of hearth smoke—and Sef had ordered them brought there rather than burned, because Sef understood what Yara was only beginning to learn: that paper could be a weapon too, and that weapons taken from an enemy should be examined before they were spent.

Yara came to the longhouse at dusk because someone had to read the papers, and because Elias was still being watched, and because sitting still felt worse than anything else she could imagine doing.

The table was buried. Contracts, requisitions, coded ledgers, correspondence in Crane's tight, joyless hand. She worked through it methodically, the way her father had taught her to work through a cargo manifest—top to bottom, no skipping, because the thing you skip is always the thing that sinks you. Outside, the valley sang its evening song: water everywhere, in the aqueducts and the millrace and the irrigation channels cut into the hillsides, the sound that had been Vessia's heartbeat for a hundred years and more.

She found the confession near midnight.

It wasn't labeled as such. It was a folder bound in gray oilcloth, tied with the kind of waxed cord sailors used for rigging knots, and when she lifted it from the stack her fingers knew the weight before her mind did—that particular density of many pages written over many years. She untied the cord. The first page was dated thirty-one years back, and the handwriting on it was her father's.

*My name is Tomas Whitlock, and I am going to write down what I did, so that someone after me will know it was done.*

She sat very still. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside a dog barked twice and went quiet.

*I was twenty-two years old and I thought I was clever. A man named Corvane—I did not know then he spoke for the consortium, I thought he was a shipping agent—paid me forty crowns to carry a letter upriver past the Karst Reach and deliver it to a survey camp above the falls. Forty crowns was half a season's wages. I asked no questions. That is the whole of my crime and none of it excuses me: I asked no questions.*

*The letter contained the location of the source valley. Not a guess. Coordinates, soundings, the safe landings, the hidden channel through the gorge. Someone had drawn it from a Vessian chart, and I carried it, and eleven months later the consortium's engineers came up the gorge along the route I had delivered to them, and they built the dam, and the water rose.*

Yara's hand went flat against the table as if the page might blow away, though there was no wind. She read on. She would remember afterward that she never once considered stopping.

*I learned what I had done the way everyone learns: too late and completely. My own mother's people came out of those hills. I had cousins whose names I never knew, drowned or scattered before I could ever ask. I told myself for years that I hadn't known, that ignorance was innocence, and then one night on the Meridian—she was new then, barely launched—I stopped lying to myself and started keeping this book instead.*

*Corvane came back to me every few years with work. He believed I was his. Perhaps I was, at first. But every run he paid for, I took, and every run I took, I also carried something he did not know about: names, records, coin, small salvations, passed quietly to the scattered ones. When I found the chest's contents—found, or was given, I will write honestly that I do not remember which—I understood what the last work of my life should be. The treaty must reach the source or no one. If I could not undo the drowning, I could at least see the deed returned to its rightful owners.*

*If you are reading this, you are either Crane, in which case burn it and be damned, or you are someone the river chose. To the second sort I can only say: I loved the river better than I deserved to, and it carried my sins for me a long way, and I am sorry. I am sorry. I am sorry.*

The last line was written in a shakier hand than the rest, dated five years ago, three weeks before his heart gave out on the dock at Marrow Bend with Yara holding his head and telling him, stupidly, uselessly, that the doctor was coming.

She did not cry. She wanted to, in the ordinary way, the way grief usually arrived—a wave, a sobbing, a clearing. But this was not that. This was the other thing, the deep-water thing, where the pressure simply increased until everything inside you reorganized itself around it. Her father had betrayed a nation. Her father had spent thirty years paying for it, coin by coin, run by run, in secret, while raising his daughter on the same river he had helped drown. Both things were true. They sat together on the page like two boats sharing an anchor, and neither one canceled the other out.

She thought about the chest, waterlogged and sealed, riding the spring flood straight to her nets. She had thought it chance. She had thought it fate. Now she understood it was neither—it was design, patient and desperate, a dying man trusting the only courier he had ever trusted fully: the current itself. And it had worked. It had worked because she had hauled in the net, because she had said yes to a stranger in a gray coat, because she had run the side-channel at Karst Reach and the Nine Sisters and given her boat's hull to the white water. The river had chosen her, or she had chosen the river; after thirty years of watching them argue, she no longer saw much difference.

"You've been reading a long time."

Sef stood in the doorway, wrapped in a borrowed wool blanket, her hair drying in dark waves. The heir of Vessia, the spy who had defected, the woman Yara had nearly thrown off her own boat four hundred miles downstream—all of it seemed very far away now, another life, another river. Sef crossed the room and looked down at the open pages without touching them. "That's his hand?"

"Yes."

"May I?"

Yara nodded. Sef read for a long time, standing, one finger resting lightly on the edge of the table. When she reached the end she closed the folder gently, the way you close a coffin lid.

"The elders will want this," she said. "When they learn what's in it."

"I know."

"There are people in this valley who lost family to the dam. Who lost *everything*. Names, houses, graves under sixty feet of water." Sef's voice was even, careful, the voice of someone choosing each word the way you choose footing on scree. "A confession like this—they'll call it justice. Proof. Something to answer for."

"He's dead," Yara said. "He answered for it for thirty years and then he died, and the only person left to punish is a memory." She heard the sharpness in her own voice and didn't take it back. "I'm not asking you to protect him. I'm asking—" She stopped. Started again. "I don't know what I'm asking yet. Ask me tomorrow."

Sef studied her for a moment, then pulled a stool over and sat, uninvited, which by now Yara recognized as a kindness. "Tomorrow," Sef agreed. "But tonight, tell me about him. Not the crime. Him."

So Yara told her. About a man who could read water the way scholars read scripture, who taught her to feel a sandbar shift through the deck plates before any sounding line confirmed it. About a man who never once raised his voice but could silence a crew with a look. About a man who kept a tin box under his bunk and put coins in it every payday and never told her what it was for, and how after he died she'd found it empty except for a scrap of paper that said *paid in full*, and how she'd laughed, thinking it was some old joke about a debt to a friend. She wasn't laughing now. The tin box had been the beginning of the restitution. Thirty years of paydays, dropped anonymously into exile communities all down the river, and she had held the evidence of it in her hands and called it nothing.

"He was a good man," Yara said finally, "who did an unforgivable thing, and then forgave himself just enough to keep living, and spent everything he had trying to make it weigh less. And it doesn't weigh less. That's the joke. All of it—all the runs, the coin, the chest, the treaty, this—" she gestured at the valley beyond the walls, at the water singing in the dark, "—and it doesn't weigh one ounce less than the day he wrote the first page. Because the drowned stay drowned."

"Yes," Sef said softly. "They do."

"But the living don't have to."

Sef looked at her.

"Crane's men will be back," Yara said. "Not tomorrow, maybe, but soon, and in greater number, and next time they won't come up the gorge like tourists. And when they do, the elders will want a reason, a grievance, a fire to light under every rifle in this valley. Your confession gives them one." She tapped the gray folder. "*His* confession. Betrayal demands reprisal. Reprisal demands blood. And this valley has bled enough for one century."

"What are you proposing?"

"I don't know yet." She laughed, short and hollow. "I'm a river pilot. I know how to read a channel and pick a line. This isn't a channel."

"It's exactly a channel," Sef said. "You just haven't sounded it yet."

They sat together while the lamp burned low. At some point Yara picked up the folder again and read the middle sections she'd skimmed past—the lists of payments, the routes, the names of the scattered families he'd found and helped, dozens of them, entries stretching across decades in her father's increasingly cramped hand. One entry made her stop breathing for a moment: a payment recorded nineteen years ago to a woman in Vell Hollow, *for the child's winter coat and the school fee*, initialed T.W. Nineteen years ago. Sef would have been a small girl then, hidden in the hills below the dam, learning to hate the consortium and never knowing that a riverboat captain's quiet coins had helped keep her fed.

She didn't show Sef the entry. Some weights a person should be allowed to set down privately, if only for a night.

Near dawn, footsteps on the porch—an elder named Maren, silver-braided and sharp-eyed, come to say that the prisoners had given up Crane's fallback position at the dam works, and that scouts reported the consortium regrouping, and that the council would convene at midday to decide what was to be done about the traitor's papers. *Traitor's papers.* Maren said it neutrally, but the word landed in the room like a stone in still water.

"They know what's in the folder?" Yara asked.

"They know a folder exists. They know whose it is." Maren's gaze moved between the two women. "The council will want it read aloud. All of it. There are families here owed the truth."

"And there are families here owed a future," Yara said, "and I'm not sure you get both from the same reading."

Maren's expression didn't change, but something behind her eyes shifted, recalculated. "Midday," she repeated, and left.

Yara stood alone in the graying light with the folder in her hands. Down in the valley the water never stopped talking—millrace, aqueduct, spillway, the whole circulatory system of a nation that refused to die—and she listened to it the way she'd listened to it her whole life, and slowly, the way a channel reveals itself to a patient eye, she began to see her line.

Her father had written the truth down so that someone after him would know it was done. Fine. Someone after him knew. She knew. Sef knew. The knowledge existed now, lodged in living minds, and it would keep, and it would weigh what it weighed. But a public reading—before grieving families, before a council arming itself for war—wouldn't be truth anymore. It would be fuel. Her father had spent thirty years converting his sin into restitution, drop by drop, and she would not stand by and watch one morning's ceremony convert it all back into poison.

There would be a cost. Maren would call it concealment. Half the valley might call it betrayal—the daughter protecting the traitor, history repeating itself in the worst way. She might lose whatever standing she'd earned here, whatever trust Sef's acknowledgment had bought her. She might lose the friendship of people she'd bled beside not twelve hours ago.

She turned the folder over in her hands. Gray oilcloth, waxed cord, her father's careful knots. He had tied it shut intending it to be opened. She could feel that intention in the binding, patient as everything else he'd done.

But he had also written, in the last shaky entry, *to the second sort I can only say: I am sorry*—and Yara understood, with the certainty she felt only on the water, that the sorry was meant for the living, not the ledger. That the confession had done its work the moment one person read it whole. That a penance completed in secret need not be performed in public to count.

Outside, the eastern sky was turning the color of pewter, and down at the gorge mouth, faint on the wind, came the sound she'd been dreading and expecting: engines. More than one. Crane hadn't retreated; he'd regrouped, and he was coming back up the river with everything he had, and the council would convene at midday whether the world was ending or not.

Yara tucked the folder inside her coat, against her ribs, where her heart could keep an eye on it, and went to find Sef.

They had a battle to plan, and a truth to decide the fate of, and not nearly enough hours to do either—and somewhere below the gorge, closing fast, came the sound of the consortium coming to collect its debts.
