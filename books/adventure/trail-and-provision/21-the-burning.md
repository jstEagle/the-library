## 21. The Burning

The valley smelled of woodsmoke and wet stone, and on the morning of the signing it smelled of bread too—someone had decided that a nation reborn deserved to break loaves together, and the ovens of Vell Hollow had been going since before dawn. Yara stood at the edge of the terraced field where the assembly had gathered, her hands still smelling of river mud from the night's work, and watched four hundred people arrange themselves into something that had not existed for a hundred years: a public.

They came down from the high pastures and out of the cedar houses and up from the fish weirs, old men leaning on granddaughters' arms, children hoisted onto shoulders so they could see over the crowd. They wore their best, which in a hidden valley meant their oldest—wool dyed in colors that had gone soft with decades of washing, embroidery worked by hands that were now dust. A woman near the front held a folded flag that Yara recognized from barroom songs and nothing else: the white stag of Vessia on a field of blue gone gray as old ice.

Sef stood on the low stone platform at the field's center, and she was not wearing her best. She was wearing the same travel-worn coat she'd boarded the *Meridian* in, sleeves rolled, wrists bare—the left one where the consortium mark had been cut away and cauterized in Vell Hollow, the scar still angry and new. The elders had offered her robes. She'd refused them, Yara had heard, with a single sentence: "I'm not going to stand in front of these people dressed as something I spent six years pretending not to be."

The treaty lay on a table before her under a cloth, and beside it, wrapped in oilskin, the confession.

Yara had not slept. None of them had, really—not since Crane's mercenaries had been driven back into the gorge by the valley's own water, not since the prisoners had been bound and the dead counted on both sides. But Sef had slept an hour, Yara knew, sitting upright against the wall of the elder house with her hand on the treaty like a woman guarding a child. And Elias had not slept at all. He stood at the platform's edge now, gray coat brushed clean, hair tied back, looking like what he claimed to be—an archivist—and Yara found herself studying him the way she studied weather. Looking for the change that would tell her what was coming.

He caught her eye. He did not smile. That, more than anything, told her.

* * *

The eldest of the elders was a woman named Maren Ostrova, ninety if she was a day, with a voice like a hinge that had been oiled but never replaced. She opened the assembly in Old Vessian, and even though Yara understood perhaps one word in ten, the sound of it moved through the crowd like current through water—heads bowing, lips moving, a child somewhere beginning to cry and being hushed not with irritation but with tenderness, as if even crying today belonged to the ceremony.

Then Maren switched to river-tongue, the common language, and said: "We will hear the treaty."

Elias unwrapped it. Yara had seen the document a dozen times by now—had carried it, defended it, nearly drowned for it—but watching it unrolled before four hundred descendants of the drowned, she understood for the first time what it actually weighed. The vellum was stained with floodwater. The seals along its lower edge, seven of them, had been pressed a century ago by men whose grandchildren's grandchildren stood in this field. It named the source valley. It granted the heirs of Vessia dominion over the headwaters, in perpetuity, against all claims—and the consortium's dam charters, every one of them, were built on claims that this document voided.

Maren read the granting clauses aloud, slowly, and the crowd made sounds Yara had never heard a crowd make—not cheering, not weeping, but a low continuous hum, like a river under ice, like something vast remembering how to move.

When she finished, she turned to Sef. "And the heir acknowledges it?"

"I acknowledge it." Sef's voice carried without effort. "Not because a paper tells me who I am. Because these people are mine, and I am theirs, and the water is ours. That's the whole of it."

The hum swelled. Somewhere a man began to sing—one of the barroom songs, Yara realized, the ones sung in dockside taverns by people who didn't know they were true—and then others took it up, raggedly, and then all together, and Yara stood among them with her throat tight and her eyes burning and thought: *my father put this chest in the water. My father sent this here.* Whatever else he had done—

Whatever else he had done.

Maren raised her hand, and the singing died.

"There is one more matter," the old woman said, "before we seal the treaty and sit down to eat as a free people." Her gaze went to the table. To the oilskin bundle. "There are those among us—we do not say this with shame—who have asked how the consortium found us after a hundred years of not finding us. There are those who have lost children in the last three days. And there is a document in our keeping that answers both questions. We have read it. We believe this assembly has the right to hear it."

Yara felt the ground tilt, though her feet did not move.

She looked at Elias. He was already looking at her, and his expression was not triumph or grief but something worse: apology. He had known. Of course he had known—he'd helped the elders unwrap the captured papers, catalogued everything Crane's men had carried, and he had known what was in the last folder and had said nothing to her, because he believed, in his careful archivist's heart, that the truth belonged to the archive, and the archive belonged to the people, and the people had paid for it in blood.

"The confession of Tomas Whitlock," Maren said, "courier of the consortium, betrayer of the source road, who sold the location of this valley to Halvard Crane thirty-one years ago—and who spent the rest of his life carrying restitution upriver until the river took him. Read it aloud. Let every ear hear what was done, and let justice be decided in the open, as a free people decide it."

Four hundred faces turned toward the table.

And toward Yara.

Because everyone in this valley knew whose boat had brought the chest. Everyone knew the courier's daughter stood among them, mud to the knees, having fought on the walls two nights ago with a boathook and a pistol. And Yara saw the question move through the crowd like wind through wheat—not hostility, not yet, but the terrible neutral curiosity of people who have suffered and want to know why.

Her father's name hung in the air over the field.

* * *

Later—much later, in the years when she told this story, which was rarely and only to people who had earned it—Yara could never fully reconstruct the next few minutes. She remembered pieces. The weight of her own boots on the grass. The way the oilskin crackled under her fingers. Sef's face, turning toward her with dawning understanding, and the small sharp motion of Sef's hand—*don't*—that Yara ignored.

She walked to the platform. No one stopped her. Perhaps no one thought to; she had delivered the chest, and delivery entitled a courier to approach the documents. Or perhaps Maren Ostrova, who was older than the consortium itself and had buried more of Vessia than anyone living, saw something in Yara's face and chose—for three seconds, five—to let events unfold.

Yara picked up the bundle.

"It's mine to carry," she said, and her voice surprised her by holding. "I've carried it since the flood gave it back. Every mile upstream. Through the locks and the rapids and the mutiny and the gorge. I carried it because I thought it was my father's gift to you." She looked out at the faces. An old man with a cedar cane. A girl on her father's shoulders, maybe eight years old, watching with the absolute seriousness of children at ceremonies. "I was wrong about what it was. I've had time to learn how wrong."

"Captain Whitlock." One of the younger elders, a broad-shouldered man with a soldier's bearing—Doran, he was called, who had lost a nephew at the gates. His voice was not cruel. It was tired. "No one blames you for your father. But the assembly has a right—"

"The assembly has a right to the truth," Yara said. "So hear it. All of it, right now, from me, because I'm the only person alive who read every page."

She had read it twice. Once in the elder house by lamplight, alone, with her hands shaking. Once again at dawn, because the first time she had been reading it as a daughter, and the second time she forced herself to read it as a captain—as evidence, as cargo, as a thing to be assessed rather than survived.

"He betrayed you," she said. "Thirty-one years ago, when he was nineteen years old and broke and furious at a world that had erased the country his mother sang to him about. He met a consortium surveyor in a dockside tavern and told him about an old trade road into the mountains, and the surveyor paid him twenty crowns, and the road led close enough to this valley that Crane's men spent eleven years searching outward from it. They never found you. But they burned four villages trying, and those villages died believing someone had sold them, because someone had."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

"He learned what he'd done. Not quickly—he wasn't noble, he was a coward, and cowards take years to look at what they've broken. But he learned. And then he spent thirty years doing the only thing he could think of: he became a courier. For both sides. He moved consortium papers when they needed moving, and he moved Vessian papers when they needed moving, and he saved money in accounts under false names, and he collected documents—treaties, proofs, records of what Crane did to drown your country deliberately, with the dams and the floodgates and the maps burned so no one would ever ask where the water went. He built this chest over decades. And when he knew he was dying, he sealed it and gave it to the river, because he trusted the river to find you better than he trusted himself to face you."

Yara's hands were steady on the oilskin. Her voice was not, quite, but it carried.

"That's the confession. That's what the elders want read aloud. And I want to tell you what else is in it, because I don't think they know—they've only skimmed it, hunting for names. The last third isn't guilt. It's restitution. Names of consortium agents. Locations of funds. Evidence that would hang Crane in any honest court. My father spent his last years building a weapon against the people he armed. And if you read this document aloud, in front of four hundred people who have just lost kin to Crane's guns, you will not use it as a weapon. You'll use it as permission. You'll read his name and you'll want a name to answer it, and the name you'll reach for is—" she gestured at the bound prisoners, at the burned houses at the valley's edge, at the fresh graves under the cedars— "revenge. Reprisals. Trials that end the same way trials always end when grief gets to write the charges."

"And if we do?" Doran again. But softly now. "What would you have us do with our dead, Captain? Sing to them?"

"No," Yara said. "Bury them. And then build something they'd have wanted to live in." She lifted the bundle higher. "You signed a treaty an hour ago that says the future belongs to you. This paper is about the past. It can't give you anything the treaty hasn't. It can only teach your children to hate a dead man instead of building the valley—which, I'll remind you, he tried to give you with his last breath, and which you now hold."

"You would spare him," someone called from the crowd. Not hostile. Genuinely asking.

"No," Yara said, and here her voice finally broke, and she let it. "I'd spare *you*. He doesn't need sparing. He's five years dead and he knew exactly what he was—I promise you, he counted the cost more carefully than any of us ever will. Burning this doesn't acquit him. Nothing acquits him. Burning this means my daughter—" she stopped, because she had no daughter, and the word that wanted to come out was *nobody*, and she corrected herself in front of all of them, honestly, "—means no child of yours ever learns to introduce themselves with an apology. Inherited guilt is just another dam. It holds back everything good too."

She looked at Sef.

Sef looked back at her for a long moment. Then she nodded once—slow, deliberate, the nod of a queen granting something, or of a friend forgiving something—and stepped aside from the table.

Yara took out her matches.

The valley was damp, spring-damp, cedar-damp, and the first match died. So did the second. On the third, the oilskin caught, and she held the burning bundle up so they could all see it—not hiding it, not hurrying it—letting the flame climb while the crowd watched in absolute stillness. The fire reached her fingers and she set the whole thing on the treaty table's stone surface and let it burn there, in full view, ash rising straight up in the windless air like something being returned.

"My father trusted the river with his penance," she said quietly, when the flames had eaten down to black lace. "I trust you with its absence. Build something worth the silence."

Nobody spoke. Nobody moved. The ash settled on the stone like gray snow.

And then Maren Ostrova, ninety years old, keeper of a nation's memory, leaned on her cane and said, in a voice that cracked across the field like a whip of pure relief:

"Well. Now we eat."

* * *

The bread was passed out and the singing started again and within an hour the field had transformed into the kind of gathering Yara had only ever witnessed from the outside—docked boats, lit windows, other people's belonging. Children ran between the tables. Doran sat with the prisoner detail and, Yara noticed, ate with them, sharing bread, which was its own kind of verdict: not mercy exactly, and not vengeance either, but custody. Justice deferred to courts that would exist once the valley dared to have them.

Sef found her at the edge of the field, where the terraces dropped away toward the fish weirs and the sound of the source river rose up constant and clean.

"You knew you were going to do it," Sef said. "Before you walked up there."

"I decided somewhere around the word 'confession.'" Yara turned a loaf in her hands without eating it. "I've been deciding it since Dead Man's Ledger, honestly. Since I first learned he took their money. Today I just finished deciding."

"It cost you." Sef's voice was gentle, and Yara hated and loved that she could see it. "That was the only proof of who he really was. The whole man. Now it's smoke."

"No." Yara shook her head. "The whole man is the chest he built and the daughter he raised and the thirty years of quiet work nobody ever thanked him for. The paper was just the worst hour of him, written down. I don't need to keep the worst hour." She finally tore the bread and handed half to Sef. "Besides. I kept the part that mattered. It's signed, and it's yours, and it's going to break Crane's monopoly from the mountains to the sea."

Sef took the bread. For a moment neither of them spoke, and the river filled the silence, and it was enough.

"Elias is leaving tonight," Sef said at last. "First light, actually. He asked me to tell you—he wants to say goodbye properly, but he's packing the archive, and you know how he is with the archive."

"I know how he is." Yara looked across the field to where the archivist stood among the elders, showing Maren something in a notebook, his gray coat catching the late light. A traitor or an heir or just a man who loved dead things too much to let them rest—she suspected she'd never settle it, and had stopped needing to. "Tell him I'll meet him at dawn. Tell him—" she paused, weighing it, then let it go with a small crooked smile, "—tell him I'll bring the boat around to the gorge mouth. If he's going downstream, he shouldn't walk."

Sef raised an eyebrow. "The *Meridian* sails tomorrow?"

"The *Meridian* sails tomorrow." Yara bit into her bread, and found she was hungry—truly hungry, the first time in weeks—and looked down the valley toward the gorge, where the river ran free at last toward the lowlands and the locks and the world that was about to change. "Crane's charter dies the day this treaty reaches a registry office. Somebody has to carry it. And I know the water."

Below the weirs, the source river gathered itself and poured westward, silver in the evening, going home.
