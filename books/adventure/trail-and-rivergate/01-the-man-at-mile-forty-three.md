## 1. The Man at Mile Forty-Three

The snow had been falling since Tuesday, which by Casper Halloran's reckoning made it Thursday's problem, and it was proving punctual.

He stood on the old grade at mile forty-three of the Kestrel Mountain branch, a line so thoroughly dead that even the maps had stopped mentioning it, and he squinted through the gray murk at a world that had been erased in white. The rails were still there under his boots—he could feel them through the packed snow, two iron threads running off into nothing—but everything above knee height had been surrendered to the weather. The mountains had taken the road back the way a tide takes a sandcastle: patiently, without malice, and completely.

"Beautiful country," Casper said to no one. "Shame about the murder."

That was a joke he told himself sometimes, out here alone. It was not entirely a joke. Five years ago this line had carried ore and men and, once, a bridge's worth of bad decisions down into Rivergate. Now it carried nothing but him and the wind, and he preferred it that way. A man who surveyed closed lines for a land company nobody had heard of was a man nobody looked for. That was the entire architecture of his life these days: small, cold, and structurally sound. He'd built it himself, plank by plank, and he was proud of it in the way a man is proud of a wall he never has to explain.

The theodolite was freezing up. He could tell by the way the tangent screw had gone stiff and sulky, resisting his fingers like a mule being asked to work past supper. He blew on his hands, flexed them, and gave the instrument one more reading—bearing 214 degrees, distance to the next stake roughly four hundred feet, visibility roughly four hundred inches—and then decided, with the wisdom of a man who had survived several winters by knowing when to quit, that the company could have its survey completed in spring.

He was packing the tripod when the dog barked.

Casper went very still. There were no dogs at mile forty-three. There were no dogs anywhere within thirty miles, unless you counted the wolves, and wolves did not bark; they announced themselves in other ways, usually too late for comment. This bark was high and cracked and came again, twice, from somewhere downslope near the old culvert, and it had the desperate, exhausted quality of an animal that had been doing this for a long time and was running out of reasons.

He should have walked away. He knew that even then, some honest corner of his mind filing the fact away like a survey note: *subject observed danger, subject elected to investigate anyway, subject is an idiot.* But the trouble with building your whole life around not being found is that you get very good at noticing things that shouldn't be there, and a dog barking in dead country was a thing that shouldn't be there, and Casper had never in his life managed to leave such a thing alone.

He left the tripod staked and followed the sound down through the pines, snow squeaking under his boots, breath hanging around his head like a thought he couldn't finish. The dog found him before he found it—a big rangy brute, part shepherd and part something hungrier, ribs showing through a coat crusted with ice. It barked at him, backed away three steps, barked again, then turned and trotted off a few yards and looked back over its shoulder with an expression Casper had seen on foremen and creditors alike: *well? Are you coming or are you useless?*

"I'm coming," Casper said. "But I want it noted I wasn't consulted."

* * *

The man was lying against the cut bank below the culvert, half in the lee of the stone, half in the open, as if he'd been trying to crawl somewhere and lost the argument halfway. His coat was good wool once, now stiff with frozen blood. One hand was pressed flat against his belly, and between his fingers, where they met the cloth, the blood was not frozen yet. That was the detail that told Casper everything he needed to know about timing. Fresh blood meant a living wound. A living wound in the belly meant hours, not days, and not many hours either.

The dog sat down beside the man and put its muzzle on its paws and watched Casper with eyes that had given up expecting anything.

"Hello," Casper said, crouching.

The man's eyes opened. They were brown and clear and furious, which surprised Casper more than anything else—the fury of them, burning steady in a face already gray as the snow. Not afraid. Angry. Angry at the dying itself, at the inconvenience of it, as though death were a railroad company that had missed a delivery date.

"You're the surveyor," the man said. His voice was gravel dragged uphill. "Saw your smoke. Two nights."

"You've been watching my camp?"

"Wanted to know who was walking my mountain." A wet cough took him, and Casper saw the red on his lips and revised his estimate downward. "Then they came. Three of them. Wanted what I wouldn't give."

Casper glanced at the man's side, at the dark bloom spreading through the wool. "They shot you."

"They shot me." The man said it the way another man might say *it rained.* "You got a stove?"

"A stove, a cot, and a tin of coffee older than the territory."

"Then you're rich," the man said, and laughed, and the laugh cost him something he couldn't afford, and Casper watched the price come due in the tightening of his jaw.

Now, here is the thing about Casper Halloran, and it is the thing he would want you to understand before you judge him: he knew exactly what was happening, and he knew exactly what it would cost, and every instinct he had honed over five careful years stood along the walls of his skull shouting *no.* A gut-shot stranger in dead country meant trouble with legs on it. Trouble that shot men didn't stop at shooting them. And Casper's entire fortune—such as it was—consisted of being a man whom trouble had no reason to visit. He paid his claims fees under a name that was almost his. He drank alone. He wrote letters to no one. When the land company asked about his history he gave them a history, smooth as ballast gravel, and they never dug because there was nothing glinting in the dirt to make them curious.

Helping this man meant smoke on the horizon. Meant questions. Meant, possibly, three men with rifles deciding that witnesses were also inventory to be disposed of.

"I can get you to my cabin," Casper heard himself say. "It's a mile. Can you walk?"

"With help," the man said. "And I'll need you to listen while we walk, because I don't think I'm going to be doing much sitting."

* * *

He weighed maybe a hundred and sixty pounds dry, and he was not dry. Getting him upright was like raising a felled tree that objected to the process. The dog circled them both, whining, until the man snapped "Bramble, quit it," in a voice that had clearly commanded dogs and crews for years, and the dog fell in at their flank like a soldier.

They moved slowly. The man's arm was over Casper's shoulders, and every twenty steps they stopped, and during the stops the man talked, because he had promised himself he would talk and he was keeping the promise the way he seemed to keep all his promises, which was stubbornly and regardless of expense.

"My name's Eben Marsh," he said, during the fourth stop. "I run a crew. Ran. Prospecting the high basin past the washout—silver, mostly, and don't let anyone tell you different, it's silver, good veins, we staked her proper." A pause for breath, ragged as a torn sleeve. "Six of us went in last summer. Six of us are still alive up there. Barely. We got snowed into holding over and our supply line got cut, and I came out to bring back help and provisions, and—" he gestured vaguely at his middle, "—this happened."

"Who shot you?"

"Men paid to shoot me. I won't insult us both by pretending I don't know whose money it was." His eyes came around and fixed on Casper, and there was that fury again, banked now, burning low and long like a coal seam. "Surveyor. What's your name?"

"Halloran."

"Just Halloran?"

"Just Halloran."

Something flickered across the dying man's face—not suspicion, quite. Recognition, maybe, of a fellow practitioner. A man who kept his name close was a man with a reason, and Eben Marsh, bleeding out against a stranger's shoulder, apparently decided he had no standing to object.

"Fair enough," he said. "Halloran. I've got a map."

"I don't want a map."

"Nobody wants a map. Maps are how trouble finds you." Another cough, longer this time. "I'm giving you mine anyway."

They walked. The mile stretched. Snow filled their tracks behind them as fast as they made them, which Casper found himself absurdly grateful for, as though the weather were complicit, as though the whole white world were leaning in to keep the secret a few hours longer.

* * *

The cabin was one room, iron stove, bunk, desk, and a window Casper had positioned to face the track rather than the view, because he liked to see anything coming. He got Marsh onto the bunk, got the stove fed, got water heating, and got out the bottle of rye he kept for medicinal purposes and celebrations, of which he had had none in three years, so the level was honest.

Marsh drank a little, held it down, and then pulled open his coat with hands that shook only slightly, which Casper understood to be a triumph of will over physics. From an inner pocket, wrapped in oilcloth tied with wire, he drew a package the size of a thick book.

"Now you listen," Marsh said. "Because I'm going to say this once, and then I'm going to start getting stupid, and after I get stupid nothing I say counts."

"I'm listening," Casper said, and hated how easily he said it.

"This map shows the closed lines. All of them. The Kestrel branch, the High Fork spur, the service road over the shoulder—they call it abandoned, it isn't, it's passable if you know where the trestles will hold and where they'll drop you into the gorge." His finger, when Casper unwrapped the oilcloth, traced lines inked over an old railroad survey, annotated in a cramped, certain hand. "My crew's camped past the washout at the old High Fork junction. Four months they've held. They're out of flour. They're eating pine bark and whatever Bramble catches, and Bramble's tired too." The dog lifted its head at its name. "There's a way in and a way out, and it's on this map, and I am the only other person alive who knows the map exists."

"Why are you giving it to me?"

"Because you're the only soul I've seen in two weeks who isn't carrying a gun or wearing a suit worth more than my outfit." Marsh's laugh was a dry rasp. "And because I watched you pack up your instruments today, and you did it neat, and you checked your stakes twice, and a man who checks his stakes twice is a man who finishes what he starts."

"That's a thin foundation for a dying wish."

"It's the only foundation anybody ever gets." Marsh leaned back against the wall. The color had left his face entirely now, and his voice was starting to lose its footing, sliding toward the soft edges. "Halloran. Here's the promise. You take the map. You go in past the washout. You bring my crew out alive. Six people. Ruth Okafor, she's second—engineer, better than me, tell her I said so and watch her argue. Then there's Dill, and the Ferro brothers, and a boy named Anselm who's seventeen and hasn't complained once, which worries me more than the flour situation." His eyes were closing between sentences now. "You bring them out. That's the price of the map. That's the price of the coffee and the mile and the shoulder. You bring them out alive."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll haunt you," Marsh said, with perfect seriousness. "I've got nothing but time coming."

Casper stood at his own stove in his own cabin, holding a dead man's map, and felt the careful architecture of his life develop its first crack. He thought about saying no. He rehearsed it. *I'm a surveyor, Marsh. I measure things. I don't rescue them.* It was a good speech. It was true, more or less, in the way his name was true—almost, mostly, close enough if nobody dug.

But he looked at the map, and he saw the annotations, and he recognized the hand of someone who had walked every foot of those lines and written down what he found so that others might live. And he looked at the man on his bunk, who had crawled a mile and a half through snow with a bullet in his gut not to save himself—there was no saving himself, and both of them knew it—but to deliver six strangers into the care of a seventh stranger he'd known for one hour, on the strength of a neat instrument case and a checked stake.

Some debts, Casper thought, you inherit whether you signed for them or not.

"I'll need supplies," he said. "Rope, medical kit, food for eight going in and eight coming out. That's a week's outfitting in Kestrel Falls, and Kestrel Falls has eyes."

"Buy slow. Buy scattered." Marsh's voice was thinner now, each word set down carefully like a foot on river ice. "And Halloran—one more thing. My crew didn't just find silver." His hand found Casper's wrist, grip astonishingly strong, a foreman's grip, a grip that had hauled men out of shafts. "They found papers. In the gorge, below the old bridge. Company papers. The kind that explain why the Rivergate Bridge fell, and why the town emptied, and why nobody was ever charged." His eyes opened fully, one last time, blazing. "The kind men get shot for. So you don't go in loud, and you don't trust any man in a good coat, and you get my people out before whoever bought those bullets comes looking for what they were bought to bury."

The fire ticked. Outside, the snow went on falling, patient and total, erasing the mile of their tracks, erasing the culvert, erasing, inch by inch, the whole white indifferent world.

"Eben," Casper said. "Who do I tell them sent me?"

Marsh smiled. It was a good smile, crooked and unafraid, the smile of a man who had audited his accounts and found them, if short, at least honest.

"Tell them I kept the promise first," he said. "Tell them I sent them an engineer."

And then, because the rye and the wound and the winter had finally finished their negotiation, Eben Marsh closed his eyes—not the way the dying close them, not yet, only the way the exhausted do—and slept, and Casper Halloran sat down at his desk with the oilcloth package in his hands and unrolled the map of closed lines by lamplight, and read, and read, and did not sleep at all.
