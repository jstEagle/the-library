## 2. A Map of Closed Lines

The map smelled of pine pitch, blood, and tobacco—the biography of a man in three ingredients.

I laid it out on my cabin table that night with the lamp turned low, the way you handle a thing you're not sure you want to be holding. Outside, the wind worked at the eaves like a debt collector who knew I was home. Inside, the stove ticked and settled, and the dead man's handwriting crawled across the paper in pencil gone soft with years of thumbing.

His name, as far as I ever learned it, was Hollis. He'd written it twice on the map's border—*H. HOLLIS* once in careful capitals, and again lower down, smaller, as if he'd forgotten he'd already signed it. Dying men repeat themselves. I've seen it before. The body starts letting go of things in reverse order: first the future, then the present, and what's left is a man standing in his own past saying his own name over and over like a knock on a door nobody answers anymore.

I want to be clear about something, because it matters to what came after: my first instinct was to burn it.

Not study it. Not trace its lines with my finger like some schoolboy dreaming of mountains. Burn it, fold the ashes into the stove, and go back to being nobody, which was a profession I had practiced for five years with considerable dedication. Nobody doesn't get visited by dying men. Nobody doesn't end up holding maps that smell like blood. Nobody eats his beans alone and sleeps like a stone and wakes up owing nothing to anyone.

I even got as far as opening the stove door.

Then I looked at the map one more time, and that was the end of me. Curiosity is a slow poison, but it works fast enough when the dose is right.

* * *

Here is what I saw, laid flat under lamplight on a table scarred by ten years of other men's boots and mugs:

The map covered perhaps forty miles of the northern range, drawn in a surveyor's hand—not Hollis's, or not only his. The base work was professional, inked, precise, the kind of drafting they teach you and either beat into you or don't. Over it, in pencil, someone had layered a second life: camps marked with X's, elevations noted in a cramped sideways script, dates, and arrows showing travel that ignored every trail the railroad had ever cut.

And the lines themselves—there were four of them, penciled heavy where the original ink ran faint—were lines I knew.

That's the part I haven't said yet. That's the part I sat with for an hour while the stove cooled and the wind kept up its collection efforts.

The Rivergate line. The upper spur. The maintenance road along the gorge. And here, crossing the river at a place marked only *WASHOUT*, the old trestle approach.

Officially, none of those existed anymore. Officially, the Rivergate Bridge had failed in the spring flood five years ago, taking eleven crewmen and most of a town's prosperity with it, and the company had done the sensible thing: closed the line, abandoned the spur, struck the whole watershed from the working maps and from the conversation. I'd read the report. Everyone had. It was a clean piece of writing, the kind engineers produce when they want a disaster to have a shape—act of God, unprecedented meltwater, scour beneath the north pier, no fault assignable. Clean as a fresh grave.

I know how reports like that get written, because I wrote one once. But that's a story for later, and maybe never.

The point is this: when a railroad closes a line, it doesn't just stop running trains. It stops existing. The bridges come off the ledgers. The grades come off the section maps. Men who worked the line learn to say they worked somewhere else. A closed line is a company's way of agreeing, formally and forever, that a thing never happened—and everyone who signs on to that agreement gets to keep their pension and their sleep.

Hollis's map disagreed with the agreement. His pencil lines ran straight through country the company had erased, and his notes treated those lines not as ghosts but as roads—passable, useful, *known*.

In the margin beside the washout crossing, in that cramped sideways hand, he'd written:

*Trestle gone but piers stand. Crossable with care. Camp beyond—see N. Fork fork. They will not come out for anyone less than a promise.*

Underlined twice. *A promise.*

He hadn't been talking about money. He'd been talking about me, though neither of us knew it yet when he pressed the paper into my hands with fingers already going cold. Bring my crew out alive. That was the promise. And now here was the rest of the sentence, in his own hand, telling me where the crew was and what it would cost to reach them.

I made coffee I didn't drink and told myself all the sensible things.

* * *

The sensible things went roughly as follows.

One: a dying prospector's map is worth exactly the trustworthiness of a dying prospector, which is to say unknown. Men in that condition have been known to trade fantasies for a warm coat and a sympathetic ear. Maybe there was no camp. Maybe the "crew" was two half-frozen claim-jumpers and a dog. Maybe the whole thing was a fever dream drawn in pencil.

Two: whatever was beyond that washout belonged to the past, and the past, in my experience, is a creditor best left unacknowledged. You don't answer its letters. You don't open its maps. You certainly don't walk forty miles into frozen mountains carrying its handwriting against your chest like a letter of introduction.

Three—and this was the argument I leaned on hardest, the load-bearing lie of the evening—I owed Hollis nothing. I'd given him water. I'd stayed while he died, which is more than the mountains usually provide. A stranger's last request is a weight, yes, but it isn't a contract. Promises extracted from the unwilling are just wishes with better posture.

I got all three arguments out in good order. I stacked them up neat as cordwood. And then I noticed what I was doing with my hands, which was tracing the old rail grade with one finger, mile by mile, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

Because here's the trouble with knowing a thing professionally: you can't unknow it on demand. I looked at Hollis's note—*piers stand, crossable with care*—and my mind did what it always does, what it had been trained across fifteen years and three railroads to do. It started calculating. Load paths. Frost heave in the pier footings after five winters unmaintained. Whether the washout had taken the abutment or just the span. Where a man could rig a line, where the snow would drift against the fill, where the grade would hold a sledge and where it would drop you into the gorge without so much as a by-your-leave.

I caught myself estimating the crossing. Actually estimating it, pencil in hand, before I'd decided anything at all.

That's the moment I should have paid attention to. When your hands commit before your head has held its vote, the election is over whether you admit it or not.

* * *

I slept badly, which surprised me, because I'd expected not to sleep at all. Instead I dropped into dreams full of water and timber, the gorge running brown and fast under a bridge that wasn't there anymore, and woke before dawn with the particular clarity that comes from having lost an argument overnight without hearing it concluded.

The map was still on the table. Of course it was. Maps don't leave. They wait.

I lit the lamp and did the unforgivable thing: I studied it properly.

The base survey was dated eleven years back—company work, pre-collapse, and beautifully done. Whoever drafted it had loved that country. Every bench and bend rendered with affection, contour lines flowing like watercolor. And the pencil overlay, Hollis's layer, tracked the seasons: small dates climbing the margins like rungs. *May—snowline at 6,200. June—N. Fork open. Sept.—camp moved above the slide.* Five years of a life lived in erased country, recorded in graphite.

Five years. The camp had been out there since the collapse. Since the bridge went down and the company closed the books, someone had stayed behind the washout and kept living, kept moving with the seasons, kept notes.

Why?

People stay in the wilderness for gold, for furs, for grief, or for hiding. Hollis had been prospecting—that much the pick marks on his hands confirmed—but prospectors follow color, and color doesn't hold a man in one drainage for five winters. Grief and hiding, then. Or both. Or something else entirely, something a dying man couldn't explain and a map couldn't carry.

Beside the camp notation, one more line, written smaller than the rest, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper:

*They found what the river kept. That is why no one comes for them.*

I read it four times. The lamp hissed. The wind gave up on the eaves and went to work on the chimney instead, and the smoke curled wrong in the cabin, and I sat very still with a dead man's sentence in front of me like a trap I'd already stepped in.

*What the river kept.* The river kept everything, that spring. Eleven men, a bridge, a town's future, and—according to the official record—nothing else. Nothing worth naming. Nothing worth closing four lines of railroad over and erasing from every map in the company's possession.

But companies don't erase things that are merely sad. Companies erase things that are dangerous. Sadness costs them a memorial plaque; danger costs them everything.

I'm not a conspiracy man. I want that on the record too. I've known too many cock-ups honestly made to believe every failure hides a villain, and most disasters are just arithmetic wearing a disguise. But I also know what a clean report smells like, because I've written one, and Hollis's sentence had the smell of a man who'd found the mess underneath the tidiness and understood what it would cost him to know it.

They found what the river kept. And the company, presumably, preferred the river keep it.

* * *

Morning came gray and businesslike. I did chores. There's always wood to split, and splitting wood is the great solvent of indecision—you can't argue with yourself properly while swinging an axe, and by the time the pile is stacked, the argument has usually starved.

It hadn't. By midmorning I'd rebuilt it twice, both sides, complete with citations. The prospector might have lied. The camp might be bones. The notes might mean nothing. Going meant forty miles of winter range, a crossing over a collapsed trestle, and—if there was anyone alive out there—the responsibility for their lives laid across my shoulders like wet canvas. Staying meant beans, solitude, and the slow comfortable rot of a man who has chosen to be no one.

I hated that I wanted to go. That's the honest center of it, the thing I'd dodge if I were telling this story to make myself look better. Some part of me—some part I'd fed on silence for five years—was starving for exactly this. A reason. A direction. A job with stakes. An engineer without a railroad is a strange animal, all knowledge and nowhere to put it, and I had spent those five empty years pretending the hunger wasn't there, the way you pretend the wind isn't cold by refusing to shiver.

Hollis hadn't chosen me because I happened along Mile Forty-Three. Looking at that map, I began to suspect he'd chosen me because I was the kind of man who stops at Mile Forty-Three—who walks closed lines in winter, alone, checking grades that no longer officially exist. He'd seen it in me before I'd admitted it myself. Dying men are economical. They don't waste their last promise on a man who'll break it.

*They will not come out for anyone less than a promise.*

Well. I had been promised at. That was nearly the same thing.

* * *

What finally decided it wasn't courage, and I resent how long it took me to see that. It was arithmetic.

I sat down that evening with the map and a fresh sheet of paper and did what I do: I planned. Distances, elevations, food weights, daylight hours. The numbers came together the way numbers do when the route is real—too neatly, too completely, the figures clicking into place like couplers. Forty-one miles to the washout by the old maintenance road. Three days' travel in decent weather, six in bad. The trestle crossing feasible if the piers stood, and Hollis said they stood, and Hollis had crossed them himself within the season, because his dates said so.

Six days out, six back, plus time at the camp. Provisions for twelve days, fourteen to be safe. My sled would carry it. My legs would manage it. The route itself was one I could have walked blindfolded in summer, and in winter it would demand everything I had—which, I noticed with something like embarrassment, was precisely the appeal.

The plan built itself, and a plan is a decision wearing work clothes. By the time I finished the provisioning list, going was no longer a question. It was an itinerary.

I allowed myself one last look at the stove, where the map had almost ended its career as fuel, and felt a laugh start somewhere under my ribs—dry, unwelcome, mine. Five years of careful nothing, undone by a sheet of paper that smelled like another man's blood.

There was a postscript I hadn't yet deciphered, squeezed vertically up the map's western edge where the paper had been folded soft with handling. I smoothed it under the lamp and read it through twice before its meaning landed:

*If I fail, find Vane. He pays for what's buried. Ask why.*

Vane. The name meant nothing to me then. Remember that. Whatever came later, whatever I chose or failed to choose, I walked into it ignorant, with nothing but a name, a promise, and a route through country that had been officially declared never to have existed.

I packed that night. Rice, flour, salt pork, tea, the small tin of morphine I keep for emergencies I hope never to define. Rope. The good axe. My father's compass, which points true and judges nothing.

At dawn I locked the cabin, which was a formality—no one comes out this far to steal from a nobody—and set my face north toward the washout, with a dead man's map folded against my chest.

Behind me the wind finally quit, as if it had only been waiting to see what I'd do.

Ahead, forty-one miles away, a camp of strangers was waiting on a promise. And somewhere between us, in the erased country along the closed lines, waited whatever the river had kept—and whoever wanted it kept.
