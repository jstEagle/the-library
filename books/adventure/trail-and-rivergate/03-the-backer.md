## 3. The Backer

Aldous Vane believed that a man's fortune could be measured in the things he never had to say aloud.

He had said very little for sixty-one years, and he had grown rich on the silence. Other men talked — at dinners, in boardrooms, over cards — and Aldous listened the way a sluice listens to water: patiently, without appetite, letting everything pass through while the gold settled at the bottom. It was remarkable, he often thought, how much gold a man could gather simply by declining to speak. Remarkable, and cheap. Words cost nothing to hear and everything to give away.

He was thinking about this now, standing at the window of his office above the Territorial Bank & Trust, watching a surveyor's mule pick its way down Front Street with the resigned air of an animal that had heard every argument there was to hear.

Below him, Rivergate lay spread out like a ledger waiting to be balanced.

Not that anyone called it Rivergate anymore. The maps said *unincorporated territory*. The county records said *dissolved, 18—, by act of the directors of the Cascade & Northern Railroad Company*. The people who had lived there said nothing at all, because they were gone — bought off, frightened off, or in eleven cases buried under new names in a cemetery two counties east, their headstones purchased by a charity that did not exist. Aldous knew this because he had paid for the headstones himself. He considered it a point of professional pride that no one had ever thought to thank him.

Five years since the bridge came down. Five years since the town went with it. And in five years, Aldous Vane had done what he always did with other men's catastrophes: he had bought them.

It had not been difficult. Catastrophe depressed prices. The claims around the gorge — timber rights, mineral rights, water rights, the sad little parcels of land where houses used to stand — had changed hands for pennies, sold by widows and creditors and men too broken to argue. He had acquired them through four different agents under three different names, so that if you drew his holdings on a map they would look like scattered accidents rather than a ring drawn around a secret. A ring around a grave, more precisely. But Aldous did not traffic in precision of that kind. Precision was for engineers.

Engineers. His jaw tightened at the word, which was inconvenient, because he would be needing one shortly.

* * *

The trouble — and Aldous Vane was a man who believed all troubles were simply profits wearing work clothes — had begun six weeks ago, when a prospector named Elias Marsh walked into a saloon in Corduroy Flats and started buying drinks he couldn't afford.

Aldous heard about it the way he heard about everything: late enough to miss the moment, early enough to fix it. A mining clerk, three drinks into his own evening, mentioned that old Marsh was back from the high country, talking strange, saying he'd found something in the gorge worth more than gold and that he meant to see it "put right" before he died. The clerk laughed when he said it. Aldous had not laughed. Aldous had sent a wire before the clerk finished his fourth drink.

Because Aldous knew what was in the gorge. He had put it there.

Not the ore — the ore was a fiction, or near enough. What was in the gorge was paper. Telegrams. Invoices. A set of inspection reports that had been altered, and the drafts that showed how. Correspondence between a director named Vane and a chief engineer whose name Aldous made a point of forgetting, discussing load limits and timber costs and the difference between what a bridge should carry and what a bridge must carry if the quarterly numbers were to hold. Eleven tons of rail stock over a span rated for seven. The arithmetic had been simple. The arithmetic had been someone else's job.

The engineer's name had been Halloran. That much Aldous permitted himself to remember, because Halloran had done the useful thing afterward: he had vanished. Disgraced, blamed, hunted by his own conscience if he had one — gone into the territories like smoke into wind. For five years Aldous had assumed the man was dead, and had found the assumption restful.

Then Elias Marsh went into the high country with a crew of nine and came back talking about putting things right, and Aldous's rest ended.

* * *

The report from his man in Corduroy Flats had arrived on a Tuesday, written in pencil on the back of a hotel bill.

*MARSH CAMP ATTACKED. DISPUTE OVER CLAIM OR PAPERS — UNCLEAR. MARSH SHOT, GUT WOUND, WALKED OUT ALONE TOWARD THE OLD SURVEY LINE. CREW REMAINED AT HIGH CAMP, NUMBERS UNCERTAIN, CONDITION UNCERTAIN. MARSH TALKED OF A MAP BEFORE HE LEFT. NO ONE HERE HAS SEEN IT.*

Aldous read it twice, then fed it to the stove and watched the pencil marks curl and go black.

So. Marsh had been attacked — by whom, Aldous could guess within a narrow margin. There were only so many parties interested in the gorge, and Aldous had been careful to make himself the least visible of them. Perhaps the attack had even been meant for him, in its way; violence was a language other men spoke when they grew impatient with patience. It didn't matter. What mattered was that somewhere between Corduroy Flats and the washout, a dying man was carrying a map that could hang him, and a crew of witnesses was sitting in the snow guarding papers that could hang him faster.

And somewhere on that same closed line, according to the railroad's own employment rolls — Aldous kept current copies, as a matter of habit — a surveyor named Casper Halloran was measuring culverts nobody would ever build.

Well. Thought Aldous, turning from the window. Providence, if it existed, had a sense of humor. He had never believed in it, but he had learned to bill for it regardless.

* * *

He received Halloran in his office on a Thursday morning, because Thursdays suggested routine, and routine suggested innocence.

The man who came up the stairs was taller than his file photograph and thinner, with the particular weathering of someone who had spent years outdoors deciding not to be recognized. He stood when invited to sit and then didn't sit, which Aldous noted and approved of; wary men were honest men, or at least expensive ones. His eyes went around the room once — the ledgers, the map case, the window — and then fixed on Aldous with the flat attention of a man counting exits.

"Mr. Vane," he said. His voice was low and unhurried. "Your letter said you wanted a route surveyed."

"I want several routes surveyed." Aldous gestured to a chair again, and this time the man took it, perching on its edge like a bird that hadn't decided against flight. "Please. Sit properly, Mr. — forgive me, your letter of application gave your name as..."

"Casper Nystrom."

"Nystrom." Aldous smiled the smile he kept for signatures. "Scandinavian."

"My mother's people."

"And your father's?"

"Railroaders," said Nystrom, in a tone that closed the subject like a door.

Aldous let the door stay shut. He had prepared this conversation the way he prepared all conversations: knowing more than he said, saying less than he knew. He opened the map case and drew out the territorial sheet, spreading it across the desk with both hands, an old man's deliberate slowness that invited the younger man to lean in. Nystrom leaned in. Good. Curiosity was a hook, and hooks worked best on men who believed themselves uncatchable.

"The Cascade & Northern built into these mountains twenty years ago," Aldous said, tapping the line as it climbed toward the gorge. "Timber, silver, coal. Then the collapse at Rivergate, five years back, and the company pulled out of everything above the washout. Abandoned eighty miles of good roadbed. Do you know why?"

"Bridges are expensive. So is maintenance at altitude. Most companies cut the line the moment the tonnage drops."

"Most companies," Aldous agreed warmly. "But I am not most companies, Mr. Nystrom. I am one man, and one man can be patient. I have spent five years acquiring claims along this corridor" — his finger drifted across the paper, touching the ring he had drawn without drawing it — "and I believe the line can be rebuilt profitably. Timber alone would pay for the rails. But before I spend a dollar, I want the ground looked at by someone who understands what he is looking at. Washouts. Slides. The condition of the trestles. Someone thorough."

Nystrom studied the map. Aldous watched him study it, and saw the thing he had hoped to see and dreaded seeing in equal measure: recognition. The man's eyes snagged on the gorge the way a boot snags on a root. Five years, and the place still had teeth.

"You know this stretch," Aldous said mildly. It was not quite a question.

"I've surveyed parts of it."

"Parts." Aldous folded his hands. "Then you'll know better than most what we're walking into. Which brings me to the second matter, and I'll be direct, because I find directness saves money." He paused, savoring the small irony. "I am given to understand that a prospector may be lost on that line. Elias Marsh. An old fool chasing a phantom claim, by all accounts, but he has family in the territory and the family has money and sentiment, and sentiment, Mr. Nystrom, occasionally pays for expeditions. If your survey should happen upon the man — or any survivors of his party — you are authorized to render assistance. Provision them. Bring them out. Whatever the situation requires."

Whatever the situation required. Aldous watched the phrase land. It landed softly; Nystrom's face gave up nothing, which told Aldous more than a face ever could. A man who showed you nothing had already decided what you were.

"The fee," Nystrom said.

"Three hundred dollars for the survey, payable on completion. Fifty now, for outfitting. Expenses covered. And—" Aldous slid an envelope across the desk, fat and unremarkable, "—a bonus of two hundred dollars for each member of Marsh's party brought out alive. Dead men are of no interest to me whatsoever, but living ones reflect well on everyone involved."

It was, he reflected later, almost true. Almost was the load-bearing word in nearly everything he said.

Nystrom took the envelope but didn't pocket it. He weighed it in his hand, looking at Aldous with those flat gray eyes, and for a moment Aldous felt the peculiar sensation — rare, unwelcome, almost nostalgic — of being surveyed himself. Measured for load. Rated for span.

"Why me?" Nystrom asked. "There are other surveyors."

"There are other surveyors," Aldous agreed, "who will take twice as long and ask twice as many questions. You come recommended as a man who works quietly and doesn't gossip. In my experience, quiet men find quiet things. And the mountains, Mr. Nystrom, are full of things that prefer not to be found." He smiled again, wider this time, letting a little warmth leak through, because warmth was the best mortar for a lie. "Besides. You knew the line. Who better to walk it?"

Something moved behind Nystrom's eyes — a door opening onto a dark room and closing again before the light reached it. "When do you want me to start?"

"As soon as you can provision. Within the week, if possible. Spring won't wait and neither, I suspect, will the weather." Aldous rose, extending his hand, and Nystrom rose to meet it. The grip was dry and strong and brief. "My nephew Thomas will accompany you. He's eager, a little green, and entirely my responsibility, so I'll thank you to keep him alive. He'll handle the accounts."

"He'll handle the accounts," Nystrom repeated, without inflection.

"He's family," said Aldous, as though that explained anything, because it explained everything, and because explanations were the one currency he never spent at full value.

At the door, Nystrom paused. "One question, Mr. Vane. The Marsh family. Who exactly are they? I'd like to know who I'm working for."

"A widow in Denver," Aldous said smoothly. "Mrs. Marsh. She wants her husband's people home. Don't we all."

The stairwell swallowed the surveyor's footsteps, slow and even, a man thinking hard with his boots. Aldous returned to the window and watched him emerge onto Front Street, pause, look up once at the bank building — at the window, Aldous was nearly certain — and then walk away toward the outfitters with the envelope still un-pocketed in his hand.

* * *

That afternoon, Aldous wrote two letters.

The first was to his attorney in the territorial capital, instructing him to prepare certain documents relating to a warrant — dormant, dusty, five years old — concerning one C. J. Halloran, formerly of the Cascade & Northern, wanted in connection with falsification of inspection records and, by extension, the deaths of eleven persons at Rivergate. *Hold in readiness,* Aldous wrote. *May require prompt service.* A warrant was a tool like any other, and tools kept best oiled.

The second letter was shorter, and he burned it after reading it aloud once to the empty room, which was his habit with letters that must never exist:

*TOM — YOU WILL ACCOMPANY NYSTROM AND SEE THAT THE MAP COMES TO ME AND ONLY TO ME. IF THERE ARE PAPERS AT THE CAMP, THEY ARE TO BE DESTROYED, NOT READ. IF ANYTHING SHOULD HAPPEN TO THE GUIDES, IT WILL BE AN ACCIDENT OF THE MOUNTAINS, AND NO ONE WILL QUESTION IT WHO IS NOT ALIVE TO QUESTION IT. DO NOT LET YOUR FEELINGS INTERFERE. YOU HAVE NONE WORTH THE NAME.*

He hesitated over the last line, pen hovering. Then, because even a man who measured everything in silence was capable of one extravagance, he added:

*BE CAREFUL. YOUR UNCLE, A.V.*

He sealed it, rang for the boy, and sent it down to the telegraph office marked urgent.

Then Aldous Vane poured himself a measure of the good brandy — the bottle he kept behind the false ledger spines, because a man ought to hide something honestly — and stood at the window while the light went amber over the rooftops of a town that had forgotten it used to be called something else.

Three hundred dollars to buy a dead man's ghost. Two hundred a head for witnesses delivered breathing. It was, he admitted, an extravagant price for silence — but silence, properly secured, appreciated forever. Land could flood. Silver could play out. But a grave that stayed shut paid dividends until the end of time.

He raised the glass a half-inch toward the mountains, where the last light was dying on the shoulder of the gorge.

"To Mrs. Marsh of Denver," he said to the empty room, and drank to a woman who did not exist, for the health of a secret that could not be allowed to.

Below, on Front Street, the lamps came on one by one, and somewhere among them a tall surveyor was buying rope and hardtack and asking quiet questions of the outfitter about the old line above the washout — questions that would reach Aldous by morning, because everything reached Aldous by morning, except, perhaps, the one thing he could not buy: the fact that Casper Nystrom had looked up at his window before walking away, and had looked, Aldous would think later, the way a man looks at a bridge he has already decided is going to fall.
