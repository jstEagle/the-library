## 4. Six Strangers and a Mule

They assembled in the rail yard at Dunmore Flats on a morning so cold the frost had climbed the fence posts like a slow white fire, and I stood there counting my troubles and finding that six strangers and a mule came to more trouble than I'd budgeted for.

Vane had told me to expect "a small party." Small, in Aldous Vane's arithmetic, apparently meant seven souls, forty days of provisions, a photographic apparatus the size of a church organ, and an animal named Biscuit who regarded me from beneath her lashes with the flat contempt of a creature who has already decided I am not to be trusted. She was right about that. Nobody in this outfit was to be trusted, least of all me.

"Mr. Halloran." The first of them came across the yard with his hand already extended, which told me he'd been raised in houses where hands were extended as a matter of course. Young, maybe twenty-four, with his uncle's same long jaw but none of the frost behind the eyes — or at least not yet. "Tom Vane. My uncle said you'd be leading us."

"Your uncle said a lot of things," I said, and shook the hand anyway. It was warm. That was something.

"He speaks highly of you."

"He pays me," I said. "That's a different kind of speaking."

Tom laughed — a real laugh, quick and unguarded, gone almost before it arrived — and I made a note against my better judgment: *might be salvageable.* Then I made a second note: *nephews who laugh easily are usually the ones carrying the heaviest orders.* I'd been somebody's instrument once myself. I knew the weight by its shape.

The cook arrived next, or rather announced herself before she arrived, because I heard her before I saw her: a voice like gravel going downhill, directed at a freight handler who had set down one of her crates with what she judged to be insufficient tenderness.

"You want the beans powdered? You want them ground to dust and blown across three counties? Then by all means, son, treat them like cordwood."

She was fifty if she was a day, built low and wide like a bridge abutment, with forearms that suggested she'd done more than cook in her years and eyes that suggested she'd enjoyed it. She planted herself in front of me, looked me up and down once, and delivered her verdict without ceremony.

"You're the engineer."

"Casper Halloran."

"Ida Brandt. I cook. I don't sing, I don't make conversation before coffee, and I don't abide men who whistle before sunup." She stuck out a hand like a knuckle sandwich and I took it. "Your uncle's man says you know the closed lines."

"Not his uncle," Tom said mildly.

"His money's uncle," Ida said, unbothered. "Same thing out here."

I liked her immediately, which frightened me a little, because liking people is how they get under your ribs, and I had spent five years keeping my ribs locked up tighter than a paymaster's safe.

* * *

The guides came together, which is to say they came arguing.

One was big and quiet, a Swede by way of somewhere further north than Sweden, named Halvard Lindqvist, who answered questions with shrugs calibrated to a precision I came to respect. The other was small and loud, a half-breed Cree-Frenchman called Baptiste Foye, who answered questions nobody had asked, at length, in two languages. They'd worked together eleven years, hated each other openly, and moved through country like a single animal with two heads. Within the first hour I understood that whatever else this expedition had going for it, those two were the difference between coming home and becoming a story other people told.

"The washout trail's gone since the melt," Baptiste informed the air generally, while loading Biscuit with the insolence of a man who had never been kicked by anything that mattered. "We go the high bench, add two days."

"We go the river path," Halvard said.

"The river path is mud to the knee."

"Mud dries. A broken ankle does not."

They looked at each other with the weary hatred of old married men, then both looked at me, because I was holding the map, and the map was the closest thing any of us had to an opinion that mattered.

"River path," I said. "But we rope the packs where the bank narrows past the old mile markers. If the melt's come down hard there'll be water standing in the cuts."

Baptiste threw up his hands as though I'd personally insulted his mother. Halvard gave me a nod worth ten of anyone else's approval, and that was that. I was learning the arithmetic of the trail: you don't need everyone to agree, you need the ones who matter to nod.

The last of us was the photographer, and he was late.

He came running down the platform road at half past eight with his coat flapping and his hat held on with one hand, apologizing to the yard at large, to Biscuit specifically, and finally to me, breathless, twenty-two years old at most, with ink stains on his fingers and glass plates packed around his body like armor.

"Elias Wren," he gasped. "Photographic survey. Mr. Vane engaged me to document the route. I'm terribly sorry — the train —"

"The train left at six," I said.

"Yes. Well. Yes."

He stood there panting, cheeks pink, looking for all the world like a boy who'd wandered off a Sunday school picnic into a war, and I felt the familiar itch of responsibility start up in a place I'd thought I'd cauterized. Somebody would have to keep this child alive. Apparently the somebody was me.

"You'll walk beside the mule," I told him. "Biscuit has better sense than either of us and she'll let you know when the footing turns bad. Listen to her."

"I don't — do mules talk?"

"They talk plenty," Ida said, shouldering past him with a crate. "Mostly they say you're a fool. Best listener you'll meet on this trip."

Elias blinked after her, then at me, then wrote something in a little notebook, because of course he did. A man who photographs everything also writes everything down. I made a mental note to find out sooner rather than later exactly what Vane had hired him to document — the country, or the company we kept.

* * *

We made four miles the first day, which was three fewer than I wanted and two more than Baptiste predicted, and by the time we camped in the lee of a cut bank I had learned the following:

Tom Vane worked harder than a rich man's nephew ought to. He carried double loads without being asked, pitched in on the ropes, and watched me the way an apprentice watches a journeyman — hungry for instruction, careful not to seem hungry. But twice that day I caught him checking our backtrail, and both times his face did something complicated and quickly put away. Men look back for two reasons: fear of pursuit, or guilt about cargo. We weren't pursued. So what was he carrying?

Ida cooked a stew that could have reconciled nations, and served it with the warmth of a customs officer. When Elias complimented it, she said, "Eat it, don't marry it." When Halvard asked for seconds, she gave him thirds without comment, which I understood was the highest decoration her kitchen awarded.

Halvard spoke perhaps thirty words a day and every one of them load-bearing.

Baptiste talked enough for the whole party and somehow, buried in the flood, told me things worth knowing: that the melt had come early and heavy; that trappers downstream had stopped working the gorge since winter; that there were stories about the closed line beyond the washout that even he didn't repeat around fires he intended to sleep beside.

And Elias Wren photographed everything. The cut banks. The mule. My hands, when I thought no one was watching, tracing the old mile markers along the grade. He was good, too — patient, exact, with an eye that found angles I wouldn't have seen in a year. Vane hadn't hired him for scenery. You don't pay a man that rate to photograph trees.

* * *

It was on the third morning, crossing a slide field below the high bench, that the friction found its edge.

The slide had left a fan of broken rock angling down toward the river, maybe eighty yards of it, the kind of ground that punishes hurry and forgives nothing. I went across first to test it, then waved them over one at a time, the way you do. Halvard crossed clean. Ida crossed with a sureness that surprised nobody who'd looked at her arms. Baptiste crossed talking, which should have killed him and didn't, on principle.

Then Tom slipped.

Not badly — a rock turned under his boot, he dropped, caught himself, slid six feet in a rattle of stone before stopping against a deadfall. Nothing broken. But the pack had shifted wrong, and he hung there on the steep part with the river loud below him, and I saw it happen: the moment of pure animal panic, the wide eyes, the frozen limbs. Every green hand knows that moment. The ones who live are the ones who get talked through it.

"Halloran—" His voice cracked on the second syllable.

"Don't move." I was already moving, down the fan on the angle, talking the whole way because talking is the rope. "You're fine. You're stopped. That deadfall's doing its job. Now give me your right hand — no, don't reach, wait until I've got you — there."

I got a grip on his wrist and walked him up the slope step by step, his boots finding the holds I called out, until we stood on solid ground and his legs remembered their trade. He sat down hard on a boulder and put his head between his knees, and the whole party pretended very carefully to be busy with straps and buckles while he shook.

"Thank you," he said, when he could.

"That's the job."

"No." He looked up at me, and there was something raw in his face that hadn't been there before, some door opened a crack by the fall. "My uncle said you were the best man on the mountain. I'm starting to think he undersold it."

*Your uncle,* I thought, *says many things to many people, and I'd give a month's wages to know what he said to you.*

What I said was: "Rope your pack shorter next time. A loose load kills more men than loose rock." And I walked back up the line, and behind me I heard him take a breath and stand up and shoulder his gear, and I understood that something had shifted between us — not trust, not yet, but the groundwork of it, the way frost prepares ground for planting whether you mean it to or not.

That night Ida handed me coffee without being asked and sat down across the fire, which she had not done before.

"You handled that well," she said.

"I've pulled greener men off worse slopes."

"Mm." She looked into the flames. Her face did something I recognized, because I'd seen it in my own reflection for five years — the look of a person deciding whether to open a box they'd nailed shut. "My brother worked the mountains," she said at last. "Rail crews. He liked to say the rock only kills the ones who forget it's there." A pause. "He'd have liked you."

"Where is he now?"

The fire popped. Ida turned her cup slowly, once, all the way around, and did not answer, and I did not ask again, because I knew the shape of that silence. It was the same silence I kept about my own name. Two people sitting at a fire, each guarding a grave, each recognizing the other's guard for what it was.

"Well," she said finally, standing, brushing off her skirts. "Somebody on this trip is carrying grief besides you, Halloran. Thought you ought to know you're not alone in it."

She went to her tent. I sat with the coffee a long while, watching the coals, feeling the strange and dangerous warmth of being *known*, even partially, even wrongly, and telling myself it didn't matter because in forty days these people would scatter and I would go back to being nobody, which was the whole point, which had always been the whole point.

* * *

On the fifth day we reached the mouth of the gorge, and the trail bent toward the washout, and everything changed.

Baptiste felt it first — he always felt things first, being mostly instinct wearing a man's clothes. He stopped in the middle of the path, head cocked, and said, quietly, in French, something I didn't catch, and Halvard's hand went to his rifle without a word passing between them.

"What is it?" Tom asked.

"Smoke," Halvard said. "Old smoke. Two days, maybe three."

I smelled it then too, faint under the pine: woodsmoke gone cold and sour, the smell of a fire someone had tried to hide. And beneath it, when the wind turned, something else — iron. Old iron, wet. The smell of a place where metal had been torn apart and left to rust in the weather.

We came around the last bend above the river path in the gray light of late afternoon, and there it was.

The trestle.

Or what the official reports called a collapsed trestle, casualty of spring flood, tragic failure of timber, act of God, amen. Five years of weather had softened it, but from where I stood, engineer's eyes narrowing against the light, I could see the piers rising out of the gorge below — and I could see that they had not failed.

They had been *cut*. Clean, deliberate, methodical cuts, the kind a crew makes with saws and patience, not the kind a river makes with fury. And scattered down the slope, half-swallowed by alder and slide, lay wreckage that no flood report had ever mentioned: a work train's tender. A caboose, burned to its iron skeleton. And smaller shapes among the trees that I refused, for one more hour, to name.

Nobody spoke. Even Baptiste had run out of words.

Behind me, I heard Elias Wren raise his camera, and stop, and lower it, as if even he understood that some things must be seen before they can be recorded.

"Mr. Halloran," Tom said at last, very quietly. "The report said the bridge fell."

"The report," I said, "said a great many things."

And far down in the gorge, where the river ran brown with snowmelt past the murdered piers, I saw — or thought I saw, at that distance, in that light — a thread of smoke rising thin and straight from the trees on the far side.

Someone was still alive down there. Someone had been waiting five years for us to look.
