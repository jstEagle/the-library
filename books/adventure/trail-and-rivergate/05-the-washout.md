## 5. The Washout

There is a particular silence that lives in country where the railroad used to be. I don't mean quiet — the mountains are never quiet; they mutter and crack and drip all day like an old man reading aloud to himself. I mean the absence of a sound you didn't know you'd been listening for your whole life. The rhythm of wheels on rail. Once you've spent twenty years hearing it in your sleep, its absence rings louder than a bell.

We'd been following that silence up the valley for two days.

The trail kept company with the old grade the whole way — sometimes riding atop it, smooth as a parlor floor, sometimes dropping into the cuts where the snow had drifted ten feet deep over ties that hadn't felt a wheel since before the war ended. Tom Vane complained about his boots. Ida complained about Tom complaining about his boots. The guides, Halloran's men — no relation, thank God, though I got asked twice — said nothing at all, which I was coming to understand was their highest form of praise. And young Merritt, the photographer, kept stopping every quarter mile to expose a plate of scenery that looked exactly like the last plate of scenery, until I began to suspect he was photographing time itself and simply couldn't help it.

Six strangers and a mule named President, because Ida said he had the same appetite for trouble and half the sense. We were not yet a party. We were a line of people walking in the same direction, which is a different animal entirely.

But somewhere past the second night, around Ida's fire, something had started to change. It happens on trails. Strangers stop being strangers not through any act of friendship but through the slow accumulation of small debts — someone shares their dry socks, someone else takes the worse watch without being asked, someone laughs at your joke when it wasn't funny, and by God that's worth more than money out here. I'd been careful not to incur any debts. Debts meant people. People meant questions. Questions were how a man named Casper Halloran got found out.

I mention this so you'll understand what I was feeling on the morning we came around the shoulder of the ridge and saw, three hundred feet below us in the gorge, the bones of the Rivergate trestle.

I was feeling nothing. That was the plan, anyway.

* * *

"Lord," Ida said beside me. She'd gone very still, her hands flat on her hips like she was bracing against a wind that wasn't blowing. "That's it, then."

"That's it," I said.

From up here it looked almost gentle. A gray thread of fallen timber laid across the white gorge, dwarfed by the walls rising on either side, the river running beneath it in a channel of green ice and black water. Five years of snowfall had buried most of it. You could almost pretend it was driftwood. You could almost pretend nobody died there.

Almost. Because even from this distance I could see what the snow couldn't cover: the near abutment, standing whole. The far abutment, standing whole. And between them, nothing. A bridge is a conversation between two banks, and this one had been interrupted mid-sentence.

"The report said the center span went down in the flood," Merritt said. He'd climbed up beside us with his camera already out, hungry as ever. "Ice jam took the piers. That's what the papers printed."

"The papers print lots of things," Ida said.

I said nothing. I was doing arithmetic instead, the kind engineers do without meaning to, the way other men count sheep. The height of the abutments. The span between them. The depth of the gorge. My mind was laying track across that gap whether I wanted it to or not, and every measurement came back wrong. Not impossible-wrong. Worse than that. Cheap-wrong.

"We'll descend on the north side," I said. "There's a switchback road the construction crews cut. It should still hold."

"And if it doesn't?" Tom asked.

"Then we'll invent a better one," I said, and started down.

* * *

The old construction road held, mostly, in the way that old things hold — grudgingly, with complaints. By early afternoon we stood on the riverbank beneath the ruin, craning our necks, and the gentleness was gone. Up close, the wreckage was enormous. Timbers as thick as men lay stacked and splintered along the bank like the aftermath of some argument between giants. Iron bolts the size of my forearm jutted from the snow. The cold coming off the river had a smell to it — stone and iron and old water — and underneath that, faintly, something organic and patient. Rot, maybe. Or just the memory of rot.

Merritt worked his camera in reverent silence. The guides spread out along the bank with the instinct of men who'd been paid to find things before. Ida stood at the edge of the debris field with her arms crossed tight, and I understood, without being told, that she was looking for a specific piece of timber among all that timber. A place where a specific man had stopped being alive. Some griefs need coordinates.

I should have stayed with her. Instead I did what I always do when a thing frightens me: I walked toward it.

The official report — I'd read it, everyone in the trade had read it, it had been reprinted in the engineering journals as a cautionary tale — held that the flood of '79 had driven an ice jam against the center piers, scouring their foundations until the spans above lost support and collapsed under the weight of two work trains and the crews riding them. Forty-one men. The report was thorough. It included drawings. It included the testimony of three surviving witnesses, none of whom, I now recalled, had actually seen the collapse itself — only heard it, from downstream, through the storm.

Drawings, I thought, climbing the first fallen span. They drew pictures of piers that weren't there anymore. Convenient.

The snow on the wreckage was crusted hard enough to bear my weight. I followed the spine of a fallen truss out over the ice-choked channel, boots crunching, breath smoking, until I stood above the spot where the first center pier should have been. Should have been. I looked down.

Where a pier's stump should have risen from the riverbed — broken, yes, toppled, but present, a foundation doesn't evaporate — there was instead a clean-edged hole in the ice, and below it, black water moving with terrible patience over stones.

I crouched. Chipped at the ice rim with my hatchet. And there, just below the surface, wrapped in five winters of river growth, I saw steel. Not timber. Steel caisson work, the kind the company had sunk for the original footings — and the top of it was not torn. It was not crushed. It was not scoured away by any ice jam God ever made.

It was cut. Sheared off clean, a few inches below the mudline, the way a man cuts a fence post rather than dig it out. One cut. Deliberate. Efficient. The kind of cut that saves money on dynamite and leaves no splinters to tell stories.

I sat back on my heels in the snow and let the cold climb up through me while my mind did the rest.

A pier doesn't fail clean. A pier fails ugly — cracks, sags, lists, groans, gives warning after warning before it goes. But a pier that's been severed below the waterline fails instantly, catastrophically, with no warning at all, taking whatever stands on it straight down. Two work trains. Forty-one men. Standing on a bridge whose legs had been sawed off in the dark by somebody who knew exactly what would happen and exactly who would be standing on it when it did.

And then a flood conveniently arrives to wash the evidence, and a report gets written by men who never waded out here, and the journals reprint it as a lesson about nature's treachery, and everybody agrees that the wilderness is cruel and the company is blameless, and forty-one families get a check and a closed file.

The river ran on beneath me, indifferent, keeping its mouth shut the way rivers do.

"Casper." Ida's voice, behind me. Close. She'd followed me out onto the span without my noticing, which should have worried me more than it did. "You've been sitting there five minutes looking like a man reading his own obituary. What is it?"

I could have lied. I'd built a life out of lying; I was good at it; lying was load-bearing masonry in the house of Casper Halloran. But there was something about Ida — the flat way she looked at you, like she'd already priced your excuses and found them wanting — that made lying feel like bringing counterfeit coin to a woman who minted the real thing.

"Come look," I said. "Carefully. The ice is rotten at the edges."

She came. She crouched where I crouched. She looked where I pointed, and I watched her face do what mine had done: nothing, then everything. Grief and arithmetic arriving together, which is a combination I wouldn't wish on anyone.

"That's caisson iron," she said quietly.

"It is."

"Piers don't shear off like that. Even in a flood. Even in—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "My brother wrote me a letter, six weeks before it happened. Said the bridge was fine. Said the company men had been up twice measuring things, and the crews were joking about it, saying they were being measured for a coffin. Joking, Casper." Her voice didn't break. It went harder, which was worse. "He thought it was funny."

"I'm sorry," I said, uselessly.

"Don't be sorry. Be exact." She turned to face me fully, and the wind off the river pulled at her gray-streaked hair, and her eyes were the driest things in that whole gorge. "Tell me what you see. Exactly. Like an engineer."

So I did. The cut. The angle. The depth below the mudline, chosen so no casual survey would find it. The report that blamed the ice. The witnesses placed downstream. I laid it out piece by piece the way I'd lay out a repair estimate, and I watched her take each piece and fit it into the shape of her brother's last six weeks, and when I finished she nodded once, slowly, like a judge accepting a plea.

"They killed him," she said. "Not the river. Them."

"I don't know that."

"You know it exactly. You just said it."

"I said the pier was cut. I didn't say who cut it or why or—"

"Casper." She put one weathered hand on my sleeve, and her grip was stronger than her age suggested. "I've cooked for railroad men for thirty years. I've heard them talk in kitchens the way they never talk in offices. Men don't cut piers for fun. Men cut piers because a bridge that fails costs less than a bridge that stands, and dead workmen cost less than living ones who might testify." She released my sleeve. "Now. Are you going to tell me why you really took this job? Because a surveyor doesn't sit on a murdered bridge looking like the ghost of Christmas future."

The question hung there in the cold. Behind us, faintly, I could hear Merritt's camera shutter click, click, click — the boy documenting everything, bless him and curse him. Somewhere upstream a raven made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling.

"I'm not a surveyor," I said finally. Which was true, and also the most dangerous sentence I'd spoken in five years, and I immediately regretted how much of a door it opened.

"No," Ida agreed. "I didn't think so."

"I'm a man who used to be something else. Who made a mistake that got written down in places I can't reach, and who has spent five years being nobody in towns too small to ask questions." I kept my eyes on the water. "That's all you get. That's the whole confession. There's a dying prospector's map in my pack and a promise in my pocket and neither of them is your business."

Ida considered this for a long moment. Then she snorted — actually snorted, the first almost-laugh I'd heard from her — and stood up, brushing snow from her knees.

"A map and a promise. Lord help us, that's how every disaster I've ever heard of got started." She offered me her hand and pulled me to my feet. "Fine. Keep your secrets. But hear me, Casper-whatever-your-real-name-is: if there's proof on this mountain of who cut that pier, I'm not leaving without it. You can be nobody all the way home for all I care. But those forty-one men are going to be *somebody* when this is done, or I'll haunt every one of you personally, and I cook, so believe me, I have methods."

It was, I realized later, the closest thing to a vow of loyalty anyone had offered me in five years. It came wrapped in a threat, from a woman I'd known nine days, standing on the grave of forty-one men. That's the wilderness for you. It strips a person down to the honest parts fast.

* * *

We found the second pier an hour before dark, and that's when dread stopped being a feeling and became a fact.

The guides located it — or rather located its absence — fifty yards downstream, another clean-cut stump of caisson iron hidden under shelf ice, identical in every particular. Two piers, both severed. Both below the survey line. Both invisible to any report written by men in warm offices. Merritt photographed them from four angles with frozen fingers and a look on his face that had stopped being hunger for scenery and become something older and grimmer.

Tom Vane, I noticed, had gone quiet. Not shocked-quiet. Calculating-quiet. He stood apart from the rest of us at the fire that evening, turning something over in his mind the way a man turns a coin he's deciding whether to spend, and twice I caught him looking north up the valley — up toward the camp beyond the washout, toward whatever the prospector's crew had been guarding for five years — with an expression I recognized because I'd worn it myself once, in another life, in a office where men discussed costs the way butchers discuss cuts.

He knew something. Or he suspected. Either way, when our eyes met across the fire he smiled at me, quick and easy, and the smile fit him like a borrowed coat.

I told myself it was nothing. I told myself a lot of things that night, lying awake under the ruined trestle with the river talking to itself below and forty-one men somewhere under the ice keeping their own counsel. I told myself the map in my pack led to survivors, not evidence. I told myself Aldous Vane had hired me for my knowledge of closed lines and nothing more. I told myself the wilderness was the only thing out here hiding bodies.

Then I remembered the cut piers, and the report, and the smile that fit like a borrowed coat, and I understood that I'd walked into this gorge believing the danger was behind us — the storm, the altitude, the cold — when in truth the most dangerous thing on this mountain had signed my pay voucher, and the second most dangerous thing was sleeping twenty feet away wearing his nephew's face.

Above us, the fallen bridge creaked in the wind, settling deeper into its grave. And far up the valley, where the map said the survivors were waiting, something — a signal fire, a lantern, a trick of starlight on snow — blinked once, twice, and went dark.

Nobody else seemed to see it.

I decided not to mention it either. Some promises, I was learning, you keep by knowing exactly when to shut up.
