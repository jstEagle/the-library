## 6. Ida's Kitchen

There is a particular kind of silence that settles over a camp when the fire is fed and nobody has a reason to speak. I had spent five years cultivating that silence in my own cabin, tending it like a crop, and I will tell you honestly that I resented finding it here, among six strangers and a mule, because it felt like something of mine being done better by other people.

We had made good distance that day. The trail out of the washout country climbed through lodgepole pine, the kind of forest that grows back after fire in straight, stubborn rows, as if the land itself were trying to look orderly for whoever came to inspect it. Tom Vane rode ahead pretending he wasn't checking on us. The guides—Hollis and Breck, two men who communicated mostly in grunts and hand signals—had found us water by mid-afternoon, which was more than my map had promised. The photographer, Elias Wren, took eleven pictures of a hawk and none of anything useful. And Ida cooked.

I want to be clear about what "cooked" meant in this context, because the word does not do the woman justice. We had left the railhead with flour, salt pork, beans, coffee, and a sack of dried apples that Hollis swore by. From these materials, most men would have produced glop. Ida produced dinner. There was a Dutch oven involved, and sourdough starter she kept in a crock wrapped in her bedroll like an infant, and there was a technique to her handling of the coals that I recognized, distantly, as the same attention I gave to a boiler gauge. She read heat the way I read pressure. Neither of us would have put it that way, but it was true.

By full dark we had eaten things I could not name and did not wish to question, and the others had rolled into their blankets. I stayed up, as I always did, because sleep and I had been estranged since Rivergate, and because somebody sensible ought to keep an eye on a fire in timber country.

Ida sat down across from me without asking.

She was a broad woman, fifty or so, with forearms that suggested she had wrung the necks of animals larger than herself, and gray hair pinned up with what appeared to be a fork. She poured herself coffee from the pot without offering me any, which I respected—it was understood the pot belonged to whoever was awake.

"You hold your cup wrong," she said.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Two fingers through the handle, thumb on top. Railroad man's grip. You've been holding it like a gentleman all week, but tonight you're tired, and tired men forget their manners." She sipped. "So. Which line?"

I looked at her. Firelight, pine knots popping, the mule stamping somewhere in the dark. "I don't know what you mean."

"Sure you do." She said it mildly, the way you'd tell a man his bootlace was dragging. "You walk like a railroad man, you sit like a railroad man, you looked at that collapsed trestle on Tuesday the way a doctor looks at a wound he didn't make but knows exactly how it got there. So which line did you run?"

The honest answer was *none, lately*, and the truer answer was *the one you're thinking of*, and I gave neither. "I survey," I said. "Closed lines. Abandoned grades. It pays poorly and asks nothing of anybody."

"Mm." She drank her coffee and let the silence do its work. Ida understood silence the way she understood coals—that it was not emptiness but a tool, and that if you held it steady over a thing long enough, the thing would open up on its own.

I lasted four minutes, which I counted as a personal failure.

"The Rivergate & Northern," I said. "Before."

She nodded slowly, unsurprised, and refilled both our cups, which was the first kindness anyone had shown me in longer than I cared to calculate. "My brother worked the Rivergate bridge crew," she said. "Oskar Lindqvist. Big Swede, hands like shovels, laughed at his own jokes before he got to them. He sent money home every month and letters every Sunday, and then in the spring five years ago the letters stopped, and the company sent a card instead. *Lost in the course of duty.* Four words. Cost them maybe a penny to print."

"I'm sorry," I said, and I was, and it was insufficient, and we both knew it.

"Don't be sorry yet. Hear the rest." She turned her cup in her hands. "They told us the bridge failed. Ice scour, they said, undermined the piers, whole span went down with eleven men on it. They paid out settlements—small ones, quick ones—and they closed the line, and everybody said what a tragedy, what a shame, and life went on. Except Oskar wrote me three days before it happened. Regular letter, nothing strange in it, except one line at the bottom. He said—" her voice did something small and careful, like a hand testing ice— "he said, *If anything happens to me, ask why the piers were poured light.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope a branch cracked under snow load, and neither of us moved.

"Poured light," I repeated. My mouth had gone dry in a way that had nothing to do with the altitude. Concrete work was my trade before it was anyone else's scandal. A pier poured light—shorted on aggregate, stretched with water and cheap filler—would pass inspection on paper and fail under load. It would fail exactly the way the official report described: suddenly, catastrophically, as an act of God or winter. Nobody would ever need to say the word *cut* aloud.

"That's what I thought too," Ida said, watching my face. "I wrote to the company. Twice. First time they sent the settlement papers again. Second time they didn't write back at all, and a man came around asking my neighbors whether I'd been drinking." Her jaw set. "So when Mr. Vane's advertisement went up—a cook wanted, expedition into the closed lines, wages triple the going rate—I signed my name and I packed my crock and I came along. Because somebody is finally paying to send people into that gorge, and I intend to find the pier my brother died on, and I intend to stand on it."

"And if what you find isn't ice scour?"

She looked at me across the fire with eyes that had already done their grieving and come out the other side into something harder and cleaner. "Then somebody poured my brother's grave on the cheap," she said, "and I'll want to know who signed off on it."

Here is the thing I had not planned to tell anyone, sitting in that circle of firelight with a woman whose grief wore the same coat as mine: I knew who signed off on inspection reports for the Rivergate & Northern. I had been one of them. And the report certifying those piers sound—the report that let eleven men walk out onto a structure built to fail—bore a signature I had spent five years trying to stop seeing when I closed my eyes.

I said none of this. What I said was, "The wreckage didn't match the report."

Ida set her cup down. "Go on."

"At the washout. The fallen span. Ice scour undermines a pier from below—you get settling, tipping, the span drops into the current more or less intact. What I saw was piers sheared clean at the base, and shear marks that ran horizontal, not diagonal. That's not water. That's tools." I heard my own voice go flat and professional, which was how I kept it from shaking. "I told myself I'd been away from the work too long. That five years rusts a man's eye."

"Has it?"

"No," I said. "It hasn't."

She exhaled through her nose, long and slow, and for a moment she looked less like a camp cook and more like what I suspected she had been before grief remade her—a woman who had run some establishment, some kitchen or boarding house, where things were kept in order and nonsense was not tolerated. "Eleven men," she said. "And a company that paid out quick and closed the books. And now a rich man buying up every claim within twenty miles of the gorge, sending his own nephew and a pack of hired strangers in to fetch out a prospector's map he swears he doesn't care about." She tapped the ground beside her with one finger. "You don't spend that kind of money on land you think is worthless, Mr. Halloran. And you don't cut piers and call it weather unless there's something under the bridge worth more than the men on it."

*Halloran.* I hadn't given her that name. None of them had asked; Vane's letter of engagement had, and I had never checked what name it bore. For one cold instant I wondered what else the letter contained, and what else Vane knew, and then I decided I was too tired and too far from anywhere to be paranoid, and poured more coffee instead.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked. "You've known me eight days."

"Because you're the only one in this outfit who looked at that trestle like a man reading his own handwriting." She gathered her cup and the pot and stood, brushing off her skirts with brisk efficiency, conversation clearly concluded on her terms. "And because you made a promise to a dying man to bring his crew home, and men who keep promises to strangers can be trusted with worse." She paused at the edge of the firelight. "Get some sleep, railroad man. Tomorrow's a climb."

* * *

Sleep declined the invitation, as usual, so I walked the perimeter instead, which was my habit and my penance both. The night was hard and clear, stars in the brutal abundance you only get away from towns, and I found myself doing arithmetic I had forbidden myself years ago: eleven men on the bridge, plus whatever crew the prospector had lost, plus a town evacuated under pretense—how many? Two hundred souls? Three?—all of it stacked against the signature on one inspection certificate, and the certificate against the man who had held the pen.

Me. The man holding the pen had been me.

I want to explain something about coercion, since history tends to flatten it into either cowardice or heroism, and it is usually neither. In the spring of my disgrace I was a junior engineer with debts and a sick mother and a superior named Aldous Vane's chief inspector who explained, over brandy, precisely what would appear in my file if the Rivergate piers failed their certification review. Not that I'd falsified anything—heaven no—but that irregularities had been *noted*, that my credentials bore certain questions, that a man in my position might find himself unemployable from here to the coast. And then he slid the draft report across the desk, already typed, already favorable, needing only my signature, and he talked about my mother's health in a voice full of concern.

I signed. Eleven men died. And the machinery that had squeezed the pen out of my hand was so well-oiled that no one ever even needed to threaten me again—they simply paid me off, closed the file, and let me disappear, which was the one thing I discovered I wanted more than air.

Until a gut-shot prospector pressed a map into my hands and asked me for a promise, and I, being a fool, gave it.

I stood at the edge of camp until the cold drove sense back into me, and then I banked the fire properly—which is to say Ida's way, ash raked over coals in a spiral, because she had corrected my method on day two and I had adopted it without comment—and I lay down and slept, for the first time in months, without dreaming of bridges.

* * *

Morning came gray and cold, with frost furring the tent canvas and the mule expressing opinions about breakfast. I was up first, or nearly—Ida was already at her stove, crock uncovered, starter breathing in the chill while she worked flour and water with hands that moved like they'd done this ten thousand times and intended to do it ten thousand more.

"Sit," she said, without looking up. "You'll want the first loaf hot. Climbing days run on bread."

I sat. Around us the camp stirred: Hollis checking hitches, Breck saying nothing eloquently, Elias Wren emerging from his tent looking like a question mark that had been slept on. Tom Vane appeared last, immaculate despite the wilderness, and surveyed the camp with the expression of a man auditing it.

"Miss Ida," Tom said, "is there any chance of eggs?"

"There's a chance of bread," Ida said, "and a chance you'll eat it and be grateful."

He blinked, decided—correctly—that this constituted a complete answer, and retreated to see to the mule. I caught Ida's eye and she permitted herself half a smile, the first I'd seen from her, and it transformed her face entirely, and I understood that Oskar Lindqvist had probably been a very happy man right up until the spring five years ago.

While the loaves baked we packed, and while we packed I found myself orbiting Ida's stove the way I'd once orbited a locomotive cab—not because I was needed, but because near it was where the order was. She handed me a burlap sack without explanation; I filled it with the pots. She pointed at the crock; I wrapped it in her bedroll, exactly as she'd shown me, snug as a child in a blanket. Neither of us remarked on any of this. Some partnerships are negotiated in words, and some are negotiated in bread and dishwater, and the second kind holds better.

"Your guides," she said quietly, while the others were out of earshot. "Hollis and Breck. They're Vane's men, same as the nephew. You noticed."

"I noticed."

"They take their orders at night, apart. I hear bits. Last night Hollis said something about 'once we're past the washout country' and Breck said 'the old man wants it handled quiet.'" She kneaded dough with terrible gentleness. "Handled quiet. Like a kitchen problem. Like a spill."

"Could be innocent," I said. "Expedition logistics."

"It could," she agreed. "And my brother's piers could have been scoured by ice." She shaped the loaf, scored it, opened the oven door on a breath of heat that smelled like civilization insisting on itself in the middle of nowhere. "Watch your back, Halloran. And watch mine. Between the two of us we might manage to watch everything worth watching."

I took the first loaf when it came out, because she insisted, and ate it standing in the frost with steam rising off it, and it was absurdly good, and I thought: *this is what I traded away, five years ago, when I chose invisibility over everything—this, exactly this, a hot loaf and a woman who watches your back and a morning with a purpose in it.*

I had just finished the crust when Hollis came down from the ridgeline at a pace that was almost running, which for Hollis was the equivalent of another man screaming.

"Trouble," he said, and pointed north, where the trail bent toward the high country. "Smoke. Ahead of us, off the old grade. Somebody's camped in the closed line—and they've got a signal fire going. Three puffs." He frowned. "That's a distress call, mister. Old rail code. Somebody out there wants to be found."

Ida looked at me. I looked at the smoke rising thin and deliberate against the gray sky, three columns, evenly spaced, the signature of someone who knew the old codes—who knew, in fact, the exact grammar of the closed lines.

Somebody beyond the washout was calling for help. And judging by the look on Hollis's face as he glanced back toward Tom Vane, somebody in our own party had already begun deciding what to do about it.
