## 7. Tom Vane's Ambition

My uncle Aldous Vane had a way of giving orders that made them sound like favors. He'd lean across the desk in his study — mahogany, imported, the kind of wood that had never seen weather it didn't pay for — and he'd say *Tom, my boy* in that voice like warm brandy, and by the time he finished talking you'd have agreed to something you'd have shot a stranger for suggesting.

I was twenty-six years old and I had agreed to theft, obstruction, and possibly manslaughter, all before breakfast on the day we left.

* * *

It had happened three nights before departure, after the guides were hired and the mules bought and Ida engaged to poison us all slowly with her cooking, which was apparently the best money could buy. My uncle dismissed the servants, poured two glasses of his good whiskey instead of the swill he kept for guests, and told me to sit down.

That alone should have warned me. Aldous Vane did not pour his good whiskey for anyone he intended to treat honestly.

"You've been idle too long," he said. "A young man needs purpose."

"I have purpose," I said. "You're sending me into the mountains with six strangers and a mule."

"Seven strangers," he corrected, "if you count Halloran, which I do." He turned the glass in his fingers without drinking. He did that — held liquor like a prop, never touched it when business was on. "The engineer is the reason for this expedition, Tom. Everything else is scenery."

"The dying prospector's map," I said. "You want it."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not surprise exactly. More like the satisfaction of a man whose dog has finally learned a trick. "I want what's in that camp beyond the washout. The map is simply how we find it. And once we've found it, certain materials will need to come back with us. Papers. Documents. Whatever the crew pulled out of that gorge."

"And if they won't hand them over?"

"They'll hand them over." He smiled, and I wished he hadn't. There are smiles that invite you in and smiles that close a door behind you, and my uncle's smile had always been the second kind. "But if persuasion fails, then yes, Tom. You will take them. Quietly. While everyone sleeps, while the guides are snoring off their rotgut, while Halloran is off being noble somewhere. You'll take the papers, and you'll bring them to me, and no one will ever know you had them."

"And if someone does know?"

He set the glass down at last, untouched, and looked at me the way he looked at ledgers. "Then someone will have to take the blame. That's what the guides are for."

I remember the fire crackling. I remember thinking, absurdly, of a hunting trip when I was twelve, when my uncle had shot a bird on the wing and made me fetch it out of the cold river because *a gentleman doesn't wade*. I had been fetching things out of cold water for him ever since. This was just a deeper river.

"Why me?" I asked, and hated how young my voice sounded. "Why not hire someone? You hire everyone else."

"Because hired men can be bought by the other side," he said, "and family cannot." Then, softer, almost kind, which was worse: "Because you're mine, Tom. You always have been. I'm not asking you to do anything I wouldn't trust to my own blood."

There it was. The whole architecture of my life in one sentence. Not *I trust you* but *you are mine*, said the way a man says it about a horse or a pistol or a deed of land. I took the whiskey he finally poured me and drank it and said nothing, and silence, in my experience, is how Vanes sign contracts.

* * *

Out on the trail, the mountains didn't care whose blood I was.

That was the thing nobody tells you about country like this — it flattens you. In the city I was somebody's nephew, somebody's name, a young man of prospects who'd never quite found one. Out here I was a pair of sore legs and a bad attitude, hauling a pack up switchbacks while the mule gave me looks of profound editorial criticism.

Casper Halloran rode ahead most days and said little. I studied him the way my uncle had taught me to study men — looking for the seam, the place where a person comes apart. He was harder to read than most. A man hiding something usually overplays the ordinary: laughs too easily, works too hard at being unremarkable. Halloran didn't perform ordinariness. He simply was it, thoroughly, the way a stone is a stone. But twice on the third day I caught him studying the terrain with an expression that wasn't ordinary at all. It was grief. Or guilt. With engineers, I was learning, those came in the same crate.

Ida cooked over a fire she built like she was punishing it. The photographer — Wren, a boy barely older than me with ink-stained fingers and a camera he treated like a sick relative — asked questions constantly, cheerful ones, oblivious ones, the kind that made everyone else flinch. *Why is this line closed? What happened at Rivergate, exactly? Has anyone ever been prosecuted?* I watched Halloran answer none of these and Ida answer them with her back turned, and I thought: my uncle would hate this boy. Which meant I liked him, in the grudging way you like anything that annoys your owner.

And the guides — Brannock and Reyes, two hard quiet men my uncle had hired through intermediaries so their names wouldn't appear anywhere near his correspondence — watched everything and reported nothing, at least not to me. Though I noticed Brannock watching me, too, with a flat professional patience, like a man keeping inventory of tools he might need later.

Tools. That was the word my uncle had used for them, in the letter he'd slipped me at the last relay station. *The guides are tools. Tools break. When they break, make sure they break away from us.* I'd burned the letter in the cook fire, and Ida had glanced at the burning paper and then at me, and said nothing, and I had understood that Ida missed nothing, and that this was going to be a problem.

* * *

On the fifth night, camped under a shelf of rock with the wind coming down off the peaks like something with intent, I couldn't sleep. I got up to check the mule — an excuse; the mule needed checking the way I needed a haircut — and found Halloran awake, sitting on a rail tie some previous crew had abandoned, looking east toward the washout we'd reach tomorrow.

"Can't sleep either?" he said. Not unfriendly. Not friendly. A door left ajar.

"My uncle snores like a landslide," I lied. My uncle wasn't here yet. He'd follow with his own party once we confirmed the route — that was the arrangement, though he hadn't told the others that part. "Besides, I keep thinking about what we're walking into."

Halloran considered me for a moment. Firelight moved on his face. "And what do you think that is?"

"A dead man's promise and a rich man's interest," I said, before I could stop myself. "Those don't usually end well together."

Something shifted in his expression — a flicker of respect, maybe, or recognition. One instrument acknowledging another. "No," he said quietly. "They don't." He stood, brushed off his coat. "Get some sleep, Mr. Vane. Tomorrow we see what the river left."

He walked back toward the fire, and I sat on the cold tie and understood that I had just been weighed and measured by a man who knew exactly what I was, even if he didn't yet know what I'd been sent to do.

The worst part — the part I kept turning over like a stone with something crawling under it — was that I couldn't decide whether I wanted to warn him.

* * *

Here is what I resented, when I let myself look at it straight, which was rare and usually at night: not the danger. Danger, out here, had a strange honesty to it. A cliff wants to kill you and makes no pretense otherwise. No — what I resented was the arithmetic. My uncle had done sums on every person in this expedition, and I had heard him do them. The guides were expendable and deniable. The photographer was a nuisance who might see too much. Ida was loyal to her own grief and therefore predictable. Halloran was a variable to be managed, bought, or buried, whichever came cheapest.

And I was the sum itself. The number the whole equation was supposed to produce. *Family cannot be bought,* he'd said, and he'd meant it as a compliment, and I had lain awake understanding that what he actually meant was: family has already been purchased, long ago, at a price so low I've forgotten paying it.

My father had died owing Aldous Vane money. That was the beginning of it. My mother had died owing him gratitude. And I — I had inherited the debt the way you inherit a farm or a feud, without signature or consent, and every year the interest compounded in small humiliations: the schooling he paid for, the position he arranged, the name he lent me whenever mine wasn't enough on its own. People said *Tom Vane* and meant *Aldous Vane's boy*, and I had stopped correcting them around the age of nineteen, which is the age at which a man either becomes himself or becomes an annex.

I had become an annex. A very well-dressed one, with a saddle of its own.

And yet. And yet I feared him more than I loved my own reflection, and fear, I have since learned, is the only inheritance some families leave you that keeps its value.

* * *

The morning we reached the washout, I woke before dawn with my uncle's instructions sitting in my chest like swallowed stones.

*Once the camp is reached.* Not before. He'd been specific. Let Halloran do the dangerous work of finding it, let the survivors reveal where they'd hidden whatever they'd hidden, and only then — in the dark, in the confusion, with the guides conveniently positioned to be blamed for any accident that might occur — was I to act. *Accidents,* my uncle had said, and tapped the table twice, a habit of his when a subject was finished. *Mountains are full of accidents, Tom. Loose rock. Bad rope. Men who walk too near edges.*

I had nodded. God help me, I had nodded.

Now I stood at the edge of our camp watching the light come up over the gorge, and I rehearsed the future the way you rehearse a lie until it feels almost true: I would steal from starving people. I would let two decent men hang for crimes I committed. I would hand my uncle whatever the dead had bled to preserve, and I would sleep afterward, because I always slept, because that was the Vane gift — a conscience with excellent insulation.

Wren stumbled out of his tent, hair wild, camera clutched to his chest. "Morning!" he said, with the obscene cheer of the very young. "Think today's the day?"

"Today we reach the washout," I said.

"And past it, the camp." He grinned at the mountains like they'd invited him. "Imagine what they've seen out there. Imagine what they *know*."

I imagined it. I imagined it far too well.

Down the trail, Halloran was already saddling up, and Ida was muttering at her pans, and Brannock was checking the loads with that inventory-keeper's eye, and somewhere beyond the ridge, four starving people were guarding papers worth killing for — papers my uncle had promised, in his warm brandy voice, that I would bring home.

I swung onto my horse and followed the others down toward the washout, and the terrible truth I carried with me, lighter than my bedroll and heavier than the mule's entire load, was this:

I still didn't know which way I'd jump when the moment came. And neither, I suspected, did the mountain care.
