## 8. Whiteout

The mountain gave us one day of grace, and we spent it arguing about whether the weather looked honest.

"Sky's clean as a whistle," said Hollis, the older of the two guides, squinting eastward where the peaks stood up sharp against a blue so deep it looked painted on.

"Sky's a liar," said Ida, without looking up from her pot. "That's not a clean sky. That's a sky holding its breath."

I kept my own counsel, which was becoming a habit with this outfit. The truth was I'd spent enough winters on survey crews to know that a sharp blue sky in high country in late autumn isn't a promise—it's a dare. The air had that pressed-flat quality, the silence that comes before the atmosphere remembers it owes the mountains a debt. I'd seen men bet their lives on bluer skies than that one and lose.

But we were behind schedule, and behind schedule is its own kind of weather. Vane's nephew Tom wanted miles. The photographer—Fenwick, a young man whose camera cost more than my cabin and whom he treated with the tenderness he showed no living creature—wanted light. And the guides wanted to please the man paying them, which is the oldest mistake in the mountains and the newest one every single season.

So we climbed.

* * *

The cliff face found us at midafternoon, a wall of granite the trail skirted on a shelf barely wide enough for the mule and her opinions. Below the shelf the ground fell away in a series of gray steps down to a frozen creek. Above it, the rock went up and up until it stopped mattering. It was, I'll grant, a magnificent place to die.

I was in the lead, reading the trail the way you read a page—looking for the sentence coming next—when the wind arrived.

Not gradually. Not a breeze that builds. It came around the corner of the mountain like a debt collector, and it brought the snow with it sideways, and inside of ten minutes the world was a white room with no walls and no floor. I've been in whiteouts on the prairie where you can at least lie flat and wait. This was different. This was a whiteout with geometry, a whiteout with a cliff in it, and every step was a question the mountain answered only once.

"Rope!" I shouted, and the word came back to me off the rock sounding like somebody else's voice, thinner, frightened. "Everyone on rope. Now. Hollis, you know this shelf?"

"Nobody knows this shelf in this!" Hollis hollered back, and I decided I liked him for the honesty.

We tied ourselves into a line—mule last, because she was the only member of the party with any sense, and even she was wide-eyed and unhappy. I put Ida behind me because she was the steadiest walker, Tom behind her because I wanted him where I could hear him swear, then Fenwick, then the guides. The younger guide was named Piet. He'd been quiet the whole trip, competent in the way of men who don't waste motion, and he was carrying the rope coil when the shelf betrayed us.

I heard it before I saw it—a sound like a door slamming somewhere below us. The shelf didn't collapse. It just ended. A section of it, maybe eight feet, had let go years ago or minutes ago, and under the white there was no way to tell which, and Piet, who had been walking at the tail to watch the mule, simply wasn't on the trail anymore.

The rope snapped taut and nearly took me with him. I went down on my knees on the ledge, wrapped the line twice around my forearm, and felt it burn through my coat as his weight came on. Ida was on the rope beside me before I'd finished falling, no word said, just her hands next to mine. Tom grabbed my belt. Fenwick, to his eternal credit, grabbed Tom.

"Hold!" I shouted, which was a stupid thing to shout, since holding was the entire plan and everyone was already doing it. But you shout something in those moments. Silence is worse.

We hauled. It took forever and it took no time at all. When Piet came up over the lip of the shelf he was white to the eyebrows and his left leg was bent in a place legs don't bend, and he made a sound I will hear for the rest of my life—a short, bitten-off sound, the sound of a man refusing to give the mountain the satisfaction.

"Leg's broke," he said, stating it like a survey note. "Below the knee. I can walk on it if I have to."

"You can't," I said, "and you won't. Not yet."

The storm, meanwhile, had settled in for a stay. You can hear it in the sound—the wind had stopped gusting and started simply being, a flat continuous roar with no rhythm to it. That's the sound of weather that has decided. We had maybe an hour of light, and a broken man, and a shelf that ended eight feet short of wherever it was going.

I looked at that gap in the trail, and I looked at the cliff above it, and I did the thing I had sworn off doing for five years, which was think like an engineer.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about hiding from your past: the past is a skill set, and it follows you like a dog.

I had spent eleven years building railroads before I spent five pretending I hadn't. I knew what a mountain of granite held inside it the way a butcher knows what's inside a steer. And the men who built the closed lines through this country—the men whose work I'd spent the last week studying in old survey notes—had been thorough in exactly one respect: they anchored things. Bridges, trestles, snowsheds, water flumes. They drilled holes in living rock and set iron in them, and iron, unlike men, does not lie about where it's been.

"Tom," I said. "Your uncle's papers. The old right-of-way maps. Do you have them on you?"

He stared at me through the snow. "What?"

"The maps. On your body. Not in a pack on the mule."

He dug them out of his coat, and I spread them against the rock face, and there it was, printed in faded ink like an answer to a prayer: the old snowshed line. They'd built a timber gallery along this very shelf in '81 to keep the winter drifts off the track, and when the line closed they'd pulled the timbers and left the iron. Which meant somewhere under two feet of drifted snow and forty years of talus, there was a row of rail anchors set into this cliff by men with drills and dynamite and no idea that someday a fool with a rope would be grateful to them.

We found the first one twenty minutes later, and I will confess I whooped like a schoolboy when the iron ring came clear of the snow, and Ida said, "Well, hallelujah, the dead men left us a handhold," and even Piet laughed, which I took as a good sign, since a man who can laugh with a broken leg is not yet a man in shock.

By full dark we had a camp. Not a good one—a scoop of shelter under an overhang, the mule wedged into a crevice, a fire the size of a hymnal that Ida coaxed and guarded like a hen on one egg. And we had a rope rigged from anchor to anchor along the shelf, because I had made a decision that I didn't announce as a decision, since announcing it would have meant admitting I'd been making different decisions up until then.

The decision was this: nobody was going to die on my expedition. Whatever else happened, whatever I owed or didn't owe to the dead man who'd given me the map, whatever Vane thought he'd bought when he hired me—nobody was going to die.

* * *

The storm held us for three days.

I want to be honest about what three days pinned against a cliff does to six strangers and a mule. It does not make them noble. It makes them small. The first day, everyone was cheerful in the grim way of people who've survived something and want credit for it. The second day, the cheer ran out and what was underneath was mostly fear wearing a coat. Fenwick photographed the same icicles four times because his hands shook too badly to load the plates right. Tom sat with his back to everyone, reading his uncle's maps over and over as though the answer to something were written between the contour lines. Hollis chewed tobacco and stared at the white and said nothing, which from a guide is a sermon.

And Piet lay against the rock with his leg splinted on a snowshoe frame and Ida's good brandy going into him in doses, and he apologized. That was the worst of it. Every few hours he'd catch my eye and say, "I'm sorry, Mr. Halloran. Slowed you down." As though the mountain had broken his leg out of personal loyalty to me and he'd failed to decline the honor.

The second night, I sat down next to him and said, "Piet. You know what slowed this expedition down? Me, spending a week not deciding to lead it. Your leg is the least of our problems and the easiest to fix. A broken leg heals. A broken expedition doesn't." I don't know if it helped him. It helped me to say it, which is probably why I said it.

* * *

The leg was the problem, though. You can splint a leg, but you cannot carry a grown man over mountain trail on a litter for six days with four people and a mule, not in snow, not safely. So I built a machine.

There was a second set of anchors, I'd found them on the first evening, up-slope where the old flume had run, and between the two sets I could rig a highline—a haul system that could lift Piet up over the broken section of shelf in a litter sling, hand over hand, with the rope running through blocks I improvised from the mule's harness rings and a pulley off Fenwick's tripod head, which he surrendered with the expression of a man donating a kidney.

I'd rigged haul systems before. That's the part I don't like telling. I'd rigged them on the Rivergate job, in fact, in the last season before the bridge, hauling cribbing up the gorge walls, and I had been good at it, and being good at it had been the best thing about my life back then. So on the third morning, when the wind dropped to a snarl and the light came through the snow like milk through cloth, I stood up in front of the party and explained the system, and I heard my own voice doing something it hadn't done in five years: giving orders and expecting them to be followed.

"Hollis, you're my top anchor man. Ida, you take the haul line with Tom. Fenwick, you're on Piet's litter, you talk to him the whole way up, doesn't matter what you say. I'll be at the changeover. Questions?"

There was one question. It came from Tom, and it wasn't really a question.

"Since when do you give the orders?"

I looked at him. He was cold and scared and twenty-six and carrying whatever his uncle had loaded on him in that office in the city, and I found I wasn't angry at him. I was almost gentle.

"Since the rope caught you," I said. "You were on my belt when we held Piet on the ledge. You've been taking my orders since then, Tom. I'm just saying them out loud now."

He flushed and looked away, and Ida snorted into her coffee, and that was that. It's a strange thing, how leadership actually gets transferred. Not by election, not by declaration. By a rope going taut in the dark and everybody finding out whose hands they trust on it.

The haul itself took two hours and cost us a decade of collective nerves. Piet went up the rock face in the litter sling, gray-faced, jaw set, making jokes about the view. Fenwick talked at him the whole way—about lenses, about light, about a girl in Sacramento, anything—and later Piet told me those two hours were the best conversation of his life, which says something sad about Piet's life that I chose not to inquire into. The mule went up second, hauled and slung and blasphemous about it. And then the rest of us crossed the broken shelf on the highline one at a time, with the gorge yawning below and the old iron anchors—the work of dead men who never knew our names—holding every one of us.

When the last of us was across, I sat down in the snow and discovered I was shaking, and that Ida had put a tin cup of coffee in my hands at some point without my noticing.

"You rigged that like you'd done it before," she said. Not accusing. Just noting, the way she noted everything, over the rim of her cup.

"I have."

"For the railroad."

"For the railroad."

She nodded slowly, looking out at the white. "My brother worked for the railroad," she said. "Rivergate section." And then she stood up and went to her pack and started breaking camp, because Ida told you exactly as much as she intended to tell you and not one word more, and she'd told me a whole sermon.

* * *

We made four miles that afternoon before the light went, down off the shelf and into the trees, and it was the happiest four miles I've ever traveled, because they were downhill and because nobody died. We camped under cedars with actual firewood, and Ida made something with beans and salt pork that tasted like a miracle, and Hollis played a mouth organ badly and nobody minded, and Piet got drunk on the medicinal brandy and told everyone he loved them, including the mule.

I sat apart from it, the way I'd sat apart from everything for five years, and then I noticed that I wasn't sitting apart. I was sitting at the edge of the circle, which is a different thing. The circle had simply grown to include me while I wasn't looking.

Fenwick came over and sat down beside me and showed me a plate he'd developed in his portable darkroom: the haul line against the cliff, the litter tiny against all that granite, the rope a single dark thread holding a human being above the void. It was, I had to admit, a fine photograph. It looked like something.

"What do I call you?" Fenwick asked suddenly. "I've been calling you Mr. Halloran, but Hollis says that's not your real name. He says nobody's real name is Halloran."

The old cold went through me, the familiar one, the one I'd carried since Rivergate. Every instinct I had said: deny it, laugh it off, give him a story. That was the discipline I'd built my new life on, brick by brick—never confirm, never explain, never be findable.

But I'd spent that day being findable. I'd stood in front of six people and been competent in public, and competence leaves a trail wider than any name.

"Halloran will do," I said. "It's as real as anything else I've got."

He nodded, satisfied, and wandered off to bother the mule, and I stayed by the fire a while longer, turning something over in my mind that had been turning over all day.

Because here was the thing I hadn't said out loud to any of them. The storm had cost us three days, and three days mattered, and not only because of the weather. Somewhere behind us on the trail, or ahead of us on it, or waiting at the end of it, there were men whose business was the map and the camp and whatever the prospector's crew had pulled out of that gorge. I'd felt it since the washout—that sensation of being watched by something that wasn't the wilderness. Vane had hired me too easily. Vane had asked too few questions. And a man who asks too few questions already knows the answers.

The wind was rising again, a different wind this time, out of the north, and it carried the smell of more snow, and under the snow-smell, or maybe only in my imagination, the smell of woodsmoke that wasn't ours.

Tomorrow we'd cross the divide and come down into the country beyond the washout, where the map said a camp was still holding out. Tomorrow, if the dying man's notes were true, we'd find out what his crew had found, and why a man gut-shot in the snow at mile forty-three had spent his last breath making me promise.

I looked at the fire, at Ida humming over the dishes, at Tom asleep with his uncle's maps still clutched to his chest like a child with a forbidden book, and I understood that I had already made my choice, three days ago, on a ledge in the white. Nobody dies on my expedition. Whatever that promise costs.

The wind came down through the cedars, and far off up the valley, so faint I could have been wrong, something answered it. A whistle. Long, then short, then long—the old company signal for *party in distress, come armed.*

Nobody had blown that signal on a closed line in five years.
