## 9. The Camp Beyond the Washout

We smelled the camp before we saw it, which is a thing I'd have said was impossible if you'd asked me a week earlier. Woodsmoke and something else underneath it—rendered fat gone rancid, wet wool, the particular sourness of men who've been living too close to their own fear for too long. Ida caught it first. She stopped in the middle of the trail with her head up like a bird dog and said, "Somebody's cooking over green pine," and then she said, quieter, "And they're cooking like people who've stopped caring whether it tastes of anything."

That was the tell. A man who cares about his food believes he has a future. A man who doesn't is eating to keep his body from filing complaints.

Casper—Tom, rather, because I keep wanting to call him by his family name and I refuse to give that family any more room in my head than it already has—asked why we were stopping. Ida didn't answer him. She just looked at me, and I looked at the ridge ahead, and I understood that everything easy about this expedition had just ended.

The prospector had told me four words about his crew before the snow took him: *Ruth will know the sign.* He'd scratched it into the map's margin along with a mark I'd spent two hundred miles puzzling over—a triangle with a line through it, drawn again and again in different inks, like a man practicing a signature or a prayer.

I found the answer at midday, painted on a slab of granite where the trail bent around a shoulder of the mountain: the same triangle, weathered but legible, with a fresh streak of charcoal across its base. Someone had renewed it recently. Recently meant days, not months. Recently meant alive.

"Signal," I said, and heard my own voice come out flatter than I intended. "It means someone's watching us right now, and they've decided not to shoot yet."

"That's reassuring," said Emil Brandt, the older of our two guides, who had the gift of making reassurance sound like a diagnosis.

"It's the most reassuring thing on this mountain," I said, and meant it.

* * *

The camp sat in a fold of the valley where an old rail siding had once served a loading ramp long since rotted to bones. Whoever built the shelter had used what the railroad left behind—a section house with half its roof, a boxcar body dragged off its wheels and sunk into the hillside like a coffin set on end, tarps stretched between them. It was clever work. It was also the work of desperate people, because cleverness is what desperation looks like when it's still winning.

Smoke rose thin and disciplined from a stovepipe. No fire visible. No movement. The kind of quiet that has effort behind it.

I did the arithmetic I'd been doing since Mile Forty-Three. Four survivors, the dying man had said—or I'd chosen to hear four; his mouth had been full of blood and my name wasn't in his will. Four people, five months past the washout, through a mountain winter, on whatever a closed rail line and a dead river gorge could be made to yield. If all four were still breathing, they were tougher than anyone I'd ever surveyed a line for.

"Hail them," Tom said. "Loud. Tell them we're a rescue party."

"And when they don't believe you?" Ida said. She wasn't being cruel. She was being accurate, which from Ida is nearly the same thing delivered with better manners.

"They should believe us."

"Why?" Ida asked. "Because you've got a clean coat?"

Tom shut up. To his credit—and I want to note here that there was credit due, though I wouldn't understand how much until later—he shut up without sulking. He was learning the mountains faster than I'd expected. Or the mountains were learning him. Some days I'm still not sure those are different things.

I walked forward alone. This was not bravery. Bravery would have been bringing the rifle. Walking forward empty-handed was arithmetic too: men who've survived a winter by shooting strangers don't leave survivors' camps, and men who haven't won't shoot a man carrying nothing but a rolled-up map and his own bad conscience.

I stopped thirty yards out, where the light was good and my hands were visible, and I called out the only introduction I had.

"My name is Casper Halloran! I met a man on the high line in November—prospector, gray beard, shot through the belly. He gave me his map and made me swear an oath." I held the map up over my head like a surrender flag, which in every way that mattered, it was. "He said: Ruth will know the sign."

Nothing. The smoke kept rising. Somewhere below the ridge, water talked to itself in the gorge, patient as a creditor.

Then a voice came off the hillside above the boxcar—not from the camp at all, which told me they had positions dug in and had let me walk past at least one of them—and it was a woman's voice, level and hard as rail iron.

"What did he say exactly? His words."

I closed my eyes and let the memory come back whole, because I had kept it whole the way you keep a splinter in deep: painfully, deliberately, so you can find it again.

"He said, *Tell her the ledger balances.* And then he said—" My throat did something unhelpful, and I made it stop. "Then he said, *Don't let her carry it alone anymore.*"

A long silence. Long enough for me to count my heartbeats and get bored of counting.

Then: "Come in slow. Hands where I can see them. Your people stay at the granite marker until I say otherwise."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you'll die out here with your oath half-kept," she said, "and I'll bury the map with you, because I am not sentimental, Mr. Halloran, whatever your dead man thought."

I liked her immediately, in the way you like a cold river: with respect, and no intention of swimming until necessary.

* * *

Ruth Okafor came down from her firing position—a scrape in the scree with a good field of fire and a bad view of the sunset, which tells you where her priorities sat—and she was not what the voice had prepared anyone for, least of all me. Small. Wire-thin under layers of patched wool. Gray going on white at the temples, though I'd learn later she was younger than that by a decade, the way hardship collects interest. Her eyes went over me the way I go over a bridge: load paths first, defects second, charm never.

She took the map from my hands without asking and opened it against her knee. Her thumb found the triangle-and-line mark and rested there.

"You came," she said. Not thanks. Statement of fact, delivered with the faint surprise of a woman checking a sum twice and getting the same answer.

"I swore an oath."

"People swear oaths." She folded the map and handed it back, and her fingers brushed mine, and I felt how cold they were even through my gloves. "People also die holding them. What did he promise you?"

"Nothing. That was the trick of it. He made me do the promising."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. It might have been a smile in a former life. "That sounds like Elias."

"Elias," I repeated. I hadn't known the dead man's name until that moment. Five months I'd carried him unnamed, and hearing it now was like setting down a weight I'd forgotten I was holding. "Elias. Then he's dead."

"He's been dead since November," Ruth said. "We knew it when he didn't come back before the passes closed. We buried him in April, in absentia, with a stone and a speech." Her jaw tightened. "Marta gave the speech. Marta gives all the speeches now. Grief has promoted her."

Behind me, up the trail, Ida's voice carried down the wind—she was talking to the others, keeping them steady, and beneath her words I could hear the mule complaining about existence, which was the most normal sound I'd heard in weeks. Ruth's eyes flicked toward it, cataloguing: six strangers, one mule, rifles, cookware, a camera tripod lashed to a pack. Her gaze snagged on the tripod.

"A photographer," she said.

"Jules Ferro. Young. Talks too much. Takes pictures that argue with each other."

"Why bring a photographer to a rescue?"

It was a fair question, and I had no honest answer that wasn't Vane's answer, and I was not yet ready to hand this woman the name Aldous Vane and watch what it did to her face. So I said, "Because the man paying for this expedition wanted documentation, and I didn't ask enough questions about why," and I watched her decide—visibly, the way ice decides—that I was a liar, but possibly a useful one.

"Your people can come in," she said. "Slowly. Weapons slung. And Mr. Halloran—" she stepped closer, and up close I could see the hunger in her, banked and rationed like everything else in that camp, "—before you eat our food and sleep under our roof and start feeling like a hero, you should look in the crates. Because the man who sent you, whoever he is, sent you for those. Everyone always comes for the crates."

* * *

They came out of the boxcar one at a time, and I want to record them properly, because the newspapers later got every one of them wrong.

Marta Reyes, small and round and sixty if she was fifty, with hands that shook until you put a tool in them. She'd been the crew's cook and surveyor's assistant both, and she looked at Ida across the fire circle with the instant mutual recognition of two women who have fed people through terrible winters, and something passed between them that needed no translation.

Desmond Achebe, twenty-three, one arm in a sling improvised from a cravat and a fence picket, feverish, the reason the expedition had arrived not a day too soon. He smiled at everyone with the reckless cheer of the very ill, and I learned later it was a performance he maintained so the others wouldn't have to grieve him in advance.

Old Sam Teller, who was not old, forty maybe, but had gone gray in five months the way men do when the thing they saw won't stop playing. He didn't speak for the first hour. When he finally did, it was to ask Ida whether she had any coffee that wasn't made from pine needles, and when she said yes, he cried into his hat, quietly, like a man closing an account.

And Ruth Okafor, who fed us last and ate least and watched the trail behind us the entire time, because—as she put it, when Jules asked why she kept looking north—*the mountain isn't the only thing that sends people.*

Ida got a proper meal moving within the hour, and I watched these four starving people eat slowly, deliberately, the way you eat when you've learned that gorging after famine kills, and I understood that I was looking at discipline wearing the mask of starvation, not the other way around. These weren't castaways. They were garrison.

The crates sat at the back of the boxcar, eight of them, roped and tarped and—this detail I noted with professional appreciation—sitting on split rails laid over gravel, above the damp. Guarded like children. Weighted like lead. Stenciled on their sides, faded but legible: R.G.R.R. — RIVERGATE GORGE ROUTE — BRIDGE DIVISION.

Rivergate. The word went through the camp like current through a wire. Ida set down her ladle. Tom went very still by the door. Even the mule, that profound skeptic, stopped chewing.

"You pulled these out of the gorge," I said.

"We pulled them out of the river," Ruth corrected. "The gorge just held them for us. Five years ago the company dumped a wagonload of records off the trestle during the 'cleanup.' Burned what would burn, sank what wouldn't. They forgot that paper floats, eventually, if the river is patient. And that rivers are extremely patient." She untied the nearest crate with hands that were suddenly, precisely steady. "My brother was a bridge inspector. He stayed behind after the collapse when everyone else was paid to leave. He documented everything—the piers, the timbers, the telegrams. Then the company documented him, in a sense. There's a grave upriver with no name on it that the coroner's report says doesn't exist."

She lifted the lid. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed paper, packed with the tenderness usually reserved for eggs: hundreds of pages. Telegrams with carbons. Ledger sheets. Photographs on glass plates, each one sleeved in wool. And on top, a single inspection report, dated eleven days before the collapse, signed in a hand I recognized from the margin of a certain map, because brothers teach brothers their letters.

"The official finding was flood scour," Ruth said, conversationally, the way you discuss weather with a man you intend to hang. "The piers failed because the river undermined them. An act of God. The directors took the insurance, closed the line, abandoned the town, and told four hundred people the evacuation was temporary." She looked up at me. "The piers were cut, Mr. Halloran. Sawn. My brother measured the kerfs. Do you know what a kerf is?"

"I'm a railroad engineer," I said.

"Then you know what it means when the saw marks run horizontal."

I did. God help me, I did, and somewhere behind me I heard Tom Vane breathe in sharply and not let it back out, and I thought: *he knows too, or he's about to, and whichever it is, his uncle's money is already in this room.*

"Why haven't you taken this out?" I asked. "Five months. Why sit on it?"

"Because the last three people who tried to carry it out are dead," Ruth said simply. "One fell. One drowned. One was shot, in November, on the high line, by men who then lost him in the snow." Her eyes held mine, and in them was the question she'd been aiming at me since the granite marker, the real negotiation under the polite one. "So before your expedition goes one step further, Mr. Halloran, you're going to tell me who sent you, what they promised you, and what they want with my brother's ledgers. And you're going to tell me now, while your friends are still eating my soup and nobody's pride is bleeding."

Outside, the light was failing. Up the trail, at the edge of the firelight, Jules Ferro was quietly turning his camera toward the open crate, and Ruth Okafor saw him do it, and did not stop him—which was, I would understand much later, the first test she'd set for any of us, and the first one anybody passed.

I sat down across from her, in the boxcar that smelled of woodsmoke and waxed paper and five months of held breath, and I began to talk.

And halfway through telling her about Aldous Vane—about the money, the claims, the civility with teeth in it—I saw her expression change, and it was not surprise.

"You already knew the name," I said.

Ruth Okafor folded her hands on her brother's ledgers and said, "Mr. Halloran, sit down properly. What I know about Aldous Vane is going to take longer than the soup."
