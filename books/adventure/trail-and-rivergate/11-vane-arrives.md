## 11. Vane Arrives

I had been three days at the camp beyond the washout when the mule Ida called Judas lifted his head and started braying at the treeline.

Mules have opinions about people. Most of them are bad opinions delivered at volume. But I'd learned to read this one's complaints like a telegrapher reads tape, and this was not his usual grievance about the quality of the oats. This was something new coming up the trail from the south — something with too many boots in it to be weather.

"Ruth," I said quietly.

She was already moving. That was the thing about Ruth Okafor that a man underestimated at his peril: she heard everything first. She'd spent five years listening for company men in the timber, and her ears had been sharpened to a point you could have surveyed with.

"How many?" she asked, coming to stand beside me at the edge of camp, where the pines opened onto the old rail grade.

"At least six. Maybe eight." I squinted into the green dark. "They're not trying to be quiet."

"They want us to know they're coming."

"That's generally what it means when a man stops being quiet."

She gave me a look that said my humor was noted and filed under *useless*, which was fair. Then she did the thing I'd watched her do twice already since we'd arrived: she touched two fingers to the crate of documents sitting under the oilskin by the cold stove, just briefly, the way another woman might touch a locket or a child's head. A benediction. Or a promise.

"We should move them," she said.

"Not yet. If it's who I think it is, running now tells him where to dig."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then we've hidden our supper from friends for nothing, and I'll apologize to the beans."

The trees gave them up a minute later. Eight men on foot and two on horseback, moving down the old grade in loose order, rifles slung but present the way a handshake is present. And at the front of them, riding a gray gelding too fine for the country, sat Aldous Vane.

He looked, I will grant him this, exactly like what he claimed to be: a worried benefactor come through hell and high water to check on his investment. His coat was dusty but well-cut. His boots were wrong for the trail — city boots, polished once and regretted ever since — but he wore the suffering of them like a medal. He dismounted before his horse had fully stopped, which is either enthusiasm or theater, and spread his arms wide.

"Mister Halloran!" he called, his voice carrying the way money carries. "Thank God. Thank God, sir. When your supply wire went unanswered I feared the worst."

Behind me, I felt Ruth go very still.

*Your* supply wire. Not *the*. He'd said it smooth as river stone, but I caught it, and I could feel Ruth catching it beside me, and I understood in one cold drop that Vane had known about this camp — or believed he knew — long before any of us had drawn breath at this altitude.

I walked out to meet him. It seemed important not to let him close the distance himself. There's a rule in engineering and in life both: never let another man choose the ground.

"Mr. Vane," I said. "You're a long way from anywhere."

"For good men in trouble, there is no such distance." He clasped my hand in both of his. His palms were soft as ledger paper. "I came the moment I could raise men and provisions. Where are the survivors? The prospector's crew — are they alive?"

"Four of them."

His face performed relief so perfectly that for half a second even I believed it, and I'd had three days to practice disbelief. "Four. Bless you. Bless you, Halloran. You've done a Christian work out here."

*You've no idea what kind of work,* I thought, and smiled like a man agreeing.

Ida appeared at my shoulder with coffee — black as sin, hot enough to strip paint, offered to armed strangers with the expression of a woman daring them to comment. Tom Vane hung back near the horses, and I marked that: the nephew keeping distance from the uncle, eyes down, jaw tight. Whatever had passed between them in the night, it hadn't settled anything. It had only loaded something.

Ruth came forward when Vane asked — insisted, really — on seeing the survivors. She stood in front of her people with her arms crossed and her chin up, and Vane looked at the four of them, gaunt and hard-eyed around their crates, and I watched him do arithmetic behind his face. Four mouths. One map, presumably destroyed or dispersed. And somewhere in that camp, if his information held, papers.

"Papers," he said lightly, to all of us, over the coffee Ida poured and nobody drank. "Of course, the poor souls have been through an ordeal. Anything they were carrying — survey notes, claim records, correspondence — the company will want to restore it to them properly. Through proper channels. For insurance purposes, you understand. So much paperwork in a tragedy."

"I don't understand," Ruth said. "But then I'm only a woman with a dead brother, so perhaps it's above me."

Vane's smile didn't move a hair. "I'm sure we can find someone to explain it to you."

* * *

He waited until full dark to make his real offer. Of course he did. Men like Vane believe darkness is a kind of privacy rather than a kind of witness, and they never learn better because everyone they deal with has already been bought.

He found me at the woodpile, because I'd made a show of needing firewood, and he made a show of wanting to help, and between us we stacked an amount of split pine that neither of us looked at.

"You've impressed me, Halloran," he said. "Genuinely. Getting these people out alive, through that country, in that season. I don't impress easily."

"You strike me as a man who's difficult to impress and easy to disappoint."

A laugh, warm as a bank lobby. "There it is. That dry tongue of yours. Do you know, when I hired you, I told my nephew: that engineer has buried something, and whatever it is, it's made him careful. Careful men are worth ten brave ones." He set a log on the pile with exaggerated care. "And then I thought — why does a railroad man with his credentials vanish off the face of the earth for five years? What does a man run from, Halloran? Generally it's one of three things. A woman. A debt. Or a paper with his name on it that says something he wishes it didn't."

The axe in my hands suddenly weighed more than axes weigh.

"I don't know what you mean."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't." He turned to face me, and the firelight from the camp reached us in thin fingers, and his eyes in that light were the color of a closed ledger. "So here is what I mean, and then we need never speak of it again. Somewhere in this camp there are documents. Telegrams, reports, correspondence relating to the Rivergate Bridge. They are worth nothing to the people holding them — less than nothing; they're dangerous. To me they are worth a great deal, and I am prepared to be generous about it."

"To them, you mean."

"To whoever brings them to me. But let's speak plainly, you and I, since we're the only two men here who understand what things cost." He stepped closer. He smelled of bay rum and horse sweat. "Ten thousand dollars. Cash, within the month. And a name — a clean one, a real one, with papers behind it and history behind the papers. A man could be born again at forty, Halloran, if he had the right midwife. No more looking over your shoulder on every platform. No more flinching when a conductor calls your ticket. You could sleep through the night for the first time in five years. I know you don't. Sleep, I mean. I've watched you."

Every word landed where he aimed it. That was the obscenity of it — not the money, not even the forged identity, but the accuracy. Ten thousand dollars was more than I'd earn in twenty years of honest surveying. And the name — God help me, the name. I had lain awake in a hundred bunkhouses rehearsing conversations with conductors and sheriffs and land agents, composing my answers to questions no one had asked yet, and here was a man offering to burn the whole rehearsal hall down and hand me a life where I'd never needed lines.

He saw it land. He was too practiced not to.

"All you have to do," he said softly, "is carry a crate down a mountain and forget what was in it. You've forgotten heavier things."

I set the axe down. Slowly. Partly for effect. Mostly because my hands wanted to do something else with it.

"Mr. Vane," I said, "do you know what I did with the dying man's last breath?"

Something flickered across his face — impatience, maybe, or the first hairline crack of uncertainty.

"He didn't give me a map," I said. "That's what you think he gave me. A map, a thing, an object you can buy. He gave me a promise. *Bring my crew out alive.* Those were near his last words, and I took them, and I've discovered something about promises in the last few weeks: they don't weigh anything going out, and they weigh everything coming back." I picked up the split wood instead, one piece at a time, because it gave my hands honest work. "Those papers aren't mine to sell. They belong to dead men who can't defend themselves and living ones who trusted me. You're offering to buy my silence with the one thing I've wanted for five years, and I'll tell you what I told myself every night in every bunkhouse from here to the coast: a clean name bought dirty is just a cleaner lie."

For a long moment Vane said nothing. The campfire cracked somewhere behind us. Somewhere a mule expressed an opinion.

Then he straightened his coat, and the warmth went out of him like a lamp turned down, and what remained was the thing underneath the benefactor — patient, precise, and entirely without mercy.

"You misunderstand the structure of our arrangement," he said. "This was not a negotiation. It was a courtesy. I wanted you to hear the offer from me, once, so that afterward — when it goes badly for you — you cannot say you weren't given a chance to be sensible." He gathered his reins from where he'd draped them. "Men who refuse money always imagine they've purchased something with the refusal. Character, honor, some such coin. They haven't. They've simply moved themselves to the other side of the ledger, and ledgers, Mr. Halloran, always balance."

"There's a flaw in your bookkeeping," I said. "Some debts don't get collected. Some get forgiven."

"Not mine." He swung up onto the gray gelding with more grace than I'd have credited him. "Sleep well, Engineer. Take stock of what you have. Count your people." He paused, and the smile he gave me down through the dark was almost paternal. "I intend to."

He rode back toward his men's fire, and I stood at the woodpile with my heart going like a drive rod and the cold working through my coat, and I understood with perfect clarity what I had just done. I hadn't refused a bribe. I had answered a declaration of war with a flat *no*, and wars, unlike promises, don't wait politely for you to finish stacking wood.

Ida was waiting by the fire with two cups. She handed me one without asking how it went, which meant she'd heard, which meant half the camp had heard, which meant Vane had counted on that too.

"Well?" she said.

"We're past the part where anybody gets to be neutral."

She nodded slowly and looked into the flames. "Good," she said, after a while. "I was getting tired of pretending to like his nephew."

Across the camp, Tom Vane sat apart from his uncle's men, hunched over the coals with his rifle across his knees, and when he glanced up and caught me watching, he didn't look away. He looked, I thought, like a man standing on a bridge he already knew was cut.

And far off in the dark, down the mountain toward Rivergate, I could have sworn I heard hammering — faint, rhythmic, deliberate. Men building something, or tearing something down, and no way yet to tell which.
