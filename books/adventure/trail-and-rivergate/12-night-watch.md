## 12. Night Watch

The camp beyond the washout kept its own hours. Nobody slept so much as waited for sleep to happen to them, and when it didn't, they fed the fire and stared at it like it owed them money.

Ruth Okafor woke me at two in the morning by dropping a tin cup of coffee on my bedroll with the delicacy of a woman throwing an anchor overboard.

"Your watch," she said.

I sat up. The cold came in through every seam of me like it had been waiting politely at the door. "I was having a dream where nobody needed anything from me."

"How'd that end?"

"You just ended it."

She almost smiled. Almost. It was the most generous thing she'd given anyone since we'd come up over the ridge and found her four skeletons-that-still-walked guarding a pile of crates like a dragon with a library degree. "Drink your coffee. You've got till four."

The fire was down to a red heart in a cage of black logs. I fed it a couple of splits from the woodpile—her people's woodpile, cut by hands that hadn't known if there'd be a spring—and settled onto the flat stone she'd been using as a chair. She didn't leave. That was new. For six days she'd taken her watch alone or with her brother-in-arms, a silent man named Ekwueme who communicated mostly in nods and one memorable shrug. Tonight she stayed, wrapped in a coat that had been good ten years ago, and looked out past the firelight into the dark trees like she expected them to say something.

I let the silence sit. I'd learned that about her. Silence wasn't empty with Ruth; it was load-bearing.

"You asked me once," she said finally, "why we stayed up here instead of coming down."

"I asked why you pointed a rifle at Tom."

"That too." She turned the cup in her hands. "We came down once. First winter. Got as far as the trading post at Kettle Falls, and there was a man there asking questions. Polite man. Good boots. He showed a photograph around—my brother's photograph—and asked if anyone had seen him come off the mountain."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a mule complained about the general state of existence.

"What did you do?"

"We went back up before he finished his coffee." She said it flat, but her thumb pressed white against the tin. "Good boots cost money, Mr. Halloran. Money like that comes from men who buy railroads. We understood what the photograph meant. It meant my brother's body was worth more dead than alive, and anybody who led them to it would be paid accordingly."

"Emmanuel," I said. Her brother. The one who'd stayed behind at Rivergate with a camera and a conscience.

"He was the stubborn one. I'm the reasonable one. Ask anyone." She looked at me then, and the firelight did something unkind to the lines of her face, made her look older and more tired than she ever allowed in daylight. "You understand stubbornness, though. Don't you."

It wasn't a question, and we both knew it. I drank my coffee. It was terrible—Ida would have wept—but it was hot, and heat is heat.

"My name isn't Halloran," I said.

"I know."

That stopped me. "You know."

"Ekwueme read your hands the day you arrived. Calluses. A man who's spent thirty years holding tools, not holding a rifle. And you looked at our crates the way a man looks at paperwork, not treasure. Engineers read documents like engineers." She shrugged. "Also, Tom talks in his sleep, and Ida talks awake, and between the two of them this camp has no secrets whatsoever."

I laughed. It came out rusty, like a gate that hadn't swung in years. "What exactly did they say?"

"That you go by another name. That there was a hearing, years back. A bridge inspection." She watched me carefully. "That you signed something."

The cold got into me again, right through the coffee. Five years I'd carried that sentence without saying it aloud, and here it was being handed back to me by a starving woman on a mountain, and the strange thing—the thing I'd remember—was that it weighed less outside my chest than in it.

"I signed it," I said. "The Corben Creek trestle. I was the assistant engineer. My chief inspector told me the numbers were fine, told me the stress reports were clean, and I signed the certification because he was twenty years my senior and because the company had already bought the steel and because I was twenty-eight years old and wanted to believe the world worked the way the manuals said it did." I turned the cup. "Three months later a freight crew went through it at speed. Two men died. The inquiry found the report was forged. They never proved who held the pen."

"But they proved who could have."

"They proved I had access, motive enough to keep my job, and no friends in the room. I ran before the second hearing. Changed my name, took work on closed lines—dead lines, lines nobody rode and nobody audited—because a ghost is safest where the rails don't go." I heard myself and hated how tidy it sounded, how rehearsed by silence. "That's the disgrace. That's what Vane could buy and sell if he knew. Maybe does know. Men like him collect debts the way other men collect stamps."

Ruth was quiet a long moment. Then: "Did you forge it?"

"No." And there it was, said plain, to a witness, under stars, where it counted. "I signed what I was told was true. Which is its own crime. But I didn't forge it. My chief inspector did, and he retired comfortably on a lake in Ohio, and I have hated him every day since with a hatred that has done precisely nothing to either of us."

"And the two men who died."

"Their names were Aldous Brandt and Peter Yoon. I say them to myself on the anniversary. It doesn't help them. It helps me remember why I check everything twice now." I set the cup down. "Why are you telling me things, Mrs. Okafor? Six days ago you'd have shot me on general principle."

"Ruth." She corrected it the way she corrected everything, like straightening a crooked spike. "And because you're still here. Vane's nephew tried to talk his way into the crates. Your guides ask what the cargo weighs and what it's worth. You asked me—" she paused, and something moved behind her eyes, "—you asked me what happened to the town. Not the papers. The town. Who lived there. Where they went."

"Because the papers are evidence," I said. "The town was people."

"Yes." She said it softly, like closing a door gently on a sleeping child. "Emmanuel used to say the same. He was the company telegrapher at Rivergate. Did Ida tell you? No—she wouldn't yet. He was the one who copied the wire traffic. Cost-cutting orders for the bridge piers. Then the order declaring the water contaminated, moving the whole town out on three days' notice. Then the order sealing the gorge records. He copied all of it into his own book before they shut his office, because he thought—" her voice caught, steadied, "—he thought paper would protect people. Paper and truth. He was young."

"How old?"

"Nineteen." She looked into the fire. "He stayed behind when they evacuated the last of the town, to photograph the bridge piers before the river took them. The collapse came early. They never recovered him. Or the others. Eleven men working the pier crews, and the company report called it 'an act of God, the river being unusually high that season.'" Her jaw tightened. "The river was high because the company opened the upstream sluice gates to flood the worksite and hide what the divers had found. Cut piers. Under-spec steel. My brother documented all of it, and the river kept him, and the company wrote God a thank-you note."

I had stood on the broken trestle five days ago and felt the wrongness of it in my bones—the piers sheared clean, not crumbled—and I had told myself dread was just altitude talking. Now it had a name, eleven names, and a nineteen-year-old boy with a camera.

"I'm sorry," I said, and it was inadequate, and she nodded because she knew it was inadequate and accepted it anyway, which is more courtesy than I deserved.

"Here's what I can't figure," she said after a while. "You. You built yourself a life of not-being-found. Dead lines, false names, no letters, no photographs. And then a dying stranger shows you a map, and you walk straight back into the world's attention with both arms open. Why? A sensible ghost stays a ghost."

I considered lying to her. It lasted about a second and a half; lying to Ruth Okafor felt like cheating at solitaire.

"Because he made me promise," I said. "A dying man grabbed my sleeve in the snow and made me promise to bring his crew out. And I've spent five years telling myself the worst thing I ever did was sign my name to a lie somebody else wrote. But the truth is the worst thing was easier than that. The worst thing was all the mornings after, when I saw what the lie cost, and chose my own skin anyway. Every morning. Five years of small cowardices, each one reasonable, each one easy." I fed the fire a split of pine. "So when a man bled out in front of me holding my hand and asking for his people—I couldn't do the easy thing twice. Not twice in one life. Once was already too many."

Ruth studied me across the fire for a long time. Snow ticked softly off the pines. Down in the dark, the river ran, carrying whatever it carried.

"Emmanuel believed institutions would do the right thing if shown the truth," she said at last. "I believed it too, once. Then we learned the institution wrote the lies. So now I don't much care for faith. But I'll tell you what I have instead." She leaned forward, elbows on knees. "I have four people up here who ate bark broth all winter rather than abandon those crates. I have a cook down the hill who lost a brother to the same river and packs extra food for ghosts. I have a rich man's nephew who looks at me like a dog that's been kicked and keeps coming back anyway. And as of tonight I have an engineer with a false name and a true spine." She stood, joints cracking like green wood in a fire. "That's not faith. That's inventory. And the inventory says we don't turn back."

"No," I agreed. "We don't."

"Vane will come for these crates. Not with lawyers—with men. You know it."

"I know it."

"And when he does, your name won't save you and mine won't save me, and half this camp will be running downhill in the dark carrying paper while armed men carry guns." She said it without drama, the way she'd say *it'll snow tomorrow*. "So I need to know, engineer. When it breaks—and it will break—which way do you run?"

I looked at the fire. At the tents where Ida slept with her brother's memory folded into her apron pocket, where Tom slept badly because guilt makes poor bedding, where the photographer kept her plates warm against her body like eggs. At the crates stacked under oilcloth, holding the voices of dead men in telegraphese.

"Nowhere," I said. "I run toward. I'm done running away. It's a bad habit and it never once worked."

Ruth put out her hand—not to shake, exactly. To grip, forearm to forearm, the way trail people do, the way you hold a rope you intend to trust with your life. I took it. Her hand was small and hard as a spike maul.

"Then we stand together," she said. "Whoever comes."

* * *

The rest of the watch passed in the companionable silence of two people who have exchanged their worst and found the other still standing there. Around three I taught her to read the wind off the ridge—how the pines on the north shoulder whispering meant weather inside a day—and she taught me which of Ekwueme's silences meant *yes*, which meant *no*, and which meant *I am judging you and finding you adequate*. It was, I reflected, more intimacy than I'd managed in five years of ghosthood, conducted entirely over bad coffee at two in the morning, and I found I didn't mind at all.

At four she woke her relief and I should have gone to my bedroll. Instead I walked the perimeter, checking the picket line, the cache, the sightlines down the gorge—and found myself doing it not as a hired guide counting hazards, but as a man counting what he had to lose. The distinction mattered. Hazards you avoid. What you have to lose, you defend.

I was passing the supply tent when I saw it: the fresh-cut end of a rope, pale as bone against the dark of the older fiber. The main haul line down to the lower cache. Sliced clean, deliberate, hours old at most.

And below, in the gray pre-dawn, where six mules should have been stamping and steaming at their pickets—

the picket line stood empty, stakes pulled, tracks scattering three directions into the timber like spilled marbles.

I stood very still in the cold, listening to the camp breathe behind me, and understood that the war Ruth and I had just declared had already started without us—and that whoever had struck first was sleeping somewhere inside our own firelight.
