## 13. Sabotage

I have a theory about trouble, and it's this: trouble doesn't travel alone, it travels with luggage, and it always unpacks at the worst possible hour.

Ours chose the one before dawn.

I was on second watch, which meant I'd spent four hours listening to the river argue with itself in the gorge below and trying not to think about telegrams that ordered men to their deaths. The camp was quiet in the way mountain camps get quiet—breathing, but barely. Ruth's people slept close together near the crates, the way soldiers sleep, or the way people sleep who've learned that distance between bodies is an invitation. Our expedition slept a little looser. Tom had his back to a boulder with his hat over his face. Ida snored like a woman settling debts. The mules were picketed along the treeline where the grass grew thickest.

The first thing wrong was the silence from the mules. Mules are never silent. They mutter, they stamp, they editorialize about the quality of the grass. A quiet mule line at three in the morning is like a quiet boiler room—it means something has stopped working.

Then I heard the ropes come off.

Not cut—not yet—but unfastened, the soft clatter of a buckle handled by someone who knew buckles. I came up off my bedroll with the lantern unlit because a lit lantern only tells you what you're about to lose sight of.

"Who's up?" I said, low.

Nothing. And then, out along the treeline, a shape moved—quick, bent, deliberate—and there was a sound I'll carry to my grave: the particular wet whisper of a blade going through hemp. Three of them, fast as a man can work. Then a fourth rope simply dropped its knot, untied from the inside, which told me our visitor had practiced.

The mules did the rest. Scared animals don't need instructions. They went down the slope like a rockslide with opinions, crashing through the alder, and by the time I reached the picket line there was nothing left but four cut ends and one neatly coiled rope laid on a stone like a calling card.

Someone wanted us stranded. Not scattered—stranded. You scatter a camp to slow it down. You strand it to keep it exactly where it is, until somebody arrives to collect it.

And we all knew who was coming to collect.

* * *

By full light we'd recovered two of the four mules, which sounds better than it is, because the two we'd lost were the pack animals carrying most of the food. Ida stood over the remaining stores with her arms crossed and her mouth set in a line I'd seen once before, on a woman reading a telegram she didn't want to read twice.

"So," she said. "Somebody in this camp wants us to stay put."

"Somebody *near* this camp," said Harkness, the taller of our two guides. He was a leathery man who'd been pleasant for six days and had apparently decided the vacation was over. "Could've been Vane's men already on the mountain."

"With a knife good enough to leave coil-marks like that?" Ida jerked her chin at the stone. "That's a camp knife. That's one of ours."

That landed the way she meant it to. Eleven people looked at eleven other people, and every face did arithmetic.

Ruth Okafor hadn't spoken yet. She sat on a crate—the crates, God help us, the ones worth more than everyone present—and watched us with those level eyes that made you feel like a specimen. When she finally spoke, it wasn't accusation. It was worse. It was procedure.

"We'll count everything," she said. "Food, ammunition, documents. Then nobody leaves camp alone, and nobody touches another person's gear. Not because anyone here is guilty." She let that sit just long enough. "Because someone wants us to believe everyone is."

It was well done, and it didn't help. Fear doesn't need evidence; it needs only a room and time. By noon we had three separate theories circulating: Vane's scouts, one of Ruth's crew gone bad, and—I caught the edges of it, whispered between Harkness and the photographer's boy—a theory involving me, because I was the newest stranger and strangers are where suspicion goes to be comfortable.

I let them talk. I was busy.

Here's what I had that they didn't: twenty years of reading rope. A railroad engineer spends his life around knots, hitches, lashings—the whole liturgy of things that hold. The cut ends told me the blade was narrow and freshly honed. The coiled rope told me the hand was right-handed and unhurried. But the detail that mattered, the one I kept turning over like a coin, was the fourth rope. Untied from the inside. Clean buckle work, no fumbling, done in the dark by touch.

There were maybe three people in that camp who could do it. Two of Ruth's crew had hands too scarred and stiff for that kind of fine night work—I'd watched them struggle with tent pegs for days. That left one of the guides, and me.

And Tom Vane.

Tom, who wore gloves even in warm weather. Tom, whose uncle had hired this expedition without explaining why. Tom, who had been very carefully, very competently useless since the day we left.

I didn't want it to be him. I'll say that plainly, because whatever else happened after, I want the record straight: I liked the boy. He had a dry humor he kept hidden like contraband, and he carried Ida's water without being asked, and when the guide broke his leg in the whiteout he'd held the stretcher line for six hours without complaint. You don't spend nine days in the mountains with a man and stay indifferent to him.

But liking a man and trusting him are different trades entirely, and I'd learned that lesson at cost.

* * *

I found him at the woodpile that evening, splitting kindling with more force than kindling requires. Chips were flying like he was fighting the tree personally.

"Good arm," I said.

"It's firewood, Halloran, not artillery."

"Everything's artillery if you swing it angry enough." I picked up a split piece, turned it over. "You sleep with your gloves on?"

His axe paused. Half a heartbeat. Anyone else would've missed it. "Cold nights," he said.

"Mm. Cold enough to unbuckle a mule line bare-handed, though. Buckles bite at that temperature. Skin sticks to iron." I set the wood down. "Funny thing about the fourth rope. It wasn't cut. It was untied. From the inside. Neat as a parlor trick. Takes small hands, or gloved ones, and a man who's worked rigging before."

"You're saying it was me." His voice had gone flat, which is what voices do when they're deciding whether to run.

"I'm saying I haven't decided yet," I said. "Which is why we're having this conversation by the woodpile instead of in front of the camp."

He looked past me toward the tents, toward the glow of Ida's cookfire, and something in his face cracked—not guilt exactly. More like exhaustion wearing guilt's coat. "You know what my uncle does to people who disappoint him?"

"I'm beginning to form a picture."

"He doesn't shout. That's the thing everyone gets wrong. He *thanks* you. He thanks you so warmly you spend the next year wondering what it cost." Tom drove the axe into the block and left it standing there, quivering. "I was eleven when I first saw it. A foreman shorted us on timber for a trestle. Uncle Aldous thanked him for his years of service, gave him a fine reference letter, and had him blacklisted from every road west of Denver inside a month. The man drank himself dead in two years. Uncle sent flowers."

"And now he's thanked you."

Tom laughed, and it was an ugly sound, self-directed. "Three nights ago. He has men shadowing us—has since the pass. One slipped me a note while I was watering the mules. Cut the lines. Scatter the stock. Make sure the camp can't move before he arrives." He pulled his glove off, and there it was, folded small against his palm: a strip of paper, grease-pencil on butcher's wrap, in a hand I recognized from the contract I'd signed in Vane's office. "I told myself I'd find a way to do it badly. Loosen a knot instead of cutting. Let the mules wander somewhere findable. I told myself that counted as loyalty to—" he gestured at the camp, the firelight, Ida's smoke, Ruth's watchful dark-eyed people, all of it— "to this. To you people. Isn't that pathetic? I sabotaged my own sabotage and wanted credit for it."

"It's not pathetic," I said. "It's the hardest thing a man can do, halfway through. Doing it all the way is easier. Halfway tears you apart."

"My father tore himself apart that way," Tom said quietly. "Halfway loyal to my uncle his whole life. Died halfway." He looked up at me, and for the first time since I'd met him he looked less like Aldous Vane's nephew and more like somebody's son. "What happens now? You take me to Ruth and Ida and they put me off the expedition, or worse, and my uncle wins anyway, because the camp splits and a split camp can't move."

"That depends on what you do in the next ten minutes."

"Which is?"

"Walk over there with me," I said, "and say all of that again. Louder. To the people you did it to."

He stared at me. "They'll never trust me again."

"No," I agreed. "Probably not tonight. Maybe not ever fully. Trust isn't a door, Tom, it's a bridge—you build it plank by plank and it holds weight slowly. But here's what I know about bridges." I tapped my own chest, where five years of hiding had settled like ballast. "A man who confesses under no pressure at all, with nothing to gain—that man has started building. And I'd rather cross a half-built bridge with him than stand safe on the bank with a man who's still taking orders."

* * *

The camp took it about how you'd expect.

Ida threw a tin cup at him. Missed on purpose—I could tell it was on purpose, because Ida doesn't miss—and then stood with her fists on her hips and called him a name that would've gotten him shot in certain territories. Ruth asked questions, precise and cold, for the better part of an hour: dates, times, the note itself, the location of the contact, how many men Vane had, how they were armed. Tom answered everything, flat and complete, holding nothing back, and I watched Ruth's anger bank itself down into something more useful. Calculation.

Harkness wanted him tied up. The photographer's boy wanted him gone. One of Ruth's crew, a silent woman named Adaeze who hadn't spoken ten words since we found them, looked at Tom for a long moment and then said, in her low rough voice: "My brother stayed behind at Rivergate to hold the truth. This boy came up the mountain carrying the lie, and put it down. Both count." She turned back to the fire. It was, I gathered, the closest thing to absolution she issued.

Ida took longer. She stood across the fire from Tom with her arms crossed, and finally she said, "Your uncle's money fed me for the trip up. My brother's ghost feeds me now. Don't make me choose between being grateful and being honest, boy, because honest wins." And then she handed him a bowl of stew, which in Ida's language was a treaty signed and sealed.

We sat late around the fire after that, and the camp reorganized itself in small visible ways—watch rotations doubled, gear redistributed, the crates moved closer to the center. Fractured, yes. The suspicion didn't vanish; it just found a new address, pointing downhill toward the men Vane had following us. But something else had shifted too, something I felt in the way people arranged themselves near the flames. We weren't two expeditions sharing a trail anymore. Somewhere between the cut ropes and the confession, we'd become one party with one problem, and the problem had a name, and the name was coming up the mountain.

Tom sat beside me at the edge of the firelight, turning the grease-pencil note over and over in his fingers.

"There's a part I didn't say in front of them," he said finally. "The note had a second paragraph. Instructions for after."

"After what?"

"After the camp couldn't move. It said: *when the party is fixed in place, remove Halloran.*" He glanced sideways at me. "Not kill. Just remove. Get you separated from the group somewhere on the trail. Uncle wants you delivered alive."

I fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. Delivered alive. Of course. Dead men can't sign confessions, and I was the loose thread in Vane's weave—the engineer who'd walked the closed lines, who'd seen the piers, who'd read the map. You don't burn a thread like that. You tie it off somewhere private.

"How many men does he have?" I asked.

"Eight that I know of. Maybe twelve."

"Armed?"

"Rifles. And he's got the territorial marshal's ear, or the marshal's price—one of the two."

I looked around the fire: Ida scowling at the dark, Ruth and Adaeze speaking softly over the crates, the guides cleaning rifles with grim economy, the photographer's boy pretending not to listen and failing completely. Eleven souls, two mules, half a larder, and a stack of paper worth killing for, pinned on a mountainside with a wolf coming up the trail.

"Well," I said, and heard myself smile, which surprised me. "Then I suppose we'd best not be here when he arrives."

Tom frowned. "Move how? He cut our lines to stop exactly that."

"He cut our lines to stop a *camp*," I said. "But I didn't come up this mountain as a camper, Tom. There's an old grade below the washout—construction-era tramway, before my time, mostly forgotten. Steep, ugly, and not on any map Vane bought. If we can get the crates and the people down to it by tomorrow night..." I let the sentence hang, the way you let a bridge hang before you test it.

Ruth had stopped talking to Adaeze. She was looking at me across the fire with those level eyes, and I saw that she'd heard every word.

"An old grade," she repeated. "How steep?"

"Steep enough that we'll wish we hadn't," I admitted. "Shallow enough that Vane won't expect it. We move at dusk tomorrow, light and fast, and we're gone before his wolves figure out where the scent went."

She nodded slowly, and around the fire, one by one, the others began to understand what I was proposing—and began, without anyone calling for it, to reach for their packs.

Down in the black gorge, the river kept up its argument with the stones. Up above us, somewhere past the ridgeline, Aldous Vane was climbing toward a camp that wouldn't be there.

I just hoped we'd all fit through the gap before it closed.
