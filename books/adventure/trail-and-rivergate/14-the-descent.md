## 14. The Descent

I have been shot at twice in my life. The first time was an accident—a surveyor's mule kicked a rifle off a pack saddle and the bullet went through my hat brim, which is the sort of story that improves with telling. The second time was at dawn on a mountainside above a dead man's camp, and I suspect no amount of retelling will ever make that one funny.

The first shot took the top rail off the corral the survivors had built from salvaged ties. The second went into the cook fire and scattered embers across Ruth Okafor's tarpaulin, which caught fire, which she stamped out with the same expression my mother used for spilled milk. That told me something important about Ruth: she had already made her peace with gunfire. It was weather to her.

"They're coming up the east draw," said one of her crew, a lean man called Ferro who had spent five years eating pine bark and distrust. "Eight, maybe nine. Two of them flanking along the creek."

"Vane?" I asked.

"Not yet. His kind sends the dogs first."

Ida appeared beside me with a rifle she'd taken off a guide who no longer needed convincing, and Tom Vane stood a little apart from all of us, holding his own weapon like it was a snake someone had handed him. He hadn't slept. None of us had, since the ropes were cut and the truth came out of him like a rotten tooth.

"Casper," he said. "My uncle won't stop at shooting. He'll burn the camp with them in it if he has to."

"I know."

"So what do we do?"

What we did, in the end, was what I should have suggested hours ago, before the first shot, before the argument about whether to stand or parley—arguments that feel very serious right up until the moment bullets start arriving and reveal themselves as the luxury they always were.

"We go down," I said.

Ruth looked at me. Down meant the gorge. Down meant the washout, the collapsed trestle, the river running high with melt, and the closed line—the one the company had abandoned on paper while quietly keeping its grades and cuts intact, because abandoning a railroad in the ledger and abandoning it in the earth are two different expenditures, and directors know the difference even when they pretend not to.

"The gorge is a trap," Ferro said. "Dead end."

"It's a corridor," I said. "There's a line down there. Number Three, the Rivergate branch. It was never torn up. They couldn't afford to tear it up—they just stopped admitting it existed. There are culverts, drainage galleries, a maintenance crawlway through Deadman's Tunnel. I surveyed half of it before—" Before, I almost said, before I became a man who didn't exist. "Before I left the company."

"You're telling me there's a road out of here that only you can read," Ruth said.

"I'm telling you there's a scar in the mountain that only an engineer can read. Whether that's better depends on how much you trust engineers."

"I don't trust anyone," she said. "But I trust the alternative less. Get them moving."

* * *

Getting them moving was the work of ten minutes and cost me more sweat than the next six hours combined. Four survivors who had guarded those crates for months through winter do not abandon them lightly; the crates were the only reason any of them were still alive, the only reason their dead had not died entirely in vain. Ruth settled it by putting her brother's telegrams in a oilskin wrap and strapping it to her own back, then pointing at the crates and saying, "Burn the boxes, keep the papers. We carry what matters. The rest is lumber."

Ferro hesitated over one crate longer than the others. Inside it, I later learned, was a survey brass cap stamped with the original construction date of the bridge—the physical thumbprint of the men who built it, and therefore of the men who unmade it. He wrapped it in his coat and carried it against his chest like a child.

We went out the back of the camp, single file, down a talus slope that tried its best to break every ankle presented to it. Behind us the shooting started in earnest—not aimed at us yet but at the camp itself, at the tents and the corral and the cold hearth of a place that had been somebody's whole world for five winters. I heard wood splintering and a whoop of triumph from one of Vane's hired men, the particular joy of men destroying things that cannot destroy them back.

"Don't look," Ida said, walking beside me. She wasn't looking herself. Her jaw was set the way it got when she was keeping grief in a locked room.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were. You've got the face for it."

"What face?"

"The face of a man cataloguing debts." She shifted the rifle on her shoulder. "You can settle them later. Right now your job is downhill."

Downhill. If only she knew how literally I intended to take that.

The trail Ruth's people had used for resupply—or rather, for the desperate scavenging that passed for resupply—switchbacked down through pines toward the sound of water. The river had come up hard with the melt; you could hear it before you saw it, a long white roar filling the gorge like a crowd that never stops shouting. When we reached the bottom and I saw it, my stomach performed a small professional assessment and returned its findings: too high to ford, too fast to raft, too wide to jump, and exactly the obstacle a fleeing party wants at its back.

Except.

"There," I said, and pointed.

Tom squinted. "That's a cliff."

"That's a cut. A rail cut. See the bench? Flat shelf, eight feet wide, running along the wall? That's the old grade. They blasted it out of the rock in '81. The rails are probably gone—pulled for scrap somewhere below—but the grade will hold us if the frost heave hasn't chewed it up."

"And if it has?"

"Then we fall in the river and the question resolves itself."

Ruth had come up beside us. She looked at the bench, at the water beneath it, at me. "You've walked this?"

"I've surveyed it. On paper and on foot, before the collapse. I haven't seen it since." I left unsaid the part where I'd helped write the report that closed it, the report whose honest pages had been replaced with forged ones under a director's friendly arm around my shoulder and a threat dressed as a favor. Five years I had kept that knowledge buried deeper than the town. Now I was going to lead nine lives across it.

"Then today you see it again," Ruth said. "Lead."

* * *

The grade held, mostly. It held the way an old promise holds—here and there, with gaps, with sections where the mountain had shrugged and dumped a fan of scree across the bench so that we crossed on hands and knees with the river roaring close enough to wet our boots with spray. I went first, testing each stretch, calling back the words that had once been my daily bread and now felt like a foreign language returning to me: *solid, step wide, loose rock, wait.*

It's strange, the things the body remembers. My feet knew the cant of a rail bed. My eyes knew to read the slope for signs of slide, to note where the cut walls showed fresh fracture versus old weathering, to count the culverts as we passed them—eleven, twelve—and know that the thirteenth would be blocked, because the drainage plan put the big catchment basin at mile marker fourteen and basins fill with debris and nobody maintains what officially isn't there.

At the twelfth culvert I found something that stopped me cold.

A lantern hook. Iron, rusted, bolted into the cut wall beside the culvert mouth. And below it, half-buried in gravel, a spike—driven, not dropped. Driven at an angle, the way track walkers mark a hazard.

"Somebody's been through here," I said. "Recently. Within a year, maybe less."

Ruth crouched, touched the spike, said nothing for a long moment. Then: "The crews that ran the salvage trains. After the collapse. They came up this far."

"No." I turned the spike over. The head was bright where gravel hadn't reached it. "Salvage crews use chalk. This is a walker's mark. Somebody has been maintaining this line in secret. Checking it. Keeping it."

"Why would anyone keep a dead line alive?"

I looked down the gorge toward the gray shape emerging from the mist ahead—the humped silhouette of Deadman's Tunnel, its portal half-collapsed, its mouth breathing cold air like something sleeping. I thought about telegrams ordering a bridge weakened to save costs. About a town evacuated under false pretense and struck from the maps. About a company that had learned, once, how thoroughly a thing could be erased if you controlled the paper.

"Because somebody believed," I said, "that someday the truth would need a way out of these mountains. And they wanted a road ready for it."

Nobody answered that. But Ida squeezed my shoulder once, hard, the way she did instead of agreeing aloud, and we went on.

* * *

Behind us, the pursuit announced itself the way pursuit does: too late and then all at once. A rifle crack echoed off the cut walls, flat and directionless, and a chip of stone jumped near Ferro's boot. Then another, and another, spaced like a man walking his shots up the bench toward us.

"They're on the grade!" Tom shouted. "How—"

"There's a switchback trail on the south wall," I said. "Or they climbed down where we did. Doesn't matter. Run. Single file, stay on the inside edge, don't bunch up—one bullet shouldn't get a bonus."

We ran. Or we did what passes for running on a frost-heaved rail bench above a flood: a stumbling, sliding, cursing scramble, packs banging, breath tearing. Ida ran like a woman chasing a runaway stove. Ruth ran like a woman who had decided the papers on her back were worth more than her lungs and intended to prove it. Ferro ran with the brass cap hugged to his chest and swore continuously in three languages, none of them kindly.

The tunnel portal grew. Twenty yards. Ten. And then I saw what I had been afraid I'd see, and my heart dropped through the floor of me.

The portal was sealed. Not half-collapsed—sealed. Blasted rock, deliberate, a plug of rubble stacked with an engineer's care, topped with a warning post painted the faded ghost of a red that said, in letters you could still read if you already knew what they said: DANGER — CLOSED BY ORDER.

I skidded to a stop. Behind me the file bunched and halted. Ahead, maybe four hundred yards back, our pursuers came on at a jog, unhurried, the unhurriedness of men who have watched a rabbit reach the end of its run.

"It's blocked," Tom said. "Casper, it's blocked."

"It's blocked from the outside." I was already climbing, hands finding holds in the rubble plug, reading it the way you read anything built by human hands—for its laziness. "They sealed the portal because sealing a portal is cheap and visible. Nobody seals the crawlway. The crawlway's inside, behind the first lining wall—maintenance access, thirty inches wide, runs the length of the bore. Every tunnel on this division had one. The plans called them 'inspection galleries.' The men called them rat-runs."

"Thirty inches," Ida said flatly.

"You'll fit."

"I'll fit *angrily.*"

"Then we'll have that in common. Help me move this stone."

We tore at the plug. The outer rocks were heavy but the inner ones had been set dry, without mortar—an engineer's temporary seal, meant to be opened again someday by someone who knew. I found the keystone piece, the one that locks the arch, and levered it with the iron spike from the culvert, and the whole face of the plug sighed and slumped inward, exhaling five years of cold tunnel air into our faces. Air that smelled of stone and standing water and, faintly, impossibly, of lamp oil.

Fresh lamp oil.

Somebody had been in this tunnel. Recently. Somebody had left supplies.

I didn't have time to wonder. Bullets began striking the portal's lip, spalling rock fragments that stung like thrown gravel. I shoved Ruth through first—papers before pride—then Ferro, then the others, counting them through the gap in the dark like cars onto a ferry. Ida went. Tom went, pausing one heartbeat at the threshold as if the tunnel mouth were his uncle's front door and crossing it were a decision. Then I went, last, pulling the loose stones half-closed behind us, and the mountain shut its mouth and the gunfire became a rumor, then a memory, then nothing but our own ragged breathing and the drip of water somewhere ahead in the dark.

* * *

We lit one lamp—the oil lamp, the one somebody had left, and I noted with a chill that the wick had been trimmed. Kept ready. We stood in the bore of Deadman's Tunnel, nine people and a flame, and the dark pressed in around our little circle of light like a congregation.

"How far?" Ruth asked.

"Three-quarters of a mile to the far portal. The crawlway branches at the midpoint—there's a ventilation shaft that comes up in the trees above the line. If the far portal's watched, we take the shaft."

"And if the shaft's blocked?"

"Then we're having a different conversation."

We started forward, Ida carrying the lamp, me navigating by a memory five years old and a hand trailing the tunnel wall, counting expansion joints in the lining the way a blind man counts fence posts. Forty joints to the hundred. At joint sixty-three the crawlway mouth opened on our left, a black slot in the brickwork barely wider than a coffin laid sideways, and beside it, bolted to the wall, a tin box.

I opened it. Inside: candles, matches, a coil of rope, and a folded paper gone soft with damp. I unfolded it by lamplight. It was a page torn from an inspection ledger—the old format, pre-collapse—and across the top, in a hand I recognized with a jolt that went through me like current, someone had written:

*For whoever comes after. The line is sound. The truth is heavier than the bridge. Carry both. — E.R.*

E.R. Elias Rourke. Ida's brother. The inspector whose name was on the honest version of the report, the version that got buried along with the town and the man himself.

Ida read it over my shoulder. For a long time she didn't move. Then she took the paper from my hands with a gentleness I had never seen in her, folded it small, and put it inside her shirt, over her heart, where she kept everything that mattered.

"He knew," she said. Her voice was steady, which somehow made it worse. "All this time, he knew, and he kept the road open anyway. Waiting for somebody to use it."

"Well," I said, and found my voice had gone rough. "He waited for us."

Behind us, faint through the stone, came a new sound: the crack and tumble of rock, voices echoing up the bore. Vane's men had reached the portal and were opening it—slowly, clumsily, without an engineer's touch.

Ahead of us, the tunnel ran on into the dark, toward the river, toward the valley, toward whatever waited at the far end of a dead man's road.

"Move," I said. "And everybody—stay close to the person in front of you. In this tunnel, family is measured in arm's lengths."

We went.
