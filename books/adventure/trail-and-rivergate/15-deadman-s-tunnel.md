## 15. Deadman's Tunnel

There is a particular sound a mountain makes when it decides to kill you. Most people never hear it, and I used to count that among my blessings. It isn't an explosion, exactly. It's quieter than that—a deep settling groan, like a giant rolling over in his sleep, followed by the small, almost polite patter of rockfall somewhere above you. Then the dark gets darker.

I heard it at roughly four o'clock on our second day of flight down the gorge, just as we reached the mouth of Deadman's Tunnel.

"Inside," I said. "All of you. Now."

Nobody argued. When a man who has spent fifteen years listening to iron and stone for a living starts shouting, even mules pay attention—though the mule, I'm sorry to report, was the one thing we couldn't fit through the entrance, and Ida had to tie her to a dead pine with a knot I taught her an hour earlier and which she executed better than I would have.

The tunnel swallowed us. It was bored through granite in '81, back when the company still pretended to care about its men, and it ran nearly half a mile under the shoulder of Mount Corvair before daylight again. We had perhaps forty feet of it, lit by two lanterns and the gray memory of the sky behind us, when we heard the horses.

Vane's men. Six of them, maybe seven, riding hard along the old rail bed we'd been following since dawn.

"They'll seal us in," Ruth said. She had the document crate strapped to her back like a mother carrying twins, and she hadn't set it down in thirty hours. "They brought powder. I saw it on the pack horse."

"They'd collapse the whole tunnel," Tom said. "With us inside."

"With us inside," his uncle would say, "there are no witnesses to anything ever again." Tom didn't say it aloud, but we all heard it anyway. He'd been wearing that sentence around his neck since the night he confessed on the mountainside, and it had aged him ten years in four days.

I held up a hand and listened. The hoofbeats split—one group continuing past the tunnel mouth toward the far end, where the line emerged from the mountain above the gorge. Of course. Vane knew this railway too; he'd bought enough of it. You don't need to be an engineer to understand that a tunnel has two doors.

"We're rats in a box," said Hollis, one of our guides—the younger one, quick and cheerful in the way of men who haven't yet been properly introduced to tragedy. He'd broken his arm in the whiteout and set it himself with a stick and a bootlace, laughing while he did it. I liked him enormously. Remember that. It matters in a minute.

"Not quite a box," I said.

* * *

Here is something they don't put in the newspapers: every tunnel on the Corvair line was built with a maintenance crawlway. A secondary bore, eighteen inches wider than a man's shoulders, running parallel to the main tube, connected by cross-adits every hundred yards or so. Its purpose was drainage inspection—water is the slow assassin of any tunnel, seeping, freezing, cracking—and its existence was recorded in exactly one place: the engineering folios kept in the division office at Rivergate Station. The same office that burned, conveniently, the winter after the bridge came down.

I had crawled those folios as a young man with more ambition than sense. I had walked this crawlway twice, once with a lamp and once without, when my lamp died and I learned what the dark inside a mountain actually tastes like. Cold iron and old water and your own fear, which is mostly iron too.

"There's a service bore behind the north wall," I told them. "Forty yards in, there's a cross-passage. The access hatch is hidden behind a rubble pile the company never bothered to clear because clearing it cost money and hiding it cost nothing."

"You're certain?" Ruth asked.

"I'm certain of the rubble. Whether the hatch still opens is a question God will answer presently."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we'll have a long conversation about alternatives."

Hollis grinned in the lantern light. "Engineers. You people plan funerals with contingency options."

"Somebody has to," Ida muttered. "You'd all just wing it and end up in the ground facing the wrong direction."

We moved. Behind us, faint through the stone, came the sounds of industry—men dismounting, men talking, men handling crates with the particular care of men handling explosives. At the far end, presumably, their brothers were doing the same. Vane wasn't sealing a tomb. He was building a coffin with a lid at each end, and we were lying down in it voluntarily.

Forty yards in, by my count—and my count was good, my count had always been good, it was one of the few things about myself I'd never had reason to doubt—I found the rubble. Granite blocks the size of bread loaves, spilled from some ancient roof fall, stacked against the wall in a slope. I dug into it with my hands, then with the pry bar I'd carried since the washout, and Ida and Tom hauled stones away as I loosened them, and in twenty minutes that felt like twenty years we uncovered a steel plate with a ring handle gone green with age.

It opened. I could have kissed it, and I'm not a man who kisses things.

Beyond lay the crawlway: low, dry, utterly black. Our two lanterns made small warm islands in it. The air was stale but breathable, which meant it connected to somewhere, which meant the mountain had kept its old promises even if the company hadn't.

"Ladies first," I said, because gallantry costs nothing in a hole in the ground, and Ruth went past me with the crate scraping stone, and Ida went, and the photographer—Miles, a boy who'd taken eleven pictures since leaving town and complained about the weight of his plates in every one of them—went, and Tom went, and then Hollis paused beside me at the cross-adit and looked back down the main tunnel toward the light.

"How long do we have," he said. Not a question. A measurement.

"Minutes. They'll want to place the charges carefully. Blowing a tunnel wrong just makes a bigger door."

"Right." He handed me his rifle. His good arm was splinted and strapped across his chest; the rifle had been slung over it, useless to him for days, and I realized I'd stopped seeing it there, the way you stop seeing a fence post. "Take this. And take my hat off the peg of your memory sometime, Halloran. It was a good hat."

"Hollis."

"Someone should tell them at the other end that the party's cancelled." He was already moving back toward the tunnel mouth, favoring his bad arm, moving light on his feet the way he did everything. "They'll hear shooting and think we broke out that way. They'll come running. That buys you the time to get everyone up the crawlway and out the ventilation shaft at the halfway mark—you remember the shaft, don't you? Course you do. You remember everything. Annoying man."

I grabbed for him and caught cloth and lost it. He was fast. He was so fast, and so cheerful, and he went down that tunnel toward armed men with a busted arm and no rifle and a grin I could still see in the dark after the dark should have erased it.

The first shot came before I'd finished standing there like a fool. Then several more, and shouting, and the beautiful chaos of men who expected a trapped rabbit finding instead a hornet. Hollis was making noise like five men. He'd found a signal lantern somewhere, or thrown rocks, or simply yelled—God knows—he yelled like a man announcing a train arrival, and boots thundered, and the shooting got confused and scattered, moving away from us, away, away—

Then one shot, alone, distinct. The kind of shot that ends a sentence.

The silence afterward was worse than any rockfall.

* * *

I don't remember deciding to move. I remember the crawlway: the scrape of the crate on stone ahead of me, Ida's breathing, the way the lantern flame leaned when we passed the ventilation shaft and I felt, absurdly, a thread of clean cold air from the world above, threading down to us like a lifeline thrown from a ship we couldn't see. We climbed. There were iron rungs in the shaft, rusted but honest, and we climbed them single file in the black with our hearts going like pistons, and nobody spoke, because speaking would have required acknowledging what the single shot had said, and none of us were ready to say it out loud.

The shaft let out through a grating on the mountainside, half-hidden in scrub pine, fifty feet above the rail bed and three hundred above the river. We came out of the earth like the dead climbing out of their own graves, blinking in afternoon light that felt obscene—how dare the sun be shining—and I stood aside and counted them through. Ruth. Crate. Ida. Miles. Tom. All present.

Not all present.

"He's not—" Miles started.

"I know," I said.

"We can't just leave him down there."

"We're not leaving him down there. We're leaving him *there*, which is different, though I know it doesn't feel different, and I'm sorry, son, because it should feel different and someday it will, and that day will be worse." I was talking too much. I talk too much when something breaks in me; it's how the crack whistles. "Right now we go down the mountain, because everything Hollis just bought with—"

I couldn't finish it. Ida took my elbow, hard, her fingers like coal tongs.

"Say it plain," she said. "He bought it with his life. Don't you dare soften it for our sake. He'd hate soft."

"He bought it with his life," I said. "So we spend it well."

Below us, at the tunnel mouths, smoke was rising. They'd blown both ends anyway—of course they had; Vane's orders would not tolerate interpretation—and the mountain shrugged off the insult with a rumble we felt through our boot soles. Somewhere under all that fresh stone lay the main tunnel, and the cross-adit, and the rubble pile we'd dug with our bare hands, and Hollis.

Ida took off her hat. The others, seeing her, did the same, there on the scree slope with the wind pulling at us. Even Tom, whose griefs were all still new and misshapen, bared his head.

"He set his own arm with a bootlace," Ida said. "Tell that story. Tell it true. That's the grave marker he gets."

"That's the one," I agreed, and put my hat back on, because the dead don't need hats and the living do, and we had a river to cross and a station full of trouble waiting at the bottom of the gorge, and Hollis had paid for our passage in the only coin that can't be counterfeited.

Ruth shifted the crate on her back and looked east, down the gorge, where the rails ran toward Rivergate Station and whatever Aldous Vane had arranged for us there.

"He knew the risk," she said quietly. "We all did, from the camp onward. But knowing a thing and surviving it are different animals." Her hand rested flat on the crate lid, over the telegrams and the reports and the names of the dead. "Let's make sure it was worth what it cost."

We started down. The mountain closed its mouth behind us and said nothing, and neither did I, but I carried Hollis's rifle the rest of the way down the gorge, and I carried it into the next chapter of this business, which was waiting for us at the ruined station below—with a warrant bearing my name, and Aldous Vane smiling beneath it like a man who believes he has already won.

He was about to learn what Hollis already knew: that the price of a thing only tells you what it cost, never what it's worth.
