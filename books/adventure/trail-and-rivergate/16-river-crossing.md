## 16. River Crossing

The river had been a rumor for two days before we saw it.

We heard it first—a low, continuous grumble under the birdsong, like a argument held in another room that everyone pretends not to hear. Then the ground began to tremble faintly when we walked, and Ida said what we were all thinking: "That's no creek."

By the time we came down through the alder scrub to the bank, the rumor had become a fact with teeth. The Rivergate in spring melt was not so much water as a brown, muscular animal, hauling whole trees along in its grip like a dog shaking a stick. It had swallowed its own banks overnight; willows stood waist-deep, their tops combing the current. Where the old rail grade met the water there had once been a ford—Ruth's map showed it, a neat pencil X labeled *low water, Aug.* It was August nowhere in sight.

"Well," Ida said. "That's put a crimp in it."

Behind us, somewhere up the gorge, Vane's men were doing arithmetic of their own. We'd gained perhaps half a day in Deadman's Tunnel, and paid for it with Halvorsen, whose body we'd carried as far as the ground allowed and buried under a cairn of rail spikes and stone because there was nothing else to give him. Half a day. It would not last. Men who have been ordered to retrieve documents do not stop to admire scenery.

I stood at the bank and did the engineer's arithmetic instead. Current speed—I threw a stick in and counted: gone around the bend in nine seconds, which meant better than fifteen feet a second, which meant swimming was a joke someone else could tell. Width: maybe sixty feet to the gravel bar on the far side, where the trail resumed toward Rivergate Station. Depth: unknowable, but the willows told me at least four feet and rising.

"Raft," Ruth said beside me. Not a question.

"Tunnel timbers," I agreed. "There's a pile of them back at the crawlway mouth—bridge stringers, cut for repair work that never happened." I didn't say the rest out loud, but everyone heard it anyway: cut for repairs to a bridge whose piers had been sawed through on purpose. Everything in this valley was salvage from a crime. We were going to float across a river on the bones of it.

* * *

We worked through the afternoon, and I will say this for terror: it organizes people wonderfully.

The timbers were twelve-foot creosoted stringers, heavy as sin but light enough that six people and a mule named President—who had opinions about everything and endorsed none of our plans—could drag them down the game trail one at a time. Tom worked like a man trying to outrun something. He'd been quiet since the confession at camp, since he'd stood in front of all of us and said *my uncle sent me to steal the map and let the guides take the blame*, and quiet suited him badly. He hauled timber the way other men throw punches.

Ida, meanwhile, had appointed herself quartermaster of the documents. She wrapped the oilcloth packets in her spare shirt, then in the tent fly, then in a groundsheet, layering them like she was packing a lunch for a child crossing a war. Her brother's telegrams were in there. Her brother's name was in there. I caught her once, kneeling by the pile, just resting her hand flat on the bundles—not praying exactly, more like checking a pulse.

"They'll get wet," she said, without looking up. It wasn't a question either.

"We'll build the raft high," I said. "Deck above the logs. They ride dry or we don't cross."

She nodded and kept her hand where it was a moment longer.

Ruth's crew contributed what they had: four survivors who looked like winter had been chewing on them since October, but whose hands remembered rope. One of them, a wiry man called Edey, turned out to have rafted lumber on the Skeena before the railroad ate his life, and he took one look at my lashing plan and improved it. That's the thing about engineers—I know this will wound my pride, but it's true—we are only as good as the people willing to tell us we're wrong. Edey told me I was wrong about three times before noon, and the raft was better for every one of her.

By late afternoon she stood on the bank, ugly and beautiful: eight stringers crossed by five, pegged with driftwood pins and bound with every foot of rope we owned, a deck of split planking raised on cross-blocks amidships, a steering sweep fore and aft. It looked like something a flood had built by accident and then regretted.

"It'll hold," I said, mostly to myself.

"That's what worries me," Ida said. "You sound surprised."

* * *

We loaded at dusk-light, which is to say we loaded in a hurry. The plan was simple, and simple plans are the only kind that survive contact with a river: cargo and the weakest in the middle, polemen at the bow, me and Edey on the sweeps, aim for the gravel bar, accept God's corrections.

President the mule declined the raft on principle. We swam him loose upstream with a lead rope paid out long, trusting the current to slam him against the far bank somewhere in the general neighborhood of useful. He swam like a piano being insulted, but he swam.

Then it was us.

The first ten feet were easy, which should have warned me. Rivers are like confidence men that way—they let you feel clever right up until your pockets are empty. We hit the main channel and the world tilted. The current took the raft by the throat and swung it downstream, and suddenly the gravel bar wasn't ahead of us, it was beside us and receding, and the far bank was a rumor again.

"Pole!" Edey bellowed, and the polemen drove down and found bottom and held, and the whole raft groaned like a ship of the line, and for one white moment we hung there—sixteen human beings and a dead man's truth balanced on eight sticks of stolen timber over fifteen feet a second of snowmelt—

—and then the near poleman's footing slipped, the pole skated off a boulder, and we spun.

I've been in wrecks. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is the currency I'm trying to earn back these days. I've felt a locomotive come apart under me, felt the specific silence between the first crack and the first scream. This was that silence, stretched across water. The raft went broadside. A wave came over the deck. And the documents—the crates lashed midships, Ida's shirts and tent fly and groundsheet, her brother's name in three layers of oilcloth—took the wave full in the face, and the lashing, soaked and frozen and re-soaked for two days, gave.

Not all at once. One knot. Then the corner of the groundsheet lifted like a wing.

Ida moved before anyone shouted. Later I would replay it a hundred times—how there was no decision in it, how she simply went, the way water goes downhill. She lunged across the slick deck, got both arms around the bundle as it slid, and the raft rolled under her weight, and the river reached up and took her the way it takes everything: completely, without malice, without even noticing.

The last I saw of her above water was her fist, closed white-knuckled around the oilcloth, going under between two waves.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about watching someone drown: time turns to syrup. I was moving—everyone was moving—but the river ran faster than any of us. Tom was nearest. Tom, who had spent a week being his uncle's instrument, who had confessed sabotage with his eyes fixed on his boots, who had every reason in the world to stand very still and let the water settle accounts.

He went in without dropping his coat.

I got the spare sweep lash-line free and paid it out after them, Edey hauling the bow polemen to swing the raft edgewise into the slack below the bar, and all of it happened in the space of three heartbeats that each lasted a year. The river folded Ida under once. Twice. The second time she stayed down long enough that my mind, unhelpfully, began drafting the sentence I would have to say to no one, because Ida had no one left except us, and we were all standing on a raft.

Then Tom surfaced, gasping, one arm locked around a collar of wet wool, and struck for the gravel bar with a stroke that was ugly and furious and effective, dragging her, kicking against the pull of the channel, swearing at the river in words I didn't know a Vane knew.

The line reached them. Hands hauled. And we pulled them both up onto the gravel like netted fish—Tom coughing, Ida not breathing, her fist still locked around the bundle so hard that Edey had to work her fingers open finger by finger to get the documents out dry while Ruth knelt and did the brutal arithmetic of breath and compression that rivers force on the living.

On the fourth try, Ida vomited half the Rivergate onto the stones and said, hoarsely, to no one in particular:

"The papers. Tell me the papers—"

"You absolute fool," Ruth said, and her voice cracked clean through the middle of it. "They're fine. You're fine. You *idiot*."

Ida lay back on the gravel and laughed, a wet, ragged, astonishing sound, and Tom sat down beside her as if his legs had been repossessed, and after a moment Ida put her hand on the back of his neck—just briefly, the way you'd steady a lamp in wind—and said, "Good lad."

Tom stared out at the river. His uncle's bloodline was somewhere behind us in the gorge, armed and closing, and I watched him understand, publicly and permanently, which side of the water he lived on now.

"I couldn't watch another one go under," he said, to the river, not to us. "Not after Halvorsen."

Nobody made a speech. There wasn't time, and besides, speeches are for people who haven't just proven themselves. Ruth squeezed his shoulder once, hard, and went back to counting heads.

* * *

We lost the night to shivering and small fires and Ida wrapped in every dry thing we owned, drinking coffee that Edey made strong enough to trot a horse across. The documents had survived—of course they had; Ida had packed them like a woman packing her brother's soul—and they steamed gently by the fire while Ruth turned the pages to keep them from molding, reading snatches aloud now and then in a low voice, like a vigil.

*"Per instruction of A. Vane, reduce pier loading to schedule. Do not file variance."*

*"Passengers to be advised of track inspection ahead. Town to be cleared by morning service."*

A town cleared by morning service. A bridge weakened to schedule. I had spent five years hiding from a report I forged under duress, believing my small sin was the sin. It was a splinter. This was the tree.

Toward midnight Tom came and sat next to me at the fire's edge. For a while neither of us said anything, which is the traditional language of men.

"She'd have drowned reaching for those papers," he said finally. "And I'd have watched, a month ago. I want you to know I know that."

"I know it," I said.

"He'll be at Rivergate Station." Tom fed a stick to the fire. "It's the only place the trail funnels. He thinks like an engineer, you know. That's the worst part—he's good. He'll have a warrant with your name on it. Your real name." He glanced sideways at me. "Halloran."

The name landed in my chest like a dropped coupling. I hadn't heard it spoken aloud in five years. On the fire, the documents steamed and curled at their edges, patient as scripture.

"Then we'd best not keep him waiting," I said, "before he gets comfortable."

Downstream, around the bend, the river ran dark and fast toward the ruin of the station, carrying our scent ahead of us like a promise.
