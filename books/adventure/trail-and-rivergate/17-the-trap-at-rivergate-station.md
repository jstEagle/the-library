## 17. The Trap at Rivergate Station

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in a ruined railway station. It isn't the silence of empty places—empty places hum a little, with wind and mice and the memory of timetables. No, Rivergate Station had been *stopped*, the way a clock is stopped, and everything inside it was still holding the pose it froze in: a bench facing a wall that no longer had a window in it, a ticket grille rusted open like a mouth mid-sentence, a departure board with chalk ghosts of trains that would never be announced again.

We came down out of the gorge at first light, eleven of us and what remained of the documents, and I felt that silence before I saw the station. Felt it the way you feel a change in the rails under your boots—a vibration gone wrong.

"Something's off," I said, and put up my hand.

Ruth Okafor came up beside me, rifle slung across her back, her eyes doing that thing they did where they read country faster than most men read print. "The birds," she said quietly.

I'd heard it too. Or rather, hadn't heard it. The willows along the river were full of birds and none of them were singing. A hundred yards ahead, the station sat in its clearing with its broken platform reaching toward us like a dock for ships that had all drowned.

"Could be nothing," said Ida behind me. She said it the way people say things when they don't believe them, which is to say she said it flatly, while tightening the straps on the pack that held her brother's name and every page we'd pulled out of the river gorge.

"It's not nothing," Tom Vane said. He'd changed since the mules scattered and the truth about his uncle came out of him like a splinter working loose. He stood straighter now, which surprised me; I'd have expected guilt to bend a man. Instead he looked like someone who had finally been given permission to carry his own weight. "He knew the station was the only crossing point above the falls. He'll have known since before we did."

"He's your uncle," said one of the guides—Marek, the quiet one, the one who'd lost his partner in Deadman's Tunnel and hadn't spoken more than ten words since.

Tom took that hit without flinching. "Yes. And he's waiting for us."

* * *

We held a council of war in the shadow of an overturned water tower, which is a grand phrase for six exhausted people crouching in the mud arguing in whispers while four half-starved survivors of the Rivergate crew watched us with the patience of people who had already survived worse than anything we could propose.

The options were few and ugly. Go around the falls meant two days through country with no trail and no food, carrying wounded and documents, with Vane's men somewhere behind us moving fast on horses we didn't have. Go through meant walking into whatever he'd built at the station. Wait meant starving on principle.

"There's a third way," I said finally. "The old maintenance road. It comes down on the far side of the station yard, behind the freight shed. If we're careful, we can cross the trestle spur below the falls without ever showing ourselves in the open."

"The trestle," Ruth said. Not a question. She knew which trestle I meant. The spur bridge over the river narrows—the last piece of the original line still standing, the one piece the company never bothered to demolish because officially, according to every ledger and every newspaper, there was nothing left at Rivergate worth a bridge.

"It'll hold," I said. "I've read its inspection reports. The real ones."

Nobody laughed. It wasn't a joke, exactly, but it was near enough to one that under other circumstances Ida might have snorted. Instead she just looked at me with those level eyes and said, "And if Vane's thought of the maintenance road too?"

"Then we'll know soon enough."

That was the whole of my strategy, and I want it on the record that I said it with confidence, because confidence is free and everything else on that mountain cost something.

* * *

The maintenance road was more memory than path, but my feet remembered it even after five years, and I led them down through the pines single file, past the rusted skeleton of a snowplow car that some dispatcher had abandoned where it stood, as though the whole railroad had simply lain down one winter and refused to get up.

We came out behind the freight shed just as the sun cleared the eastern ridge, and that was when I understood how completely I had been anticipated.

They had horses. Six men mounted, four more standing among the ruins of the platform with rifles held easy—not aimed, not yet, because aiming comes later, after the trap has finished closing. And in front of them all, seated on a camp chair that someone had carried up from God knows where and placed precisely at the edge of the platform as if he were waiting for a train, sat Aldous Vane.

He'd aged well, considering. Silver at the temples now, a heavier coat than the rest of us could dream of, gloves of soft leather. He looked less like a man camping in a ruin than a man inspecting a property he intended to develop.

"Mr. Halloran," he called pleasantly. "Or should I say—" and here he paused, savoring it, a man unwrapping a gift he'd bought himself years ago, "—Mr. Elias Crane? Late of the Cascade & Northern. Late, in fact, of everywhere."

My blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the morning.

Because that name—that name I had buried so deep I sometimes went weeks without remembering it belonged to me—and he said it aloud, in front of all of them, in the clear mountain air, and the sound of it carried.

"Casper?" Ida said beside me. Not accusing. Just asking. Just wanting me to tell her it wasn't true.

"It was a long time ago," I said, which was not a denial, and we both knew it.

Vane rose from his chair, unhurried, and produced a document from inside his coat with the flourish of a magician producing a dove. He unfolded it and held it up.

"A warrant," he announced, loud enough for everyone. "Issued three days ago by Judge Hobb in Fort Meridian, on sworn testimony regarding the murder of an inspection clerk named Daniel Pruitt, eleven years past. The murderer's name is Elias Crane. Also known, these days, as Casper Halloran." He smiled at me across the ruined yard. "You see the difficulty, Mr. Crane. The law has been looking for you for a very long time. I merely helped it remember."

"That report was forged," I heard myself say. "Pruitt wasn't murdered—he died in the washout with forty other men, and the only reason anyone thinks otherwise is because you needed a story that kept the blame on the engineers."

"Then you'll welcome the chance to explain it to a judge." Vane spread his hands, magnanimous as a bishop. "I'm not a monster, Mr. Halloran. Come quietly. Surrender the documents my nephew stole from me, and I give you my word the court will hear your side."

"And the word of Aldous Vane is worth exactly what the forty-one dead of Rivergate got from him," Ruth said. Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about it—it came out level and cold, like water over stone. "Nothing."

Vane turned to her, and for just a moment the civility thinned and I saw the thing underneath, the thing that had ordered a bridge weakened to save costs and a town emptied on a lie. "Mrs. Okafor. You are harboring stolen corporate property and a fugitive murderer. Whatever you imagine you've found in that gorge, it changes nothing. The company's claims are registered. The land is mine. The dead are dead. Bring me the crate, and I'll let you walk away rich."

"You think this is about money," Ruth said.

"I know it is. It always is. Everyone has a price; yours is simply higher because you've suffered more." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. He said it the way a man reads a balance sheet.

Behind me, I heard the survivors shifting, and Marek's rifle coming slowly off his shoulder, and I put my hand back without turning around. "No," I murmured. "Not here. There are too many of them and we're in the open. Anyone fires, we lose people we can't afford to lose."

"And if we don't fire?" Ida asked. "What's his plan for us?"

I looked at the eight riflemen and the six horsemen and the narrow ground between the freight shed and the river, and I did the arithmetic I'd been doing since the moment I saw the camp chair.

"He doesn't need a plan," I said. "He needs a warrant and witnesses. We surrender or we run, and either way he wins the hour."

* * *

But here is the thing about despair, which I learned that morning standing in the ruin of a town that had been erased to protect powerful men: despair is a luxury, and luxuries are for people with time.

We didn't have time. So instead of despairing, we did what railroaders do when the schedule collapses, which is improvise.

While Vane talked—talked being his weapon of choice, talk and paper—I was watching something else. I was watching the four riflemen nearest the freight shed, and I was watching their eyes. They weren't looking at us. They were looking at Vane, checking, the way dogs check their master before committing to a fight. Men hired recently. Men who had heard, perhaps, the rumors already moving down the valley—because Ruth had wired that journalist three weeks ago from the camp, and wires travel faster than warrants, and newspapers travel faster than both.

"Ida," I breathed. "How much coffee do we have left?"

She gave me a look that suggested I'd lost my mind, then answered anyway, because that was our arrangement. "Half a pound. Why?"

"Because in about ninety seconds I'm going to need these men to think we're crazier than they are."

I stepped out from behind the freight shed into the open, alone, hands visible, and walked toward the platform with the unhurried gait of a man arriving for a scheduled appointment. Behind me I heard Ida swear softly in Norwegian, and Ruth's low voice saying *let him*, and Tom starting forward until Marek caught his arm.

"Mr. Vane," I called. "Before you hand me to the law, there's something you ought to see. It concerns your investment."

His eyes narrowed, but curiosity is the one appetite that survives every age, and he gestured me closer with two fingers.

I stopped ten feet from the platform, close enough to speak without shouting, far enough that grabbing me would look like what it was. And I raised my voice—not to him, but to the fourteen armed men around him.

"Gentlemen! Before anyone earns today's wage, ask yourselves what the wage is for. Because I've seen what's in the crate this man wants. Company telegrams, signed by directors, ordering the Rivergate span built light to save costs. Orders to evacuate the town on a false quarantine so no one would ask why the bridge failed. And a list of names—forty-one names—of men the company reported as *deserted* so their families couldn't claim pensions." I let that sit. "One of those names belongs to a man standing right behind me. Ida Sorensen's brother. Reported a deserter. Died holding a lantern on the trestle trying to warn the night crew."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight.

Vane's face didn't move, but his gloved hand closed slightly on the warrant.

"Storytelling," he said softly. "The oldest trick of the guilty."

"Maybe," I agreed. "But here's the part that isn't a story, Mr. Vane. Your journalist already has copies. Wired out three weeks ago. The *Meridian Courier* ran the first column yesterday—you may not have seen it, riding up here in your fine coat, but your men's families read the paper. Ask them. Ask any one of them whether their wives have been asking questions about Rivergate."

I was guessing. I'll admit it freely—I was guessing about the timing, guessing about the reach of Ruth's wire, betting eleven lives on the speed of the territorial mails. But I watched the nearest rifleman glance sideways at his neighbor, and I watched the neighbor not quite meet his eye, and I knew the bet had paid.

Because that is the thing Vane never understood, for all his ledgers: fear is cheap currency, and it inflates. Every man he'd hired had come for wages, and wages buy bread, and bread feeds families, and families read newspapers. You cannot pay a man enough to be remembered forever as the thug who helped bury a town. Vane thought he owned the law. He did. But the law is only ever as strong as the men willing to enforce it, and I could see, plain as frost on a rail, that his strength was melting.

"Take them," Vane said, and his voice cracked just slightly on the second word, and nobody moved.

"Sir," said the senior rifleman carefully, "the warrant's for the one man. Nobody said anything about shooting women."

"They have rifles—"

"They have rifles and we have a reputation," the man said, "and as of this morning we also have a newspaper."

For a long moment, Aldous Vane stood alone on the platform of the town he had erased, holding a piece of paper that was suddenly worth no more than the chalk ghosts on the departure board behind him. And I watched him understand it—watched the comprehension arrive like a train he hadn't scheduled—and I confess that some small, ugly, honest part of me enjoyed it.

But only a small part. Because a cornered man with money and hatred does not surrender. He escalates.

Vane folded the warrant with great care, returned it to his coat, and smiled at me—a smile with nothing behind it but arithmetic.

"Well played, Mr. Crane," he said. "Truly. But you've made one error. You've told me exactly where you intend to go next, and you've spent your only surprise." He turned to his men. "Mount up. We'll meet them at the trestle."

And as he swung onto his horse, he looked back down at me once, almost kindly.

"Do hurry, Halloran. The river's high with the melt, and that old span has been standing five years without an inspection." His smile widened. "You of all people know how bridges fail."

* * *

They rode out eastward along the valley floor, taking the long way around, and we stood in the ruins and listened to the hoofbeats fade and understood, all of us at once, what he meant.

The trestle over the river narrows. The only crossing. The one structure between us and the courthouse at Fort Meridian, between us and the journalist, between the dead of Rivergate and the justice they'd waited five years for.

"He'll burn it," Tom said hoarsely. "If he can't stop us crossing, he'll cut it down behind us—or in front of us. He doesn't care anymore. He's past caring."

"No," I said. My voice came out strange and calm, the voice I used to use reading gauge readings in bad weather. "He won't burn it. Burning draws attention. He'll do what the company taught him to do." I looked up the valley, toward the black line of the old span against the morning sky, and felt the cold settle into my bones where it would stay, I suspected, for a long time.

"He'll weaken it. Quietly. A few cuts, a few bolts drawn, in the dark tonight. And tomorrow morning a bridge inspector's report will note that the structure collapsed under the weight of fugitives fleeing justice." I turned to face them—Ida, Ruth, Tom, Marek, the survivors, all of them watching me with the same question in their eyes. "He's going to make me watch it happen again. Forty-one dead at Rivergate, and he means to add us to the list using the same method."

"So we don't cross," Ida said.

"We cross," Ruth said, at the same moment, and we looked at each other.

"We cross," I confirmed. "Tonight, before he's ready. And we cross it my way—with every man roped, spaced at intervals, and me going first to read the iron." I picked up my pack. The documents inside it weighed nothing and everything. "Because there's one thing Aldous Vane has forgotten, in all his years of buying reports instead of writing them."

"What's that?" Tom asked.

"I wrote the last real inspection of that trestle myself," I said. "Eleven years ago. And I remember every inch of it—including the parts that were weak *before* he ever touched it."

Above the valley, the morning sun caught the old span, and it gleamed like a blade laid flat across the river, waiting to see which of us it would take.
