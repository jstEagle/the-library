## 18. Turning the Evidence

The morning after the trap was sprung, I woke with Tom's confession sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone, and the smell of Ida's coffee doing its best to talk me out of despair. It nearly worked. Ida's coffee had that kind of authority.

We were holding the lower camp now—the survivors' camp, the one Ruth Okafor had defended with a rifle and four days of silence before she let us past her picket line. Vane's men were somewhere above us in the gorge, and Vane himself was presumably composing offers, threats, or both on the same sheet of paper, because that was how Aldous Vane did business: the pen never changed hands, only the ink.

I'd just gotten my boots on when Ruth came out of the document tent carrying a telegram form like it was a live rattlesnake she'd grown fond of.

"Casper," she said. "You're going to want to sit down for this."

"I've been sitting down for this since Mile Forty-Three," I said. "Hit me anyway."

She held up the paper. The carbon copy of a wire, dated nineteen days earlier.

*TO E. MARSH, TERRITORIAL LEDGER, DENVER. HAVE IN POSSESSION COMPANY TELEGRAMS RE RIVERGATE BRIDGE AND TOWN EVACUATION. STOP. NAMES, DATES, SIGNATURES. STOP. PUBLISH UPON RECEIPT OF SECOND CONFIRMATION FROM ENGINEER ON SITE. STOP.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, because my eyes kept snagging on the last clause like a boot heel on a rail joint.

"Engineer on site," I said slowly. "Ruth. What engineer?"

"The one the dead man promised us," she said. "Halloran. Casper Halloran, licensed, formerly of the Rivergate district office." She looked at me with those level dark eyes that made you feel like a specimen under glass. "Ezra told me about you before he died. He said if a man came over the pass who walked like he was listening to the rails, that would be him. He said you'd come because you couldn't help it."

"Ezra," I repeated. The prospector. The gut-shot man who'd pressed a map into my hands and a promise into my conscience and then had the poor manners to die before explaining either. "He wired you from where? There's no line within sixty miles of here."

"Two years ago we ran a repair spur off the old telegraph trunk at the washout. Company line, abandoned but not cut. Ezra was an operator before he was a prospector—did you know that? Neither did his crew, until we needed him to be." She almost smiled. "I've been sending confirmations every week when the weather allowed. Marsh has been waiting on one thing to print."

"On what thing?" I asked, though I already knew, and my stomach knew it too, dropping like a car going over an uncoupled grade.

"Your name," Ruth said. "Your testimony. Your signature on the original inspection reports—the real ones, the ones they buried with the town. You certified that bridge, Casper. Which means either you lied, or your report was forged. Either way, you're the confirmation. You're the second witness the story needs to stand up under a lawyer's attack."

The camp noise went distant. I heard the river, and I heard five years ago, and both at once.

"You could have asked me," I said finally.

"I'm asking you now." She held out a pencil along with the carbon. "Marsh's last wire said publication is set for Friday unless countermanded. Today is Wednesday. All I need is your statement, in your hand, wired from the same spur. And Casper—" her voice dropped into something gentler than I'd yet heard from her, which was somehow worse than her rifle voice, "—it can't be an alias. It has to be Halloran. The man whose name is on the reports."

There it was. The thing I'd run from across two territories and three names. Sitting on a telegram form in the snow, waiting for me to pick it up.

I looked at the pencil for a long time.

"Give me an hour," I said.

* * *

Ida found me at the telegraph spur, which wasn't hard, since there was nowhere else to go and I was standing in plain sight staring at a length of copper wire as though it might apologize to me.

"You look like a man deciding whether to jump or climb," she said, handing me a tin cup without being asked.

"Is there a difference anymore?"

"There's always a difference. Jumping's quicker." She settled onto a crate beside me, her bulk making the crate complain. "Ruth told you what she wants."

"She told you?"

"Ruth tells everybody everything eventually. It's her way of loading a weapon in advance." Ida blew on her coffee. "My brother Daniel was a brakeman on the Rivergate local. Did I ever say that out loud before? I don't think I have. Twenty-two years I've carried it around like a stone in my boot, and I only started saying it out loud three weeks ago, to a woman I met at gunpoint." She snorted. "Turns out it weighs less spoken."

"What are you telling me, Ida?"

"I'm telling you I know what it costs to keep quiet. I did it for twenty-two years. Every year it cost more and bought less." She looked at me sideways. "And I'm telling you that whatever name you sign, you already signed it once. In the snow, to a dying man. Bring my crew out alive. You think that promise didn't have your real name attached? Names are just what other people call you. Promises are what you are."

I drank the coffee. It was terrible and perfect, like most true things.

"If I wire that statement," I said, "Vane doesn't just lose the documents. He loses the frame. A fugitive murderer can't testify. But Casper Halloran, inspection engineer, whose forged report is a matter of public record—" I laughed, and it came out bitter. "He'll dust off the warrant by sundown. He probably already has it. That warrant's been folded in his coat pocket since Denver, I'd bet the mule on it."

"Probably," Ida agreed comfortably. "So the question isn't whether it gets bad. It's whether it gets bad while you're still a nobody, or while you're somebody the whole territory just read about over breakfast." She stood, joints cracking like couplings taking strain. "For what it's worth, I liked you better as a nobody. But I'd follow the other one anywhere."

She left me alone with the wire and the pencil and the mountain wind, and after a while—I couldn't tell you how long, time goes strange at altitude—I sat down on the frost-heaved tie box, put the carbon on my knee, and wrote:

*I, Casper James Halloran, certify that the inspection report of the Rivergate Bridge, dated 14 March, bearing my signature, is false. I did not write it. I signed it under threat, and I have been running from that signature ever since. The bridge was ordered weakened. I can prove how, and by whom. I am coming down the mountain to say so in daylight.*

It took four tries to get my hand steady enough. On the fifth draft, I signed my actual name for the first time in five years, and the letters looked foreign and familiar at once, like a face in a train window.

Ruth sent it herself. Her fist on the key was fast and clean, and when the acknowledgment came back from the Denver relay she read it aloud: *RECEIVED. RUNNING FRIDAY. GODSPEED.*

Then she turned to me and said, "Now we just have to stay alive for two days."

Which was when the shooting started.

* * *

Not shooting, exactly. One shot—a single rifle crack from the rocks above the camp, high and deliberate, kicking snow off a boulder twenty feet from the document tent. A warning. Vane's grammar had always been unmistakable: *I can, therefore consider whether I will.*

By noon he'd sent down an envoy, which told me he was weaker than the shot suggested. Strong men don't send envoys; they send bullets. The envoy was a thick-necked man named Corliss whom I remembered from the hiring hall in Denver, all polished boots and hired patience. He stopped at the picket line with his hands visible, which fooled no one, and delivered his message to the air generally, since nobody would stand close enough to receive it personally.

"Mr. Vane says conditions have changed," Corliss announced. "He's prepared to offer full amnesty. Wages doubled. Transport out for every soul in this camp, no questions. In exchange for the documents and a signed statement that nothing improper occurred at Rivergate."

"That's not amnesty," Ruth called back, "that's a bribe with a haircut."

Corliss shrugged, a man paid by the hour. "Mr. Vane also says to inform you that a territorial warrant exists for one Casper Halloran, wanted in connection with the death of a Mr. Ellery Finch, Denver, five years past. He says the marshal's deputy is two days behind him on the trail. He says cooperation now would look favorable."

"He says, he says," Ida muttered beside me. "Man talks like a newspaper that lost its editor."

But the words landed, I'll give them that. I felt them land in the people around me. I watched the two guides exchange a glance—the long arithmetic of men who'd been hired for wages and were suddenly being asked to price their lives. I watched young Whitaker, the photographer, go pale and grip his camera case like a life vest. Fear moves through a camp faster than cold does. You can't insulate against it. You can only ground it.

So I stepped forward, out beyond the crates, into the open where Corliss could see me and I could see the whole camp seeing me.

"Tell Vane the statement's already in Denver," I said, loud enough to carry. "Tell him the Ledger prints Friday. Tell him the story has my name on it—my real name—and the names of every director who signed the cost orders, and the names of the forty-one people his company left in a town they evacuated on a lie. Tell him he can't buy it, burn it, bury it, or arrest it fast enough. It's loose. It's printing. And tell him—" and here I made myself smile, because I wanted Corliss to carry the smile back up the trail like a splinter, "—tell him the engineer he hired to find this camp for him is the same engineer who's going to take the stand against him."

Silence, except the river. Then Corliss, professionally: "That's a lot of words, mister."

"They're free ones," I said. "Vane's never gotten anything free in his life. Let him choke on it."

Corliss turned and went. But I hadn't been talking to Corliss, not really. I'd been talking to the camp—to the guides, to Whitaker, to Tom Vane standing at the edge of the fire circle with his uncle's blood and his own conscience fighting visibly behind his face. Because a threat like Vane's only works on people who believe they're alone with it. Say the danger out loud in front of witnesses, and half its weight evaporates. The rest you carry together.

Tom spoke up before the envoy was out of sight. "If there's a deputy coming," he said carefully, "he should know the warrant's built on perjury. I've seen my uncle build cases before. They come apart under light." He paused. "I'll testify to that too."

"Well," said Ida, into the stunned quiet. "Look at that. The boy grows a spine at altitude. Must be something in the water."

* * *

That evening Ruth showed me the rest of it—the correspondence she'd kept with Marsh, weeks of wires cached in oilcloth. Marsh, it turned out, had done more than wait. He'd pulled the land records, the claim transfers, the shell companies Vane had used to buy up Rivergate cheap once the town was empty. He'd found the widow of the roadmaster who'd cut the piers, living in Sacramento on an annuity that traced back, through two intermediaries, to Vane's personal account. He'd even dug up Ellery Finch—the man I supposedly murdered, the foreman who'd threatened to expose the forgery and died conveniently of a fall down a hotel stairwell.

"Finch is alive?" My voice cracked on it. Five years. Five years I'd worn that death like a coat that fit worse every winter.

"Alive and drinking in Cheyenne," Ruth said. "Marsh found him Tuesday. Finch talked for six hours and a promise of anonymity he's already broken twice. Apparently he took money to swear out the accusation against you. Apparently he's very sorry. Apparently sorry pays better now than silence did." She handed me the wire. "You were never a murderer, Casper. You were a loose end. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference."

I sat there holding a piece of paper that unmade five years, and I confess I wept a little—not dramatically, nothing worth photographing, just the leaky exhausted kind—and Ruth pretended to organize documents and gave me the dignity of not watching.

When I could speak again I said, "Why didn't you tell me any of this yesterday? Before I signed?"

"Because the signature had to be yours," she said simply. "Not bought with good news. Not traded for relief. If you'd signed to get your name back, you'd have been Vane with better manners. You signed to give the dead theirs. That's the version that holds up in court and in print." She closed the oilcloth. "Ezra picked right, sending for you."

"Ezra picked a man hiding in the mountains under a fake name."

"Yes," Ruth agreed. "He was good at seeing what people were under what they were pretending to be. It's why he survived as long as he did." Her jaw tightened. "It's also why they shot him."

Outside, the light was failing blue behind the western ridge, and Ida was ringing the mess pan, and Tom was helping the injured guide re-splint his leg with a tenderness he'd have denied under oath, and somewhere above us Aldous Vane was learning—by courier, by wire relay, by the slow unstoppable seepage of truth downhill—that the story was already out of his hands and into ten thousand breakfast rooms between here and the coast.

Men like Vane can fight warrants, witnesses, and weather. What they cannot fight is being *known*. A secret dies in the dark. This one was about to step into the light wearing forty-one names.

I was lacing my boots for evening watch when one of the guides came down from the lookout at a jog, breath smoking, and the look on his face stopped the whole camp mid-motion.

"Riders," he said. "Coming up the south trail. Six of them." He caught his breath. "They're flying a marshal's badge on a pole. And there's a woman at the front with a press card pinned to her coat who says—" he faltered, clearly finding the words improbable, "—who says she's from the Ledger, and she's here to interview the engineer before Friday."

Ruth and I exchanged a glance over the dying fire.

"Well," I said, standing, and my legs were steady, which surprised me. "Let's not keep the press waiting."

Above us, on the dark ridgeline, a signal lantern blinked twice—Vane's men, moving.
