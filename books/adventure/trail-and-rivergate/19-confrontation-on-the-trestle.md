## 19. Confrontation on the Trestle

The wind came up the gorge at dusk the way it always did at Rivergate, funneling through that stone throat like breath through a whistle, and it carried with it the smell of coal oil.

I smelled it before I saw anything. Coal oil and old timber and something underneath both—smoke that hadn't started yet but was thinking about it. Five years of running trains had taught my nose things my mind hadn't bothered to learn, and my nose said: *fire, soon, upwind.*

I was at the edge of the ruined station yard when I understood where it was coming from. The trestle. Of course the trestle. If a man wanted to murder paper, he'd choose the place where the paper had been born to die.

"Ruth," I said. She was thirty feet away, checking the straps on the document case we'd carried down out of the mountains like it was an infant and we were all new parents afraid of dropping it. "He's on the trestle."

She looked up. In the last light her face went very still, the way water goes still before it freezes. "How many men?"

"One, I think. The rest are gone or wish they were." That much had been true since noon, since Ruth's telegram had hit the territorial papers and Vane's hired guns had discovered they were working for a man whose name would soon be worth more as a headline than as a paycheck. Men will do a great many wicked things for wages. They will not do them for a corpse's reputation.

"The originals," she said. It wasn't a question. She meant the ones we hadn't wired east—the telegrams in Aldous Vane's own hand, the inspection reports with his initials in the margins, the ledger pages that showed exactly what a town full of people had been worth on his books. Less than the cost of honest steel. We'd kept them close because they were the heart of the thing, and hearts are heavy, and now the weight of carrying them had told him precisely where they were.

"He'll burn them," I said. "And then he'll say there was never any proof at all. Just rumors. Just grief talking."

Ruth slung the case across her back and cinched it tight with hands that didn't shake, though I'd have forgiven them if they had. "Then we'd better go take it away from him."

* * *

The trestle at Rivergate had been a handsome piece of work once—I could say that honestly, because I'd admired its bones in the blueprints back when admiring such things was still my trade. Four hundred feet of creosoted timber on cut-stone piers, spanning the gorge where the river ran white and mean even in autumn. Then somebody had signed off on green lumber and skimpy iron, and then somebody else had ordered the piers undercut to save six weeks of schedule, and then one night in March the whole elegant arithmetic of it had come down with forty-one people aboard a passenger special and eleven more in the company houses below.

The official report called it an act of God. I knew better. I'd written some of the lies myself—not these lies, but lies in the same family, cousins to these lies—and I had spent five years hiding from that knowledge in the empty corners of the map.

Now I climbed toward it in the dark, over fallen spans slick with frost, Tom Vane at my shoulder and Ruth behind us both, and I thought: *the wilderness forgets nothing.* The dead man at Mile Forty-Three had known it too. That was why he'd pressed a map into a stranger's hands instead of dying quiet.

Tom stopped me at the base of the second pier with a hand on my arm. "Casper." His voice was low. Nineteen days on the trail had burned the polish off the young man; what was left underneath was harder and better. "If he offers you something again—"

"He won't offer me anything this time."

"If he does. Don't listen long enough to be tempted. That's all I'm saying. I listened once." He said it flat, without self-pity, which is the only way a confession works. "I'm his blood. You're not. But tonight I'd sooner stand with you."

I looked at him for a moment. There are friendships you build slowly, over years, out of small kindnesses, and there are friendships that get forged in about four seconds when two people realize they're pointing their lives at the same thing. Ours was the second kind.

"Stay behind me until I know what he's armed with," I said. "Then do whatever your conscience tells you."

"My conscience has been giving me very clear instructions lately," Tom said. "It sounds remarkably like Ida."

* * *

We went up the broken deck single file, testing each plank. The trestle's spine still stood—a hundred feet of it, jutting out over the gorge from the north abutment, ending in a splintered gap where the far span had folded into the river. Beyond the gap, nothing but air and the sound of water going by two hundred feet below, patient as arithmetic.

Aldous Vane stood at the end of that spine with a lantern in one hand and a can of coal oil at his feet, and around his shoulders he wore a coat worth more than the station we'd slept in last night, because some men dress for their own funerals and some men dress for everyone else's.

He heard us before he turned—he was too good a businessman not to hear the footsteps of his own ruin approaching—but he took his time about turning, and when he did, his face was composed and courteous and utterly mad behind the eyes, the way it had been mad from the first day in his office when he'd shaken my hand and asked after my health with genuine warmth.

"Casper Halloran," he said. "Or should I say—" and here he smiled, thin as a rail joint, "—well. We'll get to who you really are presently, won't we? I understand there's a warrant being dusted off."

"You're late," I said. "Your warrant arrived yesterday. So did the newspapers. You should read them. You come off badly."

Something moved behind his eyes at that—the first crack in the varnish. He glanced past me at Ruth, and at the case on her back, and I watched him do the arithmetic he'd been doing his whole life: assets, liabilities, the cheapest path between them.

"There were forty-one people on that train," Ruth said. Her voice carried clean over the wind without rising. "And eleven in the houses below. My brother was one of them. He stayed behind to write down what you did, and you left him in a town you'd already decided to bury."

"I evacuated the town," Vane said, with the patience of a man explaining interest rates to a child. "Every soul was offered passage. Some chose to stay. Sentimental fools, most of them. The company cannot be held responsible for—"

"The company," Ruth said, "ordered the piers cut. Your signature is on the telegram. I've been reading it every night for three weeks. I know it better than my own name now."

"And I," said Vane, "am offering you a great deal of money to stop knowing it."

"You tried that with Casper."

"And he refused, which I confess surprised me. Men who hide under other names usually want something." He turned those eyes on me then, and for a moment—just a moment—the civility dropped away entirely and I saw the thing underneath, the cold engine of him, and I understood that I was not looking at a frightened man. Frightened men run. This man had climbed onto a trestle with a lantern and a can of oil. "You want to know the difference between us, Halloran? You buried yourself. I merely buried the evidence. One of us has been honest about it."

"That's the truest thing you've ever said," I told him, "and I intend to testify to worse."

He looked at the case on Ruth's back. He looked at the lantern. And I saw him arrive at his final figure, the sum of everything, and I knew there was no more talking to be done, because Aldous Vane had never in his life accepted a loss he could set fire to instead.

"No," I said—out loud, stupidly, as if words could reach him.

"Yes," he said, almost gently, and put the lantern down into the coal oil.

* * *

Here is what I remember of the next ninety seconds, and I have gone over them so many times since that memory and invention have worn smooth together, like a coin passed through too many hands.

I remember shouting. I remember Tom lunging forward along the deck and me lunging after him, and the two of us converging on that lantern the way water converges on a breach. I remember Vane snatching the lantern up—not to throw it, not yet, but holding it high above the open can, a threat held in suspension, a man standing at the very lip of his own damnation with his finger on the latch.

"Stop," he said, and for the first time his voice cracked, and I heard the fear in it at last, arriving late the way his conscience had. "One step more and five years of proof becomes smoke. Every page. Everything your brother died for, Mrs. Okafor—gone in a minute. Is that what you want? Walk away. All of you. Walk away and let a tired old man keep his secrets."

"You'd burn it anyway," I said. "The moment our backs were turned. You know it and I know it and God knows it."

"God," said Vane, "signed nothing."

But his arm was trembling. That was the thing. Nineteen days of his plans unraveling, his nephew turned against him, his gunmen scattered to the winds, his name printing itself into infamy in a dozen territorial papers—all of it had gotten into that arm, and the lantern shook above the can of oil, and the flame inside the glass gutted sideways in the wind off the gorge.

"Put it down, Uncle." Tom's voice. Steady. Almost kind. "Put it down and walk off this bridge and I swear we'll let the courts have you instead of the river. That's more mercy than you gave anyone at Rivergate."

Vane looked at his nephew. And I watched something pass across his face that I will be honest about, because I promised myself I would be honest in this account if nowhere else: I watched him consider it. For the space of two or three heartbeats, Aldous Vane stood on the wreck he had made and weighed surrender, and the scales tipped, and tipped back, and tipped again.

Then the wind gusted.

I felt it hit us all—a hard shove out of the north, the gorge exhaling—and the lantern flame bent flat, and Vane staggered half a step on rotten decking, and his heel came down on a plank that had spent five years weathering without maintenance, because no one had budgeted for the upkeep of a bridge that officially did not exist.

The plank broke.

He did not drop the lantern. I want that recorded plainly, because the papers later wrote it every which way and none of them got it right: he did not drop the lantern. It went with him, clutched in that white-knuckled fist, flame and oil and man together, over the edge of the span and down into the dark above the river.

For one impossible moment he hung in the air the way figures hang in dreams, coat flaring, face turned up toward us, and I swear to you his expression was not terror. It was surprise. The surprise of a man whose ledger has finally failed to balance.

Then the lantern struck the rocks below—or perhaps the water; the sound was swallowed—and a bloom of orange light opened in the gorge, brief and terrible, a flower of fire floating on the current for the length of one long breath before the river closed over it.

And Aldous Vane went into the dark after his own light, and the river took him the way it had taken the trestle and the town and the forty-one and the eleven, without malice and without mercy, because the river at Rivergate had never needed instructions.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a while. The wind filled the silence, and under the wind the water, and under the water—if you believed the way I had come to believe—everything else.

Tom reached the broken edge first and knelt there, looking down into blackness. I came and stood beside him. Below, the last glow of the burning oil slid away downstream and was gone, and the gorge was just a gorge again, ordinary and enormous.

"He didn't jump," Tom said at last. His voice was strange. Younger than nineteen days ago. Older too. "Did he?"

"I don't know," I said. And it was the truth, and it would remain the truth for the rest of my life, and I found I could carry it. "The plank broke. The wind pushed. After that, the river decides. It's been deciding things at Rivergate for a long time."

Ruth came and stood on my other side. She didn't look down. She looked out—at the gap in the trestle, at the space where a span used to be, at the shape of the absence.

"It's not justice," she said quietly. "I want to be clear about that, with myself, if with nobody else. What happened just now isn't justice. Justice would have been him standing in a courtroom hearing my brother's name read aloud, forty-one times, and having to sit there for every one of them."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't justice."

"But it's over."

I thought about that. Down in the dark the river went on doing what rivers do, which is forgetting nothing and forgiving less and carrying everything eventually to the sea. Somewhere south of here a journalist was setting type. Somewhere east a court was waiting for witnesses. Somewhere in the ground beneath our feet, under slide and snow and five years of official silence, forty-one people and eleven more were waiting for theirs.

"Not over," I said. "Just finished the hard part. Now comes the part where we say it all out loud, in front of everyone, and don't stop saying it until it sticks."

Ruth shifted the document case higher on her back. "Then we'd better start walking. Court doesn't wait, and neither do I."

We turned back along the broken spine of the trestle, three figures on a dead bridge above a living river, and behind us the gorge kept its counsel, and ahead—down the line, in the valley, wherever lamplight was already burning in a territorial courthouse—there was a room with our names in it, and mine wasn't the name I'd been traveling under.

I found, walking off that trestle, that I wasn't afraid of it anymore.

That frightened me a little, actually. But only a little.
