## 20. Testimony

The courthouse in Rivergate smelled of pine pitch, cold iron stoves, and forty years of men sweating through their collars while the law decided what they were worth. I'd been in a great many railroad offices and a fair few saloons, and I can tell you honestly that no building I ever entered made my hands shake like that one did.

"You could still run," Tom said. He stood beside me on the courthouse steps, and he didn't say it like a temptation. He said it like a man reading a timetable aloud and hating the schedule.

"I know," I said. "That's rather the point."

The morning was bright and hard-edged, the kind of mountain spring day that makes everything look sharper than it is—the snowmelt roaring in the gorge below town, the new green on the aspens, the crowd gathered on the steps and spilling into the street. Half of them had come for Vane's trial, or what remained of it. The other half had come for me. Word travels fast in territory towns, faster than any train I ever ran, and by the time we'd come down out of the mountains with the documents bundled in oilcloth and Ida's brother's name written out fair and clear in Ruth's ledger, everyone in three counties knew there was an engineer among them who wasn't who he said he was.

"Casper Halloran," Ruth said, from my other side. "Is that still your name? Or do we need to pick you out another one?"

I looked at her. She had a way of asking questions that were also doors.

"It's mine," I said. "It's been mine all along. That's the trouble."

* * *

Let me tell you how a man comes to forge an inspection report, since the court would be asking soon enough and I wanted to have the answer straight in my own mouth before I had to give it to theirs.

Five years before the washout—before any of it—I was a bridge engineer for the Cascade & Northern, young enough to believe the company cared about the same things I did: sound piers, honest steel, the lives of the men who rode our lines. I inspected the Rivergate Bridge twice a year. In the spring of that last year, I found what I found: hairline fractures in the second pier's capstone, corrosion eating at the anchor bolts, a span that flexed under load more than any span should. I wrote it up plainly. I recommended closure and rebuild.

Then I was called to the division office, and a man whose name I will not dignify here—it wasn't Vane himself, it never is the man at the top; it's always some comfortable middle officer with soft hands—explained to me, very reasonably, over very good whiskey, that the line's financing depended on the quarter's numbers, that the bridge would be repaired after the season, that my report had perhaps overstated things, and that a man with my debts might find his career considerably shortened if he insisted on his arithmetic.

I had debts. My father's medical bills, mostly, and pride besides. And I told myself the story every coward tells: that it was only words on paper, that the repair would come, that one season wouldn't matter.

Eleven months later the pier went out from under the 4:15 freight, and took the trestle, and took the town's water line, and took nine men—including a brakeman named Elias Marsh, who was Ida's brother, though I didn't know her then, God forgive me—and the company needed someone to blame. They chose the dead, mostly. Dead men make convenient scapegoats; they don't contradict the record. But the record itself needed finishing, and so they came back to me with a fresh report and a pen, and this time there was no whiskey required. Just the look in the officer's eyes, which said plainly: you helped build this lie once, engineer. You'll help finish it or you'll hang beside it.

I signed. I signed my name to a document saying the Rivergate Bridge had been sound, and then I took the money they pressed on me, and I ran. Changed territories, changed the spelling of my life, became a surveyor of dead lines where nobody asked questions because nobody cared about the answer. For five years I was a ghost with a transit and a theodolite, measuring distances between points that meant nothing, telling myself that invisibility was peace.

A dying prospector in the snow taught me better. But you know that story already.

* * *

The courtroom was full when they brought me in—not in irons; Judge Abernathy had granted me the courtesy of walking in on my own recognizance, largely because Ruth Okafor and four other witnesses had vouched for me with a collective ferocity that would have made a lesser judge nervous. Ida sat in the front row with her hands folded like a woman in church, which in a sense she was. Tom sat behind her. The photographer, Whitlow, had his camera confiscated at the door and was visibly grieving.

Judge Abernathy was a spare, gray man with the face of a dried apple and a voice like gravel poured slowly. He'd presided over cattle disputes and claim jumpings and one memorable case involving a stolen bear, and he regarded me now over his spectacles with the expression of a man who has heard every kind of confession and expects mine to be mediocre.

"State your name for the record," he said.

And there it was. The moment. Five years of aliases sat down on my chest like a load of rail iron.

I thought about all the names I'd worn. Casper Nystrom, surveyor. C. Halloran, contractor. A half-dozen signatures in half-a-dozen ledgers, each one a small door closing behind me. I thought about how easy it would be to stand there and give them one more, how the habit of disappearance runs deep in a man once he's practiced it.

Then I thought about Ruth standing in the ruins of her camp, holding telegrams that proved a company had weighed eleven lives against a quarterly statement and found the lives wanting. About Ida, who had cooked for six strangers for weeks on the chance she might find where her brother fell. About the guide we'd lost in Deadman's Tunnel, whose name I would not let become one more thing the river kept.

"Casper Elias Halloran," I said. "Late of the Cascade & Northern Railroad. Bridge engineer, second class." My voice held. I was almost disappointed. "And I am the man who signed the false inspection report on the Rivergate Bridge."

The room did not gasp, particularly. It was a territorial courtroom; they'd heard worse before lunch. But something moved through it anyway, a settling, like snow sliding off a roof—the sound of a story rearranging itself around a truth.

Judge Abernathy wrote something down. "You understand," he said, without looking up, "that this court has been convened regarding the affairs of Mr. Aldous Vane and the Cascade & Northern receivership, and that voluntary confession of an unrelated felony is not generally considered good strategy."

"No, Your Honor."

"And yet here you are."

"Here I am."

He looked up then, and for just a moment the dried-apple face did something almost human. "Proceed, counselor," he said to the prosecutor. "And somebody get this man a chair. He'll be talking a while."

* * *

They talked to me for two days.

The prosecutor, a lean, earnest young man named Fisk who chewed tobacco he was too well-bred to spit, walked me through it all: the original inspection, the pressure, the forged report, the flight. Then Ruth's documents, which I authenticated as best an engineer can authenticate—the telegrams from the division office ordering load limits quietly raised, the correspondence about deferred repairs, the memo, the damned memo, in which a company officer calculated that "full replacement ($41,000) versus seasonal risk exposure (actuarial est. $6,500)" and recommended, in the dry language of men who count other men's lives in dollars, deferral.

Six thousand five hundred dollars. That was what nine men and a town's water supply had been worth on paper. I watched Ida hear that figure read aloud, and I watched her not flinch, and I have never in my life seen such courage as that—ordinary, seated, Sunday-dressed courage.

Then they asked about the expedition. The washout, the cut piers—which matched the sabotage orders in the telegrams, confirming what I'd seen with my own eyes at the trestle. The camp beyond. Vane's arrival with armed men. His offer to me, which I recounted in full, including the phrase *a clean identity*, which caused a stir, because it suggested the extent to which clean identities could be manufactured for those who served, and dirtied for those who didn't. The warrant bearing my name, dusted off and waiting at Rivergate Station like a trap set years in advance.

Vane's counsel objected to nearly all of it. He was a silver-tongued city lawyer named Prescott, and he fought every inch, but you cannot cross-examine paper. The telegrams had the company's letterhead. The memos had initials. And the crew's testimony—Ruth's steady, unornamented account; the others' corroborations—had the weight of people who had starved in the mountains rather than surrender the truth to a river.

On the afternoon of the second day, Prescott rose for his final attempt at me.

"Mr. Halloran," he said, silkily. "You've confessed to forgery, falsification of safety records, and five years of evasion under assumed names. Why should this court credit the testimony of a self-admitted liar?"

It was a fair question. I'd been waiting for it, and I found, oddly, that I wasn't afraid of it.

"Because I'm not lying now," I said. "And because lying is precisely what I'm here to stop doing. You're right, counselor. Everything I signed for the company was a lie. Every name I wore afterward was a lie. I can't bring back the men who died because I was a coward with debts. I can't bring back the guide we lost in the tunnel, or the prospector who died pressing a map into my hands. What I can do is stand in front of this court and be exactly what I am—a forger, a fugitive, and the only living man who can read those telegrams and tell you what they mean in terms of stress and load and failure. Hang me for the first two if you must. But use me for the third."

Silence, then. The kind that has weight.

Judge Abernathy set down his pen. "Mr. Halloran," he said, "you are remanded to the custody of the territorial marshal pending disposition of charges against you. Given your cooperation, bond will be considered." He paused. "For what it's worth—and it isn't legal worth, so don't quote it—in thirty years on this bench I have rarely heard a man condemn himself so thoroughly or usefully. Court adjourned."

The gavel fell, and two marshals walked me out through the crowd, and Ida Marsh stood up as I passed and did not say anything at all, only nodded, once, the way you nod at a man across a grave.

* * *

The cell was cleaner than my cabin had been, which I suppose says something about my housekeeping. They gave me a cot, a bucket, a window with a view of the gorge, and time. Time is the strange currency of jail; you have nothing else, so they pay you in it lavishly.

Tom came the first evening. He stood outside the bars looking uncomfortable, a man raised in drawing rooms trying to find the etiquette for visiting a friend in a cell.

"I brought you a book," he said, and passed it through: a battered volume of surveying tables. "It's terrible. Ida said you'd appreciate something terrible to complain about."

"She knows me well."

He shifted. "My uncle's lawyers are saying he acted alone. That the officers were duped. That—" He stopped. "You know how it goes. The higher up the tree, the harder it is to see the rot from the ground."

"How are you?" I asked, because it seemed the question he actually carried.

Tom looked at the floor for a while. "I keep thinking about the river crossing," he said finally. "About Ida going under with the documents, and my hands knowing what to do before I did. I spent my whole life being my uncle's instrument. Turns out the instrument got to choose, once. I keep turning that over." He glanced up. "Anyway. Trial resumes Monday. Ruth says the journalist arrives Thursday. The big city papers, Casper. This is going to be everywhere."

"Good," I said, and meant it, and discovered that meaning it cost less than I'd feared.

He left, and I lay on the cot listening to the gorge roar, and I slept better than I had in five years. There is a particular rest that comes only after you've stopped running. Nobody tells you that. All the running feels like survival, right up until you stop, and then you realize you'd been paying interest on your fear the whole time, compounding daily, and the debt clears all at once.

* * *

They came for the verdict on a Friday.

Not for mine—that would take longer; the territorial process moved at the speed of molasses in January—but for Vane's, or what remained of him. He hadn't appeared in person since the first day. His lawyers said health. The newspapers, printing Ruth's story in installments under headlines I could read upside down through the cell window whenever someone obligingly held one near, said other things. The receivership had collapsed. Two directors had fled to Canada. A third had turned state's evidence and was reportedly weeping beautifully in exchange for immunity.

I heard the crowd before I understood it—a rising sound from the street, cheers and jeers tangled together, and boots on the courthouse stairs, and then Marshal Dunn, a patient man built like a grain silo, appeared at my cell with his hat in his hands and an expression I couldn't read.

"Well?" I said. "Out with it. I'm not going anywhere."

"Jury's back on Vane," he said. "Guilty on all counts. Fraud, conspiracy, manslaughter—the whole ledger. Judge gave him twenty years and no sympathy." Dunn paused, working his hat brim. "There's more. Prescott filed a motion this morning. Regarding you."

"My sentence?"

"Not yet. A motion to consolidate. See, the prosecutor's been talking to the judge, and the judge has been talking to the U.S. Attorney, and there's a federal interest now, on account of the interstate mail fraud angle." He rubbed his jaw. "Point is, Halloran, they want your testimony again—federal level, formal, the whole record. And in exchange, Fisk is recommending your cooperation weigh heavy. Real heavy. There's talk of a reduced sentence. There's even talk—" and here the old marshal allowed himself the ghost of a smile, "—of a pardon application, once the federal case closes. Seems a certain Miss Okafor has been writing letters. To judges. To governors. To a senator. That woman writes more letters than a lovesick schoolgirl, and every one of them has your name in it."

I sat down on the cot. Twenty years of Vane. A possible pardon for me. It should have felt like victory, and in part it did, but underneath it ran something colder and truer: none of it brought anyone back. Justice, I was learning, is not a restoration. It's a reckoning—an accounting, imperfect and late, in which the living agree to remember accurately. That's all it is. It turns out that's not nothing.

"When do they want me?" I asked.

"Monday. Federal examiner comes through on the noon train." Dunn put his hat back on. "One more thing. There's a woman downstairs claims she's your cook. Says you'll be needing a decent meal before Monday, jail food being an abomination. I told her regulations, and she showed me a pie, and regulations lost."

"That sounds like Ida."

"She also said to tell you—" Dunn shifted, embarrassed by the errand, "—she said, 'Tell him the table's set and his chair's not going anywhere.' I don't know what that means."

I did. I looked out the little barred window at the gorge, where the river ran white and loud over the bones of the old trestle, carrying spring melt down toward the low country, indifferent and eternal, forgetting nothing and forgiving nothing either. Somewhere down that line, men were already talking about rebuilding. A new bridge, a new town charter, claims reopened in the names of the dead. Ruth had shown me the surveys. She intended, she said, to run the first engine across herself, and I believed her.

"Marshal," I said. "Tell her I'll be there."

Dunn nodded and started away, then stopped. "Halloran. The federal examiner Monday—he's thorough, they say. Harder than Fisk. He'll pull on every thread of that old report, and yours too. You ready for that?"

I thought about it honestly, the way I'd learned to think about loads and spans: what a thing can carry, and what it can't, and where the true weakness lies.

"I spent five years being ready to run," I said. "Turns out I'm better built for standing."

Dunn grunted—approval, maybe, or indigestion—and went down the stairs, and I returned to my cot and my terrible surveying tables and the sound of the river, and waited for Monday, and for whatever the law, that slow and stumbling beast, would finally decide I was worth.
