## 21. Sentences

The territorial court smelled of pine tar and old paper, and the judge wore his authority the way Ida wore her apron—loosely, but with no mistake about who was in charge.

Judge Ellery Marsh had a face like a dried riverbed: cracked, patient, and capable of sudden floods. He had listened to eleven days of testimony without once taking off his spectacles, which I took as a good sign. A man who keeps his spectacles on is a man who intends to see everything. It was the men who kept taking them off to rub their eyes that you had to watch.

"Mr. Halloran," he said now, on the last morning, "before I pass sentence on the matter of your prior offense, is there anything you wish to say?"

There was a great deal I wished to say. Most of it I'd already said, under oath, in front of stenographers and newspapermen and Aldous Vane's lawyers, who sat at their table like crows on a fence, waiting to see what fell off the wagon.

I stood. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Five years of running teaches you to keep your hands still; it's the rest of you that wants to bolt.

"I forged an inspection report for the Kestrel Mountain line in the spring of '79," I said. "I did it because a superintendent named Brack told me my sister's hospital bills would be paid if I did, and that if I didn't, they wouldn't be. That isn't an excuse. The bridge didn't fail because of me—the piers were cut before my report ever went up the line—but my signature made it possible for the company to call it an act of God instead of an act of men. I knew that when I signed. I signed anyway. I've spent five years being nobody so I wouldn't have to be the man who signed it."

The courtroom was quiet enough that I could hear the stove ticking.

"I'm not asking for forgiveness," I finished. "I'm asking for the sentence to land where the crime landed. On me."

Judge Marsh looked at me over those spectacles for a long moment. Then he looked down at a paper in front of him, and I understood he'd been waiting for exactly that speech, or something near it, before he decided how much of the law's full weight to set down.

"Casper Halloran," he said. "For the forgery of an official inspection document, the statute provides a term of not less than three years' imprisonment." He let that sit. Half the room stopped breathing. "In consideration of your cooperation with this court—in consideration of testimony material to the conviction of Aldous Vane and four associates on charges of conspiracy, murder, and fraud—and in further consideration that the original crime was committed under documented coercion, this court sentences you to eight months, time served during remand included."

The sound that went through the gallery was like wind through a trestle—half relief, half grief, all of it human.

Eight months. Remand had been four of them, spent in a cell with a window too high to see out of and a cellmate who snored like a rockslide. Four more, then. Four months of waking up when someone else said, eating when someone else decided, going nowhere at all.

It should have felt like mercy. Mostly it did. But there was a small cold part of me that noted, with an engineer's precision, that justice here ran on the same rails it always had: it bent toward the dead only after living men made it expensive not to.

Vane got twenty years. His lawyers were already talking appeal, the way water talks about finding a crack. Two of the directors who'd signed the telegrams Ruth pulled from the gorge turned state's evidence and walked out of that courtroom lighter than they'd come in, having purchased their freedom with other men's confessions. The law, imperfect. Bending, though. For the first time in my experience of it, actually bending toward the ground where the bodies lay.

* * *

They let the visitors come on sentencing day, and afterward, in the yard behind the courthouse, I got my first hour of open sky in four months.

Ida came first. She always came first; she'd have argued with God about the queue order and won.

She was carrying a small tin box, and she held it the way you hold a thing that matters more than its weight suggests.

"They found him," she said, without preamble. "The survey crew went back into the gorge last week, where you said the second camp would've been. They found Willem's kit. And—" her voice did the thing it did, the controlled fracture, "—they found him. What was left. Under a slide, near the old mile marker. He'd been keeping a journal right up to the end. Pages and pages. Names, dates, what the water brought down and when. He was doing bookkeeping, Casper. At the bottom of the world, starving, he was doing bookkeeping so somebody could audit the sons of bitches someday."

I put my hand on the tin box. It was warm from her grip.

"The county gave him to me," she said. "Next of kin, such as it is. There's a plot up on the hill behind the Methodist church. Nothing fancy. I asked the minister if a man could be buried under his own name even though the company listed him as a deserter, and the minister said the company doesn't get a vote anymore." Something moved through her face that was almost a smile. "So. Saturday. You'll be inside, so you can't come, and I know that, and I hate it, but I wanted you to know it's happening. Willem Ida's-brother Okafor—no. Willem Brandt. Buried as himself. Headstone and everything. I ordered the stone from Kalispell. Cost me two months' wages and worth every cent."

"What does it say?" I asked.

Her jaw worked once. "'Willem Brandt. He stayed.'"

That was all. Three words. And I thought: there are epitaphs carved in marble on the graves of senators that say less true things than that.

"Saturday," I repeated. "I'll be counting hours."

"You count everything," she said. "It's why we keep you." And she hugged me, brief and fierce, smelling of woodsmoke and coffee and train yards, and left before I could do anything embarrassing with my face.

Ruth came next, with Tom trailing her like a man learning to walk in someone else's boots—which, in a sense, he was.

"We filed yesterday," Ruth said. She held up a leather folder, fat with documents. "Mining claims, water rights, timber allotments—all of it lapsed when the company declared the Rivergate district abandoned. Lapsed claims revert to prior holders or their assigns. We're the assigns. Every member of the crew, living and dead, gets their name on the deed. Silas Marsh. Efraim Toussaint. Little Pete. The ones who didn't make it out—they hold ground now. Nobody can build over them without buying from their estates, and their estates are us."

"Vane bought up that district for pennies," Tom said. There was a new quality in his voice—not the polished drawl he'd arrived with, but something plainer, harder-won. "He was going to strip the timber and bury the whole valley under a reservoir project. Those papers in the crates were the only thing standing between him and erasing Rivergate twice."

"And now the papers are public record," Ruth said, "and the claims are ours, and the reservoir proposal died in committee last Tuesday. I attended the meeting personally. I brought sandwiches. It's amazing how long a committee will listen when there are sandwiches and a woman who won't leave."

Tom shifted. "There's, ah. There's something else." He looked younger than he had on the trail, and older, both at once. "My uncle's assets are frozen pending the appeals. Which means the expedition debts—the mules, the gear, the wages he never intended to pay anyone—are in receivership. I've petitioned to assume them. Personally." He met my eyes. "I know what he hired me to do up there. I know what I nearly did. I can't undo it. But I can spend the rest of however long making sure the Vane name means what it ought to mean, starting with paying six guides and a cook what they're owed, with interest."

"Seven," Ida had said once, correcting the count. "You don't get to leave yourself off the crew list just because you're ashamed of yourself. Shame's not a work exemption."

He'd taken it like a man takes medicine. He was still taking it. I suspected he would take it for years.

"Four months," Ruth said, before she left. She gripped my forearm the way engineers grip rail—to test whether it holds. "We'll be here. All of us. Every visiting day, and every day after, until you walk out. You didn't have a family when you found us, Halloran. Now you've got one with a deed, a headstone order, and a standing reservation at Ida's table. Try escaping that."

"No one's ever gone hungry trying," I said, and meant several things by it.

* * *

The four months passed the way stone passes under a chisel—one blow at a time, mostly tedious, occasionally revealing something unexpected.

I taught the prison librarian's assistant to read blueprints. I wrote letters—dozens of them—to families of the Rivergate dead, telling each one where their person had been found, or might yet be found, and what the telegrams proved, and that the word *abandoned*, which the company had used in its filings, was a lie built to cover the word *murdered*. Some families wrote back. Some didn't. One elderly woman sent me a jar of plum preserves with a note that read, in a shaking hand: *My husband drove stage through that valley for nineteen years. Thank you for saying their names out loud.* I ate the preserves slowly and shared them with nobody, which felt correct.

Ida came every visiting day. She reported on the headstone (delivered, inspected, approved), the grave plot (sodded, fenced, planted with something stubborn that would survive snow), and the small restaurant she was opening in Rivergate Station itself—the rebuilt station, the one rising plank by plank over the summer like a promise kept late.

"Called it the Mile Forty-Three," she said. "After where you found that dying fool prospector. Seemed fitting. First meal's free for anyone who worked the closed lines."

Ruth came when the claims business allowed, which was often, because the claims business flourished. The reopened district needed surveying, and surveying needed an engineer, and she informed me with great satisfaction that the position was being held open by unanimous decision of the board—a board consisting of herself, Ida, Tom, the photographer Wren (whose photographs of the gorge evidence had run in three territorial newspapers and one back east), and, in memoriam seats kept permanently empty at the table's head, the dead of Rivergate.

"We vote in their names," Ruth explained. "Silas would have voted for the tunnel route. Efraim against anything involving dynamite. Little Pete votes yes on everything, because that was his nature." Her voice caught on his name, the way it always did, and recovered, the way it always did. "It keeps us honest. Hard to cut corners when the corner-cutters have chairs."

Tom visited least and wrote most. His letters were careful, self-auditing documents—I recognized the genre; I'd invented it—and they detailed, week by week, the slow dismantling of his uncle's empire and the slow construction of whatever Tom was becoming instead. In the last one he mentioned, almost as an aside, that he'd been learning to drive mules. "Ida says I have a talent for it," he wrote. "She may be mocking me. With Ida it's difficult to tell, and possibly the point."

And Judge Marsh, of all people, sent one letter, brief and dry as his riverbed face:

*Mr. Halloran — Your case has occasioned more correspondence to this office than any in my tenure, most of it demanding harsher treatment of you, some of it demanding none. I note for the record that the statute's minimum term exists to punish concealment, and you have done the opposite of conceal for eleven straight days. The remainder of your sentence is what the law requires. What happens after is what the law hopes for. — E.M.*

I read that one more times than I'd admit to anyone.

* * *

On the last morning, they gave me back my belongings in a canvas sack: a pocket watch that had stopped at some point I couldn't identify and seemed symbolically appropriate, a pencil worn to a stub, and the map.

The prospector's map. The one a gut-shot man had pressed into my hands in the snow at mile forty-three, along with a promise I hadn't wanted to make and hadn't been able to refuse. It had been evidence for a while, catalogued and photographed, and then returned, because the courts were done with it and Ruth declared it belonged to "the expedition, historically speaking."

I stood in the prison yard with the map in my hands and the morning coming up gold behind the wall, and I unfolded it one more time.

The lines were still there. The closed routes, the abandoned grades, the little penciled notes in a dying man's hand—*camp beyond washout, 4 alive, low on everything*. Four alive. He'd been wrong; it was five by the time we reached them, Wren having joined the survivors' number somewhere in the arithmetic of that terrible winter. But he'd been right about the important thing: that somebody should go, and that the going mattered more than the odds.

A guard cleared his throat. "Gate's open, Halloran. Has been for ten minutes. You planning to inspect it further?"

"Just admiring the engineering," I said.

Beyond the gate, parked on the road in the full insolent glory of a locomotive that had no business being there, sat a small shay engine with a flatcar behind it, and on the flatcar stood Ida, arms crossed, grinning like sin; Ruth, holding a ledger; Tom, holding mule reins with the expression of a man holding live snakes; and Wren, already photographing the gate, because Wren photographed everything now and the territory was better for it.

"Well?" Ida called. "The line to Rivergate doesn't run itself. Engineer's seat is empty. Been empty four months. We're tired of looking at it."

I folded the map and put it in my breast pocket, over the heart, where a man keeps the things that started everything.

Then I walked out through the gate, into the noise and the light and the smell of coal smoke and pine, toward the people who had waited—because the sentence was served, but the family, as it turns out, was permanent.

The engine whistled. Somewhere up the valley, the river ran on over the gorge, carrying nothing but water now, and the town above it went on building itself back, plank by plank, name by name.

I climbed into the seat, and took the throttle, and drove home.
