## 22. Trail and Rivergate

Two years is a long time for a man who spent five trying to be nobody.

I'll tell you what I learned about being somebody, since I've had the leisure to study the question: it's mostly paperwork. There was a great deal of it after the trial. Petitions, affidavits, a pardon signed by a governor whose handshake left my palm feeling like I'd gripped a wet fish and been liked by it. The territorial court forgave me my forged inspection report with one hand while charging me court costs with the other, which struck me as the most honest thing any institution did in that whole affair.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Mornings first. A man should start where he actually lives.

The kettle on the stove at Rivergate Station was singing when I came down, and Ida was already at the range, because Ida has never once in her life let a kettle out-sing her.

"You're up late," she said, without turning around. "Train's not till ten."

"It's the anniversary," I said. "I slept badly."

"Everybody sleeps badly today." She set a cup of coffee in front of me, black as a confession. "Drink that. You look like something the river declined."

The station house had been a ruin when we found it—roof open to the sky like a rib cage, platform boards curled up at the edges from five winters of snow sitting on them uninvited. Now it had a roof, a stove, four bedrooms, an office with a telegraph key Ruth had personally bullied back into working condition, and a sign over the door that Tom had painted himself: RIVERGATE STATION — EST. 1881 — REBUILT 1893. He'd wanted to put something about perseverance underneath, and Ruth had talked him down to nothing at all, on the grounds that a building that survived being murdered shouldn't have to boast about it.

The line itself runs eleven miles now. Eleven miles of track laid over the gorge on a new trestle—my design, inspected by me, signed by me, every rivet accounted for, because I am done signing things I haven't counted twice. It carries timber down from the high camps and supplies up, and once a month it carries passengers, most of whom ride it for the view and none of whom know they're crossing the grave of a town. We keep a small cemetery on the bluff above the east abutment. Thirty-one names. Ida's brother's is cut deepest, because she paid the stonecutter extra to make sure of it, and he was wise enough not to argue.

Ruth came in at half past eight with frost in her hair and a crate of documents under her arm, which is how she enters most rooms.

"Territorial land office finally processed the claims," she announced. "All four. In the crew's names. Okafor, Lindqvist, Marchetti, Boone."

"Boone," I said. "That was the prospector's name?"

"Josiah Boone." She set the crate down and looked at it a moment. "He'd have hated that his name's on paper instead of a mountain, but he'd have hated more that the mountain stayed Vane's."

Aldous Vane owned no mountains anymore. That took longer than the trial—claims are a spiderweb, and you have to burn each strand—but between Ruth's filings, the journalist's follow-up series, and a territorial legislature suddenly very interested in looking virtuous, the web came apart. Vane himself needed no help coming apart. The gorge did that. Whether he jumped or slipped or simply ran out of ledges, the coroner wrote *misadventure*, and none of us argued, because there wasn't a word in any of our mouths that would have improved on it.

Tom still doesn't talk about that morning on the trestle. He talks about everything else—loudly, constantly, usually while doing three jobs at once—but not that. Some doors a man keeps shut even in a house full of people who'd understand. I keep one or two myself.

* * *

At nine we walked up the spur to the old trestle.

They never rebuilt the original crossing—it stands yet, or most of it does, a quarter mile downstream from the new line: charred pilings, twisted iron, the bones of the thing the directors ordered weakened and then ordered forgotten. The territory wanted to demolish it. Ruth fought them to a standstill on the grounds that a crime scene is evidence, and evidence doesn't get tidied away just because it embarrasses important men. So it stands, fenced now, with a brass plate at the foot of the trail:

*HERE THE RIVERGATE BRIDGE FAILED BY DESIGN. THIRTY-ONE DIED. THE TOWN WAS BURIED TO HIDE IT. REMEMBER WHAT WAS DONE AND WHO PAID.*

Ida had suggested plainer wording. Ruth had suggested considerably less plain. The compromise satisfied neither, which is how you know a committee worked properly.

We carried flowers. That was Ida's institution, the anniversary flowers—wild columbine and paintbrush from the south slope, gathered fresh, because she said the dead could tell the difference between picked-that-morning and bought-in-town, and she would not insult them. Thirty-one small bundles. We'd done it last year too, all six of us walking up in silence, and I remember thinking then that I didn't belong among them, that grief shared is not the same as grief owned.

This year I brought thirty-two bundles. Nobody asked why. Ida just looked at the extra one, and then at me, and nodded once, the way she nods when a man has finally seasoned a stew correctly.

We laid them along the fence rail, one by one. Ruth read the names. She always reads the names; she has them by heart, in the order the telegrams listed the missing, and she reads them the way you'd read a muster roll—clear, unhurried, each man entitled to his full second of air. Tom stood hatless in the cold with his jaw working. Ida held my elbow on the icy stretch of trail, ostensibly for balance, actually because she'd decided two years ago that I was hers to look after and had never filed the paperwork to resign the position.

When Ruth finished, she said, "And Casper Halloran, engineer, who came up a mountain to keep a promise to a dying stranger and stayed to keep a bigger one." She looked at me. "Say your piece."

"I don't have a piece."

"You've had two years to write one."

So I stood there in the wind off the gorge, looking down at the water that kept the bridge timbers and the truth for five years before Josiah Boone crawled out of the wilderness carrying both, and I said: "I spent a long time believing the safest thing a man could do was be no one. I was wrong. Being no one just means the only person who suffers for your silence is you—and everyone else pays for it instead." I touched the fence rail. "Rest easy. Somebody's watching the line now."

Ida blew her nose like a foghorn. Tom pretended to study the far bank. Ruth squeezed my arm, and we started down.

* * *

The ten o'clock train went up light and came back heavy, six flatcars of dressed timber and one passenger car with a family aboard—a surveyor's wife and two children riding down to the valley for school clothes. The little girl pressed her face to the glass the whole way across the new trestle, watching the river, and I watched her watch it, and thought: that's the whole job, right there. Making sure the only thing a child ever sees from this bridge is water going by.

Afterward there was lunch, because Ida holds that grief and hunger are cousins and must be fed together. Then Tom and I walked the eleven miles with a level and a notebook, because the anniversary is also inspection day—I insisted on that pairing, and nobody argued. A man ought to check his own work on the day he remembers why the work matters.

Tom's changed. Not softened, exactly—he can still out-talk a telegraph line—but the ambition in him has been re-aimed. He used to want to be his uncle; now he wants to be better than his uncle ever was, and knows the difference is measured in whether the people under you sleep well at night. He keeps a ledger of every worker's wages and hours in his own hand, and audits it against mine monthly, and has caught me in error twice, both times with visible delight.

"Span four's settling a hair on the north side," he said, crouched at the abutment. "Half an inch since spring."

"Within tolerance. But log it, and we shim it come June."

"Yes, *sir*," he said, grinning, because he knows I hate being sired. He's twenty-six years old and calls me sir purely as ammunition.

Halfway back, at mile seven, he stopped and looked down at the old trestle below us, its black bones in the green water.

"Do you think about him much?" he asked. "Vane."

"Less than I expected. More than I'd like."

"He offered me the company once. Everything, if I'd have let those guides hang for the sabotage." Tom turned his collar up against the wind. "Some nights I dream I took it. Wake up rich and alone in a big house in Denver with everybody dead and nobody to eat breakfast with." He shrugged. "Then I come downstairs and Ida's threatening the stove and Ruth's arguing with the telegraph, and I think—best nightmare I ever escaped."

"That's the whole fortune, Tom. Breakfast with people who'd notice if you didn't show."

He laughed, and we walked on, and the rails hummed faintly under our boots the way they do when the afternoon sun warms the steel—the line talking to itself, my father used to say. Engineers' superstition. I believe every word of it.

* * *

Evening. The office. Ruth was at the telegraph when I came in, and she had the particular stillness she gets when a wire says something worth stillness about.

"From the land office?" I asked.

"From Denver." She handed me the flimsy. "Read it yourself."

I read it twice. The territorial legislature, flush with virtue and election-year money, had voted funds to reopen the upper line—all the way through Deadman's Tunnel, repaired, ballasted, running to the high camps by next summer. And appended, in the dry language of official appointment: *engineering oversight for the Rivergate extension having been recommended by the commission, the services of C. Halloran are respectfully requested.*

"They want me to build it," I said. "Through the tunnel."

"The tunnel where we nearly died."

"The same. They'll want it named something dignified. Commission on Naming Things will probably suggest Progress Tunnel."

Ruth almost smiled. Almost. "Well? Eleven miles made you an engineer again. Forty might make you ambitious."

"Ambition's what killed the first bridge."

"No." She took the flimsy back and folded it square. "Cowardice killed the first bridge. Ambition just carried the matches." She pinned the telegram to the board above the key, where the whole household would see it by morning, which was her way of calling a vote without holding one. "Sleep on it. But Casper—" she paused at the door, "—when you answer them tomorrow, answer as a man with a family, not a man with a past. They're not the same weight anymore."

She went out. I sat a while with the lamp and listened to the station settle—Ida banking the stove, Tom whistling off-key in the woodshed, the wind coming down the gorge over the water that forgets nothing but has, at least, been made to share its secrets.

Five years ago I was a dead man walking a live man's line, hiding in mileposts. Two years ago I was a fugitive testifying in a courtroom with my real name in my mouth like a hot coal. Tonight I'm the engineer of record on a bridge that holds, in a house that holds, with people who hold—each other, and me, and the memory of thirty-one who didn't get the chance.

Tomorrow I'll wire Denver and say yes. Of course I'll say yes. The wilderness forgets nothing, and neither do I, and the difference between us is that I've got people to remember with.

Outside, far up the valley, the old trestle stood black against the last light, and the river went on telling its story to anyone patient enough to listen.

We were listening. We always would be.
