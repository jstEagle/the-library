## 1. The River's Offer

The river had been rising for three days, and on the fourth morning it brought Edmund Vane a dead man's chest.

He heard it before he saw it—a knocking against the stones below his hut, rhythmic and patient, like someone asking politely to be let in. He had lived twelve years above the Greywater fork and knew its voices: the low mutter of snowmelt in spring, the flat hiss of summer shallows, the groan of ice going out. This was none of them. This was wood striking rock, deliberate as a fist.

He stood in the doorway with his tin cup half-raised and watched the flood carry it down the middle channel—dark, square, turning slowly, riding so high in the water that he understood at once it was sealed. Not a farmer's trunk with its lid sprung and its wool blankets blooming out like drowned flowers. Sealed. Iron-banded. Traveling with intent.

He should have let it go.

That was the first thought, clean and simple as an axe-stroke: *let it go*. The river took things up-valley and gave things back; that was its bargain, and he had kept his side of it for twelve years by touching nothing that came down from the high country but water and weather. A man who had been erased from every ledger in three provinces did not go wading into a flood after other people's property. Other people's property had names attached. Names were how they found you.

But the chest caught on the gravel bar below his woodpile, wedged itself between two boulders, and stayed there, knocking. Knocking. The way a thing knocks when it has been traveling a long time and believes, wrongly or rightly, that it has arrived somewhere.

Edmund set down his cup. He told himself he was only going down to look—that looking cost nothing—and even as he thought it he recognized the voice for what it was, the same reasonable voice that had talked him into opening his father's one letter, all those years ago, and reading the two lines that ended his old life. *Looking costs nothing.* Looking had cost him everything. He went down anyway.

* * *

The water was higher than his knees and moving like a living thing, shoving at his thighs, trying to take his feet out from under him with the casual strength of something that had moved mountains and was not impressed by a man. He leaned into it, boots finding the gravel he'd walked a thousand times, and reached the bar where the chest sat wedged and streaming.

Up close it was worse than he'd guessed. It was old—not river-old, but *old*-old, the kind of old that comes from being made well and then cared for. Oak staves gone black with age and water. Iron bands pitted but unbroken. And on the lid, set into the wood in brass that the flood had scoured bright, a crest: a mountain split by a road, with seven stars above the peak.

Edmund stopped breathing.

He knew that crest. Every child in the border provinces had known it once, the way children know the shapes of clouds—vaguely, superstitiously, from stories their grandparents told about the Before. A mountain split by a road. Seven stars. The arms of Kestria, the little kingdom that had been on the maps for six hundred years and then, forty years ago, was not. Erased. Not conquered in any way anyone would say aloud—the annexation was called a *union of administration* in the official histories—but wiped off the paper all the same, its name struck from the atlases, its records sealed, its people re-registered under new provinces and new surnames until the whole nation was a rumor with a crest.

And here was the rumor, sitting in his river, staring up at him out of the floodwater with its seven brass stars.

"Go away," Edmund said to it.

The chest knocked against its boulder, patient, indifferent. Water poured off its lid in sheets. Somewhere upstream, the storm that had fed this flood was still working through the high passes, and the river would rise again tonight, and if he left the chest where it was the water would either take it back down toward the lowlands—or pin it here, filling, sinking, rotting into the gravel with whatever it carried dissolving inside it.

He looked up-valley. Grey sky, the ridgeline lost in cloud, no smoke from any chimney but his own because there was no other chimney within two days' walk. That was why he lived here. That was the entire architecture of his life: altitude, distance, the absence of chimneys. Twelve years since the blacklist. Twelve years since his name had gone onto the lists that porters, wardens, and postmasters checked, the lists that meant no ferry would carry him, no border would pass him, no employer would hire him, and any constable in three provinces could detain him on sight without paperwork or reason. *Edmund Vane, associate of persons charged with sedition.* His father's crime, hung around his neck like the millstone his father had surely never worn himself, because his father had been gone eleven years by then, vanished over some horizon, leaving behind one son, one letter, and one ruined name.

The chest knocked.

"I said go away."

It didn't. Things that come down rivers rarely do what you ask. He crouched, tested the weight—lighter than it looked, which meant either small contents or a hollow—and got his hands under the wet iron bands and lifted. The gravel sucked at it, gave reluctantly, and then it was free and he was staggering backward out of the current with sixty pounds of dead Kestria dragging at his arms, and the river closed over the place where it had rested as if nothing had ever been there at all.

* * *

He put it down inside the door of the hut, on the stone floor, because he could not bring himself to put it anywhere nearer the fire.

That was the thing he noticed about himself in the hours that followed, as he went about the day's work with more noise than it needed—splitting wood he didn't need split, checking a fence line that fenced nothing, boiling water twice for tea he drank too fast. He noticed that he had placed the chest by the door. Facing out. Ready to be put back into the world.

He also noticed that he had not put it back into the world.

By late afternoon the light had gone the color of pewter and the rain had started again, soft and steady, the kind of rain that meant the flood would hold another day. Edmund sat across the single room from the chest with his back to the wall and his boots on and his coat still on, a man fully dressed to leave, watching a box.

It had drained overnight. The oak had darkened and swelled, the bands wept rust in thin orange tears down onto his floor, but the seal held. There was no lock he could find—no hasp, no keyhole—which meant it was fastened from within or fastened by craft, and both possibilities annoyed him in different ways. Around the lid ran a line of writing in a script he almost recognized: tall letters with hooked tops, dense as fencing, the old Kestrian hand that appeared on boundary stones in the high country when the weather wore the newer inscriptions away. He had traced those letters with his fingers as a boy, on the marker above the Serpent trailhead, and asked his mother what they said, and she had gone quiet in the particular way she went quiet, and finally said, *Old words. They don't matter anymore.*

They had mattered enough to carve.

He got up twice to build the fire and twice stood over the chest instead with the kindling in his hands, doing arithmetic. Dry the wood, hot fire, iron bands would hold the heat long past the point where the oak gave up. An hour, maybe less. No lock to pick, no seam to force, no risk of whatever documents or debts or dynastic embarrassments lay inside escaping into his life. Ash. The river had offered it; the fire would refuse it; the world would return to its proper shape, in which Kestria was a story and Edmund Vane was a man who owned one hut, four goats, and no history.

His mother's face came to him, the way it always did at the worst times—not her living face but the last one, gray-lipped, in the narrow bed, gripping his wrist with a strength that frightened him. *Don't be found, Edmund. Whatever happens, however they sweeten it, don't be found.* She had died believing the blacklist was a kindness compared to what else might be done to him. He had honored her for twelve years the only way left: by staying small, staying high, staying uncounted.

He fed the fire instead of the chest. Small fire. Tea fire. He sat back down.

The rain thickened against the roof. The goats complained in their shed. And the chest sat by the door with its seven stars going green-black in the damp, and its old hooked letters running around the rim like a sentence waiting to be finished, and Edmund Vane discovered that twelve years of practiced solitude had a floor to it, and he had just hit it.

Because here was the truth underneath the arithmetic: he did not want to burn it. He wanted to know. The wanting had been asleep for years—he'd have sworn under oath it was dead—but the flood had carried it right up to his door and knocked, and now it was awake, and it was enormous, and it was hungry, and it looked exactly like his father.

* * *

Night came down. He lit the lamp, which was surrender of a kind, and ate standing up, which was cowardice of another, and finally, near midnight, with the rain steady and the river roaring its one long note in the dark below, he crossed the room and knelt in front of the chest with his knife in his hand.

"Just the corner," he said aloud, to the chest, to the room, to no one. "Just to see what I'm burning."

The blade went into the seam at the front-left corner, where the swelling had opened a hairline gap. Old glue, old iron, old stubbornness. He worked it the way he worked anything—slowly, patiently, letting the tool do the talking—and the gap widened by degrees, and then the corner of the lid lifted with a sound like a sigh, a breath of air coming out of the box that smelled of cedar and lamp oil and, beneath both, faintly, incredibly, of lavender.

His mother had kept lavender. In a cloth bag, on the sill, in the last house they'd had before everything. He knelt there with his knife motionless in the seam and breathed it, and the hut fell away, and he was eight years old in a kitchen that no longer existed anywhere on earth.

Then the moment passed, the way moments do, and he was a grown man kneeling on cold stone with a hole in a dead nation's chest, and he angled the lamp light in and looked.

Papers. Oilcloth-wrapped bundles, packed tight, dry as autumn leaves—the sealing had been done by someone who understood water, which meant someone who understood rivers, which meant someone from this country, this range. He got two fingers on the nearest bundle and drew it out into the light. Heavy paper, cream-colored, written in that same tall hooked script—but this page bore a heading in a second hand, older and more formal, and beneath the heading, ruled columns, and in the first column, in ink gone brown with age:

*Registry of Births, Parish of High Serpentine, Royal Kestria.*

His eyes went down the entries. Dates. Names. A godmother here, a midwife's mark there. And then, halfway down the page, boxed in red ink by some clerk who had thought it important, an entry dated thirty-nine years ago, and the name of the child born that day to a cartographer of the royal household and his wife—

*Vane.*

Not his name. Not quite. But close enough that the floor seemed to tilt, and he read it again, and then a third time, and then he turned the page over as though the answer might be hiding on the back, and it wasn't, and the entry sat there in its red box, patient as the chest, patient as the river.

And beside the father's name, in the column for the mother, written smaller, in a hand he knew—knew from shopping lists and letters and the flyleaf of the book she'd left him—was his mother's name. Her full name. Her maiden name, which she had never used again after the annexation, which she had buried so deep that even Edmund had learned it only once, whispered, the year she died.

The lamp flickered. The rain came down. Outside, the river rose another inch against the stones, carrying the mountains down toward the sea, and Edmund Vane knelt on the floor of his hut holding a page of a registry belonging to a country that had never legally existed, in which his mother's name proved that she had been born somewhere. Registered somewhere. Counted.

Counted. Forty years after the erasure, the word landed in him like a dropped stone into a well, falling and falling. They had counted her. Someone had written her down in a fair hand and boxed her in red ink and wrapped the page in oilcloth and sealed it in cedar and lavender and committed it to the water—to *his* water—and the current had known, the way currents sometimes seem to know, and had brought it up the valley to the one door in the world where a man lived who was not supposed to be findable, and had knocked.

He looked at the chest. At the rest of the bundles, shadowed in the interior, edges of wax seals, the glint of metal—something small and round, catching the lamplight deep inside.

He should burn it. He should burn it tonight, before the wanting grew any larger, before the lavender faded from the room, before whoever had sent this thing down the river came looking to see if it had arrived.

Edmund folded the birth record along its old creases, tucked it inside his shirt, next to his skin, where his heartbeat could get at it, and reached for the lamp.

Down in the dark, far up the flooded valley, something that was not thunder rolled along the ridgeline—once, twice—and stopped.
