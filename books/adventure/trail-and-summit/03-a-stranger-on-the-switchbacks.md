## 3. A Stranger on the Switchbacks

The rain had followed Ilse Brenner for three days, and she had begun to think of it as a companion—unwelcome, relentless, but at least proof that the world still noticed her.

She climbed the switchbacks above the flooded valley with her hood down because the wind kept tearing it back anyway, and because she wanted to see. Ten years of tracing a rumor through municipal archives and burned registries and the fading memories of old women who spoke Kestrian only when they were drunk had taught her one discipline above all others: *look*. Look at the trail. Look behind you. Look at the sky, which in the Serpent Range could change its mind faster than a tribunal judge.

Behind her, far below where the switchbacks stitched themselves into the gray floodplain, two riders had stopped at the last farmstead. She had watched them from the tree line an hour ago—one tall, one broad, both wearing the oiled canvas of men paid to be weatherproof. They hadn't worn livery. Men with livery belonged to someone you could name. These two belonged to money, which was worse, because money didn't have to explain itself.

Two days behind, if they stayed lucky. Half a day, if they didn't.

Ilse counted her remaining supplies without wanting to: half a loaf gone hard as slate, a twist of salt pork, three fingers of brandy in a flask her father had carried across the annexation border sewn into the lining of his coat. Her boots had been good boots once, in another life, in another country—the country that no longer existed on any map printed after the Year of the Ledger, when Kestria's name was struck from the atlases and its people were struck from the rolls and forty years of births went unrecorded, uncounted, unwitnessed.

*Unwitnessed.* That was the word her father had used, dying, his hand around hers on the blanket. "They can take the land, Ilschen. They can take the name off the paper. But paper is only witnesses. Find the witnesses."

She had spent her whole adult life looking for witnesses. And now, somewhere above her, past the scree fields and the stunted pines giving way to nothing, there was a hut, and in that hut—or so the ferryman's drunken letter claimed—a man had pulled a chest out of the river bearing the double-headed kestrel seal of a dead kingdom.

Not a copy. Not a forgery. The letter had described the wax in detail: the deep crimson, the crack running through the kestrel's left wing like a scar, the maker's mark of the Chancery of Records pressed into the underside. Ilse knew that crack. She had seen it in a photograph taken in the last week before the fall, in the hands of the Registrar General himself, sealing the evacuation registry of the royal household.

A chest containing the last living proof that Kestria's royal line survived.

And a man who might not want to give it up. Or might already be dead. Or might never have existed at all except in a drunk ferryman's wishful thinking.

Ilse put her head down and climbed.

* * *

The switchbacks turned to stone steps, and the stone steps turned to a goat track that the mountain seemed to have made out of spite. The rain thickened to sleet around midafternoon, then thinned again, and the clouds tore open long enough to show her what she was climbing toward: a wall of gray peaks going white at the top, and below them, on a shelf of rock jutting over the valley like a jaw, a low dark shape with smoke rising thin from its chimney.

Smoke. He was home.

Her legs nearly gave out—not from the climb, from the relief. She sat down hard on a wet boulder and laughed, a small ugly sound, and wiped her face with a sleeve already soaked through, and told herself sternly that smoke meant nothing. Smoke meant a man warming his supper. It did not mean he would talk to her. It did not mean he wouldn't put a crossbow bolt through the door slats first and ask questions after. Mountain men who lived alone at this altitude lived alone for reasons.

But she had come too far and lost too much to stop at the door of the answer.

She ate half the hard bread standing up, chewing mechanically, watching the hut while she chewed. It was built low and tight against the rock, stone walls, turf roof weighted with boulders, a woodshed, a drying rack with fish split open like pages of a book. A garden plot terraced into the slope, winter kale gone ragged under the frost. Everything about it said: *a man who has decided exactly how much world he needs, and no more.*

There was a story in that too. There was always a story. In her experience, people who erased themselves from the world had usually been erased by it first.

She shouldered her pack and took the last hundred meters carefully, boots finding the ledges, and came up onto the shelf as the light was failing, the valley below filling with blue shadow like water filling a basin.

That was when she saw the man.

He was outside the hut, splitting wood, and the sight of him stopped her mid-step with a feeling she couldn't name for several seconds—and then could, and the naming of it nearly buckled her knees.

She knew his face.

Not from life. From paper. From the archive that fit in three steel boxes under the floorboards of her aunt's house in Ferrow, the archive she had memorized the way other scholars memorized scripture: the household registers of the royal court, the staff rosters, the portraits commissioned for the last anniversary of the coronation. A boy of fourteen, standing slightly apart from the other pages and attendants, dark-haired, watchful, with a jaw that hadn't yet decided what it would become and eyes that already had.

Edmund Vane. Son of Tomas Vane, Royal Cartographer to the Crown of Kestria. Recorded in the register of the palace household, entry 4471, dated the spring of the last year.

Recorded. Witnessed. Existing on paper in a world where her people had been deleted.

He was thirty-some now, broader, bearded, weathered to the color of the rock he stood on—but the face was the face. The same set of the shoulders, slightly forward, as if always listening downhill. The same way of holding his head, chin tucked, gaze level.

Ilse stood at the edge of the shelf with sleet melting in her hair and felt tears start in her eyes, absurdly, helplessly, and she understood why: it wasn't the man. It was what he was. He was a page torn from the burning book, and here he was, alive, splitting firewood, breathing. Proof that the archive was true. Proof that the past had happened, that it had *been real*, that all those names in her three steel boxes had been attached to bodies that walked and worked and grew old.

Forty years of erasure, and here stood a witness who didn't even know he was one.

He heard her, of course. He straightened from the block with the axe still in his hands and looked at her the way a man looks at something the mountain has coughed up—assessing, unhurried, already deciding.

"You're a long way off the road," he said. His voice was flat, careful, the voice of a man who had practiced saying little.

"I'm a long way off everything," Ilse said. Her voice came out cracked. She cleared her throat. "I'm looking for shelter. And I'm looking for you."

"Only one of those is for sale." He didn't move. The axe stayed loose in his grip, not threatening, not not-threatening. "Shelter's the lean-to round the side. There's a stove in it. You can be gone by first light."

"And you?"

"I'm not for sale either."

"I don't want to buy you," Ilse said. "I want to ask you about a chest."

Something moved behind his eyes—fast, quickly shuttered, like a bird crossing a window. He saw that she'd seen it. Neither of them pretended otherwise.

"There's no chest," he said.

"There is," Ilse said. "It came down the river nine days ago in the flood. Sealed with the kestrel crest, crimson wax, a crack through the left wing. The Chancery mark on the underside." She recited it the way she had rehearsed it on a thousand kilometers of road, and watched the color change in his face. "I know what's in it, Edmund Vane. I've spent ten years trying to find it. I crossed the border at Marrow Ford with forged papers and I let two men follow me up this mountain because I was too tired to lose them properly, and I am asking you—I am *begging* you—not to make me have done all that for nothing."

The wind moved through the drying fish. Somewhere below, thunder muttered along the valley floor, or maybe it was the river still eating the farmland.

"How do you know my name?" he said quietly.

"Because I've read yours," Ilse said. "Entry 4471, household register of the palace, spring of the last year. Page 212 of the second box. Your father signed beside it—Tomas Vane, Royal Cartographer. There's a portrait too, commissioned for the anniversary. You were fourteen. You stood apart from the others." She swallowed. "You look like him still. Like the boy in the painting. I'd know your face anywhere, Mr. Vane. I've been carrying it in my head for ten years."

For a long moment he said nothing at all. Then he set the axe against the block, very deliberately, the way a man puts down a thing he doesn't trust himself to hold, and looked at her fully for the first time.

"Get inside," he said. "Before I change my mind."

* * *

The hut's interior was spare almost to the point of hostility: one bed, one chair, a table scrubbed white, tools hung on pegs in ranks, a map of the local range drawn in ink on stretched hide and pinned above the hearth. No photographs. No keepsakes. Nothing that remembered anything.

He lit the lamp, put water on to boil, and sat across the table from her with his hands flat on the wood, and studied her the way he'd probably studied the weather all his life—for what it intended to do to him.

"You're Kestrian," he said. It wasn't a question. Her accent had given her away; it always did, the vowels rounded the old way, the *r*s rolled soft.

"Ilse Brenner. Archivist. My father was assistant registrar under the Chancery—he carried what records he could out in the evacuation, and what he couldn't carry, he hid, and what he hid, most of it burned anyway." She held his gaze. "What survived fits in three boxes. I've spent ten years cataloguing them and five years chasing rumors of the chest. The evacuation registry of the royal household was sealed in that chest, Mr. Vane. Do you understand what that means? Every name. Every child shipped to the conquerors' schools. Every servant scattered across the provinces with their identity struck. If that registry reaches the tribunal sitting in Vhalen at the end of the dry season—if it's authenticated—it restores us. Legally. On paper. It makes four hundred thousand erased people *exist* again."

The kettle began to sing. He didn't get up.

"The flood brought it to me," he said at last. "I didn't ask for it."

"No one ever asks for inheritance. That's what makes it inheritance."

"This isn't—" He stopped. Started again, slower. "I pulled it out of the mud because it was blocking my fish trap. I opened one corner because I thought there might be trade goods. I found a birth record with my mother's name on it, and I have spent nine days telling myself it's a coincidence, or a forgery, or a trap." His jaw tightened. "So tell me, since you've read my whole life in a box. Tell me it's a trap. Tell me it's a forgery. Because I had a life. I had twelve years of a life, and every day of it cost me something to keep, and I would very much like to go back to it."

Ilse looked at him—at the fury and the fear underneath the fury, so familiar she could have drawn it from memory, because she wore the same expression in every mirror—and she felt the tears rise again and did not fight them this time. One spilled down her cheek and she let it sit there, evidence, testimony.

"It's not a forgery," she said softly. "I know the hand that sealed it. And it's not a trap, though there are men two days behind me who will make it one. But it isn't a coincidence either, Edmund. Chests don't swim uphill. Someone sent it to you. Someone who knew whose son you are."

"My father abandoned us," he said. Flat. Final. A door closing on a room she could hear burning behind it. "When I was fifteen. I woke up and he was gone and the blacklist notice came a month later with my name on it, and I've been living above the snowline ever since because down there, my name is worth money to the wrong people."

"I know what a blacklist is," Ilse said. "My whole nation is on one."

The kettle shrieked. This time he moved, poured the water over tea leaves that smelled of pine and something older, and pushed a tin cup toward her across the table. Their fingers didn't touch. But he waited until she drank before he drank, and she noted that—a courtesy so rusted with disuse it was almost ceremonial—and something in the room loosened by a single degree.

Outside, full dark had come down. Inside, the lamp threw their shadows huge against the stone, two shapes bent over a table like figures in an old fresco.

"Show me," he said finally. "Show me what you know. All of it. And then I'll decide whether you sleep under my roof or in the lean-to."

"That's fair," Ilse said, and meant it, and reached for her pack.

But as she drew out the oilcloth-wrapped folder of copies—her life's work, her father's legacy, the three boxes distilled to paper—she heard it, faint beneath the wind, carried up the valley on the night air: a sound she had been dreading for three days and knew better than her own heartbeat.

Hooves. More than one horse. Far off still, down where the valley narrowed, but moving. Moving fast. Moving *up*.

Ilse closed her eyes. Two days behind had become one.

"Mr. Vane," she said, opening her eyes, "how attached are you to that hut?"
