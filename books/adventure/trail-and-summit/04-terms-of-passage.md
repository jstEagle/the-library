## 4. Terms of Passage

She would not leave.

Edmund had made that clear in the first hour, standing in his doorway with the chest between them like a wall, and she had made it equally clear by sitting down on his one stool, unwinding her wet scarf, and saying, "Then I'll say what I came to say while you decide whether to throw me out into the rain."

The rain had followed her up the switchbacks. It came against the shutters now in long gray sheets, and the fire he'd built—built for himself, alone, the way he built everything—hissed and spat with the damp she'd carried in on her boots. She sat with her hands wrapped around the mug of barley tea he'd given her because refusing hospitality was a habit he couldn't break even when he wanted to, and she looked at him the way no one had looked at him in twelve years: steadily, without fear, without flattery, as if he were a fact she had verified.

"Ilse Brenner," she had said, before he asked. "Archivist, third rank. Or I was, when there was an archive to rank in."

Now he stood with his back to the hearth and his arms crossed, and the chest sat on his table between them, its brass fittings still weeping river water onto the wood, its seal—that absurd, beautiful, impossible seal, the double mountain and the star—staring up at the ceiling of a hut that had no business holding it.

"You traced it here," he said. "Ten years, you said. Through what?"

"Registries. Smugglers' ledgers. A ferry manifest from the year of the annexation that listed a chest of documents crossing the Marlow with a family called Vane." She sipped her tea. "The family didn't cross. Only the chest did. It went upriver, then overland, then it disappeared into the estates of a collector named Aldous Crane, and then—forty years later, three months ago—it disappeared again. Stolen from Crane's own vault. He wants it back. Or rather, he wants it destroyed, and he's not particular about which."

"You know this how?"

"Because I've been following the people he sends to follow it." Her eyes came up. "There are four of them. Armed. Two days behind me, perhaps less now. Their leader is called Voss."

The name landed in the hut and sat there. Edmund found he had gone very still, the old stillness, the animal stillness that had kept him alive through years he did not talk about.

"Then you should keep moving," he said. "And I should burn that chest, and you should be gone before the ash cools."

"Yes," she agreed pleasantly. "That's one plan. May I tell you the other one?"

He didn't answer, which in his experience was as good as an invitation, and she took it as one.

* * *

She talked for a long time. She talked the way he imagined she did everything—precisely, without ornament, each fact laid down like a stone in a wall he could check for gaps.

There had been a tribunal. There would be one again. The Concord Powers, who had carved up Kestria forty years ago and erased it from every map and every ledger, had written into their own annexation treaties a clause so cynical it circled around to become a door: *should credible evidence emerge that persons of Kestrian registry survived as persons, their legal existence may be restored by review.* The conquerors had put it in to soothe their consciences. No credible evidence had ever emerged, because the conquerors had spent forty years making sure none survived—burning registries, scattering families, buying or burying every document that proved a Kestrian had drawn breath after the annexation.

"Except this one," Ilse said, resting her hand on the chest's wet lid. "This is a survival registry. Compiled in the last weeks before the fall by the royal cartographer and the queen's household. Names. Birth records. Lines of descent. Proof, in the conquerors' own required form, with their own required seals, that persons of Kestrian registry lived on as persons. My father helped carry it out of the capital. He died getting it to the river." Her voice didn't change, but something behind it did. "It can restore us, Vane. Not symbolically. Legally. Land titles, names on doors, the right to exist in the eyes of every court from here to the coast. The tribunal convenes at the end of the dry season. In the lowland capital. If this chest is on their table when they sit, Kestria stops being a ghost."

The fire popped. Rain ran down the window glass in braids.

"And you need me for this because—"

"Because the chest's route to the capital runs through the Serpent Range, and the dry season ends in six weeks, and I have spent ten years learning documents and no years learning mountains." She said it without shame, which he grudgingly respected. "Because every road down from this range ends at a checkpoint, and every checkpoint has my face in a book by now. And because—" she paused, and he saw her choose her words with the same care she chose everything, "—because the registry has a central figure it protects. An heir. And the heir's name, as written in the birth record you already found, is Edmund Vane."

"I read the record," he said. "It's a mistake. Or a forgery. Or—"

"Or it's your mother's name in your father's handwriting, which you know it is, because you recognized the hand. I watched your face when you pried that corner open. You knew."

He turned away from her then, to the window, to the rain. Below the hut the valley was drowning quietly, gray on gray, and somewhere in that gray were four armed men following a wet woman's trail up his mountain.

"Whatever I am," he said, "I'm a blacklisted man. You understand what that means? Not wanted. Not merely wanted—*listed*. There's a standing order. Any border, any checkpoint, any garrison clerk with a stamp and a grudge. They don't even need a reason. They see the name Vane and I go into a room, and rooms like that don't have windows." He laughed, short and ugly. "You want to carry a survival registry across six borders with a man whose name is a passkey to a cell. That's not a plan, archivist. That's a eulogy with extra steps."

"Then we don't use your name."

"The name is the face. The face is on the lists."

"Then we go where there are no lists." She rose, finally, and came to stand beside him at the window, and he hated that she didn't ask permission, and he hated more that he didn't move away. "The high passes. The old Kestrian routes your father mapped. The ones the garrisons stopped patrolling because they stopped believing anyone could cross them." She looked up at him. "You crossed them. Twelve years ago. That's how a blacklisted cartographer's son vanished so completely that even I spent a decade finding nothing but rumors. You didn't hide, Edmund. You went up. Nobody goes up."

"Nobody sane."

"Nobody free," she said.

* * *

He refused her. He wanted that on the record, whatever record there was, in whatever hut of the world kept accounts of men like him.

He refused her over the second mug of tea, and the third, and then over the cold supper he fixed because she looked like a woman who hadn't eaten properly in a week and his hands made food the way his hands did everything, automatically, while the rest of him stood apart. He refused her with reasons: the season, the mercenaries, the blacklist, the sheer arithmetic of six weeks and two hundred miles of the worst terrain on the continent. He refused her with the truth, which was that he had spent twelve years building a life with no seams in it—no debts, no names shared, no one whose grief would be his grief—and he did not intend to unpick it for a dead country and a dead father and a birth record that was probably a forgery.

"Probably," Ilse said quietly, when he ran out of reasons. "You said probably. A man certain of nothing would have said certainly."

He didn't answer that.

He refused her a final time near midnight, standing at the door with it open to the rain, and she gathered her scarf and her satchel and thanked him for the tea with a formality that stung worse than anger would have, and he said, "There's a hay barn in the lower valley, half a mile past the ford. Dry. Nobody uses it. If you're set on dying, at least die warm," and she looked at him for a long moment with those gray, unflinching eyes and said, "Thank you," again, and went out into the dark.

He stood in the doorway longer than he meant to, listening to her boots fade down the wet trail, and told himself the tightness in his chest was relief.

* * *

He didn't sleep.

He sat by the fire instead, and the chest sat on the table, and the two of them regarded each other across the small room like old enemies at a truce table. Finally, because sleep was not coming and the fire was dying and a man can only lose an argument with a box for so long, he got up and pried the lid fully open for the first time.

Oilcloth, in layers. Documents in a script he half-remembered from a childhood he'd spent trying to forget—the old Kestrian hand, tall and slanted, like the mountains it described. A locket wrapped in silk, heavy, cold. And beneath everything, folded into a packet sealed with wax, the map.

He knew before he unfolded it. He knew by the weight of the paper, by the way it crackled, by the smell of the ink that had faded but not died. His father's map. The Serpent Range, drawn in the royal hand, every pass and saddle and glacier named in the old tongue—and in the margins, in the smaller, quicker script that his father used for notes, annotations. *Water unreliable after the third col. Shelter at the chapel—chimney draws. The Stair of Winds passable only before first snow.*

His father had drawn a route. Not the official routes, the caravan routes, the ones with checkpoints and garrisons. A smuggler's route, a fugitive's route, a route for a man with no name carrying a chest that could raise a country from the dead—drawn, Edmund understood with a cold spreading certainty, *for this*. For the chest. For whoever carried it. Perhaps, in some corner of a doomed man's mind, for his son.

He sat down on the floor with his back against the table leg, the map open across his knees, and the grief came up out of the place where he kept it, the deep sealed place, and it was not only grief. It was grief and fury in one vein, twined like the double mountain on the seal, and he could not tell which was which, and he hated his father for that too.

*You left,* he said to the ink. *You left me with a name that was a sentence and a mother who never told me why, and then you died in a capital that burned, and now—what? Now you send me a map? Now you give me a road?*

The fire settled. The rain went on. And the map lay across his knees like an open hand.

* * *

He caught up with her at the ford, an hour before dawn.

She was standing at the edge of the water—it had come up with the rain, black and fast, chewing at the crossing stones—and she turned when she heard him, and she didn't look surprised. That was the worst of it. She had known, somehow, that he would come. She had bet ten years of her life on a stranger's conscience, and she stood at the flooded ford at four in the morning and did not look surprised.

"The barn was dry," she said. "Thank you."

"You can't cross that. Not tonight. Not alone."

"No," she agreed. "Not alone."

He stood there in the dark with the rain finding the gap between his collar and his neck, and he heard himself say the thing he had come down the mountain to say, the thing he had been arguing against since she first sat down on his stool, and his voice came out flat and hard, like a man reading terms.

"I'll take you as far as the first pass. The low saddle. After that you find your own way, or you turn back, or the mountain decides. I'm not an heir. I'm not a symbol. I'm a guide, and I'm a guide for six weeks, and then I'm a ghost again. Those are the terms."

"Those are the terms," she repeated.

"And I want something."

She waited. She was good at waiting; he was learning that about her.

"Who informed on my father." The words came out of him like something drawn from a wound. "You said the registry would tell me things. You said you'd spent ten years in the documents. You know the names around the fall. You know who signed the orders, who sold the passes, who gave up the queen's household to the annexation forces." He stepped closer, and the ford roared behind him, and he heard his own voice drop. "My father was arrested eight days before the capital fell. Someone told the Concord forces where he was, what he carried, who he loved. Someone made him into a traitor in the eyes of his own country and a dead man in the eyes of mine. I have spent twelve years not knowing, and I am done not knowing. You give me the name, Ilse. You give me the name, and I'll carry that chest to the capital myself."

The rain came down between them. She looked at him for a long moment, and he saw—not pity, he would have struck out at pity, but something adjacent to it, something older and more careful.

"I don't know the name," she said.

The word hit him like a fall.

"What I know," she went on, before he could turn away, "is where the name is. The registry has marginalia—the cartographer's hand, the queen's household, the people who compiled it. And there is a page, a sealed page, that my father told me about with his last breath, that names the informant. It's in the chest, Edmund. It has been in the chest for forty years, riding up rivers and into vaults and out again, waiting." She held his eyes. "I can't give you the name. I can give you the chest that holds it. Those are my terms."

The ford thundered. The rain came down. And Edmund Vane, who had spent twelve years wanting nothing, stood at the edge of the black water and understood that he had already crossed it—that the man who had walked down from his hut an hour ago had been the last of a certain kind of man, and the one standing here now, soaked and hollow and hungry, was something else.

"First light," he said. "We go at first light. And if the page is a lie, or empty, or gone—"

"Then you'll have carried it anyway," she said, "and you'll have to decide what that made you."

He turned back up the trail toward the hut, to pack, to burn what couldn't be carried, to take one last look at the twelve years of measured life he was about to walk away from. Behind him the ford roared, and Ilse Brenner stood at its edge, and far down the valley—so far he might have imagined it, but he didn't think he did—a light moved through the trees. Steady. Methodical. Searching the dark the way a man searches a room he intends to own.

Two days, she had said.

Edmund started to run.
