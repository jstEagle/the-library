## 5. Two Days Behind

The postmaster of Ferrow was a small man with ink-stained fingers and the particular sweat of someone who had spent his whole life deciding which letters to read first.

Voss let him sweat. He had learned long ago that silence did more work than questions, and he was a patient man in the way that stone is patient—without effort, without end. He stood at the counter of the village post office while rain ticked against the shutters, and he watched the postmaster's eyes travel from Voss's face to the four men standing behind him and back again, doing arithmetic with his own pulse.

"You received a parcel," Voss said at last. "Eleven days ago. By river barge."

The postmaster's throat moved. "We receive many parcels."

"Not like this one." Voss set his hand flat on the counter. He wore no gloves; his hands were the only part of him he never covered, because they were the only part of him that had never lied. "A chest. Sealed. Heavy enough that the bargeman complained about it in three villages downstream, because bargemen always complain. It came upriver on the *Kestrel's Wake*, out of Lowmarsh, consigned to no one. The manifest would say so."

"I couldn't say what the manifests—"

"Your ledgers are public record," Voss said mildly. "I've already read them. I'm not here for the ledgers."

That landed the way he intended it to land. The postmaster's gaze flicked sideways—to the back room, where the sorting shelves stood, where anything worth hiding would be hidden—and Voss watched the flick happen as if the man had drawn him a map.

"Then what are you here for?"

"The truth about what you did with it." Voss leaned forward slightly. He kept his voice level, almost kind. Men expected menace to arrive loud; the quiet version unsettled them faster. "You opened it. Or you were tempted to. A sealed chest from a drowned nation, delivered by floodwater to your door—that's not a parcel, friend, that's a temptation. And I can see it sitting on you like damp."

* * *

He had been two days behind the chest since Lowmarsh. Two days of reading the river's spoor: the bargeman who remembered hauling something too heavy for its declared weight, the ferry-woman who'd seen a stranger pay in old coin, the mud-stain on a warehouse floor shaped like a chest's corner. The chest had a way of leaving traces, because everything touched by Kestria left traces—it was a country that had been erased from maps but not from habits, not from the muscle-memory of a people who still set their tables the old way when they thought no one watched.

Voss knew this better than anyone alive. He had been one of those traces himself, once. A child with a name no one could pronounce anymore, shipped east in a column of orphans while the annexation officers burned registries in the town squares. He had arrived at the conquerors' school with nothing—not even a language to dream in—and he had been remade there, efficiently, thoroughly, into a man who belonged to no registry at all. That was the empire's gift to him. That was what he carried instead of a homeland.

And now some archivist or sentimentalist wanted to undo forty years of clean forgetting with a box of paper.

His employer had been explicit. Herr Aldous Crane owned, through eleven shell companies and two generations of careful marriage, most of the lowland grazing rights and half the river tolls that had once been Kestrian crown lands. Crane's lawyers had built an empire on the legal fact that Kestria did not exist—if the nation had never lawfully fallen, only been erased, then every title derived from the annexation was a forgery wearing a judge's signature. A single authenticated document proving survival of the royal line, presented before a tribunal with jurisdiction, would unravel all of it. Not just Crane's holdings. Every holding. Forty years of other men's property, suddenly haunted.

So the chest had to burn. Not be stolen—burned. Stolen documents could resurface; burned documents were ash, and ash argued nothing. Crane had said it plainly over brandy in a room with maps on the walls: *There is no such thing as a country that exists only in a chest, Voss. Make sure it stays that way.*

Voss had made sure of harder things.

* * *

The postmaster's name was Ellery. Voss knew this because everyone's name was knowable if you bothered to learn it, and knowing a man's name before he offered it told him exactly how much power he had in the conversation. He used it now, gently.

"Ellery. Look at me."

Ellery looked. His eyes were wet-rimmed and darting, and Voss saw the moment the man gave up whatever small resistance he'd been nursing—the moment the arithmetic finished and the answer came out unfavorable to courage.

"It went up the mountain," Ellery whispered. "Two nights ago. A carrier took it—I don't know his name, he paid cash, he had a mule. The high trail. Toward the huts above the treeline."

"Why?"

"He didn't say. But—" Ellery swallowed. "But there was a woman asking after it too. Day before yesterday. Dark-haired, road-worn, spoke our valley dialect with an accent underneath it. She asked who'd taken delivery of river freight. She climbed the same direction."

Voss absorbed this without moving. So. The archivist Ilse Brenner had found the scent at last—ten years of her sniffing along the paper trails of Europe, and here she was, two days ahead of him and climbing toward the same hut. And between them, a carrier with a mule and a chest that weighed as much as a sin.

"Describe the carrier."

"Tall. Grey in his beard. Walked like—" Ellery hesitated, then, helplessly: "Like a soldier who'd decided to stop being one. You know how they walk."

Voss did know. He had trained enough of them.

"And the woman—did she leave anything? A name?"

"She signed the freight inquiry book." Ellery's hands trembled as he turned it around. "*I. Brenner.* She wrote it like she wanted to be found."

Of course she had. Archivists believed in records the way priests believed in prayer—that writing a thing down made it true, made it safe, made it *matter*. Voss had watched a whole country write itself down and burn anyway. He had no faith in ink.

He closed the book. "You're going to forget we spoke."

"I—yes. Of course."

"No." Voss leaned closer, and for the first time let the cold come up through his voice, the cold he kept down at the bottom of himself like water at the bottom of a well. "Not *of course*. Forgetting is a discipline, Ellery. You'll practice it. If anyone asks whether strangers passed through Ferrow asking about a chest, you'll have a headache that day, and a bad memory, and no opinion. Because if word reaches the wrong ears that you talked—" he straightened, and smiled, and the smile reached none of the way up, "—well. Empires are built on paperwork, my friend. And so are gallows."

Behind him, one of his men shifted—a small sound, leather and intent—and Ellery went grey as porridge.

"We'll need horses," Voss said. "And a boy to take them back down after. Put it on Crane's account. The empire pays its debts." He paused at the door, rain rattling above him, and looked back at the little man clutching his freight ledger like a shield. "One more thing. The chest—when the carrier loaded it. Was it sealed?"

Ellery blinked. "Sealed? Yes. Wax. A crest of some kind. A bird, I think. Or—"

"A kestrel," Voss said. "Yes. I know the bird."

He stepped out into the rain.

* * *

They rode out of Ferrow within the hour, four men and Voss, up the valley road where the mountains began their long argument with the sky. The rain was turning to sleet at the higher switchbacks, and the trail ahead climbed into cloud the color of dishwater.

Sarel, his lieutenant, drew alongside. Sarel had been with him six years—competent, incurious, the kind of soldier who followed orders the way a hammer follows a swing. "The woman's two days ahead. The carrier maybe three. In this weather, on foot—"

"On foot they're slow," Voss agreed. "On foot they're also quiet, and the high country has a thousand places to hide a chest and a hundred to hide the people carrying it. We don't catch them by riding fast. We catch them by knowing where they must go."

"Which is where?"

Voss gazed up at the wall of the Serpent Range, white-shouldered under the cloud, beautiful the way a blade is beautiful. Somewhere up there, a man he had never met was carrying the last proof of a dead nation toward a pass that would close with the snows. Somewhere up there, Ilse Brenner was chasing a country she'd lost before she was old enough to remember losing it.

"There's one tribunal," he said. "It sits at the end of the dry season, in the lowlands, across the range. There's one window when the passes are open. Whatever they intend, they intend to cross before the snows—which means they follow the old Kestrian roads, because the imperial routes are watched and they know it. And the old roads all funnel through the high huts above the treeline." He turned his collar up against the sleet. "A mountain doesn't offer many doors, Sarel. You just have to be standing at them when they open."

"And if they don't use the doors?"

"Then the weather will do our work for us." Voss said it without satisfaction. The early storms had been whispering in the north for days—he'd felt it in his knees, in the way the birds flew low, in the restlessness of the horses. The dry season was dying ahead of schedule, and the Serpent Range did not forgive schedule-keepers. "Snow closes the passes, the chest freezes on the wrong side of the range, the tribunal convenes and adjourns, and Crane keeps his empire. We wouldn't even need to find them."

"But we're going to find them anyway."

"Yes." Voss watched the trail wind upward, vanishing into the cloud. "Because burning is cleaner than waiting. Ash doesn't melt in spring."

They climbed. Behind them, far below, the lights of Ferrow shrank to embers in the wet dark, and somewhere beyond those lights, down the long thread of the river, a sealed chest bearing a kestrel in wax had already changed hands, changed lives, and started a clock that nobody could see ticking—but Voss could hear it. He had heard it for forty years, ever since a column of children walked east out of a country that had stopped existing, and one of those children had grown up learning that the past was a debt collectors came for.

He meant to pay this one off in fire.

Above the treeline, the sleet thickened. Somewhere ahead, in the white blindness of the cloud, a light burned in a high hut—and two days behind it, closing like a hand, came Voss.
