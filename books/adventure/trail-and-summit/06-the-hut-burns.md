## 6. The Hut Burns

They came down from the hut in the grey hour before dawn, when the mountain gives you its coldest honesty and no lies about the day ahead. Edmund went first, breaking trail through the crust, and Ilse followed in his steps because he told her to and because she was smart enough to know that a man who had lived twelve years alone on this flank of the Serpent Range knew where the snow held and where it lied.

The trailhead was three miles below the hut, a notch in the treeline where an old logging road died against the first real slope. From there you could see the whole southern approach: the switchbacks climbing out of Ferrow valley, the black spines of pine, the frozen creek that would be their water for the first two days if they moved fast.

Edmund crouched at the edge of the trees and studied it the way he used to study a route before he'd trusted anyone else to climb it. Old habit. Old life. He hated how easily his hands remembered.

"You're not looking at the trail," Ilse said behind him. Her voice was hoarse from two days of hard walking and thin air. "You're looking at the road."

"I'm looking at both."

"Which one worries you?"

"The one with dust on it." He nodded downslope, to where the logging road curved away toward the valley. In the dry season, dust meant wheels. Wheels meant men who hadn't walked up. "Somebody's been moving on it. Two days, maybe three. The ruts are fresh enough that the frost hasn't filled them."

Ilse was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a woman counting. "Voss travels with six. Maybe eight. He hires local muscle when he needs numbers, but the core are his own—men who've been with him since the annexation archives were stripped. They don't stop. I've watched them stop for nothing in ten years of running ahead of them."

"Then we don't run ahead anymore." Edmund turned back to look at her. In the pre-dawn grey she looked older than she had in the lamplight of his hut—younger too, somehow, both at once, the way exhaustion strips a face down to what's actually there. "We go up. The Serpent doesn't care how many men Voss has. It cares whether you respect it. We move tonight, take the low saddle before they can close the trailhead, and after that it's just weather and legs."

"And your blacklist?"

"Is a problem for borders, not mountains." He said it like it was settled, because he needed it to be settled, because the other thing—the thing coiling under his ribs since he'd pried open that chest corner and seen his mother's name in faded ink—was not settled at all and might never be. "There is no border above the treeline. There's just the pass, and the season, and us."

Ilse looked at him for a long moment. Then she did something he didn't expect: she smiled, small and tired and real. "You sound like the records say your father sounded. Determination as a kind of armor."

"Don't," Edmund said.

"I'm sorry."

"No, you're not. And you shouldn't be. But don't." He shouldered his pack. "Come on. We scout the descent line while the light's flat, then get back before the sun softens the snow. If we're crossing tonight, I want to know every place this slope can kill us."

* * *

They spent four hours on the descent line, and Edmund hated how good it felt.

That was the truth of it, the truth he'd been refusing for twelve years: he loved this. Not the danger—he'd had enough danger in his old life to last several lifetimes—but the work of it. Reading a slope. Feeling the mountain's grammar come back to him sentence by sentence, clause by clause, until he could parse the whole argument of stone and ice without thinking. His body knew things his mind had tried to bury. Where the cornice would overhang. Where the wind-scour would leave blue ice under the powder. Where a man could traverse at speed and where he'd have to cut steps.

He caught himself explaining a rock band to Ilse with his hands, sketching the line in the air, and stopped mid-sentence.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing."

"You were smiling."

"I wasn't."

"You were. It was terrible. Like watching a statue try to laugh." She said it without malice, which made it worse. "You know, in the archive we have survey notes from the old royal cartographers. Marginalia. Little jokes in the margins of maps that decided where armies marched. I used to think whoever wrote them must have been half-mad, loving something so much that killed people." She paused. "I understand it better now."

"My father drew pretty maps," Edmund said. "And then he drew the ones that got Kestria carved up. Pretty lines, straight lines, the kind you draw for men with guns. So forgive me if my marginalia aren't charming."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. Nothing about this is fair. That's rather the point of the whole exercise." He started back upslope, harder than he needed to, making her work to keep pace. "Let's move. Sun'll be on the snow soon, and I want to be off the open ground."

They were a mile below the hut when the wind changed.

It came down the valley wrong—that was the first thing. Mountain wind has a schedule; it breathes uphill with the warming day and drains down at night, and any man who lives high learns to read its clock the way a farmer reads rain. This wind was downhill and early and it carried a smell that didn't belong: woodsmoke, but thick and chemical underneath, pitch and lamp-oil and something sharper.

Edmund stopped so fast Ilse nearly walked into his back.

"What—" she began.

He raised a hand. Listened. Below them, faint but unmistakable, came a sound he knew from twelve years ago and had hoped never to hear again: voices, many, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who owned whatever ground they stood on.

And above them—above them, where there should have been nothing but his hut and silence—

Smoke.

A grey feather of it, rising off the ridge behind the hut, curling into the clean morning sky.

Edmund ran.

He didn't decide to run; his body simply did it, pack and all, boots finding the old game trails he'd worn smooth over twelve years of solitary supply runs. Behind him he heard Ilse curse and follow. The slope steepened. Pines whipped past. His lungs burned with cold air and something hotter.

They broke out of the trees below the clearing and stopped, both of them, as if the sight were a wall.

His hut was burning.

Not smoldering—burning, fully, furiously, flames standing off the roof in orange banners that snapped in the wind. The oil they'd used was everywhere; the fire had gotten into the walls themselves, into the timber he'd cut and cured himself the first summer, into the stone hearth he'd built stone by stone because mortar was expensive and patience was free. Sparks streamed off the roof like a flock of departing birds. The heat reached them thirty yards out, a physical shove against the face.

And around the clearing, watching it burn with the professional calm of men doing a job, stood five figures in travel-stained gear, armed, unhurried. One of them held a jerry can. Another was feeding papers into the flames—papers Edmund recognized with a sick lurch: his ledgers, his journals, twelve years of weather notes and route sketches and the slow daily record of a contained life.

"Twelve years," Edmund heard himself say. He didn't recognize his own voice. It came out very quiet, which surprised him, because inside he was screaming. "Twelve years I built that."

"Edmund." Ilse's hand found his arm. "Edmund, listen to me. Those are Voss's men. That's the lieutenant—Krayl, the tall one. If they see us—"

"They're going to see us anyway." He was already moving, low, along the tree shadow. "Because I want them to."

"Edmund—"

"There's nothing left to protect," he said, and heard how the words cracked in the middle. "You understand? Twelve years. Every plank. Every note. Everything I owned fit in one room and they're feeding it to the fire piece by piece because some collector in the lowlands is afraid of paper." He ducked a branch. "So now there's nothing left to lose, and that makes me the most dangerous man on this mountain."

He came out of the trees at the edge of the clearing, and the nearest mercenary—a heavyset man with a crossbow slung casual across his back—turned at the sound of boots and had exactly enough time to widen his eyes before Edmund hit him.

It wasn't skill. Later, honestly, Edmund couldn't call it anything but fury wearing the shape of a tackle. They went down together into the trampled snow, and Edmund's fist found the man's jaw once, twice, and then hands were hauling him off—two of them, Krayl among them, wrenching his arms back while the heavyset man rolled coughing to his feet with blood in his mouth.

"Well," Krayl said. He was lean and grey-templed, with the patient eyes of a man who'd done ugly work for a long time and slept fine afterward. "The hermit. Voss said you'd be here. Said you'd probably even watch us do it." He glanced at the burning hut, then back. "Sentimental of you."

"Who paid for my journals?" Edmund's voice was steady. That frightened him more than shouting would have. "The ledgers. Who specifically ordered those burned?"

Krayl smiled thinly. "Orders were total destruction. Man named Voss doesn't leave loose ends. You'd know something about that, hermit—man who disappears off every map for twelve years." He leaned closer. "Here's what happens now. We let you go. That's the message. Voss wants you to run. Wants you running scared and stupid, making mistakes, leading us straight up your precious mountain to wherever you're taking the chest. Saves us the trouble of tracking you." He released Edmund's arm, stepped back, spread his hands. "So run, hermit. Run fast."

For a moment nobody moved. The fire roared. Somewhere in the collapsing structure a beam gave way with a crack like a rifle shot, and a column of sparks climbed into the morning.

Then Edmund bent down, picked up his hat from the snow where it had fallen in the scuffle, and put it on with deliberate calm.

"Tell Voss," he said, "that he just solved my biggest problem."

Krayl's smile flickered. "What problem?"

"Leaving." Edmund turned his back on all of them—an effort of will that cost him more than the fight had—and walked toward the fire.

"Hermit—"

The heat hit him like a hand. At the edge of the burn radius he stopped, and looked at what remained. The chest was inside—or had been. He'd hidden it, after Ilse arrived, in the root cellar beneath the floor, behind a false panel, because he trusted no lock and no distance, only burial. The fire had eaten the floor already; he could see the cellar mouth gaping black through the collapsed boards, and the flames hadn't reached down there yet. Fire eats upward and outward. Cold earth holds.

He waited for a gap in the flame, counted seconds the way you count between lightning and thunder, and when the roof beam groaned and swung outward he moved—three steps into the inferno's skirt, dropping to his knees at the cellar lip, reaching down into smoke and darkness. His fingers found canvas. Found rope handles. He hauled, arms screaming, lungs full of hot ash, and the chest came up out of the earth into the firelight, scorched but sealed, heavy as a coffin and twice as meaningful.

Then he was stumbling back, and Ilse was there, dragging him the last few yards, and they fell together behind the woodpile as part of the hut's front wall folded inward with a sound like a book closing.

From the tree line, Maret's voice—she'd followed them down, of course she had, the girl trailed them like a conscience these days—screamed his name. Doss appeared a moment later, white-faced, axe in hand, clearly prepared to die badly on Edmund's behalf.

"Nobody dies today," Edmund rasped, ash in his throat. "Today we leave."

He knelt beside the chest. Its seal stared up at him through the smoke haze, the crest of dead Kestria, unbroken. With hands that shook only slightly, he pulled his father's map from inside his coat—carried there since the night he'd understood what it was—and checked it against the light. Unburned. Intact. The handwriting that had abandoned him before he could walk, preserved like a saint's relic.

Everything else was smoke. The journals. The ledgers. The kettle he'd drunk from every morning for twelve years. The bed. The shelves. The small, careful architecture of a life designed never to be noticed, never to be taken, never to matter enough to destroy.

Gone in forty minutes.

He should have felt destroyed. Instead—and this was the thing he would remember, the thing that would keep him awake on the passes to come—he felt lighter. Terribly, terribly lighter. As though the fire had burned not just the hut but the twelve years themselves, the whole measured, armored, anonymous existence, down to the foundation stones. What was left was the chest, and the map, and the people kneeling around him in the snow looking at him like he was something worth following.

Ilse touched his shoulder. "Edmund. Are you—"

"No." He stood. Slinging the chest onto his back, adjusting straps with hands that had finally stopped shaking, he turned to face the others: Ilse with her archive-hardened eyes, Doss gripping his axe like a talisman, Maret wide-eyed and soot-smudged and unafraid. "But I'm done being a hermit. Krayl says Voss wants us running scared. Fine. Let's disappoint him—we'll run angry instead."

Doss almost grinned. "Where to?"

Edmund looked up-slope, past the burning ruin of his old life, to where the Serpent Range's first true ridgeline cut the sky like a blade. Snowfields. Saddle. Pass. All of it waiting, indifferent and magnificent.

"We go up," he said. "Tonight, before they can regroup. Low saddle, then the high country. And we don't stop until the mountain tells us to."

Behind them, the last wall of the hut fell in, sending up a geyser of sparks that the wind took east, toward the pass, like an omen or a blessing.

Maret slipped her hand into his—soot and all—and squeezed once.

Edmund Vane, blacklisted cartographer, orphaned son of a traitor, heir to a kingdom of ash and paper, took one long look at the smoke where his life used to be. Then he turned his back on it forever, and started to climb.
