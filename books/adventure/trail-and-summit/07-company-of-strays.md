## 7. Company of Strays

Ferrow smelled of wet wool, woodsmoke, and the particular sourness of a river town that had learned to expect floods. Ilse stood at the edge of the market square with her hood up and the chest's weight distributed across her back in a way that was starting to feel permanent, watching Edmund Vane argue with a fishmonger over the price of dried trout as though the fate of a nation were not folded into the oilcloth bundle between them.

He haggled like a man who had nothing to lose and intended to keep it that way.

"You're staring," he said without turning around. He had a habit of knowing where she was in a room, or on a street, or on a mountainside, that she found equal parts reassuring and unnerving. "Buy your own fish."

"I'm cataloguing," she said.

"Cataloguing."

"The town. Its people. Who watches us, who doesn't." She let her gaze drift along the covered stalls, the leaning timber houses with their flood-marks painted on the doorposts—*1871, 1874, 1879*—a ladder of disasters climbing toward the eaves. "A border town takes in strays. That's what I'm counting on."

Edmund finally turned, a paper twist of trout in his fist. Up close his face had the geography of a man who'd spent twelve years alone: deep lines carved by wind, eyes that did their work at a distance. "We don't need strays. We need to move. Every day we stand in a market square is a day Voss gains."

"And every day we walk into the Serpent Range alone is a day we die of something preventable." She said it quietly, but she didn't soften it. She had learned, in ten years of archival work, that the truth delivered gently was the truth most easily shelved and forgotten. "You know the passes. You don't know the lowland routes to reach them, not anymore, not since they blacklisted you. You can't buy passage on a ferry. You can't sleep in an inn. You are, legally speaking, a ghost—and ghosts get reported."

His jaw worked. The fishmonger was pretending very hard to arrange his stall.

"I know a ferryman here," Edmund said at last, and there was something reluctant in it, something dragged up from a depth he didn't visit willingly. "Or I knew one. Twelve years ago."

"That sounds like the beginning of a story."

"It isn't a story. It's a debt." He shouldered past her toward the river, and Ilse, adjusting the chest's straps, followed.

* * *

The ferry landing at Ferrow was a single rotting pier and a flat-bottomed boat that had been patched so many times its hull looked like a quilt. The man hauling weed from the mooring lines was broad as a door, gray at the temples, with forearms that could have cracked walnuts for a living. When he saw Edmund, the weed went back into the water.

"No," the ferryman said.

"Doss—"

"No." He straightened to his full height, which put him a head above Edmund, and pointed one enormous finger. "Twelve years, Vane. Twelve years I've run this crossing clean. No questions, no names, no trouble. And the first time you show your face on my pier, trouble walks behind you like a dog."

Ilse glanced downstream, casual as a woman admiring the water. Two men in oilskins stood at the far bend of the bank, smoking. They weren't looking at the ferry. They were looking at each other, which was what men did when they were pretending not to watch something else. Mercenaries posted themselves badly when they thought their quarry didn't know a town existed yet. These two knew.

"They're not mine," Doss said, following her glance. His voice dropped. "They came in yesterday. Asking after a man and a woman traveling heavy. Paid the harbormaster in new coin." He spat into the river. "New coin talks loud in Ferrow."

"Then we have until they stop being lazy," Ilse said. "Doss. That's your name? Doss, I need you to take us upstream tonight. Past the checkpoint shallows, to the old quarry landing."

"I need you to get off my pier."

"You owe this man your life." She said it flatly, an entry in a ledger. "I've read the record. River rescue, eleven years ago, spring flood. A ferryman pulled from the water below the falls at Halden Reach. The rescuer's name was redacted—blacklisted men get redacted—but the date matches, and Edmund Vane has never once mentioned it, which is how I know it's true. Men invent their virtues. They forget their debts."

Doss looked at Edmund. Edmund looked at the water.

"She does that," Edmund said. "Reads things. It's insufferable."

"You could have called it in," Doss said quietly. "The reward for turning in a blacklisted man. Could have cleared your own debts with it. Instead you dragged me out and vanished before I woke up." His big hands opened and closed. "I've hated you for eleven years, Vane. It's the only honest thing I own."

"Hate me upstream," Edmund said. "We're paying."

"We're not—" Doss stopped. Rubbed his face with both hands, a man surrendering to weather. "Quarry landing. Tonight, after the lamps go down. If those two at the bend follow us onto the water, I'll put you over the side myself and bill the fishes."

Ilse allowed herself the smallest smile. One.

* * *

They spent the afternoon in the shadow of Ferrow's granary, where the sun never quite reached and the dust made a kind of privacy. Edmund slept, actually slept, sitting upright against the timbers with his hat over his eyes—the sleep of a man who had decided, provisionally and against all evidence, that someone else would watch for danger. Ilse sat with the chest across her knees and did not open it. She had memorized its contents years ago, in copies, in fragments, in the desperate transcriptions her father had smuggled out before the annexation courts burned the originals. But the original chest had a gravity that copies lacked. It was not paper. It was forty years of a country holding its breath.

That was when she noticed the girl.

She was maybe fifteen, maybe sixteen, thin as a fence rail, crouched in the granary's lee twenty feet away, ostensibly mending a net. Except the net had been mended twice over already, and the girl wasn't looking at it. She was looking at the chest—or not at the chest, precisely, at the wax seal impression stamped into its lid, the double-headed kestrel of the old royal house, visible now through the worn oilcloth where the strap had rubbed.

And her lips were moving. Reading the small inscription beneath the crest. *Sigillum Custodum.* Seal of the Keepers.

Old Kestrian script. Dead forty years, taught nowhere, printed nowhere, surviving only in the margins of smuggled documents and the memories of grandmothers who sang lullabies with the words worn smooth off them. Ilse had spent a decade hunting living readers of that script. There were perhaps thirty in the world she knew of. Most were older than the annexation itself.

This one was picking her teeth with the netting needle.

Ilse crossed the distance slowly, the way one approaches a bird. "Where did you learn that?"

The girl didn't flinch, which was the first remarkable thing. She looked up with the flat, unimpressed eyes of someone who had been hungry in cities and had stopped expecting the world to be kind about it. "Learn what?"

"The words under the seal. You were reading them."

"Weren't."

"*Sigillum Custodum,*" Ilse said. "It means the seal belongs to the Keepers—the archivists who carried the registry out when the borders closed. It's a phrase that appears on exactly four kinds of document, all of them Kestrian, all of them illegal to own for the last forty years. So either you read Old Kestrian script, or you're the luckiest illiterate in the valley provinces."

The girl held her gaze for a long moment. Then she grinned, quick and crooked, and Ilse understood that the flatness had been armor and the grin was the person underneath, briefly surfaced. "My grandmother," the girl said. "Before she died. She used to write things down and burn them, so nobody could prove she could. Said the writing was the reason half our street got taken away. But she taught me anyway. Couldn't help herself." She shrugged. "People can't help what they are. They just hide it."

"What's your name?"

"Maret." A pause, and then, defiantly, as if daring Ilse to make something of it: "Maret Ossa."

Ossa. Ilse kept her breathing even, but inside her chest something turned over like soil. Ossa was a Kestrian name—one of the border-family names, the ones that had survived by shortening themselves, by swallowing their vowels, by becoming small enough to hide. This girl was not a curiosity. She was a remnant. She was exactly the kind of person the chest existed for, standing in the dust of a granary with a borrowed needle in her hand.

"Why aren't you home?" Ilse asked. "Fifteen-year-olds who mend nets in granary shadows are usually running from something."

"Fifteen-year-olds who mend nets anywhere are usually running from something." Maret wound the net into a coil with practiced violence. "My uncle sold me to a miller last spring. Domestic labor, three years, room and board. Room was a cellar. Board was what the pigs didn't finish." She said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "I left. Now the miller wants his property back, and the constable agrees with him, because paper beats people in this country. Always has." Her eyes flicked to the chest again, sharp as a heron's. "So. What's a woman doing carrying dead-country paper around Ferrow with two hired knives sniffing the docks?"

Ilse heard Edmund shift behind her, awake now, listening.

"That's a dangerous question," Edmund said.

"Most true ones are." Maret stood, slinging the net over her shoulder like a soldier's pack. "Here's my offer, since nobody's offered me anything better lately. I can read what's in that box—all of it, the old script, the abbreviations, the clerk's shorthand they used before the annexation. Grandmother made me copy documents till my fingers bled. Said somebody had to remember how." She stuck out a hand, grimy, steady. "In exchange, you take me wherever you're going, and when we get there, I disappear into whatever country you're building. New name. New papers. No miller."

Silence. Down at the river, the two men in oilskins had moved closer to the pier. Ilse noted it and filed it.

"You understand what we're carrying could get you killed," Edmund said. "Not might. Could. There are men whose entire fortune depends on that box burning."

"There are men whose entire fortune depends on me going back to a cellar," Maret said. "I've already picked my dangerous. Yours comes with mountains. I've heard the Serpents kill slower, but at least they're honest about wanting to." She wiggled her fingers. "Hand."

Edmund looked at Ilse. Ilse looked at the hand. Somewhere in her ribs, beneath ten years of discipline, hope was doing something undignified.

"She reads the script," Ilse said. "We take her."

"We don't know her."

"We know her grandmother wrote and burned documents to protect a family that was taken away anyway. We know she ran rather than be owned. We know she read a dead language off a moving strap at twenty feet." Ilse took Maret's hand and shook it, once, firmly. "In my profession, that's a full character reference."

Maret's grin came back, wider this time. "I like her," she told Edmund. "You should listen to her more. You've got the face of a man who doesn't listen to anybody, and it's making you stupid."

"God help me," Edmund said, "there are two of them now."

* * *

They boarded at moonrise, when Ferrow's lamps guttered low and the checkpoint shallows were guarded by one bored sentry who spent most of his shift warming his hands over a brazier. Doss poled them upstream in silence, the great flat boat sliding through black water, the chest lashed amidships under sacking, Maret crouched in the bow like a figurehead, Edmund aft with his eyes on the dark banks.

Ilse sat beside the chest and felt, for the first time in ten years, something she refused to call hope because hope was a luxury archivists couldn't afford. Call it instead a growing collection. A ferryman with a debt. A runaway with a dead language in her head. A guide with a blacklist and a map in his father's hand. Herself, with a decade of other people's smuggled paper and no country to file it in.

Strays. All of them strays, gathered by accident and necessity into the shape of something that almost resembled a household, though not one of them would have admitted it under torture.

"Quarry landing's an hour," Doss rumbled, breaking his silence for the first time since the pier. "Then you're walking. Range trail starts above the scree, and after the first frost you'll want to be over the low saddle before the ice sets." He glanced back at Edmund, and in the moonlight his expression was complicated—anger and loyalty braided together so long they'd become indistinguishable. "Eleven years I figured you for dead, Vane. Or worse, alive and rich somewhere, having forgotten."

"I don't forget," Edmund said.

"No," Doss agreed. "That's the problem with you. Never forgets anything. Never lets anything go either." He planted the pole and shoved, and the boat surged against the current. "Whatever's in that box—it better be worth what it costs. Everything costs, out here. Everything."

Ahead, the Serpent Range rose against the stars, a jagged absence where the sky should have been, and Ilse watched it grow while the checkpoint fires shrank behind them, and thought of her father's last letter, and the tribunal convening in six weeks, and the two men in oilskins who would find an empty pier come morning and start asking harder questions.

Behind them, faint on the night wind, a whistle sounded from the direction of the landing. Then another, farther east, answering.

Doss's pole paused mid-stroke. "That'll be them finding the harbormaster," he said quietly. "Hold on to something. From here on, we row like the river owes us money."

Edmund was already uncoiling the spare line, checking knots, his hands moving with the calm of a man returning to a trade. Maret leaned forward into the current like she'd been born to it. And Ilse put both arms around the chest and held on, as the boat leapt upstream into the dark, toward the mountains, pursued.
