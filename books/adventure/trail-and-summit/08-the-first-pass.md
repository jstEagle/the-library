## 8. The First Pass

Edmund woke before the light did, the way he always had in the mountains, and for a moment he lay still in the gray cold of the tent and listened to the sound that had woken him.

Wind. But not the flat, mindless wind of the lowlands that pushed at a man like a hand on his chest. This was wind moving through stone—a long exhale through the throat of the Serpent Range, pitched low and constant, the sound of air finding its way over rock it had known for ten thousand years. He had not heard it in twelve years. He had told himself he hadn't missed it.

He got up quietly so as not to wake Maret, who slept with her arms wrapped around her pack like a child holding a dog, and stepped out into the blue hour before dawn.

The saddle was close. He could feel it now, the way you could feel a door in a dark room—the way the land narrowed ahead of them, funneling toward the low notch between two shoulders of granite where the Serpent Range dipped like a serpent's spine between vertebrae. The old maps called it the First Saddle, though no map had called anything Kestrian by its right name in forty years. His father's map called it something else. Edmund had read the name three times by firelight and then folded the map away, because the handwriting had made his chest hurt in a way he didn't have words for yet.

*The Shepherd's Notch,* his father had written, in a small careful script, and beneath it: *Cross before noon. The wind owns the afternoon.*

His father had been dead to him for twelve years and dead in truth for longer than that, and still the man was giving him advice across the distance, and still the advice was good.

Edmund stood in the cold and let the wind move through him and found, to his surprise, that his body remembered everything. Where to place weight on scree. How to read the frost patterns on north-facing stone. Which of the stars overhead were worth trusting and which were liars this time of year. Twelve years of living small in a high hut, of measuring out his days like a miser measures coin—and underneath all of it, the mountain knowledge had sat waiting like a banked fire, patient, unoffended, ready to be fed.

He almost smiled. He caught himself doing it and stopped, the way a man stops himself from speaking to someone dead.

Behind him, the tent rustled. Ilse emerged, hair braided tight for travel, the chest's strap already over her shoulder—she slept with it near enough to touch, and he'd stopped commenting on it days ago. She looked up at the ridge line, then at him.

"How far?"

"Three hours to the saddle. Four if we're soft about it." He nodded east, where the sky was going from black to iron. "We'll cross before noon."

"That's ahead of your own schedule."

"The weather's ahead of my schedule too." He said it lightly, but she caught the edge under it. She was good at that. Too good, some days.

"You've been watching the sky," she said.

"I'm always watching the sky."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "You've been *watching* it. Like it owes you money."

Edmund huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh and went to wake the others.

* * *

Doss moved like a man crossing a courtroom—head down, guilty, determined to get to the other side without being asked questions. He was big through the shoulders from thirty years of hauling ferry cable and too heavy for steep ground, and he hated every degree of slope with a personal hatred, but he never complained, because complaining would have meant admitting the mountains had an opinion about him, and Doss could not bear to be noticed by anything larger than himself.

Maret, by contrast, went up the trail like water goes uphill when it's angry about it—fast, scrambling, falling twice as often as necessary and pretending each fall had been intentional. She was sixteen and made of spite and curiosity in roughly equal measure, and she had appointed herself keeper of the map, which meant she walked beside Edmund and watched his face while he navigated, cataloguing every expression for later use.

They climbed through the morning. The trail was barely a trail—an old Kestrian track, cut into the hillside and abandoned, half-swallowed by scree and dwarf pine. In places the original paving stones showed through, fitted without mortar, each one shaped to its neighbor. Forty years of nobody maintaining them and they still held. Edmund ran his boot along one and thought: *we built things to last, whoever we were.* He didn't say it aloud. He was not yet a man who said things like that aloud.

By midmorning they came up onto the shoulder below the saddle, and the world opened.

To the west, behind them, the valley they'd climbed out of lay in folds of green and shadow, the river a bright thread stitched down its middle. To the east, beyond the notch, the range fell away into country none of them had seen—high rolling tundra, brown-gold in the dry season, cut by the silver scars of meltwater streams, and beyond that, faint as breath on glass, the white teeth of the high Serpents themselves.

Maret stopped walking. Actually stopped, mid-stride, mouth open.

"It's *big*," she said, in the tone of someone who has just discovered that all previous uses of the word *big* were lies.

"It gets bigger," Edmund said.

"Shut up." But she said it softly, reverently, her eyes still wide on the horizon.

Ilse stood with the chest against her hip and looked east for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had changed—it had dropped into something older and more formal, the voice Edmund had begun to recognize as her archive voice, the voice she used for things that mattered.

"My father described this view in a letter. He crossed here in the last year, before the annexation. He wrote—" she paused, and Edmund realized she was reciting from memory, word-perfect, the way she seemed to hold entire documents in her head like stones in a wall— "*From the First Saddle the whole of the Serpent spine is visible, and a traveler might believe, for one hour, that nothing had been lost.*"

Nobody said anything. The wind moved through the notch ahead of them with its long low exhale, and it sounded, just for a moment, less like weather and more like an answer.

Edmund checked the sun, checked the cloud shadow crawling up the far valley, checked the frost line on the northern faces—all of it in about four seconds, without deciding to, the way a musician hears wrong notes without trying. Then he led them up the last pitch to the saddle itself.

* * *

The crossing took twenty minutes and was nothing—a broad stony notch, a cairn at its center stacked by hands long gone, a view on both sides. Any guide in the lowlands would have called it unremarkable.

Edmund stood at the cairn and felt something in his chest unlock that he had not known was locked.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the strange part. He'd expected—when he'd let himself expect anything—that returning to the high country would feel like opening a wound. Instead it felt like putting on a coat that fit. His feet knew the ground. His eyes knew what to look for and what to ignore. The wind came through the notch and he leaned into it slightly, automatically, angling his body the way you do after years of it, and he understood that he had spent twelve years standing crooked in a room built for someone else.

The mountains spoke to him again. That was the only way he could ever describe it, and he'd never tried describing it to anyone, because it sounded like madness or religion and it was neither. It was simply that stone and sky and snow carried information, endless information, if you had learned the language—and he had learned it young, at his father's knee, on maps drawn in a hand that had loved these shapes—and twelve years of silence had not erased a single word of it. The scree slope to the south was freshly moved; there'd been a slide within the month. The snowfield in the eastern couloir was rotten at its base; it would let go with the first real storm. The clouds building over the high peaks were pretty and meaningless, but the haze layer down-valley was not, and the wind had shifted a quarter-point north since dawn, and both of those together meant—

"Uncle Edmund is smiling."

He turned. Maret was sitting on the cairn, legs dangling, watching him with the expression of a cat that has found something alive in the woodpile.

"I'm not."

"Your face is doing a thing. I've been watching it for six days. It does exactly two things: *careful* and *carefuler*. This is a third thing." She pointed. "That's a smile. A real one. Ilse! Ilse, come look, he's malfunctioning."

"Maret."

"Doss! DOSS. Get up here, you're missing history."

Doss arrived puffing, saw Edmund's face, and stopped dead with the wariness of a man approaching a horse that might kick. "Is he all right?"

"He's *happy*," Maret said, delighted. "I think the altitude's gotten to him."

Edmund looked at the three of them—Doss clutching his pack straps, Maret grinning like a gargoyle, Ilse coming up the trail behind them with the chest and an expression she was failing entirely to hide—and he felt the smile widen despite every effort of twelve years' discipline, and he gave up fighting it.

"Yes," he said. "Fine. I'm smiling. The mountains are talking to me again. Are you satisfied?"

"What are they saying?" Maret asked immediately.

"That you talk too much."

"And?"

He paused. The wind came through the notch. Somewhere below them, a ptarmigan clucked in the rocks.

"They're saying we should cross the next valley before the afternoon wind comes up," he said. "And they're saying the snows are coming early this year."

That last part landed differently than he'd intended. The grin faded off Maret's face. Doss glanced east at the high peaks, white and serene and full of weather waiting to happen. Ilse's arms tightened around the chest strap.

"How early?" Ilse asked.

Edmund looked at the sky for a long moment, reading it the way she read documents—line by line, looking for the forgery.

"Early," he said. "We'll talk about it tonight. Move."

* * *

They descended into the eastern tundra and made good time through the afternoon, a long easy grade of brown-gold grass and stone, the trail—if it could be called that—running beside a stream that chattered over red rock. Edmund set a pace that was brisk without being cruel, and the party settled into the rhythm of it, and for nearly two hours the day was almost ordinary.

Almost, because Edmund kept glancing back.

Not at his people. At the horizon behind them, southwest, where the lowlands lay and the valley town of Ferrow and, farther than Ferrow, the estates and offices of men who wanted a sealed chest to have never existed.

He'd been seeing smoke since midday. Not cooking smoke—cooking smoke rose in a single column and dispersed. This was different. Thin black threads rising from three points along the distant foothills, spaced too evenly, burning too steadily. Signal fires. Someone was passing a message up the line of the mountains, hop by hop, the way you'd pass a bucket.

Voss. Or whatever Voss used for eyes when Voss himself was elsewhere. Either way: the message was moving faster than they were.

Edmund said nothing for a long time. He worked out distances, burn rates, how long a signal chain needed to relay a position, and the arithmetic came back ugly. Two days ahead had become perhaps one. Maybe less, if the men receiving those fires were mounted and the passes they needed were open.

"You're doing the face again," Maret said, falling into step beside him. "But it's the bad one."

"Eat something."

"I'm eating. See? Eating." She bit into a strip of dried meat with aggressive obedience. "What's behind us?"

"Smoke."

Her chewing slowed. "How much smoke?"

"Enough."

She looked back over her shoulder, squinting at the horizon until she found the threads of black. He watched her find them—one, two, three—and watched her jaw set in a way that reminded him, uncomfortably, of himself.

"Are they for us?"

"Probably."

"Then shouldn't we go faster?"

"We're going the speed we can sustain. Faster means someone breaks an ankle on bad ground, and then we go slower than slow." He nodded at her boots. "How are your feet?"

"Sore."

"Honestly."

"...Very sore."

"Tonight we tape them properly. Blisters become infections, infections become corpses, and I refuse to explain a corpse to your mother."

"I don't have a mother."

The words came out flat and quick, thrown down like a card, and then she stared straight ahead and walked faster, and Edmund understood he'd stepped on something without seeing it, the way you step on thin ice over deep water.

"Maret."

"Forget it."

"I didn't know."

"I know you didn't know. Forget it." But after fifty paces she slowed until they were side by side again, and she said, quieter, not looking at him: "That's why the valley people matter. The ones Tam talks about. People who don't have anybody. If the chest gives them their name back—" She shrugged, elaborate and teenage and transparent. "Whatever. It's not why I'm here. I'm here because you feed me."

"Of course," Edmund said gravely. "Strictly rations."

"Strictly rations," she agreed, and bumped her shoulder against his arm, once, lightly, the way you'd test a bridge railing to see if it would hold.

It held. He was surprised how much that mattered.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a granite rib, out of the wind, with the high peaks filling half the sky above them. Doss got a fire going small and disciplined, the way he did everything. Ilse sat with the chest in her lap and her fingers resting on the crest stamped into its lid—not reading it, just touching it, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal.

Edmund took the last watch before midnight, as he'd assigned himself, and sat wrapped in his blanket facing south, toward the smoke.

The fires were still burning. Three threads of black against the last purple of the dusk, patient as punctuation. And as he watched, a fourth thread bloomed—farther along, higher, up near the line of the foothills where the Serpent's lower spurs began. A new point on the chain. Closer.

Someone out there was being told exactly where the chest was going. And the telling was keeping pace with them, or gaining.

He sat with that for a long time, doing the arithmetic again even though he'd already done it, because the answer refused to change. One day. Maybe less. And ahead of them lay four days of open ground before the terrain turned hard enough to shake pursuit—the Ironbridge country, if his father's map told true, where the range pinched down to a single gorge and a single rotting span.

One day of margin, and a gorge at the end of it.

Behind him, the fire crackled. Maret murmured in her sleep. Ilse's hand stayed on the chest lid, rising and falling with her breathing, guarding the past the way a hen guards an egg.

Edmund looked at the new smoke thread rising against the stars, and felt the old mastery humming in him like a struck string—the reading of ground, of weather, of men—and for the first time since the flood had dragged the chest to his door, he was glad of it. Glad to be the man who could count the miles and the hours and the enemy's fires and know, precisely, how little time they had.

"One day," he said aloud, to no one, to the mountain. "All right. We'll spend it well."

Far to the south, the fourth fire burned steady, and answered.
