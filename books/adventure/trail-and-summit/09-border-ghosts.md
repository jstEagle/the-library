## 9. Border Ghosts

The checkpoint sat in the throat of the valley like a swallowed stone.

Doss saw it from half a mile off and felt his stomach drop the way it always did before a crossing—the old ferryman's instinct, reading water. Except this was no water. This was a timber gate across the road, two guard huts flanking it, a signal pole with a yellow pennant snapping in the wind, and four soldiers in the grey-and-black of the Marchland Authority going through the motions of inspecting a hay cart. Behind them, on the ridge to the east, a watchtower stood against the sky like a raised fist.

"Four of them," Doss said, not turning his head. "Two at the gate, one in each hut. Maybe a fifth sleeping one off."

"You can count that far?" Maret said beside him, and he could hear the nerves under her flippancy, the way her voice pitched just slightly too bright.

"I can count better than I can read," Doss said. "Which is why you're carrying the papers."

She patted the satchel at her hip where the forged documents lay wrapped in oilcloth: five travel passes bought from a forger in Ferrow who charged too much and drank too much and had, as far as anyone knew, never been caught. Five names that belonged to no one. Five seals that were almost right.

*Almost* was the word that kept Doss awake.

He walked at the back of the line, the way he'd walked at the back of ferry lines his whole life—big man, slow man, nothing-to-see-here man. Ahead of him went Ilse with her archivist's straight back and her face arranged into the polite blankness of a woman who had nothing to declare; then Maret, who looked fourteen when she wanted to and twelve when she was scared, which was a problem; then Edmund, hood up, hands in his pockets, walking like a man going nowhere in particular, which was the most dangerous thing about him because it wasn't an act anymore. Twelve years of practice.

And behind all of them, lashed under Doss's coat, flat against his chest like a second heartbeat, the chest's iron band pressed cold into his skin. He'd volunteered to carry it through the checkpoint. Ilse had argued. Edmund had ended the argument by saying, *If they search anyone, they'll search the small ones first,* and Doss had understood what he meant: the big disgraced ferryman with the prison brand on his wrist was the least likely person to be trusted with anything valuable, and therefore the safest place to hide it. There was a bitter kind of logic in being nobody. Doss had spent forty years learning its grammar.

The hay cart ahead of them cleared the gate. The guards waved it through without looking past the driver's beard. Then it was their turn.

"Halt." The nearer guard was young, maybe twenty, with a wispy attempt at a mustache and the bored cruelty of a boy given a little authority and no imagination. "Papers."

Maret stepped forward and produced the satchel with a steadiness that surprised Doss. The young guard unwrapped the oilcloth, thumbed through the passes, and frowned.

Doss counted his own breaths. In through the nose. Out slow. The way you waited between river surges, when the rope went taut and the whole world narrowed to fiber and tension.

"These are Marchland internal passes," the guard said. "You're traveling north. Northbound travelers need range permits stamped at Ferrow."

"They are stamped at Ferrow," Ilse said, mild as milk.

The guard flipped to the third page and held it up to the light. And there it was—the thing Doss had feared since Ferrow, since the forger's lamp, since the moment he'd first seen the work: the Ferrow stamp was a hair too red. Not crimson enough. More orange than blood. Anyone who handled a hundred stamps a season would see it in a heartbeat.

This boy hadn't. But the older guard in the left hut would, if the boy thought to ask him.

"Where are you coming from?" the young guard asked.

"Ferrow," Ilse said again.

"And before that?"

"The lowlands."

"Before *that.*"

Ilse paused exactly long enough. Doss watched her do it—watched her let the silence stretch until the guard leaned forward slightly, hungry—and then she said, "You know how it is after the floods. People move around. We've been a lot of places."

The guard grunted. He turned the pass over. Turned it back. His thumbnail found the edge of the seal and worried at it, the way a boy worries a loose tooth.

Doss's mouth had gone dry. Under his coat, the chest's band seemed suddenly loud, a bell against his ribs. He thought, absurdly, of the river that had started all this, the flood-churned brown water and the chest tumbling in it like a coffin, and how he'd have laughed if anyone had told him then that six weeks later he'd be standing at a border gate with a nation's whole legal existence strapped to his sweating chest while a teenager with a bad mustache picked at a forgery.

Then Edmund stepped forward and pulled back his hood.

"Edmund Vane," he said, clear and level, in the voice of a man announcing himself at a funeral. "That's my name. You'll find it on your list."

Everything stopped. The wind kept moving but everything else stopped—the guard's thumbnail froze on the seal, Maret made a small strangled sound, and Doss felt the world tilt the way a ferry tilts when the current takes the hull sideways.

"What did you say?" the guard asked.

"Edmund Vane. Blacklisted, twelfth year running. Check your board. It should be near the top—I'm told they keep me near the top." He said it without bravado, without fear, in the flat tone of a man reciting his own eulogy, and Doss realized with a cold shock that this was not a plan born of desperation. This was something Edmund had decided hours ago, maybe days, and simply hadn't told them. Had walked toward this gate the whole morning wearing patience like a coat.

The young guard looked at the older one emerging from the hut—a heavyset man with a sergeant's braid and a face like a closed ledger—and the older one looked at Edmund, and Doss watched recognition move across that face like weather.

"Vane," the sergeant said. "Well. The mountains spit you out after all."

"They usually do," Edmund said.

* * *

They took him into the left hut.

Doss stood in the trampled mud outside with Ilse and Maret and listened, because there was nothing else to do, and the listening was worse than any fighting he'd ever done. A ferryman learns to read water by sound; Doss learned to read a beating the same way. The first blow was a slap, open-handed, testing. Then the sound of a body meeting a wall—not thrown, pushed, the lazy violence of men who had all the time in the world. Then voices, low, and Edmund's voice answering, still level, still that terrible flat calm, and then a sound like a door slamming except it was a fist and it was flesh, and Maret grabbed Doss's sleeve and buried her face in his arm and he put his hand on the back of her head, big palm covering her whole skull, and stared at the hut door and hated.

He hated the guards. That was easy. What was harder, what sat in him like a swallowed hook, was that he hated himself more, because he knew—he *knew*, the way he knew riverbeds—what Edmund was doing. Every minute the guards spent on Vane was a minute they weren't spending on the papers. Every bruise they laid on the blacklisted man was a bruise not laid on the rest of them. Edmund had walked into that hut holding his name out like bait over deep water, and the fish were taking it, and the price of the catch was being paid in his own skin.

Doss knew the arithmetic of sacrifice. Twelve years ago he'd done the same sum in reverse and sold a man's route down the mountain for enough coin to cover his debts, and he'd told himself every night since that it was survival, that any man would have done it, that the blacklisters would have found another way. Standing in the mud now, listening to the sounds come through the plank wall, he understood finally that he'd never once believed it. Not really. You could carry a lie for twelve years the way you carried cargo—carefully, balanced, both hands—but it never got lighter.

Inside, the voices rose. "Where are you coming from, Vane? Who are these people? Where's the chest?"

And Edmund, breath knocked loose, laughing—actually laughing, a wet broken sound: "What chest? You people burned my house. If I had a chest, don't you think I'd have mentioned it to someone who could help me get a new roof?"

Another blow. Maret flinched against Doss's arm. Ilse stood like stone, but Doss saw her hands, saw the knuckles white around the strap of her satchel, saw her lips moving—not praying, he didn't think she prayed, but counting, or naming, or holding some word between her teeth so she wouldn't scream at a locked door.

It lasted eleven minutes. Doss counted. Afterward he would remember that he'd counted, and hate that too.

* * *

They brought him out at midday.

The sun was high and stupidly cheerful, laying gold across the mud, and Edmund came through the hut door into it blinking, one eye already swelling shut, a thread of red running from his split lip down over his chin. They'd worked his ribs—you could see it in how he held his left side, arm clamped like a splint—and there was a boot print on his shirt, gray dust shaped like a heel, printed over his heart like a signature.

The sergeant followed him out and stood on the step.

"Here's how it goes, Vane," he said, loud enough for all of them, loud enough for the record. "No warrant travels with your name this far north. Paperwork problem. Somebody in the capital will fix it eventually, and when they do, there'll be a bounty notice, and somebody poorer than me will collect it." He smiled without warmth. "So consider this a courtesy. Walk back the way you came. Go home. Whatever you're doing up here—whatever these people have talked you into—it ends today. Next time there won't be a warning. Next time there'll be a wagon."

"I understand," Edmund said.

"Do you?" The sergeant stepped close, close enough that Doss could see the man's eyes flick once—just once, quick as a fish—to the bundle under Doss's coat, and away. "Because I don't think you do. I think you're a ghost, Vane. Ghosts don't know they're dead. They keep walking around, doing whatever it was they died doing." He spat into the mud. "Stay dead. It's easier on everyone."

Then he waved them through the gate, and that was somehow the worst part—that casual flick of the hand, the gate swinging open, the whole machinery of the state deciding in one bored moment to let them pass, not out of mercy but out of contempt, because a beaten man and three nobodies with bad stamps weren't worth the ink it would take to hold them.

They walked. None of them spoke. The road bent north along the river, and the watchtower slid behind them, and only when the first switchback took them out of the guards' sightline did Maret break—she made it thirty steps into the trees and then doubled over, hands on knees, and made a sound that was half sob and half fury.

"He just—" she gasped. "He just *walked in there*. He didn't tell us. He didn't— we could have—"

"We couldn't have," Ilse said quietly. "That's why he didn't tell us."

"That's not—" Maret straightened, wiping her face with her sleeve, glaring at the ground. "That's not fair. You don't get to just decide to be hit."

"No," Ilse agreed. "But he does get to decide what he's worth. And apparently he thinks he's worth less than the rest of us." She said it without anger, and that absence of anger was more frightening than shouting. She turned and looked at Edmund, who had lowered himself onto a fallen log with the careful economy of a man whose ribs had opinions, and Doss watched her look at him—really look, the way she'd never quite let herself before, because looking required admitting what she was seeing.

She saw the scars.

Everyone had known they were there. Twelve years of exile, a blacklisting, a burned hut—of course there were scars. But knowing is different from seeing, and Edmund's collar had been torn in the beating, and below the collar, on the right shoulder and down onto the chest, the marks showed: not the fresh bruises, those would bloom tomorrow, but the old ones. Burn scars, thin and ridged, in patterns too regular to be accident. Doss recognized them because he'd seen them before, years ago, on men dragged out of Authority interrogation houses—rope burns and iron marks, the alphabet of a state that wrote its questions on skin.

Ilse went very still. Her hand rose halfway toward him and stopped, hovering, as if the air between them had turned to glass.

"When?" she asked. Just that. One word, and her voice cracked on it.

"Does it matter?" Edmund said.

"Yes."

He was quiet a moment. The wind moved the branches overhead, scattering light across his ruined face, and when he spoke his voice was still that flat, level thing, but Doss heard underneath it now, heard the effort of the flatness, the cost of holding it.

"The first winter. After they blacklisted me. I tried to cross at Lowmark with my father's maps sewn into my coat." He touched his shoulder, lightly, the way you touch an old dog. "They didn't find the maps. They found other things to look for instead. Three days." A pause. "I don't remember most of it. That's the mercy of it. The body remembers enough for both of us."

Ilse's hand completed its journey. She touched the edge of the scarred shoulder, barely, fingertips resting there like a bird landing, and Edmund did not pull away, and did not lean in either, just sat with it, enduring even gentleness the way he endured everything—as a debt he hadn't earned.

"You did that for the maps," Ilse said. "For Kestria. Before you ever knew what the chest was. Before you knew any of us existed." Her voice had gone thick. "Edmund. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means I was younger and stupider," Edmund said.

Maret made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been crying. Doss looked away, out through the trees at the river glinting below, and found that his eyes were burning, and blamed the wind, and knew he was lying.

* * *

They made camp early, in a hollow above the river where the pines grew thick and the smoke would disperse. Doss built the fire because building fires was the one thing he was unambiguously good for, and while it caught he watched Ilse work over Edmund with the focused ferocity of a woman who had decided that tending wounds was easier than tending feelings. She bound his ribs. She cleaned the lip. She put a cold cloth over the swelling eye and held it there longer than strictly necessary, and Edmund sat under her hands with his eyes closed, and neither of them said anything, and the silence between them had changed shape. Doss had spent a lifetime reading currents, and this current ran strong and new, and he filed it away with the tenderness of a man who had lost his own chances at such things and liked watching others fumble toward theirs.

Maret sat cross-legged by the fire, turning the locket over in her fingers—she'd taken custody of it after the fire incident, and guarded it like a dragon with a favorite coin. "The sergeant knew you," she said suddenly. "Not from the list. From before. 'The mountains spit you out after all'—that's not something you say to a name on a board."

Edmund opened his good eye. For a long moment Doss thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "Kestel. Sergeant Kestel. He was a corporal at Lowmark, twelve years ago. He processed me." A pause. "He's the one who signed the release form. Put in the margin: *subject released with warning, recommend monitoring.*" Edmund's mouth twisted, which hurt, Doss could see it hurt. "Twelve years, and I'm still his recommendation."

"So he remembers you," Maret said.

"He remembers everyone he's ever hurt. It's a kind of profession." Edmund shifted against the log, wincing. "Which means he also remembers what I looked like coming out of that room. And he knows I'm heading north, and he knows I didn't turn back." He looked into the fire. "A man like that doesn't file a report tonight. But in a week, when the paperwork catches up, when the bounty notice comes north—he'll write down everything he saw. Four travelers. Bad stamps. A big man carrying something heavy under his coat."

Doss's stomach dropped. "You think he noticed."

"I think everybody notices you, Doss. It's your best quality and your worst." Edmund almost smiled, then thought better of it. "We have a week. Maybe less. After that, every gate between here and the high passes has our description."

Silence around the fire. The river murmured below, patient, indifferent, carrying everything downstream the way rivers do—silt, secrets, seasons.

"Then we don't use gates," Ilse said at last. She reached into her satchel and drew out the map—Edmund's father's map, the Serpent Range inked in that precise antique hand—and spread it on her knees, weighting the corners with stones. Her finger traced the river north, past the last checkpoint, past the marked trails, and then left the ink entirely, moving up into blank paper, into the unmarked white where the mapmaker had stopped drawing and the mountain began. "My father's letters mention a route. An old drover's path over the shoulder of the range. No checkpoints because officially it doesn't exist. Unofficially—" she glanced up at Edmund, and something passed between them, some shared inheritance of dead parents and unfinished business, "—unofficially, it exists. It just kills people who take it casually."

Edmund studied the blank space on the map for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had changed—flatter than ever, but with something underneath it now, something that might have been resolve or might have been a man deciding that since the world insisted on hitting him, he would at least choose the direction he walked while it happened.

"Drover's path it is," he said. "We leave before dawn."

Doss banked the fire and lay back and stared up through the pine branches at the few hard stars, and the last thing he thought before sleep took him was of the sergeant's voice: *stay dead, it's easier on everyone.*

Easier, yes. Doss pulled his coat tighter around the chest's cold band and closed his eyes. But nobody had ever promised them easy—and somewhere out in the dark, two days south and closing, he could feel Voss coming like weather on the horizon, and the air had already begun to change.
