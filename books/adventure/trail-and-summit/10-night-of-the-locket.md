## 10. Night of the Locket

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time the others slept.

Ilse sat with her back against a granite slab, the chest open on her knees like a book she had read a hundred times and could not stop reading. Above them the Serpent Range's first foothills stood black against a sky salted with stars, and somewhere below, in the dark folds of the valley they had crossed that afternoon, Voss and his mercenaries were almost certainly moving. She should have been sleeping. Doss had told her twice. Maret had told her three times, with the particular ferocity of a fifteen-year-old who has decided someone else's health is her personal project.

But Ilse had spent ten years learning to read documents by bad light, and there was one thing left in the chest she had not yet examined closely: the locket.

It lay in the velvet-lined compartment at the chest's center, small enough to hide in a closed fist. Silver gone dark with forty years of tarnish, oval, its face worked with a pattern she had assumed was decorative—until tonight, when the firelight caught it at an angle and she saw what the pattern actually was. Not vines. A river. The River Kess, rendered in miniature, winding from the clasp to the hinge, and along its banks the tiny stamped figures of willows, and where the river met the edge of the locket, a bridge.

The Bridge of Nine Arches. Every Kestrian child learned to draw it before they learned to write their own names.

Her hands were not quite steady. She told herself it was the cold.

She had opened the compartment on the second night after Ferrow, while Edmund was checking the tether lines, and she had looked at the locket then too—looked, catalogued, closed it again. That was the archivist's discipline: record first, understand later; never let wanting something make you careless with it. But discipline was easier in daylight, with other people moving around you. Discipline was harder now, alone with the fire, holding in her palm the only object in the world that had crossed the annexation inside a royal courier's seal and come out the other side still warm with somebody's love.

There was a catch beneath the hinge. She had felt it the first night—a small resistance, a mechanism, the kind of workmanship Kestrian silversmiths had been famous for back when *famous* was still a word anyone used about them. She pressed it with her thumbnail.

The locket opened with a sound so soft it might have been imagined.

Two portraits, facing each other across the hinge like hands folded in prayer.

On the right, a man she did not know—young, severe, dark-haired, painted or photographed in the flat formal style of the last years before the annexation. His collar was high. His eyes were not looking at the painter but slightly past him, as if he'd been interrupted by something at the window. She knew without being told that this was the cartographer. This was Edmund's father. The resemblance was not in any single feature; it was in the whole architecture of the face, the way the bones were arranged as if by the same impatient hand.

On the left—

Ilse stopped breathing for a moment. It was an old habit from the archive rooms, a trick her father had taught her: when a document strikes you dumb, hold your breath and count, because the body must keep working even when the mind refuses.

She counted to six.

A woman, young, with light brown hair pinned up in the style of the old court, laughing—not posed-laughing, but caught mid-laugh, as if the portraitist had been someone she trusted. And in her arms, wrapped against her shoulder, an infant. The infant's face was turned toward the viewer, eyes half-open, one fist escaped from the blanket and curled near its own cheek.

The woman was wearing a lady-in-waiting's chain of office. Ilse knew the chain. There were three surviving examples in the registry plates her father had copied before the archive burned: Queen Sarelle's household, final composition, dated the winter before the annexation. Lady Amalie Vane, attendant to Her Majesty, listed with the notation *granted custody of certain papers*.

Lady Amalie Vane.

Edmund Vane.

The infant in the portrait had Edmund's face. Not the hard, weathered, twelve-years-alone face that watched her across campfires with the wariness of a man counting exits. The face underneath that one—the one Maret had glimpsed once when he laughed at the first pass and immediately pretended he hadn't. The unguarded face. It was right there, forty years old, wrapped in a blanket, held by a woman whose name Ilse had transcribed a hundred times into a registry no government recognized, in a country that officially did not exist.

*Oh,* she thought. *Oh, you fool. You absolute fool. You've been carrying yourself across the mountains.*

She became aware that she was crying only when a tear hit the silver and she had to wipe it away quickly, because moisture and old daguerreotype are enemies, and whatever else happened tonight, nothing was going to touch this image. She dried the locket on her sleeve with exaggerated care, the way her father had taught her to handle the last surviving land-grant of the eastern provinces, and then she sat very still for a long time, listening to the fire tick and the wind move through the pines below the scree, and tried to decide what in God's name she was supposed to do with this.

Because here was the problem. Here was the thing she had climbed two days ahead of mercenaries to say, rehearsed on switchbacks until the words wore smooth—and now that the moment had arrived, every rehearsal sounded wrong.

*You are the heir.* That was the sentence. Ten years of work compressed into four words. The chest contained the succession testimony, the queen's sealed declaration, the registry of households—all of it built around a single legal fiction made flesh: that a child of the protected line survived, carried out in a lady-in-waiting's arms, and that the line's claim lived in him. The tribunal would not restore Kestria because the documents were beautiful. The tribunal would restore Kestria because the documents pointed at a living person, and that person was sitting thirty feet away, asleep under a wool blanket with his boots on, because he was the kind of man who slept with his boots on even in a safe camp.

And he did not know. She was nearly certain he did not know. He knew his father had been a cartographer—he had shown her the map with the mixture of reverence and rage that only family can produce. But the chest's deeper papers, the ones written in court cipher, the ones naming the child—she had decrypted those slowly, over nights, and she had watched him watch her doing it and seen no recognition in his face. Only impatience, and suspicion, and underneath both, the thing she was beginning to recognize as the true engine of him: hunger. A hunger he had starved so long he'd forgotten its name.

She closed the locket gently and pressed the catch until it clicked.

Then she stood up, because some conversations cannot be had sitting down, and walked the few steps to where Edmund lay, and crouched, and said his name.

He came awake the way water comes to a boil—no start, no gasp, just sudden total presence, his hand already finding the knife under the blanket before his eyes found her face and decided, visibly, to stand down. "What."

"I need to show you something."

"Now?"

"It's always now," Ilse said. "That's the trouble with the truth. It doesn't keep."

He looked at her for a long moment. Whatever he saw in her face made him sit up. He glanced at Maret, curled beside the dead fire's outer ring, and at Doss, snoring with the rhythmic shamelessness of a man who had made his peace with the world's dangers or simply lost interest in them. Then he rose, silently, and followed her back to the granite slab, and sat down across from her with his forearms on his knees, waiting, in the posture of a man who has spent twelve years receiving bad news and expects nothing better.

Ilse held out the locket.

He didn't take it at first. He looked at it the way he had looked at the chest the day he pulled it from the river—she had gotten the story from him in fragments, on the trail, the way you get all important stories from men like him—with a kind of offended recognition, as if the object had committed a personal trespass. Then he took it. His hands, which could splice a rope in a gale and field-strip a stove at altitude, were clumsy with the small thing.

"The catch is under the hinge," she said.

He found it. He opened it.

She watched his face by firelight, and what she saw was not the collapse she had braced for. It was worse. It was stillness. A stillness so complete that the fire seemed to lean toward him. His thumb moved once across the left portrait, very lightly, the way you'd touch a wound to check if it was real.

"That's my mother," he said. His voice was level. Too level. "That's—" He stopped. Started again. "I don't have anything of hers. I have a surname and a map in a dead man's handwriting. I don't have—" He closed the locket. Opened it again, as if checking the trick wouldn't repeat itself. "Where did this come from."

"It was in the chest. Sealed compartment, center well. It's listed in the courier manifest—*one keepsake, personal, to travel with the papers.* Whoever packed the chest believed the papers and this belonged together." She took a breath. "Edmund. Do you know who your mother was?"

"A woman who died when I was four. In the lowlands. Fever." Flat. Recited. A fact kept in a drawer for forty years and never aired.

"Her name was Amalie Vane."

"Yes."

"She was lady-in-waiting to Queen Sarelle of Kestria. Final household, winter before the annexation. There are three portraits of the household staff in the registry plates, and hers is one of them, and the notation next to her name says she was granted custody of certain papers." Ilse heard her own voice accelerating and made herself slow down, the way you slow a horse going downhill. "The chest contains a declaration under the queen's seal. It establishes a succession in exile. It doesn't name the heir directly—it couldn't, if it was intercepted—but it describes him. Born in the annexation year. Carried out by a member of the household. Protected by the registry, which lists every family sworn to shelter him. Edmund—it describes you. The dates fit. The name fits. Your mother is in the chain of custody. You're not carrying an inheritance to someone. You *are* the inheritance."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the fire to settle, a small collapse of embers, a scatter of sparks rising toward the indifferent stars.

"You're wrong," Edmund said finally.

"I've spent ten years—"

"You're *wrong.*" Louder now, and Maret stirred in her sleep, and Edmund lowered his voice with visible effort, dragging it back under control like a man hauling on a runaway line. "I'll tell you what I am. I'm a blacklisted guide who can't cross a border without getting beaten in a checkpoint yard. I'm a man whose own father wrote him out of his life so thoroughly that I learned he existed mostly from the way strangers flinched at our name. My mother died in a rented room with a landlord's notice nailed to the door. Nobody protected us. Nobody carried anybody anywhere. If there was a great chain of loyal families sworn to shelter the heir, where were they? Where was the shelter?" He was leaning forward now, and the firelight made his shadow enormous on the rock behind him. "I have been nobody for twelve years, Ilse. I got good at it. Nobody is *safe*. And you want to hand me a crown I didn't ask for, built on papers written by people who are all dead, about a country that is all dead, and tell me that's who I am?"

"Not a crown," Ilse said quietly. "A name. There's a difference."

"They feel the same from the inside."

"No," she said. "They don't. I've had neither, and I've thought about it more than anyone should."

He stared at her. Something moved behind his eyes—not softening, exactly. Recalculation. The look of a man discovering the map he's using is drawn at the wrong scale.

"My father abandoned us," he said. "Whatever this is—" he held up the locket between two fingers, like evidence in a trial "—it's a relic of a man who chose a kingdom over his son. So forgive me if I don't weep over his silver."

"He didn't choose it," Ilse started, and stopped herself, because the letter in the map's margin was still encrypted, still half-read, still a thing for another night, and she could not give him half-truths tonight. Tonight had enough weight in it already. "I don't know everything yet," she said instead. "But I know the chest doesn't lie. Documents lie, people lie, memories lie. Chests don't. Someone loved your mother enough to seal her portrait with the fate of a nation, and someone trusted her enough to put a newborn at the center of it. That's not abandonment, Edmund. That's the opposite."

"You don't know what it is." He stood. He was holding the locket too tightly; she could see the tendons standing in the back of his hand. "You know what it says. Those aren't the same thing. I'm done letting paper tell me who I am. Paper is how they erased us in the first place—did you ever think of that? Edicts. Registries. Deportation orders. All beautifully written. All official." He took a step toward the fire, and Ilse understood one full second too late what he intended.

"Edmund—"

He threw the locket into the flames.

It made almost no sound. That was the terrible part—that forty years of love and conspiracy and a queen's desperate arithmetic could end with no sound at all, just a small bright arc, a hiss as the silver hit the coals, and then the fire closing over it the way water closes over a stone. The tarnish began to lift immediately, the silver brightening in the heat like something waking up.

Edmund stood looking at the fire with his fists at his sides, breathing hard, and Ilse sat frozen on the granite, grief and fury fighting for possession of her throat, thinking: *ten years, ten years of my life, and he threw it into a fire in four seconds.*

It was Maret who moved.

Later, Ilse could never reconstruct quite how the girl woke, or when she had come awake—whether the argument had pulled her up out of sleep or whether she had been listening for some time, curled in her blanket with her eyes shut and her ears open, the way children do in camps where adults forget they're there. But there was no hesitation in her. She crossed the camp in four strides, dropped to her knees at the fire's edge, and drove both bare hands into the coals.

"Maret—" Ilse was up, Edmund was lunging forward, and the girl was already pulling back, cradling something dark and smoking against her shirtfront, gasping, tears cutting clean lines through the ash on her face. The smell of scorched skin filled the little clearing. She had burned the heels of both palms, deep, the skin gone white and waxy in two places, and she held the locket against her chest as if it were a bird with a broken wing and she the only thing keeping it off the ground.

"Give it here," Edmund said. "Let me see your hands."

"Don't you *dare* throw it again." Maret's voice cracked in the middle and she pushed it back up through sheer fury. "Don't you dare. You don't get to burn it. It's not yours to burn."

"It's exactly mine to burn. That's what you don't understand—"

"It's *hers*." Maret thrust the locket toward him, toward the firelight, and the heat had cleaned the silver, and the catch had sprung open in her scorched grip, and the two portraits faced the camp: the severe young man, the laughing woman, the infant with its fist curled by its cheek. "Look at her. Look at her *face*. She's laughing. Somebody made her laugh and then somebody kept that laugh in silver for forty years so it would survive, and you want to burn it because—" her voice broke again and she forced through it, furious and crying and fifteen years old and absolutely merciless, "because you're scared. Well, I'm scared too. I've been scared my whole life. Being scared isn't a reason. It's just weather."

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Somewhere downslope, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

Maret sat down abruptly, as if her legs had resigned without notice, and held the locket out on her open, ruined palms—an offering, or a verdict.

Edmund looked at it for a long time.

Ilse watched him and understood that she was watching the actual summit of the whole journey happen right here, weeks early, at a roadside fire: not the pass, not the tribunal, not the snow. This. The moment when a man decides whether the past is a debt or a door. She had seen scholars break over smaller revelations. She had seen entire delegations fail at exactly this juncture—the moment when the abstract cause acquires a face and the carrier of the cause discovers the face is his own.

Edmund knelt. Slowly, the way a big man kneels when his knees have opinions. He took Maret's hands in his own, turned them over, examined the burns with the detached competence she had seen him use on rope ends and blistered feet, and said nothing about the locket at all.

"We need cool water," he said. "And the comfrey from the blue tin. Can you walk?"

"I can walk," Maret said, with tremendous dignity, still clutching the locket in one burned hand.

"Then walk. Ilse—" and his voice had changed, flattened back down to the guide's register, the register that brooked no discussion, but underneath it she heard something new, something that had not been there an hour ago, "—get the chest closed and lashed. First light we move. We've lost enough time."

"And the locket?" Ilse asked carefully.

Edmund paused at the edge of the firelight. He did not turn around.

"The girl keeps it," he said. "Since apparently it survives me better than I survive it."

He walked toward the stream. Maret watched him go, then looked down at the locket in her palm, and closed her fingers around it with a gentleness that made Ilse's chest ache—and then looked up at Ilse with wet, blazing eyes, and said, in a whisper pitched to carry no farther than the two of them:

"He's going to run at the pass. When we deliver it. He's planning to leave—I've watched him pack. Are you going to let him?"

Ilse looked at the girl—at the burns she would carry forever, earned in defense of a stranger's mother—and felt the last piece of the answer assemble itself, the piece she had been carrying up the mountain unexamined, like a stone in her boot.

"No," Ilse said. "I'm not."

Above the tree line, far to the north, where no fire of theirs could reach, a point of orange light flickered against the black shoulder of the range—steady, deliberate, answering another light beyond it. Signal fires. Someone was talking to someone across the dark, and the conversation was about them.

Maret saw it too. Her fingers tightened around the locket.

"First light," she echoed, and the word *first* sounded suddenly like a promise neither of them was sure the night intended to keep.
