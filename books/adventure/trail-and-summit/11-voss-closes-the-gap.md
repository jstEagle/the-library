## 11. Voss Closes the Gap

The warden's name was Pell, and he had the particular smell of a man who had already decided to be bought. Voss could smell it from across the table—under the woodsmoke and the mutton fat and the sour breath of cheap plum brandy, there was that faint, unmistakable scent of surrender. He had learned to read it the way other men read weather. It was in the way Pell's eyes kept sliding to the purse on the table instead of holding Voss's gaze. In the way his thumb worried the rim of his cup, round and round, like a man polishing something he intended to steal.

"Three men," Pell said. "Or four. Maybe five. The ferryman's girl says they took mules. Two mules, one of them lame."

Voss said nothing. Silence was a tool like any other, and he kept his tools sharp. He let the silence sit between them until the warden's thumb stopped moving.

"Five, then," Pell amended. "The ferryman, the old man, a girl, the archivist woman, and—" He paused, and Voss watched him decide how to say it. "And Vane. Edmund Vane."

The name landed in the room like a stone dropped down a well. Voss waited for the echo.

"You're certain."

"The postmaster at Ferrow kept his ledger. Vane signed it. Wrote it out plain as scripture." Pell laughed, a dry rattle. "Twelve years blacklisted and the man signs his own name. You'd think he wanted to be found."

*He did,* Voss thought. *That's the trouble with men like Vane. They spend years burying themselves and then one day they sign a ledger, because some part of them is tired of being dead.* He knew the type. He had been the type, once, in a life so far behind him it felt like something he'd read in a book about another man.

He slid the purse across the table. Pell's hand covered it with the speed of a man who had done this before, though perhaps never with a purse so heavy.

"The route," Voss said.

"They'll take the low saddle past the Shepherd's country, or the warden's trail through the spruce—no, not the warden's trail, that's closed, rockfall." Pell frowned, working at it. "If they're smart, they'll go the long way round the shoulder and come down to Ironbridge. It's the only crossing over the gorge for thirty miles, and the ferryman will know it. Ferryman always know the crossings. It's in the blood."

"And if they're not smart?"

"Then they're already dead, and you've wasted your money."

Voss almost smiled. It was the closest thing to a joke he'd heard in weeks, and he filed it away without laughing, the way he filed everything—carefully, in the dark, where it couldn't rot.

* * *

He walked out of Pell's station into the last blue hour of evening and stood a while in the cold, looking up at the Serpent Range. The peaks had gone the color of iron. Somewhere up there, five people were walking toward a bridge, and one of them was carrying a chest that could undo forty years of careful, expensive forgetting.

His employer's letter was in his breast pocket. He had read it so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. It was not a long letter. It did not need to be. *The chest does not reach the tribunal,* it said, in the elegant, distant hand of a man who signed his name with a flourish because he had never once feared a clerk would use it against him. *Burn it. Burn whatever carries it. The mountain will keep its secrets if you give it the material.*

*The mountain will keep its secrets.* Voss had turned that phrase over for days. It was the kind of thing his employer wrote without thinking—the casual arithmetic of men who counted other people the way they counted timber. But there was a truth in it that Voss had learned young, in a school two hundred miles from anywhere he'd been born: mountains kept everything. That was the problem with mountains. They kept everything, and they gave nothing back, and you could stand on them your whole life shouting your own name and they would hold it inside the stone and never say it aloud.

He checked his men. There were nine left of the eleven he'd started with—two lost to the river country, one to a bad fall, and none of them mourned, because mourning was a luxury of people with something to lose. They were camped in the lee of the station's timber wall, and they looked up when he came, nine faces lit orange by the fire, waiting the way dogs wait. Good men, in the way that knives are good. He had collected them over years the way a river collects stones, and he knew each one's weight.

"Kell," he said. "Rask. You're with me. The rest go with Brannic."

Brannic looked up. He was Voss's lieutenant, a big, patient man with a face like a closed door, and he was the only one of the nine who ever asked questions, which Voss tolerated because the questions were usually the right ones.

"You're splitting us," Brannic said. "Why?"

"Because they'll move fast and we move faster split. Pell says Ironbridge. If they cross there, we take them on the far side, in the spruce, where nobody watches. If they don't cross there—" he shrugged, "—then we've lost a day and gained certainty."

"And if they're already across?"

Voss looked at him a long moment. "Then we go up after them, and the mountain does half our work."

Brannic nodded slowly. He was turning something over, Voss could see it, some thought he hadn't finished. "The old man," Brannic said. "The blacklisted one. The bounty on him is dead paper, you know that. Twelve years. Nobody pays for a ghost."

"I'm not paying for a ghost."

"Then what?"

Voss crouched by the fire and held his hands out to it. They were good hands, broad and scarred, a workingman's hands, though he had not done a workingman's labor in twenty years. He watched the flames and thought about how much truth to spend. Truth was currency too, and he had learned to be rich in it precisely because he spent it so rarely.

"The chest," he said finally. "It's a registry. Names, births, bloodlines. Everything the annexation erased, written down and sealed before the borders closed." He turned his palms. "Forty years ago the empire didn't just take Kestria's land. It took the Kestrians. Struck them from the rolls. No nation, no name, no legal existence. Their land passed to whoever held the new titles. Our employer's titles. If that chest reaches a tribunal and the tribunal rules—" he let it hang.

"Then the titles are void," Brannic said. "All of them."

"All of them. Every farm, every forest lease, every mine. Forty years of paper, undone by one box." He stood, brushing off his knees. "So you understand what we're carrying. It isn't a bounty anymore. It's a war."

The men looked at the fire. Somewhere up in the dark, an avalanche let go with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, and every one of them flinched except Voss, who had stopped flinching at avalanches a long time ago, and sometimes wondered what that said about him.

* * *

They moved at first light, two parties, an hour apart. Voss took the high trail with Kell and Rask, the three of them moving fast and quiet through the spruce, and by midmorning they had come down to the gorge road, and Voss saw Ironbridge and understood why Pell had said *it's in the blood.*

The gorge was a wound in the earth, deep and narrow, with the river running gray-green at the bottom, fast and cold and full of last week's snowmelt. The bridge crossed it on two weathered cables and a deck of planks gone silver and rotten at the edges, sagging in the middle like a tired back. On the far side the trail climbed into the trees and vanished. It was the kind of crossing where a man could stand on one side and watch another man die on the other, and there was not a thing he could do about it except shout.

"Perfect," Rask said, meaning it as praise.

Voss didn't answer. He was looking at the bridge and doing the arithmetic of it—the angles, the sight lines, the way the near anchorage sat in a hollow where five men could wait unseen, and the way the far anchorage stood open to the sky. If he held the far side, anyone crossing came to him. If he held the near side, anyone crossing walked into his hands at the half-mark, when the planks would take no more than two at a time and turning back was slower than coming on.

He held the near side.

They made camp in the hollow above the anchorage, cold camp, no fire, and Voss set Kell on the rocks where he could see the trail coming down from the shoulder, and Rask lower, where he could see the spruce line. Then Voss sat with his back against a stone that still held yesterday's sun, and took out his employer's letter, and did not read it, because he knew it by heart, and instead let himself think the thing he did not let himself think on the march.

He had been seven years old the last time he heard the Kestrian language spoken aloud.

It had been at night, through a wall, in the transit house at the border. His mother's voice, low, singing something—the same three lines over and over, a lullaby or a lament, he had never learned which, because the next morning the wagons came, and the children went one way and the mothers went another, and the school he was sent to had a rule about tongues that were not the empire's. They did not beat it out of you. That would have been crude, and the school was not crude. They simply made each language a room, and the Kestrian room was empty, and after a few years you stopped walking down that corridor at all.

He had been Voss for so long now that the name felt like his. But it was a school name. A given name, the way the blankets had been given, the way the new birthday had been given—assigned in a ledger by a clerk who never looked up. Somewhere under it was another name, in another alphabet, that his mother had used when he was small, and he could not retrieve it. He had tried, over the years, in the hour before sleep. He would lie in the dark and reach for it the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue, and it would come close, and then slide away, and what he was left with was not the name but the shape of the reaching.

That was what the chest was, he understood. Not paper. A room at the end of a corridor. A name under a name. And that was exactly why it had to burn—not because his employer's titles hung on it, though they did, not because the tribunal mattered, though it did, but because if that room existed, if that door could be opened, then every empty corridor he had ever made peace with became a choice he had made instead of a fact he had been handed. And Voss had built his whole life on the difference between those two things, and he did not intend to find out, at his age, that the foundation was paper.

*The mountain will keep its secrets,* his employer wrote. But Voss had been a mountain child, whatever else he was, and he knew better. Mountains didn't keep secrets. Mountains kept *everything*—the dead, the names, the songs through the wall—and they gave it back when they chose, to whoever climbed high enough to ask.

He put the letter away. Down in the gorge, the river ran on, gray and patient, carrying the mountain's meltwater to the lowlands and the lowlands' secrets out to sea.

* * *

Toward evening, Kell whistled—two notes, low, the signal for movement on the trail.

Voss went up the rocks on his hands and knees and looked through the gap Kell indicated. Far below and across, on the switchbacks descending from the shoulder, five figures moved in single file. Even at that distance the formation told him everything: a big man leading—the ferryman, surely, built like his trade—a thin older man at the rear watching the backtrail like a professional, the girl in the middle where you put the one you're protecting, the archivist woman, and—

And Vane.

He knew him without being told. It wasn't the way the man walked, though the walk was wrong for a blacklisted fugitive, too upright, too unafraid of open ground. It was something else. Voss had spent his life reading men at distance, and what he read now was a man who had spent twelve years alone and was walking with people again and had not yet decided how he felt about it. The whole body said it. The shoulders said it. The space he kept between himself and the others, close enough to help and far enough to leave—that space said it.

Vane stopped at a bend in the trail and looked back the way they'd come, and for a moment, across the whole width of the gorge and the falling light, Voss had the uncanny certainty that the man was looking directly at him. He knew it wasn't possible. The distance was too great, the hollow too deep in shadow. But the certainty sat in his chest anyway, cold and specific, and he found himself crouched lower against the rock, as though a man twelve hundred yards away could see him if Voss let himself be seen.

"They'll camp on the shoulder," Kell whispered. "Cross at first light. Bridge won't take them in the dark."

Voss nodded. First light. The planks would be stiff with frost, the cables slick, the river loud. Five people on a rotten bridge, in single file, in the half-dark, with the far side climbing away into trees they didn't know.

"Rask takes the far anchorage tonight," Voss said. "Down and across before the moon sets, while the light's bad. He holds the far side. When they're committed to the middle—when the ferryman and the girl are on and the old man's not yet on—we come out of the hollow."

Brannic's voice, in his memory, asking *then what?* And the answer he hadn't given, because Brannic hadn't needed to hear it: *then the bridge decides.* A rotten deck, a panic, a crowd in the middle, cables that had sung forty years in the wind. He did not have to fire a shot. He only had to be there, at both ends, at the right moment, and let the crossing do what crossings do to people who are afraid.

It was clean, in the way his employer liked things clean. No bodies to explain, no massacre to feed a tribunal's sympathy. Just a bridge, and a river, and a mountain that kept everything.

Below, the five figures had reached the shoulder and were making camp, a fire no bigger than a cupped hand. Voss watched the small flame a long time. He thought of his mother's voice through the wall, three lines over and over. He thought of the empty room at the end of the corridor, and how he had locked it himself, and how a box of paper was coming up the trail to pick the lock.

"First light," he said to Kell, and went down to wait.

The mountain stood over all of them, keeping everything, saying nothing. And the river under the bridge ran fast and gray toward morning.
