## 12. Ironbridge

The gorge announced itself long before we saw it—a low grinding in the earth, like a millstone turning somewhere beneath the world. I had heard that sound once before, twelve years ago, on the far side of the range, and I had hoped never to hear it again.

We came down through the pines single file, Maret's hand on my pack strap the way she'd taken to doing when the trail steepened, as if I were a fixed point she could trust not to move. Behind her walked Ilse with the chest lashed to her shoulders in a harness Doss had rigged from ferry rope—she refused to let anyone else carry it, and after three days of argument we'd stopped offering. Doss took the rear, as he always did, his old ferryman's eyes reading the ground behind us the way other men read weather.

The trees broke open all at once, and there it was: Ironbridge Gorge, a wound in the mountain two hundred feet deep and barely forty across at its throat, the river below running white and furious with snowmelt. The bridge hung across it like something abandoned mid-fall—six frayed cables, slats missing in long gaps, the whole structure swaying even though there was no wind down in the cut.

"Charming," Doss said.

"It held for my father," I said. "It'll hold for us."

"That was forty years ago."

"Then we go light and we go one at a time."

I made them wait while I went down alone to the anchor post on our side. It was iron, actually—the only iron thing about the bridge, driven into living rock by engineers who'd known what they were doing, unlike whoever had strung the cables. I put my weight on it, then jumped on it. Solid. The cables were another matter. I worked along the first one hand over hand, squeezing and listening, the way you test a rope you intend to trust your life to. Most of it was sound under the rot. Two sections near the middle were soft as overripe fruit.

"We cross in this order," I said when I came back up. "Maret first—she's lightest. Then Ilse with the chest. Then me. Doss last."

"Why am I last?" Doss asked.

"Because if it breaks, I want the heaviest of us already across, pulling people up instead of falling with them."

He grunted. He knew the logic. Ferryman's logic. You always keep yourself on the dying side of the water until everyone else is safe. It was the first law of his old trade, and he'd been exiled from that trade for breaking it exactly once—for saving my life, which was the debt he claimed I owed him, though I had never once believed the ledger ran that direction.

Maret went out onto the slats like a cat, quick and low, hands on the side cables, and reached the far anchor in less time than it takes to tell it. She turned and gave us a thumbs-up with both hands, grinning, and something in my chest loosened that I hadn't known was tight.

Ilse went next. The chest changed her balance; she moved slower, testing each board before committing her weight, and halfway across the wind funneled up the gorge and set the whole bridge swinging. She stopped. I watched her knuckles whiten on the cable. Below her the river chewed at the rock walls, loud enough to swallow a scream.

"Ilse." I kept my voice level, carrying but not shouting. "Don't look down. Look at Maret. Walk to Maret."

She looked at Maret. She walked to Maret. When she stepped off onto solid stone she sat down hard and laughed—a short, shaky sound—and hugged the chest like a child hugging a door against intruders.

Then it was my turn.

I was four slat-gaps from the far side when Doss shouted my name, and I knew before I turned what I would see.

They came out of the trees on our side of the gorge—five of them, moving fast, rifles slung, and at their head a big man in a grey oilskin coat with a face like a closed ledger. Voss's lieutenant. I'd seen him once before, at distance, on the night my hut burned: the man who'd held the torch while others struck the flint. Behind him, further back in the treeline, more shapes moved. Eight, maybe ten. Voss had split his force after all, just as Tam's grandmother had warned us he would, and he'd guessed the gorge because there was nowhere else to guess. Every route east of the high passes crossed Ironbridge or added six days going around it, and the dry season didn't have six days left in it.

"Edmund!" Doss again, flat and urgent. "Move!"

I moved. No more testing boards, no more care—I ran the last of the bridge with the cables groaning around me, and behind me I heard boots hit the first slats, heard the whole structure shudder, heard a voice shout *hold your fire, you idiot, you'll drop him in the river and the chest goes with him*—which told me everything about their orders. They couldn't shoot the bridge. They couldn't shoot us on it. They needed the chest intact and, apparently, needed some of us breathing too, or at least Voss did, because the lieutenant bellowed across the gorge in a voice built for parade grounds:

"VANE! Stop where you are! We only want the chest! Walk away from it and no one gets hurt!"

It was such an ordinary lie that I almost admired it. I came off the bridge onto the far anchor stone, and Maret hauled me up by the arm with a strength that would have embarrassed her to know I noticed.

Across the gap, the lieutenant stood at the head of the bridge with his men fanned out behind him, and he looked at the cables, and he looked at the chest on Ilse's back, and he made his decision the way such men always make decisions: quickly, and with someone else paying for it.

"Two of you, across. Slow. Bring it to me."

The first mercenary stepped onto the bridge. Then the second. The cables took their weight with a sound I will hear for the rest of my life—a long fibrous creak, like a ship dying slowly—and the whole span dipped and swayed, and I saw, with terrible clarity, what the lieutenant either hadn't calculated or hadn't cared to: the bridge had carried one of us fine. It might carry two. It would not carry two and hold while a third followed, and these men were heavy with gear, and every step was driving the rot deeper.

"Doss," I said. "The anchor."

He was already looking at it. On the near side, the iron post stood in its socket of rock, and wrapped around it, above the cables' attachment, was the old release line—a counterweight rig from the days when the bridge could be dropped to stop an army, some forgotten garrison's paranoia preserved in rusted iron. Doss had seen it the same moment I had, back when we first came down to the gorge. Ferryman's eyes. He'd said nothing then. He was saying nothing now, but his hand was on the lever, and his face was doing something I had never seen it do.

"Doss." Ilse's voice, very quiet. "There are men on it."

"There are men coming for us," Doss said. His voice sounded like gravel rolling downhill. "There have been men coming for us since Ferrow. There will be men coming for us at the pass, and at the tribunal, and at every fire we build between here and spring. This is the one place in fifty miles where the arithmetic favors us."

"They'll die," Maret said. She wasn't arguing. She was stating it, the way children state things, because no one has yet taught them that adults prefer lies dressed as complications. "Both of them. That water—"

"That water doesn't leave bodies," Doss said. "I've ferried this river. I know what it does."

The second mercenary was a third of the way across. The first was at the midpoint, right at the rotten section, and I could see him feel it—one slat giving slightly under his boot, a half-inch of give, nothing, survivable, except that his face changed and he stopped and gripped the cables and looked down into the white water, frozen, exactly as Ilse had frozen, except that no one was calling to him to look at Maret and walk to Maret.

And the lieutenant, seeing his man stalled, did the unforgivable ordinary thing: he sent a third man onto the bridge to hurry him.

Three cables sang. The whole span rolled like a deck in weather. The first man screamed—not a war scream, just a man's scream—and the third man on the bank laughed, because fear does that to certain men, turns their spines to wire and their mouths to cruelty.

"Doss." I said it once, quietly. Not an order. I want to be honest about that, even now, even with myself. I did not order it. I looked at the man who owed me a life-debt, standing with his hand on a lever that would kill two men in front of us, and I said his name, and whatever he heard in it was his own affair forever.

Because here is what I have never told any of them, and what I am telling now, in the only place I can tell anything: part of me wanted it. Part of me stood on that cold stone with the wind off the glacier in my face and thought, *they burned my hut, they hunted Ilse across two countries, they put Maret's name on a list in Ferrow, they are the reason I have spent twelve years counting miles instead of living days*—and that part of me wanted the sound the bridge would make when it let go. Wanting it felt like strength. It wasn't. It was just the oldest weakness there is, wearing a soldier's coat.

Doss pulled the lever.

The mechanism fought him—forty years of rust—and for one heartbeat I thought it wouldn't move, and I confess I don't know which outcome I was praying for. Then something sheared inside the housing with a crack like a rifle shot, the counterweight dropped, and the anchor post spun.

The cables didn't snap. That was the worst of it. They *unwound*, slow and shrieking, the whole bridge sagging in the middle like a bow bending past its limit, and for nearly two full seconds the three men on it scrambled toward the near side, and for nearly two full seconds I believed—some animal part of me believed—that mercy was still mechanically possible.

Then the rotten section let go all at once, both cables together, and the middle of the bridge fell into the gorge with the men still holding it.

I will not describe the sound. I have decided, in the years since, that I am allowed to keep exactly one thing undescribed, and I choose that.

The river took them in less time than a breath. White water, a flash of grey oilskin, gone. The lieutenant stood at the broken stump of the anchor with his men around him, and across two hundred feet of roaring air we looked at each other, and I saw that he understood exactly what had happened and exactly who had done it, and I saw something else, something worse: he wasn't angry. He was recalculating. A man like that files even death under logistics.

He raised one hand—not a fist, not a threat, just five fingers spread, a message in itself: *we know where you're going, and we have time*. Then he turned and walked back into the trees, and his men folded in behind him like water closing over a stone, and the gorge was empty except for the wind and the river and the six of us standing on the wrong side of a bridge that no longer existed.

Nobody spoke for a long moment.

Maret spoke first, because Maret always speaks first when silence becomes unbearable, and maybe that's the bravest thing about her. "They're dead," she said. "You killed them. Both of them. For a bridge."

"For us," Doss said.

"Same thing," Maret said, and turned away, and sat down on a rock with her back to all of us, fifteen years old and suddenly older than every adult in the party.

Doss stood by the ruined anchor with the lever still in his fist, and I watched him try to set his face into the shape it usually wore, the flat competent ferryman's mask, and fail. His hands were shaking. Big hands, rope-callused, hands that had pulled thirty drowning men out of that same river in another life, and they shook like a drunkard's.

"It was the trade," he said finally, not to me, not to anyone. "First law. You never cut the line while a soul's on it. Never. Not for weather, not for cargo, not for your own—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Twelve years ago I broke it once, for you, and I've called it a debt ever since because calling it a debt was easier than calling it what it was. And today I broke it again, and this time there's no word for it at all."

"Doss—"

"Don't." He held up a hand. "Don't forgive me today. I couldn't carry it. Ask me again in the mountains somewhere, when my hands have remembered how to be still."

He walked to the edge of the gorge, looked down once at the water that kept no bodies, and then turned his back on it, deliberately, the way a man closes a door on a room he intends never to enter again. Whether he ever manages it, I couldn't say. I doubt he knows himself.

Ilse came to stand beside me. The chest was still on her back; she hadn't put it down through any of it, and I understood then that she never would, not through fire or flood or whatever else the range had left to spend on us. To her it wasn't wood and paper. It was forty years of erased names, and she was the last pair of arms they had.

"You're thinking it was necessary," she said quietly.

"I'm thinking I'm glad it was Doss's hand on the lever and not mine, and I hate myself for being glad."

"That's not nothing," she said. "A man who feels nothing about a thing like this—that's the man who burned your hut. Whatever Doss is carrying tonight, he isn't carrying that."

Behind us, Tam—who had not spoken since the bridge fell, who had gone the color of tallow and hadn't once looked at the river—suddenly pointed east, up-valley, where the trail climbed out of the gorge toward the shoulder of the mountain.

Smoke. Thin, deliberate, disciplined—one plume rising from the ridgeline, then a second further on, spaced like signposts. Signal fires. Voss wasn't chasing us anymore. Voss was *herding* us, marking the route we'd have to take, telling his scattered men where the funnel narrowed next.

"He knows we can't go back," Ilse said. "And he knows the only way forward is up."

I looked at the signal smoke, and at the shattered anchor, and at Doss standing apart from us with his shaking hands, and at Maret's small rigid back, and I understood that we had bought our crossing with more than two lives. We'd bought it with something inside the party itself, some seam of trust that had cracked under the load, and cracks like that don't close on their own. They travel. They wait for the next weight.

"Pack up," I said. "We climb while the light holds. Whatever's waiting on that ridge, I'd rather meet it tired than meet it dark."

We climbed. And all that evening, in the blue cold above the gorge, I kept hearing it: not the bridge falling, not the river taking them—but the creak of those cables under the first man's boots, the sound of weight testing rot, which is, when I am honest, the sound of everything any of us had been refusing to say to each other since Ferrow.

The mountains would have their say about that soon enough. Mountains always collect.
