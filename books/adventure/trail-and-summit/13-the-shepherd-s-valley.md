## 13. The Shepherd's Valley

The fog came down the mountainside like something poured, and Maret lost the party three times in an hour.

She hated that. Hated the way the others moved through white without seeming to think about it—Edmund reading the slope with his whole body, Ilse counting steps under her breath, Doss humming some ferryman's tune to keep the rhythm. Maret just walked and fell and walked again, thirteen years old and furious at the mountain for not explaining itself.

"Left of the cairn," Tam said, appearing at her elbow the way he did, soundless as a goat. "You're drifting."

"I know where I'm going."

"You don't." He said it without cruelty, the way you'd tell someone the sky was blue. "Nobody does, in fog. That's why you follow the cairns."

She wanted to snap at him, but Tam was hard to snap at. He had that valley patience, the kind that came from growing up where everyone knew everyone and nobody hurried. He'd been leading them down from the high trail since dawn, off the route marked on Edmund's map, onto paths that existed on no map at all.

"Your grandmother's valley," Maret said. "Is it true nobody's written it down?"

"Written it where?"

"Anywhere. A map, a ledger, a—anything."

Tam considered this with the seriousness he gave everything. "My grandmother says a thing written down can be read by the wrong eyes. So we keep it in the head. Heads burn slower than paper."

Maret turned that over the whole descent. *Heads burn slower than paper.* She'd been on her own since she was eleven, and she'd learned a hundred tricks for staying invisible—never sleep in the same barn twice, never give your whole name, never let anyone learn what you loved because that was the handle they'd use to drag you back. But Tam's people had made an art of it. A whole valley of people practicing invisibility for forty years, and doing it together.

She had never once imagined that could be a thing. Invisibility, but with company.

* * *

The fog broke all at once, the way it does, like a curtain yanked aside, and there was the valley.

Maret stopped walking. She didn't decide to stop; her legs just did it.

It was a green bowl cupped between two granite shoulders, maybe a mile long, stitched with drystone walls and threaded by a stream the color of bottle glass. Sheep moved on the upper slopes like weather. There were houses—low, stone-roofed, half-buried in the hillside as if the valley were trying to keep them secret even from the sky—and smoke rising from a dozen of them, thin and gray and domestic. Terraced fields climbed the south-facing slope in green steps. Someone was cutting hay in the near field, the scythe flashing with a rhythm so steady it sounded like a clock.

"Nobody lives up here," Maret said. "The surveys say nobody lives up here. I read the surveys."

"The surveys say a lot of things," Tam said.

Behind her, she heard Ilse make a sound Maret had never heard from her before—a small, cracked breath, like a woman catching a falling glass before it hit the floor. Maret turned. Ilse had both hands over her mouth. Her eyes were shining and wet, and she was staring at the valley the way Maret imagined you'd stare at a person you'd buried coming up the road.

"What?" Maret asked. "What is it?"

"The walls," Ilse said, when she could. "The way they're laid. Herringbone, with the coping stones on edge. That's Kestrian. That's *home* Kestrian, that's how my father—" She stopped. Pressed her hands harder against her mouth. Above her fingers, her eyes said the rest: *that's how my father built them, forty years ago, in a country that doesn't exist.*

Edmund came up beside them, and Maret watched his face do something complicated. He didn't cry. He went very still, the way he went still before hard decisions, and then he said, quietly, "They've kept the building styles. Forty years."

"They've kept more than that," Tam said.

* * *

The first people to meet them were children.

That was how it always was, Maret would think later. In every village, in every valley, it was the children who came out first, because children hadn't yet learned what adults knew about strangers. Six of them came running down the hay terraces, barefoot, shrieking something at Tam in a language Maret didn't know—and then, halfway through the shout, the language bent and became words she *almost* knew, words that were like the trade-tongue but tilted, vowels rounded differently, consonants softened, a song hiding inside the sentence.

"What was that?" she whispered to Ilse.

"Kestrian," Ilse said. Her voice was not steady. "That was Kestrian. Children's Kestrian—the way it's spoken when children learn it from each other and not from books. I've only ever read it. I've never—" She laughed, a wet, disbelieving laugh. "I've studied a dead language for ten years and these children are using it to argue about a goat."

The children reached them and stopped in a clump, staring. Staring at Tam first, and then at the strangers, and then—Maret felt it happen—at her. At all of them, but at her longest. She knew why. She had the look. Dark-haired, dark-eyed, the look that had gotten her called names in every lowland town and never explained, because she had no one to explain it. Her mother had died without telling her where the look came from.

One of the children, a girl younger than Maret with a smudge of hay dust across her nose, said something in the tilted language and pointed at Maret's face.

Tam answered, and the children laughed, and the girl said something else, insistent.

"What did she say?" Maret asked.

Tam's mouth twitched. "She asked if you're a cousin. From over the water. She says you have the Vellis chin."

"The what?"

"Old family name. There's a carving of a Vellis woman in the chapel with your chin exactly." He shrugged. "Could be nonsense. Could be every dark-haired stranger gets the Vellis chin. Children like finding relatives."

Maret's chest did something strange and painful. She had never been anybody's could-be relative before. She had been *orphan* and *stray* and *that girl*, but never a chin in a chapel, never a maybe-cousin. She turned away so no one would see her face do what Ilse's face had done at the walls.

* * *

The adults came slower. That was the first thing Maret noticed, and the thing she kept noticing all evening.

The children ran; the adults *arrived*. They came down from the fields and doorways at a walk, in ones and twos, and they arranged themselves in a half-circle at the valley's mouth with a deliberateness that wasn't hostile but wasn't welcome either. It was the posture of people who had done this before—who had met strangers on their threshold and measured them, and knew exactly what the measuring was for.

The elder was a woman of maybe seventy, straight-backed, with a shepherd's crook she clearly used as a scepter. Tam called her Grandmother Sera and everyone else just called her the Elder, and Maret watched the whole valley's spine straighten when she spoke.

"You bring strangers past the mist-line," the Elder said to Tam. Not a question. An indictment with the volume turned down.

"They were on the high trail in fog with the season turning. Grandmother, the man is a mountain guide. The woman is a scholar. The big one is a ferryman. The girl—" Tam glanced at Maret. "The girl has the look."

A murmur went through the half-circle. Maret felt it move across her skin like a draft. *The look.* Her whole life the look had been an accusation. Here, for the first time, it was testimony.

"The man is Vane," the Elder said, and Maret saw Edmund go still in that particular way of his. "The blacklisted guide. The one they still name at the checkpoints." Her eyes were pale gray and missed nothing. "You bring a wanted man into a valley that exists by not being found."

"I bring a man who carried a stranger's chest out of a flood," Tam said. "I bring the woman who's spent ten years carrying Kestria's papers on her back. I bring—" he hesitated, and Maret realized with a jolt that Tam, patient Tam, was nervous, "—I bring people who are going where we would not dare to go. To the tribunal. With the chest."

The murmur again, but different now. Frightened. Maret watched it travel the half-circle like wind through barley—shoulders drawing in, eyes dropping, a woman pulling a boy behind her skirts. And she understood, with the quick terrible clarity she was sometimes cursed with, that these people were afraid of the chest. Not of Edmund, not of Vane's blacklist, not of the mercenaries two days behind them. Of the *chest*. Of the thing that said *Kestria* out loud in a world that had forbidden the word.

"You've heard of it," Ilse said. She stepped forward, and Maret saw her do the thing she did when she was about to fight—chin up, hands quiet, the archivist becoming an advocate. "You've heard that it exists."

"We hear everything," the Elder said. "We hear the lowlands the way the lowlands hear the weather. We heard a chest survived the annexation. We heard it was real. We also heard, forty years ago, that Kestria was real, and look what hearing did for us." Her crook tapped the ground once, a full stop. "You'll wash and eat, because no one leaves this valley hungry—that law is older than your chest and it will outlast your trouble. And in the morning, you'll be gone the way you came."

"Grandmother—" Tam started.

"The law holds, Tam." But her eyes went, just for a moment, to the chest lashed to Doss's back. Just for a moment. Maret saw it. The Elder saw that Maret saw, and something in the old woman's face flickered—not softness exactly, but recognition. One keeper of secrets recognizing another.

* * *

They were given the chapel loft to sleep in, and supper in the longhouse, and the supper was where everything Maret thought she understood about the valley came apart.

The food was plain and abundant—barley soup, dark bread, cheese pressed with a pattern of interlocking rings, and a honey cake that made Doss close his eyes and say a word in ferryman's slang that made the children giggle. The longhouse was warm and crowded and loud, and Maret sat wedged between Ilse and a grandmother who kept putting more food on her plate without asking, which was a kind of love Maret had only ever read about.

And then, after the eating was done and the lamps were low, someone began to sing.

It was the hay-cutters first, three men at the far table, low and unhurried, a work song with a rising refrain. The room quieted. Then the grandmother beside Maret joined, her voice old and cracked and sure, and then the children, and then half the room, and the song climbed and turned and came around on itself like the switchbacks on the mountain, and Maret felt the hair stand up on her arms before she understood why.

She looked at Ilse. Ilse had gone white.

"You know it," Maret whispered.

"It's the *Vellis Lament*," Ilse whispered back. "It's in the archive. It's in *three* archives. It's the mourning song for the fall of the capital—there are maybe two written versions and scholars argued for a century about how it was actually sung, because nobody—" Her voice broke off. The song rolled over them, forty voices strong, and Ilse pressed her knuckles against her lips and Maret watched a decade of scholarship dissolve into tears.

"They know it," Maret said.

"They've always known it. They never stopped singing it." Ilse laughed and cried at the same time, silently, so as not to interrupt. "Do you understand what this room is? Do you understand what I would have given—what the archive would have given—to record one verse of this, sung true? And they sing it every night. They sing it every night and they will *not* say the name."

And that was the thing, Maret realized. That was the crack running through the whole golden evening. She listened closer, past the melody, and heard it: the song named Kestria in its refrain—or it would have, in the old words. Here the singers swerved around it, the way a tongue finds a missing tooth. The name had been lifted out and the song stitched shut around the hole, and everyone sang the hole together, fluently, without stumbling.

They had kept everything except the word. They had kept the songs and the walls and the cheese-press pattern and the chapel carvings and the children's language and the *look*, all of it, alive, for forty years—and they kept it the way you keep a fire in a room where the smoke would kill you: banked, covered, breathing thin.

After the song, the grandmother beside Maret patted her hand and said something in Kestrian, then translated herself, her accent thick as porridge: "You have the Vellis chin, little one. My mother had a Vellis chin." She smiled. "We don't say what that means. But we know. We all know, and we don't say."

"Why not?" Maret asked. It came out sharper than she meant. "Why not just *say*?"

The grandmother looked at her with eyes that had buried the reason and kept the grave. "Because the ones who took the name took everything that said it. Fields. Houses. Names on stones." She tapped Maret's hand. "We learned to keep the inside and lose the outside. It is not nothing, little one. But it is not everything either." She sighed, and for a moment she looked very old and very tired. "You are too young to know the difference. Eat your cake."

* * *

Maret couldn't sleep. She lay in the chapel loft under a sheepskin that smelled of smoke and lanolin, listening to the valley breathe, and the ceiling beams above her were carved—she'd noticed at supper, by lamplight, and now in the dark she reached up and traced them with her fingers. Interlocking rings. The same pattern as the cheese-press. The same pattern, she'd bet anything, as the seal on the chest that had started all of this.

A chapel. A real Kestrian chapel, with carvings and probably frescoes, and Ilse sleeping below her in a sleeping bag with the chest between her knees like a child, and neither of them allowed to open anything, ask anything, *name* anything, because in the morning they'd be gone.

She heard footsteps on the loft ladder and went still. Tam's head appeared through the hatch.

"Can't sleep either?" he said.

"Your valley is the most incredible place I've ever seen," Maret said, "and everyone in it is terrified. How do you stand it?"

Tam came the rest of the way up and sat across from her, cross-legged, a dark shape against the star-slit window. For a long time he didn't answer. Down in the valley, a dog barked once and gave up.

"My father wanted to say the name," he said finally. "When I was small. He'd had too much of the barley spirit one midwinter and he stood up at the longhouse and said he was Kestrian and his father was Kestrian and he was done swallowing it." Tam's voice was flat, reciting. "The Elder—my grandmother—took him out to the sheepfold and talked to him till dawn. And in the spring he took the high trail to find work in the lowlands, and in the summer the wardens came through the mist-line, the first time in nine years, and asked questions, and burned the hay terraces on the south slope. Nobody was hurt. Nobody was even touched. That was the message. They didn't need to touch anyone." He picked at a splinter in the floorboard. "He came back in the autumn and he never said it again. He sings the hole in the song like everyone else now. He sings it *well*."

Maret didn't know what to say to that. She thought of her own tricks for staying invisible, her barns and half-names, and how she'd always thought of them as survival, never as a wound. Maybe they were both. Maybe everything you did to stay alive left a mark, and the trick was just choosing which marks.

"I'm going where you wouldn't dare to go," she said. "That's what you told your grandmother. With the chest."

"I know."

"Aren't you angry? That we're taking it through and your valley stays—stays *swallowed*?"

Tam was quiet for a while. "My grandmother says a thing written down can be read by the wrong eyes," he said. "But she also says a thing never written down can be denied by the wrong mouths. Both are true. That's the whole trouble." He stood. "Sleep, Vellis-chin. Tomorrow you'll be gone the way you came, and the valley will sing the hole in the song, and that's how it is."

He went down the ladder. Maret lay back and stared at the carved rings above her and did not sleep, and somewhere past midnight she heard, from the longhouse across the yard, a single voice singing the lament alone—old, cracked, sure—and then, very softly, another voice joining it. And another.

They couldn't help it, she thought. Even afraid, even swallowed, they couldn't help singing. That was the whole valley in one sound: everything kept, everything denied, and the song going on anyway.

She was still listening when she heard, below her, the whisper of Ilse sitting up, and the soft scrape of the chest's iron bands, and Edmund's low voice saying, "Ilse. *Ilse.* Not tonight."

And then Ilse's voice, tight and trembling with something that was not fear, or not only fear:

"Edmund—the elder's grandson said the chapel. He said the *chapel* has the carvings. If there are frescoes in a Kestrian chapel from before the annexation, they might name faces. They might name *him*. The one who—" A breath. "I have to know what's in this chapel. And I think you do too."
