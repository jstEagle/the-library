## 14. The Elder's Refusal

The valley announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and wet hay and something sweeter beneath it, a flowering shrub Ilse had only ever read about in her father's notes—*kestrian bramble*, he'd called it, though the botanists had renamed it twice since the annexation. She stood at the mouth of the shepherd's path with the wind pulling at her coat and felt her throat tighten. Forty years. Forty years of exile, of forged papers and whispered names, and here was a place where the old songs had survived like seeds under snow.

Below them, terraced pastures stepped down toward a cluster of stone houses with slate roofs, smoke rising straight in the still evening air. Sheep dotted the slopes like spilled chalk. A dog barked once, twice, then fell silent as if scolded.

"They're watching us already," Edmund said quietly. He stood a pace behind her, the chest slung across his back on the frame Doss had rigged from ferry straps. His eyes moved over the ridgelines with the unhurried precision she'd come to rely on. "Three watchers. Maybe four. The one on the eastern crag has been there since we came off the glacier."

"Then they've had time to decide what we are."

"People in hiding don't decide kindly." He shifted the chest's weight. "You should know that better than anyone."

She did know it. That was precisely why she'd led them here.

Maret came scrambling up the switchback behind them, cheeks bright with cold, Tam's name already half out of her mouth before she caught herself and swallowed it. The boy who'd met them at the valley's edge—an angular teenager with his grandmother's watchful eyes—had introduced himself with a single word: *Hesta*. It meant *strangers* in the old tongue, and it was clearly all he intended to say until his elders said otherwise.

"He says we're to wait here," Maret reported. "Someone's coming up from the houses."

"Someone" took twenty minutes to arrive, which told Ilse everything about how carefully the valley moved. The woman who finally climbed the path was past seventy, broad-shouldered, wrapped in a shepherd's cloak the color of wet slate. She walked with a staff cut from blackthorn and stopped a full ten paces away—the distance, Ilse noted, of a person who has practiced being hard to reach.

Her eyes went first to the chest on Edmund's back. They stayed there a long moment. When she looked up at Ilse, something flickered behind the wariness—recognition, or its ghost.

"You wear Kestrian boots," the woman said. Her accent was thickened by decades of disuse, the vowels rounded wrong, but the words were unmistakably the old language. *Kestrian boots.* Ilse looked down at her own feet, at the stitching pattern her father had taught her to identify when she was nine years old, and felt absurdly close to tears.

"Ilse Brenner," she said, in the same tongue. "Archivist. Daughter of Aldric Brenner, who kept the registry at Vell."

"I know whose daughter you are." The woman's face closed like a door. "I am Sera Vossen. I keep this valley. And you will turn around and go back the way you came."

* * *

They were given a barn for the night—which was more hospitality than refusal, Ilse thought, and less than hope. The barn sat apart from the houses, downwind, as if the valley wanted to be able to deny them later. Hay that smelled of summer. A trough of water. A single oil lamp that Sera Vossen's grandson lit without meeting anyone's eyes, then left without a word.

Doss dropped onto the hay with a groan that came from his boots upward. "I've been thrown out of nicer places," he said. "But not many."

Maret was not so easily settled. She paced the length of the barn, running her fingers along the beams, and stopped abruptly at the far gable. "Ilse. Come look at this."

Carved into the wood, low where a child might have reached, was a row of letters in the old script. Ilse knelt. The cuts were shallow, weathered, made by a small hand long ago. *Aldar venn kestria*—*under Kestria's wing*. Beneath it, a date in the old reckoning: the year of the annexation.

"A child cut this," Maret said softly. "The same year they—" She didn't finish. She'd grown up stateless herself; she knew how sentences like that ended.

"The children here learned their letters in secret," Ilse said. "My father wrote about it. Schools that didn't exist, teachers who were 'aunts teaching needlework.' The script passed hand to hand like contraband." She touched the carved letters. "This is why we came. This is proof the people never let go. The tribunal needs living testimony, and here it is, cut into a barn beam."

"And the people need to stay alive," Edmund said from the doorway. He'd been standing there a while; she hadn't heard him come in, which still unsettled her, a man that size moving that quietly. "That's the arithmetic she's doing. You're asking them to trade forty years of survival for a document."

"I'm asking them to help carry it across the range. Nothing more. No names in any record they don't choose."

"You think Voss's employer cares about fine distinctions?" Edmund shook his head slowly. "I've seen what men like that do to villages that shelter what they want burned. I've *been* what men like that do."

Ilse turned to face him fully. In the lamplight his face was all planes and shadow, the scar from the checkpoint pulling at his cheekbone. "Then help me convince her," she said. "Because if we go around this valley, we lose two days we don't have, and if we lose two days, the passes close with us on the wrong side of them—and the chest dies in the snow along with everything it carries."

Edmund held her gaze for a long moment. Whatever he saw there seemed to settle something in him, because he sighed and lowered himself onto the hay beside Doss. "Dawn, then," he said. "Elders wake early. So do arguments."

* * *

Sera Vossen received them in her own house at first light, in a low room that smelled of wool and tallow, and Ilse understood within minutes that she had walked into a harder negotiation than any tribunal chamber could offer. Because tribunals argued about law, and Sera Vossen argued about graves.

"You want to know what happened here after the annexation?" the elder said. She sat by the fire with her hands folded on her staff, and she did not raise her voice once in the entire conversation, which somehow made every word land heavier. "I was six years old. I remember soldiers counting us. Not registering us—*counting* us, like stock. My father gave them the wrong number. They made him kneel in the square while they decided whether to believe the second count or shoot him for the first. They let him live. Two winters later the fever came through, and no doctor would come to a village of ghosts, because officially we did not exist. We buried eleven people that spring. Eleven. My mother among them. And you know what was written in the district ledger?" She leaned forward. "*No settlements recorded in this valley.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a rooster insisted on the morning.

"We survived by being nothing," Sera continued. "By singing our songs with the windows shut. By teaching the letters in cellars. By burying our dead with plain stones and keeping the true names in our mouths where no clerk could reach them. Forty years, archivist. And you climb down out of the high country carrying—" her eyes flicked to the chest, sitting against the wall where Edmund had placed it, "—the one object in the world that can get every soul in this valley killed, and you ask me to let it pass through my home."

"Not through your home," Ilse said. "Across your high pasture. One day's walk. The eastern route joins the Stair of Winds below the col—if your guides know the way, and I'm told they do."

"Who told you?"

"Your grandson has a mouth, even if he doesn't use it much."

Something almost like humor crossed the old woman's face and was gone. "Tam is fifteen and flattered by strangers. That is not an argument."

"It's the beginning of one." Ilse leaned forward, mirroring the elder's posture. "Sera Vossen. I have spent ten years chasing this chest. Ten years of archives and bribed clerks and sleeping in ditches. Do you know why? Not for the documents themselves. Documents are only paper. I do it because three years ago I watched a Kestrian woman in Ferrow die in a charity ward with no name in any register—no name, no family record, nothing—so they buried her under a number. *A number.* She sang lullabies her grandmother taught her, songs that are four hundred years old, and the state that let her die wrote on her grave marker that no such person had ever lived." Ilse heard her own voice roughen and did not fight it. "The chest can end that. The registry inside it names every stolen child, every displaced family, every title the annexation voided. Before the tribunal sits, it can give us back our legal existence—not just for the royal line. For everyone. For the woman buried under a number, and for the eleven you buried in the fever spring, and for every child in this valley cutting letters into barn beams in the dark."

Sera Vossen was silent for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—lower, wearier, the voice of the woman behind the office. "And when the tribunal fails? When the collectors' money reaches further than our truth? When the men who burned Vell come looking for the valley that helped the chest on its way?" She shook her head. "Hope is a debt, girl. And debts are always collected."

"Then let us be the ones who owe it."

"That's not how debts work."

"No," Ilse admitted. "It isn't."

* * *

The argument ran through the morning and into the afternoon, and by evening it had gathered an audience. Word traveled through a hidden valley faster than through an open one—there was nowhere else for news to go—and so the low room filled, gradually, with shepherds and grandmothers and one enormous silent man who turned out to be the smith, all of them standing along the walls pretending not to listen while listening with their whole bodies.

It was the audience that changed the argument, Ilse realized. Because every time Sera Vossen said *we cannot risk it*, someone in the room flinched. And every time Ilse said *they cannot erase us forever*, someone's chin lifted a fraction.

An old man near the hearth began, very quietly, to hum. It took Ilse a moment to place the melody, and when she did, her breath caught: it was the *Vell Lament*, the song her father had transcribed from a refugee's memory thirty years ago, believing it already lost. The hummer's wife joined him. Then a young woman holding an infant. Nobody sang words. Words were still too dangerous. But the melody rose through the smoky room like water finding its level, and Sera Vossen sat with her eyes closed and her jaw tight, and Ilse understood that the argument had left the room entirely and gone somewhere no persuasion could follow.

The elder opened her eyes. "Enough," she said, and the humming stopped. "You'll stay the night. We'll speak again at dawn."

"Sera—"

"*At dawn.*" But her voice cracked on the word, just slightly, and Ilse thought: *she is not refusing anymore. She is grieving the safety she's about to give up.*

* * *

Ilse couldn't sleep. Near midnight she climbed to the barn loft where the chest was kept under Edmund's watch—he slept in snatches, she'd noticed, never more than an hour, a habit she suspected predated the mercenaries—and found him awake, as expected, mending a strap by lamplight.

"She'll yield," Ilse said. "Not tonight. But she'll yield."

"You argue like your father wrote," Edmund said. "Everything load-bearing, nothing decorative."

"Is that a compliment?"

"It's an observation." He set down the strap. "There's something you should see. I found it this evening, in the chest's false bottom. I would have shown you sooner, but—" he glanced toward the door, toward the valley full of listening ears, "—not with an audience."

He lifted the registry's leather cover and turned to a page near the front, one Ilse had catalogued years ago in a copy of a copy, its edges damaged. Here the original was whole. And there, in the column of confiscated persons, was an entry that made the barn feel suddenly colder.

*Vossen, Marta. Of the high valleys. Taken, age seven, to the Eastern Schools. Status: erased.*

"Marta Vossen," Ilse read aloud. "Related to the elder?"

"Grandmother's sister, by the dates. Or great-aunt." Edmund's voice was careful. "The elder's family is in the registry, Ilse. The chest she wants to turn away carries her own people's names."

Ilse stared at the entry. *Taken, age seven. Status: erased.* Somewhere in the conquerors' school system, a seven-year-old girl had been renamed, re-tongued, remade—and somewhere in this valley, an old woman had spent forty years keeping the memory of her alive in secret, the way they kept everything.

"This changes things," Ilse said slowly.

"It might. Or it might make her dig in deeper—grief does both." Edmund closed the chest. "But you should go into the dawn argument knowing it. You asked me once why I carry this thing when it's brought me nothing but fire and blood." He looked at the chest, and his expression was unreadable. "This is why. It remembers people the world agreed to forget. Even the ones who'd rather it didn't."

* * *

At dawn the valley was white to the knees with ground fog, and Sera Vossen was waiting at the barn door before Ilse had finished lacing her boots.

"I dreamed of my sister last night," the elder said, without preamble. "First time in twenty years. She was seven years old in the dream. Standing in a corridor. Calling my name in the old tongue, and I couldn't answer, because in the dream I'd forgotten the words." Her hands gripped the blackthorn staff until the knuckles whitened. "I woke up angry. At you. At myself. At forty years of being careful." She turned and looked east, where the peaks above the valley caught the first light, burning orange and gold above the fog. "Show me the page."

Ilse fetched the chest. Edmund opened it. Sera Vossen read her sister's name in silence, and then she did something Ilse had not expected: she knelt, stiffly, joints cracking, and pressed her forehead briefly against the chest's lid, the way one greets an elder or a shrine.

When she rose, she was the administrator again, brisk and dry-eyed. "You'll have a guide. My grandson Tam knows the eastern route to the Stair better than any living shepherd—he's been mapping it in his head since he was ten, God help him, drawing little charts nobody asked for." A ghost of pride crossed her face. "He goes with you, and he comes back, or don't bother coming back yourself."

"Thank you," Ilse began.

"Don't thank me yet." Sera raised one finger, and her voice dropped into something older than warning—something prophetic. "You asked for my knowledge of the passes, so take it freely: the sky is lying this season. The dry wind that should hold another month died three days ago—I've felt it in my knees and my sheep have felt it in their bones, and both have out-predicted the weather clerks for sixty years. The high country will give you blue mornings to draw you up and then close on you like a trap. Your cartographer friend reads clouds well enough"—she nodded at Edmund, who inclined his head—"but even he should hear this: whatever the sky promises above the tree line, believe the mountain instead. Turn back early. Turn back often. And if the wind swings north and smells of ice—" she fixed Ilse with a stare that had outlasted an empire, "—you get under stone and you *stay* there."

"We'll remember it," Ilse said.

"See that you do." Sera Vossen turned away, then paused at the barn door, her back to them. "My sister was erased at seven years old," she said quietly. "If your chest can un-erase even one such child, then burn my caution with the rest of it. Go. And may the mountain be kinder than the men."

She strode off into the fog toward the houses, and a moment later Tam appeared in her wake, as if summoned, a bedroll on his back and a coil of good rope over one shoulder and an expression caught between terror and triumph.

"I'm to take you by the eastern route," he said, in careful, formal Kestrian, each word polished like a stone he'd practiced overnight. "Two days to the Stair, if the weather holds." He hesitated, then added, in a rush, in the border tongue: "It won't hold. Grandmother's knees are never wrong. But I know a shortcut across the glacier that saves half a day, if—" he glanced at Edmund, sizing him up with sudden boldness, "—if you're as good on ice as she says you are."

Edmund and Ilse exchanged a look over the boy's head. Above the valley, the peaks stood innocent and golden in the morning sun, promising nothing but blue.

"Half a day," Edmund said slowly, and Ilse heard the arithmetic turning over behind his eyes—the days lost, the deadline, the closing passes. "Show me this shortcut on a map, Tam. Tonight. If the route is sound, we move at first light."

Behind them, the fog was already burning off the pastures, and somewhere in the brightening valley a child was singing—words and all now, quiet and defiant, the old song drifting up the slope like smoke from a fire no one would admit to lighting.
