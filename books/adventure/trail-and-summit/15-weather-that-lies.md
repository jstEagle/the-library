## 15. Weather That Lies

The sky lied to everyone but Edmund.

It was a small thing at first—the kind of thing a man could ignore if he wanted to, and most men would have wanted to. A lenticular cloud sitting over the western peaks like a lens cap left on the sun. Wind that had blown from the south for three days straight when it should have swung east by now. The smell of the air itself, too heavy, too sweet, carrying the mineral breath of snowfields that should have been months from melting.

Edmund stood at the edge of camp with his hands on his hips and read it the way other men read letters from home.

*Early,* he thought. *The dry season is ending early.*

Behind him, the party was breaking down camp in the slow, grumbling way of people who had slept badly on frozen ground. Maret was limping. He'd watched her limp all yesterday afternoon and said nothing, because saying something would mean stopping, and stopping was no longer something they could afford. Doss was coiling rope with his big deliberate hands, taking twice as long as he needed to because Doss did everything at ferryman's pace and dared the world to hurry him. Ilse had the chest strapped across her back already, the way she always did first thing, as if she feared someone might take it in the night—though who would climb this high to steal it, Edmund couldn't imagine. Tam sat apart, sharpening his ice axe with a whetstone, watching Edmund with that flat shepherd's stare that hadn't quite warmed into trust even after the glacier fork, after Edmund had overridden his route and saved them all from the crevasse field. Respect, maybe. But respect and warmth were different animals, and Tam kept them in separate pens.

"Sky's wrong," Edmund said.

Doss didn't look up. "Sky's been wrong since Ferrow."

"It's been getting wronger." Edmund pointed west, where the lenticular cloud had begun to grow a second tier beneath it, stacked like plates. "That's a cap cloud building down, not up. Means the jet's dropped. Means there's moisture underneath it looking for an excuse." He turned. "The passes close early this year. Maybe a week early. Maybe more."

Ilse stopped adjusting the chest straps. "How much more?"

"I don't know. That's what 'early' means." He heard the edge in his own voice and didn't soften it. Softening cost time. "We have maybe six days of good travel. Seven if we're lucky, and I don't plan around luck."

"We planned around the almanac," Ilse said. "My father's notes said—"

"Your father's notes were written twenty years ago by a man sitting in a warm archive." He regretted it before he finished saying it—he saw her flinch, saw the word *father* land somewhere between them like a dropped stone—but he let it stand, because it was true, and truth was the only ration they had enough of. "I'm reading today's sky. We move fast today. Faster tomorrow. Nobody straggles."

Maret pulled her boot off and the smell of it reached him before he saw the damage: the sour-sweet reek of skin that had been dying quietly for two days. Her heel was a white crater, the blister torn and refilled and torn again, the skin around it angry red all the way to the ankle. She held the boot in her lap and looked at it with the stubborn blankness of a girl who had decided long ago that complaining was a currency she'd never be rich enough to spend.

"How long has it looked like that?" Edmund asked.

"Since the scree field."

"Two days?"

She shrugged. "It's fine."

"It's not fine. It's infected or close to it." He crouched, took her foot in both hands without asking—she hissed—and turned it toward the light. The wound was ugly but clean at the bottom. "You should have said something."

"You'd have slowed down."

"Yes," he said. "That's how it works."

"No, it isn't." She pulled her foot back and began wrapping it with the strip of linen she'd been using, gray now, stiff with dried fluid. "Not anymore. Not since you started walking like the mountain owes you money. You want us fast, we're fast. You want us quiet, we're quiet. So my heel is fine." She looked up at him then, sixteen years old with eyes like chipped flint, and delivered the rest without cruelty, which somehow made it worse: "You taught me that. First week out of Ferrow. You said the mountain doesn't care about our feelings, so neither do you."

He had said that. He remembered saying it, standing over her on the valley trail while she cried about homesickness she wouldn't admit to, and he had meant it as a kindness—a hard shell for a soft kid—and now it came back to him wearing its true face.

"Wrap it tighter," he said. "Change the linen tonight. And tell me next time before it gets to this."

"And you'll stop?"

"I'll decide," he said. "That's the deal. You report, I decide. That's what a leader's for."

Maret snorted and went back to her wrapping, and Edmund stood, and found Doss watching him with an expression the ferryman usually saved for river ice—assessing thickness, calculating load.

* *

They made eleven miles before noon, which was good, and then the mountain began collecting its fee.

The trail climbed out of the pine shadow and onto a shoulder of bare granite where the wind came off the western icefield with nothing to slow it. It hit them sideways, gusting hard enough to lean on, and Edmund watched the sky while he walked—the cap cloud thickening, its underside going the dirty yellow-gray that meant weight, water, mass. Below them, far down the southern flank, he could see the lowlands still basking in their dry-season blue, and the distance between that blue and this gray felt like the distance between the person he'd been twelve years ago and the one walking here now: same mountain, different weather entirely.

He set the pace at a march. Not a cruel one—he told himself it wasn't cruel—but the pace of a man with arithmetic in his head. Six days of good travel. Four days to the mid refuge if they pushed. Two days of margin, and margin was the only thing standing between them and a winter buried above the cloud line, and he knew what winters above the cloud line did to parties of five with one infected heel and a chest full of paper.

Maret fell behind. Then caught up. Then fell behind again, walking on the outside of her ruined heel like a crab, refusing the shoulder Doss offered three times, four times, five, until Doss stopped offering and simply walked beside her, matching her broken rhythm, being a wall she could lean on without admitting it.

By late afternoon the light had gone strange—that flat, sourceless glare that comes when the sun burns through thin cloud and gives no shadows—and Edmund called a halt on a shelf of stone with a windbreak of boulders to the west. They were two hours short of the day's target. He knew it. Everyone knew it. Nobody said it, which somehow made the two hours heavier than luggage.

Tam built the stove ring in silence. Ilse got water boiling. Maret sat down against a boulder and was asleep before the tea was ready, sitting upright, her wrapped foot stuck out in front of her like something washed up on a beach.

"She needs a day off that foot," Doss said quietly, beside Edmund at the windbreak. Not looking at him. Looking at the girl.

"She needs a refuge with a fire. One day behind us is one day closer to the storm."

"One day of walking on that heel is one day closer to losing the foot. You've seen rot set in. Don't tell me you haven't."

"I've seen it." Twelve years ago, another range, another lifetime, a porter named Anselm who'd hidden a blister until the black crept up past his ankle and the choice stopped being a choice. Edmund had carried the memory of Anselm's face ever since, along with everything else he carried. "And I've seen what happens to parties that stop moving when the sky turns. Both things are true. That's the job. Choosing which true thing kills you slower."

"That's not a job," Doss said. "That's a gamble. And you're gambling with our feet to chase—" He stopped. Chewed the words a moment, like tobacco. Edmund knew what was coming; he'd known for two days. "To chase a ghost."

"Doss."

"No. Hear it." Doss finally turned, and his face in the flat light was older than his years, river-aged, worn smooth and cracked deep. "Twelve years I've known you. Twelve years you counted miles like a miser counts coins, and never once told me why, and I never asked, because a man's reasons are his own. Fine. But now I watch you drive a child with a rotten heel up a mountain because the sky scared you, and I watch you not sleep, and I watch you look at that chest like it's going to open up and hand you back your father, and I'm asking, Edmund. Plainly. Are we climbing to save these people"—he tipped his head at Ilse, at sleeping Maret, at Tam—"or are we climbing to settle something with a dead man? Because those are different mountains. And you're the only one who knows which one we're on."

The wind filled the silence. Somewhere above them, ice calved off the western seracs with a sound like a door slamming in an empty house.

Edmund wanted to say: *both.* He wanted to say: *it's not that simple.* Both things were true, and he had learned from mountains that both-things-true was just the shape honesty takes at altitude. But Doss deserved better than altitude honesty. Doss had cut a bridge with a man screaming on it and come away with the stain still on his soul, and had kept walking, and had kept carrying, and had never once asked Edmund for anything except this.

"I don't know," Edmund said.

Doss blinked. Of all the answers, that one plainly wasn't loaded in his rifle.

"My father drew the map that got us over the glacier," Edmund said. "Every fork, every safe line—it's his handwriting, his mind, forty years old and still saving lives. And I hate him. And I trust him. Both. Every step I take on his lines is a step I take with him, and I can't put him down, Doss, because he's under my boots." He looked west, at the cloud stacking itself like masonry. "Am I chasing his ghost? Maybe. Probably. But the storm is real whether the ghost is or not. The sky doesn't care why we're up here. It just closes."

"So we go faster."

"So we go faster. And I carry Maret if her heel goes. And you keep hating me if you need to, because hatred is lighter than grief and you'll need your strength."

Doss stared at him a long moment. Then he made a sound that was half laugh, half cough, and shook his head. "You know what your problem is, Vane? You make sense at the exact moments I've decided to be furious at you. It's a gift. It's also insufferable."

"Go wake the girl. Tea's ready."

* *

They climbed into the evening because Edmund couldn't make himself stop.

He knew the arguments against it—tired climbers fall, tired judgment errs, night cold steals heat the body can't spare—but he also knew the cap cloud had begun to stream eastward off the summit like smoke off a wick, and streaming caps meant wind aloft, and wind aloft meant the system behind it was organizing, tightening, coming. He had watched that exact progression once before, eleven years ago, from the safety of a valley floor, and watched the rescue teams bring down what was left of a survey party that had trusted the almanac. Five men. He'd helped bury two of them. He had no intention of learning what his own face looked like from the burial side.

So they climbed. The trail switchbacked up a rib of dark stone, and the wind rose with them, and by full dark it was singing in the rocks with a note Edmund didn't like—a low, hollow note, the sound of air moving through a gap that shouldn't be there, the mountain clearing its throat.

They made the day's target plus the missing two hours by moonrise. Camp was a scramble: tent half-pitched, stove hissing, everyone eating with their backs to the wind. Maret woke enough to eat and change her dressing—Edmund changed it for her, kneeling in the dirt, and the wound looked no worse, which in his current ledger counted as victory—and then she was asleep again before he'd packed the old linen away.

Ilse sat with the chest between her knees, arms folded over it, watching him work.

"You're frightened," she said. Not accusing. Reading him, the way she read documents—carefully, completely, aloud.

"I'm paying attention. There's a difference."

"Not to me. I grew up with frightened people pretending to be careful. My whole village was that." She shifted, keeping her voice below the wind. "Tell me the truth. How bad?"

Edmund sat back on his heels. Above them the stars were being eaten, west to east, the cap cloud advancing under the moonlight like spilled ink. "Bad enough that I'd turn us around if turning around would help. It won't. Behind us is lower ground and longer exposure. Ahead is the mid refuge—stone walls, a roof, fuel cached if the cache hasn't been looted. If we make the refuge before the storm breaks, we sit it out warm and lose two days. If we don't make it..." He left the sentence where sentences like that should be left.

"Then we walk faster."

"We walk faster." He almost smiled. "You sound like Doss."

"Doss is right. That's the irritating thing about him." Ilse looked up at the dying stars, and her face in the moonlight was young and old at once, the way faces get when they've carried something heavy since childhood. "My father wrote that the mountain tests the plan, not the climber. I used to think that was wisdom. Now I think it means the plan is always wrong somewhere, and the climber finds out where."

"Your father," Edmund said carefully, "was smarter than mine."

"You don't know that."

"I know exactly what mine was." He said it flatly, and Ilse heard the flatness for what it was—a door closing—and mercifully let it stay closed.

* *

At midnight the wind changed.

Edmund was awake anyway—sleep had become a country he visited briefly and paid tolls to enter—and he felt it before he heard it: the pressure shifting, the tent walls sagging inward and then snapping taut, the temperature dropping through the floor of cold into a new colder country beneath. He crawled out into the moonless dark and stood facing west, and there it was.

The front. A wall of cloud from horizon to horizon, blacker than the night behind it, and above its leading edge the stars flickering in that rapid, nervous way that meant ice crystals already falling through the upper air. Snow that hadn't decided to be snow yet. Snow in transit.

He stood there long enough to feel the first spits of it—dry grains, stinging, striking his face like flung sand—and then he went and woke them all.

No gentle waking. He'd done gentle waking once, years ago, and lost forty minutes to a groggy argument about whether the sky really looked that bad. He put a hand on each shoulder and shook, and said one word: *Now.*

They broke camp in the dark with the spit-snow thickening around them, and nobody complained, which told Edmund everything about how much they trusted him and how much they feared the sky, and he supposed the ratio was healthy. Maret got her pack on with her heel screaming—he saw her jaw clench, saw her say nothing—and Doss simply picked up her share of the food without discussion, and Tam took point without being asked, and Ilse cinched the chest tight and put her head down into the wind.

They climbed.

For two hours they climbed through the gathering dark, the grain-snow becoming flakes, the flakes becoming fat and wet and numerous, the trail vanishing beneath them step by step behind their backs like a bridge being raised. Edmund navigated by feel—by the angle of the slope, the pitch of the wind, the memory of his father's map overlaid on the darkness like a second, truer world—and somewhere in the third hour, exhausted and freezing and afraid, he realized he was smiling.

Because this was the thing he'd spent twelve years pretending he didn't miss. This was the conversation. The mountain speaking, and him answering, and every correct guess a word exchanged between old friends. His body knew this work the way it knew nothing else, and even now—even hollowed out, even haunted, even driving a wounded girl up a mountain on a gambler's arithmetic—some animal part of him was *home*, and the homecoming shamed him and sustained him in equal measure.

The refuge appeared out of the murk at dawn, or what the murk generously offered as dawn: a stone hut squatting on a saddle, roof sagging, door gone, snow already drifting through the empty doorway like the mountain moving in ahead of them.

Edmund got them inside, got the stove lit with the last of Tam's tinder, got Maret's boots off and her feet elevated and hot water wrapped around them, and only then, with the wind rising to a scream against the north wall and the snow coming horizontal past the empty door, did he allow himself to count.

One day. Maybe two, if the cache held fuel and the walls held heat. Against a storm that should have been three weeks out and had arrived three weeks early, lying the whole way, wearing the dry season's blue smile right up until it opened its mouth.

Ilse knelt beside the chest, wiping the drift-snow from its lid with her sleeve, and looked up at him.

"How long?" she asked.

Edmund listened to the wind try the walls like a thief trying shutters.

"Longer than we have," he said.
