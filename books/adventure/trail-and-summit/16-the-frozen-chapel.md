## 16. The Frozen Chapel

The storm found them an hour after Tam said it would.

Ilse felt it first as a change in the light—a bruised, greenish tint spreading across the western peaks, the kind of sky that made her think of her father's last letter and the word *coerced* written in a hand that had shaken. Then the wind arrived, dropping the temperature so fast that Maret's breath turned to frost on her scarf before it could drift away. Edmund stood at the head of the column with his face tilted into it, reading it the way other men read letters, and when he turned back there was no argument in him at all.

"Shelter," he said. "Now."

Tam led them off the trail toward a fold in the mountainside that he clearly knew but did not explain, and Ilse followed, one hand on the chest's leather strap, the other on Doss's shoulder where the trail narrowed to a shelf of ice-glazed stone. Behind them the weather came down the valley like something poured. She did not look back at it. Looking back at storms was a habit for people who still believed they could outrun them.

The chapel door was half-buried in drift, and it took all four of them—Edmund, Doss, Tam, and Ilse herself, though she would remember afterward how Edmund had tried to wave her aside—to dig it free. The wood beneath the snow was black with age but sound, and when they hauled it open on hinges that screamed like living things, a breath of dead air rolled out, cold and dry and smelling of stone dust and old tallow and something else, something faint and sweet that Ilse recognized before she could name it: incense. Kestrian temple incense, juniper and myrrh, the blend the archivists of her childhood called *the smell of Sunday* even though none of them had ever been permitted a Sunday.

She stood in the doorway with snow crusting her eyelashes and wept without making a sound.

"It's a chapel," Tam said, puzzled, from behind her. "There are dozens of these in the high country. Herders use them for lambing sometimes."

"They wouldn't know what they're using," Ilse said. Her voice came out rough. She cleared her throat and stepped inside.

* * *

The interior was small—one vaulted room, its ceiling lost in shadow, its floor swept by forty years of wind-driven grit that had come in through a broken window high on the eastern wall. Someone had stacked firewood against the north wall long ago; it was damp but not rotten, and Doss was already at work with flint and the stub of a candle he carried wrapped in oilcloth. Maret went straight to the walls.

"Ilse." The girl's voice had gone strange. "Ilse, come look."

Ilse came.

The frescoes covered every wall that still held plaster. They were dimmed by grime and smoke and time, but the colors survived underneath—deep lapis blues, a red like embers, gold leaf that caught Doss's small flame and threw it back in fragments. And the figures. Processions of figures, painted with the flat, formal grace of the old Kestrian style, moving around the room in a procession that began at the door and ended—

Ended at the coronation.

Ilse walked the room slowly, the way she had once walked the archive halls of Vellmarin, cataloguing. Here: the mountain road, pilgrims ascending, their faces individual, alive, a woman carrying a lamb, a boy with a drum. Here: the river in flood, and men building a bridge, and above them a crowned figure pointing to the far shore. Here: the hall of records itself, rendered with an archivist's loving precision—shelves, scrolls, scribes bent over their desks, and in the corner, almost hidden, a small painted figure holding a chest.

"The coronation of Aldric the Second," she said quietly. "Or a copy of it. This is the composition from the Great Temple at Kestria City. I've seen engravings. But the engravings were made from descriptions. Nobody thought the original survived."

"Nobody thought any of this survived," Edmund said. He had stopped near the eastern wall, where the plaster had fallen away in a great ragged patch, exposing the raw stone beneath. His voice was carefully level, and Ilse had learned enough about him in three weeks on the trail to hear what the levelness cost him. "Forty years. Right under the conquerors' maps."

"People hide things in high places," Tam said. "That's what high places are for."

Maret had found a candle stub somewhere and was holding it up to the lowest register of the fresco, where the paint survived best. "Look at the faces. They signed them—look, here, little marks under each figure. Names. These are real people, aren't they? Not just—symbols."

"Portraits," Ilse said. "Coronation frescoes always included the witnesses. The court, the clergy, the guild masters. It was a legal document as much as a painting. That's why—" She stopped.

Because she had seen the face.

It was on the western wall, in the row of court officials nearest the throne, and it was painted larger than the others, with more care, more detail—the artist had clearly liked this face, or been paid to like it. A man in chancellor's robes, heavy-jawed, with pale eyes and a mouth that even in fresco, even flattened by the old formal style, managed to look like a mouth that had never once told the truth when a lie would serve. Under his feet, in the old script, ran his name and title.

Ilse read it twice. Three times. Her lips moved without sound.

Chancellor Evret Sarn.

"What is it?" Edmund had crossed to her side. "You've gone white as the mortar."

"You know this face?" she managed.

"No." He studied it. "Should I?"

"My father kept a wall of portraits in our house in Vellmarin. Portraits of the men who sold Kestria." Her own voice sounded distant to her, as if someone else were speaking through the cold. "He called it his gallery of traitors. He said an archivist should know the faces of the men who burned the archive. There were eleven of them. Eleven signatures on the Annexation Compact." She raised her hand and touched the painted jaw, the painted lie of a mouth. "This is the twelfth. The one who wasn't caught. The one who opened the palace gates from inside and was rewarded with land titles and a seat on the first occupation council. Chancellor Evret Sarn."

Doss looked up from the fire he was coaxing. "Never heard of him."

"Most people haven't. That was the point. The eleven who signed were executed or exiled—martyrs, eventually, even to the people who hated them. Martyrs make bad collaborators. But Sarn—" Ilse heard her own breath shake. "Sarn was smart. Sarn stayed. Sarn helped write the new laws, the ones that dissolved Kestrian legal personhood. Every registry sealed, every inheritance voided, every name struck. He built the machinery of erasure with his own hands, and then he retired to his estates and died in his bed at seventy-one, honored, wealthy, and *forgotten*, which was the only punishment anyone ever gave him."

Edmund was very still. "And?"

"And his family kept everything." Ilse turned to face him, and the words came out of her like something being pulled. "The Sarn estates became the Sarn holdings, and the Sarn holdings became the Sarn Trust, and the Sarn Trust is—do you understand what I'm telling you? The Sarn Trust is the single largest holder of former Kestrian land in existence. Every valley, every mine claim, every grazing right that was stripped from us in the annexation flowed through Sarn's signature into his descendants' hands. And his great-grandson—"

She stopped. Maret had risen from the wall, candle still burning, watching her with wide eyes.

"—his great-grandson," Ilse finished, "is Chief Justice Aldous Sarn. Presiding officer of the Restitution Tribunal. The man before whom our case will be heard. The man whose court will decide whether this chest restores us—or dissolves into ash and a footnote."

The fire popped. Wind pressed against the chapel walls and slid away, moaning.

"That can't be legal," Maret said finally. "Him judging a case about—about his own family's theft."

"It's perfectly legal," Ilse said. "That's what I'm trying to tell you. He wrote the rules. His ancestor wrote the rules. For forty years, every law governing who counts as a person has passed through Sarn hands. We aren't fighting a corrupt judge, Edmund. We're fighting a *system*, and the system has a face, and the face is on that wall, and it's smiling."

* * *

They made camp in the nave because there was nowhere else. Doss got a proper fire going in a ring of stones some herder had left, and Tam melted snow for tea, and Maret sat cross-legged before the coronation wall with her father's primer open on her knees, copying the names beneath the painted figures into her notebook by candlelight, her lips moving, her cold-chapped face lit gold.

Ilse should have slept. She had the watch after middle-night, and Edmund had ordered rest on everyone with the flat authority that had stopped surprising her sometime back on the glacier. Instead she knelt before the western wall with the locket open in her palm—Edmund's mother's face looking up out of the miniature, young and grave and holding a bundled infant—and compared it, portrait to portrait, across forty years of paint.

Lady Maerin Vane, née Callas. Lady-in-waiting to Queen Sable the Last. Painted here in the second register, smaller than the chancellor, standing just behind the throne with her hands folded, her face rendered with the same quiet gravity the miniature preserved. Ilse had walked past her three times that evening without recognizing her. Now she could not stop seeing her: the queen's lady, the cartographer's wife, the woman who had carried a royal line out of a burning country inside her own body.

Behind her, floorboards creaked. Edmund lowered himself onto the bench beside her—not close, never close, but near—and followed her gaze up to the painted court.

"She's there," he said. It wasn't a question.

"Second register. Behind the throne."

He was silent a while. The fire cracked. Somewhere in the roof beams, wind found a gap and sang one thin note.

"My mother never spoke of Kestria," he said at last. "Not once, my whole childhood. I thought—I don't know what I thought. That she was ashamed, maybe. Or that it hurt too much." His thumb moved over the locket's rim, an unconscious gesture Ilse had watched a hundred times on the trail and only now understood. "She used to sing, though. When she thought I was asleep. Songs in a language I didn't know. I asked her once what they were and she said, *old work songs, nothing.*" He exhaled through his nose, almost a laugh. "Nothing. She was the last lady of a dead court singing lullabies over a prince, and she called it nothing."

"Protection," Ilse said softly. "For both of you. Silence was the only armor she could give you."

"I know." He closed the locket. "Knowing doesn't make it less lonely."

She wanted to say something to that—something archivist-shaped, something useful—but what came instead was the truth: "My father sang too. Same songs. He'd deny it if you asked." And Edmund looked at her then, really looked, and for a moment the twelve years of measured distance between them thinned to almost nothing, two children of the erased sitting in a chapel their enemies had failed to find.

Then Maret gasped.

* * *

The girl was on her feet at the eastern wall, notebook forgotten, both hands pressed to her mouth. "Ilse. Ilse, come *look*. Under the fallen plaster—there's more painting behind it. Where the wall broke."

Ilse crossed the room. Behind the ragged patch of missing plaster, in the recess where the older wall surface survived, protected from smoke and light, another scene waited in colors so fresh they seemed painted yesterday. Smaller figures. A night scene, done in deep blues and blacks: the palace gates, open. Torches. Soldiers in the livery Ilse knew from occupation-era engravings pouring through the gate like water through a broken dam. And in the foreground, holding the torch that lit the way, a heavy-jawed man in chancellor's robes, his pale eyes catching the painted flame.

Beneath it, in the old script, a single line. Maret read it aloud, haltingly, translating as she went, her voice growing smaller with each word:

"'On the night of the ninth of Frostmonth, the gates were opened by the hand of—'" She looked up. "It doesn't say Sarn. It says—someone else. A name I don't recognize. 'By the hand of Royal Cartographer Corvus Vane, who drew the invaders' road through the mountains, in exchange for—'" She stopped.

"In exchange for what?" Edmund's voice came from directly behind Ilse. Very quiet. Very level.

Maret's eyes went to Ilse, pleading. Ilse took the notebook gently from the girl's hands and read the rest herself, because someone had to, because her father had taught her that an archivist reads the record even when the record is a knife:

"*In exchange for safe passage for his wife and unborn child.*"

The silence that followed lasted longer than any silence Ilse had ever heard. The fire burned. The wind sang its thin note in the rafters. Edmund Vane stood behind her without moving, and when she finally turned, his face was the face of a man reading his own death warrant in a language he'd only just learned.

"My father," he said.

"Edmund—"

"He opened the gates." Each word placed like a stone. "He drew their road. He let them in. And they gave him—" His hand rose halfway toward the fresco and stopped. "A mapmaker. The royal cartographer. The one man in the whole kingdom who knew every pass, every crossing, every secret road into the heart of Kestria. And he sold the map."

"To save your mother," Ilse said. "To save you."

"You don't know that."

"It's written on the wall, Edmund."

"Then the wall is wrong!" The word cracked off the vaulted ceiling, and Maret flinched, and Doss half-rose from the fire, and Edmund stood in the frozen chapel with his fists at his sides and forty years of painted truth staring down at him, and Ilse saw that he was shaking. "You didn't see him. You didn't grow up with the stories—*the traitor Vane, the cartographer who sold the mountains.* Every Kestrian child learns that name before they learn their own. My mother took that name to her grave rather than tell me what it meant, and now you're telling me the chest I dragged out of a river, the chest I've been bleeding for across half the Serpent Range—it isn't my inheritance at all. It's my father's *confession.*"

"Or his apology," Ilse said.

He stared at her.

"Think like an archivist for one minute," she said, keeping her voice low and steady the way she did with frightened petitioners in the archive queues. "Who commissioned this chapel? Who paid for frescoes of the coronation in a hidden valley, and then hid a confession behind the plaster? Someone who wanted the truth preserved but not displayed. Someone who couldn't confess openly and couldn't bear to leave it unwritten. Edmund—your father survived the annexation. He lived, he married, he had you, and at some point he came back to these mountains and he *paid painters* to bury his crime under a layer of plaster where only God and accident would find it. That's not a boast. That's a man who couldn't live with himself and couldn't undo it either."

Edmund turned away from her. Stood before the night scene, the open gates, the torchlit soldiers, the name beneath them that the whole world had cursed and he alone had loved.

When he finally spoke, his voice was scraped hollow. "The tribunal. Sarn's tribunal. If the registry proves the royal line survived—if it proves *I'm*—then the first question any honest judge asks is how the heir escaped at all. And the answer will be standing on that wall in paint." He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "The chief justice's grandfather took bribes from my father. The evidence cuts both ways, Ilse. It always cuts both ways."

"Then we make sure it cuts deepest where it should." Ilse picked up the chest, set it between them, and rested her hand on its wet-swollen lid—the lid she had chased across a decade and half a mountain range. "Tomorrow we climb. Whatever your father was, whatever Sarn's blood bought and built—the people in these paintings are real, their names are real, and the registry in this chest is real. We carry all of it. Even the parts that burn."

Edmund looked at the chest a long moment. Then, without a word, he took his bedroll to the far end of the nave and lay down facing the wall, below the fallen plaster, below the open gates, and did not speak again that night.

Ilse kept the watch until dawn, the locket in one hand and her father's final letter burning a hole in the map's margin, and she understood, watching the fire die, that the truth she had promised Edmund in Ferrow was no longer a gift she could hand him gently.

It was going to have to be pried open, like the chest itself.

And she did not know yet which of them would survive what was inside.
