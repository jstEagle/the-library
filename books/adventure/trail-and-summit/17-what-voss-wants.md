## 17. What Voss Wants

The warden's map was wrong.

Voss knew it the way he knew weather before it came—a pressure behind the eyes, a taste of iron at the back of the throat. He spread the man's chart across the folding table in the command tent and ran one gloved finger along the route it claimed the fugitives had taken: south fork of the Ironbridge gorge, then east through the scree fields toward the mid refuge. Neat ink. Confident lines. And wrong in three places that mattered.

"You're certain," he said. It wasn't a question. He never asked questions he didn't already own the answers to.

Range Warden Pellik stood at parade rest in the corner of the tent, sweating despite the cold, his warden's badge polished to a shine that said *I have never done anything worth hiding* and his eyes said otherwise. "Certain as I can be, sir. My boy saw them at the refuge two nights past. Five of them. The big ferryman was trading for rope."

"Your boy." Voss did not look up from the map. "And your boy sees well."

"He's got eyes like a hawk, sir."

"Then he has better eyes than you, because this route—" Voss tapped the eastern line twice, hard enough to crease the paper, "—is a lie someone told you, or a mistake someone made, and either way it will get my men killed on ground they don't understand. The scree field east of the refuge funnels into a box canyon. There is no way out of it except the way in. If they'd gone east, they'd be standing in a dead end waiting for us. They are not stupid people."

Pellik's throat worked. "Then—then where, sir?"

Voss straightened. Outside the tent, the wind moved through the pines with that long dry whisper that meant the season was turning ahead of schedule, and somewhere below them, down in the green dark of the valley, the river they'd crossed a week ago was running lower every day. The passes would close early this year. Everyone in these mountains could feel it—the marmots, the shepherds, the old women who read clouds the way priests read scripture. Everyone except, apparently, the men who worked for him.

"They went north," Voss said. "Toward the high country. Toward the chapel ruins. Because there's an old Kestrian road up there that isn't on any map your bureau ever printed, and because the woman leading them is an archivist who has spent ten years learning things your bureau paid to forget." He rolled the map closed with a snap. "You'll be paid what we agreed. You'll also forget my face by morning, or I'll come back and make the forgetting easier for you."

Pellik left quickly, which was the correct response to almost everything Voss said.

*

He sat alone after that, which was how he preferred it, though preference had nothing to do with why.

The tent lamp guttered. He trimmed the wick, poured himself the last of the brandy from the flask his employer's quartermaster had issued him—good stuff, wasted on him, since drink had never once in thirty years managed to put out whatever burned underneath—and took out the letter.

It lay where he always kept it, inside the breastplate pocket over his heart, because his employer had a sense of humor and Voss had learned not to flinch at it. The seal was broken. The words were short. He had read them eleven times now, and each time they said the same thing, and each time they said it more loudly.

*Voss. The window closes with the snows. If the chest reaches the tribunal, everything my family built in those provinces reverts, and I do not intend to explain bankruptcy to my children. Burn it. Burn whoever carries it if you must. I am told you were thorough in the annexation years; be thorough now. —A.*

*I am told you were thorough in the annexation years.*

Voss set the letter down on the table and looked at it for a long time.

Thorough. Yes. That was one word for it.

He got up and stepped outside into the cold, because the tent had become too small to hold both him and the letter, and stood looking north up the black shoulder of the range where the fugitives were climbing toward a ruin he had passed once, forty years ago, in the back of a government wagon, too young and too numb to know what he was looking at.

"I want to tell you something," he said to the mountain, quietly, in a language he hadn't spoken aloud in thirty-eight years. The words came out rough, half-rusted, but they came. "Since no one else is listening. Since the only other person who'd understand it is up there carrying a chest full of paper, and I'm paid to make sure she never opens it in front of the right people."

The wind answered. The wind always answered; it just never agreed.

"I was born in Kestria."

There. Said. To nobody. To pine trees and starlight and the frozen shoulder of a mountain that had been renamed twice since he was born beneath it.

"My mother's name was Sera Vossek. Not Voss—Vossek. She was a dyer, in a town called Merenhold, which does not exist anymore under that name, and neither does the street she lived on, and neither does the well she drew water from every morning of her life until the morning the wagons came." He turned the flask in his hands. "I was six years old. Do you know what they told us? They told us we were being relocated for our protection. They put us in wagons with the windows painted over so we couldn't see the road, because seeing the road meant knowing where home was, and knowing where home was meant wanting to go back, and wanting to go back was the disease they were curing."

He laughed, briefly, without any humor in it at all.

"The schools were very good. That's the thing no one tells you about erasure—it's not violence, mostly. Violence is the cheap part, the loud part, the part they let you remember so you'll hate them instead of mourning. The real work was quieter. New names. New language. Punishment for the old tongue—not beatings, at first, just... subtraction. You speak Kestrian, you eat alone. You speak Kestrian again, you stand in the yard all night. By the third winter you stop noticing you've stopped speaking it. By the fifth winter you dream in their language and wake up ashamed of something you can't name, and the shame is the last piece of the old country still alive in you, and then one day even the shame goes quiet, and that's when they're finished. That's when you're theirs."

His breath hung in the air, silvering, dissolving.

"Sera Vossek died in the second year. Fever, they said. I was seven. I don't remember her face anymore—I want to be honest about that, mountain, since honesty is the one luxury I've never been able to afford anywhere else. I remember her hands. I remember that she smelled of dye and woodsmoke. I remember that she sang while she worked, and I cannot remember the song, and I have spent forty years listening to every song in every valley of this range hoping one of them would be it, and none of them ever is, and I keep listening anyway. That is what hope is. I learned that from her without learning anything else."

He drank. The brandy burned going down and warmed nothing.

"So when your employer writes to me, A., and says *be thorough*, I want you to understand what thorough means coming from me. Thorough means I have spent my whole life being the instrument of the thing that unmade me. Thorough means I have burned villages' worth of records—baptism rolls, land claims, marriage lines, the whole paper skeleton of a nation—and felt nothing, because feeling nothing was the only way to survive doing it. Thorough means that when they gave me this job—find the chest, burn the chest—I said yes before he finished the sentence, because saying yes is the only prayer I know."

He looked north again. Somewhere up there, past the black teeth of the ridgeline, five people were climbing through the dark with a sealed chest lashed to a sledge, and inside that chest was a registry, and registries were lists, and lists were the most dangerous objects ever invented, because a list said *you existed*, and existence was the one thing they had never been able to take cleanly.

"And here is the part I have never said out loud, not to him, not to anyone, not even to myself at three in the morning when the walls are thin." His voice dropped. "At first it was money. I won't pretend otherwise. A man who owns nothing learns to price everything. But somewhere between the postmaster's village and the Ironbridge—somewhere around the time I watched that ferryman cut his own bridge rather than let us cross, watched him choose those strangers over his own safety, watched him carry the weight of it on his face afterward like a man wearing a stone around his neck—somewhere in there, the money stopped mattering. And I kept moving anyway. Faster. Harder. Spending my men like coin I didn't have."

"Why?" he asked the mountain. "Why does a man chase a thing he no longer wants to catch?"

The wind gusted, flattening the pines, and in its voice—for a moment, only a moment—he heard singing. Dye and woodsmoke. Then gone.

"Because hope hurts," he said. "That's the answer. That's the whole ugly truth of it, and I'll thank you to keep it between us. Hope hurts worse than anything they ever did to us in those schools. Hunger ends. Cold ends. Even grief, given enough years, settles into something you can carry. But hope—hope is a wound that stays open on purpose. Every year, some fool Kestrian descendant in some valley stands up at some festival and sings the old songs with the new words, and every year I have to decide whether to report it, and every year I don't, and every year I hate myself for the mercy and hate them for needing it. Because as long as Kestria stays dead—as long as it stays a story, a song, a shame—then what happened to me is just weather. Just something that happened. But if that chest opens in front of a tribunal—if the registry is real, if the names are real, if the legal machinery of the world turns its great slow head and says *these people existed and exist still*—then it wasn't weather. It was a crime. A crime with my name in the margins of it. A crime I spent my life completing."

He pressed his palm flat against the breastplate pocket, against the letter, against the place where a locket might once have hung if lockets survived painted windows and fever winters.

"If they win, I lose everything. Not the money—I lost the right to care about money years ago. I lose the story. The story where I was a survivor of misfortune instead of a servant of a crime. The story where the boy in the wagon with the painted windows grew up to be anything other than the wagon driver's whip. That's what I want burned, mountain. Not paper. Paper is nothing. I want the version of me that could have been different burned, before I have to look at it."

He stood there a long time after that, letting the cold do its patient work, until his hands stopped shaking and his face stopped doing whatever it had been doing, and he was Voss again—thorough, reliable, empty as a swept floor.

Then he went back inside, struck the map open on the table, and began to work.

North, then. Past the chapel ruins. The archivist would know the old roads; his employer's maps wouldn't. Which meant he needed someone who knew the high country the way she did—and there was exactly one such person within three days' ride, and the man owed Voss a favor from the annexation years that Voss had never called in, precisely because calling it in would mean admitting he remembered the annexation years.

He took out fresh paper. He wrote four lines. He sealed them with wax and no crest, because crests could be traced and ghosts could not.

Then he called for his lieutenant, and Halder came in stamping snow off his boots, young and eager and loyal in the way of men who had never been given a reason not to be, and Voss looked at him and thought, with a tiredness that went all the way down to the six-year-old at the bottom of him: *You would burn the world if I asked. That's what they made you for. That's what they made me for.*

"Halder," he said. "Split the company. Take six men and the fast horses up the north trail tonight. I want the chapel ruins watched by dawn, and I want the party taken alive if it can be done—but Halder." He held the man's eyes. "If it comes to the chest, the chest burns first. Before prisoners. Before wounded. Before anything. Am I understood?"

"Understood, sir." Halder grinned, hungry for it. "Burn first, ask never."

"Get out of my tent."

When the boy was gone, Voss sat alone with the lamp and the letter and the silence, and found that his hands had started shaking again, and that for the first time in forty years he could not tell whether the shaking was fear—or the beginning of something that might, in a kinder life, have been called hope.

Up north, faint through the canvas, the wind was singing again.

He did not listen.
