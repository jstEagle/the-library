## 18. Tam's Shortcut

I had walked the glacier twice in my life, both times with my grandmother, and both times she had made me stop at the lip of the ice and say a word to it. Not a prayer exactly—she was careful about prayers, after what prayers had cost our people—but a greeting. *Old one, we cross your back. Forgive our weight.* I had thought it foolish when I was nine. I did not think it foolish now.

The others stood behind me where the valley trail gave out against the icefall, and they were looking at me the way people look at a bridge before they trust it. Edmund Vane had his arms crossed and his eyes on the glacier's surface, reading it the way Ilse read her documents—not the words but the truth underneath them. Maret was shivering and pretending not to be. Doss spat into the moraine gravel and said nothing, which from Doss was a kind of courtesy. And Ilse Brenner watched me with that patient, terrible attention of hers, as if I were a page she hadn't finished.

"The high trail adds three days," I said. "Three days we don't have. My grandmother showed me this route when I was young. The glacier cuts straight under the Horn and comes out above the second refuge. We make up two days, maybe more if the weather holds."

"And if it doesn't hold?" Edmund asked.

"Then we're on ice instead of rock, and ice is honest."

He almost smiled at that. Almost. "Ice is honest," he repeated. "That's the first true thing anyone's said to me on this mountain."

* * *

My grandmother had taught me the glacier the way she taught everything: by making me look longer than I wanted to. *The ice tells you what it is,* she'd say, *but only if you ask slowly.* She pointed out the difference between the white of old snow, which bridges crevasses and lies about them, and the blue-grey of wind-scoured ice, which shows you its bones. She taught me to listen—to stop walking every hundred paces and just stand, ears open, because the mountain speaks first and collapses second. A groan is a conversation. A crack like a whip is a goodbye.

She also taught me that no one crosses the Old One alone, and no one crosses it twice the same way. Glaciers move. The route my feet remembered from six years ago was not the route that existed now. I knew this. I knew it the way you know a thing and then set it down somewhere and forget where.

We roped up at dawn—Edmund insisted, and for once nobody argued. He put himself at the front, then me, then Maret, then Ilse, then Doss at the rear with the chest lashed tight to his pack frame, wrapped in oilcloth against the meltwater. "You've done this before?" Doss asked me, nodding at the icefall ahead, where the glacier poured down from the col in broken slabs like a river deciding whether to be water or stone.

"Twice," I said.

"With who?"

"My grandmother."

Doss looked at Edmund. Edmund looked at me. "Twice," Edmund said, "with your grandmother." It wasn't a question. It wasn't quite an accusation either. It was the sound a man makes when he decides to trust someone and wants the decision witnessed.

"She crossed it forty times," I said. "She taught me everything she knew."

"Forty times," Edmund said softly. "Then she knew it never teaches you the same lesson twice. Lead on."

* * *

The first hour was good. Better than good—it was the kind of morning that makes you believe the world owes you something. The sun came up red on the Horn and turned gold, and the glacier stretched ahead of us, smooth and pale, a road laid down by God and forgotten. Maret found her rhythm. Ilse hummed something under her breath, an old song, half-swallowed—I caught the shape of it and my chest hurt, because it was a valley song, a song my grandmother sang while spinning wool, and hearing it from a stranger's mouth was like seeing your own face on someone else.

I stopped us every hundred paces, the way I'd been taught. Listen. The ice groaned beneath us, long and low, a sound like a door opening in a house with no doors. Maret flinched the first time. By the tenth time she was stopping on her own, tilting her head, waiting for the groan to finish before she took another step. She learned fast. I noticed that about her early and liked her for it.

"Your grandmother," Ilse said, falling in beside me during one of the listening stops. "She kept the name?"

"In her mouth only," I said. "Out loud, in the valley, we were shepherds. High-valley people. Nothing else. She sang the songs when the men were drunk or asleep and taught me the letters with a stick in the ash of the hearth. But if a warden came up the trail, the stick went in the fire and the songs went underground." I shrugged, though it wasn't a shrug-kind of thing. "That's how we survived. Small. Quiet. Unwritten."

Ilse was quiet for a moment. Then: "Unwritten isn't the same as erased."

"No," I said. "It's the same as waiting."

* * *

The trouble began past the great medial moraine, where two arms of the glacier met and ground each other into a grey ribbon of stone. On my grandmother's route—and in my memory, which is a different country with worse weather—we bore left there, along the flank of the Horn, where the ice ran clean and fast to the upper basin.

But the glacier had been drinking. I could see it even before I understood it: the snowline had crept higher than it should be in the dry season's last weeks, and meltwater ran everywhere, in silver threads and sudden gushes, and where water goes, ice goes hollow. The left flank, my grandmother's clean road, had a different sound now. When I stopped and listened, the groan came back wrong—too wet, too loose, a gurgle under the note like breath through a drowned throat.

I told myself it was nothing. I told myself glaciers change and routes change and a man who leads people onto ice has to commit, because hesitation on a rope team kills faster than error. So I led us left.

For an hour it held. The ice was white here, old snow over the blue, and we moved quickly, too quickly, boots squeaking, the rope slack between us. Maret laughed once at something Doss said. The sun climbed warm on our backs. I remember thinking: *two days made up by noon. Grandmother would be proud.*

Then Edmund raised his fist and we all stopped, because you stop when the point man stops, that's the law of the rope, and his fist had gone up like iron.

He didn't say anything at first. He crouched, pulled off a glove, and pressed his bare palm flat against the snow. Then he did the strangest thing—he lay down. Full length, cheek nearly to the surface, ear against the ice, one arm outstretched along our line of travel. He stayed that way so long that Maret whispered, "Is he asleep?" and Doss said, "If he is, we bury him here and tell no one," and even Ilse smiled.

Then Edmund got up, and he wasn't smiling.

"How deep is the snow here?" he asked me.

"I don't—" I started, and he cut across me, gentle but absolute, the way you'd talk to someone standing on a trapdoor.

"Bridging. All of it. This whole bench is bridging." He pointed with his axe back the way we'd come, then forward, tracing a line I couldn't see. "Snow fell heavy two winters back, then a dry summer, then this melt. The water's undercut the whole flank. What we're standing on is a roof." He looked at me, and his voice dropped so only I could hear it. "Tam. How many times did you say you'd crossed this?"

"Twice."

"When?"

"Six years ago. In spring. Hard freeze."

He nodded slowly. "Spring hard freeze, this flank is a highway. Now it's a lie with a pretty face." He straightened and turned to the others. "We turn around. Back to the moraine, then right—the long way around the seracs. It costs us half a day."

"Half a day!" The word came out of me before I could catch it, hot and stupid. "Half a day is the whole reason we're on the ice at all. The storm's coming early—you said it yourself, everyone said it, the elder said it—we don't have half a day to give away because you got scared lying in the snow like a—"

"TAM." Doss's voice, flat and hard.

But Edmund didn't flare. That was the worst part. He just looked at me with those grey eyes that had seen twelve years of empty miles, and he said, quietly, "Come stand where I stood."

"What?"

"Come stand where I stood, and listen." He walked me ten paces forward, off the rope line, testing each step with his axe before committing his weight, and stopped at a place that looked identical to every other place on the white bench. "Put your ear down."

I hesitated. He waited.

I knelt. I lay down. I put my ear to the snow.

And I heard it.

Under the silence, under the wind's thin whistle, there was water. Not a thread of it—a river of it, moving, chuckling, patient, somewhere below my body, below the beautiful white roof of it, and the ice above the water made a sound I had never heard my grandmother's glacier make: a slow, structural sigh, the sound of something holding itself up out of habit.

I lay there until my ear went numb. Then I got up, and I couldn't look at him.

"Back to the moraine," I said. My voice came out small. "Right flank. Your call."

"It was always going to be somebody's call," Edmund said. "Today it was yours, and you made it with what you knew. Tomorrow you'll make it with what you know now. That's the whole trade." He paused, then added, almost too quietly for the others to hear: "Your grandmother would have lain down too. Forty crossings—that's forty chances to learn that the mountain doesn't care whose grandson you are."

* * *

The right flank was everything the left had pretended not to be: naked blue ice, wind-stripped, every crevasse showing its dark mouth honestly, no bridges, no lies. Slow going. We front-pointed up short walls of ice; we stepped over cracks wide enough to swallow a mule. Twice the rope came tight and meant something, and both times it was Maret slipping on glassy slope, and both times Doss hauled her back with one arm and a curse and a laugh, all three at once.

The ice talked to us all afternoon. Groans rolled up the glacier from below, deep and tectonic, and cracks snapped above us near the seracs, sharp as rifle fire, and between the two the Old One muttered continuously, like an old man turning in his sleep. Maret stopped flinching. By late afternoon she was naming the sounds. "Door," she'd say, at a groan. "Whip," at a crack. Once, at a sound none of us could classify—a long descending moan that seemed to come from under our own feet—she said, very seriously, "Grief," and nobody laughed, because it was exactly right.

We camped that night on a moraine island, rock in a sea of ice, and I took first watch because I didn't want to sleep. Shame keeps strange hours.

Edmund came and sat with me near midnight without being asked. He handed me tea, scalding and awful, and we drank it looking at the glacier moonlighting under the stars, blue-black and vast.

"You hate me right now," he said.

"No," I said. And found it was true. "I hate me right now. You were just the mirror."

"Mm." He blew on his tea. "Six years ago, spring crossing, with your grandmother. Did she take the left flank?"

"Yes."

"Was there a river under it then?"

I thought about it. Really thought, going back down the years to a boy's ears against the ice, a woman's hand on his shoulder saying *listen longer*. "No," I said finally. "There was a river under it, but it was frozen. She tapped the ice with her staff the whole way across. Listening for hollow spots. I forgot that part." I stared into the cup. "I forgot the staff."

"Six years is a long time to carry a route in your head," Edmund said. "Routes are like people. They change while you're not looking." He drained his tea and stood. "Get some sleep. You'll take point again tomorrow."

I looked up at him. "After today? After I nearly walked us onto a roof over a river?"

"That's exactly why," he said. "You'll listen better than any of us now. That's how the mountain pays tuition." He walked back toward the tents, then stopped, silhouetted against the starfield, and said the thing I have kept ever since, the thing I will say to my own grandchildren if the valley gives me grandchildren:

"My father drew maps of this range. Best cartographer Kestria ever had, maybe. And do you know what I remember? Not the maps. Him coming home from survey trips and sitting silent for a day, letting the mountains drain out of him slow. The mountain gets into you, Tam. Today it got into you a little deeper. That's not failure. That's the fee. Pay it and keep walking."

* * *

We made the upper basin by noon the next day, and I took point the whole way, and I listened the whole way, and I heard things I would have walked over blind a week ago. At one fork I stopped the team for a full five minutes over a patch of snow that looked perfect, and when Edmund came up beside me and lay down and listened, he looked back at me and nodded once, and we went around, and twenty minutes later, from the safe side of a rib of blue ice, we watched the snow patch sag, sigh, and drop away into darkness with a sound like a held breath released.

Maret grabbed my arm. Hard. "You heard that," she said. "Before. You heard it before."

"I heard something," I said.

"You *knew*." Her eyes were enormous. "Teach me. Teach me to hear it."

So I did. Right there on the ice, with the Old One groaning its grief-sounds under our boots, I put her ear against the glacier and told her what to listen for, the way my grandmother told me, the way Edmund had reminded me without knowing he was doing it. Ilse watched us and wrote something in her little book. Doss pretended not to watch and watched anyway.

And that was when it curdled over, the resentment—just turned, like milk, like weather, into something else. Not respect exactly. Respect is a cold word. It was more like recognition: the feeling of standing next to someone and realizing you are on the same side of the same ice.

We came off the glacier at dusk, onto rock and scree and the smell of living stone, two days' journey eaten in one and a half. Below us, far down, the second refuge showed its smoke. And beyond it, beyond the pass we still had to win, the sky in the northwest had gone the color of a bruise, and the wind had changed its mind, and it was carrying, faint and unmistakable, the smell of snow.

Edmund stood at the edge of the ice looking at that sky for a long time. Then he said, to all of us, in a voice with no argument left in it:

"We rest tonight. Full night, hot food, nobody stands watch alone. Because tomorrow we climb out of the mountain's patience—and I think it's about to run out."
