## 19. The Crevasse

The glacier did not forgive you for looking away.

Edmund had learned that lesson at nineteen, on the northern icefields, from a guide who lost two fingers to frostbite and never once lost a client. *The mountain is not your enemy,* the old man had said, flexing his mitten-stump like a man showing off a scar won honestly. *It's just indifferent. Indifference kills faster than malice, boy, because it gives you nothing to fight.* Edmund had been thinking about that guide all morning, because Tam's shortcut had delivered them onto a river of ice that looked smooth as poured milk and was honeycombed underneath like old bread.

They moved roped now—had been roped since noon, when Edmund read the first pressure ridge and called a halt to redistribute the rope. Doss in front with Tam, then Maret, then Ilse with the chest lashed tight against her spine like a second ribcage, then Edmund on anchor. Four lengths of line between them, no slack, the kind of spacing that made conversation impossible and trust mandatory.

Maret hated it. She'd hated it for three hours.

"This is stupid," she said, loud enough to carry down the rope. "I can see where I'm putting my feet."

"You can see where you're *hoping* to put your feet," Edmund said. "Different thing up here."

"The ice is white. It's all white. How do you even know where—"

"Because it talks." He kept his eyes moving as he spoke, reading the surface the way he'd once read rivers: the faint blue veins where meltwater had drilled down and refrozen, the shallow saucers where snow bridges were sagging, the long gray seams of moraine dust that marked where the ice had ground itself apart and healed badly. "Snow over a crevasse sounds hollow. Ice over solid ground sounds dead. You learn to hear the difference through your boots."

"You're making that up."

"I'm making up maybe ten percent of it."

He heard her snort—a small, defiant sound—and felt something loosen in his chest that had been tight for days. Since the glacier fork, since he'd overridden Tam's route and dragged them left across a field of blue seracs rather than right along the crevasse-laced shelf, there had been a wall between him and the boy-guide. Tam walked now with his jaw set and his eyes anywhere but on Edmund, doing everything correctly and nothing gladly. But Maret—Maret had decided somewhere on the ice that Edmund's stubbornness was entertainment, and she'd appointed herself its audience.

That was when the mountain stopped being indifferent.

It wasn't a crack he heard. It was an absence—the sudden wrongness of her voice cutting off mid-syllable, and then the rope going strange in his hands. Not taut. Not slack. *Singing*—a low thrumming vibration, the sound of eleven stone falling into a hole with forty feet of line paying out behind her.

"MARET!"

He dropped to self-arrest before his mind finished the word, axe spike driven into the ice, boots braced, whole body becoming a brake. The rope burned through his grip even through the leather. Above him Doss had done the same; he could see the ferryman's big frame folded around his axe like a man praying to it. The line snapped taut between them and held, and held, and then—

Stopped.

Silence. Wind. His own pulse hammering in his ears so hard it sounded like footsteps.

"Maret!" Nothing. "MARET! Answer me!"

A cough. Then, thin and echoing and absolutely furious: "*What?*"

Relief hit him like a wave breaking over a seawall, and he let himself have exactly one second of it—one breath where his forehead dropped to the ice—before he started working.

"Ilse. Anchor. Now. Wrap the rope three times around your axe shaft, sit back, don't move no matter what you hear. Doss—you're my anchor point. Tam—" The boy was standing frozen, staring at the place where Maret had been, which was now a ragged oval of darkness in what had looked like flawless snow. "Tam. Look at me."

The boy's eyes came around slowly.

"You didn't lead us wrong. This happens on every glacier in the world. What matters is the next ten minutes. Can you give me those?"

Something shifted in Tam's face—not forgiveness, not yet, but the recognition of a task, and tasks were a language every mountain kid spoke. He nodded once.

"Then here's how it is." Edmund crawled forward on his belly, distributing his weight, until he could peer over the lip without adding his body to the load on the bridge. The crevasse opened beneath him like the throat of something ancient. Blue light fell sixty feet and died against walls sculpted by centuries of meltwater—fluted, scalloped, beautiful in the way that dangerous things are beautiful, the way a storm cell at sunset is beautiful. And at the bottom of the visible dark, small and impossibly alive, Maret hung in her harness at the end of the stretched rope, turning slowly above a deeper blackness that went down past knowing.

"I'm okay," she called up, and her voice wobbled only on the last word. "I'm—I'm okay. It's really blue down here. Did you know it's blue?"

"I know." His own voice came out steadier than he felt. "Listen to me. Don't swing. Don't try to climb. The rope's holding you and the rope is the only thing in this world that loves you right now. Can you feel your toes?"

A pause. "Yes."

"Fingers?"

"Yes."

"Good girl. We're coming to get you."

*We.* He heard himself say it and didn't take it back.

Getting him down took twenty minutes that felt like twenty years. They built the anchor off two buried axes and Doss's shoulders, backed by Ilse sitting flat with the full weight of the party's survival coiled around her fists. Edmund rigged a descending loop, checked it twice, checked it a third time because the fourth time would be too late, and then swung his legs over the edge of the world.

"Uncle Edmund's going down," Maret called up, delirious or joking or both, and nobody laughed, and nobody corrected her either.

The cold came up to meet him like water. That was the thing people who'd never been inside a crevasse didn't understand—it wasn't air down there, it was ocean, still and dense and lit from within, every sound arriving twice: once direct and once as an echo off ice polished smoother than any cathedral stone. He descended hand over hand, boots against the wall, and the blue deepened around him by degrees, and he thought, absurdly, of his father's maps—the cross-sections the old cartographer used to draw of the high glaciers, shaded in washes of cerulean and cobalt, and how young Edmund had assumed the colors were artistic license.

His father had been down here. Or someone like him had, taking the measurements, drawing the truth. The thought arrived uninvited and he put it away. There would be time for ghosts later. There was always time for ghosts; they were patient.

"Maret." She hung eight feet below him, swaying, her face tipped up—a smear of white in all that blue, lips already graying. "I've got you. I'm going to clip us together and then we're going to go up, and you're going to kick that wall when I say kick, and if you kick when I don't say kick I will leave you here for the ice worms."

"There's no such thing as ice worms."

"How do you know? Has anyone ever come back to check?" He reached her, clipped in, cinched the sling under her arms. Her hands found his jacket and gripped with a strength that surprised him—desperation refined into pure mechanical force. "On three. One—"

The mountain exhaled.

It wasn't loud. That was the horror of it. A sound like a long sigh, a settling, the glacier shifting some internal argument—and the lip of the crevasse, the edge they'd anchored to, the edge Doss and Ilse and Tam were braced behind, *let go*. Snow and ice blocks poured past Edmund in a silent white avalanche, and the rope above them went slack, then jerked, then went slack again, and they dropped six feet before the anchors above caught on something deeper, something truer, and the fall ended with a crack that rolled around the blue throat like thunder in a bottle.

Dust filled the air—glacier flour, fine as talcum, silvering the light. Through it Edmund could hear shouting from above, distorted, frantic. Doss's voice. Ilse's. Tam's, high and cracking.

"Doss!" he bellowed upward. "Report!"

A pause. Then the ferryman's voice, raw: "Anchor's holding! Lost half the lip! Edmund, I can't lower you anything, the edge is—everything's rotten, it'll all come down—"

"Then we climb." He turned Maret's face toward him. Dust powdered her lashes. Her eyes were enormous and steady, and he understood suddenly that she'd been more frightened of his fear than of the fall. Kids were like that. They could survive almost anything except watching an adult come apart. "You hear me? We climb out. You first, on my rope, and I'm below you the whole way, and if you fall I fall, so don't make me fall, I'm too old for it."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't a joke. Kick when I say."

They climbed. It took forever and it took no time at all. He fed her footholds one at a time—*left boot, hip-height seam, now right, now rest, breathe*—while the wall shed occasional kisses of ice past their shoulders and the rope above sang with the strain of three people hauling from an edge that crumbled a little more with every pull. Twice the footing under Edmund vanished entirely and he hung on arm strength alone, fifty-one years old, shoulder screaming, staring up through the dust at a circle of white sky that seemed to recede as he climbed toward it.

And then Maret's hand broke the lip, and Doss's fist closed around her wrist, and she came over the edge like a fish landed, and thirty seconds later Edmund followed, rolling onto the surface of the glacier and lying there gasping while the four of them scrambled backward from the collapsing rim until the ground finally, grudgingly, stopped trying to swallow them.

For a long moment nobody spoke. The wind moved. Somewhere deep in the ice, the glacier groaned and settled, indifferent again.

Maret sat up. Dust covered her head to toe; her braid had half-collapsed; there was a scrape on her chin she hadn't noticed yet. She looked at each of them in turn—Doss heaving for breath, Ilse white-faced with her knuckles still locked around the rope, Tam staring at the ruined lip with the expression of a boy recalculating every certainty he owned.

Then she looked at Edmund, and grinned, and said: "Told you I could see where I was putting my feet."

Edmund laughed. It came out of him sideways, cracked and involuntary, the first real laugh in longer than he could measure, and once it started he couldn't stop it, and then Doss started, and then even Ilse, helplessly, tears freezing in the corners of her eyes, until all five of them sat on the back of a living glacier laughing at the dark hole that had tried to eat them and failed.

They made camp early, in the lee of a serac the size of a chapel, because Edmund refused to spend another hour on the ice with tired people. They melted snow, forced food and sweet tea into Maret until color came fully back to her face, and checked her fingers and toes twice for frost-nip. Only her left little toe showed white, and Ilse rewarmed it against her own stomach under layers of wool, murmuring Kestrian words that might have been curses or prayers.

Night came down violet and absolute. The temperature plunged. In the shelter of the tent, packed shoulder to shoulder for warmth, the day finally caught up with them. Tam slept like the dead, exhausted by fear and relief in equal measure. Doss's breathing went slow and deep. Ilse lay with one hand resting, even in sleep, on the chest.

Edmund stayed awake, as he always did, running the watch.

"You're supposed to wake me for second shift." Maret's whisper came out of the dark beside him. She'd wedged herself against his side somewhere in the last hour, the way a cat claims territory by degrees, and he hadn't noticed until she spoke.

"You fell sixty feet today. Sleep."

"So did you, basically."

"I fell twenty feet. There's a difference. Ask any glacier."

She was quiet a moment. Outside, the wind combed the ice, and far off something boomed—a serac calving, the glacier shedding weight in its sleep.

"Edmund." His name, this time. No joke attached. "When I was down there. In the blue. I wasn't scared of dying."

"No?"

"I was scared of dying *before*—" She stopped. Started again, slower, feeling her way. "Before anybody knew me. Before I was somebody to somebody. I ran away from the mill town because nobody there would've noticed for a week. Maybe ever. And hanging on that rope, I kept thinking: if it breaks, the story just ends, and there's no one whose story I was in. Except—" Her voice caught, and she pushed through it the way she pushed through everything, headfirst and furious. "Except yours. I was in yours. You were down there getting me, and I thought, whatever happens, I was in somebody's story, and that's not nothing."

Edmund stared at the tent roof. Twelve years he had guarded his solitude like a fortress, and this dust-covered runaway had walked through the gate carrying nothing but a talent for old scripts and an unbearable honesty.

"Maret," he said. "You're not in my story."

Her breath hitched—an actual flinch in the dark.

"You're in *mine*," he said. "I've been in yours since Ferrow. I just haven't had the nerve to say it."

Silence. Then, very quietly, testing the word the way she'd test thin ice, one careful step at a time:

"Okay. Uncle Edmund."

The word landed in his chest and stayed there, glowing like a coal. He didn't answer. He didn't trust his voice. Instead he shifted the blanket higher over her shoulder, and she burrowed down, and within minutes her breathing joined the others', slow and trusting and alive.

Edmund kept the watch alone, as he always did. But it felt different tonight. Less like guarding a fortress. More like keeping a fire.

Across the tent, Ilse stirred in her sleep, and her hand slid from the chest to the map case beneath her head—the map with his father's handwriting crowding its margins. Edmund had seen her studying those margins for days, tracing lines of cipher with a fingertip, frowning. Tonight, in the low light of the storm lantern, he saw her eyes open. Saw her look at the margin. Saw her go very still, the stillness of a woman who has just recognized her own father's hand in a message meant to stay hidden.

She looked up. Met his eyes across the sleeping bodies of their family.

"Edmund," she whispered. "There's something in the map. Something my father wrote. I think—" She swallowed. "I think it's about yours."
