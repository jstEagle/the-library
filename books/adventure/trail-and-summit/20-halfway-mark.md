## 20. Halfway Mark

The mid refuge of the Serpent Range had been built by people who expected to be forgotten.

Ilse understood that the moment she stepped through its door—a door of blackened larch set into a shoulder of granite, so low that even Maret had to duck. Inside: one long room, a hearth black with forty years of soot, bunks carved directly into the stone walls like tombs, and above the lintel, faint as a watermark, a carved inscription in old Kestrian script that made her breath catch and her eyes sting before she could stop them.

*We kept the fire. Keep it after us.*

She stood there with snow melting off her shoulders and puddling around her boots, and she read it three times, because reading Kestrian in the open air—reading it anywhere outside the sealed drawers of her archive—still felt like a crime that no one had gotten around to punishing yet.

"You're doing the thing," Maret said behind her. "Where you go quiet and your face does the sad-math."

"I'm not doing the sad-math."

"Your eyebrows are doing division."

Ilse almost laughed. Almost. She shrugged out of her pack instead and set it on the nearest bunk, and heard the others come in behind her—Doss grumbling about his knees, Tam already moving to the hearth with the instinct of someone raised around fires, Edmund last, silent, setting the chest down against the wall with the care of a man laying down something sleeping.

They had made better time than she'd dared hope. Four days from the shepherd's valley, up through the switchback forests and across the scoured high meadows where the wind came off the icefields with a sound like tearing linen. The dry season was failing early—everyone felt it now, even Maret, who had never seen a mountain winter—and the refuge was a gift they hadn't planned on. Stone walls. A roof. One night where the mountain would let them stop counting miles.

One night, Ilse thought. She should have known better than to believe any night would be only one night.

* * *

By full dark the storm arrived—not the storm itself but its outriders, gusts that hit the refuge like fists and made the whole structure hum. Doss fed the fire until the room glowed amber. Tam produced dried meat and a hard cheese from the elder's stores, and Maret ate like a wolf and then fell asleep mid-sentence, sitting up, her head tipping against Doss's shoulder. He looked down at her with an expression he would have denied under torture, and did not move.

Edmund sat apart, as he always did now. Since the chapel. Since the frescoes. He'd barely spoken in two days, and when he spoke at all it was in the flat, economical voice of a man giving directions to strangers. Ilse watched him turn his father's map over in his hands for the hundredth time—the map that had started everything, drawn in Aldric Vane's precise cartographer's hand, the Serpent Range rendered in lines so confident they seemed less like drawings than testimony.

He didn't know she was watching. Or he did, and let her, which was worse.

Her father's letter was hidden in that map.

She had known it for eleven years and never been able to prove it. Brenner the elder archivist, dying too young with his mind still sharp and his hands shaking, had told her: *If you ever find Vane's field map of the Serpent Range—the original, not the copies—look at the western margin. I hid what I couldn't say aloud. The cipher is mine; you'll know it.* And then he had died, and she had spent a decade chasing rumors of a chest, a burned hut, a blacklisted guide, and a map that everyone swore had gone into the river with the rest of Aldric Vane's life.

It hadn't gone into the river. It had gone into the chest. And the chest had found its way to the son.

*You'll know it,* her father had said. She knew it. She had been reading it since she was nine years old, tracing it in the dust of the archive floor while other children played at soldiers: the alternating script, the number-pairs, the way every third character leaned left like a man flinching. Her father's cipher. His private language, invented during the annexation years, when writing anything true in plain Kestrian could get a village deported.

She waited until the fire had burned low and Edmund had taken first watch at the narrow window, his back to the room, silhouette cut by the blue glow of snowlight. Then she took the map from his pack.

She told herself it was permission she'd earn later. Archivists were good at that particular lie; it was practically a professional credential.

* * *

The margin was nearly empty to the eye—just a scatter of what looked like surveyor's marks, elevation figures, a few stray notations in trade-tongue. But held near the firelight at an angle, the paper gave up its secret the way old paper always did: the cipher had been written in a slightly different ink, one that aged darker, and now it rose off the page like footprints in frost.

Ilse spread the map flat on her bunk, took out her notebook, and began to translate.

The first lines were easy, almost tender. Her father's voice came through the cipher the way handwriting comes through a signature—instantly, unmistakably, a man she could hear.

*If this is being read, then the map reached someone who deserved it, which is more than I managed.*

Her throat tightened. She kept working.

*I am writing this in the spring of the year they erased us, in the house of a friend whose name I will not write even here, because names are how they hunt. Aldric Vane is dead to the world—they have struck him from every register, and his son with him, and I helped strike the blow, and I will carry that until the ground carries me.*

Ilse stopped.

The fire popped. Wind pressed against the shutters like something large and patient trying the door.

She read the line again. *I helped strike the blow.* It could mean many things. Cipher was generous that way; terror made men vague. She told herself it meant he'd been forced to surrender records, or identify holdings, or—

*Let me set it down plainly, because whatever courage I have left is small and must not be wasted on elegance.*

Her hands had begun to shake. She pressed them flat against the map and read on.

*In the last winter of Kestria, I was taken to a room with a table and two officers, and on the table were papers naming every member of the royal household who had gone to ground. My name was not among them. My daughter's name—Ilse, four years old, hiding in the cellar of the archive with her mother—was.*

*They offered a trade. Not my life. Hers. The queen's lady-in-waiting, Sera Vane, was carrying a child, and the child was the last blood of the royal line, and they wanted the route she would take out of the country. They wanted the name of the guide. They wanted the pass.*

*They told me: give us the route, or we take the girl.*

*And I want whoever reads this to understand that I sat in that chair and I thought about my daughter's face and I gave them the route in less time than it takes to boil water. I did not hesitate. That is the part I cannot forgive, that I did not even hesitate.*

*But hear the rest, because the rest is the only coin I have.*

*Aldric Vane came to me that same night. He had guessed—he was a man who read terrain the way priests read scripture, and he had guessed what I'd done before I could speak. And he did not kill me, though he had every right and every skill. Instead he said: they will follow the route I give them, because I will give them a different one. Let them think their informer served them. Let them chase a ghost over the eastern passes while Sera goes west, and while the child lives.*

*So I informed twice. Once for them, once against them. And the second informing worked—the child lived, Sera lived, and I have spent forty years telling myself that makes the first informing a kind of heroism. It does not. A man who saves a life by selling another man's country has not bought forgiveness. He has only bought time to beg for it.*

*Vane went east with the false route and was caught, and blacklisted, and broken, and he never once said my name. Not to the officers, not to the tribunal that ruined him, not to anyone. He carried my betrayal in his mouth for twelve years and swallowed it every day, so that his son would grow up without a traitor's name to hate.*

*Except the boy grew up hating his father instead. I have watched from a distance, the way you watch weather. Edmund Vane believes his father abandoned him. The truth is that his father abandoned everything except him.*

*The chest holds the registry. The registry holds the proof of every stolen child, every erased family, every name they struck. If it reaches the tribunal, Kestria breathes again. If it burns, we stay ghosts forever, and I will have informed on my own people twice, and both times successfully.*

*I do not ask the boy's forgiveness. I ask only that if he reads this, he understands: his father was not the coward in the story. I was. And the bravest thing Aldric Vane ever did was love his son quietly enough to be hated for it.*

*Keep the fire. Keep it after us.*

*—Tomas Brenner, archivist of a country that no longer exists*

* * *

Ilse did not know how long she sat there. The candle had burned down to a thumb's width. The wind had changed its argument with the shutters. At some point she became aware that her face was wet and that she had folded the notebook closed over her hand hard enough to leave marks.

Her father.

Not the villain of the frescoes—that face belonged to another lineage entirely, some ancestor of the chief justice, rot painted in holy colors. No. The betrayer she had spent her life hunting, the shadow she had built her whole existence around escaping, the nameless informer she had sworn to expose before the tribunal with documents and witnesses and the full weight of restored law—

Was the man who taught her to read. Who had died holding her hand. Who had hidden the truth in a cipher designed for her alone, because he could confess to no one else and live.

*Coerced to save Edmund's pregnant mother.* No—to save *her*. To save Ilse. And Edmund's father had absorbed the blow, silently, for twelve years, so that a stranger's daughter could grow up unmarked.

She thought of Edmund at the chapel, staring up at the painted face of the traitor, asking her in a voice stripped bare: *Who was he? I need a name, Ilse. I've needed it since I was sixteen.*

And she had said, *I'll find him. I swear it.*

She had found him. He was her father. And the truth he sought was worse than any lie he'd ever been told—because the lie had given him a father to hate, and the truth took even that away, and left him nothing but a man who had loved him enough to be hated wrongly, and a debt owed to the daughter of the man who broke him.

She looked across the room. Edmund stood at the window, motionless, watching the snow come down through the blue dark. The chest sat against the wall beside him. The map lay open on her bunk between them like a wound.

She could burn the letter. She was the only one who could read it; she could close the notebook, feed the page to the stove, and let him keep his grief the shape it was. Archivists preserved truth. Mothers—she was not his mother, but she was suddenly, terribly aware of how much she had come to feel like family—mothers sometimes lied to protect.

But he had said once, on the scree below the first pass, in one of the last honest sentences he'd spoken: *I don't care what it costs. I'd rather have the worst truth than one more kind lie. Kind lies are what got my father killed.*

She crossed the room.

"Edmund."

He turned. In the firelight his face was all planes and exhaustion, and beneath the exhaustion something that had been waiting eleven days for exactly this moment and dreaded it.

"There's something in the map," she said. "Something my father hid. I've translated it." She held the notebook out. "I'm not going to tell you what it says. You should read it yourself. Alone."

He looked at the notebook for a long moment. Then at her. Whatever he saw in her face made him go very still.

"It's bad," he said. Not a question.

"It's worse than bad," Ilse said. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry, Edmund. Read it, and then—" Her voice failed and rebuilt itself. "Then hate me if you have to. I'll understand."

"I could never—" He stopped. Took the notebook. "What is it?"

"The name," she said. "Of the man who betrayed Kestria. The one you asked me for at the chapel." She made herself hold his eyes. "I found him."

* * *

He read it by the window, where the snowlight mixed with the last of the fire, standing the way soldiers stand at attention for executions. Ilse retreated to her bunk and pretended to sleep and watched him anyway, because she was weak, and because if this was the last night he trusted her she wanted to witness it fully.

He read slowly. Twice. She saw him reach the middle section—the confession, the trade, the boiling-water sentence—and saw his hand tighten on the notebook until the cover creaked. She saw him reach the part about the false route, the double informing, and stop breathing for so long that she counted.

She saw him reach the final lines. *His father was not the coward in the story. I was.*

And then Edmund Vane did the thing that frightened her more than anything the mountain had thrown at them.

Nothing.

He closed the notebook. Set it on the sill. Straightened the map's curling corner with two fingers, gently, the way you'd touch a dead man's face. He looked out the window at the falling snow for a long time—at the range rising black against the stars, at the pass they still had to cross, at whatever country existed inside him now that the last wall of his childhood had come down.

Then he picked up his bedroll, moved it to the farthest bunk from hers, lay down facing the wall, and said nothing.

No rage. No questions. No curse against her father or his own. Just silence, total and load-bearing, the silence of a man who has walked out onto ice and felt it hold—for now—and is listening for the crack.

Maret stirred in her sleep and murmured something. Doss snored. The fire settled into embers.

Ilse lay awake until the grey beginning of dawn, staring at the carved words above the lintel. *We kept the fire. Keep it after us.*

Outside, the snow kept falling, and somewhere below them in the dark, patient as winter, Voss was climbing.
