## 21. Silence on the Scree

Edmund had learned, over twelve years of living alone above the tree line, that grief had a sound. Not the wailing of it, not the raw first hours when a man might tear his shirt and howl at the sky like something animal. The sound of grief was quieter than that. It was the sound of a boot on scree when the man wearing it had stopped listening to his own footsteps.

He heard it now, and did not care.

The party moved down the long gray slope below the mid refuge, a spill of loose stone fanning out from the shoulder of the mountain like the mountain itself had shrugged them off. Ilse walked second, one hand resting on the strap of the chest where it rode against Doss's back. Tam ranged ahead, reading the slope the way his grandmother had taught him. Maret brought up the rear, and Doss—Doss walked beside Edmund, close enough to catch him if the scree shifted, far enough not to speak, because Doss had learned that too, in the days since the refuge. That Edmund had gone somewhere the words couldn't follow.

He had read the letter three nights ago. His father's hand in the margin of the map, decoded by Ilse's patience and a candle stub and the old Kestrian cipher that Maret could read like a village girl reading a market list. *They had her. They said the child would be born in a cell. I gave them the road through the Veren Pass and the names of the queen's guard. Forgive me. I chose you before I chose my country.*

*I chose you before I chose my country.*

And Kestria had burned. The queen had died in a courtyard. The names in the registry had become numbers, the numbers had become ash, and a boy had grown up fatherless in a border town, believing his father had simply been a coward with a cartographer's soul and no spine at all. That had been bearable. Cowards were common. Cowards were weather.

But this—this was a father who had sold a nation to keep one life breathing, and then had not stayed to raise the life he'd bought. Had run. Had left the boy to inherit the silence.

Edmund had said nothing. He had folded the letter along its old creases and put it back in the map's margin pocket and walked out into the cold and had not, in three days, said one true word to anyone.

He was done with true words. He had a plan instead, and the plan was simple as a blade: carry the chest to the pass. Give it to Ilse. Let her take it down the far side to the tribunal, let the lawyers and the clerks and the descendants do whatever people did with nations. And then walk. Not back the way they'd come—Doss would look for him there, Maret would cry, Ilse would send Tam. He would walk north, where the maps went blank, where a blacklisted man with no name and no nation could be nobody with real conviction. He had been nobody for twelve years and he had been *good* at it. The last three weeks had been the aberration. The fever of it. The chest, the girl, the ferryman, the child who called him Uncle in her sleep.

He would go back to being a mile. A mile between himself and everyone.

The scree hissed under his boots. Somewhere above, a raven said one flat word and flapped off toward the glacier.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a fallen spine of rock, half buried in stone, its weathered face turned from the wind. Edmund set the perimeter without being asked, checked the anchors for the tarp, counted the food, did everything the way he'd done it for twelve years—precisely, competently, and from behind a door that no one had a key to.

Ilse tried first. She sat beside him at the fire with the locket in her lap, the one Maret had pulled from the flames, and she talked about her father, about the archive in Ferrow, about the way a document could hold a person the way amber held a fly. Edmund nodded at the right intervals. He answered in single sentences. When she finally ran out of patience and said his name—his *whole* name, the Kestrian name from the birth record, the one he'd only heard spoken aloud twice in his life—he stood, said he'd take first watch, and walked to the edge of the firelight.

Tam took second watch without being asked. Doss took third. Nobody argued about it, and that, more than anything, told Edmund how bad it had gotten. A party that had stopped arguing was a party that had stopped believing in each other, and he had built that silence with his own two hands, stone by stone, the same way he'd built the walls of his hut.

Maret came to him at the edge of the light on the second night.

He heard her boots first—she still hadn't learned to walk quietly, and he'd stopped reminding her, because the reminders had been a kind of talking and he'd stopped doing that too. She dropped down onto the rock beside him, wrapped her arms around her knees, and looked out at the dark valley below them, where the last light of some far settlement burned like a dropped coal.

"You haven't eaten," she said.

"I ate."

"You picked at the pot like a bird and gave the rest to Tam. That's not eating. That's performing eating." She was fifteen and she had learned to read a dead language in three weeks and she had learned, apparently, to read him faster. "Ilse's worried."

"Ilse should worry about the pass. The dry season's dying. We have maybe six days of clear weather, five if the wind shifts."

"That's not what I—"

"Then sleep, Maret."

She was quiet for a while. The wind came up the valley and moved her hair and she let it, staring at the coal of light below. When she spoke again her voice was different. Smaller. Careful, the way you're careful walking on ice.

"When I fell in the crevasse," she said, "I wasn't scared of dying. Did I tell you that?"

"You told me."

"I was scared of going back. To before. To the road, and the begging, and the year I slept in the ferry shed, and nobody knowing my name was a name." She picked at a loose thread on her sleeve. "And then you came down into the blue dark on a rope. And I thought—*he came down. He always comes down.* Even when he's got no reason. Even when it costs him. He comes down."

"Maret—"

"You called me *kid* this morning," she said. "You used to call me Maret. Or *trouble*, when I earned it." She turned and looked at him, and in the firelight her eyes were too old for her face, the way they'd been too old since the first day in Ferrow. "You don't get to do that. You don't get to shrink back down to *kid* and *sleep, Maret* and counting miles. Not after the crevasse. That's not how it works. You were in the blue dark with me. You're in it now. That's just— that's just where you live now."

Edmund said nothing. The wind filled the silence, and he let it.

"I know about the letter," she said.

His head came around. "Ilse—"

"She didn't tell me. I read the cipher, remember? I'm the one who decoded the margin. You think I didn't read the rest?" Maret's jaw was set, and there was something fierce in her face now, something that had nothing of the child in it. "Your father sold Kestria to save your mother. To save *you*. And you've been walking around for three days like a man doing arithmetic, trying to figure out how much a nation weighs against a baby, and I'm fifteen and I can tell you the answer. It weighs nothing. It weighs *nothing*, Edmund, because it wasn't a choice between Kestria and you. It was a choice they *made him* think he had. That's what they do. That's what the annexation *was*. They make people choose their own children and then they call it betrayal for forty years."

"You don't know what—"

"I know what it's like to be the reason somebody ruins themselves," she said, and her voice cracked on it, and she pushed through. "I know what it's like to be the debt. I was the debt my mother paid when she left me in Ferrow. And you know what I decided? I decided I'd rather be somebody's terrible reason than nobody's anything. At least the debt gets *carried*."

"Maret. Go to sleep."

"No."

"*Go to sleep.*"

"No," she said again, and stood up, and stood there in the wind with her fists at her sides, small and immovable as a survey marker. "I'm not done. You're planning to leave. At the pass. Don't bother lying, you've got the face of a man doing sums about the far side of a border, I've watched you do it for two days. And I want you to know that I know, and that I think it's the stupidest thing you've ever done, stupider than the crevasse, stupider than walking into the checkpoint under your own blacklisted name, and that when you do it, I'm going to be standing at the pass watching you go, and I'm going to remember that you came down into the blue dark for me, and I'm going to hate you *politely* for the rest of my life, which is a long time, because I intend to live a very long time out of spite."

"Maret."

"And one more thing." Her voice dropped, and the fierceness in it wavered, and for one second she was just a fifteen-year-old girl on a cold mountain with wet eyes. "You don't get to be a mile again."

The words went into him like a blade going into a joint—clean, deep, exactly where it would hurt the most and do the least visible damage.

"You don't get to be a mile," she said again, quieter. "You were a mile for twelve years and it made you into a man who talks to mountains instead of people. Then a chest floated down a river and ruined everything, and you got *us*, and you hated it, and then you loved it, and now you're going to give it back because your father was a coward and you've decided cowardice runs in the blood. Well, it doesn't. I would know. I've got cowards on both sides of mine, and look at me. Look at me, Edmund. I'm on a mountain. I've got a people. I'm learning a dead language so it won't be dead anymore. Cowardice doesn't run in blood. *Choosing* runs in blood. And you get to choose again. Every morning. That's the whole terrible gift of being alive."

She turned and walked back toward the fire, and her boots were loud on the scree, and he did not watch her go, because watching her go would have meant admitting she'd landed every blow.

He sat alone at the edge of the dark for a long time. The raven came back and said its one flat word. Below, the coal of light in the valley went out, or was covered, or was never there at all.

*You don't get to be a mile again.*

He took out the map, and unfolded it, and looked at his father's handwriting marking the route through the Serpent Range—the sure, beautiful hand of a man who had drawn a nation's every road and then sold one of them. And in the margin, in cipher, the confession. And beneath the confession, so faint Edmund had never noticed it before, not in the refuge, not in three days of silence, one more line of cipher, smaller than the rest, half worn away by forty years of folding.

He stared at it. He did not have Maret's gift. He could not read it.

But he could see that it was there, and that it was not the confession, and that his father had written something else—something last, something hidden even from the hidden—and that the only person in the world who could read it was asleep by the fire with her arms around her knees.

Edmund folded the map along its old creases, very carefully, and sat in the dark, and for the first time in three days, the silence in him cracked—not all the way, not enough to speak, but enough that when he finally rose and walked back toward the fire, his boots were quiet, and he stood at its edge a long moment, watching the sleepers, before he sat down among them instead of apart.

Maret, half asleep, opened one eye.

"You're still a mile," she mumbled. "But you're a mile *closer*."

"Go to sleep, trouble."

She smiled and was gone.

Edmund sat with his back against the stone and did not sleep. He watched the sky begin to gray in the east and thought about the faint cipher in the margin, and about the pass, six days out if the weather held, and about the plan he had made to vanish, which now sat in his chest like a swallowed stone that would not go down.

And then the wind shifted.

It came down off the high peaks an hour before dawn, and it was wrong—it was wet where it should have been dry, heavy where it should have been thin, and it carried a smell Edmund knew from his childhood and had hoped never to smell again on this side of the range. The smell of snow coming early. The dry season was not dying.

It was dead.

He stood, and woke them one by one, and when Ilse asked him what was wrong, he looked east, where the first cloud of the storm was building over the pass like a wall with no door in it, and said the first true words he'd spoken in three days:

"We're out of time. The storm's coming—and something else is coming with it. Get the chest ready. We move now."
