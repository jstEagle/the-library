## 22. Doss's Confession

Doss had been carrying the weight for twelve years, and like everything he'd ever carried—crates, drays, drowning men—it sat in his shoulders and his knees and the small of his back, a load that never shifted, never lightened, never let him stand straight.

He told himself it was the altitude. That was easier. The air up here in the high scree country was thin enough to make a strong man wheeze, and Doss was not a strong man anymore; he was a ferryman with bad hands and worse sleep, and he could blame the mountain for the way he woke at night with Edmund Vane's name caught in his throat like a fishbone.

But they were three days out from the mid refuge now, camped in the lee of a granite shoulder with the wind combing over the top of it in long gray strands, and there was no altitude low enough to explain what was eating him.

He watched Edmund across the fire.

Edmund hadn't spoken more than twenty words since the refuge. Since the letter. Doss didn't know what the letter said—he'd seen Ilse's face when she decrypted it, seen her go the color of old tallow, seen her fold the paper into her coat like she was hiding a wound—and he'd seen Edmund read it alone by the far wall, sitting very still, the way a man sits when the ground has opened under him and he hasn't decided yet whether to fall or climb out.

Since then Edmund led. He led mechanically, perfectly, the way a clock runs: distances measured, water counted, watches set, Maret's pace monitored, Ilse's cold hands wrapped around hot stones without comment. A machine made of competence. And behind the machine's eyes, nothing. A room swept clean.

Doss knew that room. He'd lived in one himself for twelve years.

*You don't get to be a mile again.* Maret had shouted it at him two nights ago, on the scree, her voice cracking across the stones, and Edmund had looked at her with an expression Doss would carry to his grave—not anger, not hurt, just a kind of polite blankness, as if she'd addressed him in a language he'd once known and deliberately forgotten.

Doss had turned away then. He'd busied himself with the rope coils. But the words had gone into him like a splinter working toward the heart, because Maret was right, and because Doss knew something none of them knew, something he'd carried since before any of them had found this trail, and every mile Edmund walked toward the pass was a mile Doss had sold him.

Twelve years ago. The Ferrow docks. Rain coming down like the sky meant it.

---

It had started, as these things always do, small. Doss had been drinking. Not the honest drinking of a working man at day's end but the other kind, the kind that starts when the work is gone, and Doss's work had been gone since the river authority revoked his license over a wreck that wasn't his fault—or maybe it was; he'd stopped being able to tell the story honestly even to himself. His wife had taken the children to her sister's in the lowlands. The ferry sat rotting at its moorings. And a man in a good coat had come down the dock through the rain and sat beside him on the bollard like they were old friends, and bought him a bottle, and asked easy questions.

Where does Vane keep his gear? When does he cross? Which route?

Doss had known even then who the man was. Everyone on the docks knew the blacklisters' men—the soft shoes, the dry coats, the questions shaped like conversation. Edmund Vane was blacklisted; everyone knew that too; the great cartographer who'd drawn half the border surveys and then done *something*, nobody knew exactly what, something that got his name struck from every register and his face posted at every checkpoint with a price attached. Information leading to capture. Not dead. Captured. The distinction had seemed important at the time.

Doss had told himself he wasn't selling anything. He was just talking. Just answering questions about a man who'd once pulled him out of the Whitefork current with a rope and his own two arms, a man who'd stood dripping on the bank afterward and said, *Any time, Doss. Any time you need it,* and meant it, and never once mentioned it again, not when Doss's license died, not when the creditors came, not once in nine years of friendship.

Just talking. In exchange for coin. Enough coin to buy back the ferry, or close to it.

He'd given them the route. The high trail above Ferrow Gap, the crossing window, the hut where Edmund would shelter the second night. And they'd gone up with six men and come down with Edmund in irons, and Doss had stood at his window in the dark and watched the lanterns descend the switchbacks and told himself he'd only talked, he hadn't touched anyone, ropes and irons were their work not his—

The ferry sank the following spring anyway. The money went where money goes. And the weight settled onto Doss's shoulders and never left, and every year on the anniversary he drank until he couldn't remember which of them had drowned first, Edmund's freedom or Doss's soul, and neither answer helped.

Then, last autumn, a flood had delivered a chest to Edmund's door, and Ilse Brenner had climbed into Doss's life asking after a guide, and Doss had sworn a life-debt to Edmund Vane years ago in a different lifetime, and somehow all of it had braided together into this: walking beside the man he'd sold, carrying his rope, sharing his fire, waiting for the confession to climb out of his chest and strangle him.

Tonight it climbed.

---

Maret slept first, curled against her pack with the locket under her hand—she'd fished it from the fire weeks ago and never surrendered it, and Edmund had never asked. Ilse sat awake, mending a strap, her eyes moving between Edmund and the dark. Tam was on watch, a slim silhouette against the star-scoured sky.

Edmund sat apart, as he'd sat apart for days, staring into the coals with his forearms on his knees.

Doss made himself stand. His knees cracked. Twelve years of weight, and now the moment, and no amount of carrying had prepared him for setting it down.

He crossed the camp and lowered himself onto the stone beside Edmund, close enough for the fire's warmth, far enough for honesty.

"Vane."

Edmund didn't look over. "Sleep, Doss. Long day tomorrow."

"I need to say a thing, and if I wait for tomorrow I'll find another tomorrow, and I've been finding tomorrows for twelve years." His voice came out lower than he intended, gravelled. "So you'll hear it now, and then you can do whatever you want with me."

That got Edmund's eyes. Just a flick, but a flick. The machine looked at him.

Doss stared into the fire because he couldn't stare at Edmund and speak at the same time; the words would drown.

"The night they took you. Twelve years back. The rain." He swallowed. "I told them your route."

The fire popped. Somewhere above, wind worried the granite with a sound like slow breathing. Doss heard Ilse's mending stop.

"The man in the good coat. Soft shoes. He sat next to me on the dock and bought me a bottle and I answered his questions, and I told myself it was nothing, just talk, and I took his coin, and I gave him the Gap crossing and your second-night hut." The words came faster now, tumbling, twelve years of them backed up behind a rotten gate. "They paid me thirty crowns, Vane. Thirty. For you. I couldn't even sell you dear."

Silence. The worst silence Doss had ever stood inside. He forced himself to go on, because stopping now would be mercy to himself and he deserved none.

"I watched the lanterns come down the mountain. I stood at my window and I watched them bring you down in irons and I did nothing. I spent the money. I lost the ferry anyway. And I have woken up every morning since with your name in my mouth, and I have never once—" His voice broke and he hated it, hated the wet heat in his eyes, hated the old man crying on a mountainside. "Never once said it out loud. So there it is. There's the whole ugly weight of it."

He dug into his shirt and pulled out the purse. It was not the original purse; the original coin was long spent and damned. But he'd saved these, crown by crown, over years, meaning someday—someday—to give them back, though he'd never let himself imagine the someday arriving. He set it on the stone between them. It clinked, absurdly loud.

"That's thirty crowns. It's not the same coin. Nothing's the same coin. But it's what I have."

Edmund looked at the purse. Then at Doss. His face was still the swept room, but something moved behind it, deep down, like ice shifting on a lake.

"You're telling me," Edmund said quietly, "that my exile had a price tag."

"Thirty crowns."

"And here I thought I was worth more." It wasn't a joke. It was the flattest thing Doss had ever heard a man say, and it frightened him more than rage would have.

"I'll go," Doss said. "In the morning. You don't have to say anything. I'll take the low route down and you'll not see me again, and I won't blame you, Vane, I'll carry it, I've had practice—" He was standing now, though he didn't remember rising, the old ferryman's instinct to step off his own deck before it burned. "I'm sorry. God help me, I'm sorry, and sorry is worth nothing, I know what it's worth, but it's all—"

"Doss."

He stopped.

"Sit down."

Doss sat. His legs simply quit holding him.

For a long moment Edmund said nothing. He picked up the purse, weighed it in his palm, and Doss braced for it to be thrown in his face, into the fire, off the mountain. Instead Edmund set it gently back on the stone between them, as if returning something fragile.

"Do you know what I read at the refuge?" Edmund said. His voice stayed level, but Doss heard the effort of level, the way you hear the strain in a rope before it parts. "My father's letter. My father—the man whose map brought us up this mountain—the man I've spent twelve years cursing for abandoning us—he was the informant, Doss. On Kestria. On the royal line. They coerced him. My mother was pregnant with me and they held her life over him and he gave up his king." A breath. "And then he ran, and left us anyway, and I built my whole life on hating him for it, and now I know he was a coward *and* a man trying to save his son, both at once, and I don't know what to do with that. I don't know how to hold a man who's both."

The fire settled. Doss realized his own breath was shaking.

"So understand me," Edmund went on, and now he did look at Doss, fully, and the swept room was not empty after all—it was full of wreckage, carefully arranged. "When you tell me you sold me for thirty crowns, and that you've carried it for twelve years, and that you're offering to walk into the lowlands alone rather than burden this company with yourself—I am looking at my father, Doss. I am looking at cowardice and love in the same body, and I am so tired of condemning men for being both."

"Vane—"

"I'm not finished." But there was no iron in it. "Ilse says the chest was never about blood. She said it in the storm, under the overhang, and I didn't believe her then. I'm starting to." He rubbed his face with both hands, and for a moment he looked not like the machine, not like the great cartographer, but like a tired man of forty-odd years sitting on a cold rock. "You pulled Maret out of the crevasse field. You dug Tam out of the avalanche with your bare hands until they bled. You cut the Ironbridge with a man on it and you haven't slept right since—you think I haven't noticed? I notice things, Doss. It's the family trade." A ghost of something crossed his face, not quite a smile. "A man is not his worst hour. If he were, I'd have condemned my father, and myself, and probably you, and we'd be three ghosts walking this range instead of five people."

"Four people and a boy," Doss managed.

"Maret would dispute the ordering." Now it was almost a smile, and it hurt to look at.

Doss's vision blurred. He wiped his nose with the back of his hand, graceless, an old man's gesture. "I don't deserve—"

"No," Edmund agreed, simply. "You don't. Neither did I. Neither did my father. Grace isn't a wage, Doss. Nobody ever earned it. That's rather the point." He nudged the purse back toward Doss with one finger. "Keep your crowns. Buy Maret a proper pair of boots in the valley. Her feet are ruined and she complains about it in Kestrian script, which she thinks I can't read."

Across the fire, Ilse had resumed her mending, but slowly, and her eyes were bright. Tam had not moved from his watch, but his head was tilted, listening. And Maret—Maret's breathing had changed, the too-even rhythm of a child pretending to sleep, and Doss understood that she'd heard every word, and that she would carry this night too, folded away somewhere, the way children carry the moments that teach them what forgiveness looks like.

Doss picked up the purse. His hands shook. He put it away inside his shirt, against his chest, where the weight had lived for twelve years—and found, wonderingly, that the weight had not moved out. It had only changed shape. It was no longer a secret now. It was a debt he intended to pay in boots and schoolhouses and every mile left in his legs.

"Thank you," he said, and it was inadequate, and it was everything.

"Don't thank me." Edmund fed a stick to the coals. "Thank the mountain. It seems determined to make an honest man of everyone who walks it." He glanced sideways, and there—briefly, unmistakably—was the man from the Whitefork bank, the one who'd said *any time, Doss* and meant it. "Get some sleep. Long day tomorrow."

Doss rose. His knees cracked. He took three steps toward his bedroll and then turned back, because there was one thing more, one thing that had been lodged behind the confession all night.

"Vane. The letter. Your father." He hesitated. "You don't have to answer. But are you going to be all right?"

Edmund considered the question with the seriousness he gave to routes and weather. "No," he said at last. "But I'm going to keep walking. That's not the same thing, but it'll do for now."

Doss nodded and lay down in the dark, and for the first time in twelve years, when sleep came, Edmund Vane's name was not caught in his throat.

He woke near dawn to Ilse's hand on his shoulder and her voice tight and low: the sky to the west had gone the wrong color overnight, a bruised yellow-green along the peaks, and the wind had swung north, and Edmund was already standing at the edge of camp reading it with an expression Doss had learned to fear.

The dry season wasn't ending early anymore.

It had ended.
