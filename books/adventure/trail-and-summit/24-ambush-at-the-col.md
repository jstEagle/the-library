## 24. Ambush at the Col

Voss had learned mountains the way orphans learn anything: by watching, by hunger, by never asking twice. The Serpent Range did not forgive ignorance, and it did not reward courage either. It rewarded patience. It rewarded the man who understood that stone and snow were not obstacles but materials—things to be shaped, if you were ruthless enough, into weapons.

He crouched at the lip of the high col with his field glasses cupped against the wind, and below him, small as insects on a white tablecloth, the party moved along the valley floor in their careful single file. Six of them now. He counted them out of habit, the way he'd once counted the bunks in the transit dormitory: Voss. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six.

The archivist. The ferryman. The girl. The boy from the shepherd's valley. And Vane, walking point like a man who'd been born on this ground, which perhaps he had.

Voss lowered the glasses. His breath froze in the wool of his collar. Behind him, four men waited in the lee of a rock rib—what remained of nine. Ironbridge had cost him two to the river and one to a fall he preferred not to think about, and the lieutenant's face when Doss cut the bridge still surfaced sometimes in the hour before dawn, uninvited, like a debt collector. But four was enough for what remained. Four was more than enough.

"Kessler." He didn't raise his voice. On a mountain you learned to speak low; shouting was for people who trusted the air to carry only what they wanted carried. "The col. Take three men and hold it. Nothing crosses."

Kessler was thirty years old and had the flat competence of a man who had never been loved by anyone whose opinion mattered. He nodded once and began distributing rope and pitons without being told. Good. Voss had no use for soldiers who needed speeches.

He turned back to the slope below the col—the long, open traverse the party would have to cross to reach it, a white apron of wind-loaded snow lying over a steepening base of rock. He had read that slope for an hour that morning while the others slept. He had read the way the wind combed the surface into sastrugi running the wrong direction, the way the cornice along the ridgeline above leaned fat and greedy over the apron, the way the snow sat too deep and too quiet, holding its breath.

Anyone could see it was a slope. Only certain people saw what it was *for*.

His employer's last message had come up the wire at Ferrow, then relayed by mule and bribe to the warden's station, then carried in Voss's own pocket for six days: *The chest does not reach the tribunal. By any means. You are reminded of your contract's terms.* The words had a smell to them now, a sweetness going off, like fruit kept past its season. There had been a time when those words meant money, and money meant a life with walls around it, heated, sealed against the world. Now they meant only that a man in a warm office very far away was afraid of a box full of paper.

Voss found he no longer knew why that made him angry. He filed the question away with the others—the lieutenant's face on the bridge, the registry page he had not yet let himself read—and climbed down toward the killing ground.

* * *

They came up the apron at midday, because of course they did. Vane would have known better than to cross loaded avalanche terrain at the hour when the sun softened the surface layers, but the dry season was dying early this year and everyone on that mountain knew it, and desperate people lose their patience in measurable increments. Voss watched them come through the glasses and gave each of them a name in his head, the old habit from the schools, where the instructors taught that a target was easier to manage once you'd reduced him to a function. The archivist: the document. The ferryman: the muscle. The girl: the liability. The boy: the guide. Vane: the variable.

And the mountain: the instrument.

Kessler's team was in position above the cornice by the time the party reached the midpoint of the apron, tiny dark figures roped together, moving with the hunched economy of people who had learned what weather costs. Voss watched through the glasses and felt nothing he could name, which was itself a kind of feeling, and one he distrusted.

He raised one hand. Kessler, watching for it through his own glass, would see it plain against the sky.

Below, the party stopped. Vane stood still for a long moment, head tipped back, reading the slope the way Voss read ledgers. Then Vane looked up—not at the col, not at the route ahead, but directly at the ridgeline where the cornice hung. Directly, as far as Voss could tell through the glass, at him.

For three seconds neither of them moved. Across half a mile of thin cold air, two men who had never spoken looked at each other over a slope primed to kill, and Voss understood that Vane understood, and that understanding changed nothing, because there was nowhere else to go. The valley walls funneled everything to the col. That was the whole design. That had always been the whole design of every place Voss had ever held.

He brought his hand down.

* * *

Later, Voss would reconstruct the avalanche the way he reconstructed everything: as sequence, as cause, as a chain of decisions that could have been broken at any link and wasn't. Kessler's team hit the cornice at its root, three charges of blasting powder tamped into the wind-slab where it joined the ridge—a technique from the annexation wars, from the artillery manuals Voss had studied in the conquerors' academy alongside boys who spoke his mother's language with a conqueror's accent. The cornice went with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat. Then the slab above the apron broke loose, and the whole white face of the mountain stood up and began to run.

From the col it was almost beautiful. That was the obscenity of it, and Voss had learned long ago not to look away from obscenities, because looking away was how the men who signed the orders stayed clean. The fracture line raced across the slope faster than a horse. The party scattered—Vane hauling the archivist left toward a rock rib, the ferryman shoving the girl downhill and ahead of the flow, all of them suddenly small and frantic against something that weighed more than a town. The powder cloud swallowed them whole. For ten seconds there was nothing but white thunder and the glasses showing gray.

Then the flow split on the rib, divided, spent itself against the valley floor in two long tongues of debris, and the mountain went quiet in the way it always did after violence: satisfied, indifferent, already forgetting.

Voss counted figures in the debris field. One. Two. Three. Four. Five.

Five.

He swept the glasses across the churned snow, along the fracture line, up the rib, back down. Five. The boy was gone.

No—not gone. A glove, red wool, protruding from the debris near the lower tongue. Then a shoulder. The boy was buried to the chest, twenty meters below the ferryman, who was already digging—no shovel, Voss noted, bare hands, tearing at the set snow like it had wronged him personally, screaming a name that the wind shredded before it arrived.

Voss watched the ferryman dig. He watched the girl arrive and dig beside him, and then the archivist, and then Vane, who worked with a terrible calm, excavating around the boy's head first, clearing the airway, doing everything right, everything the way the manuals said, as though he had done it before, as though he had dug someone out of an avalanche before and knew exactly what the next five minutes decided.

The boy's face came free. Even at half a mile, even through glass, Voss could see the blood on it, and see him breathe.

"Sir." Kessler had come down from the cornice, breathing hard, cheeks burned raw. "Casualties?"

"One buried. Alive." Voss lowered the glasses. "They'll lose hours digging him out properly. Hours we don't need to spend chasing them."

Kessler frowned at the debris field. "You want to go down and finish it?"

"No." Voss was already turning away, already working the problem forward, because the problem was the only thing that ever answered him. "Look at the terrain. They can't go back—the apron's cooked, it'll slide again under its own weight by morning. They can't climb. So they'll do what everyone does when the mountain closes a door." He pointed, eastward, to where the valley narrowed between two shoulders of black rock into a defile barely wide enough for the trail. "There. The Narrows. Single file, exhausted, carrying a chest and a wounded boy. It comes out at the old shepherds' ford, and the ford is overlooked by—" he tapped the map folded inside his coat, though Kessler couldn't see it through the wool—"a shelf I can hold with two men and a rifle apiece."

"A killing ground," Kessler said, without inflection.

"A funnel," Voss said. "The mountain did the work. We do the arithmetic."

He started down the ridge, and Kessler fell in behind him, and for a hundred paces neither spoke. Then Kessler said, quietly, the way men say things they've decided not to ask permission for: "The boy. In the school records, the ones from the annexation transports—there were children listed as lost in the range winters. Unclaimed."

Voss stopped walking.

"I'm saying," Kessler went on, staring at the middle distance with the fixed blankness of a man opening a door he can't close again, "I'm saying some of them had names. In the old script. My grandmother's name was in that script. She wouldn't teach it to my mother. Said it was safer unwritten." He swallowed. "Why am I telling you this, sir."

Because the mountain is talking to everyone today, Voss thought. Because snow that holds its breath lets go eventually, and so do men.

"Because you're tired," Voss said. "Keep walking."

But the words went with him, down the ridge, into the Narrows, and they did not leave. He thought about the registry page in his coat, the one he had carried for eleven days without reading past the header. He thought about names in an old script, safer unwritten. He thought about a boy buried to the chest in snow that Voss himself had sent down the mountain, and a ferryman digging with bare hands, and the question underneath all of it, the one he had been stepping over for weeks the way you step over a body in a road:

If the chest named him—if somewhere in that box of paper there was a line with his mother's name written in a dead alphabet—what exactly would it be restoring? What would be left to restore? What had forty years made of whatever it was?

He had no answer. He had a killing ground, and four men, and a contract with a sweetness going off in it, and he took those things down into the Narrows and told himself they were enough.

* * *

They reached the shelf above the ford two hours before dusk, and Voss set his men into the rocks with the care of a man laying a table. Positions with overlapping fields of fire. Escape routes none of them would use. The chest was the objective; the chest survived; everything else was negotiable, and had been for years, and the years had sanded him smooth in places he only noticed when the light was bad.

Through the glasses, far up the valley, the party emerged from the Narrows in the last copper light. He watched them come down toward the ford: the archivist supporting the boy, who walked leaning on her like an old man; the girl carrying the chest now, lashed to her own back, because apparently the ferryman's shoulders were done; Vane in the rear again, always the rear, walking backward half the time, watching the mountains behind them as if the mountains owed him an explanation.

Voss watched them and felt the shelf beneath him, solid, chosen, prepared, and understood—with a clarity that arrived the way avalanches arrive, all at once and from above—that he was no longer hunting them.

He was herding them. And they were coming to him. And some part of him, some part he would examine later, in the hour before dawn, in the company of the lieutenant's face and the unread page, had arranged it that way on purpose.

"Positions," he said softly, and the mountain held its breath, and waited for the morning.
