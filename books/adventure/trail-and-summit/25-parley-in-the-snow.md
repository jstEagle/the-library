## 25. Parley in the Snow

The camp sat in a bowl of white below the col, six tents and a cookfire bleeding orange smoke into the gray afternoon. I counted the men twice from the ridge—five visible, which meant at least two more out of sight—and then I stopped counting, because counting was how I had lived for twelve years and it had never once saved me anything.

"You're not seriously—" Doss started behind me.

"I am."

"Edmund." Ilse caught my sleeve. Her fingers were stiff with cold and they shook, though whether from the temperature or the fear I couldn't say. "He'll kill you. That's what he does. That's what he's *for*."

"That's exactly why I have to go on foot," I said. "A man sent to kill me will listen to a man who isn't running."

It sounded wiser than it felt. What it felt like was the day I'd walked out of the garrison at Vellmark with my name struck from every ledger in three provinces—walking slowly so no one would think I was fleeing, when all I wanted was to run until my lungs tore. Twelve years later, here I was again, walking slowly toward the thing that wanted me gone.

I left the rifle with Doss. I left the knife too, after he argued, and the hatchet, and finally the little folding saw Maret carried for cutting tent pegs, which she surrendered with the expression of someone handing over a beloved pet.

"If you die," she said, "I'm keeping your boots."

"Noted," I said.

Maret was fifteen and had learned grief the way mountain children learn weather—early, and without ceremony. She didn't cry. She just looked at me the way she'd looked at the crevasse after we pulled her out of it, as if memorizing the shape of the hole.

Ilse pressed something into my hand before I went: the locket, warm from her pocket. Inside it, my mother's face, painted small and sure, holding an infant wrapped in Kestrian blue. The infant was me. I still hadn't made peace with that. I wasn't sure I ever would.

"For luck," she said.

"Luck's not what this is."

"No," she agreed. "But take it anyway."

* * *

The snow squeaked under my boots, that dry-season squeak that means the cold has gotten into everything, even the friction between grains. Two hundred yards of open ground between the ridge and the picket line. A man with a rifle could drop me in half of it. I walked anyway, hands loose at my sides, breath smoking, and let the open ground do my talking for me. *Look at me. Look what I'm not carrying.*

They let me come to thirty paces before two of them stepped out of the tent shadows with rifles leveled. One was young, cheeks chapped raw, finger too tight on the guard. The other was older, gray at the temples, with the settled stance of a man who had done this work long enough to stop hating it.

"Voss," I called. "I'd like to talk."

Nobody moved. Somewhere behind the tents, a mule shifted and clinked.

"He knows who I am," I said. "Tell him Edmund Vane is standing in his front yard unarmed, and if he wants me shot, he can give the order himself. Not through a boy who'll dream about it."

The gray-templed man's eyes flicked sideways. Message passed. A moment later the central tent flap opened and Voss came out.

I had seen him twice before—at distance, across water, across firelight—and both times he had seemed less like a man than a function, a tool the collector aimed at things. Up close he was disappointingly human: broad, heavy through the shoulders, with a face built for endurance rather than cruelty, windburned, gray stubbled. He wore his coat open despite the cold, as if the weather owed him deference. His lieutenant stood at his shoulder—a narrow man named Corvane whose eyes had always reminded me of nail heads, small and bright and driven in deep.

"Vane." Voss said my name like he was testing a floorboard. "You've got nerve. Or a death wish. In my experience they look identical from forty paces."

"Thirty," I said. "And neither. I've got information."

"We don't trade."

"You'll want this one free."

Corvane shifted. "Sir, this is a waste of—"

"Quiet." Voss came down the slope a few steps, boots crunching, and stopped at ten paces. Close enough to talk. Far enough to be safe from nothing, really; I was unarmed, but ten paces is a heartbeat's distance for a desperate man, and maybe he knew I knew it. "Say your piece, Vane. You get one piece."

I had rehearsed this on the ridge, and rehearsed it again walking down, and now that the moment came, all the rehearsal fell away and what was left was the plain truth, which is usually the only thing worth saying anyway.

"The chest you've been chasing," I said. "The one your employer wants burned. Do you know what's in it?"

"Paper."

"Names," I said. "Forty years of names. Every child the annexation registries recorded as transferred, adopted out, shipped to schools in the capital, given new names by decree. It's a registry of stolen children, Voss. And there's a page in it—I've seen it—with a boy born in the annexation year, mother listed as *transferred north, subject reassigned*, father unknown. The boy's original name is on the line above the crossed-out one."

The wind moved through the bowl, lifting fine powder off the drifts in long smoke-colored veils. Voss hadn't moved. Hadn't blinked, near enough.

"My name," I said, "is on that registry too. Different page. My mother carried me over the border in a flour sack while the soldiers burned our quarter. I grew up under another family's name and never knew why until three weeks ago. So when I tell you what's in that chest, I'm not guessing at what it would mean to a man like you. I'm telling you what it meant to me."

"You don't know anything about me," Voss said. But his voice had changed register—lower, flatter, the voice of a man standing on ice and listening for the crack.

"I know you were in the conquerors' school system, because only graduates of it end up doing this work for land barons. I know you speak Kestrian, because I heard you curse your mule in it at Ferrow crossing, and it was old dialect, the kind nobody learns from books. And I know that the registry doesn't care what you became. It has your birth name, your mother's name, and the village they erased. Forty years of paper says you existed before you were theirs."

"Stop." It came out quiet, almost gentle, which frightened me more than shouting would have.

Behind him, Corvane had gone very still—the stillness of a man watching his employer's face change and calculating what it meant for his own position.

"I'm not offering you money," I said. "I'm not asking you to betray your contract. I'm telling you that the document your employer wants burned is the only proof on earth that you were ever anyone other than what they made you. He's not paying you to protect his land titles, Voss. He's paying you to burn your own mother's name."

Voss's jaw worked. For a long moment he looked past me, up the slope, toward where my people stood hidden among the rocks, and I understood he was seeing something else entirely—some northern schoolyard, some corridor, some word in a language beaten out of him and kept alive anyway in the animal part of his brain, the part that cursed mules.

"My mother," he said finally, and the word came out rusted, "died in the capital when I was nine. They told us she'd abandoned us. They made us say it—*my mother abandoned me*—every morning, in assembly, until we believed it." His eyes came back to mine. "If that's a lie—"

"It's a lie," I said. "They wrote down the truth first and burned it last. That's how erasure works. The truth goes in the archive before they destroy the archive. Your mother's name is sitting in a chest on a mountainside right now, waiting for someone to read it aloud."

Something moved through his face—not softening exactly. More like a door straining against its hinges. I watched a man stand at the edge of forty years of certainty and feel the ground go soft under him, and I thought: *this is how it ends, then. Not with a fight. With a name.*

Then Corvane raised his rifle.

Later—through the fever weeks, through the long climb, through everything that came after—I would replay those two seconds frame by frame and never make them fully add up. Voss had not ordered it. Voss had not signaled it. Perhaps Corvane simply read the wavering in his commander's face and understood that the contract, and the collector's favor, and his own future, were sliding away with every word I spoke—and decided that dead men can't renegotiate.

Perhaps it was simpler than that. Some men are nail heads. Driven in deep, they hold whatever they were driven into, and they cannot be pulled.

The shot cracked flat off the bowl walls.

It took me high in the left side, below the collarbone, and spun me half around, and the world went white and then very red at the edges, and I was on my knees in the snow before I understood I'd been falling. The pain arrived a beat late, enormous, a bell being struck inside my chest. I put a hand to it and it came away dark.

Shouting. Voss's voice, huge with a fury I hadn't heard in him: *"Who gave the order? WHO GAVE THE ORDER?"* And Corvane's voice, thin and fast: "He was a threat, sir, he was destabilizing—"

I heard the sound of a blow. Then another. I was trying to decide whether to crawl or lie still when the slope above me erupted—Doss first, big and low and moving like an avalanche himself, then Tam, then Ilse with the rifle I'd left behind, and Maret's voice screaming my name in a way that cut through everything else.

Voss did not fight them. That was the strange thing. He stood over me with blood on his knuckles—Corvane's, not mine—and let them come, arms slightly out from his sides, empty-handed, watching me bleed into the snow with an expression I had no name for then and still don't. Not guilt. Something older than guilt. The look of a man seeing the shape of a mistake he'd been making his whole life.

"Get him off my ground," he said hoarsely. "Take him up-slope. Corvane's confined. If he leaves his tent, shoot him." A pause, and then, quieter, almost to himself: "Both of you are done. Both of you hear me?"

Doss hauled me up under the arms and the pain went through me like a spear of ice and I heard myself make a sound I didn't recognize. Ilse was suddenly there, tearing open my coat, packing the wound with snow and then cloth, her hands quick and certain and shaking all at once.

"You *idiot*," she was saying. "You brave, stupid, impossible—"

"Told you," I managed. "One piece."

"Don't talk."

Above us, Maret had planted herself between me and the mercenaries' rifles, arms spread, fifteen years old and furious, and none of the men raised their weapons, because their commander was standing ten feet away looking at the sky like a man reading weather that had nothing to do with clouds.

"Vane." Voss crouched, once, at the edge of my blurring vision. Blood on his knuckles. Snow settling on his shoulders. "The page you described. The crossed-out name." His throat moved. "What village?"

"Northwater," I said. "The registry lists Northwater."

He closed his eyes. When he opened them, something had gone out of them—the thing that had made him a function instead of a man—and something else had come in, heavier and more alive.

"Go," he said. "Climb. Before I change my mind, or before he does something worse than I can prevent." He stood, turned, and then stopped, his back to me, and said the rest to the mountain: "The pass closes in four days. Whatever you're going to do, do it fast."

They carried me up through the rocks, Doss and Tam taking turns, Ilse holding pressure on the wound, Maret walking backward the whole way with her eyes on the camp below. The last thing I saw clearly, before the pain folded the world down to a single bright point, was Voss's camp shrinking behind us—and one tent flap thrown open, and a narrow figure being marched at rifle-point back inside it, and Voss standing alone in the falling snow, not giving any orders at all.

The light went. The bell rang once more, faintly, somewhere far away.

The last thing I heard was Maret's voice, close against my ear, fierce as a brand: "Stay awake, Uncle. You don't get to leave. We're not finished with you yet."

And then the snow took everything, gently, like a page turning.
