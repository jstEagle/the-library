## 26. Flight Above the Clouds

The blood on Edmund's side had stopped arguing with us by midmorning, which I took as a good sign until I realized it had only stopped because the cold was doing what cold does—freezing the argument in place.

"Keep him moving," Doss said, and there was no gruffness left in his voice at all. He had Edmund's arm over his shoulders and most of Edmund's weight across his back, and he climbed like a man carrying a debt he intended to pay twice over. "Moving keeps him warm. Warm keeps him alive. That's the whole of medicine as far as I know it."

"That's the whole of medicine as far as anyone knows it," Maret said. She had the chest now, strapped to her own narrow frame with rope and stubbornness, and she refused every offer to take it from her. She was sixteen years old and carrying forty pounds of a nation's existence up a mountain that wanted all of us dead, and when Tam reached for the strap she turned her shoulder away so sharply he nearly lost his footing.

"I've got it," she said.

"You can barely—" Tam began.

"I've *got* it."

I let them argue about it because arguing kept them climbing, and I kept my eyes on the route ahead, which existed only in my head and in a water-stained margin of my father's map that I had been reading wrong for ten years.

That was the thing no one understood—not even me, not fully, until this morning. My father had drawn two maps of the Serpent Range. The first was the official one, the beautiful one, the one that hung in tribunals and land offices and the private collections of men like Voss's employer: passes marked, elevations noted, the range rendered tame and legible. The second map lived in the margins of the first—a ghost script, tiny Kestrian characters threaded between the contour lines like veins under skin. For a decade I had believed the marginalia were notes. Corrections. A cartographer's doubts.

They were a road.

*Above the cloud deck,* my father had written, in letters so small I'd needed Maret to read them aloud, tracing each character with a finger gone white from cold. *Where the weather cannot follow and neither can any man who learned these mountains from below. The old shepherds' stair. It does not appear on any map but mine.*

And then, smaller still, almost an apology: *If you are reading this, Ilse, then the maps worked. Forgive me for making you climb them.*

We climbed them.

* * *

The trick of it—the terrible, beautiful trick—was that we had to go *up* into the storm to get above it.

The cloud deck over the Serpents sat at roughly nine thousand feet that day, a gray ceiling pressing down on the world like a lid on a pot. Below it: wind, spindrift, visibility measured in arm-lengths, and somewhere behind us Voss's men moving through the murmur of the storm like wolves who had learned to love the weather. Above it—my father promised—clear air, hard sun, and a shelf of stone running west along the range's spine that no one had walked in forty years because no one knew it existed.

Getting there meant three hours of climbing through the worst weather the mountain could produce, on a slope with no trail, guided by nothing but my memory of a dead man's handwriting.

"We should shelter," Tam said, an hour in. He had to shout it against the wind, and the wind ate half the words anyway. "There's a rock wall back—we passed it—"

"And if we go back to it, we're still below the clouds, and they're still behind us, and by tomorrow morning they'll have found our tracks." I didn't shout. I had learned young that shouting in mountains is a way of spending strength you'll want later. "My father wrote that the stair begins where the snow lies in ridges like plowed fields. Sastrugi. We find those, we find the start of it."

"How does your father know where the snow lies in ridges?" Maret called. "He's been dead twenty years!"

"Because he drew the wind!" I shouted back, and heard her laugh—one sharp, startled bark—and realized I had never told them, any of them, what my father actually was. Not just an archivist. Not just a man who copied documents in a cellar while a country died around him.

A cartographer's son knows things other people don't. My father taught me that a map is not a picture of the ground. It is a picture of everything that has ever happened to the ground—every flood, every fire, every footfall—and if you learn to read it right, the past will carry you places the present doesn't know exist.

The sastrugi appeared at noon, or what I judged to be noon, though the sky had become a single seamless gray in which noon and dusk were indistinguishable. Ridges of wind-packed snow, scalloped and hard as furniture, lying in long parallel rows like a field someone had plowed with a giant's harrow. And rising out of them, half-buried, a spine of dark stone—steps cut into it, ancient, deliberate, worn smooth at their edges by boots that had stopped coming forty years ago.

The old shepherds' stair.

Maret saw it first. She made a sound I'd never heard from her—not a word, just a note, high and clear, the sound a person makes when something they've only imagined turns out to be real. She dropped to her knees in the snow and brushed it away from the first step with both hands, and beneath the drifts we found them, one after another after another, ascending into the gray: a staircase built by people who had needed to move through these mountains unseen, in a time when being seen meant disappearing.

Kestrian work. Kestrian stone. Kestrian hands.

Nobody said anything. There wasn't anything to say that the stairs weren't already saying better.

* * *

We broke through the cloud deck at midafternoon, and the world changed the way a door changes a room.

One step, we were in the storm—white roaring chaos, cold that came at you sideways, a universe the size of your own reach. The next step, we stood in sunlight so clean and gold it felt like trespassing. Below us the cloud deck stretched to every horizon, a sea without waves, blinding white, and the peaks of the Serpent Range rose through it like islands, like the backs of sleeping animals, and the sky above was so blue and so deep that looking up felt like falling.

Edmund laughed.

It was a small sound, cracked and feverish, more breath than voice, but Doss nearly dropped him turning around to look. Edmund hung between us with his eyes closed and his face tipped toward the sun, and he laughed again, properly this time, and said, "He wasn't lying. The old man wasn't lying." And then his knees went, and it took both Doss and Tam to keep him off the stone.

"He's burning up," Tam said, hand flat against Edmund's forehead. "Ilse—he's burning."

"I know." I looked along the shelf of stone that ran westward before us, my father's ghost road, wide enough for two abreast in places and narrowing to a ledge in others, threading along the range's high spine with the cloud sea below and nothing above but sky. Somewhere along it—two hours, maybe three—my father's margin notes described a shepherd's hut, roofed in slate, with a spring that ran warm even in winter. *The mountain keeps one kindness,* he had written. *Find it.*

"We walk," I said. "Slowly. We walk slowly, and we talk to him, and we don't stop talking to him, because if he sleeps on this ledge in this wind he won't wake up. Maret—you're on stories. You're the best of us at them."

"What kind of stories?"

"Any kind. Lies are fine. The worse the lie, the harder he'll argue, and arguing will keep him awake."

So we walked the shepherds' stair above the clouds, and Maret lied to a dying man for three hours, and it was the finest storytelling I have ever witnessed in my life.

She told him that the tribunal's chief justice was secretly a Kestrian sympathizer who kept a hidden shrine in his cellar. Edmund, walking with his eyes half-closed and his feet finding the steps by some animal instinct, muttered, "Liar. His grandfather burned the chapel at Vellmark." She told him that Doss had once ferried a princess across the Sorrel River and been paid with a kiss. "Wrong river," Edmund breathed. "Wrong princess. Ask him sometime." She told him the chest was full of gold instead of paper, and he actually laughed at that one, a real laugh that cost him, that bent him double against Doss's shoulder and left him gasping—but gasping awake, always awake, one foot and then the other, one step and then the next, while the golden afternoon slid along the ridge and the cloud sea below us turned rose and then copper and then a darkness shot through with the last red light.

The stars came out. All of them. Every star there has ever been, unfiltered by any weather, hanging close enough to touch—or so it seemed to me, gasping for air in the thin bright cold, holding the strap of the chest that Maret would not surrender, watching a boy and a ferryman and a guide and a wounded stranger climb a dead nation's secret road through a kingdom of stars.

"There," Tam said suddenly, pointing. "Smoke."

I saw nothing. Then I did: a thread of paler darkness against the dark, rising from a fold in the ridge ahead, and beneath it, when we came closer, the low slate roof of a hut crouched into the mountainside like something born there rather than built. My father's kindness. The spring ran warm—I put my hand in it and could not believe it, warm water in this cold, steam curling off it faintly under the stars—and inside the hut there was old dry heather for bedding and a hearth blackened by fires lit decades ago by hands that had also, once, needed to be invisible.

We got Edmund inside. We got a fire going from Tam's precious hoard of pitchwood. We got the wet clothes off him and the blankets over him, and Maret melted snow and fed him water in sips, and through all of it he talked—fever-talk now, sliding between times, calling answers to Maret's lies from twelve years ago, from thirty years ago, from a childhood I was only beginning to understand.

"The pass closes," he said once, very clearly, staring at the roof beams. "Tell her the pass closes. Tell her I counted the miles. Twelve years of miles and none of them went anywhere."

"He's not dying," Doss said quietly, to me, by the fire. "I've seen dying. This isn't it. This is a man fighting something, and I don't think it's the wound."

I knelt beside Edmund and took his hand, and he gripped mine with a strength that surprised us both.

"Your father's maps," he said. His eyes found me, and they were clear—terribly, briefly clear, the fever parted like the clouds had parted. "You read them better than I ever could. Do you know that? I spent twelve years learning these mountains with my feet, and you just walked us over the top of them with a piece of paper." His grip tightened. "Ilse. Listen. When I'm—when this is done, whatever happens—"

"Don't," I said. "Whatever it is, say it when you're well."

"There might not be a well." He almost smiled. "Cartographer's daughter. You know better than anyone. Some routes only go one direction." He pulled me closer, and his voice dropped to something meant for me alone, under the wind outside and Maret's murmured story and the fire's small crackle. "The registry. Page forty-one. I saw it, in the chest, the night the hut burned. I didn't understand it then. I think I do now. Look at page forty-one."

Then the fever took him back, and his eyes went soft and far away, and he began counting miles under his breath—low numbers, then lower, descending like a man walking downstairs into sleep—and Maret took my place at his side without being asked, and I sat back against the wall with my heart hammering.

Page forty-one.

I knew the registry the way I knew my own name. I had catalogued its contents a hundred times in a hundred cellars and safehouses: birth records, land grants, testimony, the stolen children's lists. Page forty-one was in the annexation section. Seizure records. Property confiscated from Kestrian families and reassigned to the conquerors' officials.

Property records. What could a property record possibly—

I stopped.

Voss. Voss, whose employer's entire land empire rested on annexed titles. Voss, who hunted this chest with a fury that had never quite fit the money. Voss, who had the look of the northern schools about him—the orphan academies, the ones where Kestrian children had been shipped after the annexation, renamed, re-tongued, erased. Everyone in the borderlands knew the rumors. No one ever said them aloud.

I got the chest open as quietly as I could. Maret watched me over Edmund's sleeping form and said nothing, and her eyes were older than sixteen, and I loved her for the silence.

Page forty-one. By firelight, with my hands shaking, I read it.

Three entries. The third one stopped my breath.

*A boy, age six. Mother: Kestrian, deceased in transit. Father: unknown, recorded. Name in original: [the old script]. Reassigned name: Voss. Destination: Northern Academy, Threll.*

His mother's name was there. Her Kestrian name, written in my father's careful hand, preserved in a chest that a man had crossed a mountain range to burn.

I closed the book. I sat for a long time with my palms flat on its cover, feeling the warmth of the fire and the cold of the stone at my back and the enormous, terrible symmetry of it settling over me like snowfall. The man hunting us was not hunting a document. He was hunting his own name, and he didn't know it was in there, and when he found out—

When he found out, he would either burn the chest faster than ever, because hope hurts, hope hurts worse than anything—

Or he would stop.

Outside, far below the cloud deck, lost somewhere in the storm-dark, Voss was moving. And above the clouds, in a shepherd's hut that smelled of forty-year-old smoke, Edmund Vane slept and counted miles that no longer mattered, and Maret sang to him in the old Kestrian words she was only just learning, and I held page forty-one against my chest like a coal I didn't yet know whether to warm my hands on or throw into the fire.

Tam stirred at the doorway, where he'd been keeping watch. "Ilse," he said softly. "Come look at the sky."

I went. The night had turned utterly clear, and to the north—impossibly far, impossibly high, catching starlight on its ice—a peak stood up out of the dark like a blade. The Stair of Winds. Two days away, maybe less, if Edmund lived, if the weather held, if the lieutenant Voss had left behind us hadn't found another way up.

And above it, thin and high and wrong, a light flickered. Red. Deliberate. A signal, answered a moment later by another, farther east.

Someone was on the ridge ahead of us.

"They're above the clouds," Tam whispered. "How are they above the clouds?"

I had no answer. I only knew that my father's secret road ran both directions, and that somewhere between us and the pass, in the starlit silence at the top of the world, the hunt had found a way to keep climbing.
