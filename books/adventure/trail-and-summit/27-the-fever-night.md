## 27. The Fever Night

The wind above the cloud deck made a sound I had never heard before—not howling, not screaming, but a kind of long low singing, like someone humming through their teeth in the next room. Ilse said it was the Stair of Winds announcing itself from two ridges over. Doss said it was nothing, just air moving through rock. But that night, huddled in the hollow beneath the black shoulder of stone where Ilse had led us, I decided the mountain was singing for Edmund, because he had started to sing too.

He started at dusk, or what we guessed was dusk, since the clouds below us glowed the color of old pewter and gave no real light. He sang a few bars of something—half-remembered, off-key—and then stopped and said, very clearly, "I'm not going back to Ferrow," as if someone had asked him to. Then he tried to stand up out of his bedroll and Doss put both hands on his chest and pushed him down like he was closing a trunk lid.

"Easy," Doss said. "You're home. You're home, you great fool."

"I'm not home anywhere." Edmund's voice was strange—too loud, too certain, the voice of a man reading a proclamation. "That's the whole point of me."

I had seen him hurt before. I'd seen him take a fall on the glacier without blinking, seen him walk on a sprained ankle for six hours because stopping meant freezing. But this was different. This was the wound inside coming out through his skin. His face was flushed dark red and slick with sweat, and when Ilse peeled back his shirt to look at the wound in his side—the lieutenant's bullet had gone through meat, not bone, she said, which should have been good news—I saw that the flesh around it was angry and hot, streaked with red lines crawling up toward his ribs like ivy.

"Poisoned?" I asked. My voice came out smaller than I wanted.

"Dirty cloth," Ilse said. "Or dirty water. Or just his body giving out after everything we've asked of it." She worked as she talked, calm-handed, boiling snow over our little stove, tearing strips from the hem of her spare shirt. But I noticed her hands were not quite steady when she touched him, and I noticed she kept looking at his face the way you look at a door you're afraid will open onto something terrible.

* * *

The night divided itself into watches, though none of us slept much anyway. Doss took first watch over Edmund, and I sat close enough to hear everything, pretending to mend a strap that didn't need mending.

Edmund talked. That was the thing nobody warns you about—that fever makes people honest, that all the locked rooms in a man open at once and spill their contents into the dark. He talked to people who weren't there. He talked to a magistrate whose name I didn't know. He talked to his father. And mostly, he talked to himself, twelve years younger, in a voice full of a fury so clean and bright it almost sounded like joy.

"You want my route?" he said, sitting half upright with his eyes wide open and fixed on nothing. "You want to know where I'm crossing? I'll tell you where I'm crossing. I'm crossing where no one can follow, because no one has ever followed me, because following requires wanting company, and no one—" his breath caught, hitched, went on— "no one has ever wanted—"

"Drink this." Doss held the tin cup to his lips. Melted snow, with a pinch of the willow bark Ilse carried and a spoonful of honey from the last of our stores. Edmund drank it like an obedient child and then immediately began again, somewhere else entirely: "Read it aloud. The whole list. Read my name aloud so everyone hears it. Vane. Edmund Vane. There. Now it's official. Now I don't exist."

My stomach turned over. I looked at Doss. In the light of the little stove his face was gray, and I understood suddenly—he had told us, back on the scree, days ago now, but hearing it like this, hearing the blacklisting read out by the man it had been done to, was different. Hearing it was standing inside it.

"He doesn't know what he's saying," I whispered.

"No," Doss said. "He knows exactly what he's saying. That's the hell of it. Fever doesn't invent. It just unlocks." He eased Edmund flat again. "Twelve years I've wondered what they did to him after I sold his route. Whether they caught him, whether they beat him, whether he starved in some border cell. Turns out I get to hear it firsthand." His jaw worked. "Some gift."

"You couldn't have known—"

"Maret." Doss's voice was gentle and final at once. "Don't. Not tonight. Tonight I just hold the cup."

* * *

Ilse took the middle watch, and I stayed awake beside her because I couldn't not. She had moved the chest against the rock wall and sat with it between her knees, arms wrapped around it, chin resting on the lid. Like that, in the stove-light, she looked exactly like a woman holding a sleeping child. Maybe that was why I thought of my mother, who I hadn't thought of in months, and had to blink hard at the fire until the feeling passed.

Edmund quieted for a while—a bad sign, Ilse said, not a good one. She sponged his forehead, his neck, his chest, wringing the cloth into the snow outside the shelter so the heat would go somewhere. Each time she touched the wound he flinched even in sleep, and each flinch made her mouth tighten.

"Tell me about him," I said finally. "Before. When you found him at the hut."

She was quiet so long I thought she hadn't heard. Then: "He answered the door with a knife behind his back. Did I ever tell you that? Twelve years alone on a mountain, and when a stranger knocks, the first thing he does is reach for a blade." She smiled, small and crooked. "I showed him the seal on my letter case and he went white. Not angry-white. Frightened. Like I'd shown him a ghost. And then he lied to me, three times in a row, badly, about whether he knew anything about Kestria, while standing in front of a wall covered in maps drawn in his father's hand."

"Why lie?"

"Because hoping is dangerous." She adjusted the chest in her lap. "Because if you hope and it's taken away, that kills you faster than any fever. He'd already died once, in a way. People who've died once guard the exit." She looked down at Edmund, whose breathing had gone shallow and fast. "And now look at him. Carried on our backs across half the range, and still trying to plan his own disappearance at the pass. Still guarding the exit."

"He won't, though," I said. "Disappear. Not anymore."

"How do you know?"

I didn't have an answer ready. I thought about the crevasse, the blue dark, his hand around my wrist, the strength of it, the absolute refusal to let go. "Because he pulled me out," I said. "People who are planning to leave don't pull people out."

Ilse looked at me for a moment, and then she reached over and squeezed my knee, hard, once, and went back to her sponging, and I understood that I had said something true enough to count.

* * *

It got worse after midnight—or what we called midnight, time being mostly a rumor up there. The fever climbed. Edmund began to shake so hard his teeth chattered, and then stopped shaking and went burning-hot and still, and then shook again, cycling like weather. Ilse fed him willow bark tea between cycles. Doss melted snow endlessly, keeping three stones hot in the coals and rolling them, wrapped in cloth, along Edmund's feet and sides. We had no medicine worth the name. What we had was stubbornness, and it turns out stubbornness goes further than you'd think.

In one of the hot cycles, Edmund's eyes opened, focused—for the first time all night—on my actual face.

"Maret," he said.

"I'm here."

"They came at dawn." His voice was hoarse, scraped thin. "Four of them. I'd left by the north window. I always leave by the north window. Do you know why? Because the north window doesn't face the trail. Because I spent twelve years making sure no one could ever say they'd seen me go." A shudder rolled through him. "They burned my chair. Isn't that stupid? Of everything, I keep thinking about the chair. I carved that chair the first winter. It was a bad chair. It wobbled."

"It was a terrible chair," I said. "You sat in it like it was a throne."

Something moved across his face—almost a laugh, almost grief. "You never saw the chair."

"I saw the burn marks on the ground," I said. "When we went back for the map. There were four iron legs printed in the ash. So I know exactly how big it was, and it was a terrible chair, and I bet it wobbled."

This time it was definitely a laugh, or the ghost of one, and then his eyes lost focus again and slid away from me, back into whatever country the fever had opened, and I realized my face was wet and wiped it with my sleeve before anyone could see.

* * *

Doss's watch. The deep hours. The wind changed its song and dropped lower, almost a whisper now, and in the quiet Edmund's ravings thinned out into fragments, single sentences, names.

"Doss," he said at one point, clearly, wide awake or seeming so.

"I'm here."

"You were there. At the checkpoint. You saw what they wrote in the book."

"I saw it," Doss said steadily. "I saw every word."

"'Refused transit. Known associate of proscribed persons.' Eleven years I read that line in my head. Every border, every bridge, every ferry landing. You know what a blacklist is, Doss? It's a story other people tell about you, and you can't stop them telling it, and eventually you start telling it yourself." His hand found Doss's sleeve and gripped it. "Did I ever tell you the worst part? The worst part isn't the guards. The worst part is that after a while, when someone was kind to me—an innkeeper, a child, anyone—I'd think: they don't know what I am. If they knew, they'd stop. And I'd leave before they found out. Before they could stop. Twelve years of leaving first."

Doss was silent a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough as gravel. "And now?"

"Now there's a girl who calls me Uncle," Edmund said, wonderingly, like reporting a miracle he didn't believe in, "and an archivist who won't let me vanish, and a ferryman who cut a bridge for me, and I keep waiting for them to find out what I am and stop, and they—" his grip tightened— "they keep not stopping."

"That's because they've found out," Doss said, "and decided anyway."

Edmund stared at him. Then his eyes closed, and this time when he sank, he sank slowly, like a man lowering himself into bed rather than a man falling down a well, and his breathing—even, deep, unlabored—was the most beautiful sound I had heard in my life.

* * *

Near dawn the fever broke. We knew because he began to sweat—really sweat, soaking the blankets—and Ilse said, "That's it, that's the turn," in a voice that cracked on the second word, and Doss laughed once, a short explosive sound, and pressed his fists against his eyes like a man holding back a flood, and I sat there grinning like an idiot at nothing.

We changed the blankets. We got broth into him, a spoonful at a time, Ilse's patient hand, his terrible weak complaining—"tastes like the pot it was boiled in"—which was the best possible sign, because a man well enough to complain about soup is a man planning to live.

By the time the cloud deck below us turned rose and gold, he was sleeping a true sleep, and Ilse had gone to the mouth of the hollow to stand in the light, and Doss was asleep sitting up with his back against the rock, snoring gently, and I was left alone with the strangest feeling of my life.

I had spent the whole journey being carried. Fed when I was hungry, pulled out of holes, taught to read contour lines, given a place in a walking line. And last night, for the first time, I had been the one holding the cup. I had been the one talking a man back across the border between living and dying, one terrible chair and one wobbled-at-a-time. It wasn't a big thing. It was melting snow and stubborn talk. But I understood, sitting there in the pink light with my hands still smelling of woodsmoke and willow bark, that this was what a family actually was—not the ones who carry you, but the ones who stay up with you, and that I had joined one not by being rescued from it but by taking a shift.

Ilse came back from the light and sat down beside me. The chest was back between her knees.

"He'll live," she said.

"He'd better," I said. "He owes me a lesson. Contour lines. He promised."

Ilse smiled. Then her smile faded, and she looked east along the ridge, toward the pass, and I watched the worry come back into her face like water filling a footprint.

"What?" I said.

"The snow," she said quietly. "Look at the high peaks. Look at the color of the light on them."

I looked. The peaks that had been bare brown rock all week wore a thin white dusting, delicate as sifted flour, and the light lay on them flat and hard instead of warm.

"It's early," she said. "Weeks early. The dry season is ending right now, on top of us, and the Stair of Winds—" she stopped, and her arms tightened around the chest. "We needed four more days, Maret. We have maybe one."

Behind us, in his sleep, Edmund stirred and murmured something we couldn't catch. But when I leaned close, I caught the shape of it, and it wasn't fever-talk at all. It was a route. He was dictating a route in his sleep—*up the spine, past the chapel rocks, before the wind rises*—and even unconscious, even half-dead, he was already leading us toward the pass.
