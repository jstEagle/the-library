## 28. Voss's Reckoning

The fire had burned down to a red eye by the time Voss took the page out of his coat.

He had been carrying it for two days without reading it. That was the truth he would not say aloud, even to himself, even here, alone in the command tent with the wind worrying the guy-lines like a dog at a bone. He had carried it folded in oilcloth against his chest, and every time his hand drifted toward it—on the march, at the cold meals, in the gray hour before dawn when the sentries coughed and stamped—he had found a reason to wait. The ink might be smeared. The light was bad. There were positions to set, rations to count, a hunt to run.

There was always a hunt to run.

Now the camp slept behind him on the shelf of stone above the glacier's shoulder, thirty men wrapped in wool and misery, and Voss sat with the page flat on his knee in the last light of the coals, and there was no reason left to wait.

The lieutenant had taken it from the party's abandoned camp below the col—the ambush site, the killing ground that hadn't killed. Halleck was efficient that way. It was one of the things Voss had valued in him: a man who moved through other people's leavings like a customs officer, touching everything, weighing everything, keeping what had value. When they'd swept the avalanche debris and found no bodies but Tam's blood on the snow and a trail climbing east toward the high route, Halleck had also brought him this—a single sheet, torn from a bound registry, water-stained at one corner, dense with columns in the old Kestrian script.

*Evidence,* Halleck had called it. *Proof of what they're carrying. Names, sir. Hundreds of names.*

Voss had told him to hold it until morning.

That had been two mornings ago.

He unfolded it now. The paper was good stock, the kind that didn't come out of any print shop on this side of the range; it had been made in the lowlands before the annexation, when Kestria still had mills and a mint and a postal service and all the small machinery of a country that believed it would go on existing. Forty years dead, and its paper was still better than anything being made today. There was a lesson in that, if a man wanted to learn it.

The columns ran in two scripts side by side—the old Kestrian hand and the conquerors' trade-tongue beneath it, as though whoever had compiled the registry had known that someday it would have to be read by people who had been taught to forget. Names. Birthplaces. Dates of removal. And in the third column, in smaller script, dispositions: *shipped, fostered, apprenticed, sold.*

Voss knew what those words meant. He had been one of the words.

He read the way he did everything else—methodically, top to bottom, letting the eye do its work while the rest of him stood apart and watched, arms crossed, suspicious. It was how he read casualty lists. It was how he read ledgers. A name was a datum. A date was a datum. Nothing on a page could reach through forty years and put its hand around your throat.

He was halfway down the second column when he stopped watching himself.

*Removed from Vel Saren district, age six. Disposition: shipped, eastern schools.*

The eastern schools. Everyone from the annexation generation knew the phrase the way you knew the name of a disease that had passed through your village. The conquerors had gathered up the children of the officials and officers and anyone whose family name carried weight—carried *loyalty*, they'd have said—and put them on trains east, into boarding houses with high windows and new names issued like uniforms. Erase the language, erase the calendar, erase the birthday if it fell on an inconvenient anniversary. By fifteen, most of them couldn't have found Kestria on a map, which was the point. By twenty, some of them were working for the land bureaus, signing off on the transfer of their own grandparents' farms, and that too was the point.

Voss had been on one of those trains. Age six. He remembered the platform because he had been holding his mother's hand and then he hadn't been, and the gap between those two conditions was the first thing he could remember clearly, and the last thing he remembered of her at all. Her face had gone the way of faces do—first the details, then the shape, then even the fact of it, until she was less than a memory, more like a room he'd once stood in. Warmth without features. He had built his whole life on the foundation of that absence and called the building ambition.

His finger had stopped on the line. He noticed it the way you notice your own heartbeat when it stumbles.

The entry above it was the same district. Same removal date. Same train, probably—same cattle car, same high windows waiting at the end of it.

*Mara Voss, née Kellin. Removed with dependent child. Disposition: detained, administrative review, released to household labor, southern provinces.*

Released. Not shipped. She had not gone east with him. She had been released—to labor, to some farm or laundry or kitchen in the south—and her son had been put on a train in the opposite direction, and the registry recorded both facts in the same tidy column as if they were entries of equal weight, as if a mother walking south and a boy riding east were just two lines of bookkeeping.

She had lived. Somewhere, for some years, she had lived. Released meant alive, at least on the day of the entry. He did the arithmetic without wanting to—forty years, the mortality of labor households, the cholera years—and arrived nowhere. She could have died at thirty-five or she could be alive now, seventy years old, shelling beans on a stoop in some southern town, and there was no way to know from this page. This page only said she had existed. That someone with a steady hand had written her down, given her back her maiden name in parentheses, refused to let the state's version be the only version.

*Mara Voss, née Kellin.*

He read it four times. His hands were steady. He noted that his hands were steady, and hated them for it.

Outside, the wind shifted and came down off the icefield with a sound like a door closing somewhere far away. In the east, where the high route climbed toward the Stair of Winds, the sky was clear and viciously full of stars. They'd have a window. Two days, maybe three, before the next system came over the divide. If the party was moving, they'd be moving into the arête by then, and the Stair of Winds in snow was a coffin with a view.

That was the calculation he should be making. That was the calculation he *was* making, he told himself, laying the page beside the fire and staring at it the way you'd stare at a map. The chest contained the registry. The registry contained proof of title, lineage, existence—everything his employer's empire was built on the erasure of. Burn the chest, burn the proof, and the annexed lands stayed annexed, the deeds stayed deeds, and men like Aldous Crane went on owning half a province because a country had been unwritten. That was the job. That had always been the job, and Voss did jobs, whole ones, clean ones, and had never once asked what the job was *for*, because asking was the first symptom of the disease that ended careers and sometimes ended men.

But the job had changed shape somewhere on the ice, and he could feel the change sitting in his chest like swallowed river stones.

He thought about Crane's last message, relayed up through three intermediaries: *conclude the matter.* As if a nation were a leaky roof. As if three hundred people on a mountain were weather damage. Crane had never once asked who these people were. To Crane they were a clause in a contract, a risk with a face count. And Voss had been the instrument of that clause for eleven years, and had told himself that instruments don't bear responsibility, and had almost, nearly, mostly believed it.

And now there was a page on his knee with his mother's name on it, and the instrument had developed a pulse.

He heard footsteps in the packed snow and didn't turn. Only one man walked that heavily and that carelessly this close to Voss's fire.

Halleck came into the firelight rubbing his hands, breath steaming, and glanced at the page. "Still at that? Sir, we've got the trail confirmed past the refuge. They're moving slow—one of them's hurt. We can be on the arête approach by tomorrow night if we push."

"I know the timetable, Lieutenant."

"Then I don't understand the delay." Halleck said it easily, the way he said everything, with the confidence of a man who had never once been struck for speaking. "Crane wants it done. The storm does our work for us if we let it. Frankly, sir, I don't see why we're camping at all. Give me ten men and I'll take the high route tonight and finish it before they see the Stair."

"You'll take ten men onto a glacier at night in a rising wind," Voss said, "and I'll spend the rest of my life explaining to Crane why he lost ten men and a contract."

"They're not soldiers. It's a schoolteacher, a ferryman, a girl, and a wounded man."

"And an archivist who has outmaneuvered us twice." Voss picked up the page, slowly, and folded it along its old creases. "We move at first light. Full strength. On my route."

Halleck's jaw worked. Something flickered behind his eyes—not fear, nothing so useful as fear. Calculation. The look of a man checking whether the ground under his commander had shifted enough to matter. "Yes, sir," he said finally, and turned to go.

"Halleck."

The lieutenant stopped.

"The registry. The original document in the chest. You're certain the copy is faithful?"

"As certain as tearing a page can make it, sir."

"When we conclude this," Voss said, and heard the words come out of his mouth as if someone else were saying them, "the original comes to me. Not to Crane's couriers. To me. Is that understood?"

A pause long enough to have weather in it. "Understood, sir," Halleck said, and something in the flatness of it told Voss that the man had already begun composing the letter he would write to Crane if it became profitable to do so.

After he was gone, Voss sat alone with the fire dying and the page in his hands, and let himself think the thought he had been refusing for two days:

*She wrote it down. Someone kept her name.*

Not the state. The state had numbers for her, dispositions, a column heading. But somewhere in the machinery of the fall, some clerk or archivist or frightened functionary had entered her maiden name in parentheses, had treated her as a person rather than a unit, and that single act of stubborn accuracy had survived floods and fires and forty years of deliberate forgetting to arrive here, on a mountainside, in the hands of the man hired to destroy it.

He had spent eleven years burning things for money and calling it work. Now he held one page that proved the burning had never once succeeded—not really, not all the way down. Something always got written twice. Someone always kept the second copy. Hope, it turned out, was not a feeling. It was a filing practice.

He laughed once, short and terrible, and the sound died in the wind.

Then he did the thing that could not be undone. He took out his field notebook—the small black ledger in which he recorded dispositions, supply counts, the daily bookkeeping of the hunt—and on a fresh page, in his careful draftsman's hand, he copied the entry. *Mara Voss, née Kellin. Removed with dependent child. Released to household labor, southern provinces.* Below it, after a moment, he added: *Son located. Status: searching.*

It was not a confession. It was not redemption. It was a line in a ledger, and Voss trusted ledgers, because a thing entered in a ledger became real, became trackable, became a task with an outcome. He had turned his whole heart into a ledger years ago precisely so that nights like this one would leave no balance outstanding.

The entry was outstanding now. He could feel it like an unpaid debt.

First light came gray and windless, the kind of false calm the range offered before it took payment. Voss stood the men up, checked the rope teams himself, and gave the order to move—not toward the arête approach, not exactly, not the way Halleck expected. He set a bearing that would bring them to the high route ahead of the fugitives but below it, at a place where the trail narrowed against a rock wall and a man with thirty mercenaries could take the party alive instead of burying them.

Alive. He heard the word in his own head and examined it like a flaw in a blade. Since when did the job want them alive?

Since the registry, said the ledger in his chest. Since a page with a woman's name on it. Since hope turned out to be a filing practice, and he had just opened a file.

They climbed. The glacier creaked under them like an old ship. Behind, far below, a thread of smoke rose from the direction of the hidden valley—cooking fires, ordinary human smoke, the smoke of people who believed themselves unfindable. Voss looked at it longer than he needed to.

By midmorning they reached the narrow place, and Voss raised his fist to halt the column, and that was when the scout came down off the rocks at a run, breathless, with news that rearranged the entire board:

The lieutenant was gone. Halleck's bedroll cold since midnight, ten men unaccounted for from the rear watch, and a note pinned to the command tent's center pole in Halleck's neat customs-officer hand: *Concluding the matter myself. Crane pays for results, not deliberation.*

Voss read it twice. Around him the column waited, thirty faces turning toward him for orders, and somewhere above them, moving fast and reckless toward the Stair of Winds with fire in his pockets, was a man who would burn the chest and everyone standing near it rather than let a single page survive to reach a tribunal.

Voss folded Halleck's note into the notebook, beside the copied entry, beside his mother's name.

"Break camp," he said. "We're going after our own man."

No one asked why. They were professionals. But as the column swung onto the ice and began to climb, Voss understood that he was no longer hunting the party at all—that somewhere in the last two days, without ceremony, without permission, the errand had changed hands. He was racing his own lieutenant up a mountain to save a box of names, and one of those names was his mother's, and the door he had kept locked for forty years was open, and the wind was coming through it cold and clean and there was no closing it again.

Above them, at the edge of sight, the knife-edge of the Stair of Winds caught the morning sun and burned white, like a fuse.
