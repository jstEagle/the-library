## 29. The Stair of Winds

I woke before dawn with the wound pulling at me like a hand I couldn't shake loose.

The bullet had gone through the meat below my collarbone—Voss's lieutenant's shot, fired wild across the snow of the parley ground—and in the nine days since, it had done what wounds do in cold country: closed slow, festered a little, then settled into a dull hot argument beneath the bandage that Ilse changed every morning and every night without once letting her face show what she was thinking. She was an archivist. She had spent ten years reading documents written by frightened men. She could read me the same way, and she knew better than to say aloud what the fever charts were telling her.

So instead she said, "The sky."

I rolled off my bedroll and looked. Above the refuge hut—a stone bothy older than the annexation, its roof patched with turf—the stars were hard and close, and there was no wind at all. That was the wrong thing. Windless nights above eleven thousand feet in late autumn are not mercy. They are held breath.

"Snow by tomorrow night," I said.

"And the Stair?"

"Passable today. Only today." I stood, testing the shoulder, and the pain flared white and then banked itself down to embers. "After tonight, we wait out winter on this side of the range, or we go around and lose the tribunal everything."

Ilse looked at me for a long moment. In the dark of the hut her face was all planes and shadow, and I thought—not for the first time—that she had the kind of face carved for bad news, the kind that makes you believe the person delivering it has already survived worse.

"You shouldn't climb," she said.

"I know."

"That wasn't agreement, Edmund."

"It wasn't disagreement either." I reached for my boots. "Get them up. We move in an hour."

* * *

The Stair of Winds did not have a name among maps. My father had drawn it once—in the margin of the Serpent Range survey, in that small precise hand of his—and he had labeled it *die Treppe*, the staircase, because from the valley below it looked like steps cut by some god with more patience than sense. What it actually was: three miles of knife-edge arête, never wider than six feet, mostly two, in places less than one, with drops on either side that went down so far the mind refused the arithmetic. On the north side, the wind came off the icefields like something with intent. On the south side, the sun-softened rock shed stones all day like a man shedding a coat he'd worn too long.

You crossed it early, before the sun loosened the rock and before any weather arrived to push you off. You crossed it unroped where the edge narrowed, because a rope between two climbers on a knife-edge is not safety—it is a machine for killing you both. And you crossed it knowing that halfway was exactly as far from everywhere as nowhere.

Doss took the chest.

We had argued about it the night before, briefly, the way old friends argue, which is to say without any of the words that matter. He simply picked up the chest—heavy as sin, sealed with Kestria's crest, holding the legal soul of three hundred people in the high valley and however many more scattered across forty years of erasure—and slung it onto the frame he'd built for it, lashed it down with the same rope he'd used to cut Voss's lieutenant loose into the gorge, and said, "My back. Your hands. You'll want your hands."

He was right, which annoyed him and pleased him in equal measure.

Maret was quiet at breakfast. Sixteen years old, runaway, the only one of us who could read the old script fluently, and she had learned on this trip what fear tasted like when it wasn't about herself. She watched Tam check his crampons twice, watched Ilse divide the food into day-packs with the exactness of a woman cataloguing a fragile collection, and she didn't joke. That scared me more than the arête did. Maret not joking was a weather sign of its own.

"Hey," I said, crouching by her. The shoulder screamed. I kept my face still. "You crossed the glacier. You crossed Ironbridge. Today is just walking. Narrow walking."

"Narrow walking," she repeated. "Over a hole that goes to the bottom of the world."

"The bottom of the world is overrated. I've been there. It's mostly paperwork." I tapped the chest. "That's paperwork too. It just happens to be the kind that brings people back from the dead."

She almost smiled. "You're a terrible comfort."

"I'm a cartographer. Comfort isn't on the map."

Tam laughed—one short bark—and the sound broke the tension the way a stone breaks glass, cleanly, irreversibly. He was the elder's grandson, nineteen, and he had hated me for overriding his route on the glacier and loved me for saving them from the crevasse field, and he had long since stopped trying to sort out which feeling was winning. He shouldered his pack and said, "The mountain is lying about the wind again. My grandmother says the Stair breathes. If you can hear it breathing, don't step onto it."

"And if you can't?" Maret asked.

"Then it's holding its breath," Tam said, "and so should you."

* * *

We reached the base of the arête as the first light came over the eastern summits, and the light did what light does to high country in autumn: it set the snowfields burning gold and turned every shadow into a canyon. The Stair rose ahead of us, a spine of dark rock against the bright sky, and I felt the old thing wake in my chest—the thing twelve years of exile had tried to starve, the thing that had come back on the first pass and grown stronger on the glacier until now it stood up inside me and howled.

The mountain spoke. It always had, to me. Not words—never words—but a grammar of pressure and silence, of the way snow sat on ledges and the way wind moved through a col, and for twelve years I had lived somewhere flat enough and low enough that it couldn't reach me. Now it reached me, and I understood, standing there with a hole in my shoulder and my father's map folded against my heart, that I had never once stopped listening. I had only stopped answering.

"No rope on the narrows," I said. "Rope on the broad sections, thirty feet apart, Doss in the middle with the chest. Nobody passes anybody. Nobody stops on the edge unless I say stop. If I say *down*, you get flat and you hold rock, not each other." I looked at each of them. "Questions?"

"Yeah," Doss said. "What if you fall?"

"Then keep going. The chest goes to the council whether I'm on it or not."

Silence. Then Ilse stepped forward and put her hand flat against my chest, over the bandage, over the wound, gentle as someone handling a manuscript.

"You are not expendable," she said quietly. "Say it."

"Ilse—"

"*Say it.*"

Behind her, Maret had gone very still. Tam was studying the sky with tremendous concentration. Doss was looking at his boots.

"I'm not expendable," I said.

"Good." She took her hand away. "Because I've read enough histories to know how they end when the people carrying them decide they're disposable. They end with the chest in a museum and nobody left who remembers why it mattered." She turned toward the arête. "Lead."

* * *

The first mile was the easy mile—broad shoulders of rock, a path worn by ibex and the occasional mad shepherd, the drop on the north side hidden under cornices of blue ice. I set the pace slow, slower than I wanted, because the wound made my balance a half-beat behind my intention and I knew it. Every time I swung my right arm, the pain rang up through my teeth. So I climbed left-handed, awkward, keeping my weight stacked over my feet the way you learn to when your body can't be trusted to catch you.

Halfway up the first rise, I stopped hearing my own breathing and started hearing the mountain.

It was the silence that did it. A windless morning at altitude is not silent—it is *pre*-silent, the way a struck bell is pre-sound—and underneath it I could feel the range thinking. Snow settling by fractions on the cornices. Ice expanding in a crack somewhere below us with a sound like a knuckle cracking in a cathedral. Far off, past the col, the first gray suggestion of the front that would end the season.

And beneath all of it, fainter than any of it, the breathing Tam had warned us about. The Stair inhaling.

"We're good," I called back. "Move while it holds."

Doss came up the rise with the chest on his back, and I will remember that image as long as whatever's left of me remembers anything. He was fifty years old, a disgraced ferryman with a life-debt and a confession he'd made on the scree that neither of us ever spoke of again, and he climbed like a man carrying a child asleep on his shoulders—careful, tender, furious with anyone who might wake it. The chest rode high between his shoulder blades, and the morning sun caught the crest stamped into its lid, and for a moment the dead nation's seal blazed gold against the sky like a small risen sun.

Maret saw it too. I heard her breath catch.

"That's what they erased," she said softly. Not to me. To herself. Maybe to the mountain.

"Keep moving," I said, but gently.

* * *

The narrows began at the second rise, where the ridge pinched down to nothing and the world fell away on both sides.

I went first, alone, leaving the others at the anchor point. The rock under my boots was good—my father's map had marked the solid line in ink that hadn't faded in forty years, and I blessed him for it and hated him for it in the same breath, the way I had learned to do, the way I was still learning to do even after the letter, even after everything. His handwriting guided my feet. His betrayal had put the bullet in my shoulder. Both things were true. I had stopped expecting truth to be simple somewhere back on the glacier.

Three hundred feet of edge, never wider than a boot-length. Below me on the north, the cornice curved out over four thousand feet of empty blue air. Below me on the south, the rock dropped clean and straight into a cirque where last winter's avalanche debris lay like the bones of something enormous.

I walked it the way my father must have surveyed it: one step, test, weight, next step. The wind stayed absent. The Stair held its breath. Halfway along, a ptarmigan burst up from a rock hollow not two feet from my boot and my whole body clenched around the wound and I stood there, swaying over the abyss, laughing silently at myself until the shaking stopped.

Then I was across, and I anchored myself, and I brought them over one at a time.

Tam crossed like he'd been born on edges, which in the high valley he probably had been. Ilse crossed with her eyes fixed on the rock, methodical, refusing the view, and only when her boots hit the broad stone beyond did she look up and let me see what the crossing had cost her—not fear of falling, I realized, but fear of the chest falling, fear of ten years of work ending in a crevasse of blue air. Maret crossed third, small and fierce, whispering something to herself the whole way—I caught fragments of it later and realized she was reciting the old script, the letters she'd taught herself from stolen books, spelling her way across the sky.

Doss came last, with the chest.

He was halfway across when the mountain exhaled.

There was no warning I could have given. There was no warning the mountain gave. One moment the air was a held note; the next, the wind came off the northern icefield in a single long gust, not strong enough to knock a man down, not weak enough to ignore—forty pounds of pressure sliding across the edge like a hand checking your grip. Doss froze. The chest acted like a sail. For one full second he leaned into the void on the north side, the crest tilting toward the cornice, his crampons scraping for purchase on stone the width of a hymnal—

"*Down!*" I roared. "Flat! Hold rock!"

He dropped. All of them dropped, flattening along the broad section behind him, and I flattened too, cheek against stone still warm from the sun, and the wind poured over us for ten seconds, twenty, thirty, humming in the rock like a finger on a wet glass rim. Beneath my chest, through the bandage, through the wound, I felt the mountain's pulse. It sounded, absurdly, like a heartbeat. Like the chest had one.

Then the wind died the way it had come—all at once, embarrassed, gone.

"Up," I said. "Quickly now. It'll breathe again."

Doss rose, adjusted the chest straps with fingers I could see trembling, and finished the crossing without a word. When he reached me he didn't look at the drop or the sky. He looked at me.

"Still with us," he said.

"Still with us," I agreed.

"My grandmother used to say," Tam offered, arriving breathless, "that the Stair tests whether you're carrying something worth the crossing."

"And if you're not?" Maret asked.

"Then it's just a very long way to fall for nothing."

Nobody laughed. Everybody smiled. On a knife-edge at thirteen thousand feet, that seemed about right.

* * *

The last section was the crux—a tower of rock at the highest point of the arête, where the ridge gathered itself into a fist before opening onto the descent toward the pass. My father's map marked it with a single word: *Kletterei*. Climbing. No route drawn. Just the word, and beside it, in smaller script, a sentence I had read a hundred times in twelve years without understanding until now:

*Die Hand folgt dem Herzen hinauf.* The hand follows the heart upward.

I climbed it left-handed. There was no other honest way. The moves were moderate—big holds, generous friction—but the exposure peeled the skin off the mind, five thousand feet of nothing on the left, the whole golden sweep of the range on the right, and halfway up the tower my shoulder tore something small and hot and I hung there by three points, gasping, tasting copper, absolutely certain for two seconds that this was where the story ended: Edmund Vane, blacklisted cartographer, found in spring hanging off his father's favorite piece of rock, the chest delivered by a ferryman and an archivist and two children of nowhere.

Then Maret's voice came up from below, thin with wind and distance.

"Uncle. Move your left foot. There's a ledge. Trust the map."

Not *Edmund*. Not *hey, you*. *Uncle.*

I found the ledge. I climbed. At the top I rolled over the summit of the tower onto a platform of snow no bigger than a table, and lay there while the others came up the pitch behind me, and when Doss crested with the chest and set it down beside me, I put my good hand on the lid and felt the seal under my palm, cold and certain, and I laughed—out loud, ragged, real.

"What?" Ilse demanded, scrambling onto the platform, eyes already hunting for injury.

"Nothing. Everything." I sat up. Ahead of us the ridge descended in long easy strides toward the col, toward the pass, toward the far side of the range where the tribunal waited and the winter was already closing the door behind us. "Twelve years I counted miles. Every road, every border, every day—miles between me and capture, miles between me and my name. Up here—" I gestured at the wind, the light, the small hard knot of us on top of the world. "No miles. Just steps."

"Is that wisdom?" Doss asked. "Or blood loss?"

"Yes," I said.

Below us, far to the south, a plume of spindrift lifted off a distant peak—the storm announcing itself, right on schedule. We had perhaps four hours. Perhaps less. I got everyone moving, and we went down the far side fast and careful, and the Stair of Winds fell away behind us into the cloud that was rising to swallow it for the winter.

It was Maret, bringing up the rear, who saw them first.

"Smoke," she said, and pointed.

At the head of the descent, where the trail narrowed between two walls of rock before opening onto the pass, a thin thread of smoke rose into the still air. A campfire. Someone was waiting at the pass ahead of us—someone who had beaten a party that had climbed all night, someone who knew the route, someone who should have been days behind us or confined under guard in Voss's camp.

I looked at the smoke, and then at Ilse, and her face had gone the color of the snow.

"Voss?" Doss asked.

"No," I said slowly, watching the smoke bend in a wind we couldn't yet feel. "Voss pulled his man back on the col. This is someone else." I touched the bandage, felt the heat of the wound, and started down toward the pass with the light failing and the first flakes beginning to sift out of the gray. "Someone who thinks the chest should burn."
