## 30. The Lieutenant's Choice

The rope was cut clean, not frayed. Voss knew that before he touched it, before he crouched in the cold of the storage shed and ran his thumb along the severed end the way he'd once run it along the spines of ledgers in another life, in the school at Halden where they'd shaved his head and taught him that names were debts and debts were to be collected. A clean cut meant a knife. A knife meant intention. And intention, at this hour, in this weather, could only belong to one man.

Brann.

He stood in the doorway of the shed and let the wind hit him, the wind that came down off the Stair of Winds like something poured, something with weight and grain to it, and he looked at the empty peg where Brann's kit had hung, at the missing coil of rope, the missing rations, the missing ice-axe—the good one, the one Voss had taken off a dead guide in the Karst years ago and never told anyone about—and he felt the particular cold that was not the mountain's cold. It was the cold of a man realizing he had misjudged his own hound.

He had confined Brann two nights ago. Two nights of the man sitting in the supply tent under watch, two nights of Voss reading and rereading the page his lieutenant had stolen from the chest's registry—the page with his mother's name on it, *Mirren Voss, née Kestal, age six, transferred, Halden School, disposition: unrecorded*—two nights of Voss saying nothing while Brann watched him say nothing, and Voss had thought the silence was working. He had thought the man was absorbing it. He had thought, in the arrogance of command, that a man who had followed him for eleven years through nine ranges and three wars' worth of contracts would absorb a page of paper the way Voss had absorbed it: slowly, in the dark, like water into cracked stone.

Instead Brann had absorbed it like oil. It had sat on top of him, and it had slicked him loose, and now he was gone.

"Corporal." Voss's voice came out level. He was proud of that, distantly, the way a man is proud of his own heartbeat continuing. "Wake the others."

* * *

The camp at the high col was six men now, and it took them eleven minutes to strike it, which Voss noted with a professional's satisfaction even as his mind ran ahead of his body, up the slope, along the ridge, calculating. Brann had a six-hour lead at most. He had gone in the dark, before the moon set, which meant he had planned it, which meant he had watched the watches, which meant the man Voss had trusted with every ugly job for a decade had spent two days in a tent pretending to be broken while he counted the rotation of his guards.

And where would he go? That was the question Voss turned over as they climbed, crampons biting, breath ragged in the thin air, and the answer kept coming back the same way, the way an answer comes back when it is true: to the pass. To the Stair of Winds. To the place where the chest would have to come through, because there was no other way through the range before the snows, and Brann knew it because Voss himself had drawn the map of it on the dirt outside a fire two weeks ago, back when they were still hunting, back when the plan had been simple: take the high col, bring the slope down across the party's route, funnel them into the gorge, take the chest, burn the chest, go home, get paid.

Back when the plan had been simple, and Voss had still been simple inside it.

Now he climbed with a page of paper folded against his chest, inside his coat, over his heart, like something a schoolboy would carry, and he hated that. He hated that he knew exactly where the paper was at all times, that he could feel its corner through three layers of wool when the wind gusted. *Mirren Voss, née Kestal.* His mother had been born Mirren Kestal. He had known her as Mirren Voss, a laundress in the conquerors' city of Vhale, a woman with cracked hands who sang songs in a language she told him was nothing, just nonsense, just sounds her own mother had made, don't ask, Emeric, don't ask. He had asked once, at nine years old. She had hit him. It was the only time she had ever hit him, and he had understood it, even then, even at nine: she had hit him because the question was dangerous, because names were debts, because in Vhale a Kestrian name was a debt that got collected with interest by people like the man who employed Voss now.

And the chest—the cursed, waterlogged, impossible chest that had come down a river to a mountain hermit and started all of this—held her. Held her at six years old, listed like a sack of grain. *Transferred. Disposition: unrecorded.* Unrecorded. As if she had been weather. As if she had been a shipment lost in transit.

Voss had spent the last two nights telling himself he had confined Brann for discipline. For the theft of the page. For striking the prisoner—no, the man hadn't struck anyone. For insubordination, then. For the look on his face. And underneath all of it, in the place where Voss kept the things he did not say, he knew the truth: he had confined Brann because Brann had seen the page too, and had seen what it did to Voss, and had understood before Voss did what it meant. It meant the hunt was over. It meant the contract was dead. It meant that Emeric Voss, who had built a life on being the instrument of erasure, had discovered he was one of the erased.

Brann had not absorbed that. Brann had a wife in Vhale and a house with a mortgage and a view of the harbor, and a restored Kestria meant the land empire's titles voided, meant the company that paid them both dissolved, meant all of it gone. Brann had looked at the page and seen not a mother but a ledger closing. And so Brann had done the arithmetic that Brann had always done, faithfully, for eleven years: protect the asset, destroy the liability, go home.

Except now the asset was the chest, and the liability, as far as Brann was concerned, was Voss himself.

"Boss." Corporal Enk drew up beside him on the switchback, young, breathing hard, his face raw with wind. "The trail splits ahead. Ridge route or the couloir."

"Ridge."

"He's alone, boss. In this. He's not making the Stair."

Voss looked at the boy—at Enk, who was twenty-three and had never asked what any of this was for—and felt the strange tenderness that commanders feel for the men they are about to disappoint. "He's making the Stair," Voss said. "He's making it because he thinks he has to. A man alone moves faster than a man who sleeps. He'll take the ridge because the ridge is faster if you don't fall, and Brann doesn't fall." He started climbing again. "And we take the ridge because I want to look at his face when I catch him."

* * *

They caught sight of him at midday, a black fleck on the white shoulder of the ridge, moving with that metronome efficiency Voss had trained into him, and Voss felt something twist in his chest that was not quite anger and not quite grief. Eleven years. He had pulled Brann out of a river in the Karst. He had carried the man's gear for two days when the man's shoulder was torn open at the Salt Quarries. He had sat with him through the fever at Fort Merren and listened to him say a name over and over—the wife's name, Lissa—and had promised nothing and been believed anyway. That was what command was: a long chain of small beliefs, and you never knew which one would break first, and when it broke you found out it had been load-bearing.

They could not close the gap. That was the plain truth of the afternoon. Brann had the lead and the desperation and the light pack, and by the time the light went long and copper over the western ranges, the fleck had become a memory, and the Stair of Winds had risen out of the haze ahead of them like the spine of something sleeping.

Voss called the halt at the base of it, in the lee of a rock fin, and the men dropped where they stood, and he stood apart and looked up at the arête—the knife-edge, the famous, infamous Stair, the ridge so narrow that the wind itself seemed to have carved it, two thousand feet of exposure on either side, the lowlands of Kestria's hidden valleys falling away to the south and the gray void of the glacier to the north. Somewhere up there, in the next hours or the next day, a hermit and an archivist and a ferryman and a runaway girl and a shepherd boy would come over that edge with a chest full of names. And somewhere up there ahead of them, moving through the dark with an ice-axe and a knife and no rope, was Brann.

"Boss," Enk said quietly. "Orders?"

Voss looked at the five of them. He thought about what he could order and what he could ask and what, after tonight, he had no right to do at all. He thought about the page against his chest. *Disposition: unrecorded.* He thought: I spent forty years being the disposition.

"Here's how it is," he said. "The contract is void. I'm voiding it. Any man who wants to turn around and walk down to Ferrow and take his severance out of my own purse, he can do it now, and I'll shake his hand, and there'll be no word said about it after."

Nobody moved. The wind moved. Somewhere below them, the glacier groaned like a ship.

"Or," Voss said, "you come up the Stair with me. Not to take the chest. To make sure it gets where it's going. And after—" he almost laughed, and it came out as breath, "—after, I don't know what comes after. I've never not known before. It's not as bad as they say."

Enk looked at him for a long moment with the wind pulling at his hood. "The page you've been reading," the boy said. "The one you took off Brann. It had a name on it."

"It had my mother's name on it."

Another silence. Then Enk began, deliberately, methodically, to coil his rope for the climb, and the others followed, one by one, and Voss turned to the Stair of Winds and began to go up into the dark after his own man, to stop him from doing the thing that Voss himself had been sent to do, and he understood, with a clarity that felt almost like falling, that the mountain had finally won—that it had taken everything he was built to be and turned it inside out, and now the only thing left was to climb, faster, into the wind, before the snow came, before Brann reached the pass, before the first white flakes of the early storm—the storm the shepherd's elder had promised, the storm the hermit had read in the sky—came down out of the black and closed the Stair of Winds forever.

Halfway up the arête, with the void yawning on either side and his men strung out behind him on the rope, the first flake touched his cheek.

It did not feel like an ending. It felt like a hand, opening.
