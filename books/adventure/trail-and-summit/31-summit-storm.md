## 31. Summit Storm

The wind at the Stair of Winds did not blow so much as it *insisted*—a great hand pressing against Edmund's chest, against his wound, against every step, as if the mountain itself had been paid to stop him.

He climbed anyway.

The pass was close. He could feel it in the way the arête narrowed beneath his boots, in the way the air thinned to something almost holy, almost empty. Below them, the cloud deck churned like a white sea, and above them the sky was the color of old iron. Somewhere behind, Voss was hunting his own man through the storm. Somewhere ahead—

Ahead was the lieutenant.

Edmund saw him first as a shadow against the snow-blind glare: a dark figure standing on the knife-edge of the ridge where it widened into the summit col, where the chest was meant to go no further than a cairn and a prayer. The man had come up the eastern face alone, fast, reckless—Tam had spotted his tracks an hour ago and gone gray-faced with fear. Now here he was, waiting, one hand resting on the hatchet at his belt, snow crusting his shoulders like a second skin.

"Vane." The lieutenant's voice carried thin and strange across the wind, stripped of everything but intent. "Give me the chest."

"It's not mine to give," Edmund called back. His breath tore away sideways. "It never was."

The lieutenant laughed—a short, ugly sound. "You sound like Voss. All of you, talking about things that aren't yours. That chest is *property*, Vane. Property of my employer. And property burns."

He came forward along the ridge.

Edmund set down the climbing rope he'd been coiling and made himself stand still. His side throbbed where the lieutenant's own bullet had torn through him two days ago at the parley camp—the wound Ilse had packed with moss and bound with strips of her coat lining, the wound that had burned through him all night with fever while Maret fed him melted snow by the spoonful. He was half-healed, half-frozen, and armed with nothing but a ice axe and twelve years of stubbornness.

Behind him, Doss had the chest lashed to his back, and Tam was already pulling him toward the sheltered notch below the col. Good. Let them go. Let the chest climb the last hundred yards without him if it had to.

The lieutenant stopped ten feet away. Up close, Edmund could see the frost in the man's beard, the wildness in his eyes—the look of a man who had escaped confinement, crossed a glacier in a building storm, and climbed a knife-edge alone because he believed his master had gone soft and someone had to finish the work.

"You should have stayed dead, Vane," the lieutenant said. "A blacklisted man who keeps his head down lives a long time. I've seen it."

"I've seen it too," Edmund said. "It isn't living."

The hatchet came out of the belt.

There was no room for craft on the ridge. The arête was barely four feet wide, wind-scoured ice on both faces dropping away—a thousand feet on the north side into the cloud sea, eight hundred on the south onto blue glacier. Every footfall was a negotiation. When the lieutenant swung, Edmund didn't dodge; he *leaned*, letting the blade whisper past his ribs, and drove his shoulder into the man's chest. They staggered together, boots skidding, and for one horrible moment they were both sliding toward the northern edge, arms locked, breath mingling, before Edmund got his axe spike into the ice and arrested them both.

The wind screamed past them like something alive.

They broke apart. Circled. The lieutenant was bigger, heavier, fresher; Edmund could already feel his wound opening, warmth spreading wet and wrong beneath Ilse's bandages. But he had grown up on these ridges. He knew what the lieutenant did not: that the wind gusted in rhythms here, that it held its breath for three heartbeats between blows, that the mountain spoke if you listened.

The hatchet swung again. Edmund waited for the lull—*now*—stepped inside the arc, and hooked the lieutenant's wrist with his axe handle, twisting. The hatchet spun away into the void and was gone, swallowed white.

For a moment neither of them moved. Just two men breathing raggedly on a spine of stone, disarmed, the whole world reduced to wind and ice and the next mistake.

Then the lieutenant rushed him.

They went down together. The impact drove the air from Edmund's lungs; his wounded side exploded in white fire. They rolled, grappling, and the ridge tilted under them, and suddenly Edmund felt the edge beneath his shoulder blades—felt the drop open behind him like a mouth. The lieutenant was on top, gloved hands finding Edmund's throat, squeezing, his face contorted with something beyond orders now, something personal and desperate.

"*Burn,*" the lieutenant rasped. "You and your chest and your—"

Edmund got his thumb into the man's eye socket and pushed.

The lieutenant reared back with a howl, and Edmund bucked—and then they were both over the edge.

Not falling. *Hanging.* Edmund's right hand had caught a spur of rock frozen into the ridge crest; his left was clamped around the lieutenant's forearm, and the lieutenant's other hand was clawing at the ice, finding no purchase, his boots kicking against eight hundred feet of nothing. Below them the cloud deck waited, soft and absolute, like the lid of a coffin.

Edmund's wound screamed. His grip burned. The spur of rock groaned under the combined weight, shedding grains of ice into the abyss.

And in that suspended moment—swinging over the drop, holding the life of the man who had shot him—Edmund looked into the lieutenant's face and saw, with terrible clarity, that the man expected to die. Expected Edmund to let go. It was written there plainly, the arithmetic of their world: enemies on a cliff, one must fall, and it will be me.

"Pull," Edmund gritted out.

The lieutenant stared at him.

"PULL, damn you. Reach for the spur. *Now.*"

Something flickered across the man's face—confusion, disbelief, the collapse of a certainty he had carried his whole life about how this worked. His free hand scrabbled upward, found the spur beside Edmund's, gripped.

They hung there, three men's worth of weight on one dying hold, and Edmund felt the rock begin to give.

That was when the boot came down on the ridge above them.

Voss stood silhouetted against the iron sky, chest heaving, ice in his hair, having crossed half the range in pursuit of his own subordinate. For a long second he simply looked down—at Edmund hanging from the cliff, at the lieutenant dangling from Edmund's grip, at the arithmetic of it all.

Then he dropped to his knees, braced himself against the wind, and reached down with both hands.

"Not you," Voss said—not to Edmund, but to the lieutenant, hauling him up by the collar with a strength that seemed to come from somewhere older than the man himself. "Not today. Not for *him*."

The lieutenant came over the edge in a cascade of snow, collapsed onto the ridge, and lay there gasping like landed fish. Edmund hauled himself up after him, arms shaking, wound weeping, and knelt on the knife-edge with his head bowed, trying to remember how lungs worked.

"Why?" the lieutenant managed at last. He propped himself up on his elbows, staring at Voss with something like horror. "I'm your man. Twenty years. You'd choose *them*? A dead nation and a blacklisted smuggler?"

Voss stood over him. The wind pulled at his coat. And when he answered, his voice was quiet enough that Edmund had to strain to hear it through the storm.

"I read the registry page," Voss said. "Page forty-one. There's a name on it I've been carrying since I was six years old, in a school where they beat me for speaking it. My mother's name." He crouched, slowly, until he was eye level with his lieutenant. "You think I sent you to burn a chest. I sent you to burn the only proof that she ever existed. That any of us ever existed." He shook his head. "I was wrong. I've been wrong since the day they put me on that train. And I am not spending the rest of my life being wrong."

The lieutenant looked from Voss to Edmund and back again, and Edmund watched something break in the man's face—not surrender exactly, but the exhaustion of a man whose map no longer matched the territory.

"The employer—" the lieutenant started.

"Will learn what happened here from me," Voss said. "In writing. With my name on it." He rose, turned to Edmund, and extended a hand.

Edmund looked at it. The hand that had signed the orders, funded the hunt, torched his hut, hunted them across a nation that wasn't supposed to exist anymore. And beneath all of it, somewhere, the six-year-old on the train.

He took it.

Voss pulled him to his feet, and for a moment the two of them stood together on the spine of the Serpent Range, swaying in the wind, two men who had spent the whole story trying to kill each other, holding each other upright.

"The chest," Voss said. "Where is it?"

"Climbing," Edmund said. "With my people."

Above them, near the summit cairn, figures had stopped against the sky—Doss with the chest still lashed to his back, Ilse beside him, Maret and Tam flanking, all of them watching the ridge below with the expressions of people who had just witnessed something none of them would have believed an hour ago.

Ilse started down toward them. Then Maret. Then Tam, hesitant, and even Doss, moving carefully on the narrow ground.

Edmund stood in the storm, bleeding, freezing, laughing—a raw, cracked sound the wind nearly stole—and Voss looked at him as though he'd lost his mind.

"What," Voss said flatly, "is funny?"

"We're not going anywhere," Edmund said, gesturing weakly at the wall of cloud boiling up the southern face, thick and fast and final—the first true snow of the season, arriving hours early, sealing the pass behind them like a door swinging shut. "None of us. Not down, not back, not anywhere. Look at it."

Voss looked. And Edmund watched the understanding move across the enforcer's face like weather: the descent closed, the tribunal deadline passing on the far side of the mountain, the whole party—hunter and hunted alike—trapped together above the clouds until spring.

The lieutenant began, very quietly, to laugh too. It sounded almost like sobbing.

From above, Maret's voice came shrilling down through the wind, half terror and half triumph:

"EDMUND! THE SNOW! WHAT DO WE DO?"

Edmund turned his face up toward the summit, toward the small cluster of figures waiting there, toward the chest that had fallen out of a river into his life and refused, ever since, to let him go.

"We do," he called back, "what Kestrians do."

He picked up his ice axe and started climbing.
