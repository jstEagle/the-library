## 32. The First Snow

Ilse felt it before she saw it—a change in the pressure of the air, the way the wind coming down the pass had gone from sharp to soft, from dry to wet. She had spent eleven years in archives, but her father had taught her to read the mountains the way she read a registry: every sign a sentence, every silence a paragraph. The silence on the ridge above them had been building all morning, and now it broke.

"Snow," she said. Not a question. Not even a warning. Just the word, set down on the cold air like a stone on a ledger.

Edmund stopped ahead of her on the trail, one hand braced against the rock face, and turned his face upward. The sky over the pass had gone the color of wet slate, low and heavy, and the first flakes came down through it—fat, slow, unhurried, like things that had all the time in the world. Which, Ilse thought with a cold twist in her stomach, they did. They had all the winter in the world.

Behind her, Doss swore in three dialects. Maret said nothing at all, which from Maret was the loudest alarm there was.

They were still two hundred yards below the summit pass. Two hundred yards of switchback, and then the descent—the long descent, eleven days of trail down the southern face to the lowlands, to the tribunal hall at Vessany where forty years of erasure waited to be undone by a stamp and a signature. Ilse had rehearsed that moment ten thousand times. She had rehearsed the words she would say. She had rehearsed the weight of the chest coming to rest on the clerk's table. She had rehearsed, in her darker hours, the possibility that the tribunal would simply refuse—and she had armed herself against that too, with citations, with precedent, with her father's letters sewn into her coat lining.

She had never once rehearsed this: the deadline passing not because of refusal, not because of Voss, not because of any human enemy, but because the sky itself had decided the matter.

"Move," Edmund said. "All of you. Move now."

He took the lead and drove them up the switchbacks at a pace that tore the breath out of Ilse's chest. The snow thickened as they climbed, flakes going from fat to small and hard, the wind picking them up and hurling them sideways so that the world became a gray blur with the mountain's black rock flickering through it. Tam moved like he had been born on this slope, which Ilse supposed he had, and he kept a hand near Maret's pack strap the whole way up, steadying her when the wind shoved. Doss carried the chest. He had carried it since the Stair of Winds, lashed to his back like a hunchback's burden, and he climbed with it now as though it were the only thing keeping him upright.

Ilse counted the switchbacks the way she counted everything, because counting was the only prayer she had ever learned. Nine. Ten. Eleven. The pass was above them, a notch in the ridge line, and through the snow she could see—

She could see the far side.

It was white. All of it. The southern face, the eleven days of trail, the whole descent into the lowlands, erased. Not hidden—erased. The snow had come down the far slope in the night while they fought the wind on the northern side, and it had not dusted the mountain, it had buried it. There was no trail. There was no trail in the way that there is no word on a page that has been scraped clean.

They came over the pass at what her father's almanac would have called midday, though there was no telling it from the light, and stood in the notch with the storm screaming over them, and Ilse looked at the southern face and felt something in her chest go very quiet and very still.

"It's gone," Maret said. She had to shout it. "The trail's just—gone."

"It's not gone." Edmund's voice was flat, professional, the voice he used for facts that hurt. "It's under eight feet of snow and it'll shift three more times before spring. Nobody descends that face before the melt. Nobody sane."

"When's the melt?" Doss asked, though Ilse could see he already knew.

"Four months," Edmund said. "Maybe five."

The tribunal convened when the dry season ended. The dry season had ended that morning, in the only way that mattered, on a mountain ridge at eleven thousand feet. The tribunal would convene in nine days, and it would sit for the legal term, and it would rule on the question of whether the Kestrian people existed—and the proof that they existed would be standing on the wrong side of a mountain, sealed off by weather, watching the deadline blow past on the wind.

Ilse sat down in the snow.

She did not decide to sit down. Her legs simply ended their long argument with the rest of her and folded, and she was on her knees in the notch of the pass with snow driving into her face, and she heard herself make a sound that was not a word.

Eleven years. Eleven years of her father's letters, of the archive she had built in a cellar in Ferrow out of smuggled papers and copied names, of learning the old script letter by letter from a woman who had been beaten for teaching it. Eleven years of tracing the chest across half a continent, following rumors like a bloodhound, losing it, finding the trail again. Two months in these mountains. The Ironbridge. The chapel. The crevasse. Edmund's blood on the snow at Voss's camp, and Maret's blue lips on the glacier, and Doss's hands split open from digging Tam out of the avalanche, and Tam's grandfather's valley left behind with the old man standing in his doorway not saying the word *Kestria* out loud because he was afraid, because everyone was afraid, because fear was the only inheritance the annexation had left them—

And it had all come down to this. To a notch in a ridge. To snow.

"We could go down anyway," Tam was saying. "The old shepherds' route on the east rib—"

"In this?" Doss's voice cracked like the wind. "Boy, in this you can't see your own boots. The east rib's a cliff with delusions. We'd die to the last one and the chest would die with us, and in forty years some shepherd would find it and use it to patch a roof."

"We could wait out the storm," Maret said. "Storms pass. This one will pass, and then—"

"And then the next one," Edmund said. "And then the next. It's not a storm, Maret. It's winter. Winter doesn't pass. Winter *arrives*."

Ilse knelt in the snow and did the arithmetic she could not stop herself from doing, because the arithmetic was all she had left. The tribunal term: nine days. The earliest possible descent, if a miracle opened the mountain: four months. The difference between those two numbers was the whole of her life's work, and the difference was written in white, and there was no petition, no precedent, no citation in any archive in the world that could cross it.

Her father had died believing she would finish this. He had died with the map's margin sewn into his coat, with the confession he could never send, with the name of the man he had betrayed folded up small against his heart. She had promised him. Not out loud—she had never been able to say it out loud, even at the grave—but she had promised, the way you promise the dead, in the only language that binds.

And now the mountain had broken the promise for her, and there was no one to appeal to. The mountain did not have a tribunal.

The snow came down. Somewhere behind her, Doss was getting a fire going in the lee of the notch, his hands moving with the grim competence of a man who had decided that if the world had ended, the fire still needed building. Tam had gotten Maret under the overhang and was forcing the tea kit on her. The small machinery of survival went on around Ilse, indifferent to her, and she knelt in it and let the snow accumulate on her shoulders like a sentence being handed down.

She did not hear Edmund come over to her. She heard him laugh.

* * *

It was not a big sound. In that wind it should have been lost entirely, but it cut through the way a blade cuts through burlap, and every one of them turned. Ilse turned last, and what she saw frightened her more than the storm had.

Edmund Vane was standing at the lip of the pass with the snow streaming past him, looking down at the buried southern face, and he was laughing. His shoulders shook with it. His head was tipped back. It was not a bitter laugh, or a mad one, or the dry bark he used when Doss told a story that was too true to be funny. It was a laugh from somewhere deep and long sealed, a laugh that had been in him for twelve years with nowhere to go, and it came out of him now like water from a broken dam, and Ilse, who had catalogued every expression she had ever seen on his face the way she catalogued everything, had no entry for this one.

"Edmund," she said. "Edmund, what—"

He turned to her, and his eyes were streaming, and she could not tell at first whether it was the wind or something else, and then she could.

"We're free," he said.

"We're—" The word would not fit together with anything. "We're trapped. The tribunal sits in nine days. Everything—"

"Ilse." He crouched down in front of her, took her shoulders in his two big cracked hands. Snow was in his hair and his beard and his laugh was still in him, working its way out in pieces. "Listen to me. Listen. For twelve years I have been a man with one direction. South. Down. Away. Every morning I woke up and the first thing I did was count the miles between me and everything. You know what I did when I found the chest? I wanted to burn it. Not because of the blacklisters. Because it was a direction. Because it was *them*, reaching up my mountain, into my hut, into my one room with no miles in it."

"Edmund—"

"Let me finish. I have been counting miles for twelve years. Every trail, every border, every checkpoint—I counted. And you know what I've been doing since the chapel? Since the letter? I've been counting again. Counting the days to the tribunal. Counting the passes between us and Vessany. Counting down to the moment I could put this chest on a table and walk away from it, from all of you, back up to my hut—" he stopped, and something crossed his face. "My hut's ash. I keep forgetting. Back to a hut. Back to a room with no one in it. And I would have done it. Ilse, I would have stood in that tribunal hall, I would have seen them hand you the registry, I would have watched you win—and I would have walked out the door and started counting miles again before I reached the street. That was the plan. That was the only plan I had."

The wind screamed over the notch. Below them the southern face lay white and silent and closed.

"And now," Edmund said, and his voice did something she had never heard it do—broke open, the way ice breaks on a river in spring, all at once and all along its length, "now the mountain has taken the door away. There's no door. There's no miles. There's no south. The snow closed the pass and I'm standing here and for the first time in twelve years—" he laughed again, shorter, wetter, "—for the first time in twelve years, nobody is going anywhere. We're all going to be here. All winter. All of us. Together. And I didn't choose it, Ilse. I would never have chosen it. The mountain chose it for me, and I have never in my life been so grateful to anything."

Ilse stared at him. Around them the others had gone still—Doss with a log in his hands, Maret with the tea kit open at her feet, Tam half-risen from the overhang. The storm filled the world, and in the middle of it Edmund Vane knelt in the snow with his hands on her shoulders and his face wrecked and open, and Ilse felt the arithmetic in her chest—the terrible arithmetic, the nine days and the four months, the difference that was her whole life's work—begin, very slowly, to come apart.

"It's not liberation," she said, but her voice shook. "It's defeat. The tribunal will rule without the registry. They'll rule that we don't exist, Edmund. Forty more years of—"

"Will they?"

"What?"

"Will they rule that?" He was looking at her with that cartographer's attention now, the attention that read terrain the way she read text. "You've told me what the chest is. A registry. Proof. But you've also told me what it isn't. It isn't the people. You said it yourself, at the chapel—your father's maps weren't the mountains. They were a way of loving the mountains. The tribunal can rule on documents. Can it rule on three hundred people in a hidden valley who sing songs in a language the empire banned? Can it rule on a girl who learned the old script in secret? On an elder who won't say the word out loud because saying it would mean admitting how much he's lost?" He shook his head. "The chest was never going to convince the tribunal, Ilse. You were. The chest just gave you the right to stand up and speak. And you'll still have that in the spring."

"The spring," she whispered. "The tribunal term ends in the spring. If we miss this term—"

"Then there's another one. There's always another one. That's what a law is, Ilse—it's a thing that meets again. You taught me that. But winter doesn't meet again. Winter is here, now, and it's the only winter these people—" he gestured, at Doss, at Maret, at Tam, at himself, "—have ever been given. We've been running since the hut burned. Since before the hut burned. Running and counting and losing sleep over what's behind us and what's ahead. The mountain just said: enough. Sit down. Be where you are. And I am—" he pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes for a moment, and when he took them away he was smiling, actually smiling, and Ilse thought she might be dreaming, "—I am going to obey the mountain. For once in my life, I'm going to obey something, and it's going to be the weather."

Doss set the log down very carefully, as though it might go off.

"So that's it," he said. "We're snowed in. On a pass. In a notch. With tea for six days."

"We're not staying in the notch," Tam said.

They all turned. The boy had come out of the overhang, and the snow whirled around him, and he stood in it like it was a thing his family owned.

"My grandfather told me," Tam said. "Before we left. He made me swear I wouldn't say it unless—" he stopped, and Ilse watched him decide, watched the fear and the inheritance fight it out behind his eyes, watched the inheritance win for the first time. "Unless the pass closed. He said, if the pass closes and you're on the far side, there's a place. High valley. North and west, three days, above the cloud line. My grandfather's grandmother was born there. There are others. He said there are others."

"How many others?" Edmund asked quietly.

"I don't know. He said enough that they stopped counting strangers a long time ago."

Ilse got to her feet. Her knees ached and the snow had soaked through to her skin and none of it mattered, because her mind—the archivist's mind, the mind that had kept her alive through eleven years of cellar work and two months of mountains—had seized on the words and was turning them over, and underneath the despair something new was stirring, something that felt dangerously like a different kind of hope.

"A settlement," she said. "Unregistered. Off every map. Surviving the erasure not by hiding from it but by hiding *inside* it." She looked at Edmund. "If there are Kestrians up there—families, births, deaths, a whole community with forty years of continuous memory—"

"Then the registry isn't the only proof," Edmund said. "The registry is paper. They're people. Paper can burn." He glanced at the chest on Doss's back, and something passed over his face—not grief, not anymore; something settled and sure. "People have to be erased one at a time, and they've had forty years to fail at it."

The wind came over the pass in a long white gust, and Ilse stood in it and felt the deadline blow past her like a leaf—felt the tribunal convening in nine days in a warm hall in Vessany, felt the clerks unrolling the docket, felt the moment when the chief justice would ask whether any claimant stood forward and the silence would answer—and she found, to her astonishment, that the image did not break her. It hurt. It hurt like a hand closing on her heart. But underneath the hurt was her father's voice, the voice from the letters, the voice that had written *a map is not the mountain, my girl; love the mountain*—and underneath the hurt was Edmund's laugh, still hanging in the storm like something the wind couldn't take.

"Three days," she said. "North and west. Above the cloud line." She turned to Tam. "Can you find it in this?"

Tam looked up at the white sky, and for a moment he was afraid again—she saw it, she let him have it—and then he straightened.

"My grandfather's grandmother is buried there," he said. "I've been finding my way to her my whole life. I just didn't know it."

Edmund shouldered his pack. Doss lashed the chest tighter. Maret was already packing the tea kit with the fierce efficiency of a girl who had learned on this trail that grief and work could share the same pair of hands. And Ilse took one last look back down the pass—at the white southern face, at the closed door, at the term she would miss and the ruling that would fall in their absence—and she let it go. Not forever. She was an archivist; she let nothing go forever. But for the winter, for the first time in eleven years, she set the deadline down in the snow and walked away from it, north and west, into the storm, toward a valley full of people who had never stopped existing just because no one had written them down.

Behind them, the first snow kept falling on the pass, filling in their tracks, closing the mountain like a book.

Somewhere ahead, a whole nation was waiting to be read.
