## 33. The High Kestrians

Tam had sworn an oath of silence at nine years old, kneeling on cold stone while his grandmother pressed her thumb to his forehead and said the words that mattered: *You have no name. You are no one. That is how you live.*

He had kept the oath for eleven years. He had lied to shepherds from the low valleys, lied to tax wardens who wandered too high, lied to his own cousins when they asked why they couldn't sing certain songs above a whisper. He had even lied, in a way, to himself—had learned to think of the valley as simply *home*, without asking why home had no name on any map, why the trail in was hidden behind a waterfall curtain and a slot of rock you could miss if the light was wrong.

Now he was about to break the oath with every step he took.

He glanced back at them strung out along the ledge: the archivist Ilse with the chest strapped to her back like a child refusing to be put down; Edmund Vane walking with the stiff, careful gait of a man whose wound had closed but not forgiven him; Doss the ferryman carrying half their gear and all of their jokes; Maret, sixteen and sharp-eyed, watching everything; and somewhere far below, Voss—a man who had hunted them across a mountain range and then turned his gun around—following their trail at a day's distance, alone, untrusted, unexplained.

"You're sure about this?" Doss asked, for the fourth time since dawn.

"No," Tam said honestly. "But my grandmother sent me to find you, and my grandmother doesn't send anyone anywhere without a reason."

"She sent you before the avalanche," Maret pointed out. "Before Voss turned. Before—" She gestured vaguely at all of it: the storm, the pass, the fight on the knife-edge where Edmund had nearly died twice over.

"Grandmothers see weather coming," Tam said. "All kinds."

The waterfall came first, a white thread falling two hundred feet down a face of black stone, and Tam watched them understand what he was showing them. He stepped off the main trail onto a shelf barely wider than his boot, walked behind the falling water into a cold roar and sudden dimness, and emerged through the slot of rock on the other side. When they joined him, dripping and blinking, the sound of the world changed—the waterfall fell away behind them, and ahead there was only wind, and grass, and silence.

And then the valley opened.

Tam never tired of this moment, though he'd seen it a hundred times. The slot canyon spat you out onto a high shoulder of scree, and below, cupped between two ridgelines like water in a stone bowl, lay the valley: terraced fields stitched up the lower slopes, dry-stone walls silvered with lichen, a stream braiding through the middle, smoke rising from maybe sixty chimneys. Stone houses with turf roofs. A chapel with a bell tower—unbelled, the bell buried forty years ago under the chapel floor, because bells could be heard. Goats on the high pasture. Children, tiny at this distance, running along a wall.

Three hundred people. Give or take. Nobody counted anymore, because counting invited questions.

Maret made a sound that wasn't quite a word.

"It's not on any map," Tam said. "It's not on any census. As far as the lowlands know, this is uninhabitable ground above the treeline. We farm what we can, we trade wool down-mountain once a year through a middleman who thinks we're a single extended family of eccentric highland shepherds, and we bury our dead ourselves." He paused. "We don't sing the old songs outside. We don't teach the old script except in secret, after dark, to children who've sworn the oath. My grandmother taught me letters by candlelight with a blanket over the window."

"Why?" Edmund asked quietly. It was the first thing he'd said in an hour. His voice still carried the rasp of the fever night. "Forty years is a long time to hide. The annexation ended. The reprisals—"

"The reprisals didn't end," Tam said. "They just got quieter." He looked at Edmund, at the blacklisted cartographer who had spent twelve years learning exactly the same lesson in exactly the same way. "You know how it works. They don't need to hunt you if you hunt yourself. If every family teaches its children to be afraid, the fear does the work for free. My grandmother says erasure isn't something they do to you once. It's something you agree to keep doing, every morning, forever." He shouldered his pack. "Come on. They'll have seen us by now. And I need to be standing next to you when my grandmother decides whether you're guests or problems."

* * *

They came down the shoulder of scree and were met, as Tam knew they would be, halfway.

It was always the same six: the men and women who formed the valley's unofficial council, armed with hunting bows and the flat, unimpressed expressions of people who had survived forty years by taking nothing on faith. Old Sefa with her crook. The twins, Bran and Boran, identical and silent. Young Halli, fastest runner in the valley, already breathing hard from the climb. And in front, small and straight-backed and eighty-one years old, leaning on a stick of blackthorn, Tam's grandmother.

Yeva Brenner looked at her grandson first. Her eyes were pale gray, nearly colorless, and they missed nothing.

"You brought strangers through the door," she said.

"I brought *Kestrians* through the door," Tam said. "That's what I went to find."

"That's what you *believe* you went to find." She turned her gaze on the others, one by one, and Tam saw them take it—saw Ilse straighten, saw Doss shift his weight, saw Maret lift her chin. "Names," Yeva said. "Real ones. Not the ones you wear in the low country."

"Ilse Brenner," the archivist said. "Archivist of the Kestrian registry-in-exile. Daughter of Aldric Brenner."

Something moved in the old woman's face, quick as a fish under ice. "Brenner," she repeated. "There were Brenners in the archive service. Before."

"My father. His mother before him."

"And him?" Yeva's stick swung toward Edmund.

Edmund Vane did not flinch. Tam had watched him walk into a mercenary camp unarmed, had watched him fight on a knife-edge with a bullet-torn shoulder, and he had thought he understood the man's particular kind of courage. But now he saw something else underneath it—the courage of a man standing in front of a judge.

"Edmund Vane," he said. "Son of Tomas Vane. Cartographer to the crown of Kestria."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the wind to change direction.

Tam heard it happen before he understood it: a murmur running through the council members, Sefa crossing herself with the old sign, the wrong-way sign, the sign the lowland priests had forbidden. Halli saying, under his breath, "*Vane*," the way you'd say the name of a mountain.

"Tomas Vane drew the maps that hid this valley," Yeva said at last. Her voice had gone very quiet. "When the annexation forces swept the ranges, there were three villages that vanished from the record overnight. Rivers moved. Passes closed that weren't closed. Whole watersheds redrew themselves in ink." Her colorless eyes fixed on Edmund. "Every child here grows up on the story of the mapmaker who lied to an empire so that three hundred people could keep breathing. You're telling me you're his son."

"I'm telling you I didn't know until two months ago," Edmund said. "I'm telling you he abandoned me at seven years old and I hated him for twenty-five years, and I'm still deciding what to do with the rest of it." He glanced at Ilse. "And I'm telling you we brought something that belongs to you. To all of you. If you'll have it."

Yeva looked at the chest on Ilse's back for a long moment.

"Not here," she said. "Not on open ground. Come down. And grandson—" the stick touched Tam's chest, gently, "—you broke an oath today. We'll discuss what that costs later."

"Yes, Grandmother," Tam said, and discovered his heart was pounding.

* * *

They came down into the valley as the light went long and gold, and Tam watched his home through their eyes for the first time in his life.

He saw Maret cataloguing everything—the script carved over a doorway, worn almost smooth, the old blessing for hearths that Tam had been taught to read and taught never to acknowledge. He saw Doss looking at the dry-stone walls and muttering calculations about the stream, the ferryman's instinct for water running deep even here. He saw Ilse walking slowly, turning her head constantly, an archivist trying to drink a whole civilization through her eyes before the light failed.

And he saw the people come out of their houses.

They always did, when strangers came—which was rare enough that Tam could remember every previous occurrence, each one a shepherd or trader escorted in blindfolded and escorted out the same way. But this time the council hadn't blindfolded anyone, and word ran ahead of them down the lanes faster than they walked, and by the time they reached the square by the buried bell, there were a hundred people in the street, and more pouring out.

Tam heard the whispers.

"*Vane.* The mapmaker's son."

"They say he carried the chest over the Stair of Winds."

"They say the collector's own man turned and fought for it."

"They say—" An old man's voice, cracked and disbelieving: "They say the registry survived. They say the *registry survived*."

Tam stopped in the square and turned to Ilse. "Open it," he said. "Here. Now. Before my grandmother finds a reason to wait."

Ilse looked at him—this boy who had guided them, argued with them, nearly led them onto a crevasse field—and something in her face softened. She knelt, right there in the dirt of the square, and unbuckled the straps, and lifted the lid of the chest that had crossed a mountain range and a war's worth of history to arrive in this place.

She took out the registry.

Tam had seen it before, in candlelight in Edmund's ruined hut: a thick ledger bound in blue leather gone gray with age, its corners scarred, its spine re-sewn twice. But he had never seen it held up in front of three hundred people in the open air, in daylight, in the square where he'd learned to walk.

"The Registry of the Kestrian People," Ilse said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she steadied it and began again. "Compiled in the last year before the annexation. Every registered citizen of Kestria. Every birth, every household, every name the empire tried to erase." She opened it. "It survived. It has survived for forty years. And it is going to the tribunal in the spring, and when it gets there, every person descended from these pages will exist again—in law, in record, on maps."

Nobody cheered. Tam had expected cheering, or weeping, or both. Instead there was a silence so complete he could hear the goats on the hillside, and in the silence an old woman near the front—Sefa's sister, he thought—said, in a voice like a child's:

"Read some names."

So Ilse read names.

She read them slowly, letting each one stand in the air. Family names that Tam had heard only in fragments, in whispered songs, in the mangled versions his grandmother used when she forgot herself and sang. Names of households scattered or destroyed. And then, partway down the third page, Ilse stopped. Looked up. Looked at Yeva Brenner.

"Brenner, Yeva," she read. "Archive service, junior registrar. Husband, Dannel. Son, Aldric." She looked at the old woman. "You're in here. You've been in here for forty years. Registered. Counted. Never erased—not really. Just waiting."

Yeva Brenner stood very still. Then she walked forward, took the ledger from Ilse's hands with a gentleness Tam had never seen her use with anything, and held it the way you hold a sleeping infant.

"Read the rest," she said, and her voice was not steady at all. "Read all of them. Every name. Out loud. Tonight, by firelight, in the square. Forty years we've been nobody. Tonight we're somebody again, even if it's only on paper." She looked around at her people, at the crowd pressing close, at faces wet in the last of the light. "Paper is where it starts. My father used to say it. Paper is where everything starts."

* * *

They read names until midnight.

Tam sat on the chapel steps with Maret beside him, listening to Ilse and his grandmother trade the reading between them—Ilse's trained archivist's voice, Yeva's cracked old one, alternating, the ledger passing back and forth like a relay baton. Around the fires, people wept openly, which Tam had never seen in his life. His people did not weep in public. His people did not *have* a public. But tonight the square was a public, and the fear that had governed every hour of his childhood sat in a corner of it, ignored, like an old dog no one remembered to feed.

"Your grandmother's in the book," Maret said. "Is your grandfather?"

"Dannel Brenner. Died the winter I was four. Avalanche on the north wall." Tam nodded toward the ledger. "He's in there. So is my father. So am I—look, page ninety. *Brenner, Tam. Born year thirty-six of exile.* Somebody kept recording. All these years, somebody kept a shadow-registry, births and deaths, hidden, waiting for the real one to come back." He shook his head slowly. "I grew up thinking the chest was a story. A thing grandmothers tell children so they don't feel small. And it was sitting in a river two valleys over, and a stranger pulled it out with his bare hands."

"Not a stranger," Maret said. "A Vane."

"A Vane," Tam agreed. He watched Edmund across the square, sitting slightly apart as he always did, one arm cradling his healing shoulder, listening to the names with an expression Tam couldn't read. "You know what's strange? He looks like a man at a funeral."

"He lost his hut. Nearly lost his life. Found out his whole identity was a lie and then found out it wasn't." Maret hugged her knees. "I think he's at a funeral. For the version of himself that was nobody." She was quiet a moment. "I liked that version of him. The nobody version. He let me steal his boots."

"He let you *what*?"

"Long story. Ask him sometime. Watch his face."

Above them, the stars came out hard and cold, the way they did above the treeline, and the reading went on. Somewhere past midnight, Yeva Brenner closed the ledger, held it against her chest, and spoke the words that Tam would carry for the rest of his life:

"For forty years we told our children they were no one, so that no one would find them. We erased ourselves to survive the erasure." She looked around the square, at the fires, at the faces. "No more. Whatever comes down that mountain in the spring—soldiers, tax men, the tribunal itself—they will find three hundred people who know their own names. Let the empire practice being afraid for a change."

The square did cheer then. Tam cheered with them, hoarse and half-embarrassed, and caught Maret grinning at him, and found himself grinning back.

* * *

It was near dawn when his grandmother summoned him to her house—the stone one at the valley's head with the turf roof and the blanket permanently over the window of the back room. Inside, the ledger lay open on the table, and Yeva stood over it with a candle, and her face was terrible.

"You said the archivist's father was Aldric Brenner," she said.

"Yes."

"And the mapmaker's son is Edmund Vane."

"Yes, Grandmother."

She turned the ledger around so he could see the page. Her finger, spotted and steady, rested beneath a line of the old script—the secret script, the candlelight script, the script written in this very room.

"Then read this," she said, "and tell me what you see."

Tam bent over the page. It was an entry in the shadow-registry, dated the winter of the annexation, in handwriting he recognized because he'd copied it a hundred times in his lessons—his grandmother's hand, forty years younger. A marginal note, the kind registrars made, the kind that was never meant to be read aloud.

He read it once. Then again. Then a third time, because the words refused to stay still.

*Vane, Tomas — cartographer — entered the valley by night, alone, carrying the sealed registry. Said the chest must go where no map leads. Refused shelter. Refused food. Asked only that the valley never be recorded. Then he wept, and left before dawn.*

Tam looked up. His grandmother's eyes were bright and hard as ice.

"The man who hid this valley," Yeva said, "and the man who abandoned his son—and the man who, if the archivist's papers are true, betrayed his king to save his pregnant wife—are the same man. And he stood in this room, boy. He stood where you're standing, and he chose three hundred strangers over his own child, and he never told anyone why." She blew out the candle. "In spring, when the tribunal comes, someone in this valley is going to have to decide what to do with that. And I'm eighty-one years old, and my hands shake, and I think it's going to be you."
