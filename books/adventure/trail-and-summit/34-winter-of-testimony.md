## 34. Winter of Testimony

The snow came down for three days without stopping, and on the fourth morning the valley woke to a silence so complete that Ilse stood in the doorway of the longhouse and simply listened to it. No wind. No river-sound—the stream had gone under its ice. Just the white weight of winter pressing down on the high Kestrians' hidden world, and beneath that silence, faintly, the sound of people beginning their day: an axe against wood, a child laughing somewhere near the byres, the low murmur of voices in a language the conquerors had spent forty years trying to kill.

She had heard that murmur before, in other places—refugee settlements, border towns, her father's cramped archive rooms where old men came to whisper names they hadn't spoken aloud since the annexation. But she had never heard it like this. Here it wasn't whispered. Here it was simply the sound of morning.

"You're going to freeze standing there," Tam said behind her, handing her a mug of something hot and bitter made from pine needles and barley. "Grandmother says if you're waiting for the weather to ask permission, you'll wait until spring."

"I'm not waiting." She wrapped her hands around the mug. Steam curled up past her face. "I'm listening."

"To what?"

"To how many of them there are." She nodded toward the cluster of houses below, smoke rising from thirty chimneys, maybe more, half-buried in white. "Three hundred people, Tam. Three hundred people who have been legally dead for forty years. Every one of them has a name. Every one has a story. And every one of those stories is evidence."

Tam followed her gaze. He was nineteen and had the particular stillness of someone raised in a valley that survived by not being seen. "You want to write them all down."

"I want to *save* them all down," Ilse said. "There's a difference."

* * *

They converted the eastern end of the longhouse into what Edmund, with his dry mountain humor, called the counting house—a joke about miles that only Maret laughed at, and then everyone did, because by midwinter they were all fluent in each other's jokes. Long trestle tables. Lamps fueled with rendered mutton fat. Paper was the problem; paper was always the problem, and Ilse rationed her remaining stock like a miser until Doss solved it by trading three weeks of ferry-work at the frozen crossing for a sledge-load of birchbark sheets from a trapper two valleys over. Birchbark took ink badly and held it forever, which Ilse decided was exactly the right trade.

The work began with the oldest woman in the valley, whose name was Sera Venn and who was ninety-one years old and had been born, she announced, "in Kestria, whatever your maps say now."

Ilse sat across from her with a pen and asked the first question, the one her father had taught her to ask, the one that unlocked everything else:

"What is your full name? All of it. The name you were given, and the name you were made to take, and any name between."

Sera Venn's eyes went bright and hard. "Given: Sera, daughter of Ollen Venn, of the line of Venn, keepers of the western terraces since before the annexation, since before your grandfather's grandfather. Made to take: Sera Holm, citizen of the Protectorate, registered under decree eleven, year one." She folded her hands. "Between: I called myself Sera Venn every single night when I lay down to sleep, and no decree ever reached me there."

Ilse wrote it all down. Her hand shook slightly on the last line, and she let it shake. That was testimony too.

By the second week there was a rhythm to it. Mornings, the elders came—those who could walk through the snow, carried on sledges by their grandchildren if they couldn't—and gave their names, their parentages, their births and marriages and losses. Afternoons, the middle generation: farmers and weavers and a man who had once, astonishingly, been a customs officer in the Protectorate's own service, smuggling Kestrian birth records inside tax ledgers for twenty years out of what he described as "spite, mostly." Evenings, anyone who wanted to sing.

The singing was Maret's idea.

"It's evidence," she insisted, when Ilse raised an eyebrow. "You said yourself—songs are records. They have dates in them. Places. Names of dead people nobody else remembers. The lullaby my mother used to sing had a verse about a bridge, and I never knew what bridge, but if it's in a song it can be found."

Ilse looked at this girl—sixteen years old, runaway, half-feral when they'd found her in Ferrow, who had taught herself the old script from a stolen primer and read it better now than Ilse's own dead father had—and felt the particular ache of being out-argued by someone you'd helped raise.

"Record them," she said. "All of them. But Maret—if you transcribe a single note wrong, I will know, and I will make you do it again."

Maret grinned. "That's what Uncle Edmund said about contour lines."

"He's right too."

"We know," Maret said solemnly. "It's very annoying."

* * *

Doss built the schoolhouse in the way he did everything: slowly, stubbornly, and with an air of profound grievance that fooled absolutely no one.

He had claimed a collapsed granary at the valley's edge in the first week—"needs a roof, needs walls, needs everything, perfect"—and by the time the deep snows came he had it framed in new-cut timber, chinked with moss and clay, with a stone hearth at one end and benches split from a single wind-felled pine. He worked alone mostly, though children drifted out to watch him, and he grumbled at them, and they stayed anyway, and eventually they were holding things for him, and eventually they were learning things from him without either party acknowledging it.

Ilse came out one gray afternoon to find him teaching six children to knot rope, his huge hands guiding small ones through the loops, his voice uncharacteristically gentle.

"That's a ferryman's knot," he was saying. "My father showed me. It holds when everything else fails, and it comes loose the moment you ask it to. Remember that. It's a good rule for more than knots."

"Doss," Ilse said, when the children had run off to throw snow at each other, "you've built a school."

"I've built a shed."

"There are slate frames on the wall."

"For drying fish."

"They have letters chalked on them."

"Fishing instructions."

"'The quick brown fox,'" Ilse read, "'jumps over the lazy dog.'"

Doss's ears went red. "Foxes are a problem here," he said. "Ask anyone."

She laughed, and he glowered, and she hugged him around the middle—which he endured the way a boulder endures weather—and left him to his fish-drying instructions. Behind her, she heard him pick up the chalk again.

* * *

Edmund mapped the valley.

She noticed it first as a change in him rather than a project: he stopped walking the perimeter like a sentry and started walking it like a surveyor. He would go out at first light with a board strapped to his chest and a plumb line and a compass, and stand on ridgelines in the cold for hours, motionless, reading the land the way other men read letters. In the evenings he sat by the fire transferring his notes onto birchbark sheets pinned flat, and Ilse would sit across from him doing her transcription, and for whole hours neither of them spoke, and it was the easiest silence she had ever shared with anyone.

One night she leaned over and looked properly at what he was drawing, and her breath caught.

The lettering. The precise, upright capitals. The little conventions—the way watercourses were doubled lines with tick marks, the way elevation was shown in stacked contours feathered at the edges, the tiny stylized pines marching along slopes. She knew this hand. She had spent ten years of her life in archives, and she knew this hand the way a priest knows scripture.

"That's his style," she said quietly. "Your father's. That's the royal cartographic hand."

Edmund didn't look up. His pen moved steadily along a contour. "It's just how maps are drawn."

"No, it isn't. It's how *he* drew them. I've handled four originals. The tick marks on the watercourses—that's his signature convention. He used it on the survey of the northern passes in the last year of the kingdom." She touched the edge of the sheet, careful not to smudge. "Edmund. You never saw his work. You told me you were nine when he left."

"I didn't need to see it." Now he looked up, and the firelight moved in his face. "He talked to me about it. For nine years, that was most of what he talked about. How a map should tell the truth twice—once in the lines and once in what the lines refuse to say. How you never draw a ridge you haven't walked. How the tick marks go downstream, always, because water tells you where it's going even when people won't." He looked back down at the sheet. "Turns out I kept all of it. Forty years of his habits, stored in a man who swore he'd forgotten him."

Ilse said nothing for a moment. Then: "May I see the finished ones? When they're done?"

"Why?"

"Because," she said, "when the tribunal asks us to prove that Kestrian knowledge survived—not just bloodlines, not just documents, but *knowledge*, craft, continuity—they will want artifacts. And there is no artifact on earth like a map drawn in the royal hand, of a living community, dated this winter. You're not making a map, Edmund. You're making a relic."

He was quiet a long time. Snow ticked softly against the shutter.

"I'll finish the southern terraces tomorrow," he said finally, which was, from Edmund, a surrender complete enough to sign.

* * *

The testimony accumulated. Ilse kept the birchbark sheets in oilcloth bundles, numbered in sequence, and each night she read back over the day's record before sleeping, and each night she found herself weeping a little, or wanting to.

A woman named Petra who had given birth in a snowdrift during the flight from the lowlands and named her daughter Kestria, and had watched the girl grow up answering to a false name, and had died last spring without hearing it spoken aloud in public once.

An old man who recited, from memory, the entire roll of the valley's dead—forty-one names, forty-one dates, forty-one causes, delivered in a flat voice while his granddaughter held his hand—and when he finished he said, "Now they exist somewhere besides me," and Ilse wrote at the bottom of the page, in her own hand: *They exist here. Witnessed.*

Children's testimonies, taken gently: what songs they knew, what words their grandmothers used for snow (the valley had eleven words, and Ilse recorded every one), what they had been told about why they lived up here. One boy, seven years old, answered that last question with devastating simplicity: "Because down there they said we weren't anybody." Ilse wrote it down verbatim. Some sentences you did not improve.

And through all of it, the registry from the chest grew alongside the fresh testimony, cross-referenced, verified, the old proof and the living proof stitching themselves together page by page. Families in the valley found their names in the chest's registry and went silent with shock—an old woman touching her own maiden name written in faded ink, saying over and over, "I thought I dreamed that name. I thought I dreamed it."

"You didn't dream it," Ilse told her. "Someone wrote it down so you wouldn't have to remember it alone."

* * *

Midwinter came, and with it the festival the valley had kept in secret for forty years—the Night of Names, when families had traditionally spoken their true names aloud behind closed shutters, one night a year, a crime committed in whispers.

This year, Tam's grandmother stood up in the longhouse before the whole community and said, "This year we do it differently. This year we do it in front of the archivist, and she writes it down, and next year—" her voice cracked, steadied— "next year maybe we say our names wherever we damn well please."

They went around the room, household by household, and spoke their names. Given names, hidden names, the names between. Three hundred people, and it took until nearly dawn, and Ilse wrote until her fingers cramped and Maret took the pen from her and wrote the last fifty entries herself in her fierce, beautiful script, and Doss stood by the door the whole night with his arms crossed like a guard, though there was nothing to guard against but the dark, and Edmund sat apart with his maps and listened to every name with his eyes closed, memorizing, the way cartographers memorize terrain.

At the very end, Tam's grandmother turned to the five of them—the strangers who had come over the ice and stayed.

"And yours?" she said. "What do we call you?"

Ilse opened her mouth to give her professional title, and found she didn't want it.

"Brenner," she said. "Ilse Brenner. Daughter of Aldric Brenner, archivist of Kestria, who died carrying this work. And—" she hesitated, then committed— "friend of this valley, if you'll have it so."

"Friend," the grandmother repeated, tasting it, and wrote it in the air with one finger, an old gesture, sealing it.

Maret gave her name proudly and added, "Apprentice archivist," which no one had authorized and everyone applauded. Doss muttered his name and "ferryman," and after a pause, "schoolmaster," and went red again. Tam said his name and "guide," and looked at Edmund when he said it.

Edmund was last. He stood, and the room waited, and Ilse watched him search himself the way he searched a mountainside—for the true line, the honest route.

"Vane," he said. "Edmund Vane. Son of Aldous Vane, royal cartographer of Kestria." A breath. "And I don't know what I am yet. Ask me in spring."

The grandmother smiled. "We'll write 'ask him in spring,'" she said. "It'll hold."

* * *

Late in the winter, on a night of hard clear cold with the stars sharp as nailheads, Ilse found Edmund out on the packed snow behind the longhouse, looking south toward the pass that had closed behind them months ago. She stood beside him. Their breath rose together and hung.

"The courier route opens with the melt," she said. "Six weeks, maybe less. The registry goes down to the tribunal, and then we wait, and then—" She stopped. "You've been quiet lately. Quieter than usual, which I didn't think was possible."

"I'm thinking about what happens after." He didn't look at her. "If it goes well. If they rule for us. There'll be a delegation, clerks, soldiers maybe, flags. Everything this valley hid from for forty years, walking up the trail in daylight." He scuffed the snow. "Some of them will want that. Some of them will be terrified. And some of them—" his voice dropped— "will look at me and ask why the son of Aldous Vane gets to stand among them, when his father's signature is on the order that sold the kingdom."

Ilse was silent. She had known this was coming; she had seen him arrive at it alone, night by night, the way he arrived at everything.

"Your father was coerced to save your pregnant mother," she said. "We both know the letter now. We've had all winter to know it."

"A tribunal may not care."

"A tribunal reads evidence," Ilse said. "But a *people* decides who belongs. And I have watched these people all winter, Edmund. I watched them feed you, teach you their words for snow, put your name in their Night of Names. They decided months ago. Whatever the tribunal says about the past—" she took his arm, feeling the solid warmth of him through the wool, "—the future is already signed."

Below them, light spilled from the schoolhouse windows where Doss was running a late lesson, and Maret's voice rose above the wind, reciting the alphabet of a nation that officially did not exist, in a valley that officially was not there, to children who officially had never been born.

Edmund listened to it for a long moment.

"Spring," he said at last. "Ask me in spring."

But he was smiling when he said it, and far to the south, beyond the shoulder of the mountain, Ilse saw—or thought she saw, or perhaps only hoped—a flicker of orange against the white: a signal fire, burning on a ridge where no one in this valley had reason to be.

She told herself it was a trick of starlight.

She wrote the date in her journal anyway.
