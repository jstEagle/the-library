## 35. Voss Among Them

The cold found him first, then the dark, and last of all the shame.

Voss had walked out of Corvane's offices in the capital with nothing but the clothes he stood in, a letter of dismissal sealed in wax he was not permitted to break, and eleven days of provisions he'd bought with the last of his own coin. The letter said *services no longer required*. It did not say *you are a dead man if you return*. It didn't need to. Voss had spent thirty years reading what powerful men declined to write down.

So he had gone up. Not toward the tribunal cities where a man with his record might buy passage or find work, but up—into the Serpent Range, following the route he had once paid a range warden to betray, climbing the very passes he had tried to seal shut behind five people and a chest.

He told himself it was strategy. That the high country was the one place Corvane's reach couldn't follow. That he would wait out the winter in some abandoned shepherd's hut and decide in spring whether to disappear south or die north. He told himself these things because the alternative explanation—that he was walking toward the people he had hunted—was not an explanation a man like Voss could carry and still put one foot above the other.

On the ninth day the storm took his tent. On the tenth it took his left boot's sole, torn loose on scree, so that he wrapped the boot in rope and walked on a lurching platform of hemp and leather. On the eleventh day, half-blind with snow, he crested a shoulder of granite he recognized from a warden's map—a map he had bribed for, marked in red ink—and saw smoke rising from a valley below.

Not one fire. Dozens. A whole valley breathing warmth into the white.

He knew what lived there. He had spent a season trying to burn its future in a river gorge and on a knife-edge pass. Every rational instinct he owned said: turn, descend, take your chances with Corvane's men and the lowland jails.

Voss had never been good at listening to his instincts when they were right. That was, if he was honest, the story of his entire life.

He started down.

* * *

They found him at the tree line, or rather the dogs found him, and then the children, and then a broad-shouldered man with a ferryman's hands who looked at Voss's face for a long moment and did not call for a rope or a knife, which surprised Voss more than either would have.

"You're the one from the col," the ferryman said. "The one who pulled his own man back off the edge."

"I'm the one who sent him there," Voss said. His voice came out cracked, half-frozen. "Both things are true."

The ferryman—Doss, they called him later, though Voss had known the name for months as a line in a surveillance report—studied him the way a man studies ice before crossing. Then he shrugged out of his own coat and dropped it over Voss's shoulders.

"Can you walk?"

"I've been walking for eleven days."

"Then walk a little further. There's soup at the end of it."

That was all. No judgment, no ceremony, no questions asked while he still had the strength to lie well. Voss would think about that often in the months that followed: that they fed him before they interrogated him, and that the interrogation, when it finally came, was conducted by a girl of perhaps sixteen sitting across a table with a slate, asking him his name and writing it down.

"What is this?" he'd asked.

"Testimony," the girl said. Maret. He knew her file too—runaway, orphaned, literate in the old script, which was rarer than gold. "Everyone in this valley gives it. Names, births, losses, songs. What happened to them. What was taken. You get a page. Everyone gets a page."

"And mine?"

She looked at him without fear, which after thirty years of being the thing other people feared, undid something in him he preferred not to name.

"Yours is longer," she said. "Probably worse. But you get a page."

* * *

They kept him in the granary the first week—not locked, simply assigned there, as if imprisonment were a chore rotation. An old woman brought him bread each morning and corrected his blanket-fold each evening without comment. Children peered through the slats until a woman shooed them off, then peered again. He heard his name spoken in the lanes in low voices: *the collector's man, the one from the mountain, the one Voss.*

He waited for the hatred to arrive in some official form—a council, a tribunal of their own, a rope. Instead, on the eighth day, Edmund Vane came to the granary alone, sat down on a flour sack across from him, and said nothing for a long time.

The wound Voss's lieutenant had given him at the parley had healed into a stiffness; Vane moved his left arm carefully, like a man carrying a full cup. His face was thinner than the surveillance portraits suggested. His eyes were the same: patient, measuring, the eyes of a man who read terrain the way priests read scripture.

"You could have let me freeze at the tree line," Voss said.

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

Vane considered the question honestly, which Voss respected. "Because Doss carried you in and Ilse signed for you, and around here those two things amount to law." A pause. "And because I watched you pull Halloran—the lieutenant—back from the drop with the chest ten feet from his hands. I've spent a year trying to understand that. I thought maybe you'd want to explain it yourself."

Voss looked at his hands. They were scarred from the climb, frost-nipped at two fingertips that might never feel warm again.

"When I was six years old," he said, "they put me on a wagon with forty other children and shipped us east to the conquerors' schools. My mother's name went into a registry. Her village was renamed. By the time I was ten I couldn't spell Kestria. By fifteen I'd stopped trying to remember her face." He flexed the numb fingers. "Corvane's people found me at nineteen. Told me I was clever, gave me work, taught me that the past was a debt collection business and I could be on either side of the ledger. I chose the side that paid." He looked up. "You want to know why I pulled him back? Because when I read that registry page—my mother's name, in your chest, written down like she mattered—I understood that I'd spent my whole life burning the only document that ever said I existed."

Vane was quiet for a while.

"The registry's here," he said finally. "Ilse has it. Your mother's page. If you give testimony about the annexation records—the land transfers, how the titles were forged, where Corvane's archive keeps the originals—it arms the tribunal case. It might be the heaviest stone we can add."

"And if I do?"

"Then you get a page in the book," Vane said. "Same as everyone. Worse than most. But the same book."

* * *

It took him three days to decide, and he decided in the granary at night, by candle stub, writing on paper Maret brought him without being asked—she seemed to know what he needed before he did, the way she seemed to know everything, with that unnerving teenage competence that made him miss having subordinates and be grateful he had none.

He wrote down everything. The structure of Corvane's land empire: how the annexation had transferred Kestrian holdings to shell companies whose charters predated the annexation itself, forged backward in time. Where the original deeds were vaulted—in the Harrow Street archive, third sublevel, under a false catalog of shipping manifests. How the blacklist had been constructed, and who had administered it, and that the man who had informed on the royal cartographer Aldric Vane had been coerced under threat to his pregnant wife—which he knew because he had read the annexation files cover to cover, twice, hunting for leverage, and had never once imagined the cartographer's son would one day sit across a flour sack from him.

He wrote until the candle died. In the morning he handed Ilse Brenner forty-one pages, and she read them standing in the snow outside the granary, and when she finished she looked at him with an expression he had never received from any living person: not gratitude exactly, and not forgiveness, but recognition—the look one archivist gives another when the record has been made true.

"This convicts half the judiciary," she said.

"It convicts everyone who built the machine," Voss said. "Including me. Put my name where it belongs."

"It will be," she said. "First page of the testimony section. Under your own hand."

* * *

Winter deepened. The valley did not soften toward him so much as absorb him, the way snow absorbs a stone—slowly, completely, without ceremony. He was assigned to the wood crews because he was strong and bad at everything else. He learned to split logs along the grain instead of against it, which an old man named Pietro told him was the whole secret of life, delivered as though it were also instructions for firewood.

The children stopped fearing him around midwinter, which happened all at once, the way ice goes out of a river. One of them—a boy of seven with his father's stubborn chin—asked him outright if he had come to burn the valley. Voss knelt in the snow so their eyes were level.

"I came to burn the papers that say this valley doesn't exist," he said. "It's the same fire, pointed the other way."

The boy considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, then handed him a sled rope. Voss pulled children up the sledding hill until his arms shook, and no one remarked on it, and that was the strangest mercy of all: that his penance was not demanded, merely assumed, folded into chores, ordinary.

But unease lived in him still, and he did not pretend otherwise. Some nights he woke in the granary certain he heard wagons on the high road—Corvane's men, come at last, the ledger calling in its debts. Some nights he dreamed of the Ironbridge gorge and the lieutenant's face as the ropes parted, though Halloran himself sat in confinement two valleys over awaiting trial, and Voss had testified to that too, sparing nothing, including his own orders.

Once, near the winter solstice, Doss found him standing at the valley mouth in the blue dusk, looking back the way they had all come.

"Waiting for them?" Doss asked.

"No," Voss said. And found it was true. "Remembering."

Doss nodded as if that were a normal thing to say, and handed him half a loaf, and they ate standing in the cold like two men keeping watch, which Voss supposed they were.

* * *

In late February, a courier came down from the pass on snowshoes with a satchel frozen to his back, and the whole valley turned out in the lane as Ilse broke the seals. Voss stood at the edge of the crowd, where he belonged, close enough to hear and far enough to run, an old habit he could not seem to shed.

The tribunal had received the registry. The certified testimony—including his forty-one pages, copied fair in Maret's careful script—had been entered into evidence. The hearing would convene at the spring melt. And enclosed, in a formal hand, was a summons: the tribunal requested the presence of the witness *Voss, given name unrecorded, registry entry 4471*, to testify in person regarding the annexation archives.

Entry 4471. He read the number three times. Somewhere in a vault in the capital, a clerk had once reduced his mother to that figure, and now the same figure was summoning him—not as an instrument, but as a voice.

Maret appeared at his elbow, slate under her arm, breath steaming.

"They'll want you to read it aloud," she said. "Your testimony. In front of everyone. The chief justice especially—he'll try to make you stumble."

"He can try."

"That's what Edmund said you'd say." She grinned, and it occurred to Voss, with a jolt like stepping into cold water, that he had become a person other people discussed. That he had a place in their predictions. "There's a meeting tonight at the schoolhouse. Doss's building. Everyone's coming. You should come too."

"I wasn't invited."

"You don't get invited here," Maret said, already walking away. "You just show up. That's how it works."

That night the schoolhouse was full—every bench taken, children on the floor, lamplight on faces Voss had spent a season trying to erase and now knew by name. Ilse stood at the front with the summons held high, and the room roared, and Edmund Vane caught Voss's eye across the crowd and nodded once, the nod of a man acknowledging another man's long road.

Voss stood in the doorway, half in and half out, the way he had stood at every threshold of his life.

Then Tam—elder's grandson, glacier-walker, the boy whose rescue Doss had bought with bare hands on the col—got up without a word and made room on his bench, and patted the wood beside him, and waited.

Voss crossed the room and sat down among them, and the door of the schoolhouse swung shut behind him, holding in the lamplight and the noise and the heat, and for the first time in thirty years, Aldous Voss was on the inside of a closed door, and no one minded at all.

Outside, far up the dark flank of the Serpent Range, the snowfields held their silence—but in the lowlands, the melt had already begun, and somewhere on a courier's road, spring was climbing toward them, bringing the tribunal, the trial, and the reckoning, one thawing mile at a time.
