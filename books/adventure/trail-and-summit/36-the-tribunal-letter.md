## 36. The Tribunal Letter

The melt came all at once, the way spring does at altitude—no polite transition, just a week of dripping eaves and then a roar as the snowpack above the valley let go all along its southern faces. Edmund stood in the doorway of the archive house on the first morning of the roar and watched the stream that bisected the valley triple its width overnight, brown and muscular with winter's leavings, carrying sticks and stones and one entire section of old fencing down toward the falls.

"The courier route," Ilse said beside him. She said it the way other people said *thank the gods*—as a prayer, a fact, and an order all at once.

He had been thinking the same thing. Twelve years of reading mountains had not been erased by one winter in a valley, and he could see it plainly: the meltwater would open the low notch on the eastern wall within days, the pass the valley folk called the Shepherd's Door, and beyond it the trail down to Ferrow would be mud for a fortnight and then passable. Mud was nothing. Mud was a horse's problem.

"You'll want the registry certified before it travels," he said.

"It's already certified. I certified it in February." She gave him a look, half apology, half defiance. "I've had the packet sealed since the week the snow closed the Door. I just couldn't send it."

He understood. A thing sealed and carried is a hope. A thing sealed and sent is a wager, and you cannot take a wager back.

"When?" he asked.

"Tomorrow. At first light. I want to walk it to the Door myself."

"You'll want company. The melt's undercut the trail on the north side."

"Then come with me." She said it lightly, but her hands were working the hem of her sleeve the way they did when she was afraid, and Edmund had learned, over a winter of watching her argue with elders and copy dead men's handwriting by lamplight, to read Ilse Brenner's hands the way he read weather.

"I'll come," he said.

* * *

The packet was smaller than what it held. Ilse had wrapped it in oilcloth, then waxed linen, then a goatskin bag that Tam swore was proof against a week of rain, and inside all of it: the certified registry of the Kestrian people, three hundred and eleven names from the high valley alone, plus the names recovered from the chest, plus forty years of births and marriages and deaths recorded in hiding, plus the testimony of a disgraced ferryman, a runaway schoolteacher, a former enforcer, and an archivist who had spent a decade being told she was chasing a ghost. The tribunal in the lowlands had convened in March, Ilse said. They would sit until the autumn. A case like theirs, a whole people asking to be legally alive again, would be heard once, and heard completely, or not at all.

Edmund watched her bind the last knot with numb fingers and thought about his father's hands, which he had never seen, doing work like this—sealing, binding, sending, in the last months before everything came apart.

"You could send Tam," he said. "Or me. You've carried it far enough."

"It was never mine to carry alone," she said. "That's the whole point of the thing. That's what you taught me, if you remember. Under the overhang, in the storm." She tied off the knot and bit the cord. "You said the chest was about the people who carried it."

"I said Ilse said that."

"You agreed." She smiled, tired. "You agreed so hard you laughed. I have witnesses."

* * *

They left at first light with Tam and Maret, the four of them, because Maret had refused with a fourteen-year-old's absolute finality to be left behind for something this important, and because Doss had a bad knee that spring and had sworn, loudly, in front of the whole morning fire, that if they were going to do something historic they could at least do it while he cooked them a decent breakfast to come back to. The trail up to the Shepherd's Door was exactly as bad as Edmund had promised—the melt had eaten a section of switchback down to bare rock, and they crossed it roped together, the packet passed hand to hand across the worst of it like a child over a fence.

At the notch, the world opened. Below them the lowlands lay hazy and green, the rivers braided silver, Ferrow a smudge of smoke at the edge of seeing. Somewhere down there, in a hall Edmund had never entered, men and women in robes would eventually open a goatskin bag and read the names of the dead back to life.

Maret had brought charcoal and a scrap of board, and at the top she insisted on drawing the view, badly, with great conviction. Tam leaned against the cairn and talked about the summer pastures. Ilse stood at the very lip of the Door with the packet in both hands, and for a long moment she didn't throw it, didn't lower it, didn't move at all.

"Ilse," Edmund said.

"I'm deciding whether to pray," she said. "We didn't pray, in my family. My father said prayer was what people did instead of archiving." She laughed, wet-eyed. "But I want to say something over it. Before it goes."

"Say it," Maret said.

Ilse held the packet against her chest. "These are the Kestrians," she said, to the packet, to the valley behind them, to the lowlands below. "All of them. Every name we could find, and every name we couldn't, which is most of them, and we're sorry, and we kept looking anyway. Let them be real again. That's all. That's the whole prayer."

Then she wrapped the bag in its sling, swung it twice, and threw it down to Tam, who had scrambled twenty feet down the far side to catch it, and the four of them watched him go, small and quick and sure-footed, down into the green world with three hundred and eleven lives on his back.

Nobody spoke until he was out of sight. Then Maret said, "I'm starving," and the spell broke, and they went home.

* * *

The waiting was the worst part, and Edmund had thought he knew all the shapes waiting could take. He had waited out storms, waited out fevers, waited out twelve years of a life measured in miles and firewood. This was different. This was waiting with three hundred people, all of whom knew exactly what was in the goatskin bag, and none of whom could do a single thing about it, and the whole valley hummed with it like a struck string that would not stop.

They worked. That was the only cure anyone had found. The melt meant the planting, and the planting meant everyone in the terraces from dawn, and Edmund, who had spent a winter mapping the valley in his father's style—contour by contour, spring by spring, the old Kestrian survey marks restored to the stones where they'd been chiseled out forty years ago—now found himself surveying irrigation channels and arguing with Tam's grandmother about the grade of a ditch. It was good work. It was the first work he'd ever done that would still be feeding people after he was dead, and he tried to hold on to that thought on the bad days.

The bad days came when the courier didn't. Tam went down with the second packet in late summer—routine letters, the valley's formal petition, a copy of everything in case the first bag had drowned in a river—and came back with nothing. *The tribunal sits until autumn,* the lowland clerk had told him. *Your case is docketed for the full session. Go home. Don't come back until you're summoned.* It was the most bureaucratic sentence ever spoken, and Ilse repeated it forty times that week, savoring it like a sweet, because *docketed for the full session* meant they had read the registry. Meant the names had been entered into the record. Meant somewhere in the lowlands, in ink, three hundred and eleven people from a valley that did not officially exist had briefly, officially, existed.

But no summons came. And the autumn came instead, early and cold, and the string that was the valley hummed tighter.

* * *

It was in the weeks after Tam's empty-handed return that Edmund started going up to the graves.

The valley burying ground sat on a shelf above the eastern terraces, a hundred years of stones at least, though most of them were unmarked—wooden markers that had rotted, or stones with no names because names were dangerous for forty years and the dead had been buried in the old way, face to the mountain, name kept in the family's mouth instead of in the ground. There was a low wall around it that Doss had rebuilt that spring, dry-stone, without mortar, the way the old walls were built, and Edmund had helped, and had not known why the work sat so heavy in his hands until he was standing inside the finished wall one gray afternoon with a sickle and a bundle of kindling and no memory of deciding to come up.

The grass had gone to seed over the older graves. He cut it. That was all, at first—cut it, and cleared the stones of the winter's moss, and righted two markers that the melt had heaved, and did not think about anything at all, which was a skill he had perfected over twelve years and was only now, in this valley, beginning to lose.

He went up every few days. The work was endless in the way that was good: the grass grew, the stones settled, the mountain shed small rocks onto the wall and he carried them off. Maret came up once and asked if he was all right, and he said yes, and she looked at him with those flat, unsparing eyes she'd had since the crevasse, since she'd started calling him Uncle, since she'd stopped believing anything he said just because he said it, and then she'd helped him cut grass for an hour without another word, which was her way of saying she'd be there when he was ready. She was fourteen. She had the patience of a saint and the tact of a landslide, and he loved her like something he'd grown himself.

It was on an evening in late September, the light going long and copper across the terraces, that he found the grave.

He almost missed it. It was a stone like the others, waist-high, lichen-eaten, set apart from the rows near the wall—and then he saw the mark on it. A survey mark. The old Kestrian cartographer's mark, the one he had spent the winter restoring to the boundary stones, the one he knew better than his own signature because he had spent a winter learning to draw it the way his father had drawn it.

He stood very still.

The valley folk had buried their dead without names. But someone, once, had chiseled a cartographer's mark into a stone on this shelf, and Edmund put his hand on the lichen and felt the grooves under it, old and shallow and deliberate, and understood that he was not the first person in forty years to remember what his father's work had meant. Someone in this valley had known him. Had carried him, maybe, in the years after the annexation, the way the chest had been carried—hand to hand, in the dark, without explanation.

Edmund sat down on the cold grass in front of the stone.

He had not wept for his father. He had catalogued every other thing that man had done—the abandonment, the map, the coerced letter, the betrayal that had bought a pregnant woman's life with a kingdom's—and he had filed it all with the precision of a man building a case, and the case had never closed because the verdict had never come, and now, with his palm on a dead stranger's lichen and his father's mark under his fingers, the verdict arrived all at once, from no tribunal at all.

His father had been a coward. That was simply true. He had informed, he had knelt, he had traded a nation for a woman and a child, and the nation had died anyway, or mostly, and the woman had died, and the child had grown up counting miles because counting miles was a way of never arriving anywhere, and never arriving anywhere was a way of never being left again.

And his father had tried. That was also simply true. The letter proved it—the coerced man who turned coercion into a map, who sent his son's location to the one archivist he trusted, who built a paper trail through forty years of darkness so that a chest could find its way up a flooded river to a hut where his son was hiding from his own name. It was a coward's plan. It was also, Edmund thought, his forehead against the cold stone, the only plan a coward had, and it had worked. Every person in this valley, every name in the registry, every song Maret was learning to write down—it had all come through that one frightened man's hands, the way the packet had come through Tam's, hand to hand, in the dark.

"Informant," Edmund said aloud, to the stone, because Ilse had taught him that a thing unrecorded is a thing unfinished. "Coward. Cartographer. My father." The words came out ragged. "You tried to save us. You saved us. Both things are true. I forgive you. I don't— I don't know how to do this, but I forgive you, and I think you were forgiven a long time ago, by everyone but me."

Then the weeping came, and it was not the dry, furious weeping of the hut burning, and not the fevered weeping of the night on the high route. It was quiet and enormous, the grief of a man setting down something he had carried so long he had mistaken it for his spine, and the valley below went on with its evening—smoke rising, cattle lowing, someone singing one of the old songs with the old words put back in—and Edmund wept into the lichen of a stranger's stone for a father he had never once, in forty years, been allowed to mourn.

Maret found him there at full dark. She didn't ask. She sat down next to him, put her shoulder against his, and after a while she said, "Uncle. Breakfast's cold. Doss is making more."

He laughed—wet, cracked, real. They went down together.

* * *

He slept better that autumn than he had slept in twelve years, and he would have said it was the grief, the way a storm clears the air, except that Ilse, who noticed everything, said it was something else.

"You stopped counting," she said one night, in the archive house, over the duplicate registry she was preparing against the tribunal's answer.

"Counting what?"

"Miles. Steps. Exits. You used to sit with your back to the wall and your eyes on the door. You sit facing the room now." She said it without looking up from her ledger, the way she recorded everything, as fact. "I've been watching since spring."

"You've been watching since Ferrow," he said.

"Yes," she agreed. "But I only started understanding in spring."

He looked at her across the lamplight—her ink-stained fingers, her father's stubborn jaw, the chest itself on its shelf behind her, empty now of the registry but full of the valley's winter testimonies, waiting to be the first thing in the new capital's archive whenever the new capital existed—and he felt the strange, unaccustomed weight of a future that had actual shape to it. Not a route. Not a line of miles. A place.

The summons came eleven days before the passes closed.

Tam's younger brother brought it up from the Door, having run the lowland trail in four days on a horse Doss had told him was too old for it, and he came into the valley at dusk with the paper held over his head like a torch, shouting before he was even through the first terrace, and the whole valley turned out into the cold to hear Ilse read it by the light of every lantern in three hundred houses.

The tribunal had ruled. The delegation was climbing. And the letter's last line, in a clerk's careful hand, named the man whose survey marks had been restored to the valley stones that spring, and requested—no, *required*—his presence when the delegation reached the summit of the Shepherd's Door, "in his capacity as son of the Royal Cartographer of Kestria."

Edmund read it twice. The valley was cheering. Ilse was crying and laughing and already, inevitably, reaching for a pen.

He stood in the firelight with the paper in his hands, and he thought: *They know my name. All of them, down in the lowlands, in the halls, in the ink. They know my name, and they're coming up the mountain to say it out loud.*

He looked at Ilse. She was watching him over the top of the letter, and she was smiling the way she had smiled at the Shepherd's Door, on the day she threw the packet down into the green world.

"Edmund," she said. "They're coming for you."

And down the eastern wall, faint in the last light, he could already see them—dots on the switchbacks, lanterns in the dusk, a delegation climbing.
