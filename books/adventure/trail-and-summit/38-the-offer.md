## 38. The Offer

The letter came up with the spring couriers, wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with a crest that had not existed in any living memory until eight months ago: the double peak and river of Kestria, struck new in brass, still sharp enough to catch the light wrong.

Ilse brought it to me herself. She crossed the lower meadow where I was staking out a survey line for the schoolhouse Doss kept enlarging every time he got bored, and she held the letter the way she held everything from the old world—two hands, slightly away from her body, as if it might still be hot from the fire that had almost consumed it.

"From the capital," she said. "The tribunal seal."

I wiped my hands on my trousers and did not take it. "You read it yet?"

"It's addressed to you." A pause. The kind of pause Ilse used instead of sighing. "Edmund. It's addressed to *Edmund Vane, Royal Cartographer.*"

"They've got the name wrong already. That's a bad start for a government."

"Read it."

I read it. The paper was good stock, the kind that doesn't drink the damp, and the hand was a clerk's—precise, bloodless, the hand of a man paid by the line. The new government of the restored Kestrian territories, sitting provisionally in the lowland capital while the high valleys were mapped and counted, offered the following: first, a full pardon, striking the name Edmund Vane from every blacklist and border register in the confederation, with written apology annexed; second, the post of Royal Cartographer of Kestria, restored, the same office held by one Aldric Vane until the year of the annexation; third, passage, escort, and lodging in the capital, at the government's expense, for so long as I should hold the post.

There was a signature at the bottom I didn't recognize and a line at the very end, added in a different ink, in Ilse's own clerk-hand, because she had copied the letter before sending it up and someone had answered in the margin the way archivists do: *The post is yours if you want it. So is the pardon. Neither is a debt. —I.*

"They want me in the capital," I said.

"They want the man who mapped the Serpent Range in winter with a hole in his side," Ilse said. "They want the son of Aldric Vane, whose survey of the northern passes is still the only one anyone trusts. They want the valley's maps, which are better than anything their surveyors have produced, and they'd rather have them given than taken." She looked out at the meadow, the meltwater running silver in the rills, the schoolhouse with its new roof and its forty-one children's names chalked on the doorframe. "And they want to be seen wanting it. That matters too. A government that was built on erasure has to be seen un-erasing things."

I folded the letter along its crease. "How long do I have to answer?"

"The courier descends in nine days. He waits for no one. Not even you."

* * *

I didn't answer for six of the nine days. I did what I always did when a thing was too large to hold all at once: I measured it. I measured the schoolhouse foundation and found Doss had built the east wall crooked by a hand's width and said nothing, because Maret had helped him lay it and she'd been proud. I measured the new trail down to the shepherd's valley and flagged eleven switchbacks where the melt would cut the tread by summer's end. I measured the grave rows above the chapel, the ones I'd tended all winter, and found I could walk them now without my chest going tight—except the last one, the one I'd cut for a man I'd never met, my father, with no body in it, only a name and the year the mountains took his reputation. I measured all of it the way my father must have measured the world: to keep it at arm's length.

On the seventh day Maret caught me at it.

She was sixteen now, or seventeen—she'd never been sure, and the registry had settled it at last, which she treated as a personal insult—and she came up the slope behind me quiet as a marmot, which she'd learned from watching me, and sat down on the survey stone without asking.

"You've read the letter four times," she said.

"Three."

"Ilse says four." She picked a stem of grass and chewed it. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing."

"The thing where you stand very still and count things so you don't have to feel them. You did it before the Stair of Winds. You did it when Voss came to the valley edge last winter and everyone wanted to know if you'd let him in and you just stood there measuring the woodpile."

"He was half-dead in the snow."

"He was. And you measured the woodpile." She spat out the grass. "Are you going?"

"I don't know."

"You do know. You just don't know if you're allowed." She stood, brushed off her skirt, and looked down at me with the particular mercilessness of the young toward the people they love. "You taught me contour lines, Edmund. You said a map's no good if it only shows you the ground you already know how to walk. So." She shrugged, and went back down the slope, and left me standing in a meadow full of meltwater with a letter in my pocket that offered me my father's life like a coat I could put back on.

* * *

On the eighth day I took the locket and walked up.

The pass where it had all turned was two days' climb from the valley in spring, three in the old snow year, and I went alone, which Tam objected to until I told him that was rather the point. He gave me a rope anyway, and a look that said he'd learned more from me than rope work, and I took both.

The trail was soft with melt. Water ran everywhere, under everything, the whole mountain speaking in its spring voice, and I climbed through it the way I'd climbed through the winter of the chest—except that this time nobody was shooting at me, and nobody was dying, and the chest was a thousand feet below me in a stone archive room that Ilse had built with her own hands and defended in three separate arguments with the government's survey commission.

I made the high camp at dusk and the col at midmorning, and there it was: the knife-edge, the Stair of Winds, running east and west like a sentence someone had started and never finished. The wind was doing its work on it, as always. Snow streamed off the crest in long white banners. Below on the far side, the country fell away into cloud, and below that cloud was the capital, and the tribunal, and the clerks with their good paper, and a post with my father's name on it, waiting for mine.

I sat down in the lee of the last cairn and took out the locket.

Maret had retrieved it from the fire two years ago, and she'd given it back to me at the winter feast without a word, just pressed it into my hand and gone back to dancing, because that was Maret. The silver was fire-blacked and I'd never polished it. Inside was my mother—her lady-in-waiting's face, young, tired, holding an infant with a fistful of her collar. The queen's own hand had painted it, they said. I had looked at it maybe a hundred times since the fire, and every time I looked I saw something different: sometimes a woman running, sometimes a woman hiding, sometimes just a mother holding a child the way you hold anything you're afraid to lose.

Twelve years I had been a man with no name worth having. That had been a choice, at the start—the blacklist made it a necessity, and then the necessity became a habit, and then the habit became a philosophy, and I'd built a hut at the top of the world and told myself that a man alone was a man safe. Then the river had brought me a chest, and the chest had brought me a lie about my father, and the lie had brought me the truth, which was worse, and the truth had brought me—well. It had brought me a crooked schoolhouse wall and a girl who called me Uncle and a ferryman who'd sold me once and carried me since, and an archivist who argued with governments on my behalf without asking whether I wanted to be argued for, and a valley of three hundred people who had spent forty years pretending not to be what the registry said they were, until a chest full of paper said otherwise.

The pardon would erase the blacklist. I understood that now, holding the letter in my head the way I held a route: the clerks would strike the name, the borders would open, the twelve years would become a clerical error, corrected. And the post—Royal Cartographer, my father's office, the survey of a nation—was everything I had trained for and been refused and mourned. A younger me, the me who'd walked out of the lowlands with a split lip and a burned reputation, would have taken it in a heartbeat. Would have marched down to the capital and put my father's seal on my father's desk and called it justice.

But the younger me had thought the past was a place you could walk back into. I'd learned better on the ice, and in the crevasse, and under the overhang in the storm, and in a frozen chapel where the frescoes showed me that the rot went all the way up. You don't get to be who you were. The mountain had taught me that before any tribunal did. What you get is who you've become, and the people who became it with you, and the ground under your feet, which you'd better learn to read.

The wind came off the crest and flattened the grass in the col, and I stood up.

"Goodbye," I said to the miles.

I don't know who I said it to. To the twelve years of walking, maybe—the measured distances, the counted switchbacks, the habit of always knowing exactly how far I was from anywhere, because a man who knows the distance is never lost, and a man who is never lost never has to arrive. To the hut that burned, and the man who lived in it. To my father, who had mapped a kingdom and sold it and tried to buy back one life from the ruin, and whose post was waiting down in the cloud like an empty chair at a table. To the boy in the locket, who had never once in his life been asked where he wanted to go.

"I'm not going anywhere," I said, and the wind took it apart and carried it east along the Stair of Winds, and I found I was smiling, which surprised me more than anything the mountain had done in two years.

I put the locket back inside my shirt, against the skin, where it had lived all winter. It wasn't a burden anymore. It wasn't a gift, either. It was just a thing my mother had held, and now I held it, and that was the whole of the inheritance, and it was enough.

Then I turned my back on the pass and walked down into the valley, and I did not count the miles.

* * *

I gave my answer to the courier on the ninth day, at the valley gate, with the whole settlement pretending not to watch, which in a community of three hundred people means everyone watching from a polite distance with their hands full of props—Doss re-coiling a rope he'd coiled twice already, Tam sharpening a knife that didn't need it, Ilse holding a ledger open to a page she was not reading.

I handed the courier the letter, folded, with my answer written in the margin in the archivist's fashion, because I'd learned that from Ilse too: *With gratitude. The pardon I accept—the name was never mine to be ashamed of. The post I decline. But the maps are yours, and so am I, if you'll have a cartographer who stays. Send your surveyors here. I'll teach them the range. —E.V.*

The courier read it, frowned at the margin the way clerks frown at margins, and tucked it away. "The minister hoped you'd come to the capital," he said.

"The minister can climb," I said. "It's good for the judgment."

That got a laugh out of Doss, and broke the pretense, and then it was noise and handshakes and Maret punching my arm hard enough to bruise, and Ilse squeezing my shoulder once, briefly, which from Ilse was an oration.

But the courier paused at the gate, one boot already on the down-trail, and turned back.

"There was a second thing," he said. "Verbal. The chief justice asked me to say it to you directly—her, not the government. She said: tell the son of Aldric Vane that the registry page with his mother's name on it hangs now in the hall of records, and that she reads it every morning, and that she knows what her grandfather's face was doing in that chapel, and she is sorry." He shifted. "She said the tribunal will sit in the valley next spring. Here. If the valley will have it. She said justice ought to climb to the people it failed, not the other way round."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Above the gate, on the schoolhouse doorframe, the forty-one names chalked in Maret's careful hand caught the afternoon light.

"Well," said Ilse, and her voice was not entirely steady, and she was already, I saw, reaching for her ledger, because a tribunal sitting in the valley meant records, and records meant her, and some inheritances you don't walk away from—you build them a room. "We'll need somewhere to seat them. Doss—"

"I'm building it," Doss said. "Been drawing it up since winter."

And Maret turned to me with her eyes bright and her chin set in the way that always meant a demand was coming, and said, "You'll have to teach them the contour lines, Edmund. All of them. The judges too." She grinned. "Starting tomorrow. First lesson's at dawn. Don't be late, Uncle."

The courier went down the mountain with my answer, and the valley closed around the news like water around a stone, and somewhere above us the first soft snow of the old year was already falling on the pass where I had said goodbye—and I stood in the gate of the only place I had ever chosen, and waited for morning.
