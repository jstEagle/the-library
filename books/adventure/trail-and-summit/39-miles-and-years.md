## 39. Miles and Years

The bell above the schoolhouse door was made from a melted-down mercenary stirrup, and Maret rang it every morning with a certain private satisfaction.

"Books out," she called. "Ink caps on. And if Tam's goats get into the yard again, we are *not* stopping lessons to chase them. We learned that lesson in spring."

Twenty-two children looked up at her from their benches—Kestrian names, valley names, two or three names that had come up the new trail with pilgrims and stayed. Sunlight came through windows Doss had glazed himself, falling across slates and across the great map that covered the eastern wall: the Serpent Range drawn in Edmund Vane's hand, every contour a careful curved line, every pass labeled in the old script that Maret had once been the only child in Ferrow able to read.

Now half her students could read it better than she could.

She caught herself smiling at that—she caught herself smiling at everything now, which at fourteen she would have found disgusting—and turned to the window to do what she did every morning at this hour, the thing that had become as much a part of the day as the bell.

She watched Edmund teach.

He held class outdoors when the weather allowed, down by the stream where the ground flattened into a bench of grey stone. Today he had six of the older children arranged around a stretch of hillside, and he was crouched among them with a stick in his hand, drawing lines in the dirt.

"All right," he said, loud enough that Maret heard him through the open window. "Who can tell me what this line means?"

"That's where the hill goes flat," said little Sefi, who was seven and fearless.

"It's where the hill goes *steep*," said her brother, with the crushing superiority available only to nine-year-olds.

Edmund didn't correct either of them. He never did, not first. He drew another line inside the first, then another, nested like ripples around a dropped stone. "Both of you are half right," he said, "which is how most true things start. Now. If you were standing here—" he tapped a point between the rings "—and a storm came off the western col, where would you go?"

The children bent over the dirt. Maret leaned on the windowsill and watched his hands.

They were older hands than the ones that had pulled her out of a crevasse six years ago. There was a stiffness in the left one on cold mornings; the knife scar along the forearm from Ironbridge had gone silver-white. But they moved over the contour lines the way other men's hands moved over a beloved face, and when Sefi finally pointed to the shoulder of the slope where the rings spread wide apart—"there, because the lines are far away from each other, so it's not steep"—Edmund looked at her for a long moment and then at the sky, and Maret saw him swallow whatever he felt before he let any of it show.

"That's exactly right," he said. "Lines close together, the mountain's standing up. Lines far apart, it's lying down. The whole range is just a sentence written in that language. Your grandmother sang it. I'm teaching you to read it."

*Your grandmother sang it.* That was the thing about Edmund, Maret thought, the thing she'd understood slowly and then all at once somewhere on the Stair of Winds: he never taught anything without threading it back to them. To Kestria. As if every skill were a door and behind every door were people.

Sefi raised her hand again. "Master Vane? Is it true you used to count miles?"

Maret snorted into her sleeve. The children had gotten hold of that story years ago—the famous mile-counter, the man who measured everything, who knew the distance from the chapel to the col to the pace—and it had become legend, embroidered with each retelling until the Edmund of the story bore no resemblance to the man who still, after all these years, refused to say how long the walk from the schoolhouse to his cabin took.

"It's true," Edmund said gravely. "I counted miles for twelve years."

"Why'd you stop?"

Edmund sat back on his heels. From the window, Maret watched him consider the question with the seriousness he gave children's questions, which was more seriousness than most adults gave anything.

"Because one day I noticed," he said, "that every mile I counted was a mile going *away* from something. And I got tired of measuring my life by leaving." He tapped the map of contours in the dirt. "So now I measure differently. You'll learn how."

"How?"

"You already know. You just haven't caught yourself doing it yet."

Maret turned back to her own classroom, ringing the bell for second period, and thought: *he's talking about us.* He'd never say it. He would deny it with that flat look of his if she accused him to his face. But she had been the girl who called him Uncle for the first time on a glacier with frost in her eyelashes, and she had watched him pretend not to hear it, and then watch him hear it, and then—months later, in the first winter, when she'd fallen asleep over her copybook and woken under his coat with the fire banked high—watched him stop pretending entirely.

Some things you don't announce. You just stop counting the miles.

* * *

At midday she carried the attendance ledger up to the archive, because Ilse wanted the birth records cross-checked against the registry, and because—though Maret would have died before admitting it—she liked an excuse to walk through the argument.

You could hear the archive before you reached it. It occupied the largest building in the high valley now, a long stone hall that had been a granary in the hiding years and was now lined floor to ceiling with cedar shelving that Doss had built and rebuilt twice after the first attempt sagged. Inside it, at a table buried under documents, Ilse Brenner and Voss were having the same argument they had been having, on and off, for four years.

"The marginalia is *dated*, Voss."

"The marginalia is *undated*. There's a number. Numbers aren't dates."

"It's a fiscal-year notation from the annexation administration. I have three corroborating ledgers from the Ferrow postmaster's collection that—"

"Which were reorganized in the thirties by an archivist who demonstrably misfiled the Halvet census, so forgive me if I don't—"

"—the *Halvet census* was misfiled by *you*, you gave it to the wrong shelf for two years—"

"I gave it to the wrong shelf *once*, and I found it, which is more than your father ever managed with—"

Maret stopped in the doorway.

Voss stopped too. He was greyer than he'd been at the col, heavier through the shoulders, dressed in the plain dark clothes he'd worn since the tribunal had cleared him and the valley had—slowly, grudgingly, over the course of two hard winters—not quite forgiven him but stopped flinching. His hands were ink-stained to the wrist. He had the particular defensive hunch of a man who has been argued at daily for years by someone he cannot defeat and no longer entirely wants to.

Ilse stood across the table from him, white streak prominent in her dark hair now, holding a page aloft like a verdict.

And both of them were smiling.

It was the strangest thing Maret had ever witnessed, those first months—the fury of it, the genuine heat, and underneath it the unmistakable current of two people who had found, in each other, the only opponents worthy of the game. Ilse needed someone who checked her work. Voss needed someone whose work was worth checking. Somewhere in the third winter, Maret had realized the arguing *was* the friendship, that neither of them would trust a conversation that didn't contain at least one accusation of misfiling.

"Don't stop on my account," Maret said, setting the ledger down. "Whatever this is, it sounds almost romantic."

"It is archival methodology," said Ilse.

"It is a misdated annotation," said Voss.

"Mm." Maret flipped the ledger open to the current page. "While you two court, I need the registry cross-check for the Aldera births. Three new children since the thaw."

That sobered them both, the way actual work always did. Ilse took the ledger and read it properly, running her finger down the columns, and Maret watched her do the thing she had done for twenty years and would do for twenty more: make people real. Every name entered in the restored registry was a person the old empire had declared impossible. Ilse treated each entry like it might stand up and testify.

"Aldera," she murmured. "That's Tam's line. His mother will want the certificate hand-copied, not printed. She's old-fashioned about it."

"She's old-fashioned about everything except the still," Maret said, and Ilse laughed—a real laugh, head back—and even Voss's mouth twitched, which from Voss was a standing ovation.

"I'll copy them tonight," Ilse said. "Tell Tam's mother there's a ceremony at midsummer. Full registry reading. All the names."

"All the names takes four days."

"Then we'll need four days of cider," said Ilse, "and I expect Doss to bring his fiddle, and I expect you, schoolteacher, to lead the children's chorus, and I expect—" she pointed the rolled ledger at Voss like a rapier "—you to sit in the front row and be seen being Kestrian, whether you've finished feeling guilty or not."

Voss looked at her for a moment. Something moved behind his face—the thing that always moved there when the valley included him, surprise and grief and a hunger he'd stopped denying.

"I'll bring the annexation ledgers," he said quietly. "For the reading. People should hear what was written about them. In their enemies' own hand. And then hear their own names said back."

Ilse softened, just slightly. "Yes," she said. "Bring those."

Maret left them to it—the ledger, the argument, the long slow project of writing a nation back into existence one name at a time—and stepped out into the bright cold afternoon thinking that if someone had told her at thirteen, running from Ferrow with stolen bread in her pocket, that this was where she'd end up, she'd have assumed they were mocking her.

* * *

She found Doss at the trailhead below the valley mouth, exactly where she knew she'd find him, because it was pilgrimage season and the new trail had made him famous in a way he pretended to hate.

The trail itself was Edmund's design—switchbacks graded gentle enough for loaded mules, cairns rebuilt every spring, rest shelters at intervals marked on maps sold in Ferrow and beyond. Pilgrims came now from the lowlands and farther: descendants of the scattered, historians, the merely curious, and every year a few quiet families who climbed all that way carrying nothing visible, and stood at the valley gate, and asked in voices barely above a whisper, *"Is this where the Kestrians are?"*

Doss ferried them up. It had started as a debt—he'd told her once, drunk on festival wine, that he figured he owed Edmund a lifetime of carrying things after what he'd done on the rope bridge and before it—but debts, Maret had learned, were just love wearing work clothes. He ran two flat-bottomed sledges and a string of mules, he knew every pilgrim's name by the second switchback, and he had a fiddle strapped to the lead sledge that came out at every rest stop whether anyone asked or not.

Today he had a family of five aboard, lowlanders by their boots, all of them staring around at the peaks with their mouths open.

"Well, if it isn't the terror of the schoolhouse," Doss boomed, spotting her. "Come to inspect the freight?"

"Come to tell you you're expected at the registry reading. Four days. Bring the fiddle."

"Four days! Ilse's finally lost her mind completely." He grinned, wide and creased, and then lowered his voice and tipped his head toward the family. "This lot walked from Marren. *Marren.* Six weeks with the little ones. The boy asked me halfway up if the mountains were angry with him for coming." His grin faded into something gentler. "Told him no. Told him the mountains have been waiting forty years for people to come home. He believed me. Kids believe you when you mean it."

"Do you believe it?" Maret asked. "That they were waiting?"

Doss considered the question honestly, the way he considered everything, turning it over in big weathered hands. "I believe," he said, "that a place is just people who stayed. And some people stayed when staying was the hardest thing a body could do." He nodded up-valley, toward the smoke of chimneys, the terraced fields, the schoolhouse bell. "So yes. Waiting. Patient as stone."

One of the lowland children tugged his sleeve and asked, in a stage whisper pitched perfectly to carry, "Is it true the schoolteacher fell in a crevasse and a mountain-man went down into the ice after her?"

"It is absolutely true," Doss said solemnly, "and she screamed the whole way down."

"I did *not* scream—"

"Screamed like a kettle," Doss told the child, delighted. "Blue dark down there. Ice glowing like a lamp. And this skinny furious girl hanging on a rope, cursing in three languages, and the mountain-man going down to her calm as Sunday. Ask her about it sometime. She'll say she doesn't remember." He winked at Maret. "She remembers every word."

Maret remembered every word. She remembered the blue light and the sound of the ice groaning like a living thing, and she remembered Edmund's face appearing over the lip of the world, frost in his beard, saying her name in a voice she'd never heard him use before or since. She had been a runaway with no people then. She had not known, going down into the dark, that anyone would come after her.

She knew it now the way she knew her own heartbeat.

"Midsummer," she said. "Fiddle. Don't be late."

"Wouldn't miss it," Doss said, and snapped the mule reins, and the sledges groaned up the last switchback toward the valley gate, fiddle case rattling, pilgrims craning their necks at the peaks their grandmothers had sung about.

* * *

Evening found her where it found her most evenings: Edmund's cabin, doing lesson prep by his fire while he worked at the big table under the lamp.

The cabin was small and always had been—he'd refused every offer of better quarters, even after recognition, even after the pardon, even after the government's letters began arriving addressed to *His Majesty's Cartographer* in handwriting that made him grimace. One room, one hearth, tools on pegs, and the wall of maps. His father's original Serpent Range chart hung framed above the table now, the one from the chest, its margins dense with that cramped confident hand. Beside it hung Edmund's own survey of the high valley, drawn in the same style, the same line weights, and Maret had never had the heart—or the cruelty—to point out to him how much alike they were. He knew. She'd seen him looking at them side by side sometimes, late, with an expression she'd learned not to interrupt.

Tonight he was drafting. She could tell from the way he held his shoulders—hunched, absorbed, the good pen out. She finished her sums, let the silence sit awhile, and then said, casually, the way you feed a cat you've been fattening for weeks:

"The council sent another letter."

"I burned it."

"You did not burn it. It's under the inkwell. I watched you put it there."

A long pause. The pen scratched on. "It's under the inkwell," he conceded, "because burning official correspondence is technically a crime, and I've served my time with crime."

"They want you to come down for the capital ceremony. Ilse says the whole restoration charter gets signed in the spring session, and they want the Royal Cartographer present. In person. With the seal and everything." She turned a page. "Also they're naming a wing of the new archive after your father."

The scratching stopped.

Maret kept her eyes on her sums. This was the delicate country, and she'd learned its trails: his father, the informant, the coward, the man who tried. Six years of graveside visits and one long terrible weeping that Edmund had never mentioned and she had never pretended not to know about. The pardon had erased the blacklist. Nothing could erase the fact that the man whose maps hung on the wall had also, in terror and love, handed a kingdom to its destroyers.

"They can name a corridor after him," Edmund said at last, evenly. "Corridors are honest. Nobody pretends corridors are heroes."

"And you? Are you going down?"

"No."

"Edmund."

"Maret." He set the pen down and looked at her across the lamplight, and his eyes had the tired patience of a man who had fought this battle with everyone in the valley, including, audibly, himself. "I spent twelve years learning how to leave. I'm not going to spend the rest demonstrating I've forgotten how. They can sign their charter. They can hang their seals. The maps go down by courier, the surveys go down by courier, and I stay where the children are." A pause. "Where the schoolteacher is. Who cannot spell 'longitude' and teaches it anyway."

"My longitude is fine."

"Your longitude is a rumor."

She grinned at him, and he almost smiled back—that rare, crooked, expensive thing—and then a knock came at the door, and Sefi's voice, breathless from running: "Master Vane! Master Vane, there's riders on the switchbacks! Lots of riders! With banners!"

Edmund was up before the word banners finished, crossing to the window, and Maret rose too, her stomach doing something complicated.

Through the glass, small in the dusk, she could see them winding up the new trail: a column of riders, torchlit, and above them—her breath caught—a standard she knew only from Ilse's reproductions and the frescoes in the frozen chapel. The old crest of Kestria, the mountain-and-star, sewn large and flying fresh.

Not mercenaries. Not collectors. Banners meant ceremony, and ceremony meant the lowlands, and the lowlands sending banners up the switchbacks in autumn meant—

"They're early," Edmund said quietly. "The charter signing isn't till spring."

Behind them, down the trail, the first rider's torch flared, and Maret saw the column slow at the bend where the contour lines spread wide apart, where the mountain lay down gently to meet its visitors, and she thought: *whatever they've come to say, nothing in this valley will be the same after.*

Edmund was already reaching for his coat.
