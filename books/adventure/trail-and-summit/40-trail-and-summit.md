## 40. Trail and Summit

The chest was smaller than the first one.

Edmund had built it himself over the winter of the valley's seventh year, from pine felled on the south slope and seasoned through two summers, its planks joined without nails in the old Kestrian fashion that Tam's grandmother still remembered with her hands even if her tongue had lost the words. He had carved the crest himself too—the mountain and the river beneath it, the seal that had once stared up at him out of floodwater like an accusation—and he had taken his time with it, because a thing meant to carry a people's memory deserved better than haste.

It weighed less than the old chest had. Paper was lighter than proof, Ilse liked to say, though she never said it lightly. But it held more. Forty years of births and deaths recorded in hiding. Three hundred names sworn before witnesses in a snowbound winter. Songs written down by Maret in a hand that had grown sure and lovely. Voss's testimony, sealed and signed, the annexation's own records turned inside out like a pocket. And at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, the map Edmund had drawn of the high valley—every contour, every stream, every grave—in his father's style, because some inheritances you choose to carry forward rather than leave buried.

Now, eleven years after the flood had delivered the first chest to his door, he stood at the trailhead below the Stair of Winds and looked up at the pass where everything had turned, and found that his hands were steady.

"You're doing it again," Maret said beside him.

"Doing what."

"Counting." She nodded at his eyes, which had gone distant the way they did when he measured distance against daylight, altitude against weather, miles against the body's capacity for suffering. Old habits. Twelve years of exile had taught him to count miles the way other men counted money—as a way of keeping the world at arm's length. "You do it every time we climb. Your face goes all—" she squinted, searching for the word, "—arithmetic."

"I'm assessing the route," Edmund said.

"You're counting miles." Maret was twenty-six now and had been correcting him since she was fifteen, and she had earned the right the hard way, half-dead in a crevasse with his rope around her waist. "You promised."

He had. Somewhere in the winter of testimony, around a fire while Doss argued about schoolhouse beams and Ilse transcribed another name into the registry, he had promised to stop measuring the world as if it were something to be crossed and left behind. He mostly kept the promise. The mountains made it difficult. Mountains invited arithmetic; it was how they spoke.

"Fine," he said. "I'm not counting."

"Liar," said Doss cheerfully, shouldering the new chest onto his back with the ease of a man who had carried it, or things like it, across a decade of trails. His hair had gone grey at the temples and his ferryman's shoulders had not softened an inch. The rope scar on his palms had faded to pale lines, but Edmund knew they were there, the way he knew the scar the guards had left above his own eye. They were men who had paid for their pasts in installments and finally, somewhere along the trail, paid them off. "Weather window holds three days. Four if the wind stays west. I counted."

"You would," Maret said.

Behind them, the rest of the party assembled itself in the unhurried way of people who had done this together so many times that the doing needed no instruction. Tam, broad and quiet now, checking bindings. Two of the valley's young ones—Sefa and Bren, nineteen and seventeen, born in the hidden years, who had never seen the lowlands and had asked to come. Ilse last, as always, standing over the chest a moment longer than necessary, her palm flat on the lid, the way she touched every document before she filed it: a greeting, a promise, a small act of faith between keeper and kept.

She caught him watching and smiled. Her hair was streaked with silver now and she wore it the same as ever, tied back for work, and her eyes were the same eyes that had climbed to his hut twenty-three years ago carrying hope like a stone she refused to set down.

"Ready?" she asked.

Edmund looked up the trail. The Serpent Range stood against the morning sky, blue and white and utterly indifferent to what they had won from it, and the pass—the pass where a wounded man had fought a burning man on a knife-edge of rock while the first snow of an ending world came down—was invisible from here, folded behind the shoulder of the mountain, waiting the way all true places wait.

"No," he said honestly. "But we're going anyway."

* * *

They climbed the way they had learned to climb: early starts, long rests, water at every crossing. Edmund led, because he always led on the technical ground and everyone had long since stopped pretending this was negotiable, but the leadership sat differently on him now. It was less a burden and more a craft. He read the sky and told them what it said. He chose the line up the scree and explained why. When Sefa's footing slipped on the second day's moraine, Edmund's hand was there before the boy finished stumbling, and afterward Edmund showed him—not scolded, showed—how to place his feet so the mountain would hold him.

"The mountain isn't against you," he told Sefa, in almost the same words his father's marginal notes had used, words he had read by firelight in a frozen chapel and hated and memorized anyway. "It just doesn't care. That's better, in a way. It means it'll hold anyone who respects it."

"Did someone teach you that?" Sefa asked.

"My father," Edmund said, and found, to his mild surprise, that the name no longer tasted like ash. It had taken years. Grief, he had learned, was not a storm you waited out but a country you learned to farm.

On the third morning they reached the base of the Stair of Winds.

It had changed, of course. Everything changed; that was the mountain's whole argument. The arête was narrower where the winters had gnawed it, wider where a rockfall had rebuilt it, and the old fixed ropes they had strung on the descent eleven years ago were rotted ribbons. But the shape of it was the shape of his memory, and his memory, he had come to understand, was not only his. It was his father's, passed down through maps and margins. It was Ilse's, passed down through archives. It belonged to everyone who had ever crossed here fleeing or returning, which was to say it belonged to Kestria, which was to say it belonged to the people climbing behind him, breathing hard and laughing nervously, lashing the chest to Doss's back exactly as they had lashed the first one, because some rituals repeat themselves whether you plan them or not.

"Same order?" Doss asked, testing the lashings.

"Same order," Edmund said. "Me, then the young ones, then you and the chest, then Ilse. Tam sweeps."

"And me?" Maret said.

"You're not fifteen anymore," Edmund said. "You go wherever you want."

She grinned and clipped in ahead of him, which was answer enough.

The Stair took them four hours. The wind came up the western face in long cold breaths, as it always did, trying its old trick of making each climber feel alone, and as always the party defeated it simply by being a party—hands offered at the bad steps, voices carrying encouragement over the hiss of air, Doss's low singing, an old Kestrian walking song that Maret had rescued from a dying woman's memory and taught to everyone under forty. By the time the ridge widened toward the col, Sefa was singing too, badly, and Bren was laughing at him, and the wind could do nothing with any of it.

Then the pass opened before them, and everyone stopped talking at once.

It was smaller than Edmund remembered. That was the joke of it, the private joke he and the mountain had shared for a decade: the places that turn your life are never as large as they felt. A saddle of broken stone between two shoulders of ice, wide enough for ten people to stand uncomfortably, narrow enough that the drop on either side made the stomach speak. Snow lay in the hollows, old snow, deep-drifted, though it was high summer and the passes had been open for weeks. On the far side the land fell away in green folds toward the lowlands, toward Ferrow, toward the rebuilt capital whose cranes they would see tomorrow if the weather held, and beyond it the plains where a nation that had been erased from the maps was now printed on them again in ink that tribunals and treaties had made permanent.

Ilse came up beside him. She set down her pack, walked to the exact center of the col—he watched her find it, the archivist locating the record—and stood looking north for a long time.

"He'd have wanted to see this," she said at last. She didn't have to say which he. There were only two fathers between them, hers and Edmund's, and both of them lived in this place: hers in the maps that had shown her the route above the clouds, Edmund's in the handwriting on the old chest's map, in the marginal note that had confessed everything, in the son who had inherited the man's gift and spent twelve years pretending it wasn't a legacy.

"He saw it," Edmund said. "That's why he drew it. You don't draw a pass that carefully unless you've stood here and thought, *someone will need this.*"

"He betrayed them."

"He saved my mother. He saved me. He did it badly, and frightened, and it cost a nation forty years." Edmund crouched and picked up a flat piece of shale, turned it in his fingers. "I stopped keeping score a long time ago. Turns out people are like routes. There's the line you wish existed and the line that does, and the only sin is refusing to see the real one."

"When did you get wise?"

"About year six," he said. "You were there. You were insufferable about it."

She laughed, and the sound went off down both sides of the pass, north and south, the old world and the new, and neither side objected.

Doss set the chest down in the center of the col, where Ilse had stood, and for a moment nobody moved. The wind combed past. Below them to the south, tiny with distance, the high valley lay hidden in its fold of mountains—three hundred people, then five hundred now, then whatever it became—unseen, unseeable, and yet more present in this moment than anything visible.

"We could say words," Tam offered.

"We could," Ilse said. "Or we could do what we actually came to do." She knelt and opened the chest.

The documents inside caught the light—forty years of a people's memory, bound and sealed and certified, every page bearing the registry mark Ilse had designed herself: the mountain, the river, and beneath them a small open door. She lifted the top bundle and held it a moment, the way she greeted every document, and then she closed the lid and locked it and stood.

"It goes to the capital," she said. "To the archive. Where anyone can read it. That's the whole ceremony. That was always the whole ceremony." She looked around at them, and her voice roughened. "A chest is just wood until people decide what it carries. You all decided. Years ago. I'm only the one who writes it down."

"You write it down," Maret agreed, "and then you argue with Voss about the cataloguing system for four hours."

"That's also sacred tradition," Ilse said gravely.

Doss laughed and hoisted the chest again. "Capital by tomorrow night, if the weather holds."

And that was when Edmund understood that he had stopped counting.

Not decided to stop—stopped. He searched himself for the arithmetic and found none. No miles against daylight. No altitude against weather. No tally of what the climb had cost and what remained to pay. Just the pass, and the light on the ice, and these people breathing around him, and the strange spacious feeling of a mind that had put down its ledger and discovered it could hold the whole view instead.

Maret saw it happen. He knew she saw it, because she was Maret, and she had been watching him stop counting for eleven years the way Ilse watched documents—with patience, with rigor, with an accountant's delight in a balanced book.

"Well?" she said.

Edmund looked north, where the tribunal had ruled and the flags flew and a government offered jobs to men with his father's gifts. He looked south, where a valley full of children was learning contour lines from a man who had once believed he would die alone in a hut with a stove and a grudge. He looked at the chest on Doss's back, going down to the capital to become public memory instead of private burden. He looked at Ilse, at Doss, at Tam, at the young ones, at Maret with her eyebrows up, waiting.

"We're not going anywhere," he said.

Tam frowned. "We're literally descending to the capital right now."

"We're not going anywhere," Edmund said again, and heard how it sounded, and didn't correct it. "We're already here."

For a moment the wind was the only voice on the pass. Then Maret laughed—that laugh, the one that had started as a runaway's armor and had become, over eleven years, simply the sound of his family being amused at him—and threw her arms around him hard enough to stagger a man half his age, and Doss clapped his shoulder with a rope-scarred hand, and Ilse kissed his cheek and whispered, "Finally," and Sefa and Bren looked at each other with the expression of the young witnessing an old mystery they suspected they'd understand someday, on some pass of their own.

They came down off the Stair as the afternoon slanted gold across the ice, and camped that night in the green fold below the col, and the talk around the fire was small and warm and endless—schoolhouse beams, cataloguing systems, whether Ferrow's new bridge tolls were robbery or merely theft, whether Sefa would finally admit he couldn't cook. Edmund sat with his back against a stone his father might have leaned against, and drank tea, and let the miles go uncounted behind him like a river he no longer needed to ford.

Near midnight, when the others slept, Ilse stirred beside him and nodded south, toward the dark bulk of the range.

"Look," she said.

Above the pass they had crossed that day, above the Stair of Winds, the sky had begun to move. Clouds boiling up over the western shoulders, silver-edged in the moonlight, building with the slow confidence of weather that intends to keep its promises. The first storm of the season, days early, the mountains lying again—or telling the truth, depending on how you'd learned to listen.

"It'll close the high passes behind us," Edmund said. "By tomorrow night, no one crosses until spring."

"Mm." Ilse pulled the blanket closer around them both. "Anyone still up there will winter in the high valleys."

"Anyone still up there," Edmund said, "will be family."

Below them the valley slept. Above them the snow began to fall, soft and enormous, erasing the trail they'd climbed, erasing every trail, laying down a clean white page over the whole turning world. Edmund watched it come for a long time, and then, for the first time in his life, he turned away from a mountain without measuring it, and went to sleep.
