## 1. The River Gives Up Its Dead

The river had been eating the valley for eleven days when they finally paid me to look at it.

Not much—forty scrip and a meal ticket to the chow house at Corben's Landing, which was generous only if you'd been living on boiled cattail roots and pride, and I had been living on both since spring. The survey was simple on its face: walk the flooded bend below the old Kestrel Viaduct, mark where the water had taken the embankment, note what it threatened to take next. Nobody wanted poetry. They wanted numbers on a form they could file and forget.

I gave them numbers. I kept the poetry for myself, because a man alone with a river for three days starts talking to it whether he means to or not.

* * *

The flood had turned the valley into something between a lake and a memory. Water stood brown-green and patient over fields that had been wheat in June, over fence lines that showed as faint dark stitches just beneath the surface, over the roofs of two farmhouses that would never be farmed again. Dead cattle drifted in loose flotillas near the treeline, bloated and turning slowly, like planets in bad orbits. The smell was everywhere—mud and rot and the iron tang of drowned machinery—and by the second day I stopped noticing it, which is its own kind of drowning.

I worked the bend in a leaky skiff I'd won at cards from a man who couldn't have afforded to lose it, and I tried not to think about that too hard either. Debt has a way of following you onto the water. Three creditors in Corben's Landing held paper with my name on it, and one of them, a soft-spoken factor named Ellery Quist, had made it clear that forty scrip wouldn't even cover the interest. "You were somebody once," he'd said, not unkindly, which was worse than if he'd said it cruelly. "Engineer Lockridge. The Kestrel Line. People remember."

People remember wrong, I'd told him. People remember the collapse, not the cause.

But that was three days ago, and today there was only the river, and the river was doing something strange.

It had begun giving things back.

Floods do that—the rising takes and the falling returns, but never in the right order and never to the right people. All morning the bend had been coughing up its dead: fence posts, a church pew, a child's rocking horse riding the current with terrible dignity, a crate of preserved pears that I ate three of before deciding that was probably a mistake. And then, near the foot of the viaduct where the water ran deepest and slowest, snagged against a fallen cottonwood, something that did not belong to any farm or any church.

A chest. Iron-banded, oak-ribbed, sealed with a plate of dull metal stamped with a crest I didn't recognize and then, all at once, recognized completely.

I sat very still in the skiff and let the current rock me while my heart did arithmetic it didn't want to finish.

The seal was a sunburst split by a rail line—a wheel with rays, struck through by a single horizontal bar. I had seen it on the lintels of stations I'd helped build, on the brass buttons of conductors who no longer existed, on the last coin minted by a nation that had been on the maps for ninety years and was now on none of them. The Republic of Vaelmoor. Dissolved, annexed, divided, erased. Its rails had been pulled up or reassigned; its archives burned or scattered; its name spoken now mostly by historians and by men like me, who had laid track across its corpse and been paid in currency that stopped meaning anything a week after we earned it.

Whatever this chest was, it had been down there a long time. Long enough for the silt to weld the bands shut. Long enough for the oak to go black and dense as bogwood.

I should have marked it on the survey form and rowed away. That's what the smart version of me would have done—the version that still believed in staying small, staying invisible, staying ahead of exactly nothing. Salvage rights on a concession zone were a legal labyrinth, and I had neither the money nor the standing to navigate one. A chest bearing a dead nation's seal wasn't treasure; it was trouble with hinges.

I hooked the grapnel into it anyway.

* * *

It took most of the afternoon and both of my remaining ropes. The chest came up out of the water like a body refusing to be buried twice—heavy, reluctant, streaming black mud—and when it finally thumped into the bottom of the skiff, the whole boat settled two inches lower and I understood I'd be bailing the whole way back to shore. Fine. Some cargo is worth bailing for.

I dragged it up the bank to the shell of the old signal shed below the viaduct, a place with a roof that was mostly suggestion and walls that kept out the wind if the wind wasn't trying. I built a small fire from driftwood and broke open the lock with a cold chisel and more patience than I knew I had left.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark:

Charts. Six of them, hand-drawn on linen-backed paper, in a draftsman's hand so fine and confident it made my own hands ache with old longing. Survey charts. Rail charts. My trade, done better than I'd ever done it, showing a stretch of line I knew intimately and had never seen drawn: the Kestrel Extension, the spur that ran from the viaduct down through the Narrows to the river crossing at Halfway Tides. Closed for twenty years. Stricken from every working map. I had walked that grade myself as a young engineer, before, before, before—and here it was, inked in beautiful detail, with annotations in a language I half remembered from my apprenticeship. Old Vaelmoor script. High-register. The kind used for official documents and epitaphs.

And beneath the charts, a ledger bound in green leather, and inside the ledger, a manifest.

I read it four times. I read it by firelight, then by the gray failing light through the broken roof, then by firelight again, because I kept deciding I must have misread it.

*Aurelian Pay-Train No. 7. Departed Kestrel Central, 14th of the Harvest Month. Cargo: treasury bullion, payroll specie, bearer instruments. Route: Kestrel Extension to Halfway Tides crossing. Status:*

The status line was blank. Not crossed out. Not amended. Blank, in the way a sentence goes blank when the man writing it died before he could finish it—or decided some truths were safer unwritten.

Here is what I know, and what every railroad man of my generation knows: there was no pay-train on the Kestrel Extension. Officially. The ledgers of the successor companies record no such consist, no such cargo, no such loss. When the Vaelmoor treasury was audited after the dissolution, the missing millions were attributed to wartime plunder, to embezzlement, to the general chaos of a nation coming apart. Nobody ever mentioned a train. Trains leave records. Wrecks leave salvage claims. A train that sank in a river with a nation's payroll aboard would be the most famous wreck on the continent.

Unless someone very powerful needed it never to have existed.

I sat back against the wall of the signal shed and listened to the river move in the dark, patient as a creditor, and I felt something crack in me that I had spent seven years welding shut.

It wasn't greed. I want to be honest about that, even if honesty costs me. Forty scrip and a meal ticket does not make a man dream of gold; it makes him dream of not being hungry, and I had learned to keep even those dreams on a short leash. No—what cracked was smaller and stranger. It was the handwriting. It was the fact that some engineer, some nameless brother of the rod and level, had stood on that same closed grade with his instruments and his ink and had drawn the truth of it, carefully, beautifully, knowing it might never be read—and had hidden it where the river would keep it safe until the river changed its mind.

Somebody had trusted the water with the truth. And the water had given it to me.

I thought about Quist's paper in my pocket. I thought about the word *disgraced*, which people applied to me the way you'd apply a stamp to freight—quickly, officially, without wondering what was underneath. I thought about the collapse at Kestrel Junction, the eleven men, the inquiry, the testimony, the verdict that had my name on it because somebody's name had to be on it and mine was the cheapest.

I thought about how a blank line on a manifest can weigh more than a locomotive.

The fire burned down to coals. Out on the bend, the floodwater had begun its long retreat, and where the chest had lain, the surface of the river lay smooth and flat as a ledger page waiting to be written on.

I took out my compass—the brass one, the one that had been old Aldous Merrin's, the last thing he ever put in my hands, saying *a man who knows true north can survive being lost*—and I set it on top of the charts, and I let myself, for exactly one minute, imagine what it would mean.

Six tides. The thought arrived from nowhere and stayed like a splinter. Somewhere in the fine print of my memory, in the boilerplate of concession law I'd studied for a certification I'd never finished: unclaimed salvage in a lapsed concession zone reverts to first claimant upon expiry of the permit term. And somewhere downstream, I would have bet my last pear, someone else already knew these waters held more than drowned cattle.

Because chests don't surface alone. Rivers don't give up their dead one at a time unless something upstream has stirred them loose—and whatever had stirred them loose was going to draw every scavenger, every claim-jumper, and every gunboat on the Meridian Salvage rolls down this bend like blood in water.

I rolled the charts tight, wrapped them in the oilcloth, and slung the ledger inside my coat, against my ribs, where a man carries the things he isn't ready to admit he'd kill for.

Then I put out the fire, stepped into the dark, and started walking toward Corben's Landing—not toward the survey office, but toward the riverside dives where the men without futures drank, because if I was going to run, I'd need crew, and if I was going to need crew, I'd need the best ruined diver on the river.

His name was Oskar Vane. He owed me a debt older than mine, and he drank like a man trying to drown something that wouldn't stay down.

By dawn, I intended to find out what.
