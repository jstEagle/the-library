## 2. Six Tides

Petra did not sleep that night. The chest sat open on the floor of the rented room above the survey office, its contents spread across the bed like the organs of something dissected, and Petra sat cross-legged beside it with a lamp turned low and the shutters drawn, feeling less like a discoverer than a thief in a tomb.

The seal was the problem. It lay on the quilt now, heavy as a communion wafer gone wrong—a disc of lead the size of a saucer, stamped with the crossed hammer-and-rail of the Vellmark Republic, the nation that had drowned itself in debt and then in water twenty years ago. Every schoolchild knew the story: the republic sold its rivers to pay its armies, sold its armies to pay its rivers, and when both accounts came due on the same morning, the whole apparatus simply stopped, mid-turn, like a clock with a bent gear. Its trains had kept running for a while after—ghost schedules, ghost crews—and then even the ghosts were struck from the books.

Officially, nothing of Vellmark remained. Unofficially, here was its seal, still bearing the wax impression of a treasury clerk's thumb, pressed hard enough to leave a whorl.

Petra turned it over twice, three times, looking for the forgery. There wasn't one. That was the trouble. A forged seal could be reported, traded, forgotten. This one was real, which meant everything beneath it might be too.

The charts were the next strangeness. They were drawn on linen-backed paper, hand-inked, in a drafting hand so clean it looked printed—and it was a hand Petra recognized, because it was close kin to their own. Survey work had rules, and the old Vellmark draftsmen had followed them the way monks follow hours: contour lines feathered at the banks, soundings marked in fathoms along the channel, bridge abutments noted with their load ratings as if the bridges might someday matter again. The route ran down the Sereine Bend and past it, into the closed country—the drowned timberlands, the lock at Halfway, the long silent reach where the maps in any public office simply went blank.

And there, at the terminus of the line, circled twice in red ink that had gone the color of dried blood: *Kestrel Cut. Mile 114. Cargo manifest attached.*

The manifest was a single folded sheet, water-stained at one corner but legible. Petra read it four times. Pay-train. Not freight, not mail—pay. Eleven cars, armored tender, escort detail of twelve. And the cargo column, written out in neat figures that made Petra's mouth go dry: specie and scrip, treasury-sealed, bound for the garrison works at Kestrel Cut on the last week the republic existed. The week the garrison stopped answering. The week the train stopped anywhere at all.

No wreck report. No salvage claim. No insurance filing. Petra had spent enough years around rail offices to know what an absence like that meant—not that the train had never run, but that someone very high up had decided it should never have been recorded. A pay-train that vanished off the books didn't sink. It was *disappeared*, and disappeared things belonged to whoever made them disappear.

Petra sat back against the wall and listened to the river through the floorboards, that low perpetual conversation of water against pilings, and felt the old familiar arithmetic begin to turn over in the dark. Debt to Marlowe & Sons: two hundred forty crowns, accruing. Rent: overdue. The flood survey fee: thirty crowns, already spoken for before it arrived. Against all of it, a chest full of paper that said *eleven cars* and *treasury-sealed* and *Mile 114*.

It would be so easy to want this. That was what frightened Petra—not the danger, not the Fleet, but how quickly the wanting arrived, warm and total, like fever. Self-sufficiency had been the whole religion for eleven years. Need no one, owe no one, trust no farther than your own two hands on a throttle. And here was a dead nation's treasure offering to make all of it unnecessary, if only Petra would do the one thing the religion forbade: need other people.

The chart gave no choice about that. Petra saw it only on the third reading, in the margin notes, small as ticks: tide tables. Someone aboard that train—or someone who'd planned its route—had annotated every lock and cut with the tidal windows, and the final entry at Kestrel Cut carried a notation in a different ink, newer, sharper: *concession zone — permits lapse — six tides from issue.*

Six tides. Twelve days, give or take the moon. Whoever held the concession permits for the drowned Vellmark holdings had until then to file salvage rights, and after that the zone reverted to open claim, first filer wins all. And if the permits had been forged in the first place—if some magistrate had sold the same drowned treasury twice—then the man holding them would burn the world down before he let a railroad engineer with a stolen chest complicate his arithmetic.

Petra blew out the lamp and sat in the dark and let the river talk.

*Run or hide.* Those were the options, and Petra examined each with the cold thoroughness of a driver walking a bridge before taking a heavy load across. Hide: bury the chest, melt into the flood-season crowds, wait out the six tides and file first at the land office in Corvane. But hiding required staying unknown, and the Meridian Salvage Fleet did not stay unknown; they dragged rivers with grapples and bought magistrates by the dozen, and somewhere between the bend and the land office stood whatever agent Voss had already set watching. Hiding meant waiting to be found.

Run meant speed, and speed meant a boat, and a boat meant a crew, and a crew meant people—witnesses, liabilities, betrayals waiting to happen. Run also meant the closed lines, Petra's own old map of them, surveyed back when the closures were fresh and the gates unwatched. The Fleet would guard the open river. Nobody guarded water nobody wanted.

Petra looked at the compass lying on the quilt—brass, dented, the face clouded where a crack let damp in—Aldous Wren's last gift, *true north is a decision, not a direction* engraved inside the lid. Then Petra began, quietly, to pack.

* * *

The Saltcellar smelled of tar, bilgewater, and fried onions, which was to say it smelled like every dive bar within a mile of any working water, and Petra had chosen it for exactly that anonymity. It was past midnight. The lamps smoked. A one-armed fiddler played something slow in the corner, and half the room appeared to be asleep sitting up.

Oskar Vane occupied a table near the door with his boots up on a second chair and a bottle of something colorless in front of him, drinking the way men drink when they're working at it. He was broader than Petra remembered—hard to tell under the coat—but the hands were the same, scarred white across the knuckles, diver's hands, rope-burned and cold-swollen. He'd been famous once. Ten years ago, the name Oskar Vane had meant the deepest man on the Sereine, the one they sent down when a lock gate jammed or a dredge went over, the one who'd walked the bottom of the Kestrel narrows in a suit older than he was and come up grinning. Then came the accident everyone whispered about and nobody detailed, and the fame curdled into this: a big quiet man dissolving himself by degrees in riverside rooms.

He watched Petra cross the room without appearing to watch, which was itself a skill.

"Lockridge." His voice was gravel poured slow. "Heard you were doing surveys again. Heard they were paying you in fish."

"Heard you were drinking again," Petra said, taking the third chair. "Heard you were paying in teeth."

Vane smiled without much humor and tipped the bottle an inch. "Everybody's heard everything. Sit or don't."

"I'm sitting." Petra set the chart case on the table between them, and Vane's eyes—bloodshot, but quick underneath, always quick—went to it and stayed.

"Salvage work," Petra said. "Deep work. Wreck recovery, possibly contested waters, possibly with company."

"What kind of company?"

"The armed kind."

Vane took a slow pull and set the bottle down with a click. "You know what happened to my last contested-water job."

"I know what they say happened. I also know what they say happened to me, and I know how much of it was true." Petra unbuckled the case partway, just enough to show the corner of the chart, the red circle, the words *Kestrel Cut*. "I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you to look at this and tell me what your professional eye sees."

Vane hesitated. Then he leaned forward, pulled the chart free, and spread it flat with both scarred hands, and Petra watched ten years fall off him in the space of a breath. His finger moved down the soundings, quick and sure, reading depth the way other men read print. Past the bend. Past Halfway lock. To the circle.

"Mile 114," he murmured. "Kestrel narrows. Forty-one fathoms at the cut—" He stopped. Looked up. "Nobody dives forty-one fathoms on standard air."

"Then we don't use standard air."

"You don't know what you're asking."

"I know exactly what I'm asking. That's why I'm asking you." Petra held his gaze. "There's a pay-train down there, Vane. Vellmark treasury, sealed cars, never reported, never claimed. Concession expires in six tides. After that, anyone can file—and the Meridian Fleet is already dragging the bend looking for whoever pulled this chest out of the silt. Which was me. So I have six tides, no boat, no crew, and a head start measured in hours."

Vane sat back. The lamplight moved on his face, and behind the drink-ruddiness Petra could see the machinery turning, the old calculations of a man who had spent his life weighing water against air.

"Why me?" he said finally. "You hate divers. You told the whole Corvane engineering society that diving was 'gambling with the only body you get.'"

"I was younger and stupider, and I've since learned there are worse gambles." Petra tapped the chart. "You've worked these narrows. You're the only man alive who's seen the bottom at Kestrel Cut and come back to describe it. And I need someone who isn't afraid of deep water, because everything else about this job is going to be terrifying enough."

Vane laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after years shut. He looked at the chart again, and his finger came to rest on the red circle, and stayed there.

"Split," he said. "Not wages. Split. Whatever comes up, a diver's share, negotiated before we start and written down."

"Written down," Petra agreed.

"And I pick my own rigging. And if I say the water's wrong, we surface, no argument."

"No argument."

Vane drained the bottle, stood, and dropped coins on the table with the exaggerated care of a man making a point of being steady. Then he paused, one hand flat on the chart, and looked at Petra with something that was almost the old grin.

"Six tides," he said. "Where do we find a boat fast enough?"

"That," Petra said, shouldering the chart case, "is the next problem. There's a girl on the closed line who's been listening to the Fleet's telegraph traffic—and either she's our way through, or she's the reason they're already ahead of us."

Outside, the rain had begun again, fine and silver over the black water, and down at the bend—far off, patient as a shark—a gunboat's riding lights burned low on the tide.
