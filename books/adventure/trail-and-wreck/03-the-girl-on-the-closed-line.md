## 3. The Girl on the Closed Line

The tower had been dead for eleven years, and Wren had been keeping it company for six weeks.

She knew every sound it made. The wind through the louvers in the old signal room, a low moan like a man remembering something painful. The drip of rainwater down the stairwell, counting seconds into the dark. The creak of the great semaphore arms outside, frozen at *line closed*, rusted mid-gesture like a hand that had stopped waving and never finished. At night, when the fog came up off the river and swallowed the valley, the tower felt less like a building and more like a ribcage she was living inside—hollow, patient, waiting for something to breathe it back to life.

So she breathed for it.

Every night she climbed the ninety-one steps to the operating room, sat down at the brass key that no one was supposed to hear anymore, and listened. The main line still hummed two miles east—the Meridian Salvage Fleet ran its traffic hot and heavy along the river corridor, coded bursts and commercial chatter and, lately, something tighter and meaner underneath, messages sent in short angry groups with priority flags stacked three deep. Wren had taught herself the Fleet's private cipher from a manual she'd stolen at fourteen, and now, at seventeen, she could read their traffic the way other girls read letters from home.

Tonight the traffic was wrong.

She'd felt it all evening—a pressure in the wire, the way you feel weather coming in your teeth. Then, just past midnight, the burst came through: *MERIDIAN CENTRAL TO ALL STATIONS, RIVER SECTOR. CONCESSION ZONE K-9. TWIN MANIFEST UNACCOUNTED. SUSPECT SALVAGE IN PROGRESS. DETAIN AND SEARCH ALL VESSELS. AUTHORIZATION VOSS.*

Wren's pencil stopped.

*Twin manifest.* She knew that phrase. Not from the Fleet traffic—from the chest.

Her chest. Well. Not hers, not exactly, not legally, but hers in the way that mattered: she'd found it three months ago, wedged in the crawl space beneath the tower's floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth and hidden by someone who'd never come back. Inside had been papers bearing the seal of a nation that no longer appeared on any map, a bundle of salvage charts drawn in a careful engineer's hand, and a manifest listing cargo—*pay-train, sealed strongbox, bullion ledger*—on a line that official records insisted had never existed. She'd spent six weeks cross-checking it against Fleet traffic, against the public registers she could pull off the open wire, against her own memory of everything she'd overheard before she ran.

And now Meridian was hunting its twin.

Which meant someone else had pulled the other half out of the river. Which meant the clock she'd thought was theoretical had started ticking.

Wren sat very still in the dark, listening to the tower breathe around her, and did the arithmetic of fear. If Meridian wanted the manifests badly enough to flood the sector with search orders, then the papers under her floorboards were worth more than her life. And if Magistrate Voss had signed the authorization himself—

She copied the message twice, once in plain cipher and once in her own shorthand, folded both copies into the lining of her coat, and went downstairs to decide whether she was going to run or make her first deal.

* * *

She didn't have long to wait. The river gave her one the next morning.

She saw the boat first—a battered survey skiff working the flooded bend below the tower, moving too slow and too low in the water, hugging the drowned timber line like it had reasons to avoid open channel. A woman at the tiller, broad-shouldered, gray coming into her hair, with the particular economy of motion Wren had learned to recognize in people who'd once held important jobs and lost them. Survey gear lashed down. No Fleet markings. No concession pennant either—which meant she was working water she had no permit to work.

Wren watched her through the cracked lens of the tower's old spyglass and made her decision the way she made all of them: fast, and then terrified about it afterward.

Getting down to the bank took an hour. She went the long way, through the alder scrub, carrying the twin manifest wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs, rehearsing and discarding openings the whole way. *Excuse me, ma'am. I have information you'll want. I know what you're looking for.* All of it sounded like a trap, because that was exactly how she'd have read it if someone said it to her.

In the end she didn't say anything clever at all. She simply stood on the mudflat where the skiff would have to pass, close enough to the drowned timber that the boat would swing wide toward her, and when it did, she raised one arm and called out across the water:

"K-9! Concession zone K-9! You're being searched for!"

The boat came about so hard it shipped water. The woman at the tiller had a boathook in her hand before Wren finished the sentence, and her eyes—pale, sharp, tired—went over Wren like a surveyor reading bad terrain: the too-big coat, the mud to the knees, the tower looming behind her on its bluff.

"Who are you?" the woman called back.

"Wren." She made herself stand still. Running now would look guiltier than staying. "I run the tower up there. Or I live in it. Same thing."

"Towers don't run. They were decommissioned."

"I decommissioned it back the other way." She patted the oilcloth bundle. "I read the wire. I've been reading it for six weeks. And last night Meridian put out a sector-wide order—detain and search, all vessels, authorization Voss. They're looking for a twin manifest. I'm guessing you know why."

The silence that followed had weight to it. The woman brought the skiff in slow, nosing it against the mudflat without quite beaching, keeping one boot on the gunwale like a door she might need to leave through.

"Petra Lockridge," she said finally. It wasn't offered as a name; it was offered as a test. Anyone who'd worked the river corridors would know it, and anyone who knew it would have a reaction, and the reaction would tell Petra Lockridge everything she needed to know.

Wren gave her none. "I know the name," she said. "I also know the Fleet's private cipher, the location of every patrol picket between here and the halfway locks, and the fact that your permits—if you're filing any—expire in five tides. So maybe we skip the part where you decide whether I'm worth talking to, because I already decided about you."

Something moved behind those pale eyes—not warmth, not yet, but the flicker of a person recalculating. "You intercepted Fleet traffic."

"I live on a hill full of copper wire and dead equipment. It talks if you ask it nicely."

"And the manifest?"

Wren unwrapped the oilcloth just far enough to show the corner of the paper—the seal, the watermark, the careful engineer's hand. Petra Lockridge went very still, the way water goes still over something deep.

"That's the twin," Wren said. "That's what they're searching boats for. And there are gunboats at the lower bend already—I heard the picket reports at dawn. You can't file salvage rights without running their gauntlet, and you can't run their gauntlet without knowing where the pickets aren't." She let that sit. "I know where they aren't."

* * *

They talked on the mudflat for an hour, because neither of them trusted the other enough to do it anywhere enclosed.

It was, Wren thought later, like watching two animals circle the same carcass—except the carcass was trust itself, and both of them had been starved on it. Petra asked questions like a magistrate taking testimony: How long in the tower? Why run? Who taught her the cipher? Who else knew she was here? Wren answered some, deflected others, and noticed that Petra never once lied to her—dodged, yes, went silent, yes, but never lied—and decided, in the reckless chamber of her heart where decisions got made before the rest of her could vote, that this mattered more than the answers.

"Why should I take you?" Petra said at last. It wasn't cruel. It was honest, which was worse. "You're seventeen. You're a runaway. The moment Meridian figures out where their leak is, that tower becomes a noose, and anyone standing near it gets hanged with it. Passage costs me nothing to refuse and everything to grant."

"You're right," Wren said. "Refuse, then. Go run the lower bend blind and hope the pickets are asleep." She watched that land. "Or take me, and I'll thread you through every gap in their net from here to the wreck. I want passage out of the territory—that's all. Drop me past the jurisdiction line at Graywater, and I disappear, and whatever you pull out of that river is yours. I don't want gold. I don't want a share." She hesitated, and then told the truth, because it was the only currency she had left. "I want to be somewhere they can't send me back."

Petra looked at her for a long moment. Out on the bend, the fog was lifting off the water in rags, and somewhere downstream—too far to see, close enough to imagine—a gunboat was doing its morning sweep.

"My mentor used to say," Petra said quietly, almost to herself, "that the wire doesn't care who's holding the key. It just carries what's true." She looked at the tower on its bluff, then back at Wren. "You've been carrying true things alone up there for six weeks. That's a kind of courage. It's also a kind of dying." She stepped back into the skiff and held out one calloused hand. "Get your kit. Whatever you can carry in ten minutes. We leave with the tide."

Wren stared at the hand. Trust, she thought, was a bridge you crossed knowing it might drop you. Her whole life she'd waited for bridges that were guaranteed.

She took the hand.

* * *

Ten minutes later they were running the drowned timber line with the engine muffled, Wren at the little chart table in the skiff's stern copying picket positions onto Petra's map in her quick telegrapher's shorthand. The tower shrank behind them on its bluff, its semaphore arms still frozen at *line closed*, and Wren found she couldn't look away until the fog took it.

"It'll keep," Petra said, following her gaze. "Towers keep."

"That's what I'm afraid of," Wren said. "That everything I leave behind just keeps, waiting for me to come back and be caught."

Petra was quiet for a moment. Then: "First lock is forty miles down. There's a man named Oskar Vane joining us there—diver, drunk, better at his job than he lets on. After that we run the closed lines." She tapped the map, the old hand-drawn routes beneath Wren's fresh ink. "My map. Drawn before I had reason to be ashamed of it."

"What happened to you?" Wren asked, before she could stop herself. "On the river. Why does everyone know your name the way they do?"

Petra's jaw tightened, and for a moment Wren thought she'd spent her new alliance on one question. But Petra only said, "Ask me again when you've earned the answer," and turned the skiff's bow toward the gap in the picket line Wren had marked, and the current took them both.

Behind them, far upstream, a Fleet gunboat's smoke rose thin and straight against the morning—heading for a decommissioned tower on a bluff, finding it empty, and reporting back in short, angry groups that their ghost had grown wings.

By nightfall, Wren knew, that report would reach Magistrate Voss. And whatever mercy the river had shown them so far, she doubted it extended to men who signed orders with the word *Voss* attached.
