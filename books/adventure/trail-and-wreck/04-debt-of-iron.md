## 4. Debt of Iron

The closed lines had a smell all their own. Petra had forgotten that—rust and standing water, creosote gone soft in the sun, the green rot of timber sleepers no wheel had touched in nine years. It was a smell like a held breath, like a house where someone had died and the family had simply locked the door and walked away.

They walked the line because there was nowhere else to walk it. The Meridian gunboat patrolled the open river now; Petra had seen its running lights twice since dawn, sweeping slow along the far bank like a lighthouse looking for something to warn away from itself. So they kept to the old grade instead, single file through the drowned willows, Wren ahead with the manifest wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, Oskar behind with his dive bag over one shoulder and a limp he pretended was a stroll.

Petra carried the map. Not the salvage charts from the chest—those were Voss's world, stamped and sealed and worth killing for—but her own, the one she'd drawn by hand across three seasons of survey work before the collapse, before the inquiry, before Brann Hale stood up in a hearing room with his cap in his hands and said, in a voice like an apology already rehearsed, *the trestle failed because Lockridge signed off on rotten piling.* Her map was soft at the folds now, ink gone brown, but every closed bridge, every washed-out culvert, every mile of rail pulled up for scrap was still marked there in her own careful hand. She had drawn it believing she'd be the one to reopen the lines someday. That was the cruelest part of it, looking back. Hope, in ink.

"Bridge ahead," Wren said, without turning around. She'd heard it before any of them—the faint change in the wind where a span interrupts it—and Petra felt the old professional pride stir and immediately distrust it. The girl was useful. Useful things got people hurt.

The Kestrel River crossing came out of the mist all at once: two stone pylons and a hundred feet of iron truss between them, black with rust, hung with dead vines like rigging on a hanged man. And beneath the near abutment, where the roadbed narrowed to a single lane of rotted planking, someone had built a camp. A stovepipe smoking. A tarp strung between a derrick truck and the truss itself. A lantern burning in broad daylight, which meant only one thing on a closed line.

Someone wanted to be seen.

Petra stopped walking. Oskar came up beside them, squinted, and said quietly, "That's a dredge winch. That's not a scavver's setup."

"No," Petra said. "It isn't."

Because Petra knew the silhouette of the man who stepped out from under the tarp before knowing anything else about the morning. Broad through the shoulders even now, gray coming into the beard, moving with the deliberate economy of a man who had spent thirty years walking catwalks. Brann Hale set down the tin cup he was holding, looked at the three of them across forty feet of rotten planking, and smiled the way a gate closes.

"Well," he said. "I heard the flood gave up some interesting things this spring. I wondered who'd come walking my line."

*My line.* Petra's jaw tightened. "You're squatting on a decommissioned crossing, Hale."

"I'm *maintaining* it." He spread his arms at the truss above him. "Every bolt checked twice a year. Every tie replaced where it's gone soft. You think the river leaves a bridge alone just because the trains stopped? Somebody has to keep it alive so somebody can come back." His eyes went to Wren, then Oskar, then settled on Petra with a weight like freight. "Somebody did."

"We need to cross," Petra said. Flat. Businesslike. As if the last time they'd spoken hadn't been across a courtroom.

"You always did state the obvious like it was a discovery." Hale walked to the edge of his planking and sat down on a cable drum, unhurried, making them wait. "Here's what I know, Lockridge. I know a chest came out of Miller's Bend with a dead nation's seal on it. I know the Meridian Fleet has been paying cash for information about anyone seen carrying charts. I know Magistrate Voss's office filed a salvage claim on waters that aren't his to claim, and I know what happens to witnesses these days." He picked up his cup again, blew on it. "And I know you. Which means whatever you're carrying, you'll carry it straight into the fire, and you'll want to use my bridge to do it."

"The bridge is public right-of-way."

"The bridge is mine," Hale said pleasantly, "until the railway company remembers it exists. Toll's simple." He looked at Petra's coat pocket—at the shape inside it, because of course he knew, he'd always known where everything was on a person, that was half of what made him good—and his voice gentled into something worse than menace. "The compass."

The morning went very quiet. Somewhere under the truss, water ticked against stone. Wren glanced between them, sensing the shape of a thing she didn't understand yet. Oskar shifted his weight off his bad leg.

"That compass belonged to Ilse Marchetti," Petra said. Each word placed like a spike, driven true. "She gave it to me the day she certified me. She's dead, Brann. You know she's dead. You were at the funeral."

"I carried a corner of her coffin," Hale said, unmoved. "Which is why I know exactly what it's worth to you. That's the whole point of a toll, Petra. It's supposed to cost."

"It didn't have to be your testimony." The words came out before Petra could stop them, nine years of pressure finding the crack. "One word different—one word—and the inquiry goes after the contractor who swapped the piling spec. You knew. You *told me* you knew, afterward, when it didn't matter anymore. You let them hang it on me and you went home and slept."

Hale set down the cup. When he spoke again, his voice had lost its pleasantness entirely. "I testified to what the report said. What I've regretted since is a longer conversation than this one." He stood. "Toll's the compass. Cross or don't. But if you turn back, the Fleet's gunboat is working the bend below, and I won't lie to you about your odds."

Petra's hand went to the coat pocket without permission. Through the wool, the brass casing was warm from body heat, worn smooth at the bezel where Ilse's thumb had rested ten thousand times. *North is a promise you keep to yourself,* she used to say, pressing it into Petra's palm the day of certification. *Everything else is negotiable.*

Nine years of never unpacking the bags. Nine years of odd surveys and cheap rooms and creditors' letters, all of it downstream of that hearing room, all of it bought with the weight of Hale's testimony. And now here was the bill, presented in person, on a bridge that should have been theirs to cross free.

"Petra," Wren said softly. Not a warning. Just the name, offered like a hand.

Petra took out the compass.

It was smaller in daylight than it lived in memory, brass gone dull, the glass scratched where a coal spark had kissed it in '61. Petra turned it over once, feeling the lid's familiar resistance, the click of the catch that had become the sound of being certain about something. Then held it out.

Hale took it. For a moment—just a moment—his thumb found the same worn place on the bezel, and something moved behind his eyes like water under ice. Then it was gone, and the compass disappeared into his own coat, and he jerked his head at the truss.

"Mind the third panel from the far tower. Tie's sprung. Step where I step." He was already walking, not waiting, giving them nothing else to look at. "And Lockridge—you'll want to move quick once you're over. Voss's men ride the levee road on the far side twice a day. Next pass is within the hour."

They crossed in silence. The truss hummed faintly under their boots, a note too low to hear and impossible not to feel, and below them the Kestrel ran brown and patient, carrying whatever it carried toward the sea. At the far abutment Petra stopped and looked back once. Hale stood on his cable drum with the tin cup, watching them go, and raised the cup—not a salute, Petra decided. A measurement. Weighing what he'd taken against what he'd taken it *from*, and finding the ledger not yet balanced.

Then the mist took him, and the three of them turned onto the levee road with the hour bleeding away behind them.

Wren fell into step beside Petra, close enough that her shoulder nearly brushed. "You didn't have to give it to him," she said.

"Yes, I did."

"Why?"

Petra watched the road ahead, where the drowned timber began, black and skeletal in the fog. "Because he was right about what it costs," they said. "And because a man who holds a grudge that carefully—" the words tasted like rust— "is a man who hasn't decided yet whose side he's on."

Oskar laughed once, short and humorless, from behind. "Then pray he decides before the Fleet does."

Ahead, faint through the mist, came the sound none of them wanted: hooves on the levee road, more than one horse, coming on at a trot.

* * *

They got off the road with ninety seconds to spare, down the embankment and into the drowned timber where the horses couldn't follow, water to the knee and rising, cypress knees snagging at their boots like knuckles breaking the surface of a buried fist. From the cover of a fallen trunk they watched the riders pass above—four of them, Meridian gray, carbines slung, moving with the bored confidence of men paid to find rather than to look.

Wren's breath was shallow beside Petra. "They're between us and the lock," she whispered.

"They are," Petra said. And then, watching the last rider crest the rise, noting the dispatch case strapped to his saddle and the way he rode like a man carrying orders rather than mail: "And they know we crossed the bridge. Which means either Hale sold us—" 

"—or someone told them to expect us," Wren finished.

The water ticked against the cypress. Somewhere far downstream, a boat whistle sounded, long and low, and Petra thought of the chest, the seal, the six tides bleeding away one by one, and of a compass riding north in another man's pocket.

"Hale wouldn't sell us," Oskar said. "He'd have charged *them* too."

But Petra wasn't listening anymore. Because in the mud at the water's edge, half sunk where the riders' lead horse had paused, lay a scrap of paper, rain-softened but legible—a telegraph flimsy, torn from a message blank, with a signal-tower code header that Petra had last seen on a map drawn in their own hand.

Wren saw it at the same moment. Went white.

"That's my tower's code," she breathed. "Nobody uses that header anymore. Nobody *knows* it."

Petra picked the flimsy out of the muck, careful as lifting a coal from ash, and read the few surviving words aloud:

*"…chest confirmed… subject carries twin manifest… take at halfway tide…"*

The boat whistle sounded again, closer now. And somewhere ahead, at the mid-route lock, someone was waiting who already knew exactly what they carried—and exactly where they'd have to stop.
