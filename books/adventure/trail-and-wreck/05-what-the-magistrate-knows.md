## 5. What the Magistrate Knows

The rain had been falling on Corvel Voss's window for three days, and he had come to think of it as a kind of applause.

He stood in the upper study of the magistrate's residence with a glass of plum brandy he was not drinking and watched the Meridian River swell against its levees below. From this height the water looked orderly, obedient—a long gray ribbon stitched through the valley, carrying barges and lantern-light and other men's fortunes toward the sea. It did not look like what it was: a witness that never slept, holding its secrets down in the dark silt until some fool with a rope came along and asked it questions.

Someone had asked it questions.

The report lay on his desk where his clerk had left it an hour past, weighted beneath a brass paperweight shaped like a scale—the emblem of his office, purchased by his office, from funds his office had invented out of thin air and dead men's signatures. *Chest recovered at Kettle Bend. Seal intact. Salvor unidentified.* The words were small and dry and they sat on the page like a knife laid flat.

Voss turned from the window and crossed to the desk without hurrying. He had learned, over nineteen years of public service, that hurry was a confession. He lifted the paperweight, read the report again as though it might have improved, and set it back down with a click that sounded, in the quiet room, like a lock engaging.

Kettle Bend.

Of course it was Kettle Bend. The river always gave things up at Kettle Bend; it was why he had chosen the stretch in the first place, all those years ago, when the charts were still warm and the nation whose treasury he was about to sell twice was not yet three years drowned. The current slowed there, pooled, forgot itself. Anything heavy sank into that soft gray patience and stayed. He had counted on it staying.

He poured two fingers of brandy and did not drink that either. The glass was for his hands, which wanted something to hold.

*Sold twice.* That was the phrase his own mind kept returning to, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Once to the Meridian Salvage Fleet, in a contract signed under a concession permit bearing the seal of a government that no longer existed to object—forged so cleanly that even Voss sometimes admired it, the way a man admires a counterfeit he has printed himself. And once, before that, to the private ledgers of the pay-train's last manifest, which recorded eleven strongboxes of sovereign gold consigned to a garrison that had already burned, a payment made to soldiers who were already dead. Two sales. One treasure. A sum of arithmetic that could only be resolved if one of the buyers never collected.

He had arranged for neither buyer to collect. That had been the elegant part. The train went into the water; the records went into the fire; the permit went into the archive with his own careful hand on it; and the gold went nowhere, which meant it went to him, eventually, whenever he chose to reach down and take it. Nineteen years of patience. Nineteen years of rising—one magistracy, then another, then the concessions, then the Fleet's gratitude, then the governor's ear—all of it built on the assumption that the river would keep its mouth shut.

And now a chest with an intact seal had come up out of Kettle Bend like a cork from a bottle, and somewhere downstream a salvor who did not know their own good fortune was prying at the lid of everything he had ever built.

Voss set the glass down and rang the bell.

His clerk arrived with the promptness of a man who had learned that arriving slowly was also a confession. "Sir."

"Two messages." Voss spoke to the rain rather than the clerk; it helped him find the right register, the cold administrative calm that frightened people more than shouting ever could. "First, to Fleet Commandant Ilsa Reyne, personal cipher, not the fleet band. Tell her the Kettle Bend salvage is unauthorized. Tell her the permit holders are forgeries—" and here he permitted himself the smallest pause, the kind of pause a surgeon allows before the incision, "—and that the individuals responsible are to be detained. Detained," he repeated, as though tasting the word. "Not arrested. Arrested requires paperwork, and paperwork requires courts, and courts ask questions. Detained is quieter."

"And if they resist, sir?"

"Then they will have resisted lawful authority during a flood emergency, and the Fleet will regret the necessity." He turned from the window at last and gave the clerk the full weight of his attention, which was famously worse than his displeasure. "Commandant Reyne will understand the phrasing. She has understood my phrasing for six years."

"Yes, sir. And the second message?"

"The second goes to the archive." Voss drew open the desk's lower drawer and removed a slim folio wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed cord. He had kept it there through three changes of government and one attempted audit, close enough to touch and far enough to deny. Inside were the true manifests—the ones that matched nothing, contradicted everything, and would hang him from the customs crane at Meridian Quay within an hour of public reading. "It is to be moved tonight to the strong room at Grayharbor. By cart, not rail. No manifest of its own."

The clerk's eyes flicked to the folio and away, quickly, the way one looks away from a wound. "Shall I note its contents, sir?"

"You shall note that it contains agricultural assessments." Voss smiled, and the smile reached his eyes, which was how people knew to be afraid. "Agriculture is dull. Dull things survive."

* * *

After the clerk had gone, Voss allowed himself the luxury of memory, because memory was where he kept his courage.

He remembered the night the pay-train went into the water—not the wreck itself, which he had watched from the bluffs through a surveyor's glass, the locomotive going down bow-first with its lamp still burning, a bright coin dropping into a dark pocket—but the morning after, when he had walked the flooded bend at low tide and seen, snagged in the willows, a single strongbox lid torn loose, empty, its lock sheared clean. Someone else had gotten there first. A diver, perhaps. A looter. It had taken him four days and two bribes to learn that the box had been recovered by the train's own fireman, who had drowned a week later in a tavern brawl that Voss had paid five crowns to arrange and had never once lost sleep over.

That was the lesson he had carried since: the river gives up its dead, but the dead can be made to stay dead, provided one is willing to be thorough. Thoroughness was the whole of statesmanship. Everything else was decoration.

So why, then, did his hands want the glass again?

He took it. He drank this time, and the brandy sat in him like a hot coal, and he admitted to the rain what he would admit to no living soul: that it was not the gold that frightened him. Gold could be replaced, relocated, laundered through a dozen honest-seeming enterprises until it came out the far side wearing a suit of respectability. What frightened him was the seal. An intact seal meant provenance, and provenance meant witnesses, and witnesses meant that somewhere on his river—*his* river, chartered, leased, patrolled at his expense—there were people who had touched the past and lived, and who now knew things they should not know existed.

People could be bought. People could be frightened. But first they had to be found, and finding them meant time, and time was the one cargo the tide would not wait to carry.

Six tides. His own forged permits, the beautiful forgery, the masterpiece—he had written the expiry himself, six tidal cycles from the spring flood, a window generous enough to look legitimate and narrow enough to keep rivals out. He had never imagined he would be racing his own paperwork. Somewhere downstream, whoever held that chest had only to carry it to any registry office in the territory and file, and the fiction would collapse like wet cardboard: a concession claimed by a dead nation's genuine seal, superseding a live magistrate's false one. Every audit, every inquiry, every enemy he had made in nineteen years would descend on him like gulls on a gutted fish.

Unless the chest went back into the water. Unless the hands that pulled it out went with it.

Voss crossed to the writing cabinet and unlocked the compartment where he kept the things that were not quite official: the Fleet's standing retainer, the names of three lockkeepers on his payroll, and a folded chart of the closed lines—hand-drawn, annotated in a cramped confident script he recognized instantly and had hoped never to see again. *P. Lockridge.* The disgraced engineer. The woman whose testimony had once been worth buying and whose silence had been worth keeping, until the collapse at Cinder Viaduct had made her worthless and dangerous in the same breath. He had assumed her finished—drowned in debt and drink like all the others he had fed to the machinery.

Apparently the river had other ideas. Apparently the river had handed her, of all people, the very chest.

He laughed once, softly, without humor. There was a symmetry to it that he might have appreciated in another life, one where he had been an honest man with honest enemies. Lockridge had the map. Lockridge had the chest. Lockridge had, if the reports were accurate, nothing left to lose—which made her the most dangerous creature on the water, more dangerous than any gunboat, because a person with nothing left to lose does not stop to negotiate.

"Detain them," he murmured to the rain. "All of them. Leave no witnesses."

But even as he said it, drafting the order in his head with the fluency of long practice, some cold animal part of him—the part that had survived the audit, the brawl-bribes, the nineteen years—whispered that orders sent downriver had a way of arriving late, or arriving wrong, or arriving exactly on time to find the bird already flown. Words on a wire could be intercepted. He had read enough intercepted traffic to know.

Which meant, he thought, turning back to the window, watching the brown water climb another hand's width up the levee stones, that he would have to go himself.

The thought settled over him like the rain: unwelcome, persistent, inevitable. Commandant Reyne was competent but ambitious, and ambitious subordinates sometimes filed the wrong papers. The Fleet answered to his concessions but not to his conscience, assuming he had one, which he had long ago converted into more liquid assets. If the ledgers were to burn—and they would burn, they must burn, along with everyone who had read them—then he wanted to stand on the deck and watch the smoke with his own eyes.

He returned to the desk, drew a fresh sheet of the thick cream paper reserved for warrants, and began to write. His pen moved without hesitation. That was the thing his enemies had never understood about Corvel Voss: he did not hesitate, because hesitation was a confession, and he had spent a lifetime ensuring that the only confessions in the territory were the ones he authored himself.

*By order of the Magistrate of the Meridian Concessions,* the pen scratched, *all salvage operations at Kettle Bend and points downstream are declared unlawful...*

Outside, the river rose. Somewhere down that swollen dark ribbon, a chest was open, a map was unfolding, and six tides were counting themselves down one by one like coins dropped into a well.

Voss sealed the warrant, rang for the clerk, and gave the order that would set the whole valley burning:

"Signal the Fleet. Full dispatch. And tell Commandant Reyne—" he smiled at the rain, and this time the smile did not reach anywhere at all, "—that I will be joining her aboard the flagship by morning tide. There are witnesses on my river. I intend to meet them personally."

The clerk bowed and withdrew. The rain applauded. And downriver, unseen, the first lanterns of the Meridian Salvage Fleet were already swinging out into the flood, black hulls and running lights, coming on like a slow cold tide of iron.
