## 6. Halfway Tide

Oskar Vane had spent thirty years learning what water sounded like when it lied.

A lock told you everything, if you listened. The groan of the gates, the gurgle in the culverts, the way the current shouldered against the masonry—all of it spoke a plain language of hydraulics and neglect. This one, the Halfway Lock on the old Meridian cutoff, was speaking wrong. Too clean. The gears above the gatehouse were greased. The lamp in the tower burned steady instead of guttering, which meant someone had trimmed the wick recently, and nobody trims wicks on a line that's been closed for nine years unless they're expecting company.

He said nothing about it. Not yet. He'd learned that lesson too—on another river, another life, when he'd named his suspicions aloud and a man he loved had gone into the water for it.

The *Kestrel's Debt* came down the cut at dusk, riding low with coal and mistrust. Petra stood at the tiller with that compass of hers open in her palm like a prayer she didn't believe in anymore. Wren sat on the cabin roof with her knees drawn up, and she hadn't spoken to anyone since morning. Hale's bridge was two days behind them now, and whatever Petra had paid there—the compass, Oskar had seen it go, had watched the girl watch it go—had settled over the boat like fog.

"Lock looks tended," Petra said, easing back on the throttle.

"Tended," Oskar agreed, and let the word sit there, heavy as bilge water.

Wren looked up. She was quick, the girl; quicker than either of them liked to admit. Her eyes went from the greased gears to the trimmed lamp to Oskar's face, reading the same sentence he was.

"Closed lines don't get greased," she said quietly.

"No," Oskar said. "They don't."

Petra brought the boat in anyway, because there was no other way through, and because Petra Lockridge would rather drown than admit a road was shut to her. That was the thing about her that Oskar both admired and intended, someday, to survive: she treated every closed door as a personal insult.

* * *

The lockkeeper came out wiping his hands on a rag, and he was all wrong.

Oskar knew the breed. Real lockkeepers on dead lines went gray and stooped and half-deaf, their hands cracked from cold iron. This man was forty, square through the shoulders, with the particular stillness of someone who'd been taught to stand so his weight stayed off his heels. His boots were government issue. His rag was cleaner than his job required.

"Evening," the man called up. "Through-passage is a shilling, plus inspection. New regulations."

"On a closed line," Petra said flatly.

"New regulations," the man repeated, pleasant as milk.

Oskar crouched at the rail and made himself busy coiling rope while his eyes did the talking. Sidearm under the left lapel of the coat—he could see the print of it when the man raised his arms. And on the gatehouse wall, half-hidden by ivy, a signal pennant hung furled but fresh-rigged, the hoist line still bright. Meridian colors. He'd served under them long enough to know the weave.

He caught Wren's eye and tipped his head a fraction toward the pennant. She saw it. Her face did something complicated and then went very still, the way it did when she was filing something away in that telegraph mind of hers.

The lock gates groaned open ahead of them, and the *Kestrel's Debt* slid into the chamber like a coin into a purse.

* * *

It happened fast, the way bad things do—no thunder, just a small wrong sound and then the world rearranged.

The lockkeeper's hand found a lever inside the gatehouse, and the far gate, the one they needed open, clanked shut instead. Chains rattled. Behind them, the near gate began to close with the patience of a trap. Water started draining from the chamber—not the normal draw-down but a hard pull, the culverts opened wide, and Oskar felt the deck tilt and understood: they meant to pin the boat on the sill, crack her hull on the masonry, and pick through the wreckage at leisure.

"Meridian!" he shouted, and threw himself at the gatehouse door.

The lockkeeper was faster than he looked. The sidearm came out smooth, no shouting, no warning—a professional's gun—and Oskar took the first shot across the shoulder as he crashed through the doorway, wood splintering, and then they were grappling between the levers, two men wrestling in a room full of machinery designed to crush things.

"Petra—" he got out. "Gates—reverse the—"

He didn't finish, because a fist like a dock bollard took him in the mouth.

But he'd said enough. Through the gatehouse window, warped glass and all, he saw Petra at the stern hauling on the manual override, veins standing out in her forearms, and Wren—Wren was running along the gunwale toward the near gate's chain housing with a hatchet from the galley, hacking at the pawl like the world's smallest, angriest shipbreaker.

And then the lockkeeper's voice carried, impossibly calm, pitched to carry, over the noise of the water:

"Ask her about Brann Hale! Ask her what she traded at that bridge! Ask her who sold the survey crew's route to the Fleet nine years ago!"

Oskar, bleeding onto the floor plates, felt the words land even before he saw them land on Wren.

Because he knew. God help him, he'd known since the bar, since the night Petra bought him with three drinks and a chart. He'd heard the stories in the riverside dives—*the engineer who walked away from the collapse, the engineer whose testimony Hale gave and whose route the Fleet somehow had.* He'd chosen not to ask, the way you choose not to look at a tumor. There was always a reason a good engineer was broke. He'd decided hers didn't matter.

Wren had stopped hacking. She stood on the gunwale with the hatchet hanging loose, staring at Petra, and the distance between them was suddenly wider than the lock chamber.

"Wren!" Petra's voice cracked like green wood. "The chain! *Now!*"

The girl didn't move.

That hesitation cost them everything. The near gate sealed with a boom that Oskar felt in his teeth. Boots on the towpath—four men, six, coming out of the dark where the ivy hid the pennant, coming with lanterns and rifles and the unhurried confidence of people closing a jar.

Oskar did the arithmetic he'd spent thirty years doing. Boat pinned on the sill in twenty minutes. Crew captured or dead. Charts seized, chest seized, everyone hanged by noon on some forged charge or simply disappeared, which was Meridian's preferred paperwork.

Unless somebody made noise.

He rolled, took the lockkeeper's wrist, and put the man's own pistol barrel against the window glass, and fired twice at the culvert control—the great iron valve wheel on the chamber floor, the one feeding the drain. The wheel wasn't the valve, but the pipe junction beside it was old cast iron, and old cast iron, given two bullets and a prayer, will fail.

It failed beautifully. Water burst upward through the grating in a black column, flooding the gatehouse knee-deep in seconds, and every lantern on the towpath swung toward the explosion of spray and noise.

"GO!" Oskar roared, and meant it for both of them.

He saw Petra move—saw her haul Wren bodily off the gunwale, saw the girl resist for one horrible heartbeat, saw something pass between them that was not forgiveness but might have been its raw material. Then the pinnace, the little tender lashed to the roof, splashed down into the churning chamber, and Petra shoved Wren into it and jumped after.

The lockkeeper hit Oskar across the back of the head with the pistol butt.

The world went white, then sideways, then wet. He came to on the grating with floodwater in his mouth and boots splashing around him and someone saying *the diver, keep the diver,* and he thought, with the strange clarity of a man already halfway to drowning: *they want me alive because I know the wreck site.*

Well. Let them want.

There was a maintenance grate in the chamber wall—he'd marked it on the way in, because marking exits was the only religion he'd kept. He pulled himself to it on arms that shook, wrenched it open, and slid into the cold black throat of the spillway with a bullet tearing across his calf on the way, a hot bright line that barely registered against the rest of him.

The current took him. It was filthy and freezing and it wanted him dead, and Oskar Vane had never been more at home.

* * *

He came out through the overflow sluice half a mile downstream, spat out onto a gravel bar like something the river had chewed and rejected. The rain had started, fine and needling. Upstream, the lock glowed with lantern light, and the *Kestrel's Debt* sat pinned and listing on the sill, her name catching the light—*Kestrel's Debt*, Christ, he'd never asked her why she'd named her that, and now he supposed he never would.

No sign of the tender. No sign of Petra or the girl. Either they'd run the spillway behind him or gone overland through drowned timber, and either way they were afoot, wet, hunted, and half a family short.

Oskar lay on the gravel and pressed his palm against his calf and felt the blood pulse between his fingers, steady as a telegraph key. Somewhere north, a Fleet gunboat was turning. Somewhere south, a magistrate was sleeping badly. And somewhere in between, a girl who believed Petra Lockridge had sold a crew to die was following that same woman into the wilderness, carrying a hatchet and a grudge and God knew what else.

He laughed, and it hurt, and he laughed anyway, because the alternative was thinking too clearly.

Then he got his feet under him, tore a strip from his shirt, and tied off the leg, and started limping south after his crew—after the only family he'd managed to ruin in under a week.

Behind him, faint through the rain, he heard the lock gates begin to grind open, and voices calling orders, and he understood that they weren't chasing the boat anymore.

They were letting it sit there as bait.

And somewhere ahead, soaked and silent and splitting apart, Petra and Wren were walking straight toward it.
