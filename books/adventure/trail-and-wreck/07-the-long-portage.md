## 7. The Long Portage

The drowned timber began where the lock's smoke ended.

Wren had never walked through a forest that was also a river. The pines stood to their shoulders in brown water, their bark furred green with algae, and between them the current moved with a patience she found worse than speed—slow, deliberate, like something reading over her shoulder. The portage trail was a rumor of a trail: a sandbar spine that surfaced at low water, marked every hundred yards by a stake someone had driven decades ago, each one leaning at a different angle of surrender.

She carried the coil of telegraph wire on her back, forty pounds of it, because Petra said the line might still run the length of the portage if any of the old insulators held, and because carrying it made Wren feel useful when everything else made her feel like a traitor.

Oskar rode the salvaged skiff they'd hauled along the sandbar on rollers cut from fence posts, his leg bound in a splint of oar-stock and linen, his face gray as driftwood. He hadn't spoken since the lock. He looked at Wren only once an hour, and each time it was the same look—not anger, which she could have borne, but arithmetic. The look of a man recalculating what a person was worth.

Petra walked point, machete in hand, cutting deadfall from the stakes without being asked twice. Petra hadn't blamed her either. That was the worst part. At the lock, when the false lockkeeper's lie had come apart in Wren's hands—*he sold Hale out, girl, ask him about the Kestrel Bridge collapse, ask him why his own engineer took the fall*—Wren had frozen, and the boat had been seized, and Oskar had bled for it. And afterward Petra had only said, *We move. Grief is a luxury we'll buy later,* and started walking.

But grief kept its own schedule. It caught up with Wren somewhere around the fourth mile, in a stand of cedars so thick the water beneath them went black, and it arrived not as guilt exactly but as a question she couldn't put down:

What if the lie was true?

* * *

They made camp at dusk on a rise the flood hadn't reached—a gravel bar under a leaning oak, dry enough to matter. Petra built a fire small enough to hide and large enough to heat the tin of beans, and while it burned down, Petra talked.

Not lecturing. Wren noticed that. Petra talked the way people talk to a fire, sideways, watching the flames instead of the listener.

"You want to know about Kestrel Bridge," Petra said. "You've been chewing on it since the lock. So."

Wren sat very still.

"Kestrel Bridge was mine. My span, my crew, my signature on the inspection reports. Three years ago, spring thaw, a freight train went into the gorge with eleven men aboard." A pause. The fire popped. "The official finding was faulty inspection. My inspection. I'd signed off on timbers I'd supposedly checked myself, and the report said I'd falsified the load ratings to save the company money on replacement iron."

"But you didn't," Wren said. It wasn't a question. She'd heard enough of Petra by now to know the shape of the truth before it was spoken.

"I didn't. The timbers were sound. What failed was the pier footing—the stone work, contracted out, paid for twice on paper and built once in fact. Somebody skimmed the footing contract down to mud and pocketed the difference, and when the bridge went down, somebody else needed a scapegoat fast, because the man who'd signed the footing contracts was—" Petra turned a stick in the coals. "Was Corvel Voss. Then a land commissioner. Not yet a magistrate. Hungry."

The name landed in Wren's chest like a dropped wrench.

"He forged your report?"

"He didn't have to forge much. He had access to my signature—he processed the paperwork through his office. He changed three numbers and let the inquiry do the rest. Eleven men, Wren. The territory needed someone to hang it on, and a disgraced engineer with no family and no friends in high places was cheaper than a commissioner with a career ahead of him." Petra finally looked up, and the firelight did something complicated to that face. "I lost my license, my pension, and every job above ditch-digging. Hale testified against me. That part's true too. Whether he knew he was lying—I've spent three years deciding not to decide."

Wren thought of the lockkeeper's voice, warm and reasonable: *ask him about the betrayal.* She had wanted so badly for it to be true. A true betrayal would have excused hers. If Petra was rotten, then Wren freezing at the lock, doubting the person feeding her, was just good sense instead of what it actually was.

"And you never told anyone," she said. "You just carried it."

"Telling requires someone who'd listen. Disgrace is a story people already believe. You can't argue with a story they're enjoying." Petra fed the stick to the fire. "Voss counted on that. It's how he does everything. He doesn't build lies, he builds audiences."

Wren wrapped her arms around her knees. The wire coil dug into her back where she'd set it down, and she was suddenly, fiercely glad of the weight of it. "At the lock," she said. "When the agent told me—I believed him. For almost a whole minute I believed him, and I froze, and Oskar—"

"Oskar chose to cover us. That was his arithmetic, not your fault." Petra's voice gentled by a degree, no more; Petra's voice was a machine that ran hot and governed itself. "You want to make it right? Then learn this: Voss will use you again. He'll use your doubt, your youth, whatever's loose in you. Next time he offers you a truth, check whose hands it makes clean. His lies always come pre-forgiven."

Wren lay back on the gravel and stared up through the oak branches at the first stars, and she did not sleep for a long time, and when she slept she dreamed of telegraph keys clicking in empty towers, sending forgiveness to stations where no one was listening anymore.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of tin, and the water had risen overnight—the flood country breathing in its sleep—and the sandbar spine had narrowed to a hand's width in places. They waded. Oskar cursed steadily and creatively from the skiff, which everyone pretended was a good sign.

It was near midday, crossing a wide slack pool where the drowned timber opened onto gray sky, that Wren saw the tower.

It rose out of the water a quarter mile east: a signal tower, decommissioned like her own, its ladder legs submerged to the cross-braces, its roofline tilted, but standing. And hanging from its stub mast, barely moving in the still air, a signal flag. Faded, but rigged fresh—the halyard line was bright against the rot.

Someone maintained this tower. Someone still climbed it.

"Petra." Her voice came out strange. "That tower has a working halyard."

Petra shaded her eyes. "So?"

"So decommissioned towers don't get new rope unless someone's using them. And if someone's using them—" Wren was already unshouldering the wire coil, heart hammering, "—there may be a live line running the portage after all. The old maintenance trunk. It followed the trail stakes. If even one segment survived the flooding—"

Petra looked at her for a long moment, and something passed across that guarded face that Wren would spend years learning to read: hope, handled carefully, like a loaded tool.

"How long to rig it?" Petra asked.

"Give me the tower and an hour."

* * *

The climb was misery. The lower ladder rungs were underwater and slick with weed; Wren went up barefoot, boots slung around her neck, testing each rung like a liar tests a floorboard. Halfway up, a rung gave with a crack and she hung by her hands over the brown water, lungs full of cold terror, until her heel found the next bracket. Above the waterline the tower dried out and steadied, and the little instrument room at the top smelled of dust and bird droppings and, impossibly, faintly, of oil—someone had oiled something here within a season.

Inside: a key. An actual brass key on a wooden base, wired to a battery jar gone cloudy but intact, and a line coming in through the wall on ceramic insulators, green with age but unbroken as far as she could follow it toward the west—which was toward the closed lines. Toward Hale's territory.

Her hands shook as she grounded herself, tested the circuit, and got—she pressed her ear to the old headphones, cracked at the band—faint line hum. Alive. Degraded, lossy, half-dead, but alive, the way a river under ice is alive.

She could send. She couldn't be sure anyone would hear.

And then she thought of the flag, and the fresh halyard, and the oiled key, and she understood: someone *did* hear. Someone kept this tower the way she'd kept hers. And there was only one code in all the flooded territories that a keeper of a dead tower would still honor, because it was the code the tower keepers had used among themselves, before the companies, before the concessions—the old relay cant, the tower-talker's shorthand, station-to-station, man to man, that no commercial operator bothered to learn and no Fleet officer would recognize.

Hale had come up through the towers. Everyone knew that. Before the bridges, before the betrayals, Brann Hale had been a tower boy on the upper lines. He would know the cant. He might hate her for using it. He might ignore it entirely.

He might also be the only soul on this river who would answer.

Wren thought about the lock. About Oskar's blood on the planks. About Petra saying *check whose hands it makes clean*, and about the look on Petra's face when she'd said *eleven men*—not self-pity, Wren realized now, but inventory. Counting the dead she'd been made to carry.

Her fingers settled on the key.

She sent it slow, because the line was bad and the cant demanded clarity: *TOWER TO TOWER. STATION DARK THREE YEARS. REQUEST RELAY. TRAFFIC CONCERNS COMMON ENEMY. WILL HOLD WATCH UNTIL ANSWER OR SUNDOWN.*

Then she waited, headphones pressed to her ears, the river breathing below her, the sun crawling west.

* * *

Sundown was twenty minutes off when it came.

Not words first—just a shift in the hum, a texture change, the way the line itself seemed to lean in. Then, halting, rusty, spelled in the old cant like a man remembering a language from childhood:

*TOWER TO TOWER. IDENTIFY.*

Wren's eyes stung. She keyed back: *WREN. SIGNAL GIRL OFF THE CLOSED LINE. TRAVELING WITH PETRA LOCKRIDGE AND TWO OTHERS. MERIDIAN FLEET BEHIND US WITH SEIZURE ORDERS AND LEAVE-NO-WITNESSES INSTRUCTIONS FROM VOSS HIMSELF. WE HAVE EVIDENCE OF FORGED PERMITS ON THE SALVAGE CONCESSION. WE ARE ONE DAY OUT OF YOUR DREDGE YARD ON FOOT. HALE. I KNOW WHAT YOU DID AT THE INQUIRY. SHE KNOWS TOO. SHE CAME ANYWAY.*

A long silence. Long enough for the sundown to start going purple in the west, long enough for Wren's hope to begin the familiar business of dying quietly.

Then the key below spoke again, slower now, and she could hear—in the rhythm, in the hesitations—something that was not hostility. Something more like a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy.

*SHE CAME ANYWAY,* Hale repeated. *THAT IS EITHER FORGIVENESS OR A TRAP.*

*IT'S A PORTAGE,* Wren sent back. *WE'RE OUT OF BOAT AND NEARLY OUT OF TIME. ONE TIDE LEFT AFTER TOMORROW'S.*

Another pause. When the answer came, it was short, and it was all she needed:

*BURN YOUR FIRE HIGH TONIGHT. MY BARGE RUNS THE OLD CHANNEL AT FIRST LIGHT. IF THIS IS A TRAP, GIRL, I'LL SEE YOU HANG BEFORE THEY DO.*

Wren sat back, headphones sliding down around her neck, and laughed—one cracked, disbelieving note that startled the pigeons out of the eaves. Below, the campfire was a spark on the darkening water, and she could see Petra's silhouette standing watch beside it, and Oskar's shape propped against the skiff, and she understood that she had just done the bravest and most frightening thing of her life, which was to bet on people.

Then the key chattered again, unprompted, and the humor had gone out of Hale's rhythm entirely.

*GIRL. ONE MORE TRAFFIC. FLEET GUNBOAT PASSED MY YARD AT DUSK RUNNING DARK, HEADED DOWNRIVER. IT WASN'T FOLLOWING YOU.*

Wren went cold. *THEN WHO—*

*IT HEADED FOR THE WRECK SITE. VOSS ISN'T CHASING YOU ANYMORE. HE'S GOING TO FINISH THE JOB BEFORE YOU GET THERE. FIRST LIGHT, GIRL. DON'T BE LATE.*
