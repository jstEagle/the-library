## 8. Hale's Choice

The fog came off the water at dusk the way it always did on the Sallow Reach—slow, deliberate, like something rising from a grave it had second thoughts about. Brann Hale stood on the deck of his dredge barge *Iron Matron* and watched it swallow the drowned timber line by line, and he told himself, for the hundredth time that day, that what he was about to do was business.

Meridian's contract sat in his breast pocket, folded twice, warm from his own body heat. Three pages of clean legal language promising him a salvage share large enough to buy back everything the river had ever taken from him: his yard, his crew, his name. All he had to do was hold the narrows until the Fleet's gunboat arrived and hand over four people who would be dead by morning regardless of whether he was the one holding them.

That was the arithmetic Voss's agent had given him. Dead by morning either way. Your hands clean or your hands dirty, but the ledger balances the same.

Hale had spent eleven years believing in ledgers.

He heard them before he saw them—the particular slap of oars wrapped in cloth, muffled, someone trying to be quiet on water that carried every sound like a church carries a cough. He raised one hand and his two hired men killed their lantern, and then the fog gave them up: a longboat riding low, four shapes in it, moving through the narrows like a wound moving through cloth.

"Easy," Hale called into the dark, and his voice came out rougher than he'd intended. "You're in my channel."

The oars stopped. A long silence, the kind that has weight. Then Petra Lockridge's voice, low and level as a surveyor's line: "Brann."

"Petra." He let the name sit there between them, all its history in it. "Come alongside. Slow. My men have rifles and I've got no patience left to waste."

* * *

They came aboard with their hands visible, which was a courtesy he hadn't earned. Lockridge first—he'd know that silhouette anywhere, that particular way of standing like a man braced against a wind no one else felt. Then a big diver favoring one arm, blood dried black along his sleeve. Then a girl, sixteen maybe, with wire-burned fingers and eyes that went everywhere at once, cataloging exits. And last, hunched under an oilskin, a fourth figure Hale didn't get a good look at before Lockridge stepped sideways to block the sightline.

Protecting them. Even now. Even walking into a trap they plainly suspected was a trap.

"You knew," Hale said. "Coming through the narrows. You knew I'd sold you."

"I figured the odds," Petra said. "Voss doesn't leave loose ends, and you were the loosest end on this stretch of water." Those gray eyes found his and held, and there was no hatred in them, which was somehow worse than hatred. There was just tiredness, and underneath it something that might once have been respect, buried so deep it had gone to fossil. "I'm not here to beg, Brann. Do what you're paid for."

And God help him, Hale almost did it. Almost whistled his men out of the dark. Eleven years of interest on one night's work: the testimony, the inquiry board, the way Lockridge had stood in that hearing room and taken the collapse onto those shoulders without a word of defense while Hale read his prepared statement in a voice that never shook. The compass had been in his pocket that day too. He'd told himself it was evidence. He'd told himself a lot of things.

"Search them," he said instead, because the script demanded it, because the Meridian man was watching from the pilothouse shadow and the contract was warm against his ribs. His men went through the longboat's meager cargo: a chart case, a coil of telegraph wire, a canvas bag of tools. No gold. Of course no gold—they hadn't reached the wreck yet. They were running on nothing but belief.

It was the girl who broke first. Not with tears—with fury, which Hale respected more. "You took her compass," she said, stepping forward before the diver could catch her arm. "At the bridge. She paid your toll and you took the last thing her mentor ever gave her, and you're going to stand there and sell us anyway? What are you, Brann Hale? What's left under the rust?"

"Hush, Wren," Petra said quietly.

"No." The girl's voice cracked across the deck like a whip-crack on ice. "No, I intercepted his traffic too, same as Meridian's. I know what he signed. I know what he gets. I want him to say it out loud. Say what a man is worth."

Hale looked at her—at this runaway child calling down judgment from a dredge deck—and felt something shift in his chest, some plate of armor he hadn't known was riveted there coming loose with a sound only he could hear.

Then the fourth figure moved.

* * *

The oilskin came back and Hale's breath stopped, because beneath it was a woman in her sixties with iron-gray hair and a face he had seen in dreams for twenty years. Maren Oduya. His teacher before she was Petra's. The woman who had taught them both to read water, both to love the closed lines, both to believe a railroad was a promise made in steel and kept by hand.

She was supposed to be dead. Everyone said so. The flood, three winters back, the whole lower settlement written off.

"You," Hale managed.

"Me." Maren's voice was gravel and river-bottom. "Surprised you didn't check the body, Brann. You always were quick to sign off on things without looking." She held out her hand, palm up. In it lay his old brass compass—not the one he'd taken as toll, but its twin, the pair their mentor had commissioned decades ago, one for each of the students she'd believed in. Hale's had been lost with the *Sallow Queen*, or so he'd thought. "Petra sent word ahead. Through tower codes, on a frequency only you'd honor. You remember the old signal-line codes, Brann? The ones we used before Meridian bought the spectrum?" Her mouth twisted. "Of course you don't. You sold that part of yourself along with the rest."

"The girl sent it," Hale said slowly. Understanding arriving like cold water. "The message that came through yesterday. *Old code, third relay, requesting verification.* I thought it was a ghost."

"It was," Maren said. "Ghosts of the men we used to be. Petra asked me to give you this when the time was right. Said you'd know what it meant." She pressed the compass into his hand and closed his fingers over it, and her grip was still strong enough to hurt. "It means somebody kept faith with you for twenty years, you stubborn wreck of a man. Kept faith even after you helped bury her. Now decide what you're worth. Out loud, since the girl wants to hear it."

The fog pressed close. Somewhere downstream, faint as a heartbeat, Hale could hear the Fleet gunboat's engine idling against the current—coming for its cargo, right on schedule.

He opened his hand. The compass lay in his palm, needle swinging, finding north the way it always had, the way it had on every survey he and Petra had ever run together, back when the future was a thing they drew maps of instead of traps for each other.

* * *

What happened next took less than a minute, though Hale would spend the rest of his life remembering every second of it.

He reached into his breast pocket and pulled out the Meridian contract—all three clean pages of it—and walked to the *Iron Matron*'s firebox, where the boiler's belly glowed orange through the grate. Voss's man lunged out of the pilothouse shouting, and one of Hale's own hired men—Toma, who had been with him since the yard days, who had never once asked why the money had stopped being enough—put out an arm and stopped him, gentle as a fence gate.

"Brann—" the Meridian man started.

"You tell Corvel Voss," Hale said, feeding the contract into the fire page by page, watching the ink curl and bloom into flame, watching his salvage share and his clean hands and his eleven years of careful arithmetic go up together, "that the narrows are closed. Tell him the engineer who holds them remembers whose water this used to be." He turned, and the firelight made his shadow enormous against the fog. "And tell him the *Matron* will be at the wreck site by the last tide. With witnesses. Plenty of witnesses."

The man fled over the side into his own launch, and the fog took him, and Hale stood there breathing hard like a man who had just set down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying.

"Well," said the diver, Oskar, pressing his wounded arm and grinning through the pain. "That's the most expensive piece of paper anybody ever burned."

"It wasn't expensive," Hale said. "It was free. That was the problem. Everything worth having, I got free, and everything that mattered, I sold." He looked at Petra. The old wound opened between them, all its scar tissue glowing. "I owe you a testimony, Lockridge. Twenty years late. It won't un-drown anyone."

"No," Petra said. "But it might hang the man who did the drowning." A pause. The fog dripped from the rigging. "I'll take the barge instead."

Hale almost laughed. "The *Matron* draws nine feet loaded. The upper channel to the wreck site silted shut two seasons ago—that's why nobody's run salvage in there. You'd need a dredge to open a path, and you'd need it done inside one tide."

"That's why I need you," Petra said simply. "Not your forgiveness. Your boat. And your hands on her controls, because nobody alive knows that channel better." Those gray eyes didn't look away. "I'm asking, Brann. Not ordering. Asking."

The girl—Wren—had gone very still, watching him the way you watch a fuse burn.

Hale looked down at the compass in his hand. North, steady. He thought of the hearing room, of the statement he'd read, of the way Maren had turned her back on him afterward and how he'd deserved it. He thought of the gunboat coming upriver with orders to leave no witnesses, and of four people in a leaking longboat who had come through his narrows anyway because the map they trusted ran through him.

Redemption, he understood suddenly, wasn't a thing you were given. It was a thing you steered toward, against the current, with your hands full of other people's lives.

"Get your gear aboard," he said. "All of it. We make way within the hour, on the flood tide, and we don't stop." He was already moving toward the pilothouse, already feeling the old commands come back to his hands like a language he'd pretended to forget. "Oskar—I'll want your eyes on the suction lines when we start cutting silt. Girl—Wren—you're on the wireless. If the Fleet moves early, I want to know before they do."

"And me?" Maren asked, from the rail, smiling like the river itself.

"You," Hale said, "are going to tell me exactly how badly I've been steering these past twenty years. Start with the worst."

* * *

They cast off into the fog with the *Iron Matron*'s lamps burning wide and defiant, a lit thing on a dark river, impossible to mistake for anything but what she was. Hale took the wheel himself. Behind him he could hear the crew settling—Petra spreading charts on the mess table, Wren's key starting its bright insect chatter, Oskar laughing at something despite the arm—and the sound of it went through Hale like warm water through cold steel.

Downstream, the gunboat's engine note changed. Coming about. Coming for them.

Hale rang for full steam and put the bow into the current, and the old dredge answered like she'd been waiting eleven years for someone to ask.

"One tide," he said to the wheel, to the river, to the ghost at his shoulder. "Hold together, old girl. We're going home the long way."
