## 9. One Tide to Spare

The storm came up the valley like something with a grudge.

Petra felt it before she saw it—a pressure behind the ears, a taste of iron on the wind, the river going strange and glassy in the last light. Then the clouds broke over the western ridge and the whole sky turned the color of wet slate, and the first fat drops hit the barge deck like thrown gravel.

"Surge's coming down the gorge," Hale shouted from the wheelhouse, one hand on the throttle, his voice half-swallowed by the rising wind. "Meridian will know it too. They'll run for shelter or they'll run for us."

"They'll run for us," Petra said. "Voss doesn't shelter. Voss spends other people."

The dredge barge plowed up the flooded channel with her old survey lines humming under Petra's hands—lines she'd drawn herself, years ago, when this stretch of the Meridian watershed was still open country and she was still a name people said without spitting. The map rode in her breast pocket, soft as cloth now, its corners furred from handling. Every bend, every drowned fence line, every cut where the water ran deep enough to hide a man from a gunboat. She'd drawn it as an engineer. Now it was a smuggler's chart, and it fit her hand like it had always meant to.

Wren crouched in the signal shack Hale had bolted to the aft deck—a salvaged telegraph office no bigger than a coffin, its brass fittings green with river rot. She'd been keying steadily for an hour, tapping out test bursts toward the relay towers downstream, listening to the storm chew the static.

"Line's still alive," she called. "Half the stations are abandoned but the wire remembers. It always remembers." Her eyes were bright and feverish in the lamplight. "You give me the word, I can put Voss's forged permits into every operator's ear between here and the coast."

"The word comes when we have proof in hand," Petra said. "Not before. If we broadcast early, he scuttles the wreck and we're left with nothing but a story."

"And if we wait too long?"

Petra looked at Oskar, who sat on the hatch coaming checking his dive lines for the third time, his big hands moving with a gentleness that never failed to undo something in her chest. The wound in his shoulder from Halfway Tide had closed to an angry pink seam. He'd insisted he could dive. He'd looked at her when he said it, daring her to treat him like cargo.

"We won't wait too long," she said.

* * *

The gauntlet began at the Narrows, where the channel pinched between two drowned hillsides and the Fleet had anchored a picket boat athwart the current, its searchlight sweeping the rain in long white blades.

"Hold her in the lee of the near bank," Petra told Hale. "There's a deadfall eddy behind the old mile marker—current runs backward there. We drift through stern-first and their light reads us as debris."

"Debris," Hale repeated. His mouth twisted—not quite a smile, not quite a sneer. "Twenty years I've wanted to see you run a river like a thief, Lockridge. Never thought I'd be the one steering."

"You wanted to see me fail. This is close enough."

He laughed then, short and rough, and put the barge into the eddy, and for four minutes that lasted a season they drifted backward through the searchlight's blind seam while the picket boat's crew stared straight through them at empty rain. Petra counted her own heartbeats because counting anything else would have been surrender.

Then the Narrows spat them out, and the wreck lay two miles upstream, and the tide clock in Petra's head began to ring.

Six tides, the charts had promised. Five spent running. The sixth tide—the salvage window, the legal window, the only window—closed at moonrise. Whatever came up out of that water before moonrise belonged to whoever held paper on it. And the only paper in the world that mattered was sealed inside a chest at the bottom of a flooded rail cut, forty feet down, in a storm surge.

"Oskar," she said.

He was already standing. Already pulling the suit up over his shoulders. Rain streamed off his bare skull and he grinned at her through it, that gap-toothed grin that had survived a dive bar, a betrayal, a bullet-graze, and twenty years of bad luck.

"Forty feet, dirty water, surge current," he said. "You know how many times I've dived worse?"

"None. You've never dived worse. That's why you're going to come back up."

"I'm going to come back up," he agreed, "because you're going to be so angry if I don't that you'll dive down after me yourself, and you swim like a brick." He gripped her forearm, engineer to engineer, the old grip of men who trusted each other with loads. "Stall Voss. Wren sings. I'll bring up your nation."

He went over the side with barely a splash, and the river took him, and Petra stood at the rail watching the bubbles rise until the rain erased even those.

* * *

Voss came aboard at the wreck site itself, which told Petra everything about how far the man had fallen from caution.

His launch ground against the dredge's hull in the heaving dark, and he climbed the ladder with two Fleet marines behind him, his magistrate's coat soaked black, his face pale as drowned flesh. He stepped onto the tilting deck as though the deck should apologize to him.

"Lockridge." Even shouting over the storm, his voice had that dry-paper quality, the voice of a man reading someone else's death warrant. "You are operating in a restricted concession without permit. I am authorized to seize this vessel and detain its crew."

"Authorized by whom?" Petra called back. "Show me the concession papers, Corvel. Show me the seal."

Something flickered behind his eyes. Just a flicker. But Petra had spent a career reading stress in iron—in bridge trusses, in boiler seams, in the first hairline whisper before a structure gives—and she saw it now in him: the load bearing, the metal singing.

"You know very well the permits are filed with the territorial registry."

"Filed by your own clerk. Witnessed by your own cousin. Dated three days before the dead nation's treasury was declared forfeit—which means, Magistrate, that you sold salvage rights to a train wreck before the train was legally allowed to have sunk." She took a step toward him across the pitching deck, rain hammering her shoulders. "I've read the chest, Corvel. I've read the ledgers. You didn't sell the treasury once. You sold it twice—to the Fleet and to the coastal syndicate—and the second buyers don't know about the first."

Below them, faint through the hull, the air pump chattered. Oskar was down there. Oskar was working.

"You have nothing," Voss said. "No court will hear—"

"Every station on this line will hear." Petra jerked her chin toward the signal shack, where lamplight glowed and Wren's hands hovered over the key. "The girl you never bothered to count is sitting on your confession, and the wire runs through every town you ever cheated. You built your empire on a line nobody watches anymore, Corvel. That was your mistake. Dead lines keep secrets. Live ones don't."

The deck lurched. A wave came green over the starboard rail and everyone staggered, and in the staggering Voss's hand went inside his coat, and one of the marines raised his rifle—

And stopped. Because Wren's key had begun to sing.

They all heard it—that thin metallic chatter threading through the storm like a sewing machine stitching the sky shut. Dot and dash, dot and dash, going out along the copper veins of the territory, into every relay tower, every depot, every sleepy night operator with a cold cup of coffee and a habit of writing things down.

Wren wasn't sending code. She was sending plain language. Names. Dates. Permit numbers. The words *forged*, *twice-sold*, *Corvel Voss* repeated like a hammer on a bell.

"Cut her wire!" Voss screamed.

The marine turned—and Brann Hale stepped out of the wheelhouse doorway with a fire axe in both hands, planted between the marine and the signal shack like a bridge piling.

"Touch that shack," Hale said pleasantly, "and we'll find out what you float like."

* * *

It was Oskar who ended it, though none of them saw him do it.

The air line went slack, then taut, then sang—and the winch Hale's crew had rigged began to turn. Out of the black water, streaming and gleaming, rose the chest: smaller than legend, heavier than sin, its dead-nation seal still bright under the river's scum. It swung over the rail on the groaning davit just as the moonrise broke through a tear in the storm clouds—full, white, exact.

And in that same instant, every telegraph station from the gorge to the coast heard Wren's final transmission: *Salvage claim filed by right of prior discovery, witnessed on the open wire, timestamped this tide.*

The permit clock ran out with the chest swinging above the deck, dripping, between Petra and Voss like a pendulum deciding whose time was up.

Voss looked at the chest. At the moon. At the girl at the key who had made the whole territory a witness. And Petra watched the arithmetic finish behind his eyes—the moment a man understands the structure has already failed, and no order he shouts can hold it up.

The marines lowered their rifles on their own. Men who serve stolen concessions know the smell of a falling house.

"This isn't over," Voss said—but quietly, and to no one, the way men talk to themselves on sinking decks.

"It never is," Petra said. "That's why you build things that last."

* * *

They stood around the chest in the wheelhouse afterward, soaked and shaking, while Hale poured liquor into tin cups with hands that weren't quite steady. Outside, the storm was dying over the water, and the river ran silver under the clearing sky.

Oskar sat wrapped in blankets, blue-lipped and grinning, being fed brandy by Wren, who kept touching his shoulder as if to confirm he was real. The chest sat on the chart table between them all, water pooling beneath it, its seal catching the lamplight.

"So that's it," Hale said. "The gold."

Petra put her hand flat on the lid. Under her palm, through the wet wood, she could feel the shape of everything—the ledgers, the proof, the fortune that could buy her a new life on some coast where no one knew her name. The debt that had chased her for ten years, paid off in one lift of a davit. Bags packed forever, finally unpacked.

She thought of the closed lines on her own hand-drawn map. Of the signal towers standing empty. Of rails under floodwater, waiting for someone to remember them.

"No," she said, and lifted the lid.

Inside, on top of the ledgers, lay a document she hadn't seen in the river-dark: the original concession charter itself, signed by the dead nation's last minister, transferable by right of claim. Blank in one field. Waiting for a name.

Petra picked up the pen Hale offered, and the whole crew leaned in, and outside the tide turned and began, slowly, to go out.

"What are we building?" Wren asked softly.

Petra wrote her name—and then, beneath it, kept writing.
