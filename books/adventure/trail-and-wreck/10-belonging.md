## 10. Belonging

The rain had stopped by the time the magistrate's men came for Voss.

Petra watched it from the deck of the dredge barge, arms folded against the morning chill, as two skiffs of survey officers—real ones, with real writs and real brass buttons—rowed out to where the *Meridian Pride* sat listing against the wreck buoy. Voss stood at the rail in his soaked gray coat, still trying to argue, his voice carrying thin across the water like a gull's cry. He was saying something about jurisdiction. About forged documents being a matter of interpretation. About who Petra Lockridge thought they were.

Nobody answered him. The officers simply took his elbows and put him in the boat, and the river closed over the place where he'd been standing as if it had never held anything at all.

Wren stood beside Petra at the rail, telegraph key wrapped in oilcloth under her arm like a sleeping bird. She hadn't slept. None of them had. Her eyes were red-rimmed and bright, and she kept glancing at the water where the Fleet gunboat had been, then away, as if she didn't trust it to stay gone.

"He was still talking when they took him," she said. "I could hear him on the wire all night. Every station from here to Graywater repeating what I sent. He couldn't even get a word in edgewise." A small, fierce smile. "First time I've ever liked the sound of Morse."

"You did well," Petra said.

"I did what you'd have done." Wren shrugged, but Petra saw how the words landed on her, how she straightened a little under them, the way a young tree does after wind. "Mostly. You'd have done it with more swearing."

"Considerably more."

Below them, muffled through the hull, came the sound of Oskar laughing—actually laughing, a rough, delighted sound that Petra hadn't heard from him since the halfway tide, since before the lock, since before the wound in his side had opened like a second mouth. He was down in the hold with Hale, arguing about salvage shares over a bottle of something Hale had been saving for a celebration he'd never believed he'd live to have.

The chest sat between them on the deck, still dripping, its seal broken now not by force but by right. Inside: ledgers. Treasury marks. The dead nation's double-sold fortune, and the paper trail of every man who'd sold it twice.

And beneath the ledgers, folded in oilskin, the concession itself. Signed by a magistrate whose signature was now worth less than river mud. Waiting for new hands.

* * *

The survey office at Graywater Junction smelled of ink, cold tea, and forty years of pipe smoke soaked into the walls. The registrar was a small woman named Aldous-Pratt with spectacles thick as portholes and a manner that suggested she had personally outlasted three governments and expected to outlast this one too.

"The concession is void," she said, laying the old permit on her desk like a corpse at an inquest. "Forged. Which means the zone reverts to open claim. Which means—" she looked up over her spectacles at the four of them crammed into her office, dripping on her floorboards, "—any party may file. First complete filing takes it. Salvage rights, line rights, easements, the lot."

"And the gold?" Oskar asked. His arm was in a sling and he leaned against the doorframe like a man who'd learned to make furniture carry half his weight. "There's treasury gold down there. Real weight. Enough that none of us ever has to look at a flooded river again."

Aldous-Pratt's mouth thinned. "Salvage of state treasury assets reverts to the provisional government. There's a finder's fee. It's generous." A pause. "It is not enough that none of you ever has to look at a flooded river again."

Silence settled over the little office. Rain ticked against the window. Somewhere down the hall a clerk was stamping papers with the patience of a man burying time one blow at a time.

Petra looked at the others.

Hale stood by the window with his back to the room, arms crossed, watching the yard where his dredge barge was tied. He'd burned his Meridian contract in the barge's own boiler—Petra had watched him feed it in page by page, unhurried, like a man paying off a debt he'd owed himself for years. He hadn't said much since. He didn't need to. But he'd given back the compass that morning, pressed it into Petra's hand without ceremony, without apology, and that had said everything the man had left to say.

Oskar wanted the gold. Of course he did. His hands were ruined from thirty years of cold water; his body was a ledger of debts written in scars. He'd earned the right to want it.

Wren—Wren was seventeen years old and had run from a signal tower with nothing but a key and a head full of other people's secrets. She wanted safety. A door that locked. A name that belonged to her.

And Petra—

Petra looked down at their own hands. The same hands that had drawn the map of the closed lines, years ago, in a survey office very much like this one, before the collapse, before the blame, before the long gray exile of odd jobs and creditor letters. The map was still in their coat. It had survived the flood, the lock, the portage through drowned timber, the storm surge. It was soft as cloth now at the folds, worn thin along the routes they'd traveled, and somewhere along the way it had stopped being a record of what was lost and become a plan of what might be.

"We file for the concession," Petra said. "Not just salvage. The whole thing. Line rights included."

Oskar pushed off the doorframe. "Petra—"

"There's a finder's fee. It'll clear my debts. All of ours." Petra met his eyes. "And there's a line down there—closed, drowned, forgotten—that runs from Graywater to the coast through country nobody else wants. Timber country. Ore country. Country the Fleet can't patrol because the Fleet doesn't know it exists except as a mark on a chart we're holding." They laid the old hand-drawn map on the registrar's desk, smoothing it flat with both palms. "We don't take the treasure and vanish. We reopen the line. We become the thing the treasure was always supposed to pay for."

"A railroad," Hale said quietly, without turning from the window. "You want to build a railroad."

"I want to finish one."

Hale turned then. For a long moment the two of them looked at each other across the little office, across the years and the testimony and the bridge toll and the compass passed back and forth like a coin neither could spend. Then Hale snorted—a short, rough sound—and crossed the room to stand at the desk beside Petra.

"Your gradients were always better than mine," he said. "God help me. File it."

* * *

They signed in the afternoon, when the rain came back soft and steady, drumming on the office roof like applause from a great distance.

Aldous-Pratt read out each clause in a voice like a train over points—clack, clack, clack—and they initialed the margins. Concession of the Meridian Bend Zone, including salvage rights, easement rights, and reconstruction rights for the closed line, granted jointly to four named parties. Four names. Petra wrote theirs first, and the pen moved in the same hand that had drawn the map—the same sure strokes, the same slight forward lean, as if the hand had been waiting eleven years for paper worthy of it.

Then Oskar signed, wincing, using his good arm, and made a joke about his signature looking like a drowning man's last words. Then Wren, who hesitated only a moment before writing her whole name—Wren Halloway, full and unabbreviated, the first time she'd put it on any document she hadn't stolen—and Petra watched her do it and saw her shoulders come down half an inch, like a knot easing.

Hale signed last. He studied the line above his name for a moment—Petra's name, Oskar's, Wren's—and then added his own beneath them, large and square, like a cornerstone.

"Well," he said, capping the pen. "Partners."

"Don't make it sentimental," Oskar said. "I've seen your books. You'll be the death of us."

"I've already been the near-death of one of you. I'm diversifying."

Wren laughed—really laughed, high and startled out of her—and Oskar grinned despite the sling, and outside the rain kept falling on the yard, on the barge, on the drowned line running east toward the coast, patient under the water, waiting to be raised.

* * *

That evening they walked out to the bend together, all four, because some things needed doing in company.

The river had dropped. The flood was going out of it the way fever leaves a body, slowly, leaving the banks scoured and pale and strewn with the season's wreckage. Downstream, the wreck buoy bobbed where the pay-train lay in its grave of silt and story, its gold soon to be inventoried by men with clipboards, its ledgers already bound for the archives as evidence. Its work, at last, was done.

Petra crouched at the waterline where the chest had come up, and let the cold water run over their fingers, and thought about bags.

Eleven years of never unpacking. Eleven years of keeping the kit by the door, boots laced loose for a fast exit, money sewn into the lining, nothing on the walls because walls were things other people had. Self-sufficiency, they'd called it. Petra knew another word for it now—one they'd learned somewhere between the halfway tide and the long portage, carried home through drowned timber on a bad leg with a runaway girl reading the stars and a rival's barge lights burning green ahead of them.

*Company.*

"Petra." Wren's voice, behind them. "Oskar says there's a tavern in Graywater with a piano that's only slightly haunted. He's buying. Well—he's buying with the advance Hale extorted out of the registrar, so technically Hale's buying, which he's furious about."

"Hale's coming to a tavern?"

"Hale's pretending he isn't, which is the same thing."

Petra stood. Brushed the wet sand from their knees. Behind them the others waited on the bank—Oskar with his sling and his grin, Wren with the telegraph key slung across her back like a rifle, Hale with his collar up against the drizzle and his face arranged into something almost, nearly, approaching contentment.

The compass sat in Petra's pocket, warm from their body. Their mentor's last gift. North, and true, and no longer the only fixed point in the world.

They turned their back on the river and walked up the bank toward the light of the town, and for the first time in eleven years, Petra Lockridge did not check the road behind them.

Tomorrow there would be surveys. Gradients to walk, trestles to price, a hundred miles of drowned iron to raise out of the dark water inch by stubborn inch. Tomorrow the real work would begin, and it would be hard, and it would be slow, and it would be theirs.

Tonight there was a piano, and a fire, and four names on a piece of paper drying in a registrar's office.

Tonight, at last, they would unpack.
