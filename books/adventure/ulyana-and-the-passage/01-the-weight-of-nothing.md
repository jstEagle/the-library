## 1. The Weight of Nothing

The dust in Solmere never settled. It hung in the air like a held breath, catching the late light and turning the whole border town the color of old bone, and Xanthe Okafor had learned, over eleven years of coming and going through its single muddy street, to stop noticing it. That was the trick of a life like theirs: not seeing what you could not afford to see.

They sat in the back booth of the Salt Larder, where the tea was weak and the windows were thick with grime, and they watched the door the way they watched everything—without appearing to watch at all. A ranger's license let you carry a Ministry badge into places badges weren't welcome. It also required you to be forgettable. Xanthe had gotten very good at forgettable. Average height, average build, dust-colored coat, dust-colored hair cut short enough to need no tending. A face that made people reach for a description and come up empty. They had spent years sanding themselves down to this: a person shaped like an absence.

It was easier that way. The Ministry preferred its debtors quiet.

The estate sale notice had been pinned to the Solmere assay office board for six days when Xanthe finally allowed themselves to read it past the first line. *Estate of Maren Okafor, deceased. Effects dispersed per Ministry directive.* Deceased. The word sat on the page like a stone on a grave that had no body under it. Eleven years since her disappearance into custody, and the paperwork had finally done what paperwork does—it had declared a living woman finished, closed her accounts, sold her things. Her things. Three crates of them, catalogued by a clerk who had never met her: two coats, a box of letters the Ministry had already screened, a set of range instruments worn smooth by thirty years of survey work.

And the compass.

Xanthe had known before they reached the assay office that it would be gone. Some part of them—the part that still flinched at the word *deceased*—had hoped anyway. Hope was the one habit they'd never managed to sand away. The clerk, a young man with ink-stained cuffs and the apologetic manner of someone who delivered bad news daily, had checked his ledger twice, then a third time, as if the missing item might be embarrassed into reappearing.

"Item forty-one," he said. "Brass field compass, maker's mark Aldous & Vane, engraved case. Sold Tuesday." He glanced up, then quickly down. "To a private buyer."

"Name."

"I'm not permitted—" He caught Xanthe's expression and wilted. "She paid cash. Didn't give one. But the assessor remembered her. Red hair, cropped short. Gloves she never took off, even indoors. Bought supplies after—rope, cold-weather rations, a climbing rack." He hesitated. "Paid for all of it with notes from the eastern mint. Nobody uses those out here."

Eastern mint. The Ashfall side of the border, where the money was old and the mountains were older. Xanthe had stood in the assay office doorway for a long moment, doing arithmetic they didn't want to do. A stranger who bought a dead woman's compass and immediately provisioned for high altitude. Who wore gloves indoors, in a town where the temperature hadn't dropped below sweltering in a month. Someone heading somewhere cold, and fast, and carrying something that belonged to Maren Okafor.

Someone who knew what the compass was. Or thought they did.

Now, in the Salt Larder, Xanthe turned their cup a quarter-turn on the saucer and let the pieces arrange themselves. The tea was terrible. That was good. Terrible tea kept you present.

The door opened at half past the hour, exactly when Devereux always arrived, because Devereux believed punctuality was a form of authority. He came in shaking dust off his gray coat, spotted Xanthe without scanning the room—he always knew exactly where they were, which Xanthe had long ago stopped finding uncanny and started finding diagnostic—and crossed to the booth carrying two cups of tea he hadn't ordered yet. The serving girl brought them anyway. Everyone in Solmere knew Devereux's habits. Habits were how the Ministry marked its territory.

"Okafor." He slid into the bench across from them, unhurried, arranging his cups. He was a broad, comfortable-looking man with a face built for benevolence, which was the most dangerous thing about him. "You look tired."

"You look well."

"I am well." He said it without irony, sipped, and set the cup down with a small click. "The estate matter. You've been to the assay office."

Not a question. Xanthe held their face still. "I read the notice."

"And?"

"And nothing. She's dead. The effects are sold. I came to sign the release forms." They had signed them that morning, in fact, in handwriting that had stayed steady through every line except the one that read *next of kin*, where the pen had hovered for four full seconds before they wrote their own name. Next of kin to a woman the Ministry insisted was dead. There was no one else to write.

Devereux studied them over the rim of his cup. He had a way of looking at people that made them feel inventoried. "The compass," he said.

Xanthe's pulse did something small and traitorous. They kept their voice level. "What about it?"

"It wasn't in the crate when the effects were processed. It went missing between the estate and the assay office—or so the report says." He turned the cup another click. "The Ministry takes a dim view of irregularities in sealed estates, Okafor. Especially estates with outstanding obligations attached."

"There are no outstanding obligations. The debt was settled when—" Xanthe stopped. They knew better than to finish that sentence. The debt was never settled. The debt was a river with no far bank; it only changed shape. Their grandfather had incurred it, some border infraction decades old that no one alive could name precisely, and the family had been paying it ever since—in service, in silence, in a mother surrendered to custody for refusing, the file said, to cooperate with a mineral survey. And now the debt had passed, like eye color, like the shape of their hands, to the next of kin.

"The obligation transfers with the estate," Devereux said mildly, as if reading the thought off their face. "You know this. Your license is renewed annually against it. Your access to the ranges, your right to carry arms, your exemption from conscription levies—all of it rests on the account remaining in good standing." He smiled, and it was almost kind. "I'm not here to threaten you. I'm here to remind you. There's a difference, and I'd like us both to pretend there isn't."

"What do you want, Devereux?"

"A thief." He reached inside his coat and produced a folded sheet of paper, sliding it across the table with two fingers. "A woman calling herself Voss. Ulyana Voss. She's been working the eastern districts for eight months—estate thefts, mostly. Small, clean jobs, no violence, no witnesses. Until last week, when she lifted a certain item from a certain estate inventory in transit." He tapped the paper once. "Your mother's compass, Okafor. The item you just told me you have no interest in."

Xanthe looked down at the sheet. A sketch, done by a clerk's hand but from life: sharp jaw, red hair cropped close, eyes rendered as two careful blanks because clerks were forbidden to guess at expressions. Below it, a list of known aliases, three towns, a pattern of movement that any ranger could read at a glance. The pattern ran west. Toward the border. Toward the scrublands, and beyond them the blue-gray wall of the Ashfall Range, already wearing its first white at the shoulders.

"She bought mountain gear," Xanthe said. "In Solmere. Yesterday."

Something flickered behind Devereux's benevolent face—not surprise; confirmation. "So the assessor reported. Which means she's not fleeing across the flats where anyone could run her down. She's going up." He leaned back. "Into your country, Ranger. The one place in this territory where a Ministry tracker can't follow without a licensed guide. Where the passes close in five weeks, maybe less if the snows come early, and where a lone thief with a climbing rack and no local knowledge has perhaps a one-in-four chance of seeing spring."

He let that sit. Xanthe let it sit too, watching the dust turn gold in the window light, feeling the weight of the thing Devereux was not saying settle onto the table between them like a third cup.

"You want me to go get it," they said.

"I want you to recover Ministry property." Devereux's voice stayed pleasant. "The compass is flagged. Its engraving contains survey references from the original Ashfall assessment—old data, obsolete, worthless to anyone but a collector or a sentimentalist. Still, it's flagged, and flagged items don't wander. You'll track her, you'll take the item, and you'll file a standard recovery report." He finished his tea and stood, settling his coat on his shoulders. "Do this cleanly and the Ministry will note your cooperation favorably when the annual review comes around. Fail, and—" He spread his hands, an open book with nothing written in it. "Well. You've always been reliable, Okafor. I expect that won't change."

At the door he paused, half-turned, backlit by the bone-colored afternoon. "For what it's worth—and it isn't worth much—I'm sorry about your mother. She was a difficult woman, but the Ministry respected difficult women once." Then he was gone, and the door swung shut behind him, and the dust went on hanging in the light as though nothing had passed through it at all.

* * *

Xanthe did not go back to their room. They walked.

Solmere at dusk was a town pretending to be more than a waypoint: lamps lit early against the dust, a freight crew arguing outside the livery, the smell of tallow and hot iron drifting from the smithy. Xanthe moved through it unseen, as always, nodding to no one, noticed by no one, and felt the familiar weight settle over their shoulders—that weight of nothing, the specific gravity of a life pared down to function. No house. No family. No name that meant anything beyond a ledger entry. A mother declared dead by men who knew she wasn't, and a son or daughter—Ianthe had stopped correcting people decades ago; the paperwork simply said *Okafor*—who had learned to call the leash a lifeline.

That was the lie they had lived inside for eleven years: that the debt protected them. That as long as they served quietly, the Ministry would keep its word, keep its records straight, keep Maren Okafor alive in whatever comfortable custody the files implied. *Cooperative detainees are housed according to rank of compliance,* the letter had said, in the only communication Xanthe had ever received. One letter. Eleven years ago. They had written back fourteen times. The replies, when they came at all, were stamped acknowledgments.

And now the woman in the sketch had taken the one object left in the world that Maren Okafor had touched every day of her working life—a brass compass, Aldous & Vane, its case engraved with coordinates in her own hand, coordinates she used to trace on the kitchen table with one finger while telling Xanthe, *Someday I'll show you what these mean, little wren. When you're old enough to keep a secret.*

Xanthe had been nine. The secret had never come.

They stopped at the edge of town where the packed dirt gave way to scrub, where the last lamp gave out and the first stars came up hard and cold over the western flats. Somewhere out there, a red-haired thief in gloves she never removed was walking toward the Ashfalls with a dead woman's compass in her pocket, and either she was a collector chasing salvage, or she was something else entirely—someone who knew the engraving was more than decoration.

Xanthe unslung their pack, checked it by feel in the dark: rope, rations, the thin sleeping roll, the range glasses. Old habits. Their hands knew the inventory without light.

Devereux wanted the compass recovered and a report filed. Fine. Xanthe had filed a thousand reports, each one a small brick in the wall of their invisible life. But they were done letting the Ministry stand between them and the last true thing their mother had left. Whatever Voss wanted with that compass, Xanthe would be there first. They would take it back with their hands, feel its weight, read the engraving their mother's finger had traced—and if the thief knew something, anything, about why a stranger would bleed eastern money for a piece of flagged brass, then Xanthe would get that out of her too, before Devereux's report ever saw ink.

The debt could wait. The debt was patient; it had been patient for three generations. But the snows were coming early this year—they could smell it on the wind off the flats, that mineral cold that meant the high passes would close soon and stay closed—and whatever window existed between the thief and the summit was narrowing by the hour.

Xanthe turned west and started walking, and the dust of Solmere fell away behind them, and ahead the Ashfall Range stood black against the stars with its new white shoulders, waiting, keeping whatever it kept.

By the second hour out, on bare stone where nothing should leave a mark, they found the first footprint.
