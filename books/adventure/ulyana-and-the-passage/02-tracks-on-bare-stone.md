## 2. Tracks on Bare Stone

The scrublands began where Solmere's last fence post leaned drunk against the wind, and Xanthe Okafor read them the way other people read letters from home.

Sign was a language with no lies in it—only truths spoken by things that couldn't speak. A broken stem told you the hour of passage by how far its sap had dried. A displaced pebble told you weight. A scuffed heel at the edge of a cattle track told you someone had stepped off the path deliberately, and recently, and alone.

Xanthe crouched at the base of that fence post and let the morning tell its story.

One set of prints, small and even, boots with a patched sole—the left one dragging a half-moon of dust heavier than the right. The same print pattern they'd lifted from the dead woman's estate in Solmere, photographed and memorized before dawn while Devereux's clerk drank tea in the parlor and complained about the heat. Ulyana Voss. Heading east. Not south toward the river crossings where any sensible thief would run, not west toward the coast towns with their anonymous crowds and their ships.

East. Toward the Ashfall Range.

Xanthe straightened, pressed their palm flat against the fence post's sun-warmed wood, and felt the old familiar ache settle into their chest like a stone dropped into a well. Three days out of Solmere already, and the trail hadn't wavered once.

*She isn't running,* Xanthe thought. *She's traveling.*

There was a difference, and the difference mattered. Running left panic in the sign—broken branches at shoulder height, doubled-back loops, water sources hit hard and carelessly. This trail had none of it. Every step was placed. At the dry wash two miles back, Voss had crossed on the stones instead of the sand, leaving nothing but faint scuffs on rock that would be erased by the next wind. At the abandoned well, she'd drawn water with a cup instead of dipping her bottle, so the mud around the lip stayed undisturbed. She'd even kicked her own bootprints closed behind her in the soft clay near the stock pen—a trick border smugglers used, and one most of them did badly.

Voss did it well.

Xanthe found themselves cataloguing the skill the way they'd catalogue any quarry: *trained, or self-taught over years; patient; knows pursuit is possible but not certain; plans her route in advance.* And then, unbidden, a different kind of thought arrived, unwelcome as a thorn through a boot sole:

*I'd have liked to meet her under other circumstances.*

They crushed the thought and walked on.

* * *

The scrublands were honest country. No trees to block sightlines, no cities to swallow a person—just low grey-green brush, red earth, and sky so wide it made a body feel like an insect pinned to a map. By midday the heat came up off the ground in visible waves, and Xanne adjusted their stride to conserve water, sipping from the bladder at their hip in measured pulls. Ranger discipline. You didn't drink when you were thirsty; you drank on schedule, because thirst lied and schedules didn't.

The Ministry had taught them that. The Ministry had taught them everything worth knowing about survival, and in exchange—

In exchange for the debt. For the license that hung around their neck like a yoke dressed up as a medal. For the arrangement that kept their mother's name on a list of *persons in protective custody* rather than a list of the disappeared, and cost Xanthe ten years of doing whatever Devereux asked, going wherever Devereux pointed, being whoever the job required and nothing else.

*You're not a person, Okafor,* Devereux liked to say, in his mild way, over paperwork in airless offices. *You're an asset with excellent field instincts. Try not to develop opinions.*

Xanthe had gotten very good at having no opinions. It was a kind of erasure, practiced daily until it fit like skin. Out here, though, with nothing but the trail and the heat and their own thoughts, the erasure wore thin in places, and underneath it something older stirred—the child who'd watched a brass compass get wrapped in cloth and tucked into a drawer, who'd been told *this belonged to your mother, keep it safe, she'll want it when she comes back*, and who had kept it safe for twenty years through every posting and every purge of belongings, until a thief with a patched left sole had lifted it from an estate sale like it was just another dead woman's trinket.

It wasn't a trinket. It was the only object in the world that proved Xanthe Okafor had ever been loved by anyone who chose to love them.

They walked faster after that thought.

* * *

By late afternoon the light went strange.

Xanthe noticed it first in the color of the horizon—not the flat white glare of ordinary heat haze but a bruised yellow-brown band sitting low along the eastern sky, thickening as they watched. Then the wind changed. It had been blowing steadily from the northwest all day, the same wind that carried scent and sound and made tracking harder; now it stuttered, died, and reversed, coming hot off the desert flats like breath from an opened oven.

Haboob. Or haboob's meaner cousin, born where the dry season met the first cold front sliding down off the Ashfall high country. Xanthe had seen storms like this eat whole caravans. They checked the sky again, did the arithmetic—two hours, maybe three—and turned their eyes across the scrub for shelter.

There. Two miles northeast, a dark line breaking the horizon's clean edge: the ruins of Karst Station, an old relay post from the survey era, abandoned sixty years and half-swallowed by the earth. Stone walls meant shade, and shade meant surviving the storm without burying yourself alive in a dugout.

Xanthe lengthened their stride and tried not to think about the fact that Voss, if she'd read the sky the way any competent traveler would, was almost certainly headed for the same walls.

* * *

Karst Station resolved out of the heat shimmer like a shipwreck surfacing. What remained was a main hall with its roof mostly intact, a collapsed watchtower, and a scatter of outbuildings worn down to knee-height suggestion. The stonework was good—survey-era construction, built by people who expected to stay. Someone had carved a date into the lintel: a year Xanthe's mother might have been alive to see.

They circled the ruin wide, reading the ground.

No fresh tracks leading out. That was the first thing. Whatever had gone in—or whoever—hadn't left. And there, at the southern wall where the plaster had crumbled away to expose the bare stone beneath: a scuff mark, hours old, deliberate. Someone had climbed in through the breach rather than use the door, and had brushed the dust smooth behind them.

Xanthe stood in the shadow of the wall and felt the hair rise on their arms despite the heat.

*She's inside.*

Every ranger instinct said the same thing: wait. Let the storm pass, let the quarry sleep, take her at dawn on open ground where there was nowhere to run. But the storm wouldn't wait, and neither, Xanthe realized with a cold twist in the gut, could they afford to. If Voss slipped out during the chaos of the haboob—if she used the storm the way she used everything else, carefully and deliberately—they'd lose her in the Ashfall foothills, in country full of canyons and blind draws, and the compass would vanish into the mountains forever.

So Xanthe did the thing a careful person wouldn't do. They climbed through the breach.

* * *

Inside, the main hall was dim and smelled of dust and old bird droppings and, faintly, of woodsmoke—recent woodsmoke, a day old at most. Shafts of amber light fell through gaps in the roof slates. Xanthe moved along the wall, silent, hand resting on the short baton at their belt rather than the knife. The Ministry preferred recoveries quiet and injuries minimal. Paperwork either way, Devereux said, but one kind generated less.

"You're standing in my light."

The voice came from the far corner, level and unhurried, and Xanthe froze—not from fear but from the sheer wrongness of it. Nobody heard a ranger come through a breach. Nobody heard Xanthe Okafor do anything.

In the corner, seated against the stone with her back to the wall and a tin cup balanced on her knee, sat a woman in a dust-grey coat. Dark hair cut short and practical. Eyes the pale green of high-altitude ice, watching Xanthe with what looked disturbingly like amusement. Her hands were empty and visible, and she held them that way deliberately, the way you'd show a card player your fold.

"Ulyana Voss," Xanthe said.

"The famous debt collector." She took a sip from the cup. "I wondered how long you'd circle. You spent twenty minutes reading my entry scuff. Most people would've missed that I brushed it."

"I didn't miss it. I read it." Xanthe kept their distance, keeping the light behind them. "You wanted me to know someone was here."

Something flickered across her face—there and gone, too fast to name. "Did I?"

"That's the only reason to leave sign that clean. You're careful enough to leave none at all. I've followed you for three days. I know exactly how careful you are." Xanthe let that sit. "So either you got sloppy today, or you wanted company."

Outside, the wind rose. It came all at once, the way these storms did—not a build but a wall, a brown roaring darkness swallowing the amber light shafts one by one until the hall went the color of deep dusk. Sand hissed against the stone. Somewhere loose sheet metal, sixty years rusted to nothing, tore free and clattered away into the roar.

Voss didn't flinch. She just looked at Xanthe through the gathering gloom, and the amusement in her face cooled into something more appraising. More serious.

"Well," she said. "Looks like we're both staying for dinner."

* * *

They divided the ruin without discussing it, the way soldiers divide a trench. Voss took the southeast corner; Xanthe took the northwest, with the breach in view. Neither slept. The storm screamed for six hours, packing sand against the walls in singing drifts, and in the intervals between gusts Xanthe listened to the small sounds of the other occupant: the click of a tin cup set down on stone, the whisper of a pack being opened, once—just once—the soft exhalation of someone talking quietly to herself, or to something she carried.

To something she'd stolen.

Xanthe's hands curled into fists in the dark. Twenty years. Twenty years of keeping that compass safe, of taking it out on the worst nights of every posting and holding it under lamplight, reading the fine engraved lines on its case face that weren't decoration, that had never been decoration, though Xanthe had never understood what they were. Their mother's handwriting on the paper tag: *For when I'm home.* And now some stranger had it in a pack in a ruined station, and had probably never once thought about whose hands had held it before hers.

When the storm finally eased toward grey false-dawn, Xanthe spoke into the quiet.

"Why the Ashfall Range?"

Silence. Then, dry: "Good morning to you too."

"It's a month early for the passes. The snows will close them before you're through unless you climb fast and lucky, and nobody climbs the Ashfall lucky. There's nothing in those mountains worth dying for except mineral claims, and you don't strike me as a prospector." Xanthe shifted forward, letting the strengthening light find their face. "You stole from a dead woman's estate in Solmere and ran *toward* the hardest crossing on the continent. Thieves run away from trouble. You're running into it. So either you have business in the range—"

"—or I'm the best liar on foot in the history of the scrublands." Voss rose, brushing dust from her coat, and for the first time Xanthe saw her fully in the light: lean, weathered, a scar splitting one eyebrow, hands that had done years of work no softer than a ranger's. "Maybe I am. Maybe I'm leading a very persistent ranger on a very long walk to nowhere."

"Nobody walks into the Ashfall for fun."

"No," she agreed, and something moved behind her eyes—grief, Xanthe thought, real grief, worn smooth by time the way river stones are worn. "Nobody does."

She shouldered her pack. Xanthe's hand went to the baton.

"I could take you in right here," Xanthe said. "Storm's down. Ground's soft outside. One set of tracks going nowhere."

"You could." Voss looked at them, and there was no fear in it, which was somehow worse than fear. "But you won't. Not yet."

"And why is that?"

"Because you've been following me for three days, ranger, and in three days you've had a hundred chances to call it in to your handler and bring a retrieval team instead of walking alone." She tilted her head. "You didn't. Which means whatever this compass is to you, you don't want the Ministry touching it any more than I do. And that—" she smiled, thin and sharp and tired, "—makes us the strangest kind of allies, doesn't it? Both of us protecting the same little piece of brass from the same people."

Xanthe said nothing. The silence said it for them.

Voss walked to the breach and paused there, silhouetted against a washed-clean sky, the storm's debris glittering like scattered glass across the red earth.

"There's a place in the foothills," she said, not turning around. "Old survey camp. If you're going to follow me anyway—and we both know you are—you'll want to see what's there. Bring your radio. You're going to want to hear what your Ministry says about it."

Then she dropped through the breach and was gone, her patched left sole printing a neat half-moon in the sand, heading east, toward the mountains, and Xanthe stood alone in the ruin of Karst Station with their heart hammering a rhythm they refused to name.

Respect, they told themselves. It was respect. Nothing more.

They followed her into the scrublands anyway, and the mountains rose ahead of them, patient and grey and keeping whatever secret she'd promised.
