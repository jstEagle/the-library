## 3. What the Thief Carried

Ulyana Voss had known the ranger was behind her for three days.

She'd felt it first as a wrongness in the scrublands south of Solmere—a jackrabbit bolted from cover a half-mile too early, a kestrel circling where no carrion lay. Then, on the second morning, she'd found the sign: boot prints pressed into the clay pan beside her own, spaced with the unhurried economy of someone who wasn't chasing so much as *following*. A ranger's stride. Long, even, patient as erosion.

Most people would have run. Running told a tracker everything—fear made people sloppy, and sloppy people left stories written all over the ground. Ulyana did not run. She adjusted. She walked through loose shale instead of hardpan when she wanted to be read, and over bare stone when she didn't. She camped cold twice and let her fire smoke once, deliberately, feeding green sagebrush into the flames until the column rose fat and gray against the afternoon sky like a flag planted for anyone with eyes to see.

*Come on, then,* she thought, watching the smoke drift east toward the Ashfall Range. *Come see what you're following.*

Because that was the thing the ranger didn't know yet: Ulyana had chosen this direction before she'd ever set foot in Solmere. The compass—the brass compass sitting warm against her sternum inside her coat, riding there like a second heartbeat—hadn't been stolen on impulse. It had been stolen because of what was engraved under its faceplate, and what was engraved under its faceplate pointed at the one place in the world her brother had died trying to reach.

The Ashfall Range. Which meant she needed someone who knew the Ashfall Range.

And rangers, whatever else they were, knew their ranges.

* * *

The night before she'd taken the compass, rain had come down on Kettering Street like the sky was trying to wash the city away, and Ulyana had stood in the doorway of her brother's flat with his death certificate in one hand and a Ministry envelope in the other.

Tomas Voss. Age twenty-nine. Cause of death: fall while surveying, Ashfall Range access corridor. Body recovered by Ministry contractors. Effects returned to next of kin: one duffel, one field journal (incomplete), one letter (unopened).

The letter had been addressed to her. It had also been opened, read, and resealed by someone whose hands were not Tomas's hands—she could tell by the crease, too crisp, folded by an institutional machine rather than a grieving brother. Inside, in his cramped leftward-slanting hand:

*Ulya — if you're reading this then they let it through, which means either they've stopped caring or they want you to see it. Either way. I found something in the readings. Not ore. Not water. Something moving under the range, and the numbers don't match anything we were told to look for. Devereux's office pulled my originals. I kept copies. You know where. Don't trust the estate sale — trust what's engraved. T.*

That was all. No signature flourish, no *love*, nothing soft. Tomas had never wasted words, especially not at the end of things.

*Trust what's engraved.*

She hadn't understood it until six weeks later, standing in the marble foyer of the Aldervane estate in Solmere, cataloguing lots for the auction house that paid her in cash and asked no questions. Lot 114: brass surveyor's compass, provenance unknown, consigned by the estate of M. Aldervane, deceased. She'd picked it up to log its condition and her thumb had caught on something beneath the hinged faceplate—a seam, fine as a hair. She'd waited until the housekeeper stepped out, then pried it open with a fingernail.

Inside the lid, etched into the brass in a hand so small she'd needed the window light to read it: a ring of characters. Numbers and letters interleaved, arranged in concentric arcs around a central starburst. And beneath them, smaller still, almost an afterthought:

*M.V. — the passage holds.*

M.V. Marta Voss? No—she'd checked the consignment records that same night, heart hammering. The estate belonged to a woman named Marta Aldervane. But Ulyana had spent two years digging through Ministry personnel files for any trace of her brother's team, and she knew that name. Marta Aldervane. Formerly Marta Voss. Senior geodetic surveyor, Ashfall Range baseline study, twelve years ago. Discharged from Ministry records without explanation. Her file ended mid-sentence, the way files did when someone upstairs decided a career was over.

And the surname—*Voss*. The same as hers. Coincidence, probably. The borderlands were full of Vosses. But Tomas had worked under the baseline study's successor program. He would have known her name. Maybe he'd sought her out. Maybe—

It didn't matter. What mattered was the engraving: a coordinate cipher, she was almost certain, keyed to some reference system she couldn't crack. She'd spent four nights with Tomas's incomplete journal spread across her bed, cross-referencing his marginalia against the arcs of characters, and gotten nowhere. The cipher used a grid notation that matched nothing in any public map series. It matched, however—if you squinted, if you believed—the private survey grids the Ministry reserved for classified terrain work.

Terrain work in the Ashfall Range.

So she'd stolen it. Slipped lot 114 out of its velvet cradle and into her coat during the viewing hour, walked past the dozing attendant, and vanished into Solmere's warren of alleys feeling less like a thief than like a woman reclaiming mail that had been stolen from her first.

She'd told herself that, anyway. Some nights it even held.

* * *

Now, five days out from Solmere, she sat on a shelf of red sandstone above a dry wash and watched the ranger make camp below her.

They were good. That was the part that gnawed at her. Ulyana had robbed auction houses, courier depots, and one memorable Ministry annex in Calderis, and she could count on one hand the people who'd tracked her more than a day. This one had done it through a sandstorm, across bare stone, without losing the thread. They moved like water finding its level—no wasted motion, camp pitched with the speed of long habit, a small cookfire built in a hollow so its light wouldn't carry. When they finally sat back on their heels and ate, they ate facing the way they'd come. Watching their backtrail even now.

Through the last gray light Ulyana studied them. Mid-thirties, maybe. Lean in the way of people who'd burned off everything nonessential years ago. Dust-colored clothes, dust-colored hair bound back, a face that might have been handsome if it ever relaxed—and from what she'd seen through three days of glass, it never did. A ranger's badge glinted dull silver at the collar. Their gear was Ministry-standard, worn but meticulously maintained, every strap mended, every buckle polished. Someone took care of what they owned because they weren't allowed to own much else.

*Xanthe Okafor,* she'd learned from the gossip in Solmere's last watering hole, delivered by a drover who talked too much. *Border ranger. Quiet one. Keeps to the high country. Word is the Ministry holds their papers—family debt, old business. Nobody knows what.*

Family debt. Ulyana had turned that phrase over all evening, turning it like the compass itself. A person leashed by paper. Owned by an institution the way a mule is owned by its harness.

Which meant Xanthe Okafor might hate the Ministry as much as Ulyana did—or might defend it to the last breath, because the leash cuts both ways: it chokes, but it also holds you up.

She wouldn't know until she asked. And asking meant getting close, and getting close meant letting herself be caught.

She'd already decided. That was the strange part—how easy the decision had been, once she'd stopped pretending she had another option. The cipher needed a surveyor's knowledge to break, or at least a surveyor's maps, and the only surveyor within two hundred miles was currently building a cookfire in a hollow below her. The passes into the Ashfalls closed with the first snows, and the first snows were early this year; she'd read it in the larches' yellowing, in the way the birds had gone quiet along the foothills. Alone, she'd die up there, or worse, wander the high country until winter finished what the altitude started.

With a ranger, she might live long enough to find what Tomas died for.

Below her, the ranger banked their fire and rolled into their bedroll. Ulyana waited until the stars wheeled past midnight, until the coyotes started up in the far washes, and then she climbed down.

She didn't sneak. That was the point. She came down the scree slope loud enough to wake the dead, boots ringing on stone, and stopped ten feet from the ranger's bedroll with her hands visible and empty.

Xanthe Okafor came up out of the bedroll like a drawn blade—one motion, knife already in hand, body between Ulyana and the pack. In the starlight their eyes were wide awake, which told her they'd heard her coming and let her arrive. Interesting.

"You're the thief," the ranger said. Voice low, level, neither question nor accusation. Just a fact being placed on the table.

"I'm the thief." Ulyana kept her hands where they were. "And you're the one who's been walking in my footprints for five days, which is either impressive or pathetic, depending on how bored your posting is."

"Give me one reason not to arrest you."

"Because arresting me gets you a thief." Ulyana crouched slowly, deliberately, and drew the compass from her coat. She held it up between two fingers so the starlight caught the brass. "But what I'm carrying gets you something else."

She watched the change come over the ranger's face—not greed, nothing so simple, but a kind of hunger with grief underneath it, the look of someone seeing a dead woman's handwriting on a living person's skin. Their eyes fixed on the compass and stayed there.

"That's hers," Xanthe said quietly. "That's my mother's."

"I know whose it is." Ulyana turned it slowly in the light. "I know what's engraved inside it, too. And I know you've got surveyor's training, because nobody reads sign the way you do without it. So here's my offer, ranger. I'll trade you the compass—free and clear, no conditions—for one thing."

"What?"

"Take me into the Ashfall passes. Before snowfall. To coordinates I can't decode alone." She let that sit, then added, softer, because it was true and true things landed harder: "My brother died out there trying to follow these numbers. The Ministry says he fell. I say the Ministry pushed. And I think your mother's engraving and my brother's death are the same story, Xanthe Okafor. I think somebody buried it once already."

For a long moment the ranger said nothing. The fire ticked. Somewhere down the wash, a coyote laughed at all of them.

Then Xanthe's expression closed like a door. "You stole from a dead woman."

"I rescued from a dead woman. There's a difference."

"There isn't." The knife hadn't lowered. "You want a guide, hire one in Kettlebrook. You want the passes, buy a map. What you don't get to do is stand in my firelight holding my mother's compass and call it a negotiation."

"It's not a negotiation," Ulyana said. "It's an invitation. There's a storm coming out of the west—you can smell it, can't you? Same as I can. We can argue about this for two days in the ruin at the head of the wash, or we can argue about it somewhere warmer. Your choice."

Something flickered across the ranger's face—recognition, unwilling, that she'd read the wind right. Ulyana smiled, and hated herself a little for how much of the smile was performance, and hated the performance a little less because Tomas had smiled the same way when he was scared.

"The compass stays with me until I'm standing in the pass," she said. "After that, it's yours. Every word I've told you is true, Okafor. Check it however you like. But decide fast."

Above the western rim, the stars were going smudged and brown. The wind had turned. It carried iron and dust and the smell of a storm still hours away but coming on like a debt called due.

Xanthe looked at the compass. Then at Ulyana. Then, longest of all, at the darkening west.

"You sleep on the far side of the fire," the ranger said at last. "And if you move before dawn, I'll hear you."

"That," Ulyana said, settling onto the cold stone with the compass against her heart, "is the idea."

She closed her eyes and listened to the wind rise, and beneath the wind, faint and steady, she could almost hear Tomas's voice reading his own last letter aloud: *trust what's engraved.* Tomorrow the storm would pin them together in the ruin, and she would have two days to turn a jailer into an ally, or die trying.

Either way, she thought, pulling her coat tighter, the truth was going into those mountains.

She just hoped she wouldn't have to bury anyone else to carry it there.
