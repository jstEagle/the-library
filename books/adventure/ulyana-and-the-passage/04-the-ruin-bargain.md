## 4. The Ruin Bargain

The storm hit the ruin an hour after sundown, and it did not knock.

It came off the high desert like something with intent, a wall of grit and howling air that turned the moon to a smudge and the world to a brown roar. Xanthe had seen haboobs before—had sheltered from worse in worse places—but this one had teeth. It stripped the last loose tiles from the ruin's broken roof and sent them cartwheeling into the dark, and it drove sand through every crack in the old masonry until the air inside tasted of stone and ancient dust.

Xanthe sat with their back against the eastern wall, pack between their knees, knife drawn out of habit more than need, and watched the shape across the ruined chamber watch them back.

Ulyana Voss had not run when the storm came. That was the first thing Xanthe had learned about her that wasn't written in her tracks. She could have bolted into the whiteout—she was fast enough, Xanthe had measured that much over three days of pursuit—and taken her chances in country where no ranger alive could follow sign. Instead she'd walked into the ruin ahead of them, unslung her pack, and built a small fire in the lee of a collapsed stairwell, feeding it with broken door-wood as if she'd been expected.

Now she sat on the far side of that fire, cross-legged, hands loose around a tin cup, and looked at Xanthe with the unhurried attention of someone taking inventory.

"You're bleeding," she said.

Xanthe touched their cheekbone and found grit embedded in a raw scrape they didn't remember earning. The storm had thrown them against the ruin's doorway on the way in. "I'm fine."

"It's going to scar."

"Everything does." Xanthe shifted, keeping the fire between them, keeping the knife visible but not threatening. "You knew I was behind you."

"Since the second day." Ulyana sipped from her cup. Water, by the smell of it. No tea, no luxuries. A traveler who rationed even her comforts. "You walk like you're apologizing to the ground. Very quiet. But you check your backtrail at the same intervals, every time, like clockwork. Clockwork is easy to hear."

The observation landed with uncomfortable precision. Xanthe had spent nine years learning to move through wild country without leaving a whisper, and this thief had read them inside forty-eight hours. "And yet here I am."

"Here you are." Ulyana set down the cup. In the firelight her face was all planes and shadows, younger than her file had suggested and harder too, with a thin white line crossing one eyebrow that no Ministry record would account for. "So let's not waste the storm pretending we're strangers. You want the compass. I know why—I read the estate inventory before I ever touched it. *Voss lot 14, brass field compass, engraved, provenance: the Okafor estate.* Your mother's name was on the deed, Ranger. I did my homework."

Something cold moved through Xanthe's chest, slow as groundwater. "Then you know what you took."

"I know what I *carried*," Ulyana said. "There's a difference."

Outside, the wind found a new register, a low moaning note through the broken archway that sounded almost like speech. Sand hissed against the walls in long sustained sheets. The fire bent sideways and recovered.

"Offer me your trade," Xanthe said. "You've been building up to it since I walked in. I'd rather hear it than wait for it."

Ulyana smiled without warmth. "Direct. All right." She reached into her coat—not fast, deliberately slow, telegraphing every motion—and drew out the compass.

Even now, even after everything, the sight of it went through Xanthe like a hook behind the sternum. Brass worn bright at the edges from decades of handling. The hinged lid with its dented corner where it had once been dropped—or struck—on stone. Their mother's compass. The only object in the whole emptied estate that Xanthe had wanted, the only thing they'd asked Devereux to leave untouched, and the Ministry had catalogued it anyway, boxed it, auctioned it with the rest of a dead woman's life because a debt had to be settled in full and sentiment was not legal tender.

Ulyana held it up, turning it so the firelight ran along the engraving on the case. "This trade. You guide me through the Ashfall passes. High route, the ones that don't appear on survey maps—the ones only range people know. We cross before the snows close them. When we're through, on the far side, the compass is yours. Untouched. And I disappear from your life permanently."

"No."

The word came out flat and immediate, and Xanthe was surprised by their own voice—how little negotiation lived in it.

Ulyana tilted her head. "No counteroffer? No *what's in it for me*? You've chased me four days across open ground. You know what these passes cost to cross alone. I'm offering you your mother's compass and a guide's fee of nothing."

"I'm offering you nothing," Xanthe said, "because I don't make bargains with thieves, and I don't lead strangers through the Ashfall for reasons that have nothing to do with you. Those passes are closed to unauthorized travel for a reason. There are—" They stopped. What was there? Instability. Restricted zones. Devereux's clipped radio transmissions about seismic survey work and keep-out coordinates. *Ministry business, Ranger. Not yours.* "There are dangers you don't have clearance to understand."

"Clearance." Ulyana laughed, short and bitter, and the sound cracked against the walls. "Of course. You're one of *those* rangers. The kind who thinks the Ministry's stamp makes a mountain safe or dangerous depending on what's printed under it." She stood, still holding the compass, and the firelight made her shadow climb the ruined wall behind her like something growing. "Let me tell you what I know about clearance, Ranger. My brother had clearance. He worked a Ministry survey crew in those same mountains, and eight months ago he stopped writing letters, and six months ago the Ministry told my family he never existed on any roster. Clearance is the thing they hand you right before they decide you're disposable."

"That's not—"

"Don't." Her voice dropped, and somehow the quiet was worse than the shouting would have been. "Don't defend them to me. Not tonight. Not while you sit there wearing their license like it's skin."

Xanthe rose too. The knife was still in their hand; they became aware of it and hated the awareness. "Give me the compass, Voss. Put it down and step away from it, and I'll forget the theft charge. I'll write it up as found property, returned. You walk. That's the only deal on this table, and it's better than you deserve."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I take it back."

Ulyana's eyes flicked to the knife, then back to Xanthe's face, and what Xanthe saw there wasn't fear. It was calculation, cold and quick, followed by something that might have been disappointment. "You'd cut a person for a piece of brass."

"I'd arrest a thief for stolen property. How that goes depends entirely on the thief."

They circled each other around the small fire, two orbits tightening, and outside the storm screamed against the ruin like it wanted in. Xanthe watched Ulyana's weight, her hands, the angle of her feet—reading her the way they'd read her tracks for four days—and understood, with a strange detached clarity, that the woman was good. Balanced, patient, hands empty but positioned. Whoever Ulyana Voss had been before she was a thief, she had been trained by somebody in something.

She moved first—a feint left, real weight going right—and Xanthe caught the feint because they'd spent nine years reading animals, and animals always lied with their eyes and told the truth with their hips. They caught her wrist as she went for the grab, twisted, felt her counter-twist come back twice as hard, and then they were both moving and the fire scattered sparks and the fight was ugly and close and silent except for breathing.

She fought like water. No wasted force, no pride in it—when a hold failed she abandoned it instantly, flowing into the next, and twice she nearly put Xanthe down with leverage that shouldn't have worked on someone their size. Once her elbow caught them under the ribs and the world went white at the edges. Once Xanthe's shoulder drove her back into the wall hard enough to shake dust from the lintel, and she used the impact, rolling off it, turning defense into distance.

They broke apart at opposite ends of the chamber, both breathing hard, both having learned something.

"You've done this before," Ulyana said, wiping her mouth.

"So have you."

"Not like you. You fight like someone who was taught to end things." She straightened her collar with two fingers. "I fight like someone who was taught to leave."

"The compass," Xanthe said. "Last time."

Ulyana looked at them across the ruined room, and Xanthe saw her weigh it—saw the exact moment the arithmetic finished and came up wrong. She was fast, but Xanthe was stronger and armed and desperate, and desperation narrowed a person down to something very simple. She opened her hand—

—and the roof came off.

Not all of it. A section of the western wall, already cracked, already leaning, chose that moment to surrender to the wind. The collapse was enormous and sudden, a boom of falling masonry and a blast of sand-laden air that snuffed the fire and filled the chamber with a darkness so total it pressed on the eyes. Something large glanced off Xanthe's shoulder and slammed them into the floor. Dust choked everything. The storm poured through the new breach in a brown flood, and somewhere in it Ulyana coughed, and swore, and went silent.

For a long moment neither of them moved. There was no point. Moving in a collapsing structure during a haboob was how people died stupidly.

When the immediate roar settled into the storm's steadier howl, Xanthe fumbled a shuttered lamp from their pack and lit it. The light showed a chamber transformed: half the west wall gone, a drift of sand already piling against the fallen stones, and the fire dead beneath a burial of grit.

It also showed Ulyana Voss sitting on the floor ten feet away, hair full of dust, a fresh scrape along her jaw to match Xanthe's, holding the compass in both hands like it was the only solid thing left in the world.

Their eyes met over the lamp flame.

"Well," Ulyana said hoarsely. "That settles the argument."

* * *

They rebuilt the fire together, which neither of them acknowledged as cooperation. It was simply that the fire needed rebuilding and there were only two people in the world, as far as the storm was concerned, and the storm did not care about warrants.

Ulyana fed the flames while Xanthe dragged a fallen door slab across the breach to break the worst of the wind. The work was heavy and mindless and exactly what Xanthe needed, because their thoughts kept circling back to the moment she'd opened her hand—she had been giving it back, Xanthe was almost sure, in the half-second before the wall fell—and they did not know what to do with that. Thieves didn't give things back. People who gave things back weren't supposed to fight like she fought, or carry grief like the grief Xanthe had heard underneath her words about her brother, grief with the same grain and weight as their own.

By the time the fire stood again, steady in the sheltered corner, the unspoken truce had hardened into something with structure. They sat on opposite sides of it. The compass lay in Ulyana's palm, and she looked at it instead of at Xanthe.

"Two days," she said. "Maybe three, if it's a big one. Storms like this don't quit until the pressure breaks. You know that better than I do."

"I know that."

"So we can spend two days not talking, or we can spend them talking. Either way, when it clears, we walk out of here and go back to being what we are to each other." She turned the compass over. Firelight caught the engraving—fine lines, geometric, deliberate—and for just a moment her thumb moved over them the way a person touches a scar. "But I'll tell you one true thing tonight, since we're stuck with each other. I didn't steal this to sell it. I've had offers. Good ones. Enough to vanish on for a decade."

"Then why?"

"Because of what's engraved on it." She held it up, angling the lid toward the light, and Xanthe saw—for the first time in years, really *saw*—the pattern etched into the brass. As a child they'd assumed it was decoration, some maker's flourish. Now, older, trained in terrain, they saw it differently: contour lines. Intersecting bearings. A reference grid compressed into an engraving small enough to pass unnoticed.

"My brother spent two years trying to decode markings just like these," Ulyana said quietly. "He copied them from documents he wasn't cleared to copy. He thought they pointed to something the Ministry was hiding in the Ashfalls. He died before he finished." Her jaw tightened. "And then I find this compass at an estate auction, wearing the same key, and the estate ledger says it belonged to a Ministry surveyor named Okafor who officially died eleven years ago—except the death record has no body attached, no cause, no site. Just a name and a closed file." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "Your mother and my brother were working the same puzzle, Ranger. From different ends, years apart. And they both ended up erased."

The wind moaned through the breach. Xanthe found they couldn't speak. Every instinct honed by nine years of Ministry service said *report this, flag it, hand it upward, let the institution handle it*—and every one of those instincts sounded, suddenly, like a man telling himself a story he needed to keep walking.

"That's not true," Xanthe said finally, and heard how thin it came out.

"Probably not," Ulyana agreed, with a gentleness that was worse than mockery. "But you'll think about it. For two days, you'll have nothing to do but think about it." She closed her hand around the compass and tucked it away inside her coat, and Xanthe, watching, did not reach for the knife.

"Sleep," Xanthe said. "I'll take first watch."

"You don't trust me awake and unsupervised."

"I don't trust anyone. It's kept me alive." Xanthe settled against the wall, lamp dimmed, eyes on the dark shape across the fire. "Watch means watch. Don't mistake it for conversation."

Ulyana lay back, arms behind her head, and for a while there was only the storm and the fire's small stubborn crackle. Then, half-drowsy, almost to herself:

"She's alive, you know. Your mother. The file says dead, but files lie. I've read enough of them to know." A pause. "People who are really dead get bodies attached."

Xanthe stared into the fire and said nothing at all, and the silence said everything, and above the ruin the storm ground on toward morning like a debt still being counted.
