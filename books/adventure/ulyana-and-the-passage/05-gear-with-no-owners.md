## 5. Gear With No Owners

The Ashfall Range announced itself three days before we reached it.

First it was a smudge on the horizon that Ulyana swore was cloud and I knew was stone—clouds don't hold their shape for two days in this country. Then the wind changed and carried down the smell of it: cold rock, old ash, the mineral tang of snowmelt still locked in high couloirs. By the fourth morning the peaks stood over us like a jury, white-capped and waiting, and I caught myself reading them the way my mother taught me to read anything—looking for what they were hiding.

Ulyana walked ahead of me. She'd been walking ahead of me for two days now, ever since the storm broke and left us standing in the ruin with nothing settled between us except the fact of each other. The bargain she'd offered—the compass for safe passage—still hung unspoken in the air, neither accepted nor withdrawn. She hadn't given me the compass. I hadn't turned her in. We walked, and the mountain grew, and neither of us named what we were doing.

Her trail was better than it had been in the scrublands. That was the thing about her that kept snagging in my mind like a burr in wool: she improved under pressure. In open country she'd been careful but readable—a thief's habits, weight on the outside edges of her boots, checking her backtrail at intervals so regular I could have set a watch by them. But as the ground steepened and the scrub gave way to scree and stunted pine, her sign got cleaner. Shorter strides on loose rock. Weight distributed. She stopped leaving resting prints where a tracker would look for them—in the lee of boulders, beside water—and started leaving them where no one would think to look, in plain sight on bare slabs where her boots left almost nothing at all.

She'd learned mountains somewhere. Not from books.

"Your Ministry trains its rangers to stare," she said without turning around. She'd known I was watching her backtrail-reading; of course she had. "You've been looking at my feet for an hour."

"Your feet are the most honest part of you."

That earned me half a glance over her shoulder, something wry pulling at the corner of her mouth before she killed it. "Flatterer."

We climbed. The pines thinned, then bent, then gave up entirely, and by midday we were walking through a world of gray stone and rust-colored lichen with the wind pushing at our shoulders like a hand trying to turn us around. And then Ulyana stopped so suddenly I nearly walked into her back.

"What—" I started, and then I saw it too.

Smoke.

Not wildfire smoke—that moves, breathes, leans with the wind. This rose straight up from behind a shoulder of rock in a thin, disciplined column, the kind of smoke a stove makes when someone is feeding it on purpose. Someone was camped on the lower slopes of the Ashfall Range, in October, above the treeline, burning fuel they'd had to pack in on their backs.

Nobody camps up here for pleasure. Nobody camps up here at all.

I put my hand out and Ulyana went still beside me, both of us crouched into the wind-shadow of a granite fin, and I did what twenty years of ranger work had burned into my bones: I read the ground before I trusted my eyes. There was a trail—not a game trail, not a climber's scramble, but a cut path, switchbacked, graded, with drainage ditches cut along its edges. Government work. I'd walked enough Ministry roads to know the signature. Somebody had built a proper path up this slope, and recently, because the cuts were still sharp-edged, the spoil heaps not yet washed smooth.

"That's not on any map I've seen," Ulyana murmured.

"I survey these ranges." My voice came out flatter than I intended. "There's no station here."

"Then either your maps are wrong or your Ministry is lying." She said it lightly, testing, the way you tap ice to see if it'll hold. I didn't answer, and I felt her file the silence away.

We moved up along the path's edge, off it, using the drainage cuts for cover the way their builders never intended. The column of smoke thickened. The smell of woodsmoke gave way to something else underneath it—kerosene, and under that, faintly, the ozone-and-hot-metal smell of running instruments. I knew that smell from a hundred Ministry field stations. It made my chest tighten in a way I couldn't immediately name, because it was the smell of competence, of order, of the institution that had fed and leashed me my whole adult life, and there was no reason on earth for it to be sitting on this unnamed slope.

We came around the shoulder of rock and looked down into the bowl, and I stopped understanding what I was seeing.

* * *

It was a survey camp. A full one. Four large tents in the Ministry's gray-green canvas, a mess shelter, an instrument line staked out across the bowl in a precise arc—gravimeters, a magnetometer rig, two seismic recording units with their solar arrays angled south. A generator shed. Cable runs flagged with the orange tags the Geological Service used. Everything squared away, everything in its place, everything exactly where a well-run survey team would leave it.

And nobody there.

I counted twice, scanning every shadow, every tent flap, every angle of dead ground. Nobody. The smoke came from a stove pipe in the mess shelter, fed and banked and fed again—which meant someone had been tending it, hours ago at most. The generator shed hummed. The seismic units' paper drums turned slowly, pens tracing their patient lines, recording whatever the mountain was telling them for no one to read.

"Xanthe." Ulyana's voice had gone quiet in a new way. She was looking at the mess shelter, at the table visible through its open flap. Plates. Half-eaten meals, the bread gone stale at the edges, a pot of tea gone cold and filmed over. Chairs pushed back—not neatly, not stacked, just pushed back the way people push back chairs when they stand up mid-meal.

"No tracks," I said. My own voice sounded far away. I was already moving down the slope, reading the ground around the camp in widening spirals, and the ground was wrong in a way I had no word for yet. Soft volcanic soil here, six inches deep, the kind that holds a boot print for a season. The team's own traffic had churned it to paste between the tents and the instrument line—weeks of it, dozens of boots, supply hauls, the whole biography of a working camp written in mud.

And then, at the edge of all that churned ground, it simply stopped. A clean line, like a tide mark. On one side, weeks of prints. On the other side, nothing. Not leaving tracks. Not arriving tracks. As if eight or ten people had walked out of their own footprints and been lifted off the face of the earth.

Ulyana crouched at the tide mark and didn't touch it. "Tell me this is normal," she said. "Tell me teams pack out like this all the time."

"Teams pack out *on trails*. They leave a final sweep log. They close the site—pull the instruments, drain the fuel, cache or carry the records." I heard myself reciting procedure and hated how much it sounded like prayer. "They don't leave a live generator running. They don't leave data drums turning. And they don't—" I gestured at the mess shelter, at the cold tea, at the bread going green at its edges. "Nobody leaves a meal."

The wind moved through the bowl and the tent walls breathed in and out, in and out, like the camp itself was sleeping, or pretending to.

I went into the tents because that was my job, because the alternative was standing at the edge of the churned mud forever. The first two were sleeping tents—bedrolls made, personal gear gone, not a scrap of paper. Stripped, but not looted; looters take the mattresses. The third held the instrument spares and a crate of seismic charges, unfused, seals intact. The fourth—

The fourth was the office. Maps pinned to the frame wall, survey grids inked in neat hands, and a logbook lying open on the folding desk. I read the last entry twice because the first time refused to mean anything.

*Day 41. Anomaly confirmed at depth. Requesting instructions re: dissemination. Team advised to await escort.*

No date after it. No signature. *Awaiting escort.* Eight or ten trained government surveyors had eaten lunch, logged an anomaly, and waited for an escort that apparently arrived without leaving footprints.

Behind me, Ulyana had gone very still at the instrument line. She was staring at the seismic drums, and her face had changed—had gone the way faces go when recognition arrives ahead of understanding. Her lips moved slightly, reading the trace lines the way I read sign, and whatever she saw there made her hand rise halfway toward the drum and stop.

"Don't," I said.

She looked at me. "You felt that too? Last night, at cold camp—the ground hum. Low. You sat up."

I had sat up. I'd told myself it was the wind settling the rocks. Rangers tell themselves things like that; it's how we stay sane in country that talks in its sleep. "This range has always been restless. Old volcanics. Thermal activity. It doesn't mean—"

"It doesn't mean what?" She wasn't arguing. That was the worst part. She was asking, genuinely, holding the question out to me flat-handed like something she wanted me to take and examine myself. "What does it not mean, Xanthe? Because your Ministry built a road up here, ran a forty-one-day survey, found something at depth, asked permission to tell people about it—and then everyone vanished between one footprint and the next. What exactly are you defending?"

I opened my mouth to defend it and discovered I had nothing to put in my mouth. So instead I did the other thing I knew how to do. I worked.

* * *

I spent two hours documenting the camp, because documentation was the shape my loyalty took. Photographed the logbook. Sketched the track-tide mark with distances and compass bearings. Counted the boots' sizes from the churned prints—nine distinct sets, maybe ten. Catalogued the instruments still running, the charges still sealed, the absence of any sweep log, closure order, or extraction record. It was good work, thorough work, the kind of report Devereux would have praised, and I built it plank by plank while Ulyana watched me with an expression I refused to interpret.

At dusk the radio crackled.

It was a scheduled check-in—I'd reported at dawn and dusk since Solmere, terse position calls on the Ministry band, standard practice for a licensed ranger operating under supervision. I keyed the handset and Devereux's voice came through with the usual texture of dry paper and patience.

"Quintana. Position."

"Ashfall lower slopes, west aspect, grid nine-four-seven." I gave it cleanly. Then, because the truth was already in my throat and I couldn't seem to swallow it: "Devereux, I've found something. Survey camp, Geological Service markings, fully equipped. Instruments running. No personnel. No extraction record. Tracks terminate at the camp perimeter. Nine, possibly ten individuals, unaccounted for."

Silence on the band. Long enough that I checked the handset's battery light. Then Devereux, in the same dry paper voice, saying words that rearranged the furniture of my life:

"Understood. Note the location. Do not enter the structures further. Do not remove materials. Report findings complete and move on. Your survey schedule stands."

"Move on." I heard my own voice climb. "Devereux, there are ten missing government employees up here. Their meals are still on the table. That's not a move-on situation, that's a search-and-recovery—"

"*Quintana.*" The patience was gone. What was underneath it wasn't anger, exactly. It was something more tired than anger, and older. "The Ashfall survey is a closed administrative matter. You have your orders. Confirm."

The wind pushed at my back, toward the camp, toward the cold tea and the turning drums and the nine sets of boots that had walked nowhere. Behind me I could feel Ulyana watching, not gloating—worse than gloating, she was just *watching*, letting me see myself do whatever I was about to do.

Twenty years. Twenty years of reports filed true, of positions called accurate to the hundredth of a degree, of being the person whose word the Ministry could weigh like coin because it had never once come up light. It was the one thing I owned outright, the one thing the debt hadn't touched. My mother had taught me that a ranger's word was the only property that couldn't be repossessed.

"Confirm," Devereux said again.

I looked down at the logbook entry in my own handwriting. *Anomaly confirmed at depth. Requesting instructions re: dissemination.* Ten people had asked their institution a question and been erased between footprints. And the institution was asking me, in Devereux's tired paper voice, to walk past the question and keep my license.

"Findings complete," I said. "Camp noted. Nothing significant. Moving on per schedule."

The lie went out of me like a tooth coming out—clean, shocking, leaving a hole my tongue kept returning to. On the band, a pause I couldn't read. Then: "Confirmed. Dawn check-in, Quintana. Devereux out."

The radio hissed static into the darkening bowl.

* * *

Ulyana didn't say *I told you so*, which was somehow louder than if she had. She busied herself with the mess shelter—she lit the stove, actually, fed it the split wood stacked beside it, and put water on for tea, because according to her the tea was already ruined and someone ought to drink it properly. She moved through the dead men's camp—or the vanished people's camp—with a strange gentleness, setting the abandoned pot upright, righting a fallen chair, and I understood she was doing for these strangers what no one else was going to do: acknowledging they had existed.

"They're alive," she said at last, quietly, across the stove flame. "Whoever took them. You don't feed a stove for dead men. You don't seal crates and leave beds made for corpses. They were taken somewhere, Xanthe. Taken *carefully*."

"By their own government."

"By *your* government." She handed me tea I hadn't asked for. "Which makes you ask yourself a question, doesn't it. The one you've been not-asking since the ruin."

I drank the tea. It was bitter and perfect. Above the bowl the first stars were coming out over the Ashfall crest, and the seismic drums kept turning in the dark, writing down everything the mountain said to no one.

"The debt," I said finally. It was the closest I'd ever come to saying it aloud to another living soul. "My family owes the Ministry. It's why I wear the badge. It's why my word has been worth something—because they own everything else, so I kept one thing clean." I turned the tin cup in my hands. "Tonight I lied to Devereux. First time in twenty years. And do you know what frightens me? It wasn't hard."

Ulyana watched me over her own cup, firelight moving in her eyes, and for once she didn't have a blade ready.

"If they can make ten surveyors disappear off a mountainside," I said, "then the leash I've been wearing my whole life isn't attached to anything. It never was. There's no institution at the end of it. There's just—" I gestured at the empty camp, the tide-marked mud, the whole patient machinery of erasure. "Just this."

The wind came down off the glacier and the tents breathed around us, in and out, and somewhere below the floor of the world the mountain shifted its weight and the drums wrote it down.

Ulyana set down her cup. "Then tomorrow," she said, "we should find out what they didn't want anyone to hear."
