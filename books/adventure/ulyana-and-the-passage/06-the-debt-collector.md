## 6. The Debt Collector

Ulyana woke to the sound of Xanthe Okafor sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening.

They'd made camp in the lee of a granite shoulder where the scrub gave way to the first stunted pines of the Ashfall foothills, and the ranger had taken the watch without being asked, which was its own kind of message. *I don't trust you asleep near me.* The whetstone whispered against steel in long, patient strokes, and the sound filled the grey dawn like something patient and hungry.

Ulyana sat up in her bedroll and watched them for a moment. Xanthe Okafor was not what she'd expected when she'd lifted the compass from her brother's apartment and started following the trail it pointed down. She had expected a Ministry creature—soft-handed, paper-souled, a person who filed reports on other people's lives. What she'd gotten instead was this: a rangy, weathered figure with dust worked so deep into the creases of their face it looked geological, hands that moved with the economy of someone who had learned every motion cost something, and eyes that never quite settled on anything for longer than a heartbeat. As if stillness itself were a debt they refused to owe.

*You erase yourself,* she thought, watching them. *Even your own face.*

The knife stopped. "You're up."

"I'm up."

"Water's on. We make the lower slopes by midday if we push." They didn't look at her. The whetstone resumed.

Ulyana poured herself water from the dented pot and let it scald her palms through the tin. Two days in the ruin at Salis Well had burned off some of the hatred—or rather, had transmuted it into something more useful, something with edges. The storm had pinned them together in a room barely wider than a grave, and they had spent those two days circling each other like dogs chained too close, snapping whenever the chain allowed. But chains also teach you the length of your reach. She knew now how far Xanthe would go, and it was farther than she'd guessed, and in directions she hadn't anticipated.

She knew, too, that the ranger wanted the compass back with a hunger that went past property. And Ulyana Voss had survived eight years of thieving by knowing one thing above all others: *want* was leverage.

"You should eat," Xanthe said.

"So should you."

"I ate."

"Liar." She tore the flatbread and held half across the fire. For a long moment she thought they wouldn't take it. Then they did, without thanks, and ate it in four efficient bites, and something about the efficiency made her chest ache in a way she refused to examine.

* * *

They climbed through the morning into country that changed underfoot. The soft sandstone of the scrublands hardened into grey shale, then into ridges of black basalt veined with quartz that caught the light like old scars. Xanthe moved through it the way water moves downhill—unhurried, inevitable, reading the ground with their whole body. Twice they stopped and crouched over stone where Ulyana saw nothing at all, and twice they adjusted their line by degrees that meant nothing to her and everything to them.

It was on the second ridge, with the Ashfall Range finally rearing up ahead of them—a wall of dark peaks with snow already clinging to the high saddles like salt on a wound—that Ulyana decided to spend her leverage.

"The survey team," she said.

Xanthe didn't break stride. "What about them?"

"The one that vanished. Up here. Three weeks ago." She watched their shoulders. "You knew about it before Devereux ever radioed you. You've been angling toward their last camp since the ruin. I thought you were following me." She smiled, thin as a blade. "Turns out we're both following something."

Now the ranger stopped. Turned. The wind came off the peaks and flattened the dry grass around their boots. "Say what you mean, thief."

"My name is Ulyana."

"Say what you mean, *Ulyana.*"

She reached into her coat—not fast, deliberately slow, telegraphing every inch—and drew out the compass. It sat in her palm like a small brass heart, dented at the rim, the glass scratched nearly opaque. Her brother's fingers had worn the casing bright once; now her own were doing the same work, and she hated how natural it felt.

"This isn't a compass," she said. "Or it is, but that's the least of what it is. Open the bezel. Under the needle plate there's an engraving—a series of marks. Coordinates. My brother spent two years trying to decode them."

Something moved behind Xanthe's eyes. Not recognition exactly. More like a door opening onto a lit room they'd believed dark for years.

"Your brother," they said carefully.

"Tomas Voss. Field technician, Ministry Geological Survey, third division." She recited it flat, the way you recite a headstone. "He was assigned to the Ashfall assessment team. The one that walked into these mountains three weeks ago and stopped existing."

The wind again. Somewhere below, a hawk cried once and gave up.

"You're lying," Xanthe said.

"I'm many things, but I don't lie about him." She turned the compass over, thumbed the hidden catch Tomas had shown her a hundred times in a hundred letters that had grown shorter and stranger over his final year. The bezel popped. Beneath the trembling needle, etched fine as spider silk into the brass: a lattice of numbers and glyphs that meant nothing to anyone who hadn't been taught to see them.

"Tomas decoded half of it before he died. He sent me what he had. A grid reference." She looked up, met Xanthe's eyes straight on, and drove it home. "It matches a coordinate string in the survey team's data packet. The one the Ministry says doesn't exist because the team never existed because there's no record of them past the last supply drop." She closed the bezel with a snap. "So either my dead brother was chasing ghosts, or your Ministry erased seven people off the face of the Ashfall Range, and you've been helping them do it."

* * *

To her lasting surprise, Xanthe didn't explode.

That was the thing she was learning about them: the anger didn't come out loud. It came out cold, compressed, like rock under pressure—store enough of it and it didn't shatter, it *shifted*, whole landscapes of it moving at once. They stood very still on the ridge with the wind pulling at their coat, and when they finally spoke their voice was level in a way that frightened her more than shouting would have.

"The Ministry doesn't erase people."

"No?"

"No. It's inefficient. Erasure creates questions, questions create investigations, investigations cost more than whatever you're burying." They said it like arithmetic, like a lesson learned young and paid for in full. "What actually happens is simpler. People get reassigned. Records get misfiled. Teams get redirected to new sites and the old site gets closed for 'environmental reasons.' Nobody vanishes, Ulyana. They just stop being where you're looking."

"And that's better?"

"It's *true*. Which matters if you're going to accuse people." They started walking again, faster now, boots striking the shale hard. "Tomas Voss was probably rotated to a northern survey. Letters get lost. Mail between divisions is a disaster—you'd know that if you'd ever—"

"He's *dead*, Xanthe."

The name landed harder than she'd intended, and she watched it land—watched the ranger's stride hitch, just slightly, the way a horse hitches when it steps in a hole.

"They found his effects in a Ministry locker in Solmere," she said, catching up, unable to stop now that the floodgate was open. "His jacket. His notebook. His boots. Do you understand? His *boots*. Nobody keeps a man's boots unless they're done needing the feet inside them. That's why I took the compass from the estate auction—I tracked it there after he died, because it was the last thing he—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, hated showing this crack in front of this person, and she pushed through it raw. "He wrote me eleven days before the end. He said he'd found something in the Ashfall data that 'changed the shape of the map.' Those were his words. *Changed the shape of the map.* Then nothing. Then a locker full of clothes."

Xanthe had stopped. They stood facing the mountains with their back to her, and for a moment Ulyana thought—hoped—they might be hearing it. Really hearing it.

Then they turned, and their face was the face of the institution itself: composed, reasonable, immovable. "Teams go dark in the field all the time. Comms fail. Resupply gets delayed. The Ashfall Range eats radio signals for breakfast—the geology alone—"

"The geology." Ulyana laughed, and it came out ugly. "Yes. Let's talk about the geology. Because here's what I think, ranger. I think your Ministry knows exactly what's under these mountains. I think my brother found it, and I think that's why he's dead, and I think that survey team found it too, and that's why they're gone. And I think you know it. Deep down, in the place you keep everything you don't look at—I think you've known since the day Devereux told you to come find me, and you came anyway, because coming was easier than asking why a stolen compass matters enough to send their best tracker into winter range for it."

"That's not—"

"What does the debt say?" She stepped closer. "Tell me. You've never told me. Two days in a storm shelter and six days on the trail and you've never once said what your family owes them. What is it? Money? Land? Blood?"

For the first time since she'd known them, Xanthe Okafor flinched.

"There it is," Ulyana said softly. "There it is. You won't even say it. You'll kill yourself climbing this mountain for them, you'll drag me back in irons if you have to, but you can't speak the debt out loud, because speaking it means looking at it, and looking at it means seeing what it's *made of*."

"You don't know anything about my family."

"I know they're gone."

The words came out before she could weigh them, and the instant they left her mouth she knew they were cruel, and some small sane part of her screamed to pull them back—and the rest of her, the part that had buried a brother with no body and no answers, let them fly.

"Your mother's estate sold a dead woman's compass at public auction, Xanthe. Your handler owns your license, your movements, your *name*—you go by initials on your own registration papers, I've seen them. You live like a ghost in a town full of ghosts, tracking a thief across a desert for a piece of brass, and you want me to believe the Ministry that owns all that is just a big imperfect family that misfiles things?" She shook her head. "You're not defending them because you believe them. You're defending them because if they're rotten, then everything you gave them was wasted. Your whole life. And that's a price no one can afford to look at. Believe me. I've priced it."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted a long time.

Xanthe stood with the wind at their back and the mountains rising behind them, and Ulyana watched a lifetime of compressed things move behind their face like weather seen from very far away. When they finally spoke, their voice had dropped to almost nothing, scraped down to the grain.

"My mother worked for them."

Ulyana said nothing. She had learned, in the ruin, that silence pulled more out of this person than any question.

"She was a surveyor. Before me. Before the debt." Xanthe's hand drifted toward their coat pocket—the pocket where the compass had lived for twenty years, the pocket that was empty now, and the gesture was so involuntary, so naked, that Ulyana felt it like a hook behind her ribs. "She used to carry that compass on every traverse. She taught me to read contour lines before she taught me letters. And then one year she went into the range on contract and didn't come out, and the Ministry told us she was lost, and the debt arrived six months later with her signature on it." A breath. "Her signature, Ulyana. On a document dated nine days *after* she disappeared."

The wind keened along the ridge. High above, a cornice of snow calved off a distant peak with a sound like a muffled shot, and neither of them flinched.

"So don't tell me what I'm defending," Xanthe said. "I know exactly what I'm defending. I've carried it since I was fourteen years old. It's the only thing standing between the Ministry and—" They stopped. Jaw working. "It's the only thing I have of hers. The compass. The debt. All of it. It's the last proof she existed, and you stole it, and you stand there telling me it's evidence in your brother's tragedy like mine doesn't count."

"It counts," Ulyana said quietly. "That's the point. It counts so much it can't be the only thing. Don't you see? If the compass is all you have, then they've already won. They've reduced your mother to an object you can hold in one hand. But if it's evidence—if it points somewhere, if it opens something—then she's not a loss anymore. She's a witness. So is Tomas. So are seven people freezing somewhere in these mountains right now, maybe, if they're alive, waiting for someone to come who isn't carrying orders."

Xanthe looked at her for a long, long moment. And Ulyana saw it happen—the shift, the landscape moving, the thing under the pressure finding a new fault line. Not surrender. Not belief. But a crack, hairline-fine, running through the foundation.

"If we find that camp," Xanthe said slowly, "and there's nothing in their data but mineral assays and bad handwriting—"

"Then I'll give you the compass and let you arrest me. Fair trade."

"And if there's something else?"

Ulyana held their gaze. Below them, miles down the slope, a thread of smoke rose from a hidden valley—Kettlebrook, the maps said, though the maps marked it so small it was barely a smudge. Above them, the pass waited, white and silent, holding whatever answer the Ministry had buried there.

"Then you decide," she said, "who you've been working for all these years."

Xanthe turned away first. Shouldered their pack. Started up the switchback toward the treeline without another word—but Ulyana noticed they set a pace she could keep, and chose a line that spared her the worst of the scree, and when the trail crossed a stream an hour later, they waited on the far bank until she'd crossed.

Waiting. For her. The debt collector's hound, waiting.

That night, camped under the first true stars of the high country, Ulyana lay awake listening to Xanthe breathe in the dark ten feet away, and she took out the compass and opened the bezel and watched the needle swing, swing, settle—pointing not north but up-range, into the heart of the Ashfall, toward a coordinate that existed in exactly two places in the world: engraved in brass by her mother's hand, and buried in the files of seven missing people.

In the morning, they would reach the survey camp.

In the morning, one of them would be proven right.

And as she finally drifted to sleep, Ulyana realized she could no longer remember which outcome she was afraid of more.
