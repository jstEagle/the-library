## 7. Cold Camp

The treeline gave up without ceremony.

One hour the slopes were dark with pine, the next the trees simply stopped—stunted, wind-bent things at first, then nothing but stone and lichen and a sky gone the color of old tin. Ulyana stopped at the edge of it and looked back the way they'd come, as if she expected the forest to explain itself.

"Snow by tomorrow," Xanthe said. "Maybe tonight."

"You said three days."

"I said three days yesterday." Xanthe shrugged the pack higher on their shoulders and kept climbing. "The mountain doesn't file amendments with me first."

They made camp at dusk in a hollow below a granite shoulder, where the wind split around them instead of through them. There was no wood to burn up here, no fuel of any kind, so Xanthe did what rangers had done on the Ashfall for a hundred years: they pitched both tents nested together, the small one inside the large, with a gap of dead air between the canvas layers that held warmth like a jar holds water.

Ulyana watched them rig it with an expression Xanthe couldn't read. "One tent," she said finally.

"Two tents is how people die up here. You share body heat or you don't wake up." Xanthe staked the last guy-line and tested it with a boot heel. "I'll sleep against the wall side. The cold comes off the rock, not off you."

"I wasn't complaining."

"No," Xanthe said. "You were deciding whether to be insulted. Don't bother. It's arithmetic, not courtesy."

That shut her up, which was becoming a habit Xanthe didn't entirely trust themselves to enjoy.

* * *

The storm arrived two hours after full dark, and it arrived like a debt collector—no warning, no negotiation, just pressure. The wind came down off the summit ridge in long flat shoves that leaned on the tent until the poles groaned, and snow came with it, dry and granular, hissing against the outer canvas like sand poured from a great height. Inside the nested walls the temperature held, barely, and the sound was enormous: a white roar that erased the world beyond arm's reach.

Xanthe lay awake and listened to it and thought about lying.

They'd been doing it for three days now. Small lies, professional lies, the kind a ranger tells civilians to keep them calm on bad ground. *It's not far.* *The crossing is routine.* *We'll make the pass before weather.* Each one had come out smooth, and each one had sat in Xanthe's chest afterward like a swallowed stone, because for eleven years the Ministry's leash had been the one thing about themselves they could count on: Xanthe Okafor does not lie to the Ministry, does not lie for anyone, does not give the collectors a reason to pull.

And now there was a woman asleep four feet away who knew about the survey camp. Who had seen the instruments still running, the meals half-eaten, the absence of tracks. Who had watched Xanthe key the radio and tell Devereux, in a voice steady as poured concrete, *nothing here, moving on* — the first lie of Xanthe's career, told in front of a witness, told for reasons Xanthe still hadn't let themselves examine.

Ulyana stirred. "You're thinking loud enough to hear."

"Go back to sleep."

"Can't. Cold makes my brother's knee ache." A pause. "My knee. Cold makes my knee ache." She said it flatly, correcting herself, and Xanthe heard the flinch under it—the ghost of a dead man surfacing in a pronoun. She'd been doing that all week, talking about Tam as if he were alive, catching herself, going quiet. Grief with nowhere to put itself.

"The cold will break by midmorning," Xanthe said. "Then we move. The coordinates you want are another day up, maybe less if the scree field hasn't shifted."

"And after that?"

"After that we'll see what your brother died looking at."

Ulyana was quiet a long moment. When she spoke again her voice had changed—lower, stripped of its usual blade-edge irony. "You don't believe me. About the Ministry erasing them. You think I'm chasing ghosts and dragging you into it."

"I think," Xanthe said carefully, "that I saw a camp with no tracks out of it, and I lied to my handler about it, and I haven't figured out yet which of those facts frightens me more."

The wind shoved the tent. Snow ticked against the inner canvas, fine as sifted flour.

"That's the most honest thing you've said since Solmere," Ulyana murmured.

"Don't get used to it."

* * *

Sleep wouldn't come. Xanthe gave up on it somewhere past midnight and found Ulyana awake too, sitting propped against her pack with the compass cradled in both hands, turning it slowly in the faint gray light of the storm-lit tent. She wasn't reading it. She was holding it the way people hold things they're afraid to look at directly.

"Give it here," Xanthe said, and surprised themselves by saying it gently.

Ulyana handed it over without argument, which frightened Xanthe more than any fight would have. Up close, in the dimness, the brass was worn smooth at the edges—worn by thumbs, by years of thumbs, by a specific pattern of handling Xanthe recognized the way you recognize a voice through a wall. Their mother's compass. Their mother's wear.

"She used to hold it like that," Xanthe heard themselves say. "At night. When she thought I was asleep. Turn it over and over, like she was asking it a question."

Ulyana went very still. "You remember her."

"I remember everything about her. That's the problem."

The words came out before Xanthe could weigh them, and once they were out there was no taking them back, and something in Xanthe—some load-bearing wall they'd been bracing against for eleven years—simply decided it was done.

"They told me she died," Xanthe said. The storm roared; they spoke into it, low, almost under it. "When I was nineteen. Official notice, sealed envelope, condolence stamped by some clerk who spelled her name wrong. *Regret to inform you.* I got the estate, such as it was, and the debt, and a ranger's license conditional on good service. That was the arrangement. Serve, pay down the family obligation, keep your head down, and the Ministry considers the matter closed."

Ulyana's eyes hadn't left their face. "But she didn't die."

"I went looking. Four years ago. Quietly—quietly being the only way I know how to do anything." Xanthe turned the compass over. The needle swung, settled, pointed north with its usual idiot confidence. "There's no death record. There's no grave. There's a transfer order, redacted, dated eight days after the condolence letter. She was moved into Ministry custody and every document after that has been burned clean. She's alive, Ulyana. Or she was. And the debt—" Xanthe's throat closed, and they had to start again. "The debt isn't money. Money would be simple. The debt is *her*. Her name is collateral. As long as I serve and stay invisible, she stays breathing in whatever room they keep her in. The day I stop—" They spread their hands. Empty hands. "Devereux has never had to say it out loud. He's never had to. That's how you know it's true."

The silence that followed was long and full of wind.

"So when you defend them," Ulyana said at last, very quietly, "when you tell me the Ministry doesn't erase people—it isn't faith. It's hostage logic."

"Yes."

"And you hate it."

"Every hour of every day." Xanthe laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Eleven years I've been a ghost with a badge. No family listed. No attachments declared. No questions asked. I made myself into nothing because nothing can't be leveraged—and then you walked into my jurisdiction and stole the one object on this earth I actually loved, and I chased you across forty miles of scrubland telling myself it was duty." They shook their head. "It was never duty. Duty is what I call it so I don't have to admit I wanted something."

Ulyana reached over and took the compass back—not pulling it away, just closing her hands around Xanthe's hands around it, so for a moment they were both holding it, and neither was holding it alone.

"My brother decoded half that engraving before they killed him," she said. "He used to do the same thing with it. Hold it at night. Ask it questions." Her voice caught and steadied. "I took it from the estate because it was the last thing he touched that mattered, and because I needed someone who knew the Ashfall better than he did, and you were the best ranger on the registry. I told myself it was strategy too." Her thumb moved, barely, against Xanthe's knuckle. "It wasn't all strategy."

Outside, the storm screamed. Inside, the small space between them had gone warm in a way that had nothing to do with layered canvas, and Xanthe became acutely aware of the geography of the tent: six feet of floor, two bedrolls, the distance between her shoulder and theirs measured now in inches rather than intentions.

"We should sleep," Xanthe said, and made no move whatsoever toward sleeping.

"Probably," Ulyana agreed, equally motionless.

Neither of them named it. Naming it would have made it a thing with edges, a thing that could be reported, leveraged, taken. It stayed unnamed—a heat without a shape, kindling quietly in the dark between two people who had spent their whole lives learning how to disappear, discovering that they didn't want to disappear from each other.

Eventually Ulyana lay back, and after a while Xanthe did too, closer than arithmetic required, and the storm wore itself out against the mountain while they listened to each other breathe.

* * *

Dawn came blue and windless and blinding, the whole world repainted overnight in unbroken white. Xanthe crawled out to check the sky and felt the cold go through them like a blade finding the gap in armor. The scree field below the shoulder was gone—buried. The back-trail was gone. Every landmark they'd used on the ascent had been smoothed into a single featureless slope, and the air had the dense, ringing stillness that meant more snow stacked up behind the ridgeline, waiting its turn.

Ulyana emerged, squinting, breath smoking. "Bad?"

"There's no retreat anymore," Xanthe said. "Only forward." They shouldered the pack and found, examining the feeling honestly for the first time, that it didn't frighten them. It felt like a door closing behind them that they'd been meaning to close for years.

They climbed. By late morning the terrain matched the numbers Ulyana recited from her brother's notes, and by early afternoon they stood on a shelf of black rock beneath a cliff face seamed with quartz, and Ulyana's voice shook as she said, "This is it. This is where the survey team's data points."

Xanthe unslung the radio out of pure habit—to check weather, to log position, to perform the ritual of accountability—and froze with their thumb on the switch.

The channel light was already blinking. A message, queued, waiting: priority flag, Devereux's call sign, timestamped before dawn.

Ulyana watched their face. "What?"

Xanthe pressed playback, and Devereux's voice came through thin and precise in the frozen air:

*"Okafor. We know you didn't clear the survey site. Return the Voss woman and the compass by the fastest route, abandon all inquiry, and this remains a personnel matter. Fail to comply, and the protection arrangement regarding your mother will be reclassified effective immediately. You have until the snowline closes. Devereux out."*

The radio hissed static into the silence. Somewhere above them, high on the ridge, the wind changed note—and the first flakes of the next storm began to fall, fat and slow, onto the black stone where a murdered man's coordinates waited under the snow.

Ulyana's hand found the compass at her neck. Xanthe looked up the slope, then back the way they'd come, then at the blinking channel light, and understood that the trap had closed around them both—and that only one direction remained.

"Keep walking," Xanthe said.
