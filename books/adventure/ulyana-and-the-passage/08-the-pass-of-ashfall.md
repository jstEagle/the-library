## 8. The Pass of Ashfall

The coordinates took them up through country that had forgotten it was ever gentle.

Ulyana felt the change in the mountain before she saw it. The scrub pines thinned to twisted krummholz, then to bare gray stone furred with lichen the color of old ash. The wind came off the high ridges carrying a smell she'd learned on other ranges and never trusted—sulfur and cold iron, the breath of rock that was not entirely asleep.

Xanthe walked ahead, as they always did now, reading the slope with a ranger's patience. Three days above the treeline had worn them both down to essentials: rope, stove, the brass compass riding in Ulyana's inner pocket against her ribs like a second heartbeat. They spoke little. But the silence between them had changed since Cold Camp, since Xanthe had said *my mother didn't die* into the dark of the tent and meant it as a gift. It was no longer the silence of two people guarding separate fires. It was the silence of people climbing toward the same one.

"Here." Xanthe stopped where a shelf of granite jutted from the mountainside like the brow of a buried face. Below them, half-hidden in a fold of scree, stood a cairn—not a trail marker but something older, deliberate, its stones fitted with a care no survey crew wasted on wayfinding. "Your brother's numbers put the station here?"

Ulyana unfolded the oilcloth packet again, though she knew the figures by heart. Tomas's handwriting, cramped and hurried, the last page of his last letter: *If I don't come back, take this to the Ashfall Range. The engraving is the key. Find the cairn below the split peak. He'd drawn the peak—a summit cloven by some ancient collapse—and Ulyana looked up now and found it exactly there, two gray horns against a white sky.*

"That's it," she said. "The split peak. And he wrote that the cairn would be *below*, not beside. That someone wanted it overlooked."

Xanthe crouched at the base of the stones. Their hands moved over the rock with a tenderness Ulyana hadn't seen them give anything except the compass, back when it had still been theirs. "These aren't stacked. They're keyed." A fingertip traced a hairline seam. "There's a hollow."

It took both of them twenty minutes and one skinned knuckle to work the capstone free without bringing the whole structure down. Inside, wrapped in waxed canvas gone stiff with age, lay an iron box no larger than a bread loaf. No lock. No Ministry seal. Just a name scratched into the lid in a woman's hand:

**M. VOSS — FOR THE ONE WHO CLIMBS**

Ulyana's breath went out of her all at once.

Not her own name. Her mother's.

"I don't—" She heard her voice crack and hated it. "That's my mother's initials. Mariya Voss. She died when I was nine. Tomas was twelve. We were told it was fever."

Xanthe said nothing for a long moment. When they finally spoke, their voice was very quiet. "Open it."

* * *

Inside the box: a field journal bound in oiled leather, a bundle of photographic plates, and a folded map of the Ashfall Range so detailed it made Xanthe inhale sharply—the kind of mapping that took years, that took *love*. The journal's first entry was dated nineteen years ago. The handwriting was her mother's; Ulyana knew it the way you know a face in a dream, from letters saved in a drawer, from the labels on jam jars long emptied.

She sat down on the cold stone because her legs informed her standing was no longer among their services.

*Field Notes, Ashfall Seismic Survey — M. Voss, Lead Geodesist.* Page after page of readings, sketches, careful prose. And threaded through all of it, growing denser as the pages turned, a single recurring word, underlined, circled, written in the margins in increasingly urgent script:

*The Suture.*

"The fault," Xanthe said over her shoulder. They'd taken the map and were holding it against the actual horizon, matching ridge to line. "This isn't a mineral survey at all. Look at the transects—they're not looking for ore. They're listening to the mountain." They tapped a column of figures. "Microquakes. Swarms. Depth-consistent movement along a plane running the whole length of the range beneath Kettlebrook valley. Your mother mapped a live fault system."

Ulyana turned pages faster. The entries grew shorter, harder. *Third swarm this month along the eastern suture. Amplitude increasing. Requested extension of study period; denied.* Then: *Ministry review board visited today. They were not interested in the swarms. They were interested in how many copies of my data exist.* Then, near the end, in handwriting pressed so hard the pen had torn the paper:

*I have been ordered to classify the eastern suture as dormant. I will not. There are eleven thousand people in Kettlebrook. Relocation is expensive. Funerals are cheaper. That is the arithmetic they gave me, in those words, in those words, in those words—*

The last entry stopped mid-sentence. The remaining pages were blank.

Ulyana became aware that she was making a sound, low and continuous, and that Xanthe had knelt beside her and put a hand on her shoulder and left it there. She let herself lean into it for exactly three breaths. Then she closed the journal and stood up, because grief was a luxury with a price tag and she had already paid too much of it sitting down.

"Tomas found this," she said. "Or found the trail to it. The compass key—it matches the coordinates in the survey team's data, the team that vanished last month. He wasn't decoding treasure, Xanthe. He was following her. Our mother started this, and the Ministry buried her for it, and twenty years later he picked up the thread and they buried him too."

"They didn't bury him," Xanthe said slowly. "They *detained* him. If the survey team is alive—if that's what we find down-valley—"

"Then he can tell me why he never sent the letter." Her voice came out flat and hard, and she recognized the tone as her mother's, from the journal, from the torn page. Good. Let it be hers. Anger with a spine in it was worth more than tears.

She took out the compass.

Up here, in the thin gold light of late afternoon, the brass casing caught the sun and threw it back warm. She'd carried it for months as a talisman, then as a burden, then—since the tent, since Xanthe's confession in the dark—as something more complicated than either. Now she turned it over and looked at the engraved underside with new eyes. She'd assumed the fine etched lines were decoration, or a maker's mark, or the map reference Tomas had died trying to read. But with her mother's journal open beside her, the pattern resolved the way stars resolve into constellations when someone finally shows you where to look.

"It's not a code," she breathed. "It's a seismogram. Look—the spacing of these lines, the depth marks. She engraved the fault itself. The whole eastern suture, compressed onto the back of a compass. Anyone who could read it would carry the proof in their pocket." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, wet at the edges. "She hid the mountain inside an instrument for finding your way. Because a compass looks innocent. Because nobody searches a compass."

Xanthe had gone very still. "And the needle," they said. "You said it doesn't point north."

"It doesn't." Ulyana held it level. The needle swung, hesitated, and settled—pointing not north but east-southeast, down the long axis of the range, toward Kettlebrook. Toward the suture. "It points at the fault. It's been pointing at the fault the entire time. My mother built a compass that refuses to lie about where the danger is." She looked up at Xanthe. "Do you understand what that means? Every reading in this journal, every number the vanished team collected, every coordinate in Tomas's letter—it all says the same thing. The Ministry knows. They've known for twenty years. And they're going to let it happen because moving eleven thousand people costs more than burying them."

The wind gusted across the shelf, sharp with the coming season. Somewhere below them, a deep percussive groan rolled through the rock—not quite sound, more a pressure felt in the teeth—and neither of them flinched, because after everything, the mountain's voice was the only honest thing left on this range.

"We should keep moving," Xanthe said. "Snow's coming early. I can smell it."

"One more thing first." Ulyana turned to the back of the journal, to the pocket sewn inside the cover that her fingers had found while Xanthe studied the map. In it: a single photograph, and a letter.

The photograph showed a younger woman standing on this same shelf, or one like it—she recognized the split peak behind her—with a brass compass held up in one hand and an expression that was equal parts defiance and joy. On the back, in her mother's hand: *Mariya, final ascent, the day we finished the suture map. They can classify what I write. They cannot classify what I climb.*

The letter was addressed to no one. Or rather, to whoever climbed high enough to find it.

*If you are reading this, then the Ministry failed to stop the truth from reaching altitude, which pleases me enormously. Know this: the eastern suture is live. I measured it until they took my instruments. My children will be told I died of fever; if you meet them, tell them instead that I died refusing to lie, and that lying is the only death I fear. The compass will show you the way down. Follow the needle. It points at what matters.*

Ulyana read it aloud, twice, because the first time her voice broke on *my children* and she needed to hear it whole.

When she finished, the light had gone copper and long. Xanthe stood at the edge of the shelf looking down-valley, toward Kettlebrook, toward the smoke of woodstoves rising from a town that did not know it lived on a held breath. When they turned around, their face was doing something complicated—grief for a stranger who had turned out not to be a stranger at all, anger arriving late to a crime two decades cold, and underneath it all, Ulyana realized, guilt. Ranger's guilt. Institutional guilt. The look of someone recalculating every year of service against a ledger that had just been revealed as fraudulent.

"My handler," Xanthe said quietly. "Devereux. The orders. *Report findings and move on.* They've been managing me up this mountain the whole time, haven't they. Keeping me close to whatever I found." A pause. "Keeping us both close."

"Then we've been ahead of them since the ruin," Ulyana said. "Let's stay ahead."

She wrapped the journal in its oilcloth, tucked the photograph inside her shirt next to the compass, and slung her pack. The needle swung gently in its housing, patient as her mother must have been, pointing down the dark seam of the world toward eleven thousand sleeping lives.

Below them, far down the eastern flank, a thread of woodsmoke rose from the valley town—thin, ordinary, unbearably fragile. And beyond it, on the road they'd have to descend, Ulyana saw something that pulled the breath tight in her chest: a line of vehicles crawling up the switchbacks, government-gray, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who owned the road and everything it led to.

Headed for Kettlebrook.

"Xanthe," she said. "How fast can you get us down this mountain?"
