## 9. Snowline

The snow came in the night, and it came like a thief.

Xanthe woke to a silence so complete it felt physical—a pressure against the eardrums, the kind of hush that only falls when the world has been muffled under fresh white. For a moment they didn't know where they were. The tent walls glowed faintly gray with pre-dawn light, and their breath hung in the air in slow silver plumes, and somewhere very close Ulyana was still asleep, her breathing shallow and even, one hand curled near the stove they'd let burn down to embers.

Then Xanthe understood what the silence meant, and their stomach dropped.

They unzipped the tent flap and cold poured over them like water. The world had been remade overnight. The scree slopes below their camp—the ones they'd spent two hard days climbing, the ones whose every handhold and loose slab Xanthe had catalogued with a ranger's meticulous memory—were gone. Buried. In their place lay a smooth, featureless sweep of white running down into cloud, unbroken, unreadable. No tracks. Not theirs, not anything's. Their back-trail, the whole laborious record of their ascent, erased as cleanly as if they'd never climbed at all.

"Early," Xanthe said aloud, to no one, because saying it made it real. "Too early."

The almanacs put first snowfall on the Ashfall high routes at mid-month, sometimes later. This was eight days ahead of the earliest year on record. Eight days was nothing. Eight days was the difference between a pass you could cross and a pass that closed behind you like a door.

Behind them, inside the tent, Ulyana stirred. Xanthe heard the rustle of the sleeping bag, the small metallic sounds of her reaching for her boots, and felt—unwillingly, helplessly—that same pull in the chest they'd been feeling for days now, the one that said *she's awake* like it was good news.

They'd shared a tent for four nights. They'd shared heat when the temperature fell, food when the rations thinned, and somewhere above the treeline, in the blue dark of a cold camp, Xanthe had told her about their mother—not the estate, not the debt, but the truth: that Vessa Okafor hadn't died, that she'd been taken into Ministry custody twenty-two years ago and never returned, and that the brass compass in Ulyana's pack was the last thing she'd ever left behind. Ulyana had listened without flinching. She'd said, quietly, *then we're both chasing ghosts*, and something had passed between them in the firelight that Xanthe had spent every day since refusing to name.

Naming things gave them weight. Weight slowed you down. And they could not afford to be slow now.

"You felt it too." Ulyana emerged beside them, hair wild, breath steaming, eyes going immediately to the buried slope the way Xanthe's had. She didn't waste words on surprise. She read the snowfall the way she read everything—fast, hungry, already calculating. "How long do we have?"

"If it holds off? Two days to the Pass of Ashfall. Three if the wind picks up." Xanthe crouched and pressed two fingers into the snowpack. Six inches, dense, sitting on bare ground. That was bad. Snow on warm earth meant melt-refreeze cycles, meant crust, meant slides. "If it starts again, we find shelter and we pray."

"And if the pass is closed on the far side?"

"Then we winter on the mountain." Xanthe stood, brushing wet from their knees. "Which is another way of saying we die on the mountain, just slowly."

Ulyana looked at them sidelong. There was frost in her lashes. "You're cheerful this morning."

"I'm honest this morning. It looks similar from a distance."

That earned the ghost of a smile, there and gone, and Xanthe turned away from it before it could land anywhere permanent. They broke camp in efficient silence—two people who'd learned each other's rhythms without meaning to, Ulyana striking the tent while Xanthe coiled rope and checked the stove, handing her the pegs before she asked, taking the pot she'd washed without being told. It should have alarmed them, how smooth it had become. Instead it frightened them in an entirely different way.

They were on the move by full light, climbing through a world gone strange and new. Snow changed everything about a mountain. Sound traveled differently; distance lied; familiar rock became treacherous under a skin of white that hid the ice beneath. Xanthe led, probing each step with the pole, reading the slope through their boots, and Ulyana followed in their track exactly, close enough that their shadows overlapped on the bright surface.

It was near midday, on a shoulder of ridge with the whole western range spread out below them—white to the horizon, beautiful and merciless—that the radio crackled.

The set rode in Xanthe's pack, Ministry-issue, heavy as sin, and it had been silent for six days. Silence from Devereux was never good. Silence meant he was either satisfied or patient, and Xanthe had known him long enough to bet on patient.

The crackle came again, then his voice, dry and precise as a ledger line: *"Okafor. Report position."*

Xanthe stopped. Ulyana, ten paces back, went still.

They lifted the handset. "Ridge nine, west aspect. Elevation three-one-five-zero. We're moving toward the pass."

A pause long enough to hear the static thinking. *"We,"* Devereux said.

"The thief. Voss. I have her in custody."

*"Do you."* Another pause. Somewhere behind Devereux's voice, Xanthe could hear the particular quiet of a Ministry office—no traffic, no clatter, just the hum of a room where decisions got made about people who would never hear them being made. *"And where is the compass?"*

Xanthe's hand tightened on the handset. Below them the valley fell away into cloud. Above them the pass waited, a notch of gray sky between black peaks. And somewhere south of here, in a facility with no name on any map, a woman who might or might not still be their mother sat in a room, alive or not-alive in whatever way the Ministry permitted, her continued existence a line item balanced against Xanthe's obedience.

Twenty-two years. Twenty-two years of erasing themselves—no attachments, no records beyond the required ones, no home longer than a season, a ranger's license held like a leash by a family debt Xanthe had never once been allowed to see the terms of. All of it for her. Every empty year, purchased with silence.

"We found a survey camp," Xanthe said. "Six days ago. Abandoned. Instruments running, meals half-eaten, no tracks out. I reported it."

*"You reported finding gear,"* Devereux corrected mildly. *"You did not report what happened next. You did not report that you turned off your radio for six days. You did not report that instead of descending to Solmere as ordered, you climbed higher, into a restricted survey corridor, in the company of a known thief."*

The static breathed. Ulyana had drifted closer, close enough to hear, and Xanthe didn't wave her back. That, too, was an answer.

"There are seismic instruments in that camp," Xanthe said. "I've been reading them. Devereux, the fault under this range is live. It's not dormant, it's not theoretical—it's loading. The survey team wasn't mapping minerals. They were measuring how much time Kettlebrook has."

He was quiet for a moment. When he spoke again, his voice had not changed at all, and that was the most frightening thing about it. *"Okafor. Listen carefully, because I will say this once.*"

The snowfield stretched out white and silent around them. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

*"Return the compass. Descend by the east route and surrender the device and the woman to the station at Solmere. Do this, and your report will be accepted as filed, your deviation noted as weather-related, and the matter closed. Your mother's protection continues uninterrupted."*

"And if I don't."

*"Then the debt protection lapses at month's end. You know what that means. The estate reverts. The custody arrangement—"*

"Don't say it like an arrangement. Say what it is."

Devereux paused. For the first time in twenty-two years, Xanthe thought they heard something almost human in the silence—regret, maybe, or its well-rehearsed imitation. *"She is sixty-three years old, Okafor. She has been comfortable. She has been safe. What happens to comfortable, safe people when their protection ends is not something I decide, but it is something I have seen. Come down. Bring the compass. Let this go."*

Xanthe looked at Ulyana.

She was watching them with an expression they'd seen once before, at the cold camp, in the hour before dawn—open, unguarded, offering nothing and asking nothing. She had a brother dead in Ministry custody and a compass engraved with coordinates that proved the government had known about the fault for years. She had every reason to want Xanthe to defy the order. She said none of them. She just stood there in the snow, letting Xanthe choose, which was somehow worse and better than arguing.

"Devereux," Xanthe said slowly. "One question. Twenty-two years of protection. Twenty-two years of payments on a debt I've never seen the ledger for. Has she ever—" Their voice caught, and they hated it, and pushed through. "Has my mother ever actually been *alive*? In any way that matters? Has anyone visited her? Has she spoken? Has she asked for anything, ever?"

The static ran on for five seconds. Ten. Long past the point where a man with an answer would have given it.

"That's what I thought," Xanthe said.

*"Okafor—"*

"No. You listen now." Something in Xanthe's chest had come loose, some load-bearing beam finally giving way, and what rushed into the space was terrifyingly clear. "You can keep the leash. You can keep the license and the debt and the whole quiet invisible life you built for me out of my mother's bones. But I'm telling you what I'm going to do, because you taught me always to file accurate reports. I'm crossing that pass. I'm decoding what's on that compass. And when I get to Kettlebrook, I'm going to stand in the town square and tell every person living in that valley exactly how much time they have, and exactly who decided they weren't worth warning. File that."

Silence. Then, softly, almost gently: *"You're choosing a stranger over your own blood."*

Xanthe looked at Ulyana—at the frost melting on her lashes, at the compass-shaped weight in her pack that had started all of this, at the person who had listened to the worst truth Xanthe had and hadn't looked away.

"I'm choosing the truth over the leash," they said. "Turns out those aren't the same thing. They never were."

They keyed the radio off. Then, after a moment's thought, they unbuckled it from the pack entirely and set it down in the snow—a black box on white ground, visible from the air, unmistakable. Let Devereux look at it. Let him explain to his superiors why their ranger had left it behind like a collar slipped.

Ulyana exhaled, long and shaking. "Your mother," she said carefully. "What happens to her. If they follow through."

"I don't know." Xanthe shouldered the pack. The strap bit into the wound at their side, the one from the ruin fight, and they welcomed the pain the way you welcome anything honest. "Maybe nothing. Maybe she's been gone for years and the threat was always hollow. Or maybe—" They stopped. Started again. "Or maybe I just traded her life for a valley full of strangers, and I'll spend the rest of mine knowing it. Either way, standing still wasn't survivable anymore. Some debts you pay by walking away from the collector."

Ulyana touched their arm—brief, warm through the glove, gone again. "Kettlebrook first," she said. "Then we find out what she'd have wanted."

"She'd have wanted the data buried," Xanthe said. "That's why they took her. She knew, Ulyana. She was on the original study. Whatever's on that compass, she carried it into hiding and never used it, because using it would have cost—" They gestured at themselves, at the whole architecture of a sacrificed life. "*This*. Everything it cost me, it cost her first, and she paid it for twenty years to protect people who'll never know her name."

"Then we'll know it for them," Ulyana said simply.

Above them the pass stood open, a blade of gray sky between the peaks, and the wind had begun to rise, lifting fine snow off the ridgelines in long white banners that streamed east like smoke. Xanthe studied those banners with a ranger's eye and did not like what they read. The air had that pressed-flat quality, that held-breath quality. Somewhere over the western ranges, more weather was gathering, and it was coming early, and it was coming fast.

They climbed.

By late afternoon they'd gained the col below the pass itself, a saddle of naked rock between two walls of black stone, and there Xanthe called the halt—not from mercy but from arithmetic. Crossing the final pitch in failing light, on loaded snow, with storm signs building, was how rangers died and were found in spring. They made camp in the lee of a boulder the size of a chapel, melted snow, ate half-rations, and watched the light die out of the sky in bands of copper and bruise-purple while the temperature fell like a stone down a well.

Ulyana had the compass out. She always had it out now, in the evenings, turning it in the firelight, tracing the engraving with a fingertip—the fine etched lines along the case's inner rim that neither of them could fully read yet, the key that matched coordinates in a dead man's notes. Her brother's notes. Tonight her thumb moved over the metal slower than usual, and Xanthe realized she was talking to it, or to him, her lips moving without sound.

"It'll work," Xanthe said. "The decoding. At the survey coordinates. We'll read it."

"I know." She didn't look up. "I'm not worried about the compass."

"What, then?"

She raised her head. Firelight moved in her eyes. "Tomorrow we cross, and tomorrow night we're on the far side, and everything changes. Whatever we are to each other up here—" She stopped, jaw tightening, annoyed at herself for saying even that much. "Up here, the mountain decides things. Down there, people do. I wanted one more night where it's just us and the weather."

Xanthe looked at her for a long time. The stove hissed. Outside, the wind had begun to sing against the boulder in a low, rising note that Xanthe's bones recognized before their mind did.

"Ulyana," they said quietly. "Listen."

She listened. The singing grew. And then, from the west, over the shoulders of the peaks, the clouds came—not drifting but pouring, a white flood spilling across the stars, and within minutes the first flakes were ticking against the tent fly, soft as fingertips, then harder, then not stopping at all.

The second snowfall. The one that wouldn't lift for days. The one that would fill the pass behind them and seal it shut.

There would be no retreat now. There was only forward, down into the valley, toward Kettlebrook and the truth and whatever the Ministry sent next—and as Xanthe lay awake in the dark listening to the storm bury the world, one thought kept returning, calm and cold as the snow itself:

Devereux had sounded sorry. And men like Devereux were only ever sorry when the order had already been given.
