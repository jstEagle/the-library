## 10. The Long Descent

The mountain let them go grudgingly.

Ulyana felt it in her knees first—the Ashfall Range did not release climbers so much as surrender them, foot by foot, down switchbacks of broken scree that shifted and hissed under their boots like something alive and annoyed at being woken. Three days above the snowline had left their world reduced to white and wind and the small warm country of a two-person tent. Now, as they dropped below the treeline, the world came back in pieces: the smell of pine resin, the chatter of meltwater, a bird she couldn't name scolding them from a dead snag. Her body catalogued each one like a woman returning from a long illness.

Xanthe walked ahead, as always, reading the slope the way Ulyana read a locked room—every stone a sentence, every broken twig a clause. They hadn't spoken much since the snow. Since the radio. Since Devereux's voice had come through the static like a knife wrapped in velvet: *Return the compass. Abandon the investigation. Or forfeit the protection.*

Ulyana had watched Xanthe's face while the words landed, and what she'd seen there had frightened her more than the storm had. Not fear. Something older and sadder. The look of a person discovering that the leash they'd worn so long they'd stopped feeling it still had a choke at the end of it.

"Water," Xanthe said now, without turning, and crouched at the stream they'd been paralleling since midmorning. Ulyana crouched beside them, filled the flask, and drank. The water was snowmelt—cold enough to ache behind her eyes, clean enough to taste of nothing at all. She thought, not for the first time, that her brother Tam would have loved this country. He'd loved anything that asked something of him.

She was thinking about Tam when she saw the smoke.

Not chimney smoke. Chimney smoke rose lazy and domestic. This rose in a thin, disciplined column from a fold of the valley below, straight as a plumb line, the kind of smoke made by people who did not want to be seen and had calculated exactly how visible they could afford to be.

"Xanthe." She said it quietly, and something in her voice made the ranger's head come up before the word finished.

They lay in the shadow of a granite shoulder for a long time, saying nothing, watching. Through the gap in her field glasses Ulyana counted: a compound of six canvas structures arranged with military neatness around a central fire, a wire perimeter strung between steel stakes, and—her breath went thin—figures moving inside the wire. Eight of them. Nine. Dressed in the grey-blue of Ministry field issue, moving with the aimless, diminished walk of people who had nowhere to go and knew it.

Not guards. Prisoners.

"Those are survey uniforms," Xanthe said. Their voice had gone flat, the ranger's voice, the one that gave away nothing. "Field-issue parkas. That's the Voss team's kit pattern—I saw it in the abandoned camp."

"Voss." Ulyana lowered the glasses. Her hands were not steady. "You're certain."

"The camp we found was theirs. The instruments were theirs. The half-eaten meals were theirs." Xanthe's jaw worked. "I told you what I saw. No tracks out. No bodies. Gear with no owners."

"And now the owners." Ulyana's heart was hammering against her ribs like a fist on a locked door. "Alive. They're *alive*, Xanthe. All this way I thought—after the fault data, after what we decoded, I thought the Ministry had killed them and buried the numbers with them. But they didn't. They *kept* them."

"That's not mercy." Xanthe hadn't taken the glasses back. They were watching the compound with their naked eyes, and Ulyana realized they'd already memorized it, the way they memorized everything—trail, ruin, storm, her. "That's containment. Dead men can't sign statements, but they can't be produced for an inquiry either. The Ministry doesn't kill witnesses anymore, Ulyana. It just makes sure no one ever asks them a question."

"Then we ask them one." The words came out of her before she could weigh them, hot and fast, the way her brother's words used to come out of him. "We go down there tonight. The perimeter is wire, not stone. Two guards on the gate, maybe three on rotation—I counted the watch rotations, they change every four hours, there's a gap at the changeover when—"

"*Ulyana.*"

"—when the relief is still walking and the post is empty, I've seen it done, I've *done* it, a dozen times, and Tam is down there, Xanthe, or if he isn't then the men who worked beside him are, and every one of them knows what's under this valley—"

"Ulyana." Xanthe turned from the slope and took her by both shoulders, and their hands were cold from the granite and steady as bedrock. "Stop. Breathe. Look at it again. Really look."

She wrenched free, angry, and put the glasses up because she had to prove them wrong.

And she saw it.

The wire perimeter wasn't the only perimeter. Beyond it, spaced along the treeline in a shallow arc, were four more structures she'd taken for supply caches—until she saw the glint of glass in the one on the left. Observation posts. Sight lines converging on the compound from every approach, including the one they'd have chosen. And between the compound and the valley floor, half-hidden by a rise, the shapes of vehicles. Not the boxy transport trucks of a survey outfit. The low, dark, armored silhouettes of Ministry contractors' rolling stock.

"They're not guarding the surveyors," Xanthe said quietly. "They're guarding the *approach* to the surveyors. This isn't a prison, it's bait in a box. Anyone who comes for those people walks into a kill zone with a clear conscience, because officially there's nothing here at all. Officially the team vanished. Officially anyone who finds them is a trespasser with a story nobody will live long enough to repeat."

Ulyana lowered the glasses. The compound sat in its fold of valley, smoke rising straight and disciplined, and it looked different now. Not a prison. A trap with people inside it, and her brother's name possibly among them.

"Tam would go in anyway," she said.

"I know," Xanthe said. "That's what I'm afraid of."

* * *

They made cold camp two ridges up, out of sight of the smoke, in a hollow of boulders that held the day's last heat like a cupped hand. Ulyana couldn't eat. She sat turning the brass compass over and over in her fingers, its worn case catching the firelight—no, not firelight, they had no fire, only the last grey light through the boulder gap—and she thought about Tam's hands. He'd had their mother's hands, quick and clever, always taking things apart. He'd taken the compass apart once, when they were young, before he'd understood what it was, and she'd screamed at him, and he'd put it back together perfectly and never touched it again without asking.

*It's a key, Lya,* he'd told her, the last time she saw him, in a rented room that smelled of cheap tea and printer's ink. *I know it's a key. I just haven't found the lock yet.*

He'd found the lock. He'd found it in the Ashfall survey data, and then he'd vanished, and for two years she'd believed the mountain had taken him, because that was the kind lie, the survivable lie. The mountain takes people. The mountain is indifferent. The mountain is nobody's fault.

The mountain, it turned out, had been the Ministry all along.

"They're alive," she said to the dark. "Eight or nine of them. That's not a rounding error, Xanthe. That's eight or nine people who can stand in front of an inquiry and say *here is what we found, here is what we were ordered to bury, here are the projected casualty figures for Kettlebrook.*"

"Or eight or nine people who can be disappeared permanently the moment anyone makes a move," Xanthe said. They were sitting across the hollow, knees drawn up, and in the thin light their face was all planes and shadow. "You're not hearing me. If we go down there and it goes wrong—and it will go wrong, because that's what kill zones are *for*—then the Ministry gets everything it wants. The witnesses die in an accident. The thieves die in the same accident. The data stays buried, the fault stays secret, and Kettlebrook keeps sleeping under a loaded spring, and nobody ever knows enough to be afraid."

"Then we don't let it go wrong."

"You can't guarantee that. Nobody can guarantee that." Xanthe's voice didn't rise. It never rose. It just got lower and more precise, each word set down like a stone in a wall. "I've spent eleven years learning how the Ministry handles problems. I've been one of the tools they handle them *with*. They don't lose fights like this, Ulyana. They don't even have fights like this. They have cleanups."

"Then what's your answer?" She was on her feet without remembering standing. "We walk away? We take the data to Kettlebrook and warn them, and when the Ministry asks how the town found out, the trail leads straight back to eight people in a wire cage who get a bullet and a shallow grave? You want to buy the town's warning with their lives and call it *consequence*?"

"I want you to not die on a slope I chose for you!" It cracked out of them before they could stop it, loud in the hollow, and then the silence after it was enormous. Xanthe never raised their voice. In all the miles, all the storms, all the fights, they had never raised their voice, and now they sat in the wreckage of it looking almost as surprised as she was.

Ulyana sat back down slowly.

"That's not the whole reason," Xanthe said, quieter. "But it's a reason. You should know it's a reason."

"I know it's a reason." Her throat was tight. "It's a good one. It's not the only one."

"No. It's not." Xanthe looked at the compass in her hands for a long moment. "Every bridge we have left burns if we do this. Devereux. The license. The debt. Whatever protection your mother—" They stopped. Ulyana watched them stop, watched them choose not to finish the sentence, watched the sentence go down inside them like a stone into deep water. "My mother's safety is the collateral on everything I am. If I help you hit that compound, Devereux doesn't send radio messages anymore. Devereux sends people."

"Then don't help me."

The words came out before she could soften them, and she didn't try to take them back. That was the thing about her that Tam had always said would get her killed: she couldn't lie to save a moment, couldn't smooth a thing over, couldn't let a hard truth sit unspoken just because it hurt. Xanthe had spent eleven years learning to disappear. Ulyana had spent two years learning that disappearing was what the Ministry wanted from everyone.

"You could walk down to Kettlebrook tomorrow," she said. "You could report the compound exactly like a good ranger. You could hand over the compass and the data and the thief who led you to both, and maybe Devereux keeps their word, and maybe your mother walks free, and maybe—" Her voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through. "Maybe that's the right call. I'd even understand it. But I'm going down there, Xanthe. Tonight or tomorrow or the night after, when I've found the gap in their rotation, I'm going down there, because my brother spent his last two years trying to get that data out, and those people in the wire are the only living proof of what the Ministry did, and I did not cross the Ashfall Range in the first snows to stand on a ridge and do the math on who's worth saving."

The hollow was very quiet. Somewhere below, the stream went on talking to itself.

"You've already found the gap in the rotation," Xanthe said at last.

Ulyana blinked. "What?"

"You said *when I've found the gap.* Past tense construction. You've been counting their watch changes since the ridge." Xanthe's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "You've had the plan since before I took your shoulders. You were just waiting to see if I'd say yes."

Ulyana looked down at the compass. The needle inside it, freed of its north, swung slowly and settled on nothing—on the hollow, on her, on the man across from her. "I was waiting to see if you'd say yes," she admitted. "Because I don't know this country. Because the contractors' patrol patterns mean nothing to me and everything to you. Because—" She stopped, and it was her turn to choose not to finish a sentence, and she chose wrong, and finished it anyway. "Because I don't want to do it without you."

The silence stretched. Ulyana counted her own heartbeats in it, the way Tam had taught her to count seconds between lightning and thunder, and thought: *that's how close it is. That's how close the strike is.*

"Here's what burning every bridge actually means," Xanthe said finally, in the low precise voice, the stone-by-stone voice, and Ulyana understood with a lurch that they had started building. "It means no radio contact with Devereux from this moment. It means we do this knowing the Ministry will name us traitors by morning, and there will be no license to lose because we'll already be past it. It means if we get those people out, they can't go to any Ministry office, any regional station, any authority in the province, because every one of them belongs to the people we're robbing. We don't free them *to* anything, Ulyana. We free them *from* something, and then the mountain and the snow and the truth are all they've got. Same as us."

"Same as us," she echoed.

"And it means Kettlebrook." Xanthe's eyes came up and held hers. "The whole reason the Ministry buried this is that relocation costs more than the funerals will. If we pull the surveyors out and the data with them, and the town still doesn't move, then we've spent everything for nothing. The descent doesn't end at that compound. It ends in Kettlebrook, in front of people who won't want to hear it, and we have to make them hear it. That's the harder climb. That's the one I don't know how to do."

Ulyana turned the compass over one last time and closed her hand around it, the brass warm now from her grip, her brother's key, her mother's—no, *Xanthe's* mother's, the woman who had refused to bury this same data twenty years ago and been erased for it, the whole terrible inheritance of it sitting in her palm like a coal that refused to go out.

"Then we'll learn," she said. "Together. The way we learned the range."

Xanthe looked at her for a long moment. Then they reached across the hollow, took the stick of charcoal from the survey kit, and began to draw on the flat face of a boulder: the compound, the wire, the treeline, the vehicles, the arc of the observation posts, all of it from memory, exact as scripture. Their hands moved without hesitation.

"Changeover's at the fourth hour after dark," Ulyana said, crouching beside them. "There's ninety seconds when the gate post walks the wire to meet the relief and no one's watching the gate itself."

"Ninety seconds." Xanthe marked it. "I've gotten through worse with less."

"I know," Ulyana said. "I've watched you."

Xanthe glanced at her then—quick, startled, caught—and looked back at the boulder too fast. Ulyana hid her own smile in the shadow of the charcoal work and did not say anything else, because some things, once said, couldn't be unsaid, and there would be time. There had to be time. First the wire. First the people in the grey-blue coats. First her brother's colleagues, his proof, his unfinished work.

Below them, in its fold of the valley, the compound's disciplined smoke rose straight into the darkening sky, and somewhere inside the wire a man was pacing a perimeter he couldn't leave, carrying the truth in his head like a stone he couldn't put down.

"Hold on, Tam," she whispered. "We're coming down."
