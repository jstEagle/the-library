## 11. Kettlebrook

The valley opened below us like a held breath.

We had come down out of the high country through two days of scree and switchback, the snowline chasing our heels, and when the trees finally thickened into true forest and the ground gentled, I felt the particular relief a ranger feels at lower altitude—the air thickening, the world becoming generous again. Ulyana walked ahead of me on the trail, her brother's letter folded against her heart in an inner pocket she touched every hour or so, the way a person touches a wound to confirm it still hurts. She hadn't told me what was in it. She didn't have to. I had watched her read it by firelight, watched her face do the thing faces do when grief arrives in a language they almost understand, and I knew it confirmed everything: the fault, the survey team, the coordinates that matched the engraving inside my mother's compass like a key cut for a lock no one was meant to open.

Kettlebrook announced itself before we saw it—woodsmoke first, then the sound of water being put to work, then the smell of bread and tanneries and livestock that every town carries like a signature. We came down the last mile through pastureland where cattle stood hip-deep in fog, and the fog burned off as we walked, slow and theatrical, until the town lay bare in morning light: two hundred rooftops along a river bend, a mill wheel turning with the patience of something that had been turning for a hundred years and intended to turn for a hundred more.

I stopped at the edge of the last field and looked at it the way I look at everything now—with the fault data folded in my jacket, the numbers my mother may have died refusing to bury.

"They don't know," Ulyana said beside me. Not a question. She'd been quiet since the pass, since the seismic readings, since we'd sat on cold stone doing arithmetic that ended in projected casualties rounded to the nearest hundred.

"No," I said.

"Two hundred rooftops." She said it flatly, but I heard the arithmetic running underneath. "Mill families. River families. How long has this town been here?"

I didn't answer. I didn't need to. The church spire answered, and the cemetery behind it with its leaning stones gone soft with moss, and the orchards that only make sense when someone's grandmother planted them.

We walked in.

* * *

Towns like Kettlebrook are easy to enter if you know how. Rangers are trained for it—not infiltration exactly, but a kind of studied unremarkability. You wear your license openly enough to be trusted and quietly enough to be ignored. You ask questions that sound like weather talk. You let people tell you things because people are desperate to tell things to anyone who might carry them somewhere.

Ulyana, I was learning, was better at this than I was. She didn't even try. She simply became interested—genuinely, warmly interested—in whatever was in front of her, and interest is a currency that opens more doors than authority ever will.

The miller's wife fed us breakfast because Ulyana admired her garden. The ferryman told us about the river levels because Ulyana asked whether the spring melt ever flooded the low quarter, and he talked for twenty minutes about the flood of his grandfather's day, how the whole east bank had gone under, how they'd rebuilt, how you rebuild when the river is your livelihood. A boy selling apples outside the general store adopted us for an hour and gave us the complete genealogy of Kettlebrook in the breathless manner of children who have memorized their town the way other children memorize constellations: whose grandfather built the mill, whose family had farmed the north fields "since forever," which house was oldest, which family had lost sons to the war and sons to the mountain both.

"The mountain takes people sometimes," the boy said, matter-of-fact, handing me an apple I hadn't bought. "Old Man Corren says the range is restless. He says you can hear it grumble some nights, down by the hot springs."

My hands went still around the apple.

"Grumble," Ulyana repeated, casual as anything. "Like thunder?"

"Like the earth clearing its throat." The boy shrugged, delighted with the phrase, clearly borrowed from Old Man Corren. "Nobody minds much. It's been doing it since my granddad's time."

Since the original survey, I thought. Since my mother stood on this stone and measured what it was saying and wrote it down and refused to bury it.

I looked at the boy—at his chapped cheeks and his apple crate and his absolute, untroubled belonging to this place—and something in my chest began to ache in a way I recognized from the ruin, from the cold camp, from every moment since the survey camp where instruments ran for owners who would never come back. It was the ache of watching someone stand on ground that powerful men had already decided was expendable, and not know it, and hand me an apple.

"Where does Old Man Corren live?" I asked.

* * *

He lived in a cabin above the hot springs, up a trail steaming with sulfur breath, surrounded by stacks of split wood arranged with the precision of a man who had made an enemy of idleness. He was eighty if he was a day, built like the stump of something that had once been much larger, and he looked at us over the top of his spectacles with the unhurried suspicion of a man who had outlived everyone entitled to lie to him.

"Ranger," he said, reading my license before I offered it. "Haven't seen one of you up here since—" He stopped. Recalculated. "In a while."

"How long is a while?" Ulyana asked.

"Thirty years." He said it easily, but his eyes had changed. "Last one came through asking about the springs. Woman. Measured everything twice. Said the water table was shifting in ways she didn't like." He took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, slowly. "She stayed for supper. Played cards with my wife, rest her soul. Then she went back over the pass and I never heard of her again."

The world narrowed to the size of that sentence.

"What was her name?" My voice came out strange. Distant. Like someone else's.

Corren looked at me for a long moment, and I understood that he had already guessed—that rangers' faces are not so different from other faces, and mine was doing something unmistakable.

"Voss," he said. "No. That's not right. Vane. Vane, I think. First name started with an M. Mara? Maren?" He shook his head. "It's been thirty years, ranger. Names go. The measuring doesn't. She told me the mountain was going to shrug someday, and that the people downstream should be told, and that she was going to make sure they were told." He set his spectacles back on his nose. "I asked her why she cared, a Ministry woman caring about a valley like this. And she said—" he paused, and I could see him reaching for the exact words across three decades, and finding them, because some words a man keeps the way he keeps a scar— "she said, 'Because somebody has to be the one who isn't invisible.'"

* * *

I walked down from the hot springs without speaking. Ulyana let me. She had learned, somewhere on the mountain, when to walk beside a person and say nothing, and I registered dimly that this was its own kind of intimacy—the silence of someone who knows your grief well enough not to interrupt it.

Maren. Maren Vane. My mother's name spoken by a stranger in a voice worn smooth with age, attached to a supper and a card game and a prophecy, and then to nothing. To a gap. To the same gap I had spent my whole life circling, telling myself the Ministry had taken her for debts, for politics, for reasons that had nothing to do with a valley I had never heard of until this month.

She had come here. She had sat where I was sitting. She had looked down at these two hundred rooftops and tried to save them, and the Ministry had erased her for it, and then the Ministry had erased the erasing, and then the Ministry had handed her child a leash and called it a license and sent them out into the world to be useful and unseen.

Invisible. Somebody has to be the one who isn't invisible.

That had been her creed, or her rebellion, or her joke—I couldn't tell which, and maybe she couldn't either. But I had built my entire life on its opposite. I had made invisibility a discipline. I had sanded myself down year by year, name by name, debt payment by debt payment, until there was almost nothing left to collect on. I had thought it was survival. Standing in the market square of Kettlebrook, watching the miller's wife laugh with her neighbor over a fence, watching the apple boy weigh fruit for a customer with the gravity of a man performing sacrament, I understood it had been surrender. Worse—it had been complicity. Every year I kept my head down, the ledger stayed balanced, and the ledger's balance required that places like this stay unwarned, unmapped, unmentioned. My silence wasn't neutral. It was a payment. I had been paying my mother's ransom with their lives, installment by installment, without ever seeing the account.

"Xanthe." Ulyana's hand on my arm. Light. "You've stopped walking."

"I'm fine."

"You're not."

I looked at her. Her face was drawn from the pass, shadowed under the eyes, and she was holding herself carefully, the way people hold themselves when they're carrying something breakable—and I realized she was afraid, not of the contractors we knew were somewhere behind us, not of Devereux, but of me. Of what I might do. Of what the collapse of a lifetime's faith does to a person standing in the middle of the thing that faith was sold to protect.

"Say it," she said quietly. "Whatever it is. Say it out loud. It's worse in the dark."

So I said it, right there in the market square, between the apple cart and the fountain, my voice low and shaking and utterly certain:

"They're going to bury this town the way they buried her. And I helped. Every year I didn't ask, I helped. I was the best kind of asset they had—the kind that never looks up." I swallowed. "She crossed this range to warn them, Ulyana. Thirty years ago. And I crossed it to chase a thief over a compass, and I told myself that was all it was. Theft. Debt. Clean categories." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "There are no clean categories. There's just the mountain, and the people on it, and whoever decides they're worth writing down."

Ulyana didn't flinch. She stepped closer, close enough that I could see the exhaustion and the iron underneath it, the same combination I'd first read in her tracks in the scrublands and misread entirely.

"Then stop being what they made," she said. "You're a ranger. You can read sign nobody else can read. So read this." She gestured—at the rooftops, the mill, the church spire, the cemetery with its soft stones. "Read the town. Tell me what you see."

And I did. Because it was the only prayer I had left.

I saw foundations set on alluvial soil that would liquefy when the fault moved. I saw the mill race and the ferry landing and the low quarter the ferryman had described, all of it inside the zone the survey data had shaded red. I saw the school, small and new-roofed, someone's pride. I saw the orchards, decades deep. I saw cemeteries full of people who had chosen this valley on purpose, generation after generation, the way my mother had chosen truth on purpose, and been erased for it.

I saw a community that had never been given the one thing every one of them would have needed to choose for themselves: the truth.

"They haven't been warned," I said. "Not by the survey. Not by the Ministry. Not by anyone." I turned to Ulyana, and the last of the ranger's studied calm went out of me like heat off stone. "Devereux will come. You know that. The moment the team escaped with copies, the clock started. Contractors first, then worse. They won't leave witnesses to a broadcast, and they won't leave a town that might believe one."

"Then we warn them ourselves," Ulyana said. "Tonight. Before the contractors arrive. Publicly. In a way that can't be unsaid."

"It'll cost us everything. My license. Your freedom. Maybe more."

Ulyana smiled, and there was no humor in it, only a fierce and terrible clarity.

"Xanthe. My brother died for these numbers. Your mother vanished for them. What exactly do we have left that they can take?"

I opened my mouth to answer—and stopped. Because across the square, past the fountain, a government vehicle had just rolled to a halt at the edge of town, grey and unmarked, and a man was stepping out of it who moved with the unhurried certainty of someone who owned every road he drove.

Devereux.

He saw me across the distance, and raised one hand—not a wave. An invitation.

"He's early," Ulyana breathed.

"No," I said, watching my handler walk toward us through the market crowd, townsfolk parting around him without knowing why. "He's right on time. He came to buy the town."
