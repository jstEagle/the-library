## 13. What Devereux Knows

The rain came to Kettlebrook two days before Devereux did, and Xanthe knew it was him before the knock landed, the way you know a storm is coming by the way the horses go quiet.

They were staying in the back room of the tannery, because Ulyana had a rule about never sleeping anywhere with a front door facing the street. The room smelled of lye and wet wool and the river beyond the walls, that low mineral breath the Kettlebrook water carried even into town. Xanthe had spent the morning cleaning their wound again—the contractor's blade had opened a shallow furrow along their ribs at the ford, ugly but honest—and listening to Ulyana argue with a freed survey technician named Pell about transmission frequencies through the thin plank wall. Their voices rose and fell like weather. It was almost domestic. That was what made it unbearable.

The knock came at four in the afternoon, when the light had gone the color of old tin.

Three knocks. Evenly spaced. No urgency in them at all.

Xanthe was on their feet before the third one finished, hand finding the knife under the cot out of pure reflex, and then they made themselves stop, made themselves breathe, because they already knew. You didn't knock three times evenly if you were Ministry enforcement. Enforcement came through doors. This was something else. This was courtesy, and courtesy from the Ministry was always more expensive than force.

"Through there," Xanthe said quietly, jerking their chin at the connecting door where Ulyana had already appeared, silent as spilling water, Pell's frequency charts still in her hand. "Back window. Go."

"No," Ulyana said.

"It's my handler. My debt. This is mine—"

"And she's here for both of us." Ulyana set the charts down on the tannery bench with terrible calm. "Whatever she's selling, we buy it together or not at all. That was the deal at the ford. You held the river; I held my end." Her eyes were steady, dark, unblinking. "You don't get to go back to standing alone. I won't allow it."

There was no time to argue, which was fortunate, because Xanthe wasn't sure they could have. They nodded once, and opened the door.

* * *

Devereux stood in the tannery yard under a grey umbrella she did not need, because the rain had thinned to a drifting mist that beaded on her coat without seeming to touch her. She looked exactly as she always looked: silver-streaked hair pinned with bureaucratic precision, a long charcoal coat, hands folded, face arranged into an expression of professional regret that she could have worn to a funeral or a promotion and no one would have known the difference. Behind her, at a respectful distance, waited a single government skiff on the road, engine idling, one driver visible. One driver. She wanted that counted. Everything about her posture said: *I came alone. I came to talk.*

"You're bleeding," Devereux said, by way of greeting. Her eyes flicked to Xanthe's side, where the bandage showed through the torn shirt. "Ribs?"

"Ribs," Xanthe agreed.

"I taught you better field dressing than that."

"You taught me field dressing for wounds I was supposed to survive."

Something moved behind Devereux's eyes—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. She turned her gaze to Ulyana, and Xanthe watched her do the arithmetic: the thief, the compass, the data, all of it assembled into a single assessment. Twenty years of handler's training, weighing assets and liabilities in the space of a breath.

"Ulyana Voss," Devereux said. "Your brother was a good surveyor. Better than his file suggests."

"Don't," Ulyana said softly. "Don't you say his name like you knew him."

"I read his file every quarter for six years. In this work, that's as close to knowing someone as most of us get." Devereux folded her umbrella, shook the mist from it, and leaned it against the wall with the care of a woman who intended to stay a while. "May I come inside? What I have to say shouldn't be said in a yard."

"There's nothing you can offer that we want," Xanthe said.

"That's true," Devereux agreed pleasantly. "And yet I'm going to offer it anyway, because the alternative is that I leave, and forty-eight hours later this town stops being a town. So." She gestured at the door. "Inside, Ranger. Or whatever you are now. Inside."

* * *

They sat in the tannery office, a cramped room with one window fogged with steam from the curing vats, and Devereux took the only chair while Xanthe and Ulyana remained standing, shoulder to shoulder, which neither of them had planned and neither of them moved to correct.

Devereux produced a folder. Not a tablet, not a datachit—a paper folder, thick, tied with cord, the kind of thing that existed outside networks, outside broadcasts, outside the reach of anything Ulyana might transmit. Xanthe understood the gesture immediately and hated how good it was. Paper couldn't be jammed. Paper couldn't be copied across twelve regional stations in ninety seconds. Paper was silence you could hold in your hand.

"The survey data," Devereux began. "All of it. The original fault readings, your mother's—" she inclined her head fractionally toward Xanthe, "—seismic work from thirty years ago, the Ashfall deep-core samples, everything the team collected before their detention. Copies exist, yes. We know about the broadcast attempt. We know about the technicians you freed. None of that matters, because none of it is verified. Unverified data is rumor, Ranger. Rumor fades. What matters is the originals, and the originals are in this folder, and this folder can be yours."

"To what end?" Ulyana asked.

"To burn it." Devereux laid the folder on the desk between them, square and patient as a headstone. "You return the compass. You surrender any remaining copies. You sign a silence covenant—not a gag order, those leak; a covenant, which is voluntary, witnessed, and legally clean. And in exchange—"

"My mother walks free," Xanthe said.

"Your mother walks free." Devereux let the words sit there. "Full pardon. Custody transferred to family. A house, a stipend, medical care—the works. She'd be released within the month. You could collect her yourself. I'd drive you to the facility personally."

The room was very quiet. Through the walls came the smell of the curing vats, the distant sound of the river, Ulyana breathing beside Xanthe—slow, controlled, the breathing of someone holding themselves very carefully in place.

It was everything. That was the obscene thing about it. It was everything Xanthe had wanted since they were nineteen years old and first understood what the debt meant: their mother, alive, home, the leash cut, the quiet life finally permitted. Thirty years of erasing themselves—no name on publications, no face in records, a ranger license held hostage year over year—and here it all was, offered back in a paper folder by a woman who had held the other end of the leash the whole time.

And it required burying Kettlebrook.

It required standing in the valley where Xanthe had walked yesterday past a schoolyard—past children pumping swings, past a woman hanging wash that flapped like surrender flags—and knowing the ground under all of it was a held breath, a fault the Ministry had measured and modeled and costed, and decided that relocation was more expensive than funerals.

"They'll die," Xanthe said. "When it slips. You know the projections. You've seen them. How many?"

Devereux's silence stretched—one second, two, three—and then she said, with terrible gentleness: "The models have wide error bars."

"How many, Devereux."

"Four hundred. Possibly eleven hundred. Depending on the season and the hour." She said it the way she reported weather. "Which is precisely why the number has never been allowed to become official. An official number requires an official response. An official response costs more than the valley is assessed at. That's the arithmetic, Xanthe. I didn't write it. I've just been carrying it longer than you've been alive."

"You could have put it down," Ulyana said. "Any time. You could have been the one who carried it up the mountain instead of down."

"Yes," Devereux said simply. "I could have. I'm offering you the chance to make the same choice I made, so that you can understand it from the inside. Most people take the folder, Voss. Almost everyone takes the folder. The ones who don't—" she paused, "—the ones who don't usually tell themselves it's because they're braver. It's rarely bravery. It's usually that they have less to lose. Your brother had less to lose. He chose the truth, and the truth got him a file I read every quarter."

Ulyana went white, and Xanthe stepped half in front of her—not consciously, just the body moving ahead of the mind—and Devereux watched them do it, and something in her face shifted, some ledger closing.

"There it is," she said quietly. "Twenty years I've wondered what would happen if you ever found something worth standing in front of. Now I know." She tapped the folder once. "You have until dawn. The skiff leaves at first light, with or without you. If you're on it, your mother lives free and this valley keeps its secret and everyone gets to keep living the life they were living—which, I'd remind you, they were doing happily, unaware, for thirty years before either of you came down the mountain. If you're not on it, I transmit the detention order at dawn, and contractors move on this town by noon, and everything after that happens whether you broadcast or not. The broadcast won't save them, Xanthe. It will only make certain the people who buried them are punished afterward. Ask yourself honestly whether the dead care who's punished."

She rose, settled her coat, retrieved her umbrella. At the door she stopped, her back to them, and for a moment the professional regret slipped and something older showed through—weariness, deep as strata.

"For what it's worth," she said, "I hoped you'd take it. Not for the Ministry's sake."

Then she was gone, and the mist took her, and the skiff's engine faded down the road toward the low country.

* * *

Night came down on the tannery. Ulyana sat on the cot turning the compass over in her hands—Xanthe's mother's compass, brass worn bright at the pivot where thirty years of thumbs had rested—and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the folder on the desk hadn't already said louder.

Xanthe stood at the fogged window and watched the lights of Kettlebrook come on one by one across the valley. A bakery. A chapel. The schoolhouse, dark now, its yard empty of swings. Four hundred. Possibly eleven hundred. Depending on the season and the hour.

"She lied about one thing," Ulyana said at last. "Maybe two."

"The error bars aren't wide. She knows the number."

"She knows the number. But that's not the lie that matters." Ulyana set the compass down and looked up, and her face in the lamplight was stripped of everything except the question. "She said she'd release your mother. Transfer custody. A house, a stipend." Her voice dropped. "Xanthe. Has your mother been *alive*—in any way that matters—in twenty years?"

Xanthe had avoided the question for two decades. Had built a career out of avoiding it, out of *someday*, out of *when the debt clears*. Every year the license renewed, and every renewal carried the same implicit promise: keep serving, keep invisible, and eventually the door opens and she walks out. It was the engine of their whole life. They had never once, in twenty years, allowed themselves to look directly at the engine.

Because if you looked at it, you saw what Ulyana was seeing now.

Letters had come, early on. Formal, typed, unsigned by hand. Then fewer. Then form letters. Then, eight years ago, nothing at all—and when Xanthe had asked, Devereux had said *she's resting, she doesn't want contact, give her time*, and Xanthe had given her time, years of it, because the alternative was to ask the next question and the next question was this one.

"They kept her," Xanthe said slowly. "After the study. After she refused to bury the data. They kept her somewhere, and she—" Their voice caught on something. "A person who refuses for thirty years. Who stops writing. Who *can't* write. That's not custody. That's not even imprisonment. Imprisonment has edges. A prisoner knows the day, knows the year, counts toward something." They turned from the window. "What's left after that isn't a person waiting to walk out a door. It's—"

They couldn't finish it. Ulyana crossed the room and took their hands, and her grip was warm and hard and did not let go.

"Ask her," Ulyana said. "Not tomorrow. Tonight. Make her answer before dawn, because if you sign that covenant without asking, you'll spend the rest of your life not knowing, and that's exactly what they designed. The not-knowing is the leash, Xanthe. It always was. The debt was just the collar they hung it on."

So Xanthe walked out into the mist, down to the road, to where the skiff sat dark except for one lamp in its cabin, and Devereux was there—of course she was there, she had known they would come, she had probably timed her departure around it—and Xanthe stood in the wet gravel and asked the question they had spent twenty years not asking.

"Is she alive, Devereux? Not on a manifest. Not in a file. Alive in any way that matters. Can she speak? Does she know my name? Would she know me?" Each word came out level and cold. "Answer me. You owe me one true thing, and then I'll decide about your folder."

Devereux looked at them for a long moment. Rain beaded on her silver hair. And she did not speak. She did not say *yes*. She did not say *I'm sorry*. She opened her mouth, and closed it, and her silence filled the whole valley, and it was the fullest, most complete answer Xanthe Okafor had ever received in their life.

The leash snapped. Not with a sound—with a stillness, the stillness of a man who has carried a weight so long he has forgotten it isn't part of his body, and suddenly sets it down and feels the air move against skin that was never free before.

"Thank you," Xanthe said quietly. "That's the last honest report you'll ever get from me."

They turned and walked back up the road toward the tannery, toward the light in the window where Ulyana waited, toward the folder lying on the desk like a headstone—and behind them, in the dark skiff, Devereux sat alone with her silence, and did not follow.

Dawn was six hours away. The skiff left at first light.

Xanthe had a fire to build, copies to hide, and a town to wake.
