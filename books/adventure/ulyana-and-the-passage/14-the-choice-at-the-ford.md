## 14. The Choice at the Ford

The lamps of Kettlebrook went out one street at a time.

Ulyana watched it from the loft of the wool warehouse, chin on her folded arms, the way you'd watch a tide come in. First the mill road, then the square, then the long row of houses along the river where the windows had been lit gold all evening—families eating late suppers, arguing about harvest prices, children not yet asleep. The dark moved through the town like a hand closing fingers. Not a power failure. Power failures were messy, uneven, accompanied by shouting. This was orderly. This was scheduled.

"They're cutting the lines first," Xanthe said beside her, voice low. They'd been lying shoulder to shoulder in the dark for an hour, and Ulyana had learned to read them by sound alone: the creak of leather, the slow measured breath that never quickened no matter what was said. "Telegraph, then the road out of town. They'll want us cut off before anyone thinks to send word."

Below, in the yard, three trucks idled with their lights doused. Ulyana counted shapes moving between them—eight, ten, twelve—and the flat gray of their gear even in starlight. Ministry contractors didn't wear uniforms; they wore equipment. Same thing, if you looked at it right.

"How long do we have?"

"Before they start going door to door? An hour. Maybe less." A pause. "They'll take the surveyors first. Then the town council. Then anyone who's read what we've read."

Ulyana's brother's face rose unbidden—the way he'd looked at sixteen, hunched over the compass engraving with a jeweler's loupe, tongue between his teeth, certain the answer was one more night away. He'd been certain about a lot of things. Certainty hadn't saved him.

"Then we don't wait for their schedule," she said.

* * *

They planned it in the warehouse office by the light of a single shuttered lantern, drawing the town in spilled tea on the tabletop because neither of them dared strike a match near paper. The river made a great lazy S through Kettlebrook, and the ford sat at the narrow waist of it—the only crossing heavy vehicles could use this time of year, the water still high from autumn rains. Everything the contractors brought in or took out would pass over those stones.

"You hold the ford," Xanthe said, tapping the wet ring their cup had left. "I take the detention house."

Ulyana stared at them. "You've seen the guard rotation. Four men inside, two on the door, dogs. You can't free six detainees alone."

"I wasn't going to be alone." Xanthe's finger slid across the map to the old tannery flanking the detention house. "The Kettlebrook mill boys have been watching us since we got here. Half of them have brothers on that survey team. I talk, they act. I just need to be the one who knows which walls are load-bearing."

"And me? Alone? At the ford?"

"You're the better liar and the worse fighter," Xanthe said, without cruelty, the way they stated weather. "At the ford you won't need to fight. You'll need to make twelve men believe that crossing is the most expensive mistake of their careers. False fires. Cut guide ropes. Noise from directions that don't exist. That's thief's work, Voss. It always was."

It was, and they both knew it, and something in being *known* like that—catalogued so precisely by someone who'd spent three chapters of her life hunting her—settled strangely in Ulyana's chest. Not insult. Something warmer and more dangerous.

"And when they get past me?" she asked. "Because they will, eventually."

"Then they get past you slowly, and angry, and having lost their night advantage. Every hour you buy is an hour the surveyors spend running for the hills with copies of the data." Xanthe looked up, and the lantern light caught the scar through their eyebrow, the one Ulyana had never asked about. "Every hour is the whole plan. There's no version of tonight where we win. There's only versions where we lose slower than they expected."

Ulyana laughed despite herself—a short, sharp sound she smothered against her wrist. "You're a terrible motivator, Okafor."

"I'm an honest one. Rarer."

* * *

They broke camp in the last hour before moonrise, and it was then, shrugging into her pack by the warehouse door, that Ulyana felt it pressed into her hands.

Brass. Cold as river stone. She knew its weight the way she knew her own heartbeat—had carried it for months, had dreamed of it, had killed a man's memory of his sister over it in an argument on a mountainside. Her fingers closed around the compass before her mind caught up.

Xanthe stood with their palm still open, already turning away toward the door, as if handing over the thing their whole life had orbited were a small logistical matter, like passing ammunition.

"What is this," Ulyana said. Not a question. An accusation.

"It points where we need it to point now, not north." Xanthe kept their eyes on the dark street. "You'll need it at the survey station. The archive lock takes the key, and the key is the engraving, and the engraving—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "Your brother died decoding half of it. Your mother—" A breath. "My mother spent her last free years making sure it couldn't be buried twice. It should be your hands that finish it. Mine will be busy holding a river."

"Xanthe." Ulyana's voice came out rougher than she intended. "This is the only thing you have left of her. You told me—you told me you'd rather lose your license than—"

"I lied a lot this year." Now they did turn, and in the starlight their face was unreadable and completely legible at once, the way mountains are. "I told Devereux I'd found nothing. I told myself I was tracking a thief. I told my mother, every night for fifteen years, that staying invisible was keeping her safe." Their jaw worked. "None of it held. None of it was ever true. So take the compass, Ulyana. Take it freely, because if you make me say it's a debt or a trade or a bargain, I'll take it back, and we don't have time for me to be proud."

Ulyana looked down at the brass in her hands. The needle swung gently behind its glass, restless, seeking, pointing nowhere and everywhere the way it always had—as if it remembered being part of something larger and couldn't settle for less.

Her brother had wanted this object decoded. Her mother—no, *their* mother, that was the terrible arithmetic of it now—had wanted it broadcast. And Xanthe Okafor, who had crossed a desert and a mountain range and their own entire identity chasing it, was giving it away in a doorway while contractors loaded trucks in the dark.

"Why?" she asked anyway. Because some questions you ask knowing the answer will cost you.

Xanthe was quiet long enough that the river filled the silence, running loud and cold under the stars.

"Because I spent my whole life holding things so tightly they turned to ash," they said. "Time to find out what happens if I open my hand."

Then they stepped off the porch and into the dark, heading for the tannery and the mill boys and the detention house, and Ulyana stood alone in the doorway with her heart hammering and a dead woman's compass warm in her grip, understanding suddenly that trust given that freely is not a gift at all.

It's a surrender. And the person surrendering has decided, in advance, that you are worth the loss.

She ran for the ford.

* * *

The river was louder than she remembered, or maybe fear had tuned her ears. Ulyana came down through the alders on the south bank and went to work with a thief's economy: three pitch-pine knots soaked in lamp oil, set on stones fifty yards apart along the bank, none of them lit yet but all of them ready. The ford's guide ropes—the thick hemp lines strung post to post so carts wouldn't drift in the current—she cut at both anchor points and re-lashed with a single whisper of cord each, so they'd look intact until weight tested them. The far approach road she seeded with every noise-maker in her kit: trip-tins, a fishhook line rigged to a bell salvaged from the chapel, a deadfall of stacked slate balanced over gravel that would sing like a rockslide if disturbed.

Then she climbed the willow at the ford's elbow and waited, compass against her sternum, watching the north road.

They came at the second hour past midnight. Twelve of them, moving in the loose professional file she'd studied from rooftops in three cities, and ahead of them the two supply trucks with canvas-covered beds that could carry anything—or anyone. They reached the ford and stopped, exactly as she'd known they would, because the lead man raised a fist and the file froze, and every one of them looked at the water the way soldiers look at a bridge that might be mined.

Ulyana struck the first fire.

The pitch knot caught with a whump that echoed off the bluffs, and in its sudden orange bloom she stood up in the willow branches, silhouetted, arms spread wide, and shouted in her best border-town accent:

"That's far enough! First man in the water dies, and I don't mean from drowning!"

A rifle snapped up. She dropped behind the trunk as a round splintered bark above her head, and lit the second fire, farther east, so that suddenly there were flames on both flanks of the ford and no way to know how many people stood behind them.

"There are thirty of us!" she called, pitching her voice off the rocks so it seemed to come from everywhere. "Ministry inspectors! This crossing is sealed under emergency order! Turn around and no one files a report!"

Lies, all lies, delivered with the flat bureaucratic confidence that made men hesitate—because everyone fears paperwork more than bullets. The lead contractor conferred with his second. Flashlights swept the banks, found fires, found shadows, found nothing solid. One of them started forward to test the guide rope.

The rope parted at a touch. The man staggered, windmilled, and went into the river to his chest with a splash and a string of curses, and the current—high, cold, snow-fed—took him ten yards downstream before his team hauled him out, and after that nobody was eager to test the stones.

Ulyana lit the third fire, west now, and began to move between her positions, firing a shot here, ringing the chapel bell there, shouting orders to imaginary squads in a voice hoarse with conviction. *Second squad, hold the east bank! Let them commit and then take the trucks!* She heard the contractors' discipline fray, heard the lead man radio for instructions and get static, because Xanthe had been right—they'd cut the telegraph first, and now it cut both ways.

An hour. She bought them an hour, and then another, moving through the willows like something the river dreamed, and when finally the lead man lost patience and ordered the file across the stones on foot, guns high, they came slow and scattered and furious, and by the time they reached the south bank the trucks were gone from the north side—

No. The trucks were still there. But the drivers were gone, and the beds stood open and empty, and somewhere upstream a freed technician named Sorrel was already pedaling the station's motorcycle toward the hills with the first copies of the data strapped to her back, and Ulyana laughed out loud in the dark, wild and breathless, because empty trucks meant the detention house had fallen, which meant—

Which meant the south-bank contractors were fanning out toward her fires with rifles and dogs, and the hour she'd bought was spent.

She ran.

* * *

She found Xanthe at dawn, or Xanthe found her—there was no longer much difference. They lay in the ruins of a shepherd's hut above the valley road, and Xanthe was gray-faced with a blood-soaked sleeve and a mill boy named Tam pressing a wad of cloth to their shoulder, and when they saw Ulyana stumble into the hut they smiled, actually smiled, crooked and exhausted and alive.

"The surveyors?"

"Gone. Six of six, scattering with copies. Sorrel's halfway to the relay station." Ulyana knelt, took the cloth from Tam's shaking hands, and pressed it herself, feeling the heat of the wound, the too-fast pulse beneath. "You're hit."

"Flesh wound. The dogs were the problem, not the men." Xanthe's eyes drifted closed, opened again. "Did it hold? The ford?"

"Long enough." Ulyana's throat tightened. She pulled the compass from her shirt, where it had ridden all night against her skin, and set it in Xanthe's unwounded hand, folding their fingers over it. "But you should keep—"

"No." Xanthe's hand pushed it back, gentle and immovable. "It's yours. I gave it. I don't take back gifts." They managed the ghost of their dry smile. "Besides. You held a river with it in your pocket. It's yours now. It chose."

Outside, the sun came up over the Ashfall Range, gilding the snowline, and down in Kettlebrook the contractors were regrouping around empty trucks while six vanished surveyors carried the truth toward every regional station in the territory—and at the old survey station, waiting under lock, powered by a battery bank that would last perhaps one day, sat the final archive.

The one the compass key would open.

Ulyana looked at the brass in her hands, then at the mountain, then at the woman bleeding quietly in the straw.

"We have to reach the station before Devereux does," she said. "And Xanthe—we have to reach it before the Ministry jams the airwaves. Whatever's in that archive, whatever your mother recorded—it has to be heard."

Xanthe's eyes, fever-bright, fixed on hers.

"Then we'd better fly," they said.
