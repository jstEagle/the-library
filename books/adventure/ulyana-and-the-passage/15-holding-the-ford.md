## 15. Holding the Ford

The river spoke first.

I'd been listening to it for an hour—lying flat on the shingle bar below the ford crossing, cheek against stones still holding the day's heat, letting the Kettlebrook's cold breath wash over me. A river is honest in a way nothing else is. It tells you when something heavy has entered the water upstream. It tells you when boots have disturbed the gravel shallows. It told me, at half past midnight, that the contractors had stopped being patient.

Three lanterns came down the east bank road, hooded, moving in the slow deliberate way of people who wanted to look like farmers and didn't. Behind them, further back where the road bent through the alder stand, I caught the dull gleam of more. Eight. Maybe ten. Devereux hadn't come herself this time. She'd sent professionals—the kind who don't argue, don't explain, and leave no paperwork behind.

I checked my watch by feel, no light. Ulyana should be at the survey station by now, cutting the detained team loose. Forty minutes, maybe fifty, before she had them moving. My job was to make those forty minutes cost the Ministry everything they had.

I'd spent the evening preparing the ford like a woman setting a table for enemies. Three fire pits along the west bank, stacked with split alder and packed with dry sage, each one soaked in lamp oil, each one rigged with a slow-match of twisted cord that would burn down to the kindling at intervals I'd judged by testing three cords against each other all afternoon. False camps. From across the water, in the dark, they'd read as sentries' fires—a garrison where there was only me.

Upstream, forty yards above the ford proper, I'd strung the first rope. Not to hold anything—to fall. The old footbridge cable, rusted half through anyway, its last anchor bolts cut to threads so that a hard yank on the tripline I'd tied to it would drop thirty feet of steel cable into the shallows with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat. Downstream, the fish weir stakes, loosened. And beneath the water itself, in the dark, the thing I was proudest of: I'd rolled three boulders out of the old channel scar and into the main current line, narrowing the deep channel so the ford's footing shifted. Anyone crossing tonight would cross blind, on ground that had moved since sundown.

All of it borrowed from twenty years of tricks used for smaller purposes—turning stock thieves, herding poachers, buying an hour here, a night there. Tonight every trick I knew was aimed at one purpose: keep these men off the station road until the truth got loose, or die making them late.

I chose it. That was the difference, and I held onto it like a handrail. For years I had done what the debt required. Tonight I did what I required. The ferocity sitting in my chest like a swallowed coal—it wasn't duty. Duty had never burned like this.

* * *

They hit the false fires first, as I'd known they would.

A whistle from the alders—two notes, low—and the lanterns went dark. Shapes spread out along the east bank with the smooth economy of men who'd done this before: not soldiers exactly, but close enough. Contractors. Ministry money bought competence, if nothing else.

Then the first slow-match reached its oil.

Fire bloomed on the west bank—not one fire but the suggestion of many, flame climbing the alder stacks and throwing our side of the river into orange relief. I watched the shapes on the far shore go still. One of them raised a hand, and I heard it carry across the water, flat and unhurried:

"West bank's held. At least four."

Four. There was one of me, and I felt something that wasn't quite laughter move through me. Let them count shadows. Every minute they spent counting was a minute Ulyana spent cutting locks.

They tested the ford anyway. Of course they did. Two men started across at the traditional crossing point—the wide gravel tongue where the water ran shin-deep—and I waited until they were committed, mid-channel, weight on the moved ground, and then I pulled the tripline.

The bridge cable came down forty yards upstream with a boom that rolled off the canyon walls like artillery. In the same instant both men went down—one into the narrowed deep channel, which now ran waist-high where it had run knee-deep, the current suddenly fast around my rearranged boulders. Shouting. Splashing. Hands hauling him out. Nobody drowned; I hadn't built it to drown anyone. I'd built it to teach them the river was mine tonight.

It worked better than I'd hoped. They pulled back to the east bank entirely. I heard a man's voice, tight with authority: "Nobody crosses without lights and ropes. Sweep the bank first."

Sweep the bank. Good luck. I was already gone, low-crawling through the willow scrub toward the downstream end of their line, leaving my fires burning behind me to hold my ghost-garrison in place.

* * *

What followed was two hours of the hardest work I've ever done, and almost none of it was fighting.

Ranger work is patience weaponized. I cut their rope cache loose and let it slide into the river. I opened their lantern box and scattered the shutters. When two of them circled upstream looking for a second crossing, I let them find the fish weir stakes—loosened, sharp, ankle-height under black water—and listened to the swearing as one went down hard. When their officer sent three men along the west bank to clear my "sentries," I moved ahead of them through terrain I've walked since I was sixteen, dragging a bundle of sage through the dry grass so that every shadow seemed to shift, every bush to breathe. They fired twice at nothing. I counted their shots. Eleven rounds gone on ghosts.

But they weren't stupid, and they had numbers, and numbers grind. By the third hour they'd found the pattern in my fires—that the sentries never moved between them, never called out, never answered a challenge. Their officer figured it out loud enough for me to hear across the water.

"There's nobody over there. It's one person. It's *one person* doing all of this."

And then he said the thing that turned my blood to ice water:

"Forget the ford. Half of you up the gorge trail to the station. Move."

The gorge trail. I'd left it unguarded because guarding two things with one body isn't possible, and I'd gambled they'd stay fixated on the ford—the obvious route, the road, the place where rivers funnel armies. But this man had stopped playing the board I'd set.

I ran.

Not along the bank—in the river, where my tracks would die, thigh-deep in current that tried to take my feet out from under me, teeth chattering, hands gone wooden, the gorge trail paralleling me on the east side maybe thirty yards up. I could hear them above me, boots on stone, six men moving fast with the confidence of people who thought the night was already won.

There's a place where the gorge narrows—the Narrows, we called it as kids, though no kid of mine ever played there. Walls close to eight feet apart, the river compressed and loud, and the trail cut into the east wall with a sheer drop to the water. Old mining country. Old mining hardware.

There was a hoist frame up there once, and part of it still stood: a timber gallows-frame leaning out over the trail, hung with the rotted remains of an ore chute and, crucially, a tangle of steel cable thick as my thumb, anchored to a boulder the size of a cart horse. I'd noted it coming down that morning and thought, idly, *that cable could stop a wagon.* Now I climbed for it in the dark, fingers finding holds by memory, and looped the cable's free end across the trail at chest height, then took the slack down to a piton I drove into the rock with the flat of my hatchet, three swings, each one loud as a gunshot in the gorge.

Loud was fine. Loud was the point.

They heard the hammering. They sped up. And when the lead man came around the bend at a jog, the cable caught him across the chest and took him off his feet entirely—into the chute debris, into the frame, and the whole rotten structure came down with him in a crash of timber and rust that blocked the trail hip-deep. The men behind him piled up. Someone screamed about his leg. Someone else started shooting blindly at the canyon walls, and rock spalled down around my hiding place like hail.

I lay in the wet scree with my heart slamming and did the arithmetic. Six men, one down, one trail blocked—but trails can be cleared, and legs can be carried, and I had bought minutes, not victory. Across the river, downstream, I saw the rest of them finally committing to the ford, lights and ropes, wading into my rearranged channel in a disciplined line.

So I did the last trick. The one I'd saved.

Above the Narrows, the river bends against a bluff of soft ash-tuff, and decades of spring floods had undercut it, leaving a shelf of overhang maybe four feet thick, propped on a pillar of the same rotten stone. I'd seen it that morning and thought what any ranger thinks: *hazard, post a warning.* Tonight I thought: *lever.*

I wedged the hatchet into the seam behind the pillar and threw my whole weight sideways, once, twice, a third time—and the pillar went, and the shelf went, and four tons of bluff dropped into the head of the ford crossing with a detonation I felt in my ribs through the ground. The river slammed against the new dam and rose, fast, brown with silt, and the crossing that had been shin-deep became thigh-deep became chest-deep while the contractors were mid-channel.

They made it out. I'll say that for them—they were professionals, and they made it out, hauling each other up the east bank coughing and furious, and no one died, because I had drawn a line in my own mind days ago and I meant to keep it: stall them, bleed their time, break their tools, but no bodies. Not tonight. Tonight the story had to be simple enough that even an inquiry couldn't muddy it. The Ministry sent armed men against a town. We sent a river.

But the river had cost me too. Somewhere in the scramble down from the bluff—I couldn't even tell you when, the whole descent is a smear of sliding rock and white water—a stone had opened my scalp above the left ear, and I'd landed badly on my right side coming off the last ledge, and something in the shoulder had torn with a hot wire-pull of pain that made my arm nearly useless. I sat in the willows afterward, shaking with cold, blood sheeting into my eye, and laughed at myself quietly, because I'd promised myself I'd hold the ford and instead I'd destroyed it. Held by ruin. Close enough.

* * *

Dawn came gray and grudging, snow-smelling, the first flakes drifting down through the gorge like ash falling upward.

From my perch on the west bluff I watched the contractors regroup on the east bank—muddy, diminished, one man limping badly, their officer standing at the ruined ford staring at the new rapids where his crossing used to be. He looked, for a long moment, like a man recalculating his entire career. Then he turned and gestured upstream, toward the long detour through the high saddle, a route that would cost them half a day in fresh snow.

Half a day. I'd promised myself I just needed to buy time. I hadn't dared ask how much.

And then the answer came walking down the station road.

First one figure, then five, then nine—moving fast, packs bouncing, no discipline to their stride because they weren't soldiers, they were surveyors, gaunt and blinking in the daylight like men released from a cellar, and among them a smaller figure in a patched coat who kept glancing back up the road, scanning the bluffs, looking for me.

Ulyana found me before they did. She always could—she'd tracked me across half a desert, after all. She came up the bluff path at a run and stopped dead at the sight of me: the blood-crusted hair, the dead arm, the way I was leaning against the rock like the rock was doing the standing.

"You're hurt."

"It's mostly river." I nodded past her, at the surveyors spreading along the road below, dividing the copied data packets among themselves, splitting into pairs, taking different routes—north ridge, valley road, the old shepherd paths. Scattering like seed. "Is that—"

"Every page. Three copies each, memorized routes, no two going the same way." She crouched beside me, and her hands were steady as she peeled back the collar of my coat to look at the shoulder, and her voice wasn't. "Xanthe. The transmission went out. All regional stations. Your mother's testimony included. It's done. It's *out*."

Below us, the last pair of surveyors vanished into the tree line. Above us, the snow thickened. Somewhere beyond the saddle, half a day away and closing, the contractors were still coming, and somewhere closer than that, Devereux would be hearing the news and deciding what she was willing to do about it.

Ulyana got her shoulder under my good arm and took my weight without asking, the way she'd taken the compass—like something freely given.

"Can you walk?"

"I held a river all night," I said. "I can walk."

We started down toward Kettlebrook together, into the snow.
