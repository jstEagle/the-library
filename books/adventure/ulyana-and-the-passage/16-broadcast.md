## 16. Broadcast

The survey station sat on a shelf of rock above Kettlebrook like a bird of prey that had landed and forgotten to take off again—concrete and corrugated steel, antenna mast leaning against the stars, windows dark except for one, where a generator coughed itself awake under the hands of a freed technician named Ilya Petrov.

Ulyana watched him work from the doorway, the compass heavy in her coat pocket. Below them, the valley was a scatter of lamplight in blackness. Somewhere down there, contractors were regrouping after the ford. Somewhere up the mountain, Xanthe was bleeding and moving and buying her this hour with their body.

"Generator's temperamental," Ilya said without looking up. "Been sitting cold for three weeks. It'll hold."

"You're sure?"

"I built half this rig." He wiped his hands on trousers that had been gray once and were now something closer to the color of surrender. Three weeks in Ministry detention had done that to a man. "They never bothered to lock the transmitter room properly. Why would they? The data's encrypted, the station's off every map, and nobody was supposed to come looking." He glanced at her, and there was something raw and amused in his face. "Then you came looking."

"Not me alone."

"No," he agreed quietly. "Not you alone."

She left him to the generator and went inside. The transmitter room smelled of dust and ozone and old coffee, a smell so ordinary it hurt. Desks pushed against walls. A chalkboard still bearing someone's handwriting—a decay curve, half-erased, abandoned mid-thought by people who had expected to come back tomorrow. She ran her fingers along the edge of the board and thought about her brother doing exactly this, in exactly this room, twenty months ago. Thinking tomorrow was a thing he'd have.

The data drive was in her inner pocket, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from her body. Every regional relay station in the basin. Every town office. Every ranger post. Ilya had spent two days in detention memorizing frequencies before the rescue at the ford gave him a chance to use them.

She set the compass on the desk beneath the single working lamp.

It looked smaller than it had in Xanthe's hands. Brass worn bright along the equator where a thumb had worried it for years—their mother's thumb, then theirs. The lid opened on the needle she'd watched all through the mountains, steady as a heartbeat, pointing not north but forward, toward whatever came next.

And beneath the needle plate, when you knew to look: the engraving. A spiral of characters fine as spider silk, the key her brother had died trying to decode, the key that had turned out to be no treasure map at all but an index—an archive reference into the survey team's own records.

Ilya appeared in the doorway, flexing his fingers. "Generator's holding. Give me ten minutes to bring the array up and we can start pushing packets." He nodded at the compass. "That's really it? The thing everyone bled for?"

"This is the thing everyone bled for."

"It's a compass."

"It's a key," Ulyana said. "And keys don't look like much until you find the door."

* * *

The array woke with a hum Ulyana felt in her teeth. Ilya worked the console with the economy of long habit, screens blooming green across the dark room, and outside the antenna began its slow sweep, ticking through the bearings like a clock counting down something.

"First transmission window in four minutes," he said. "Relay stations at Solmere, Redgate, and the ferry crossing will be listening whether anyone official wants them to or not. After that we rotate bearings every ninety seconds. Even if the Ministry jams us from Kettlebrook, they can't jam the whole basin at once—not fast enough."

"How long to push everything?"

"The seismic series? Twenty minutes if nothing drops. The full survey archive—" he hesitated, "—longer. There's more in here than the fault data, Voss. Personnel files. Communications logs. A sealed testimony file flagged restricted, dated six years back. I can't open it without the index key."

Ulyana's breath went shallow. "Six years?"

"Dated the same month the original study was buried." Ilya turned in his chair. "Whoever recorded it used the survey team's own encryption. Whoever *buried* it either didn't know it existed or couldn't crack it either." His eyes went to the compass. "Until now."

Her hands were not quite steady as she lifted the brass instrument and carried it to the console. Six years. Her brother had been decoding fragments of this for two years before they killed him—or before they erased him; she had stopped pretending those were different words. And all that time, the answer had been sitting in a dead woman's estate in Solmere, in a compass her son carried like a wound.

Xanthe's mother hadn't just refused to bury the data. She had recorded everything. And then she had hidden the only key to hearing her voice inside the one object she knew her child would never let go of.

*Oh, you clever woman,* Ulyana thought. *You knew they'd take you. You knew your kid would spend their whole life carrying this. You trusted a thief you'd never meet to steal it.*

She entered the index sequence from the engraving, character by character, while Ilya read the checksums aloud. On the third attempt the seal broke. The screen filled with a file manifest—one entry, audio, duration forty-one minutes—and a name in the metadata that stopped Ulyana's heart mid-beat.

**VESNA OKAFOR — TESTIMONY — RECORDED UNDER DURESS PROVISION 9**

"She recorded it the night before they took her," Ulyana said. Her voice sounded far away. "Provision 9. That's the custody protocol. That's the night she disappeared."

Ilya said nothing. He routed the audio to the room's old monitor speakers, and the transmission window opened, and somewhere below them in the valley a man named Devereux was almost certainly reaching for a radio.

Ulyana pressed transmit.

* * *

The voice that came out of the speakers was tired and precise and alive.

*"My name is Vesna Okafor. Surveyor second class, Ashfall Range geological study. What follows is my formal account, recorded under duress provision nine, which means the Ministry believes no one will ever hear it. They are wrong, because I have made sure of it, and if you are listening, whoever you are, then I was right too."*

Ulyana closed her eyes. Beside her, Ilya had gone very still over his console.

*"The Ashfall fault system is live. Not dormant, not theoretical—live. My team measured slip events in March, June, and August of the study year. The eastern scarp moved eleven centimeters in a single night in August. The Ministry's own models put a major event inside fifteen years. Their own models. I wrote the appendix myself."*

A pause on the recording, and when the voice resumed it was flatter, harder.

*"I was ordered to sign the burial order tonight. I was told the relocation cost for Kettlebrook and the valley settlements exceeds the projected casualty valuation. I asked who did the valuing. I was told that was above my grade. So I am saying it here, at my grade, for whoever comes after: they priced the people of this valley the way you price gravel. Tons times rate. That is what my government decided my neighbors are worth."*

*"They will tell you I resigned. They will tell you I emigrated, or defected, or died of natural causes. None of that is true. In the morning I will be taken into custody under provision nine, and my child will be told I abandoned them, and a debt will be attached to their name to keep them small and searchable and afraid. If my child is ever listening to this—I could not leave you the truth openly. They watch what a person inherits. So I left it where only someone who chose to take it would find it. Forgive me the shape of the gift. It was the only shape they left me."*

The recording ran on—coordinates, readings, names of every member of the survey team, spoken slowly and clearly, a roll call meant to survive her. Ulyana listened to her brother's name spoken in a stranger's steady voice and felt something in her chest crack open that had been sealed since the mass grave notice arrived with no body in it.

*"Last thing,"* the voice said. *"To whoever holds my compass: the needle doesn't point north. I fixed it that way on purpose. It points along the fault line, straight at the truth, because I wanted my child to spend their life walking toward it even if they hated me for it. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. But a person who has been told the truth owes the truth a voice. That debt is the only one worth collecting."*

Static. Then silence, except for the generator and the tick of the antenna sweeping its bearings, and Ulyana realized her face was wet and had been for some time.

Outside, far below, the lights of Kettlebrook flickered—once, twice—as somewhere a jamming array powered up and found, too late, that the signal had already scattered across the basin like seed on wind.

"They're trying to kill the relay at the ferry crossing," Ilya said, fingers flying. "Doesn't matter. Solmere has it. Redgate has it. Two ranger posts are logging it right now—they think it's routine traffic. By dawn it's copied past recall." He looked up at her, and his eyes were shining. "It's done, Voss. Whatever happens in the next hour—it's done."

Ulyana stood in the green light of the console, holding a dead woman's compass, listening to the dead speak at last across six silent years.

Then the stairwell door banged open below, boots on concrete, and Ilya grabbed her arm and said, "Someone's coming up," and Ulyana recognized the tread even before the voice did—uneven, favoring one side, climbing anyway.

Xanthe stepped into the doorway, bloodied, snow-dusted, upright by sheer refusal to do otherwise, and behind them, filling the frame, weapon drawn and face unreadable, came Devereux.
