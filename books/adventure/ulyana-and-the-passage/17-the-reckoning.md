## 17. The Reckoning

The transmission room smelled of dust and ozone and forty years of neglect.

Xanthe stood at the door while Ulyana worked, her hands moving over the old console with a certainty that made their chest ache. The freed technician—Marek, a surveyor with a bruised face and steady hands—had the transmitter open, its guts exposed to the lamplight. Outside, the night was still full of noise: the river at the ford, the distant shouts of surveyors scattering into the dark with copies of data they'd been imprisoned for gathering.

"Signal's clean," Marek said. "The station's old enough that the Ministry forgot it existed. Their jamming grid doesn't cover this band."

"Then they'll come here to remember," Xanthe said.

Ulyana didn't look up. "Then we'd better be fast." Her fingers found the keyhole of the archive cabinet—the brass compass fitted into a recess worn smooth by hands decades dead. Xanthe's mother's hands, maybe. The needle inside the compass trembled, then settled, pointing not north but at the cabinet, at the truth locked behind it.

The lock clicked. The cabinet swung open.

Inside: reels of tape, paper folders gone soft with age, and a single recording cylinder labeled in a hand Xanthe knew better than their own. *V. Voss — Final Testimony.* Voss. Ulyana's brother's name. But the handwriting—

"That's my mother's script," Xanthe said. Their voice came out strange, flattened, as if heard through a wall.

Ulyana looked at them then, and whatever she saw in their face made her go still. "Xanthe—"

"Play it." 

Marek threaded the cylinder. The machine hissed, popped, and then a voice filled the room—low, dry, unhurried, the voice of a woman reading coordinates to someone she trusted to write them down.

*"This is Vera Okafor, senior geodetic surveyor, Ashfall Range study, year eleven. If you're hearing this, then someone finally opened the cabinet, which means someone finally decided the mountain mattered more than the Ministry's balance sheets. Listen carefully, because I'll only say this once, and they will only let me say it once."*

Xanthe's knees went loose. They gripped the doorframe.

*"The fault is live. It runs under Kettlebrook, under the ford, under three valley towns the Ministry has classified as acceptable losses. I refused to certify the suppression order. They've taken my compass and my license and they're taking my name next. If my children ever come looking—and I hope to every stone and star that they don't—tell them this: I was never lost. I was silenced. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point."*

The recording crackled. Ulyana's hand found Xanthe's and held it, hard.

*"To whoever carries my compass: it was never a gift. It was a key. Keys are meant to be used. Stop reading sign and start reading the record. And to the Ministry man who will inevitably listen to this before destroying it—Devereux, if you're still the one they send—I knew you'd come. You were always the most honest of them. That's why they kept you on the leash too."*

The cylinder clicked off.

For a long moment, nobody breathed. Then Ulyana turned to the transmitter, her jaw set, tears cutting clean lines through the dust on her face. "Broadcast it. All of it. The fault readings, the suppression order, her testimony. Every regional station on the band."

Marek's hands were already moving. "Signal goes out in ninety seconds."

And that was when the door at the end of the corridor opened, and boots came down the hall, unhurried, deliberate, and Xanthe turned to see Devereux standing in the doorway with a sidearm drawn and an expression like a closed ledger.

* * *

Devereux had not changed in fifteen years. The same grey coat, the same grey eyes, the same careful stillness of a man who had learned long ago that movement was a form of expenditure. The pistol in his hand was pointed at the floor, which was, Xanthe understood, its own kind of message. He had not come to shoot. Not yet. He had come to negotiate.

"Step away from the console," Devereux said.

"No."

It was the first time Xanthe had ever said the word to him. Fifteen years of *yes, sir* and *understood, sir* and *of course, sir*, fifteen years of being the Ministry's quiet instrument in the empty places, and the word came out of them now small and rusty and absolutely clean.

Devereux's eyes flicked to the transmitter, to the reel turning, to the red lamp that meant *live*. He did the arithmetic faster than anyone Xanthe had ever known. His jaw tightened.

"It's already going out," Xanthe said. "Every regional station. Kettlebrook. Solmere. The valley towns. There's no jamming grid that covers this band, and even if there were, it's too late. The data's in the air. You can't recall a broadcast any more than you can recall an avalanche."

"I can recall you." Devereux's voice was level. "Your license. Your protection status. Your mother's file. Everything the debt holds. Walk away now, and I can still file this as equipment malfunction. Still keep your name out of it. Still keep her—" he paused, and for the first time in fifteen years, Xanthe saw something move behind the grey eyes, something that might have been shame—"still keep her in a place where she's warm and fed."

"She's dead, isn't she."

It wasn't a question. It had stopped being a question somewhere on the mountain, in a tent above the treeline, in the moment Xanthe had finally said it out loud to a thief in the dark. But saying it here, to Devereux's face, with their mother's voice still hanging in the room like smoke—that was different. That was the last splinter going in.

Devereux said nothing.

The silence was the answer. It had always been the answer. Xanthe felt it land in them not as a blow but as a settling, like snow finding its final resting place on a slope. Their mother had been dead for years, and the debt had been a leash attached to a ghost, and they had worn it, and obeyed it, and shrunk themselves around it, all for nothing. All for a lie the Ministry had found cheaper than the truth.

"You can't collect on a person who's already told the truth," Xanthe said.

Devereux's grip shifted on the pistol. "That's not how debt works, Quintana."

"No," Xanthe agreed. "That's not how *your* debt works. But mine's void. You held it against a woman who was silenced for refusing to bury data, and you held me against a promise to keep her safe, and she's been gone so long that even the Ministry stopped pretending. There's nothing left to collect. There never was." They took a step forward, into the space between the muzzle and the transmitter, arms loose at their sides. "So shoot, or stand down. Those are the only two things left on your ledger tonight."

"Xanthe." Ulyana's voice, low, warning.

"It's all right." They didn't take their eyes off Devereux. "He's not going to shoot. Are you, sir? Because if you shoot me, you shoot me in front of a live transmitter, in front of a witness—" they nodded at Marek, who had gone very still, very pale, and very obviously memorizing every detail—"and the broadcast becomes a murder instead of a malfunction. And you were always the most honest of them. My mother said so. She recorded it, years ago, in a room just like this one. She knew you'd come. She knew you'd listen. She knew you'd stand there with that same expression doing the same arithmetic."

Something flickered across Devereux's face—something old and tired and, beneath the exhaustion, almost relieved.

"The numbers changed," he said quietly. "An hour ago. While I was driving here. The regional stations started acknowledging receipt. Kettlebrook's mayor is on the band demanding confirmation. Two of my own contractors have gone silent, and the other three are asking me for written orders." He exhaled, long and slow, and lowered the pistol until it pointed at nothing at all. "I've spent twenty years executing orders that were written by men who never once stood on the ground they were signing away. Tonight the ground stood up." He holstered the weapon. "I'm not standing down, Quintana. I'm standing *aside*. There's a difference, and your mother would want you to know I know it."

The transmitter hissed. Marek leaned forward, hand pressed to one earpiece, and his face changed.

"Sir—ma'am—you need to hear this. It's coming through on the emergency band. It's—" he looked up, and there was something in his expression Xanthe hadn't seen in a very long time, something that in another life might have been called hope. "It's the Ministry. Central. They're issuing a formal acknowledgment of the broadcast. And they're requesting—" he swallowed—"they're requesting an immediate inquiry. Public. With witnesses."

The room was very quiet. Outside, the river ran on over the ford, indifferent and eternal, and somewhere up the valley a bird was singing before dawn, hours early, as if it too had heard.

Ulyana's hand found Xanthe's again. Neither of them spoke. There was nothing left to say that the airwaves weren't already saying for them.

Devereux stood by the door a moment longer, a grey man in a grey coat in a grey frame of a doorway, and then he reached into his pocket and set something on the console with a small, precise click.

A key. Old brass, worn smooth.

"Your mother's house in Solmere," he said. "It was never sold. It was sealed. Evidence, they called it." His eyes met Xanthe's, and for one unguarded moment they were not handler and instrument, not leash and led, but two people who had both spent too long carrying things that were never theirs. "The debt's void, Quintana. What you do with what's left is your own business now."

He walked out into the dark, and his boots went down the corridor, unhurried, deliberate, and did not come back.

Xanthe looked at the key. Then at the compass, sitting in its cradle in the cabinet, needle steady, pointing forward.

Ulyana's shoulder came to rest against theirs, warm through the cold-stiffened cloth.

"So," she said. "What now?"

Outside, the first grey light of a truth-filled dawn was coming up over the Ashfall Range, and the mountain stood silent, waiting to see what its children would do with the morning.
