## 18. After the Snows

The snow kept falling for three days after the broadcast, as if the mountain itself were trying to bury what had been said into the air.

Xanthe watched it come down from the window of a rented room above the Kettlebrook dry goods store, the glass fogged with their own breath, the valley below turning white in long slow strokes. Down at the ford, the river ran dark between banks of new snow, and along the near bank the Ministry's contractors were gone—trucks, wire, floodlights, all of it pulled out in the night like a tide going out. What they'd left behind: boot-prints filling with white, a trampled stretch of willow, and the town.

The town, waking up to find itself famous.

Xanthe's shoulder ached where the contractor's round had clipped them at the ford. Not deep—a gouge across the trapezius, ugly but honest, the kind of wound that bled a lot and meant little. The Kettlebrook midwife, a broad woman named Sera Ilo with forearms like a stevedore's, had cleaned and closed it two nights ago by lamplight while the transmission towers on the ridge crackled with the last of the data going out. She'd used more thread than strictly necessary, she said, because she'd heard what was on the broadcast and figured Xanthe would want to live long enough to see the end of it.

"You'll scar," she'd said.

"Good," Xanthe had answered, and meant it. A scar was a record. A scar was proof you'd stood somewhere.

* * *

The Ministry's response, when it came, came the way winter came—inevitable, impersonal, and from a great height.

First the denial, drafted in the flat bureaucratic language Xanthe had grown up reading on official notices: *preliminary seismic data is under review; no conclusions should be drawn from unauthorized transmissions.* Then the correction, quieter, buried on the third page of the regional bulletin: *evacuation assessments are underway for communities within the Ashfall seismic corridor, including Kettlebrook.* Then the announcement, printed large because there was no longer any way to keep it small: a formal inquiry into the conduct of the Ministry of Lands and Surveys, with a commissioner appointed from outside the region, hearings to convene in the spring.

Ulyana had read the bulletin aloud in the dry goods store's back room, standing on a crate so the whole gathering could hear, her voice carrying that hard bright edge it got when she was furious and winning at the same time. The room had been full of Kettlebrook's people—farmers, mill workers, the ferryman, old Halvard the retired surveyor who'd recognized the compass and started all of it—and when she finished, nobody cheered. That was the thing that stayed with Xanthe. Nobody cheered. They just looked at each other, the way people look when the ground under them has been named, officially, as ground that might open.

An inquiry was not an evacuation. An inquiry was a promise to *study* the thing that might kill them.

"They'll stall," Halvard said afterward, in the store's cold back room, turning his teacup in his hands. "That's what inquiries are for. Stalling. The snow melts, the water rises, and by the time the commissioner writes his first report, half this valley will have decided it's easier to believe the mountain is quiet."

"It's not quiet," Xanthe said. "I've stood on it. I've felt it."

Halvard looked at them with the weary patience of a man who had spent thirty years making maps the Ministry redrew behind his back. "I know that. You know that. The question is whether knowing it is worth more than the price of leaving."

* * *

The license came off in a letter.

Xanthe had expected a summons, a tribunal, a man in a grey coat reading charges aloud in a room with no windows. Instead it came as a single page slipped under the dry goods store's door, Ministry letterhead, Devereux's signature—Devereux, who had stood down at the survey station with a weapon in hand and orders dying in their throat, who had watched the broadcast go out and made the only choice available to someone whose superiors had already abandoned them.

*License of X. Quintana, Ranger Class Two, revoked pursuant to conduct unbecoming. Familial obligations of record are hereby discharged. No further action will be taken.*

No further action. Four words that contained Xanthe's entire life, folded up and handed back. The debt—the leash, the inherited weight, the thing that had made them erase their own name from every record they could reach, that had kept their mother's file sealed and their own movements logged for fifteen years—discharged. Void. *No further action.*

They sat on the store's back step with the letter in their hands for a long time, long enough for the snow on the step to melt in a ring around their boots. Free, then. Free the way a man is free when the rope has been cut but the rope's groove is still worn into his wrists. They had spent so long being no one—X. Quintana, initials only, a ranger who moved through the border country leaving no sign, taking no name, keeping no house—that the license had been the last official thing tying them to the world at all. Now the Ministry had taken even that, and what was left was just a person. A person with a scarred shoulder, a brass compass, and no debt.

It should have felt like falling. It felt like breathing.

* * *

Ulyana found them on the step at dusk.

She came around the side of the store with her collar up and snow in her dark hair, carrying two cups of the terrible coffee the dry goods store sold in tins, and she handed one over without asking and sat down on the step beside them, close enough that their shoulders almost touched. Below, the valley was going blue with evening. Somewhere a dog barked, and a lamp came on in a farmhouse window, and the smoke from the mill chimney rose straight up in the still air.

"Read it, then," she said. Not a question. Word traveled fast in a town this size, and the storekeeper's wife had seen the letter first.

"Revoked," Xanthe said. "Discharged. No further action."

"Hm." Ulyana blew on her coffee. "Fifteen years of your life, and they let you go with a form letter."

"They let everyone go with a form letter. That's the whole point of them." Xanthe turned the cup in their hands. "I keep waiting to feel different. Lighter. I keep waiting for the weight to lift off and it just—hasn't. I think the weight might be me."

Ulyana snorted. "You're not the weight. I've carried you across a river. You're maybe forty kilos soaking wet." She sipped. "The weight is fifteen years of holding still. That doesn't lift in a day. It lifts in inches. You'll wake up one morning in a month or a year and realize you walked somewhere without checking who was watching, and that'll be the first inch."

Xanthe looked at her. Snow was settling in her hair again and she wasn't bothering to brush it off, and the lamplight from the store window caught the fine lines at the corners of her eyes, the ones that hadn't been there at the ruin, at the cold camp, at the ford where she'd run through gunfire to put copies of her brother's data into the hands of strangers. Grief had made her hard. Purpose had made her harder. But sitting here on a cold step with terrible coffee, she looked—tired. Tired in the way of someone who had finally set something down.

"Your brother's letter," Xanthe said quietly. "You read it at the station. I only heard the last part."

Ulyana was quiet for a moment. "He wrote that if anyone was listening to this, it meant the mountain had spoken louder than the Ministry. And that he was sorry. For all of it. For the fault, for the silence, for—" her voice caught, just slightly, and she smoothed it flat, "—for leaving me the compass to figure out on my own." She turned her head. "He wasn't sorry. He did the right thing. But he was sorry I had to be the one to carry it, and I understand that now. Carrying it was the heaviest thing I've ever done, and I'd do it again."

They sat with that. The snow came down. Down in the valley, a second lamp lit, then a third.

"The inquiry," Xanthe said. "Halvard's right. They'll stall. And when the stalling fails, they'll offer money. Relocation payments, quiet settlements, a new valley somewhere with no fault under it. And some people will take it, because taking it is sane, and the town will thin out, and in twenty years Kettlebrook will be a name on an old map."

"And the ones who stay?"

"Then the mountain had better be worth staying for." Xanthe flexed their bad shoulder, felt the stitch of the stitches. "I've been thinking about what I know how to do. I can't be a ranger anymore. But I know this range better than anyone alive who isn't buried in it. I know where the ground is honest and where it lies. I know how to read a snowpack, how to read a slope, how to tell the difference between a mountain settling and a mountain *waking up*." They looked at Ulyana. "The Ministry's instruments are gone. But instruments aren't the only way to listen."

Ulyana's eyes had gone bright and sharp, the way they did when a plan was forming behind them. "You're talking about staying. Teaching. Watching."

"I'm talking about a town deciding for itself, with real information, whether its valley is worth the risk. No Ministry. No redactions. Just people reading their own ground." Xanthe paused. "It's not much. It's not a broadcast heard in six regions. It's a weather vane and a notebook and me, walking the slopes every week until my knees give out."

"It's not nothing," Ulyana said. "Nothing is what they wanted for this valley. Anything else is a fight."

* * *

The town meeting was held in the mill on the fourth day, when the snow stopped and the sun came out hard and white, and Xanthe stood at the back of it with their shoulder bound and their hands in their pockets, saying nothing, because it wasn't their valley and it wasn't their decision. It was the strangest feeling of their whole strange life: to stand in a crowd and have no official role, no license, no handler, no duty. Just a person, listening.

They argued for three hours. The ferryman wanted to take the settlement money and go to his sister's in the lowlands. The mill owner wanted to stay—the mill was three generations of his family and the river wasn't going anywhere the fault couldn't take with it. Sera Ilo stood up and said she'd delivered half the children in that room and she wasn't leaving them to grow up on Ministry rations in some relocation camp. Halvard laid out the seismic data on a plank table, his old surveyor's hands steady, and said the honest thing, the hard thing: *I don't know if it will go in our lifetime. I know it can. I know they knew, and they chose to let us sleep on top of it.*

And then a boy—fourteen, maybe, mill owner's son, the one who'd carried water to the ford during the fighting—stood up in the back and said, in a voice that cracked in the middle: "The ranger stayed. The thief stayed. Everybody with a reason to run stayed and told the truth. Why would we leave the only valley anybody ever told the truth about?"

It wasn't logic. It was fourteen years old and it cracked in the middle. But Xanthe watched it move through the room like weather, and when the vote came—stay, and fight for the valley, and put the town's name on the inquiry's witness list—it wasn't close.

Afterward, in the mill yard, in the slanting gold of late afternoon, Ulyana found Xanthe leaning against the fence with their eyes closed, exhaustion sitting on them like a second coat.

"You heard the vote," she said.

"I heard a boy," Xanthe said. "The vote was just the town agreeing with him."

"He's right, you know. About you staying." She was quiet a moment. "I'm staying too. There's nothing back east for me but a grave and a debt of my own. And somebody has to make sure the inquiry actually hears the surveyors. They're scattered now, the ones we freed—scattered and scared. Somebody has to find them and keep them brave."

Xanthe opened their eyes. The light was doing something to her face, gilding the edges of her, and their shoulder hurt, and they were so tired, and underneath the tiredness, in the place where the debt used to live, something warm had moved in and made itself at home.

"We'll need a map," Xanthe said. "Of everything. The slopes, the springs, the old slides, the ground that hums. An honest one. The kind my mother would have drawn."

Ulyana smiled—the real one, the rare one, the one that started in her eyes. "Then we'd better start before the melt. The mountain won't map itself."

"No," Xanthe said, and looked up at the Ashfall Range, white and vast and no longer lying about what it was. "But it's finally telling the truth. The least we can do is write it down."

Behind them, the mill bell rang the hour, and far up the valley, so faint it might have been imagination, the ground gave one long, low shudder—quiet, patient, and heard, this time, by everyone.
