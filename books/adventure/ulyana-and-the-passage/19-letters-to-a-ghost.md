## 19. Letters to a Ghost

The snow had stopped sometime before dawn, and when Ulyana pushed open the survey station's door, the world outside had been made new and merciless—white to the ridgelines, blue-shadowed in the hollows, so bright it hurt to look at. She stood on the threshold with her brother's satchel in both hands and let the cold find the places the fire hadn't reached.

Behind her, Xanthe was still asleep, or pretending to be. Their wound had bled through the bandage twice in the night, and she'd changed the dressing by lamplight while they bit down on a leather strap and said nothing, because that was how they both said most things now.

She walked out past the antenna mast, past the collapsed instrument shed, to the flat shelf of stone where the survey team had once set their theodolite to watch the mountain breathe. The ground beneath the snow was shallow here—scree over bedrock—and she'd already chosen the spot yesterday, when she'd come out alone with a pry bar and chipped through the frost line until her hands went numb. A grave for things that weren't a body, because there had never been a body. That was the whole cruelty of it. The Ministry took her brother the way it took everything: completely, and without leaving anything behind to bury.

Except this.

She knelt and opened the satchel.

Tomas's effects. What the contractors hadn't confiscated when they detained the team—what he'd left in her keeping years ago, before any of this, when he'd still believed the Ministry was a place you could work inside of and stay clean. A folding knife with a cracked bone handle. A tin whistle he'd played badly and loved anyway. A photograph of the two of them as children, squinting into a Kettlebrook summer, her arm slung around his neck, his grin missing a tooth. And the letter—the last one, the one that had come folded inside a technical report like contraband, written in the shorthand they'd invented as kids so their mother couldn't read their secrets over their shoulders.

She'd read it a hundred times since the pass. She had never once read it aloud.

Ulyana dug her hands into the snow and cleared the stone shelf, then laid the objects out in a row: knife, whistle, photograph. Her breath came out in silver ribbons. Somewhere below, a raven argued with the silence and lost.

"You'd hate this," she said to the mountain, to the grave, to him. "A ceremony. You always said ceremonies were just lies with better posture."

The wind moved through the antenna wires with a low harmonic tone, almost like an answer, almost like nothing.

"But I don't know how else to put you down," she said. "I've been carrying you for six years, Tomi. In my teeth. Like something I was afraid someone would take." She laughed, and it came out wrong, wet at the edges. "And then someone did. Not the way I thought. They took the *reason* I was carrying you."

That was the strange arithmetic of the last weeks—that the thief who stole a compass from a dead woman's estate had stolen back her own purpose without knowing it. That the ranger who chased her across bare stone had been carrying a ghost too, and neither of them had recognized the weight in the other until they were sharing a tent above the treeline with the temperature falling and nowhere left to hide from the truth.

She unfolded the letter. The paper had gone soft at the creases, translucent where her thumbs had worried it. His handwriting started neat and deteriorated fast, the way it always did when he was excited or afraid, and reading it now she could not tell which.

"I'm going to read it out loud," she told him. "Because you wrote it to be heard. You were too much of a coward to send it to anyone official, so you sent it to me, which means you wanted it in the air eventually. So. Air."

She began to read, and her voice carried strangely in the cold, flat and clear across the snow.

---

*"Lyu—if you're reading this, I've done the stupid thing. Don't be angry. Be angry later, after you've finished being scared.*

*"The readings are real. I need you to understand that first, because everything else depends on it. The fault under the Ashfall Range is live. Not dormant, not theoretical—it moves. It moved three times in the four months we've been up here, and each time the regional office told us our instruments were miscalibrated. They weren't. I checked them against the old study, the one from thirty years ago, the one nobody talks about. Someone mapped this fault before we did. Someone named Voss is even in the acknowledgments—not us, another Voss, can you believe it, some distant cousin maybe—and her data matches ours almost exactly. And then the file disappears. The whole study. Pulled from the archive, access restricted, the surveyor's name scrubbed from the project rolls like she never existed.*

*"I asked Devereux about it directly. He looked at me the way you look at weather coming in. Lyu, I think they knew. I think they've known for thirty years, and they ran the numbers, and the numbers said the valley wasn't worth saving.*

*"Kettlebrook has nine hundred people in it. Nine hundred. I counted the school roster myself. There are children in that town who will be exactly my age when the mountain decides, if the mountain decides soon, and the Ministry's contingency plan—the one I wasn't supposed to see—is titled 'Managed Regional Depopulation.' Managed. As if the word makes it kinder.*

*"I'm going to copy the data tonight. All of it. If I'm careful, no one will know until I'm down the mountain, and then it won't matter what they do to me because it'll be everywhere. If I'm not careful—"*

*There the handwriting broke off mid-line, and resumed smaller, tighter, pressed hard enough into the paper to tear it.*

*"If I'm not careful, find the surveyor. Find the other Voss. She refused to bury it once. Maybe her ghost will help me haunt them."*

---

Ulyana lowered the letter. Her eyes were burning, and the cold made the tears sting worse, and she let them come because there was no one to perform composure for except a brother who had been dead for six years and a woman sleeping twenty meters away who had seen her cry already, more than once, and had never once looked away.

"He found you," she said softly. "He found your name in a file that was supposed to be erased, and he wrote it down like a prayer, and then they killed him for it. Or disappeared him—I still don't know which, and I suppose now I never will, because Devereux is gone and the inquiry lawyers ask questions in the passive voice." She wiped her face with the back of her glove. "But your data got out. Both of yours. It's on every regional station from Solmere to the coast. Nine hundred people in Kettlebrook know, and the school roster is being evacuated, and the inquiry has your title page framed in its hearing room like a relic."

The wind gusted. The antenna sang.

"So rest," she said. "I mean it. Rest. I'm putting you down not because I'm done carrying you but because you're not cargo anymore. You're a name on a monument. You're chapter one of a very long reckoning. And I—" Her voice caught. "I have a life to get back to. Apparently. If I want it."

She placed the letter on the stone, weighted it with the tin whistle, covered everything with the photograph face-up so the two squinting children could watch the sky. Then she built the cairn. She'd gathered the stones yesterday, river-smoothed ones from the station's old foundation wall, and she stacked them with a mason's care, each one chosen and seated, until the small pile stood knee-high and would stand until spring and long after. Snow began to fill the gaps between the stones immediately, sealing them like mortar.

She stood there a long time. Long enough for her knees to ache, long enough for the light to shift one degree along the ridge.

When she turned back toward the station, Xanthe was standing in the doorway.

They had their arm braced against the frame, favoring the wounded side, wearing the blanket around their shoulders like a disreputable cloak, and they had clearly been there long enough to hear all of it. Ulyana felt the old reflex rise—the flinch, the deflection, the joke as armor—and set it down deliberately, the way she'd set down the satchel.

"How much did you hear?" she asked, though she knew.

"From 'you'd hate this' onward." Xanthe's voice was rough with sleep and pain. "Your brother had good instincts. Terrible operational security, but good instincts."

"He got that from me."

"No," Xanthe said. "You have excellent operational security. You just spent six years using it to hide from the wrong people." They pushed off the doorframe and came down the steps carefully, boots crunching, and stopped an arm's length from the cairn. They looked at it for a while. Then they did something Ulyana didn't expect: they pulled off one glove and pressed their bare hand flat against the top stone, the way rangers touched stone to read it, to greet it, to take its measure.

"She was in the acknowledgments," Xanthe said quietly. "My mother. Thirty years ago. Tomas found her name in a file the Ministry erased, and I spent my whole life thinking she abandoned us, and the truth was she was erased right alongside the data. Same file. Same hand." They lifted their hand from the stone. "Your brother tried to finish her work. I've been trying to outrun it. And somehow the two of us ended up in the same storm shelter arguing about a compass."

"The universe has a sense of humor."

"The universe has a filing system," Xanthe said, "and it's crueler than any Ministry."

* * *

They spent the morning doing the small work of survival, because grief and love and revolution all still required melted snow and split firewood. Ulyana chopped kindling while Xanthe, forbidden by her from lifting anything heavier than a spoon, sat by the stove and re-read the broadcast logs for the third time—confirmations ticking in from station after station, each one a small bell of proof that the truth, once loosed, ran faster than any jammer.

It was Xanthe who finally broke the comfortable silence, in the way they broke everything: sideways, obliquely, staring into the fire.

"When I was tracking you," they said, "across the scrublands. Before the storm. I kept telling myself it was about the debt. Get the compass back, satisfy Devereux, keep my license, keep the arrangement that kept my mother—keep the lie that kept my mother safe, I should say. That was the story. Clean transactional logic. Ranger retrieves property, debt service continues, invisible life continues."

"And?" Ulyana set down the axe.

"And somewhere around the third day I realized I wasn't tracking a thief. I was tracking the only person I'd met in ten years who was going somewhere on purpose." Xanthe turned to look at her, and the firelight moved in their eyes. "Everyone else in my life was either hiding or collecting. You were hunting. You had a dead brother and a broken code and a compass that wasn't yours, and you walked straight *toward* the Ashfall Range when every sane person walks away from it. I told myself it was obsession. Professional fixation. But that's not what it was, and I've had a lot of altitude and a lot of blood loss to be honest with myself, so."

"So," Ulyana said. Her heart was doing something unprofessional.

"So I need to say the thing plainly, because we've spent three weeks saying everything sideways, and my mother died—or was erased—for the crime of things left unsaid, and I refuse to inherit that particular debt." Xanthe's jaw tightened, and Ulyana realized with a jolt that the ranger was nervous. Xanthe Okafor, who had held a ford alone against twelve contractors with a rifle and a bag of false fires, was nervous. "What grew between us on that mountain. It isn't duty. It was never duty. Duty is what Devereux called it, and duty is a leash, and whatever this is, it's the opposite of a leash. It's the first thing in my life I chose when no one was making me choose it."

The stove ticked. Outside, the snowfield blazed white and silent.

"I know what it is," Ulyana said. "I've known since Cold Camp. Since you told me about your mother and then handed me the last of the tea like it was nothing, like you hadn't just given me the truest thing anyone had ever said to me." She crossed the room and sat down across from them, close, knees nearly touching. "I didn't say anything because I was waiting for it to become convenient. For the investigation to end, for the inquiry to close, for my brother to be properly buried, for some imaginary day when I'd be a person with a future instead of a person with a mission. And then I watched you hold that ford, and bleed into a frozen river, for people you'd never met, and I understood that the convenient day doesn't exist. There's only the day you're in."

"That's very wise," Xanthe said. "Painfully wise. Did you learn it from a book?"

"I learned it from a ranger who lies badly and shoots well and sleeps curled up like a question mark." Ulyana reached out and took their hand—the unwounded one—and laced her fingers through. Xanthe's grip closed around hers, careful and certain at once. "So let's say it plainly. No names for it if you don't want names. Names can wait. But plainly: I'm not leaving in the spring. I'm not taking the compass and vanishing over the pass. Whatever comes next—inquiry, evacuation, whatever the Ministry does when it stops bleeding—I want to do it standing next to you."

Xanthe exhaled, long and slow, like someone setting down a load carried so long they'd forgotten its weight. "Next to me," they repeated. "You understand I'm unemployed as of Tuesday. Stripped license. Criminal record pending. My only marketable skill is reading sign, and half the sign I know how to read is now classified."

"You'll manage. You're marrying up."

"Marrying—" Xanthe's head came up sharply, and for one unguarded second their face was entirely open, startled and lit from within, and Ulyana laughed for the first time in days, really laughed, until her ribs hurt.

"Not today," she said. "Today you're going to eat soup and change your bandage and stop looking at me like I proposed on a mountainside."

"I wasn't—"

"You absolutely were. It's fine. I'm keeping the proposal. We'll call it a promissory note." She squeezed their hand. "You can pay it forward whenever you like. I'm told that's how debts work now."

Xanthe shook their head, but they were smiling—the rare full smile, the one that rearranged their whole severe face—and they leaned over until their forehead rested against hers, and they stayed like that while the fire settled and the wind moved over the cairn outside and the mountain held its peace around the little station like a cupped hand.

"One condition," Xanthe murmured against her hair.

"Name it."

"When the melt comes. When we cross the pass together. We do it the way she meant to. Mapping. Recording. Honestly." They pulled back enough to meet her eyes. "Your brother's route and her old survey lines, overlaid. The passage documented properly, start to finish, in both our hands. So that the next person who goes looking for the truth in those files finds ours instead of ash."

Ulyana looked at them—at the exhaustion and the stubbornness and the terrible earnest hope—and thought: *Tomi, look what your ghost dragged home.*

"It's a deal," she said.

It was late afternoon when the radio crackled. They both froze—old reflexes, the sound of that frequency meaning Devereux, meaning orders, meaning the leash—but the voice that came through was unfamiliar, clipped and official and new.

*"Station Ashfall-Three, this is Inquiry Field Office. Be advised: Ministry contractor units in your sector have been ordered to stand down pending investigation. Additionally—" a pause, papers shuffling "—we have a request. The original fault study, thirty years prior, was authored under a sealed name. Records indicate the author may have family in the region. If anyone at your station has information regarding the current whereabouts of the Voss family or the Okafor family, the tribunal requests contact at earliest convenience. They have questions only surviving kin can answer."*

Ulyana stared at the radio. Then at Xanthe.

Xanthe was already reaching for their coat, wincing, eyes bright with something between fear and ferocity. "Surviving kin," they repeated slowly. "Which means somewhere in those sealed records there's a file that says whether she's alive."

Outside, the sun was dropping toward the pass, and the snow gave back its light like a promise.
