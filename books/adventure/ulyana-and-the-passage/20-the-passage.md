## 20. The Passage

The melt came all at once, the way spring does in high country—no gentle thaw but a week of warm wind off the southern plains that turned the Ashfall Range into a running wound. From the ridge above Kettlebrook we watched it happen: snowfields slumping, gullies roaring brown with last year's dust and this year's grief, the whole mountain shedding its white silence like a coat it had worn too long.

"Three days," Ulyana said, squinting at the pass. "Maybe four before the meltwater drops enough to cross the fords."

"Two," I said. "The high shelf drains east. We go up the spine and we'll hit bare stone by tomorrow noon."

She looked at me sidelong, that half-smile she'd never quite learned to aim anywhere but at trouble. "You're eager."

I was. That surprised me more than her saying it out loud. For eleven years I had moved through country like a man walking through a room he didn't own—reading it, respecting it, leaving no more mark on it than breath on glass. Eager was a thing other people got to be. Eager belonged to people whose names meant something.

But my name meant something now. It was in the inquiry filings, in the transcripts Devereux's superiors were burning through faster than they could shred them, in the mouths of Kettlebrook's millworkers who nodded to me in the street as if I'd always lived there. *Okafor.* My mother's name, which had been a debt, then a wound, then a weapon pointed at my throat—and was now, slowly, becoming just a name again. Mine.

"I'm eager," I admitted.

* * *

We left on the third morning, when the light came up gray-gold over the eastern scree and the river below town had dropped from brown to the color of strong tea. Ulyana carried the survey instruments wrapped in oilcloth—the good ones, the ones the freed technicians had rebuilt from the wreckage of the abandoned camp, calibrated against the archive data. I carried rope, rations, and the compass.

It hung at my chest on its cord, where it had hung since the night at the ford when I'd pressed it into her hands and told her to take it north while I held the water. She'd refused to keep it. She'd shoved it back at me in the survey station with blood still drying on her knuckles and said, *It was never mine to carry,* and I hadn't argued, because some arguments you win by losing.

The needle was repaired. A watchmaker in Kettlebrook—a woman named Sorrel who had lost two cousins to the fault she now knew about, and who worked on the compass for three nights without charging us—had re-magnetized the pin and trued the card until it swung clean and settled without waver. But something else had changed in it too, or in me, or in the way the two of us looked at each other across a small brass lid. When I opened it now, the needle didn't feel like an accusation. It felt like a question.

My mother had carried this through these same mountains thirty years ago. She had stood on these ridgelines with a brass instrument and a head full of readings the Ministry wanted buried, and she had chosen—knowing what it would cost—to refuse. They took her name for it. They took her life, or whatever pale administrative thing they kept instead of a life, in a facility Devereux would never name and I would probably never find. But they never got the data. They never got the truth. It crossed the pass ahead of them, encoded in engraving, sleeping in a dead woman's estate until a thief with a murdered brother cracked it open.

Inheritance is a strange current. It runs underground. You don't get to choose whether it flows through you; you only choose what you build on top of it.

Ulyana climbed ahead of me, testing holds with the economy of someone who'd grown up scrambling slate in a mining district, and I watched her and thought: *she built a key out of her brother's death. I can build a map out of my mother's.*

* * *

The pass itself opened like a held breath released.

All winter it had been a white notch between black walls, impassable, a door the mountain kept locked. Now the melt had stripped it to its bones: a broad saddle of shattered granite stitched with old snowfields, blue ice gleaming in the couloirs, meltwater threading down every crack in silver wires. The air smelled of wet stone and sun-warmed lichen and, faintly, of sulfur—the range's old name made literal, a reminder that the ground beneath us was not finished with its arguments.

We stopped at the saddle and did what we'd come to do.

Ulyana set up the theodolite on its tripod while I paced out the sightlines, and for two hours we worked in the easy wordless rhythm we'd found somewhere between the cold camp and the ford—her calling bearings, me marking stations, cairns going up at intervals like a sentence being written across the saddle. Not Ministry survey marks, stamped and proprietary. Ours. Plain stone stacks, unregistered, legible to anyone who knew how to read a range honestly.

"Bearing two-seven-one," she called. "Mark it."

I stacked the stones. "You know they'll resurvey all of this themselves. Once the inquiry finishes. They'll put their own brass pins in and call it official."

"Let them." She shielded the eyepiece with her hand, squinting. "But they'll be surveying our line. Following our cairns. Every reading they take will pass through what we wrote first." She glanced back at me. "That's how you rewrite an inheritance, ranger. You don't burn the ledger. You make yours the page they have to copy from."

I laughed—actually laughed, out loud, at four thousand meters with the wind pulling at us—and the sound went off down the eastern face and didn't come back. It occurred to me that I couldn't remember the last time I'd laughed on a ridge. Probably because there wasn't one. Eleven years of professional invisibility, and here I was making noise on open stone like a green recruit.

"Your mother surveyed this exact saddle," Ulyana said, quieter now. She'd dug the original field notes out of the archive copy, transcribed in a hand I recognized with a jolt that never quite dulled—my mother's small, slanted script, the same hand that had written my childhood letters. "Right here. Her station twelve. She logged a vertical displacement anomaly and flagged it *requires follow-up, do not close file.*"

"Do not close file," I repeated.

"They closed the file."

"We're reopening it."

She smiled and handed me the notebook. "Write your initials under hers, then. Station twelve. Let the record show somebody followed up."

I wrote them. *M.V.O.* above, *X.O.* below, thirty years apart, the ink freezing slightly in the pen before it flowed. Two names on one line of a mountain's memory. It was the smallest possible act and it undid me more than the ford had, more than the broadcast, more than watching Devereux lower his weapon with the whole world listening. Because the ford and the broadcast were done *against* the Ministry, and this was done *for* her—for the woman who'd carried a brass compass up here believing someone, someday, would finish the thought.

* * *

We camped that night on the sheltered side of the saddle, in a hollow the melt hadn't reached, and the cold came down honest and clean the way high-country cold does—not malicious, just true. Ulyana built a fire small enough to hide from the sky and we ate heated ration bread and drank tea brewed from the last of the Kettlebrook leaves, and neither of us said anything for a long time, because some silences are work too.

The stars came out hard and close. Below us to the west, far down, a smudge of light marked Kettlebrook—still there, still lit, still deciding. The town council had voted last week to stay and fight for the valley: reinforce the mills, petition for engineered evacuation routes, make the cost of abandoning them so public and so loud that no ministry could quietly write it off again. It might not work. Faults don't negotiate. But they'd chosen their fight standing up, and there was a stubbornness in that which I recognized the way you recognize family.

"Xanthe." Ulyana's voice, low, across the fire. "What happens after? To you. To us."

I fed a stick to the flames and let myself look at her straight on. Firelight did things to her face that daylight kept secret—softened the sharpness, showed the tiredness underneath, the grief that she carried the way I used to carry the debt: constantly, silently, convinced it was structural.

"After," I said, "there's the rest of the range. The eastern valleys nobody's mapped properly since the Ministry redrew the charts. Communities that got the broadcast in time and evacuated and are now sitting in borrowed halls wondering if anyone will ever tell them the truth about their own ground again." I turned the compass over in my hand, brass catching firelight. "Somebody has to map it honestly. Somebody has to write the page they'll have to copy from."

"That's a job for two people."

"It's a job for exactly two people. One who reads stone and one who reads locks." I looked up. "And I'm not asking, Voss. I'm telling you the survey plan. You can lodge objections through official channels."

"What official channels?"

"The fire. Right there. Lodge away."

She laughed, and threw a pebble at me, and I caught it, and the space between us across the small fire was warm and narrow and entirely unnameable, and we both let it stay that way—not because we were afraid of the name, but because we'd both spent enough of our lives having words assigned to us by institutions. Debt. Duty. Thief. Ranger. Whatever this was, it would get named in its own time, by us, or it wouldn't need naming at all.

Later, banked fire, her breathing gone slow and even beside me, I opened the compass one last time under the stars.

The needle swung. Settled. And I noticed what I'd been noticing for days and refusing to say aloud: it didn't point north. Not truly. Sorrel's repair had been masterful, but the old magnetization ran deep in the metal, laid down in some forge decades ago, and the card settled a degree and a half east of true—leaning, always leaning, toward the pass. Toward the range's heart. Toward the fault my mother had found and refused to bury.

A broken compass, by any instrument-maker's standard.

Or a faithful one, by ours. Pointing not north but forward. Not toward where the world said we should go, but toward the thing we'd sworn to see clearly, no matter what it cost, no matter who wanted it dark.

I closed the lid. Held it a moment against my sternum, where it had hung through storm and ford and fire.

"Goodnight, Mother," I said, very quietly, to the mountain and the stars and the woman who had walked this saddle thirty years ago with the truth in her pocket. "We've got it from here."

* * *

We crossed the summit of the pass at midmorning on the second day, and the eastern side opened beneath us like a country out of a story no one had gotten to finish.

Green valleys. Unmapped drainages. Smoke rising from a settlement in the middle distance—one of the broadcast towns, the ones that had listened and lived—and beyond it, range after range fading into haze, territory no Ministry chart had ever rendered honestly, waiting for the first truthful page ever written about it.

Ulyana stood beside me at the top, wind pulling her hair sideways, and unrolled the blank survey sheets across a flat stone.

"So," she said. "Where do we start?"

I opened the compass. The needle leaned forward, faithful and stubborn, into all that unmapped light.

"Here," I said. "We start here."

And below us, at the foot of the descent, we saw them: figures on the trail, climbing toward us—a small party, moving fast, and at its head a tall silhouette in a Ministry-gray coat that I knew better than I knew my own heartbeat.

Devereux had come up the mountain. And she was carrying papers.
