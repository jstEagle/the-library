## 21. Where the Range Ends

The pass let them down gently, as if the mountain had decided, after everything, to be kind.

Xanthe felt it in their knees first—the grade softening from the brutal switchbacks of the high country into long, patient slopes where the snow gave way to wet grass and then to gravel bars stitched with meltwater. The air thickened. Somewhere below, out of sight behind a shoulder of grey stone, water was running hard enough to hear it: the sound of a valley that had not frozen shut.

Ulyana stopped on the last ridge and shaded her eyes with one hand. She'd been quiet for two days—not the hostile quiet of the ruin or the frightened quiet of the snowline, but something new. A silence like held breath.

"There," she said.

Xanthe looked.

The valley opened below them like a hand uncurling. A river ran its length, braided and bright with spring melt, and along its banks stood a town—Whitlow, according to the old survey maps Xanthe had memorized years ago and never expected to matter. Two hundred souls, maybe three hundred. Barns, a mill, a church spire, fields just beginning to green. Everything the Ashfall fault would have swallowed when it finally moved.

Everything empty.

"Empty," Ulyana said, and her voice cracked on the word. "Xanthe—it's *empty*."

It was. No smoke from chimneys. No figures in the fields. But no ruin either, no burned roofs, no scattered belongings marking panic. The town stood tidy and abandoned, doors shut, windows latched, the way people leave a place when they expect to come back to it.

They descended fast, too fast for caution, boots skidding on wet scree, because both of them understood at the same moment what they might be looking at—and neither dared say it aloud until there was proof.

The proof was nailed to the meetinghouse door.

A broadsheet, printed on a Ministry field press by the look of the type, weather-warped but legible:

**ORDER OF EVACUATION — REGIONAL AUTHORITY. By direction of the Office of Surveys, all residents of Whitlow and surrounding holdings will remove themselves east of the Kettle Road by [date smudged]. This order follows receipt of geological findings under active review. Compliance is mandatory. Return prohibited pending inquiry.**

Below it, someone had pinned a second sheet, handwritten, in a schoolteacher's careful script: *We went to Marrow's Ford. All of us. We are together. We will come home when it is safe, or we will make it safe. — E.*

Ulyana read it twice. Then she sat down right there on the meetinghouse steps, in the mud, and put her face in her hands, and laughed—a ragged, disbelieving sound that had grief in it somewhere, buried deep, but mostly had relief so large it didn't know what shape to take.

"They got out." She looked up at Xanthe, eyes streaming, grinning like a fool. "They *got out*. The broadcast reached them. It reached them in time."

Xanthe stood very still.

They had crossed the range on a wager—that truth, once loose, would move faster than the Ministry could bury it. They had gambled the survey data, the compass key, Ulyana's brother's decoded coordinates, Xanthe's mother's recorded testimony, all of it flung out over the regional stations on a night when contractors were closing on Kettlebrook and Xanthe was bleeding at the ford, buying minutes with false fires and cut ropes and the river itself. They had never known if it was enough. They had walked out of Kettlebrook ahead of the inquiry's summons without an answer, and the whole descent since had been silence on that question.

Now the answer stood nailed to a door in an empty, unburned, unbuilt-over town.

Three hundred people were alive at Marrow's Ford because a dead woman had refused to stay silent, and a thief had refused to stop decoding, and a ranger had finally, at the very end, chosen to be seen.

"It worked," Xanthe said. Their voice came out strange. Flat. They cleared their throat and tried again. "It actually worked."

"Don't you dare stand there sounding surprised." Ulyana got up, mud on her knees, and jabbed a finger at them. "You held a river crossing against eleven armed men with a bruised rib and a borrowed rifle. You don't get to be modest now."

"I wasn't being modest. I was—" Xanthe stopped. What were they doing? Searching for the feeling and finding only a vast, ringing emptiness where the fear used to live. "I think I'm waiting for it to be taken back."

Ulyana's grin faded into something gentler. She knew that waiting. She'd carried it since her brother died—the certainty that anything good was provisional, that the Ministry's ledger always balanced in the end.

"It can't be," she said. "Three hundred witnesses, an inquiry with subpoena power, your mother's voice on every station in the region. There's no burying this one. There's no hole deep enough." She touched the broadsheet, smoothing its curled corner. "She did that. Your mother. Years ago, she started this, and she just needed someone to finish carrying it."

Xanthe looked at the handwriting on the second sheet. *We will come home when it is safe, or we will make it safe.* Ordinary people, farmers and millers, writing defiance in pencil because a stranger's broadcast told them the ground under their children was a lie the government intended to keep.

"We should walk through," Xanthe said. "Check the wells, the grain stores. If anyone comes back early, the town should be ready."

It was ranger's work. It was also, they both knew, a way to be useful while the enormity finished landing.

* * *

They spent the day in Whitlow the way you'd tend a sleeping patient.

Xanthe checked the headgates on the irrigation channel and found them closed clean—someone had known what they were doing when they left. The granary was sealed and mouse-proofed. In the schoolhouse, slates still hung on their hooks and a half-finished arithmetic lesson waited on the board, *7 × 8 =* with no answer, a small ghost that made Xanthe's chest ache. On the teacher's desk lay a stack of the same broadsheet, and beneath it, a child's drawing: a mountain with smoke coming off it, and stick figures walking away holding hands, and above them, in wobbling letters, **THE RANGER TOLD US THE TRUTH**.

Xanthe stood over that drawing for a long time.

*I'm not a ranger anymore,* they thought. *I sent the badge away.* But the thought wouldn't hold. Whatever the Ministry stripped from them, whatever license they'd surrendered or would surrender when the inquiry was done with them, the work itself—the reading of stone and water and weather, the duty to the people who lived inside both—had never actually belonged to Devereux to give or take. That was the thing their mother had understood, standing in some Ministry office years ago, refusing to sign. The work belonged to whoever did it honestly.

In the late afternoon they found the telegraph office unlocked, its line still strung east toward Marrow's Ford. Ulyana, who had learned more about signal work in one night at the survey station than most technicians learned in a year, tapped out a query on the dead line out of habit—and then froze, because the line answered.

A relay station, still manned, still receiving. The operator's reply came back in crisp official code, then, sensing perhaps who might be on the other end, in plain language:

***WHITLOW PARTY? CONFIRM STATUS. INQUIRY COMMISSIONER EN ROUTE MARROW'S FORD. STATEMENT REQUESTED FROM INDIVIDUALS RESPONSIBLE FOR BROADCAST. SAFE CONDUCT GUARANTEED PENDING TESTIMONY.***

Ulyana looked at Xanthe. "*Responsible for the broadcast.* That's us. That's a summons dressed up nice."

"It's also safe conduct," Xanthe said slowly. "In writing, on an open line, where every relay between here and the capital can see it. They can't disappear us now. Not with the whole region watching." They took the key from Ulyana's fingers. "And they need our testimony more than we need their protection. Remember that. Walk in like it's true."

Xanthe tapped back a confirmation, coordinates, estimated arrival. Then, after a pause, added a line of their own devising, plain language, unsigned:

***THE PEOPLE OF WHITLOW SHOULD BE TOLD THEY MAY RETURN WHEN THE INQUIRY CERTIFIES THE FAULT MONITORED. THEY LEFT ORDERLY AND THEY WILL COME HOME ORDERLY. SEE TO IT.***

The relay acknowledged. Whether anyone with power would heed it was another matter—but the words were on the wire now, part of the record, and records had a way of surfacing. Xanthe's mother had proved that.

* * *

That evening they camped in the meetinghouse, because the beds in Whitlow belonged to other people and neither of them could shake the feeling of being guests.

Ulyana built a fire in the iron stove and cooked the last of the trail ration into something almost edible. Xanthe sat by the window with their pack open in their lap, going through it the way you go through a dead relative's effects—slowly, touching everything.

At the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, was the brass compass.

It had come back to them at the ford, pressed into their hands by a bleeding, laughing Ulyana in the grey hour before dawn, and Xanthe had carried it since without opening the wrapping. Now they unwound the cloth.

The needle swung, settled, pointed north. Steady. Faithful. The case bore the old scratches, the dent from a fall Xanthe's mother had taken on some ridge decades before Xanthe existed, and the engraving around the rim that had started everything—a key that was never meant to open locks, only to open silence.

"You keep looking at it like you're deciding something," Ulyana said from the stove.

"I already decided." Xanthe turned it over in their hands. "Back in Kettlebrook I packed my badge to send to the inquiry. Evidence of authority misused—or authority abandoned, depending on how they want to read it. Let them argue about which." They looked up. "But the compass stays with me. Not because it's mine. Because it isn't. It was hers, and then it was the debt, and now it's—" They searched for the word.

"Forward," Ulyana supplied quietly. "You said once it points forward. After we fix the needle."

"The needle doesn't need fixing." Xanthe watched it swing, settle, hold. "I did."

Ulyana came and sat beside them on the window bench, shoulder warm against theirs, and for a while neither spoke. Outside, the first stars came up over the Ashfall Range, and the mountain that had tried to kill them stood black and enormous and, for once, honest—its fault lines mapped, its dangers published, its secrets dragged into the light by a dead surveyor and a living thief and a ranger who had learned, far too late and exactly in time, that invisibility was just another kind of grave.

"When we testify," Ulyana said at last, "they'll ask you why you lied to Devereux at the survey camp. First lie of your career. They'll want to know if you're trustworthy."

"What will you say?"

"That's your question to answer, not mine."

Xanthe turned the compass over once more, then wrapped it back in the oilcloth—not hiding it, just keeping it safe, the way you keep a promise.

"I'll tell them the truth," they said. "I lied to an institution that was lying to everyone. And then I stopped lying to myself, which was harder, and mattered more. If they want a ranger they can trust, they should ask whether I'd do it again." A breath. "I would. Faster, next time."

Ulyana laughed softly and leaned her head against their shoulder. "Faster," she agreed. "Next time."

There would be a next time. Both of them knew it. The inquiry would grind on, Devereux would answer for orders given, names would be read into record—Ulyana's brother first, and Xanthe's mother beside him—and none of it would bring anyone back or make the ground under Whitlow any less restless. Peace without absolution. The mountain didn't forgive; it only permitted. But three hundred people were sleeping safely at Marrow's Ford tonight, and a town stood ready behind clean-latched doors, and somewhere east a commissioner was drafting questions that Xanthe Okafor had spent a lifetime learning how to answer.

Xanthe set the compass on the windowsill, needle north, facing the road they would take in the morning.

Outside, far off down the darkening valley, a light appeared on the Kettle Road—lanterns, many of them, moving toward Marrow's Ford. Word traveled fast on an open wire. The people of Whitlow, it seemed, were not waiting for permission.

They were already coming home.
