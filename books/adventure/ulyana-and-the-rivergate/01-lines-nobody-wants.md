## 1. Lines Nobody Wants

The last stake went into the ground at half past four, and Coral Sterling drove it home with three blows of the maul, no more, because that was how Aldous had taught her—one to set, one to seat, one to swear by. The iron rang against stone beneath the loam, a small clear bell in the hush of the valley, and when she straightened up she found the whole crew had gone quiet behind her, the way they always did at the end of a job, as if they were listening for something.

Nothing came. No whistle, no telegraph clatter, no voice out of the pines. Just the wind moving through the high grass like water finding its level, and somewhere below, the creek they'd have to name on the map—*unnamed tributary*, or maybe *Sterling Creek* if she was feeling vain, which she never was, not anymore.

"Done," she said.

"Done," said Okafor, and took off his hat, and held it against his chest for a moment, which was his way of praying without anyone having to know about it.

They had spent eleven days surveying a spur that would probably never carry a single rail. It branched off the Meridian line at a junction so minor that the railroad's own timetable didn't bother listing it, ran four miles up into timber country that the company had logged once, badly, and abandoned, and ended—at least on paper—at a mill town called Harrow that had burned down before Coral was born. The client was a land syndicate in the capital that wanted to know if the grade could be reworked for a narrow-gauge orchard line. It couldn't, not really. The numbers said so. She'd write the report anyway, honest and dry, and they'd pay her, and nobody would ever ride the line, and that was exactly the kind of job Coral Sterling took.

She looked back down the cut they'd blazed through the brush—ribbon marks pale in the failing light, stakes standing at intervals like a congregation—and felt the particular satisfaction that came from finishing work nobody wanted done. There was a purity in it. A forgotten spur asked nothing of you except accuracy. It didn't care who you'd been. It didn't read newspapers.

"All right," she said, turning. "Somebody tell me we still have the good coffee."

"We have the *bad* coffee," said Wren, who handled their instruments and most of their sarcasm. "We finished the good coffee Tuesday, during the rain, when morale was a matter of survival."

"That was medicinal," said Okafor.

"That was *hoarding*," said Wren.

Coral let them argue. This was the sound of a job well closed: her people bickering on the walk down, packs creaking, boots finding the old deer trail without needing to look for it. Six of them now, counting herself. Okafor, who had run track gangs for the Southern Consolidated until a foreman's negligence put two of his men under a slide and the company's lawyers put the blame on him; he'd walked off with nothing but his tools and a temper he'd learned to bank like a stove. Wren, who had been fired from three railroads for correcting engineers senior to them, and who could shoot an elevation with a transit in fog. The Ferro twins, Marta and Ilsa, who'd come up out of the harbor works with hands like knotted rope and a talent for bridges that bordered on the devotional—they talked to structures, quietly, the way other men talked to horses. And young Piet, nineteen, who had joined them two seasons ago off a wrecking crew and never said why, and whom nobody asked, because that was the code.

The code wasn't written anywhere. It didn't need to be. It had assembled itself over six years the way silt builds a bar—grain by grain, each of them arriving with some name they needed to leave downstream. Take obscure jobs. Stay invisible. Never look back. Three lines, and every one of them load-bearing.

Other crews chased the big contracts—the trunk lines, the mountain tunnels, the jobs that got your firm's name stamped on brass plaques in station houses. Coral's crew chased the work that fell between the cracks. Drainage surveys for counties too poor to argue with the figure. Reconnaisance runs for land speculators who wanted plausible deniability more than they wanted rails. Spur lines to nowhere, for mills that wouldn't last the decade. They did the work cleanly, billed fairly, filed their reports, and vanished before anyone thought to ask where they'd come from or what they'd left behind. In six years they had built a reputation in the only circles that mattered—small, precise, unremarkable—and kept their names out of all the others.

It hadn't started as philosophy. It had started as survival. But somewhere along the way, Coral had noticed that the invisibility had hardened into something else, something almost like happiness, and she had stopped questioning it. You could live a whole life in the margins of the map. She intended to.

* * *

Camp that night was in a saddle below the ridge, a flat they'd used on the way up, fire ringed with stones they'd carried themselves because leaving no trace meant leaving no trace. The bad coffee got brewed anyway, stretched with chicory, and Marta produced a tin of honey cakes she'd been hiding since the second day, and that was the celebration. That was always the celebration. Nobody toasted. Toasting invited attention. Instead they ate in the firelight while Ilsa sketched the crossing design in the dirt with a stick, arguing grades with Wren, and Piet sat close enough to the flames that his face shone, laughing at things he mostly didn't understand yet, and Okafor hummed something low under his breath that might have been a hymn or might have been a work song, and Coral sat slightly apart, as she always did, holding her cup with both hands and watching her family be warm.

This was the wealth. She knew that. Not the money, which came and went like weather, but this—the fire, the argument about grades, the honey cakes divided seven ways among six people because Marta always claimed there were seven and dared anyone to count. Eleven days in wet brush and blackflies, and here they were, unbroken, unhunted, unknown.

"You're doing it again," Wren said, dropping onto the log beside her.

"Doing what."

"The thing where you look at us like you're memorizing us. Like we're a view you're going to have to leave."

"I don't do that."

"You've done it every closing night for six years." Wren blew on their coffee. "One of these days I'm going to ask you what you're afraid of, and you're going to say *nothing*, and I'm going to believe you, because you're the best liar I've ever worked for."

Coral smiled and said nothing, which proved the point.

The telegram came after full dark. They heard the rider before they saw him—a single set of hooves on the switchback road above camp, unhurried, which was somehow worse than a gallop. He came down with a lantern swinging, a boy really, sixteen maybe, courier's satchel across his chest, and he asked for Sterling's crew like he'd been told the name twice and rehearsed it.

"Didn't know anybody knew we were here," Okafor said mildly, as the boy fumbled the envelope out.

Neither did Coral. That was the first wrong note. Their clients wired ahead to towns; they did not send riders up logging roads at night. She signed the receipt, tipped the boy, and watched his lantern climb away until it was just another star going the wrong direction.

Then she stood by the fire and opened the telegram, and the warmth went out of the evening the way heat leaves a stone after sundown—slowly, and then all at once.

The message was short. Telegrams always were, but this one had been composed with a knife:

**STERLING SURVEY STOP RIVERGATE LINE REOPENING STOP OFFERING YOUR FIRM TRIPLE STANDARD RATE FULL AUTHORITY STOP REPLY VOSS DEVELOPMENT CO STOP — U.V.**

She read it twice. The letters didn't change. *U.V.* Two initials, and six years collapsed like a bad trestle.

Ulyana Voss. Who had stood second in their class at the Institute while Coral stood first, and had never once forgiven the ordering. Who had gone east and then further east, collecting contracts the way coral collects—no, the way *she* collected—harbors, concessions, ministers' signatures. Whose name appeared in the financial pages now beside words like *consortium* and *concession*, and whose face appeared beside them too, handsome and hard, photographed at ribbon-cuttings with men whose titles changed whenever the government did.

And Rivergate. Rivergate, which was not a place to Coral but a season of her life, a washout, a bridge gone in forty seconds of brown water, and Aldous Renner going with it, and a report afterward with her own signature at the bottom taking the blame, because someone had to sign it and the alternative was a crew of kids losing their first contract and their futures both. She had signed. She had carried it. She had built a career in the margins of the map specifically so that no one would ever ask her to go back.

Now the woman who knew her best and liked her least was offering triple pay to do exactly that.

"Coral?" Marta's voice, careful. "Bad news?"

"It's work," Coral said. Her voice came out level. She'd had practice. "A big one."

"How big?" That was Piet, leaning forward, firelight in his eyes, hungry the way nineteen-year-olds are allowed to be hungry.

"Rivergate," she said. "Reopening the Rivergate line."

The fire cracked. Somewhere down the valley the creek they'd surveyed all week went on talking to itself, indifferent, already forgetting them. Okafor set down his cup. Wren stopped chewing. Even the twins went still, and Ilsa's stick stopped mid-grade in the dirt, because everyone on this crew knew what Rivergate meant—not the geography, but the weight of it. They knew there was a reason their boss never took river work. They knew there was a reason she flinched, very slightly, at flood season, at the smell of high water. They had never asked. That was the code.

But hunger is also a code, and winter was coming, and the coffers were down to what the Harrow spur would pay, which was rent and beans and not much else. Coral watched the arithmetic move behind their eyes—Okafor counting, Wren calculating, the twins thinking of the bridge work a line like that would mean, Piet simply thinking *triple*.

"Triple rate," Okafor said quietly. Not a question. An assessment.

"For Voss Development," Wren said, and said the name like spitting.

"She knows we're here," Marta said. "That's the part I don't like. Nobody knows we're here."

Coral folded the telegram along its creases, once, twice, until it was a small hard square she could close her fist around. The firelight moved on her face and she let it, and inside her chest something that had been sealed for six years—with mortar, with habit, with a signature in ink gone brown—felt the first hairline crack run through it.

"No one answers anything tonight," she said. "Sleep. We'll talk in the morning."

She lay awake long after the others, listening to the creek, which sounded, in the dark, exactly like a bigger one she had spent six years trying not to hear. Triple pay. Sealed records, if Ulyana still had them—Ulyana, who collected things. Coral turned the little square of paper over in her fingers in the dark and understood, with the flat certainty of a level bubble finding center, that the offer was not an offer at all.

It was a hook. And somewhere out east, a woman who never forgot an ordering was waiting to see if she'd bite.
