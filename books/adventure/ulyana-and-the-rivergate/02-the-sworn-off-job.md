## 2. The Sworn-Off Job

The telegram sat on the mess table between the coffee pot and the tin of dried apricots, and nobody touched it for a full hour.

Coral had read it twice, then folded it into quarters and set it down as if it might bite. The paper was good stock—thick, cream-colored, the kind that came with a company seal. Ulyana Voss's seal. She could feel the crew's eyes on her the way she felt weather coming: not looking directly, but all of them angled toward it.

"Triple pay," said Fen, finally, because Fen had never in his life met a silence he couldn't break. He was whittling a tent peg he didn't need, curls of cedar dropping onto his boot. "I've never seen triple pay. I've seen double once, on the Kestrel spur, and we all got boots out of it."

"I saw the boots," said Marta from the doorway, where she stood with a dish towel over her shoulder like a general's sash. "They fell apart by spring."

"These would be better boots."

"You don't know that."

"I know what triple buys."

Coral let them go on. That was the trick of leading this crew—you let the talk run its course like water finding its level, and you learned more from where it pooled than from anything anyone said outright. Fen wanted the money and wasn't ashamed of it. Marta was against it and hadn't yet said why, which meant she knew why, which meant Coral should listen to her hardest.

Junie said nothing at all. Junie, who was nineteen and had been with them two years, who cooked like an angel and ran a transit like someone twice her age, sat with her hands wrapped around her mug and watched Coral with those steady gray eyes. Waiting. Junie always waited for Coral to speak first, and Coral hated how much weight that put in her own throat.

"It's the Rivergate," Coral said at last.

The name landed in the room like a dropped tool. Even the fire seemed to lean back from it.

"We don't do the Rivergate," Marta said. "That's not a rule we wrote down, but it's a rule."

"I know what our rules are."

"Then say it."

Coral looked at the telegram again without unfolding it. She didn't need to. *Voss Survey & Contracting requests Sterling party for reopening of Rivergate line. Triple standard rate. Reply by wire, Fort Amberly.* Twelve words of business and one name that was twelve years old and still bleeding.

"The money's real," Coral said. "The offer's probably even honest, which is worse. But I'm turning it down."

Fen set down his knife. "Just like that?"

"Just like that."

"And we don't get a say?"

Coral felt something shift under her ribs—the old ache, the one shaped like Aldous. "You get a say in everything else we do. This one's mine."

"That's not the pact," Fen said quietly. He wasn't angry; that was the trouble with Fen, he went quiet instead of loud, and quiet Fen was always right. "We voted on the Kestrel spur. We voted on the Marsh Fork. We voted when Junie joined and when the Hendersons left. We've voted on every job since the day you found us eating cold beans off a single spoon."

"We voted on jobs," Coral said. "This isn't a job. This is a grave."

Nobody answered that. Outside, the rain had started—not monsoon rain, nothing so dramatic, just the thin autumn drizzle that turned the camp mud into something between porridge and glue. Somewhere up the valley a grouse was complaining about it. The stove ticked as the pipe heated.

Marta hung up her towel and sat down across from Coral, folding her hands. Marta was fifty-one and had hands like tree roots, and when she folded them like that it meant she'd decided to be reasonable, which was somehow more frightening than when she decided otherwise.

"Coral," she said. "We have forty dollars and eleven cents."

"I can count."

"And winter coming, and no contract past the first snow, and Junie needing new glasses because she won't tell you but I've seen her squinting at her field book like it's written in another language." Marta's voice stayed level, almost gentle. "I'm not saying take the job. I'm saying look at us while you refuse it."

Coral looked. It was a cruelty, doing what Marta asked. Fen in his patched coat. Junie trying to make herself small, which she never managed, being tall as a fence post. Old Tam's empty chair by the wall—he'd gone home to his daughter's in September, bad lungs, and they were eleven now instead of twelve, and eleven people eat through forty dollars like a creek eats through a roadbed.

"I'm looking," Coral said.

* * *

That night she didn't sleep. She took her coat and walked out past the instrument tent to the cut bank above the creek, and she sat down in the wet grass, and she did what she had sworn off doing, which was remembering.

Twelve years ago, in another October much like this one, she had been twenty-four years old and certain of everything except herself. Aldous Renner had picked her out of a survey school graduating class of thirty for exactly that reason, he told her later—"You're the only one who checks her work twice and trusts it zero times." He'd been sixty then, white-bearded, built like a bridge abutment, with a laugh that carried across three switchbacks. Everyone called him the Old Man of the Gate because he'd surveyed the original Rivergate line himself, back when the valley was young and the railroad companies were hungry and nobody yet understood what the river did in flood season.

She had been his chainman. His number two. His—she'd thought, though neither of them ever said it—almost-daughter.

The washout happened on the fourth of October, in the dark, in the rain.

It hadn't even been the big storm. That was the thing nobody believed afterward, the thing the inquiry couldn't fit into its neat boxes. It was a medium storm, an ordinary autumn rain, the kind the valley got six times a season. But the river had been running high for nine days, and the fill on the lower trestle approach had been built fast and cheap eight years before by men who were gone by then, and the water had spent nearly a decade finding the flaw the way water finds everything.

Aldous had gone out that night to check the culverts. He always did that—he'd say *the river tells you its plans if you stand in the rain long enough*, and everyone laughed, and everyone knew he'd do it anyway. Coral had offered to go with him. He'd waved her off. "You've been in the wet for fourteen hours, girl. One of us should sleep."

So she slept. And at some hour past midnight the hillside above the lower approach let go—a slide no bigger than a barn, the later report said, as if size mattered to the dead—and took two hundred feet of fill and the trestle's north abutment with it, and Aldous, walking the culvert line below in the dark with his lantern, had been under it.

They found the lantern first. Unbroken. Still burning, sheltered under a slab of timber, the flame bent sideways by the draft. Coral would remember that flame for the rest of her life: patient, absurd, going on about its business in the wreckage like nothing had ended at all.

They found him at dawn.

And then the machinery started. The company needed the line's failure explained, and explained cheaply, because the insurance schedules and the bondholders and the territorial land grants all depended on the washout being an act of God or an act of negligence—but not an act of engineering, not an act of the company's own cost-cutting. There was an inspection record, Coral learned later, a note in Aldous's own hand flagging the fill as suspect, filed three weeks before the slide and buried somewhere in the district office. If that note surfaced, the company owed everything. So the note had to stay buried, and someone had to be standing close enough to the disaster to bury it under.

Someone young. Someone with no family, no lawyer, no standing. Someone whose grief made her easy to manage and whose loyalty made her predictable.

The inquiry report ran forty pages. Coral appeared in it four times, each time as a variation on the same sentence: *the assistant surveyor failed to file her supervisor's field observations in accordance with procedure.* It wasn't even a lie, exactly—that was the elegant cruelty of it. She *had* handled Aldous's paperwork. She *had* missed the note, or so she'd believed for years, carrying the guilt like a stone in her chest, checking her memory of that autumn ten thousand times for the moment she'd failed him. Her name went into the record. The line closed. The valley lost its crossing. And Coral Sterling learned the most important lesson of her life, which was that records are weapons, and the safest place to stand is where no one is keeping any.

That was the pact, really. Not just obscure jobs and invisibility. It was *never again be somewhere a document can find me.*

She sat on the cut bank until the drizzle soaked through her coat, watching the creek run black under the last gray light, and she understood—with the terrible clarity that only comes at night, alone, in the cold—that Ulyana Voss knew all of this. Had to know. You didn't offer triple pay for the Rivergate to Coral Sterling unless you wanted something more than a survey. Unless you were fishing.

The question that kept her awake after that, staring at the stars when the clouds tore open, was simpler and worse:

What does Ulyana Voss want?

* * *

In the morning the vote happened anyway.

Coral called it herself, which surprised them—she saw it in their faces. She stood at the head of the mess table with the telegram unfolded flat, weighted at the corners with a tin cup, a knife, a rock, and Junie's spare pencil.

"Here's how it is," she said. "I refused this job last night. I'm refusing it again this morning, on the record, with reasons. The Rivergate killed my mentor. My name is in the dirt of that inquiry, and Voss knows it, and I don't trust a woman who baits a hook that carefully. If we take this job, we're not taking a survey. We're taking a side in something I don't understand yet."

She looked around the table. Eleven chairs, ten filled, Tam's empty one at the end like a missing tooth.

"But Marta's right about the arithmetic, and I won't pretend otherwise. Forty dollars and eleven cents. Winter. Junie's eyes. So we vote. All of you. And if the yeses carry, I'll lead you down there myself, because I don't leave my people to walk into anything I wouldn't walk into first. That's also the pact."

Silence. The stove ticked. Rain on the canvas overhead, soft as footsteps.

Fen spoke first. "Yes." No speech, no apology. Just the word, laid down like a rail.

"Yes," said Okafor, who rarely spoke and never wasted it.

"Yes," said the Delgado twins, together, as they did everything.

Marta was last before Junie. She looked at Coral for a long moment, and whatever passed between them wasn't anger—it was older and sadder than that, the look of a woman who has fed people for years and hates that hunger makes decisions for families. "Yes," Marta said. "But I'm writing down that I argued for your reasons first. Somebody in this crew has to keep the record straight."

A sound went around the table that wasn't quite a laugh. Coral almost managed one herself.

Then Junie.

Junie had been looking at her mug the whole time, both hands around it, and now she raised her head and those gray eyes were dry and very clear. "Before I vote," she said, "can I ask you something? Just you. It doesn't have to be answered out loud."

Coral nodded.

"Is the job dangerous because of the river," Junie asked, quietly, "or because of her?"

The table went absolutely still. Nineteen years old, and she'd cut straight through to the bone in one question, the way she did with a transit—no wasted motion, dead on the line.

"Both," Coral said. "Mostly her."

Junie nodded slowly. Then she set her mug down and said, "Yes. But I want it noted that I'm voting for the crew, not the river."

"Noted," Marta said, and wrote it down, actually wrote it, in the margin of the supply ledger, because Marta kept the ledger and Marta kept everything.

Eight yeses. Two abstentions—old Wrenn, who abstained from everything on principle, and Tam's ghost of a chair. The motion carried. The Rivergate was theirs, or they were its; Coral genuinely couldn't tell which.

"All right," she said, and heard her own voice come out steadier than the inside of her felt. "Okafor, wire Fort Amberly tonight. Standard acceptance language, nothing warm. Fen, pull the gear inventory—we'll need the good levels, and I want the boat situation looked at hard before we get near that water. Marta—"

"I know," Marta said. "Food. More food than seems sane."

"More food than seems sane."

The crew broke apart into motion, the particular purposeful clatter of people with a job, and Coral stood a moment longer at the head of the table with the telegram under her hand. Triple rate. A sealed envelope's worth of unspoken terms. A rival's name signed in ink that smelled, faintly, even now, of smoke.

Outside, the rain was thickening. Upstream, somewhere beyond the ridgelines, a river she had sworn off was already rising toward her.

She went to pack.
