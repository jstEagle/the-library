## 3. Terms of the Rival

The office at Voss & Meridian smelled of cedar oil and old rain, and Ulyana Voss had spent eleven years making certain it smelled that way. Cedar because it covered the river-damp that no amount of coal heat ever fully drove from the building's bones; rain because she wanted to remember, every working hour, what water could do when it decided to take something back.

She stood at the window with her third coffee going cold in her hand and watched Coral Sterling's telegram reply sit on her desk like a coiled snake.

*Declined.*

Two words, typed in a telegrapher's flat hand, and beneath them nothing—no apology, no counteroffer, not even the courtesy of an insult. Ulyana had read it four times. She had held it up to the light as though the paper might confess some second meaning hidden between the fibers. *Declined.* As if the Rivergate were a dinner invitation. As if Coral Sterling had ever once, in all the years Ulyana had known her, done anything so simple as decline.

"You expected otherwise," said Marsh from the doorway.

Marsh was her second-in-command the way ballast was cargo—dense, reliable, and without any opinions worth consulting. He stood with his hat in his hands, which he only did when he thought he'd brought bad news, and Ulyana found it tiresome. Bad news was the only news worth paying for. Good news told you nothing about anyone.

"I expected pride," Ulyana said. "Pride is predictable. Pride is a kind of terrain—you can survey it. I did not expect her to leave money on the table. Her crew eats when she says they eat, and I am told they have been eating thin since spring."

"Maybe she's changed."

Ulyana turned from the window and let herself smile, which she knew from long experience unsettled people who worked for her. "No one changes, Marsh. They only get better at hiding where they've been wounded." She crossed to the desk, picked up the telegram, and fed it to the stove grate with two fingers. It curled and went orange and was gone. "Which is why we will not ask her again by wire."

"Then how?"

"We're going to go and stand on her doorstep in the rain until she opens the door."

* * *

It took nine days to find them, which told Ulyana more than any dossier could have. A crew that wanted to be found could be located in an afternoon; a crew that didn't want to be found required patience, bribed stationmasters, and a memory for small things—which spur lines had recently been walked, which boarding houses had suddenly acquired guests who paid cash and asked no questions. The Sterling outfit moved through the country like water through limestone: invisible, patient, carving something slowly out of solid rock.

Ulyana traveled to meet them with one trunk, one clerk, and Marsh, and she arrived at the survey camp in the last grey hour of evening, when the mist came off the creek bottoms and everything smelled of woodsmoke and wet canvas. She saw the camp before the camp saw her—a scatter of tents under a stand of birch, a tripod silhouetted against the last light, a cookfire burning low and blue. Four people around it. Five counting the dog, which lifted its head first and stared at her through the trees with yellow eyes before anyone else noticed she existed.

Coral Sterling stood up from the fire the way a deer stands—not quickly, but completely, all at once, as if her body had received some single instruction. She was thinner than Ulyana remembered. Her hair was shorter. There was a new scar along her jaw that Ulyana catalogued immediately and filed away, because scars were dates and dates were stories and stories were leverage.

"Voss," Coral said. No surprise in it. That was its own message: *I knew you'd come eventually. I've known for years.*

"Sterling." Ulyana stopped at the edge of the firelight, deliberately, letting them see her hands empty. The other three crew members had risen too—a big quiet man with forearms like dock rope, a young woman with a transit case gripped in both hands like a weapon, and a boy barely twenty whose eyes went from Coral to Ulyana and back again, reading his captain's face for instructions. Loyal, all of them. That was the problem with Coral Sterling. She collected loyalty the way other people collected debts, and she never seemed to notice she was doing it.

"You got my answer," Coral said.

"I burned your answer. It was two words long; it made poor reading." Ulyana let her gaze travel over the camp—the mended tent seams, the boots gone thin at the sole, the pot of something that was mostly barley pretending to be stew. "Triple pay, Sterling. I said it in the telegram and I'll say it standing in your mud. Triple pay, winter wages through the thaw, and hazard compensation on top. Your crew would eat beef this winter instead of barley."

"They'd also be working the Rivergate."

"Yes," Ulyana said. "They would."

One of the crew shifted—the big man—and Coral's hand moved, a small motion, a rudder correction, and he stilled. Ulyana watched it happen and felt the old fascination rise in her chest like groundwater. Eleven years ago they had been rivals in the proper sense: two junior engineers competing for the same contracts, trading barbs across bid rooms, each convinced the other was the luckier and the lesser. Then the washout, and Aldous Fen dead in the water, and Coral Sterling's name dragged through the inquiry like a body through wreckage—and after that they had not been rivals anymore. After that, Ulyana had risen and Coral had descended, and it had always sat wrong in Ulyana, the way a poorly graded road sits wrong underfoot. You could travel it. You always knew.

"The line's cursed," Coral said now. "You know it. Everyone in three counties knows it. The valley flooded once and took a bridge, a trestle, and my mentor, and you want to run a railroad through its grave because the grades are good."

"The grades are *exceptional*," Ulyana said. "And I want to run a survey first. Nothing more. Boats, instruments, paper. If the river says no, the river says no, and I'll pay you for asking politely." She paused, and then she took the risk she had traveled nine days to take. "But we both know the river isn't why you refused."

The fire popped. Somewhere down the creek an owl called, twice, and quit.

"Careful," Coral said quietly.

"No," Ulyana said. "I don't think I will be careful. Careful is what got us here—eleven years of careful, two careers built on top of a hole in the ground full of drowned men and paperwork." She reached into her coat, slowly enough that the boy by the fire tensed, and drew out a slim folder wrapped in oilcloth. She did not offer it. She simply held it, visible, in the firelight. "Do you know what this is?"

Coral's face did something very small and very controlled. Ulyana watched it happen the way she watched a needle settle on a grade: the tremor before true level.

"That's company property," Coral said.

"It's the sealed incident record of the Rivergate washout. Original testimony. Aldous Fen's last field reports, the ones that were never entered into the inquiry. The engineering correspondence from the six weeks before the collapse—including three memos from our own chief engineer warning that the cut above the trestle wouldn't hold." Ulyana turned the folder over in her hands, unhurried. "I've read it, Sterling. All of it. I've had it in my safe for four years, and I have read it perhaps forty times, and I will tell you what I concluded."

"Don't."

"He didn't die because of your error." Ulyana kept her voice level, almost gentle, which was worse than cruelty and she knew it. "He died because the report was falsified before the ink dried, and someone chose to carry that lie rather than bury five careers with it. Someone young. Someone with a crew to feed. Someone whose name was cheaper than the truth."

The silence off the water was total. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"You're lying," Coral said, but her voice had gone thin, and Ulyana heard the thing underneath it—not denial. Hope. Hope was the cruelest instrument Ulyana owned, and she had carried it nine days through the rain to use exactly here.

"I lie about bids and schedules and the price of gravel," Ulyana said. "I do not lie about this. Ask yourself why I kept those papers in a safe instead of a furnace. Ask yourself why a woman who profits from the past would spend four years reading a dead man's handwriting." She extended the folder then, arm straight, offering it into the space between them like a bridge offered across a gorge. "I'm not selling you a job, Coral. I'm selling you the door out of the cell you've lived in for eleven years. The records come with the contract. Read them at the headwaters, where it happened, with the water in your ears. And if you still want to walk away afterward—" she shrugged, "—walk away. Triple pay for the attempt. My word on it, witnessed by your crew."

Coral did not take the folder. Her eyes had gone somewhere else—to the washout, Ulyana guessed, to a wall of brown water and a man's hat turning in the current—and Ulyana understood, with a clarity that felt almost like tenderness, that she had just done something irreversible. You could not show a drowning woman the surface and then withdraw it. Whatever Coral decided tonight, the shape of her life had changed.

"Why?" Coral asked at last, and her voice cracked on it, just slightly, just enough. "Why do you care? We hated each other."

"We competed," Ulyana said. "Hating takes time neither of us had." She set the folder down on the nearest crate, weighted it with a stone from the fire ring, and stepped back. "My father's company built the original trestle, Sterling. My name is in that correspondence too—I signed the requisition for the timber that failed. I was nineteen years old and I signed where I was told to sign, and Aldous Fen taught me drafting that summer in a room that smelled like yours smells right now." She let that land. Let it sit in the smoke. "So no. This isn't charity, and it isn't rivalry. It's a debt, and I have carried it badly for a long time, and I intend to pay it while I'm still alive to watch it land."

She turned to go. Behind her, the crew had not moved, and the dog watched her with its yellow eyes, and the mist was coming up the creek bottom thick as wool.

"The boat leaves from Harrow's Landing on the first of the month," Ulyana said, without turning around. "There's a vessel waiting there already, surveyed and provisioned. Bring your instruments. Bring your ghosts. They're all coming downriver anyway—they always were."

She was nearly to the tree line when Coral spoke, and Ulyana stopped, because the voice behind her was not the voice of a woman declining a contract. It was the voice of a woman standing at the edge of high water, testing the depth with one foot.

"The records stay sealed until I say otherwise," Coral said. "Not you. Me. If I open that folder and it says what you claim, I decide what happens to it. Not your lawyers. Not your board. Me."

Ulyana smiled at the dark, where no one could see it, and found to her surprise that the smile was real.

"Agreed," she said. "That's the whole point, Sterling. For once in your life, it should be yours."

She walked out into the rain, and behind her the fire crackled, and on the crate the oilcloth folder gleamed faintly, like something pulled from a riverbed—still holding the shape of the hand that had dropped it, waiting to tell the water what it remembered.
