## 4. Downriver Contract

The road to the Rivergate headwaters was less a road than a rumor with ruts. Three days out of the railhead at Kessel's Halt, the truck gave up its argument with the mud and they walked the rest, crew of six strung along a track that the jungle had been quietly eating for eleven years. Ferns grew through the ties of an old service spur. Somewhere ahead, water talked to itself in the dark.

Coral walked point because she always did, and because walking point meant she didn't have to look at anyone's face.

They had voted. That was the thing she kept circling back to, the way a tongue finds a chipped tooth. She had stood in the mess car at Kessel's Halt and told them no — no in her flat surveyor's voice, the one that ended arguments about benchmarks and began arguments about everything else — and they had voted yes anyway. Four to two, with Odile abstaining, which from Odile was practically a sermon.

*We need the winter money,* Ren had said, not meeting her eyes. *And you need something too, boss. You just won't say what.*

She hadn't answered that. She'd packed her instruments instead, and now here they were, six small warm bodies moving down a green throat toward the river that had eaten Aldous Ferro and, in every way that mattered, had eaten her too.

"You're doing the thing," Odile said, falling into step beside her. Odile was fifty-three and built like a bridge abutment, and she had been with Coral longer than anyone — since before, since the before.

"What thing."

"The thing where your shoulders climb up around your ears like the hills are coming for you."

"The hills *are* coming for us. That's rather the point of a valley survey."

Odile grunted, unconvinced, and let it lie. That was the courtesy of the crew: they carried each other's silences without asking what was in them.

By late afternoon the trees opened and there it was — the Rivergate headwater, wide as a lake and moving with the slow muscular patience of something that had all the time in the world. The water was the color of strong tea, tannin-dark, and it smelled of wet stone and rot and, faintly, of flowers. On the far bank, half-swallowed by vines, stood the bones of the old gatehouse: brick arches, a crane arm rusted into a question mark.

"Eleven years," Ren said softly, beside her. "You'd never know a whole line died here."

"The river knows," Coral said. "The river keeps excellent records."

The camp Ulyana Voss had promised was real, at least. Canvas on frames, crates stenciled with the Voss Consolidated hawk, a cook stove, a radio set under oilcloth. Too much, Coral thought, looking at it. Too new, too complete, for a survey that was supposed to be preliminary. This wasn't a camp; it was a statement of intent. Ulyana hadn't sent them to look at the line. She'd sent them to reopen it.

And then there was the boat.

It sat moored to a stake at the bank's edge, a flat-bottomed work scow maybe thirty feet, painted a cheerful institutional gray that was already blistering. Coral walked its length once, slowly, and put her palm flat against the hull the way a doctor lays a hand on a chest.

"Boss?" Ren called. "What's the verdict?"

"She's sick," Coral said.

*

They found the ferryman the next morning, or he found them — it was never entirely clear with Idris which way these things ran.

He came poling out of the morning mist upstream of camp, standing easy in a long dugout that rode the current like it had grown there, and he brought his boat alongside their rotten scow with the casual precision of a man docking a thought. He was perhaps sixty, brown as old rope, with white in his beard and a scar that split one eyebrow and gave him a permanently skeptical expression, as though the world had made a claim once and he'd been checking its paperwork ever since.

"You're the survey people," he said. Not a question.

"We're the survey people," Coral agreed.

"Voss's people." He said the name the way other men spat.

"You know her?"

"I know the name." He looked past her at the scow, and something moved behind his eyes — not quite pity. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man seeing an old lie wearing new paint. "That's her boat."

"It is."

"Did she tell you it would float?"

Coral felt the corner of her mouth twitch despite herself. "She told us it was surveyed and certified last season."

"Mm." Idris stepped from his dugout onto the scow without asking permission, crouched, and pressed his thumb into the planking near the waterline. It went in like a thumb into overripe fruit. He straightened, wiped his hand on his trousers, and looked at her with the weary satisfaction of a man who has been proven right so many times it has stopped being interesting. "Certified. Yes. I expect someone certified it."

"Who are you?" Odile asked, arms crossed, from the bank.

"Idris. I run this stretch. Passengers, freight, the occasional corpse." He said it without drama, a man reading a menu. "Your predecessor crews used me. Before the washout. After it, nobody came, and I fished." He turned back to Coral, and now the skepticism sharpened into something more deliberate. "You're going upriver in this."

"That's the plan."

"In monsoon season."

"The season's three weeks off," Ren protested. "The almanac says—"

"The almanac," Idris said, "is written by people who live where the sky behaves." He nodded upstream, toward the wall of green and cloud where the river came from. "Birds moved early this year. The hornbills came down-valley a month ago. The figs ripened wrong. And three nights ago the water rose four inches overnight with no rain here — which means rain in the high country, which means the monsoon is already walking down the mountain, and it does not care what the almanac promised anybody." He paused. "It also doesn't care whose name is on a contract."

Silence, except the river, talking its endless low talk.

Coral studied him. He met it without flinching, and she recognized the posture — a man who had decided something about her already and was waiting to see if she'd prove him right or wrong. She'd worn that posture herself, across a negotiating table, opposite a woman with cold gray eyes and a smile like a drawn blade.

"Then advise us," Coral said. "You know this water. What would you do?"

"Me?" Idris almost smiled. "I wouldn't go. But I'm not paid triple rate to lose my nerve." He let that land, watched it land, and Coral felt the hook of it settle somewhere under her ribs. He knew about the pay. Of course he knew. Small valleys keep better books than companies do. "If you go, you go in my boat, with me, and you go when I say and turn when I say. Not when the paper says. The paper doesn't have to swim."

"And your price?"

"For you?" He looked at her a moment too long, and she had the sudden vertiginous sense that he was not pricing the job at all but weighing something else entirely — her, her face, her name, some ledger of his own. "Same as anyone. Plus one condition. When we pass the drowned trestles at Kilometer Forty-One, you'll want to look away. Everyone does, first time. You don't look away."

The river said nothing. The birds had gone quiet somewhere in the green.

"Why?" Coral asked. "Why does it matter whether I look?"

"Because the water keeps what happened," Idris said, "and most people come up this river hoping it will forget on their behalf." He stepped back into his dugout, unhurried, a man with all the time the river had. "I'll be back tomorrow at first light. Decide who's brave and who's just hungry. They look similar from the bank, but they behave very differently in the water."

He poled away into the mist, and the mist closed behind him like a door.

*

That evening the crew argued by lamplight, and Coral sat slightly apart, cleaning instruments that were already clean.

"He knows things," Ren said. "About the washout. Did you hear him? *Keeps what happened.*"

"He knows river gossip," Marsh said. Marsh was Ulyana's second, assigned to the expedition as liaison, a neat careful man with neat careful hands who had spent three days being helpful in the specific way of a man cataloguing weaknesses. "Superstition and grievance. Half these valley people blame the company for weather."

"The company did give them cause," Odile said mildly, and Marsh smiled his neat smile and said nothing, and Coral noted the nothing, filed it.

Later, when the others slept, she walked down to the water alone. The scow lay against its mooring, breathing with the current, and she knelt and pressed her own thumb into the planking where Idris had shown them. Soft. Softer than soft — there was rot in the frame beneath, she could feel it, the structural kind, the kind that holds until exactly the moment it must hold most.

Ulyana had certified this boat. Ulyana, who did nothing without calculation, who had once — eleven years ago, in another life — been the only person who wrote to Coral after the washout, a letter of five lines, four of them knives and the fifth, strangely, almost kind.

*A trap,* Coral thought. Or a test. With Ulyana the two had always shared a border.

Out on the black water, something large turned over and sank again. Upstream, in the high country she couldn't see, the rain was already falling on ground that would send it down to her, patient and total, keeping every record it had ever taken.

First light came gray and early. Idris was waiting at the bank, pole in hand, and behind him the mist was lifting off the river in slow sheets, and Coral heard herself say the words before she had fully decided them:

"Load the boat."

Ren grinned. Odile sighed. Marsh wrote something in his little notebook.

And Idris looked at her — that long measuring look, the ledger opening — and said, quietly enough that only she could hear:

"When we reach Kilometer Forty-One, surveyor, remember what I asked. The water's been waiting eleven years to see if you would."
