## 5. What the Water Keeps

The river told Idris things the way other people told secrets—slowly, and only if you already knew how to listen.

He had been listening for forty-one years, since the morning his father set him in the bow of a ferry no bigger than a coffin and said, *The water is older than your name. Be polite to it.* He had been polite ever since. He knew where the current ran fat and where it ran thin, where the gravel bars shifted after every flood like furniture in a house owned by a restless ghost, where the eddies spun counterclockwise in dry years and clockwise in wet ones, as if the river itself could not make up its mind about which direction the past should flow.

This was going to be a wet year. He had known it since February, when the snowmelt came down the high valleys brown instead of white, carrying soil that should have stayed put until April. He had known it when the frogs started calling three weeks early, a chorus so loud along the oxbow that the dogs in the river settlements went hoarse trying to answer. And he had known it, most of all, when the old men at the ferry landing stopped joking about the water and started looking at it instead. That was the surest sign there was. When the men who had laughed at the river for sixty years went quiet, it was time to count your boats and your children and make sure you had more of the latter than the former.

So when the survey crew came down from the rail camp with their brass instruments and their folded maps and their hired boat that sat wrong in the water, Idris took one look and said what he always said to people who brought the wrong boat to his river.

"You'll drown in that."

The woman in charge—Sterling, the one with the burned look around her eyes, the one whose name the older boatmen still said in a lowered voice, the way you said the name of a man who had died badly—hadn't flinched. She'd looked at the boat, then at him, and said, "Then you'll row."

He had. He was rowing them now, three days upriver from the landing, and the boat Ulyana Voss's company had provided was worse than he'd told them. He could feel it through the oars, a softness in the stroke, a give where there should have been none. The planking below the waterline had been soaked and dried and soaked again until the wood had forgotten what it was for. Caulking squeezed out of the seams like old grout. Somebody had painted the hull recently—he could still smell the linseed—but paint over rot was a lie you told with a brush, and the river did not accept lies. The river accepted weight, and current, and time. Nothing else.

*Somebody wanted this boat to look sound,* he thought, and let the thought sink without pulling it up. He was not a man who pulled thoughts up before their time. The river would bring them back around.

* * *

The drowned trestles began at the second bend past the burned landing, where the valley narrowed and the water quickened from green to the color of old bottle glass.

"Keep her bow into the seam," Idris said, nodding at the faint line where two currents met and argued. "There's deadwood under the surface on the left. The river keeps it there. It's been there eleven years and it'll be there eleven more."

Sterling leaned forward. "Eleven years. You've run this stretch that long?"

"I've run it longer than that." He feathered the oars, feeling the boat answer, feeling the rotten planking flex beneath his feet like something breathing. "But eleven years ago I stopped running it in flood season. Eleven years ago the river took a piece of railroad and eleven men with it, and after that I decided the river and I would keep different schedules."

He felt the boat go still in the way a boat goes still when everyone aboard stops moving without agreeing to. The young one with the freckles—Quist, they called her, the level-runner—stopped chewing. The old surveyor, Okafor, set down the cup of tea he'd been nursing since morning. And Sterling looked up at the trestle.

It rose out of the water ahead of them like the ribcage of something enormous that had lain down in the river and never gotten up. Blackened timbers, furred with moss, tilted at angles no engineer had drawn. Two spans still stood above the waterline, holding nothing, going nowhere—the track on them long since twisted into the current. The third span was simply *gone*, sheared off at the waterline, and Idris knew without looking that the pilings beneath it were still down there, a submerged forest of broken teeth that had holed three boats since the washout and would hole a fourth if somebody got careless.

"Rivergate trestle," he said. "Or what the river left of it."

Sterling's voice came out flat and careful, a voice walking on ice. "The report said the trestle failed first. The abutments washed, then the spans came down."

Idris turned and looked at her. He was not a rude man, but he was a river man, and river men did not soften a thing that needed saying.

"The report," he said, "was written by men who never put a boat in this water."

* * *

They made camp that night on the gravel bar below the trestle, on ground that would be under four feet of water within the month. Idris built the fire where the driftwood line said the floods reached in ordinary years—a habit, a kind of argument with the river, *this much and no more*—and the crew gathered around it the way tired people do, shoulders touching, a family made of choice rather than blood. He watched them while he cooked the fish he'd taken from the set lines at dawn. He had ferried survey crews before. They were usually strangers to one another even after months in the field, all elbows and ledgers. These were not. These passed the salt without being asked. These knew which one of them wanted the tea strengthened and which one wanted it left alone.

*These are the ones who took her blame,* he thought, looking at Sterling's profile against the fire. *Or she took theirs. Out here the story changes depending on which bank you stand on.*

"The old people upstream," he said, because it was time to say it, and he was a man who did things in their time, "still call this bend the Weeping Reach. Before the railroad, before any of you were born, the river used to change course here every few years. Cut a new channel, leave the old one to silt. The valley people built nothing permanent on this bank. Nothing. Not a house, not a shrine, not a fence. The river owned this bend, and everyone knew it."

Okafor stirred the fire. "And the railroad came anyway."

"The railroad came anyway." Idris turned a fish on the coals. "The engineers who surveyed it were good men. I ferried two of them. They knew the bend was unstable. They wrote it down—I saw the survey notes once, when I was a boy, in the company office at the landing, before they burned the old records. They recommended a bridge two miles downstream, on bedrock. Solid ground. Ground that holds."

Sterling had gone very still. "The line was built here instead."

"Cheaper here," Idris said. "Shorter here. And the man who approved it wanted the line opened before the dry season ended, because his bonus depended on it." He looked up. "That's not in your report either, is it."

"It's not in *any* report I've seen."

"No. Reports are written on paper, and paper dries out and blows away. The river writes on different paper." He gestured with the fish fork at the dark water sliding past the gravel bar, at the drowned trestle black against the last light. "The river's copy is still there. Ask it."

* * *

He told them the rest in the morning, on the water, because some things were better said in the place where they happened, and because he wanted to watch Sterling's face when he said them.

"The washout didn't come down the valley the way the company said. The company story is a wall of water from the storm, a wall that took the trestle in one blow. That's what the newspapers printed. A wall of water. Very dramatic. Very nobody's fault." He shipped the oars and let the boat drift through the seam, the current doing the work, the rotten hull flexing under his boots. "But I was on the river that night. I was nineteen. I had a boat and a torch and no sense, and I went out to see what the noise was."

The crew had stopped pretending to work. Even the instruments were lashed down. Quist had her hands folded in her lap like a girl in a schoolroom.

"The trestle didn't fail all at once. It failed in pieces, over an hour. I could hear it. Timbers going one at a time, like something cracking its knuckles in the dark." Idris pointed upstream, to where a scar in the valley wall showed pale through the green—the old slide, healed over with eleven years of brush but still legible if you knew how to read ground. "That slope let go first. Not from the rain. From the cut. The railroad cut into the toe of that slope to save four hundred yards of track, and for nine years the slope held, and then one night in a rain no worse than a dozen others, it didn't. The slide took out the abutment on the upstream side. The spans didn't wash away. They *fell*, into water that was barely over its banks. The flood came after, and the flood got the blame, because the flood couldn't be sued."

He watched Sterling. He had ferried enough people to know the look of a woman hearing something she had half-known for years and never let herself finish knowing. Her knuckles were white on the gunwale. Her eyes were on the pale scar in the hillside as if she could read it, and he thought: *she can. She's a ground-reader. That's her whole trade.*

"The eleven men," she said. Her voice was rough. "The report said they were lost when the trestle went. That it was sudden. That they never—"

"Two of them drowned when the spans fell. The rest were on the track above the slide, and they spent the better part of an hour trying to warn the night train before it came through." Idris let the boat drift a moment longer, then took up the oars. "The train was warned. A brakeman ran two miles down the bank with a torch and flagged it down at the lower signal. That man's name was Aldous. He lived in the valley afterward. He drank at my uncle's table. He never went near a railroad again, but every year on the anniversary he walked down to the trestle and stood there until dark, and once he told me—" Idris paused, choosing the words the way you chose footing in a scree field, "—once he told me that the report had it wrong, and that he'd signed what they told him to sign, because the men who signed otherwise lost their contracts, and the men who lost their contracts had families."

The boat creaked. The river slid by, brown-green and patient.

"Aldous," Sterling said. Just the name. Nothing else. But the way she said it was the way you say the name of a man whose grave you've been standing on without knowing it.

"He's dead now," Idris said gently. "Nine years. But his field notes aren't. He kept his own book—every reading, every cut slope, every warning he filed that got refused. His daughter has it, up at the mission on the high bank. She's kept it eleven years waiting for somebody to ask." He looked at Sterling, and then at the water, because some looks were too heavy to hold. "The river kept its copy. He kept his. Paper dries and blows away, but not all paper. Some paper was written by a man who wanted it found."

* * *

They ran the Weeping Reach that afternoon, past the drowned trestle, past the scar on the slope, past the lower signal post where the brakeman's torch had once stopped a train full of sleeping people. Idris rowed and said nothing, because he had said what the river had told him to say, and the rest was not his to carry.

But he watched Sterling, and what he saw troubled him more than the rotten hull, more than the early monsoon, more than the softness in the planking that he had not yet told her about.

She wasn't grieving. Grief he knew how to row people through.

She was *recalculating*. Every landmark they passed, her eyes went to it, then to the map, then to something behind her own eyes, and each time her jaw set a little harder. She was a woman discovering that the ground she'd been standing on for eleven years was not ground at all, and a ground-reader who learns that does one of two things: she sits down, or she starts surveying the truth.

By the look of her, Coral Sterling was going to start surveying.

And Idris, poling the rotten boat through the seam where two currents argued, thought of the hull flexing under his feet, and the brown snowmelt in the high valleys, and the old men gone quiet at the landing, and he understood that all of it—the boat, the season, the job, the woman with the burned eyes—was converging on the same bend of the same river at the same time, the way water converges before a flood.

The river was going to write its copy again this year. The only question was who would be holding the paper.
