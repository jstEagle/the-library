## 6. The Leaking Boat

The storm came up the valley like something with a grudge.

Coral felt it before she saw it—a pressure in her back teeth, a hush along the water that made even Idris stop talking. He had been mid-sentence, explaining how the river braided and re-braided around the drowned trestles of the old line, when he went quiet and tilted his head, listening to a sky that hadn't made a sound yet.

"Get the canvas on," he said. "Now."

They were three hours below the last camp, deep in the gorge where the cliffs leaned close enough to trade echoes. The river ran green-black and muscular between them, swollen with rain that had fallen days ago in country they'd never see. The boat—Ulyana's boat, the one delivered with a bow tied on it like a joke nobody laughed at—rode low already, riding lower every hour, though Coral had told herself it was just the weight of their gear. Six of them in her: Coral, Okafor, Junie, Pell, young Tam at the tiller, and Idris standing at the bow like a figurehead who happened to know where all the bodies were buried. Literally, possibly. The water had taken plenty.

The first drops hit like flung gravel. Then the sky simply opened, the way monsoon skies do—not raining so much as relocating an ocean—and the world shrank to the size of the boat.

"Bail!" Coral shouted, and the word was almost instantly absurd, because they were all going to be bailing soon enough without being told.

* * *

It started as a trickle they could ignore. Junie found it first, crouched amidships with her hand flat against the planking, feeling for the seam she'd been suspicious of since the headwaters. She'd said nothing then because saying nothing was the pact, but now her face came up pale through the drumming dark.

"Coral. She's weeping."

"Weeping where?"

"Everywhere." Junie's voice had the terrible calm of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't want finished. "The caulking's gone to dust. This boat wasn't rotten, Coral. She was *hollowed*. Somebody scraped the seams and filled them with something that would hold for exactly as long as it needed to."

Lightning walked down the far cliff and the thunder arrived late, rolling through the gorge like furniture being moved in a giant's house. In the flash, Coral saw them all: Okafor bailing with the dented kettle they cooked rice in, Pell passing buckets, Tam white-knuckled on the tiller, Idris reading the water ahead with an expression that had stopped being caution and become something older. Prayer, maybe. Or apology.

"Radio," Coral said. "Marsh's radio. Get it up, get a call to base camp—"

"It won't matter," Idris called back over his shoulder, not turning. "In this gorge, in this weather? No signal under these walls. You'd be shouting into stone."

"Then we shout anyway!"

But when Pell cracked open the case Ulyana's expedition had provided—the good radio, the expensive one, the one Marsh had insisted on packing himself and carrying himself and never once letting anyone else touch—the look on his face stopped Coral's heart before the river got the chance.

"The aerial's gone," Pell said. He held up the coiled wire, snapped clean near the base. Not frayed. Snapped. Cut, if you knew what a cut looked like, and Pell had been a signals man in another life. "And the spare batteries are full of sand. Packed sand, Coral. Someone poured it in through the vent holes."

The rain filled the silence for a moment, which was generous of it.

"Marsh," Tam said from the tiller. Just the name, nothing else, the way you'd name a disease.

"He's not here," Okafor said, still bailing, steady as a metronome. "He stayed at base camp. With the second radio. And the maps. And—" He paused, kettle halfway through its arc, water sheeting off it. "And the only other way anyone knows where we are."

Coral stood very still in the pitching dark and let the thought finish itself, because no one else was going to say it out loud.

Ulyana Voss had given them a hollowed boat, a gutted radio, and a route through monsoon country in a season that everyone—including Idris, including the ferryman's own grandmother, including probably the fish—had known was coming early. And Marsh, her careful, soft-spoken second, had packed the sabotage himself, smiling, handing out coffee.

This was never a survey. It was a funeral with a contract attached.

"She wanted us dead?" Junie's voice cracked on it. Not fear—something worse. Wounded arithmetic. "Triple pay. She sat across from me and offered triple pay and wrote out terms in her own hand—"

"No." Coral heard herself say it and surprised herself with how certain it sounded. "No, listen to me. If Voss wanted us dead, there are cheaper ways than a river. This isn't murder. It's—" She searched for the word and hated the one she found. "*Insurance.* We're not meant to die. We're meant to fail. To fail catastrophically, publicly, with our names on the manifest. The Rivergate survey lost in high water—another crew that bit off more than the river could chew. Another reason the line stays closed, another report with our deaths or our ruin as the footnote proving the crossing can't be done." Her jaw ached; she realized she'd been clenching it since the first drop fell. "She needs the Rivergate to stay impossible. Because if it's possible, someone will ask why it was ever abandoned. And the answer to that question is sitting in a sealed file with her company's stamp on it."

Thunder again. The boat lurched as the current grabbed a submerged log and spat it past them close enough to touch. Idris swore in three languages and steered into it rather than away, which seemed wrong until Coral understood: he was aiming for the slack water behind it.

"Debate later," he called. "Bail now. Grief later. Bail now."

So they bailed.

* * *

The night is hard to describe honestly, because honest description sounds like exaggeration.

They ran the gorge by lightning-flash, Idris calling the water in a low continuous stream—*left channel, hard left, rock garden, trust me, LEFT*—while the rest of them formed a bucket chain that became less a technique than a religion. Okafor bailed. Junie passed. Pell passed. Coral took the lowest station, kneeling in six inches of freezing river inside the hull, pressing her palms against the planking to feel for the next seam to give. Tam steered when Idris let him and prayed when he didn't, which was most of the time.

Somewhere past midnight, the second seam opened.

Coral felt it under her hands like a bone cracking—a long shudder through the hull, and then the trickle became a voice, a cold insistent whispering up through the boards. She jammed her jacket into it. When that soaked through, she jammed her shirt. When that soaked through, she pressed her bare forearm against the gap and held it there, skin against wood, and the river drank from her instead.

"Coral." Okafor's hand on her shoulder, pulling her back. "You'll freeze."

"I'm holding the boat together with my arm, Okafor. Freezing is a later problem."

"Then we rotate." He knelt beside her, rolled his sleeve, and pressed his own arm to the seam above hers. "Family rotates."

And that—that stupid, stubborn, two-word sentence—is what Coral remembered afterward, more than the lightning, more than the roar of the rapids, more than the moment the boat clipped a drowned piling and spun a full turn with the bow swinging out over black air. *Family rotates.* No pact written down, no oath sworn, just six people in a leaking coffin of a boat deciding, without discussion, that no one would hold the cold alone.

They passed the drowned trestle at two in the morning. Coral knew it was the trestle because Idris told her, and she knew Idris was right because she could feel it—a change in the river's voice, a widening, the water spreading confused over pilings that had stood in this current for twenty years, holding up nothing. In a lightning flash she saw them: black teeth rising from the flood, and caught in the highest one, snagged and bleached, a length of chain and part of a winch drum. Aldous's equipment. Aldous's river. The place where the record said her mentor's error had killed him, and where Idris's quiet stories had begun insisting otherwise.

She looked at it for one flash, two, and then the dark took it back, and she put her arm back against the seam and kept bailing.

* * *

Dawn came gray and merciful and found them wedged in an eddy behind a gravel bar, the boat half-sunk and tied to a cottonwood, everyone alive, everything important soaked. They lay on the bar like wreckage themselves while Idris built a fire out of wet wood through some alchemy he refused to explain, and Junie counted heads twice, and Tam laughed once—a single wild bark of a laugh, the kind that means *I am going to live and I don't know what to do with that yet.*

Coral stood at the waterline, wringing out her jacket, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding.

The boat was done. The radio was done. The maps were at base camp with Marsh. The monsoon had weeks left in it, maybe months. By any reasonable measure, the survey was over, the contract voided by the river itself, and the smart move—the invisible move, the pact-honoring move—was to walk out, take the loss, and never speak of the Rivergate again.

That was the old code. Take obscure jobs. Stay unseen. Never look back.

She turned and looked at her crew. Okafor was asleep sitting up, mid-sentence somehow. Junie was mending a torn pack strap with dental floss. Pell had salvaged the radio's guts and was laying them out on a rock like a surgeon studying a patient he intended to save. Tam was sketching the gorge in his notebook, because Tam sketched everything, even the things that tried to kill him.

They weren't looking back. But they weren't leaving, either. She could see it in the set of their shoulders. They were waiting to hear what she'd decided, and whatever it was, they'd follow it into the water again.

"Voss sent us out here to fail," Coral said quietly. Not to anyone. To the river, maybe. To Aldous. "And she nearly got her wish."

Idris crouched at the fire, feeding it sticks. "Nearly," he agreed. "But the river does funny accounting. It takes what it takes and gives back what it gives." He nodded upstream, toward the drowned trestle, toward the gate beyond it. "Storm's pushed everything loose. Come high-water recession, things wash out of the banks that have been buried twenty years. Boxes. Records. A man's field notes, if a man was careless about where he cached them." His eyes came up to hers, dark and level. "If a man suspected the report about him would be a lie, and hid the truth somewhere only the river would know to keep it."

Coral's breath went out of her slowly.

"You know where Aldous cached his notes."

"I know where the water keeps things," Idris said. "Same thing, most years."

Above them the clouds were already rebuilding, stacking themselves against the peaks for the next assault. The river kept rising. Somewhere downstream, at the old gate structure, the flood was climbing toward its peak—and somewhere at base camp, Marsh was reporting them lost.

Coral looked at her crew, asleep and mending and salvaging on the gravel bar, and made the decision the pact had never allowed her to make before.

"We're not walking out," she said. "We're going to the gate. Before the water drops. Before Marsh decides we're dead enough to be official."

Junie didn't look up from her dental floss. "Knew you'd say that," she said. "Pell's fixing the radio. We go tonight."
