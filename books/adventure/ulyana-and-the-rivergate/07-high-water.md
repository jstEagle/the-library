## 7. High Water

The river had been talking to her for two days.

Ulyana Voss noticed it first as a change in pitch—a low note beneath the usual murmur, the way a train changes its voice when it takes a grade too steep. By the second morning the note had become a sentence, and by evening it was an argument. The Kessume did not rise politely. It rose like a creditor.

Base camp sat on a gravel bench forty feet above the waterline, or what the survey maps insisted was the waterline. The maps were drawn in a dry year by men who never came back in a wet one. Now the brown water had climbed the bank, swallowed the willow scrub, and was working patiently at the tent stakes of the supply line, pulling them loose one thread at a time, the way water does everything—without hurry, without malice, without once considering that you might have other plans.

"Radio's still dead," Marsh said from the doorway of the equipment tent. He stood with his collar up and his jaw set at the angle he used when he wanted her to know he'd already decided something. "Antenna's down. Could be the storm on the ridge. Could be."

"Could be," Ulyana said, and let him hear how much she believed him.

He left. She watched him pick his way across the mud toward the cook shelter, stepping exactly where the ground was firmest, because Marsh always knew where the ground was firmest. It was one of the things she'd hired him for. She was beginning to compile a list of things she should have asked about instead.

The rain came down in long gray sheets off the escarpment, and through it she could see the far bank only as a suggestion, a charcoal smudge where the old Rivergate alignment climbed toward the pass. Somewhere upriver—two days' run in Idris's boat, if the boat still floated, if the crew still lived—Coral Sterling was doing precisely what Ulyana had paid her to do, which was to be out on the water when the water turned.

She had told herself it wasn't sabotage. She had told herself a great many things in the last three weeks, and they had all had the smooth professional texture of things that are technically true. The contract specified a flood-season survey. The insurance riders specified the risks. The rotten hull was an oversight; the failing radio was weather. If she repeated the list often enough, in the right order, it almost held together, the way a dam holds together right up until the moment it doesn't.

She went into her own tent, the large one at the camp's center, and opened the field chest she had carried from the company archive without telling anyone why.

Aldous Renner's notes.

She had pulled them six weeks ago, on a night much like this one, in an office that smelled of cold coffee and other people's mistakes. Her stated reason had been due diligence: before spending company money reopening the Rivergate, review all prior documentation. Her actual reason she had not written down anywhere, because her actual reason had a name and a date and a body count, and Ulyana Voss did not put things like that in writing.

Now, with the river arguing below and nothing left to do but wait, she finally untied the oilcloth.

* * *

The notebooks were smaller than she remembered them being, though she had never actually held them before—only the typed summary, the clean official digest that the company produced after the washout, eleven years ago, the document that ended Aldous's career posthumously and Coral Sterling's career publicly. The summary was four pages. The notebooks were nine. Nobody had ever read all nine, or if they had, they had preferred not to mention it.

His handwriting started neat. Surveyor's hand, every bearing recorded to the minute, every elevation cross-checked twice. *Left bank cutbank unstable above km 14,* he wrote in April of that year. *Recommend riprap before any ballast is laid.* And further on, in May: *Company directive received re: schedule acceleration. Expressed concerns re: spring freshet timing. Concerns noted and overruled.*

Ulyana turned the page slowly.

*Overruled again,* he wrote. *Told that delay costs exceed risk costs. Asked who calculated risk costs. Was told not to ask.*

There it was, in a dead man's level hand: the whole architecture of the disaster, built beam by beam in blue-black ink while everyone downstream went on believing the river was the problem. The washout had not been an act of God. It had been a decision—one made in a boardroom she now occupied, by men whose signatures were probably still in some drawer of hers, ordering ballast laid against his recommendation so the line could open before a rival's line did, so that a quarterly number would look the way a quarterly number needed to look.

*Freshet early this year,* Aldous wrote in June, three weeks before the end. *Snowpack heavy. Have moved my crew's camp to high ground regardless of directive. Will not ask them to sleep below the cutbank. If the company wants someone sleeping there, the company can send someone.*

He had saved his crew. That was the thing the official record got most elegantly wrong. The report described a night of chaos, a camp caught unprepared, a section chief—Coral Sterling, then twenty-six years old, Aldous's protégée—whose failure to evacuate had cost Aldous his life when he went back for stragglers. Four pages of clean type, and nowhere in them the fact that the camp had been moved, deliberately, against orders, by the woman the report blamed.

Ulyana set the notebook on her knee and looked at the rain for a while.

Because here was the arithmetic she could not avoid: if the camp was moved to high ground, then the only person who went back down to the water that night—the only person the flood found where the report said everyone had been—was Aldous himself. And the only way the report could blame Coral Sterling for his death was if someone needed her to be guilty. Someone needed a name on the failure that wasn't the company's name.

She remembered the year after the washout. She had been young then too, hungry in a different direction, watching the Rivergate collapse take down careers the way the flood had taken down trestles. She remembered thinking, with the cold clarity she had mistaken back then for intelligence, that Coral Sterling must have been incompetent—that no one took a fall that complete unless they'd earned it. She had built part of her own rise on that certainty. There had been dinners, in those years, where she had said the name *Sterling* in a certain tone and watched doors open.

And all along, the truth had been sitting in a box in the archive, in nine notebooks nobody wanted to read, because reading them would have cost the company everything the lie had protected.

She kept turning pages. Near the end, the handwriting loosened, hurried, the entries compressed:

*Sterling refused to sign the acceleration order. Told her it would go in her file. She said put it there.*

*Offered her the section chief's chair if she'd carry the evacuation narrative. She laughed at me. Actually laughed.*

Then, the last entry, dated four days before the flood:

*If anything happens, the girl takes none of it. I'll see to that. But I am old and the company is patient, and I suspect patience wins. If it does—if they hang this on her anyway—then whoever finds these books should know: she was the only one in that valley telling the truth, and she knew what it would cost her, and she paid it so the rest of us could keep our first contract and our names. That is not incompetence. Write that down. Somebody write that down.*

Ulyana closed the notebook.

Outside, the river had changed its argument into something simpler and louder. A shout went up from the cook shelter—the near tie-rack was gone, simply gone, swept off the bench as if it had never existed. Lanterns swung in the rain. Marsh's voice cut through it, giving orders, sounding competent, sounding exactly like a man who had planned for the radio to fail.

She sat alone in the lamplight with a dead man's handwriting and understood, at last, the full shape of what she had done. Not the sabotage—she could almost forgive herself the sabotage; that was just business, ugly but legible. What she could not forgive was the reason. She had hired Coral Sterling to run a doomed survey not merely to bury the Rivergate question, but because some part of her had wanted to watch the great Coral Sterling fail at last, fail at the river that ruined her, confirm the story Ulyana had dined out on for a decade. She had spent triple pay to purchase a confirmation of a lie she hadn't known was a lie.

Guilt arrived, punctual as ever, too late to be useful and too heavy to set down.

* * *

Near midnight the water reached the cook shelter's floorboards, and the camp became a small lit island doing arithmetic with sandbags. Ulyana worked alongside the laborers without being asked to, hauling bags in the rain until her shoulders burned, because her hands needed a job that wasn't thinking.

It was past two when she came back into her tent, soaked, and found Marsh waiting inside. He had dried himself. He had helped himself to her coffee. He sat in her chair with Aldous's ninth notebook open on his knee, and the sight of it went through her like a spike through a rail joint.

"You've been reading," he said.

"Give it back."

"I've been reading it longer than you think." He turned it around so she could see the last entry, the one about the girl. "You know what this is? This is the end of the company's indemnity claim. The falsification finding voids the settlement, voids the land option, voids everything we're out here pretending to protect." He closed the book and held it loosely, the way he held everything, as if it were already his. "So the question is whether these books reach the surface. Accidents happen on rivers. Especially in flood season."

The lantern hissed. Underneath everything, the river spoke its single enormous syllable.

"And Sterling?" Ulyana said quietly. "Is she part of the accident too?"

Marsh smiled with great reasonableness. "Sterling's already out there. I'm sure the water will sort her out. It has a talent for it—you'd know, you counted on it."

She heard it then, plainly, without the smooth professional texture: he had known about the falsification all along. He had been the company's patience, all these years, keeping the archive quiet, keeping the summary clean—and she had handed him a flood and a sabotaged radio and a rival on the water, and called it strategy.

Marsh stood, tucked the notebook inside his coat, and paused at the tent flap.

"Get some sleep, Miss Voss. Big day tomorrow. The gate structure won't hold past noon if the gauge keeps climbing—and I'd hate for anyone to be standing on it when it goes."

The flap fell shut behind him. Ulyana stood in the lamplight, dripping, and understood that whatever happened next would happen fast, upriver, in the dark—and that the only people who might still stop it were a woman she had tried to drown in her own history, and a ferryman who read water better than any map she owned.
