## 8. Rivergate

The river told them before the sky did.

Coral heard it first as a change in the boat's rhythm—a stutter in the current, like a pulse skipping—and then Idris's hand closed on her shoulder, and his voice came low over the rain: *There. That's the gate water.*

She looked up and the world went white with it. The Rivergate gorge opened ahead of them like a wound, walls of black basalt sweating in the downpour, and through the middle of it the old gate structure stood—or what remained of it. Two stone piers, a shattered spillway, the bones of a trestle clawing out of the flood like the ribs of something that had drowned a decade ago and never been allowed to rest. Water hit the piers and climbed them in ropes of foam, higher than any survey marker, higher than the high-water line Aldous had penciled into his field notes with that dry little joke of his: *If the river reads this, it wins.*

It was winning now.

"Hold her into the eddy!" Coral shouted, and the crew moved without needing the words repeated—Wren on the bow line, Sefa and Okafor bailing with anything that held water, Tam hauling on the sweep oar until the wood groaned. They'd been bailing since midnight. The hull below the waterline was a lattice of splits Ulyana's "survey-grade" boat had grown on the first day, patched with tar and prayer, and every hour the river rose another hand's width and the patches gave a little more.

Idris read the eddy the way other men read print. He pointed with his chin. "Behind the second pier. The stone breaks the current—there's a pocket, dead water, maybe ten feet of it. We come in on the back of the wave, not the face. You steer, I throw."

"You throw what?"

"Us." His teeth flashed, white in the gray light. "One chance. Miss it and the river takes the boat, and we swim, and nobody swims this water twice."

Coral looked back at her crew. Wren's face was sheet-gray but set. Okafor had stopped bailing long enough to knot the document case to Coral's chest with a length of cord, the recovered field notes of Aldous Reeve pressed in oilcloth against her sternum like a second heartbeat. Tam spat over the side, an old riverman's blessing or an old riverman's insult, and said, "Do what the ferryman says, boss. He hasn't been wrong yet."

"Once," Idris said. "I was wrong once."

"When?"

"You'll meet it someday. Steer."

The boat swung toward the gorge.

The noise inside it was total—a sound that wasn't sound so much as pressure, the river's whole weight funneled between the walls and screaming through. Spray hit Coral's face like thrown gravel. She picked her line through the chaos the way Idris called it—*left of the boil, left, now hard right where the stone shoulders out*—and the boat climbed a standing wave that shouldn't have existed and slammed down the other side hard enough to crack a thwart. Then the pier was there, huge and wet and streaming, and the dead-water pocket opened behind it like a held breath, and Idris threw the bow line around a rusted stanchion with one motion that had clearly been performed a thousand times on a thousand rivers, and they were in.

Stillness, of a kind. Rain drumming. The boat shuddering against its line. The gorge roaring past them close enough to touch.

Coral put her hand on the pier stone. It was warm. That was the thing nobody believed about the Rivergate—the water came down off the high country so cold it ached, but the basalt held the summer, and the stone under her palm was blood-warm, alive, like touching the flank of a sleeping animal. Aldous had written that too. *The gate is warm. Everything else about it lies.*

"Boss." Tam's voice, flat and careful. "Look up."

The catwalk.

It had been a maintenance walkway once, bolted to the upstream face of the eastern pier, running from a stone landing near the waterline up to the old gatehouse at the top—a climb of maybe eighty feet, iron and rot and rust, half of it hanging loose over the flood. And on it, halfway up, moving with the desperate speed of a man who knew exactly what was below him and didn't care, was Marsh.

He had a canvas satchel slung across his back, and Coral knew what was in it, because she had counted the notes last night at the camp they'd found—Aldous's originals, pulled from the dry-stone cache Idris had led them to, the cache the official record said had been lost with everything else. Aldous's hand. Aldous's numbers. Aldous's account of the washout, written in the hours before the water took him, naming the falsified gauge readings, naming the company men who'd ordered them, naming the girl on his crew who'd signed the report under threat because the alternative was that forty people lost their first contract and their first winter's bread.

Naming Coral. Not as the cause. As the scapegoat.

"He's going for the gatehouse," Wren said. "There'll be a stove up there. He'll burn them."

"He'll burn ten years of my life because Ulyana's afraid of ten pages of paper." Coral was already out of the boat, boots on wet stone, cord of the document case biting her chest. "Idris—keep them alive. That's the job now. Not the survey. Them."

"And you?"

Coral looked up at the catwalk, swaying, dissolving in the rain. "Me," she said, "I'm going to go be honest for once."

* * *

The iron sang under her hands.

Every rung was a decision. The bolts had wept rust down the pier face in long orange streaks, and where the catwalk's brackets met stone, the stone itself had been cracked by the flood years and patched by men who'd known they were patching a corpse. Halfway up, a whole section of railing hung out over the water like a door blown open, and Coral went through the gap on the inside, hugging the pier, rain driving into her teeth. Below her the river ran white and total. She did not look at it. Aldous had taught her that—*the water is a liar, Coral-girl. It shows you the drowning and hides the swimming. Look at your hands. Your hands know the work.*

Her hands knew the work. She climbed.

Marsh heard her before he saw her—she saw him hear her, saw his shoulders bunch—and he turned on the narrow walk with the satchel clutched to him like a child, and his face was not cruel. That was the worst of it. His face was frightened, and tired, and loyal to something that had never once been loyal to him.

"Stop there, Sterling." He had a voice made for boardrooms and it cracked anyway. "You come one step and I drop them. All of it. Right into the water."

"Then drop them." Coral kept climbing. "You think that river hasn't already eaten the truth once? It ate Aldous. It ate the trestle, the town's trust, my name. It's been eating this for ten years, Marsh, and it's still hungry, and you're standing on its throat with a bag of paper thinking paper is what matters."

"You don't understand what's at stake—"

"No," she said, and she was close now, close enough to see the rain running off his jaw, close enough to see his hands shaking on the satchel straps. "I understand exactly. Because ten years ago I stood where you're standing. Ulyana handed me a report and a choice, and I signed the lie because the truth was going to cost forty people everything, and I have been paying for that signature every single day since, and here is what I learned, Marsh, and I'll give it to you free: the lie doesn't protect anyone. It just picks who gets eaten."

The catwalk moved.

It wasn't wind. It was the river—the flood peak, arriving exactly when Idris had said it would, the water rising through the gate structure with a sound like a mountain clearing its throat. A wave came up the spillway channel and hit the pier, and the whole eastern face shuddered, and a bracket below them let go with a crack like a rifle. The catwalk dropped two inches. The railing Marsh had been leaning against swung out over the flood and hung there, and Marsh went with it—satchel and all, one hand on the loose rail, boots skidding on wet iron, nothing under him but white water.

Coral grabbed his arm.

The physics of it were insane—her braced against the pier, his whole weight plus the satchel swinging out over the flood, the rail bending, the bolts shrieking—and for one long second the river had them both, and Coral felt with total clarity what Aldous must have felt in his last minute: the water below, patient and enormous, entirely willing to wait.

"Let go of the bag," she shouted.

"I can't—"

"Marsh. *Let go of the bag.*"

He looked at her. And she watched the arithmetic finish behind his eyes—the company, the pay, the sealed records, Ulyana's voice in his head telling him what was at stake—and then she watched something else win, some older arithmetic, the one about not dying on a river with a stranger's hand on your arm. His fingers opened. The satchel dropped, turned once in the air, and the flood took it without a ripple of ceremony.

Then she hauled, and Wren was suddenly there above them—God knew how Wren had climbed the far side, Wren who was afraid of heights, Wren who had never once in six years let Coral be the only one on a rope—and together they dragged Marsh back onto the walk, and the rail tore free behind him and cartwheeled down into the gorge and was gone.

They lay on the iron, four hands and a traitor, while the flood peak went through the gate below them like a train through a station.

* * *

They came down to dead water and the boat, and Idris took one look at Marsh's face and said nothing, which was its own verdict and its own mercy. The satchel was gone. The originals were gone. Aldous's hand, gone into the same water that had taken Aldous.

And Coral found that her chest, where the case hung, was light.

Because the case wasn't empty. Because the night before, in the dry-stone cache, she had sat up by lamplight and copied every page in her own hand—slow, careful, surveyor's lettering, the numbers checked twice against her memory of the original report she'd signed, every discrepancy marked. She had told no one. She hadn't known why, then. Now she knew: some part of her had understood, ten years late, that the river always takes the original. That was its nature. The truth was never meant to be kept in a box. It was meant to be carried out, in a body, by someone willing to be the copy.

"Boss," Wren said quietly. "The records Ulyana's holding. The sealed incident files. You could still trade. Your name for the contract, clean, the way she offered. Nobody would ever know what you just did up there."

Coral stood in the rain at the foot of the warm stone, and the flood was falling now, already falling, the river spending its anger the way rivers do, and she thought about the pact—the code that had kept them alive for six years: take obscure jobs, stay invisible, never look back. Never look back. She had built a whole life on the assumption that the past was a country you could simply decline to enter.

But the past had a ferryman, and he was standing right in front of her, wringing out his hat.

"No," she said. "No trades. No sealed records. When we get downriver, I'm going to stand in front of every man and woman on this water and read Aldous's numbers out loud, my name included, all of it—and Ulyana Voss can keep her contract, because I'm done being bought, and I'm done being invisible, and I am *done* letting a dead man's handwriting drown twice."

She looked at her crew. Six faces, rain-worn, exhausted, owed triple pay they were about to lose.

"Anybody who wants off the boat at the landing, I'll write the reference myself."

Tam started laughing first—wet, cracked, relieved laughing—and then Wren, and then Okafor, deep as a drum, and Marsh stood apart with his empty hands and did not laugh, and did not ask to stay, and Coral did not ask him either. That would be Ulyana's accounting, not hers.

Idris pushed off from the pier into the easing current, and the Rivergate fell away behind them, and Coral stood at the bow with Aldous's truth pressed against her chest and the first gray light of the flood's second day coming up the gorge like a promise.

Downriver, at base camp, the river was already at Ulyana Voss's doorstep.
