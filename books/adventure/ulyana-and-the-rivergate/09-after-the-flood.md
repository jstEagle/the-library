## 9. After the Flood

The river went down the way a fever breaks—slowly at first, then all at once, leaving behind a landscape that looked like something had died there and been turned over.

Ulyana Voss stood on the platform of the Rivergate base camp and watched the water retreat from the pilings it had swallowed whole. The brown scum line ran six feet up the trestle legs, marking the flood's high tide like a bruise. Downstream, where the old gate structure had stood for forty years, she could see the catwalk hanging in a torn ribbon, one span dropped into the shallows like a broken wing. The crew was down there somewhere—Sterling's people, and the ferryman, hauling what they'd salvaged out of the mud. She could hear them faintly over the water: hammers, voices, someone laughing in a way that sounded like relief rather than joy.

She had not slept. The field notes were still open on her desk inside, Aldous Merrick's cramped handwriting marching across pages gone soft as cloth with damp. She had read them three times through the night, and each time they said the same thing, and each time it landed in her chest like a stone dropped from a great height.

*The cut bank is failing above mile 41. I've wired the company twice. They will not authorize the delay. If we run the survey through as scheduled we are gambling with lives to save a season.*

And then, in a different ink, added later, almost gentle:

*I have told Coral to keep her head down and say nothing. This is my mess. Let it be my name if it must be a name.*

It had never been his name. It had been hers—Coral Sterling's—stamped into the official record in Ulyana's own company's typeface, because a dead man's warnings made poor reading and a scapegoat made excellent ones. Ulyana's father had signed off on the falsified report two weeks before Aldous drowned. She had been twenty-three years old then, already learning to read ledgers the way other people read scripture, and she had signed the routing slip beneath her father's signature without reading past the first page.

She had told herself for eleven years that she hadn't known. That was the architecture of it—the whole elegant structure of her career built on a foundation of *I didn't know*, load-bearing and load-bearing, until last night when the notes came up out of Marsh's satchel dripping and she had known, finally and completely, that she had always known. Some part of her. The part that kept hiring Sterling's crew for jobs nobody wanted. The part that couldn't stop watching her across negotiation tables like a woman watching weather come in.

Marsh knocked at the doorframe. He looked terrible—mud to the knees, a bandage on his forearm, and the particular gray composure of a man who has been caught and knows it.

"Ma'am. The Sterlings are asking whether we're proceeding with the downstream staking once the water drops."

Ulyana closed the field notes. "Are they."

"They're asking because you haven't come down to camp since the storm." He paused. "They're asking because you sent me to sabotage their radio, ma'am, and they know it, and they're being polite about it, which is worse."

"Yes," Ulyana said. "It usually is."

She picked up her coat. Outside, the air smelled of silt and cut grass and the green rot of things the flood had killed, and beneath it, faintly, woodsmoke—someone had already lit a stove, already begun making coffee, already begun the ordinary work of the morning after the end of the world. She walked down through the camp without hurrying. There was no hurry left in her. That was the strange thing; she had expected panic, or fury, or at minimum the cold strategic recalibration that had carried her through every disaster of her life, and instead there was only this flat, clean quiet, like the sky after rain.

Coral Sterling was standing at the edge of the water, sleeves rolled, arguing with the ferryman about the angle of a cable. She looked thinner than she had in the office in the city, and sunburned, and entirely unimpressed by Ulyana's approach—which was, Ulyana reflected, exactly what she deserved.

"Sterling."

"Merrick," Coral said, using the name deliberately, the way you'd press on a bruise to check it was real. "Come to see what your boat did to my crew?"

"Your crew is alive."

"Because of Idris. Not because of your boat. Not because of your man with the wire cutters." Coral turned to face her fully, and her eyes were tired and hard and very clear. "You never wanted the survey. You wanted us on that river during flood season. Why?"

Ulyana had prepared answers on the walk down—deflections, partial truths, the small change of a lifetime's practice. She found she couldn't spend any of them.

"I wanted the records opened," she said. "Aldous's notes. And I knew you'd never go back to Rivergate for money alone, and I knew Marsh would make sure you found them whether you meant to or not, because Marsh does what I pay him to do, which is the ugly arithmetic." She stopped. Started again, plainer. "My company falsified the washout report. My father signed it. I routed it. I was twenty-three and I didn't read past the first page, and I have spent eleven years not reading past the first page. You took the blame to protect your first contract. I let you carry it because it was convenient, and then because it was useful, and then because by then admitting it would have cost me everything I'd built on top of it." She held Coral's gaze. "So. Now you know why I hired you. It wasn't the line."

For a long moment Coral said nothing. Behind her, the ferryman had stopped working and was watching with the frank interest of a man who understood rivers better than conversations but recognized a flood when he saw one.

"You could have just asked me," Coral said finally. "Eleven years, Voss. One letter. *Did you do it?*"

"And if you'd said yes? If you'd said *yes, it was my fault*?" Ulyana shook her head. "Then I'd have gone on believing the report, and it would have been easier, and I would have deserved that ease. I couldn't risk finding out I'd been wrong, so I arranged to find out in a way where I couldn't look away." She glanced at the ruined gate, the torn catwalk, the brown water running quiet now over its drowned bones. "It nearly killed you. All of you. I know what that costs. I'm not going to pretend a retraction balances it."

"No," Coral agreed. "It doesn't."

"No. But it's what I have."

* * *

She did it publicly, because privately was worthless.

Three days later, when the roads dried enough for the company motorcar, she convened the hearing in the Rivergate freight depot—not a hearing, really; there was no board present, no counsel, just a stenographer, a handful of valley landowners who'd lost fences and orchards to the original washout and had waited eleven years for anyone to say a word to them about it, and Coral's crew lined along the wall with their hats in their hands, looking like men and women who had wandered into the wrong church.

Ulyana stood at the front beside the original report, mounted on an easel, its seal bright and official and rotten as the boat.

"This document states that the mile-41 washout resulted from surveyor error in assessing the cut bank," she said. Her voice carried in the tin-roofed room better than she intended. "That statement was false when it was written. The surveyor, Aldous Merrick, identified the failure in writing, twice, and requested a delay that this company denied for reasons of schedule and cost. After his death, this company altered the record to place responsibility on his assistant, Coral Sterling, who was twenty-six years old and had done nothing wrong except work for us." She turned to the easel and, in front of everyone, tore the report from its mounting and folded it in half, and half again, until it was small enough to disappear into her pocket. "This company withdraws its claim on the Rivergate line. The contract with Miss Sterling's crew is dissolved, with full payment rendered for work completed. There will be no grand line through this valley. What gets built here gets built by the people who live here, and I am finished pretending otherwise."

The silence afterward had a texture to it—dust settling, a roof ticking in the heat. An old orchardist near the back began, very quietly, to cry, and did not wipe his face, and nobody looked away from him, which Ulyana understood was the only kindness available.

Marsh was waiting outside afterward, hat in hand, composed as ever.

"You'll want this handled quietly," he said. "References, severance, the usual—"

"You'll want to be on the morning train," Ulyana said. "There's no severance. There's no reference. If I ever hear your name attached to another crew's radio, I will spend the rest of my considerable resources making sure the industry hears about Rivergate, and this time with names in it. Yours first."

He studied her for a moment, weighing her, the way he weighed everything. Then he put on his hat. "You're different," he said, almost curious.

"I'm expensive," Ulyana said. "Go."

* * *

She found Coral at dusk on the depot platform, waiting for nothing in particular, watching the light go amber on the wet hills. Ulyana sat down beside her on the bench, an arm's length away, and for a while neither of them spoke. Eleven years of rivalry sat between them, and it turned out most of it had been scaffolding around this one plain thing.

"The crew voted," Coral said at last. "Before the hearing. Whether to take the dissolution or fight it for breach." She smiled faintly at the rails. "They voted to let it go before you even tore the paper. Turns out they were staying for me, not the money. Took me eleven years to learn that too."

"We're slow learners, Merricks and Sterlings."

"Don't." Coral's voice sharpened, then gentled. "Don't make yourself a Merrick. He'd have hated that."

Ulyana looked out at the valley—at the drowned trestles going soft in the evening haze, at the thread of smoke rising from the ferryman's fire down by the water, at the small stubborn lights of farms coming on one by one along the banks. Somewhere down there, Aldous Merrick's notes were drying on a line inside Coral's tent, page by page, straightening out.

"There's a crossing," she said. "Downstream of the gate. The valley's wanted one for thirty years—a foot-and-freight bridge, nothing grand. There's money in a trust my father set up for community works, blood money by any honest accounting, sitting untouched because no one's ever asked for it properly." She reached into her coat and set a folded paper on the bench between them. Not a contract. A request, unsigned, addressed to no one yet. "I'm not offering you a job. I'm asking if you'll take it to the valley and let them decide. I won't be there. I don't get to be there."

Coral looked at the paper for a long time. Then she picked it up, and folded it smaller, and put it in her shirt pocket over her heart, which was, Ulyana thought, more than she deserved and exactly right.

"I'll bring it to them tomorrow," Coral said. "Idris knows everyone. And Voss—" she stood, and offered her hand, and after a moment Ulyana took it, "—when it's built, there's a spot on the north abutment where the masons put names. His goes first."

Ulyana nodded, and could not speak, and watched her go down the platform steps into the blue dark, toward the firelight and the river and the people who had chosen her.

She stayed on the bench until the stars came out over the water, doing the only work left to her: learning how to be a woman who repaired things instead of acquiring them—and discovering, as the first lamp flickered on in Coral Sterling's camp below, that the valley had already begun answering the question she'd been afraid to ask.
