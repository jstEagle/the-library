## 10. A Smaller Crossing

The dry season came to the valley the way forgiveness comes—slowly, and then all at once, as if it had only been waiting to be asked.

Coral stood on the east bank in October light and watched the Rivergate run low and brown-gold between its stone shoulders, a different river entirely from the one that had tried to drown them in June. Where the flood had been a roar you felt in your teeth, this was a murmur, a long exhalation, water sliding over gravel bars that hadn't seen sun in half a year. The drowned trestles downstream had surfaced like bones. Idris said the old-timers called October *the month of remembering*, because the river gave back everything it took.

She was beginning to believe the valley kept its own records.

Behind her, the crossing was taking shape—not Ulyana's grand line, not the iron spine she'd wanted to drive through the gorge with her company's name stamped on every mile, but something humbler and more stubborn. A foot-and-freight crossing: timber trestle on stone piers, gauge narrow enough to turn on a coin, built to carry grain sacks and mail crates and the occasional stubborn mule, not to conquer anything. The valley had asked for exactly this, in the end—asked in the shy way valleys ask, through farmers who drifted down to watch and stayed to hold a level, through the schoolteacher who measured the span for them with a surveyor's chain borrowed twice and returned once.

"Stake four's off by a finger," Fen called from mid-river, hip-deep in the shallows, sleeves rolled, hair tied back with what appeared to be a piece of signal flag. "Maybe two."

"Then pull it and set it true," Coral called back. "We're not building it fast. We're building it once."

Fen grinned—that same grin from the spur survey, from a hundred forgotten jobs where being invisible had been the whole point—and hauled the stake up dripping. Two years ago, off by a finger would have been good enough, because good enough meant gone before anyone learned their names. Now the whole crew worked in daylight, in full view of the road, and Coral had discovered that being watched was not the same as being hunted.

* * *

They had come back in September, after the flood season broke and the telegraph lines were strung again, and the first thing Coral did was walk the washout.

Not the way she'd walked it before—in the dark of memory, at night, alone, the way you visit a grave when you're ashamed of the epitaph. She walked it in morning light with Idris beside her and the crew trailing behind, and she stood on the scar where Aldous Reyes had died eleven years ago, and she told them all of it.

The rain that wouldn't stop. The readings Aldous had taken that showed the cutbank failing above the line—the readings that meant the section should have been closed, trains stopped, the company's first big contract delayed into ruin. The report he'd written saying so. The fire in the survey tent afterward, and the ash where his field notes should have been, and the official finding that read *surveyor error*, and the name attached to that error, young and convenient and hers.

"I signed what they told me to sign," she said. Her voice didn't shake, which surprised her; she'd expected it to shatter. "I was twenty-four and I thought if I took it, the crew would keep eating. And then I thought if I ever told the truth, it would be an admission, so I made the lie into a life. We hid. That was the pact. Nobody looks back." She looked at them, one face at a time—Fen, Okafor, little Wren with her chalk-stained fingers, Marsh's empty spot like a missing tooth. "I'm looking back now."

The silence went on long enough for the river to say something underneath it.

It was Wren who spoke first, because Wren always spoke first when it mattered. "You carried it alone for eleven years," she said. "That's the part I can't forgive. Not the lie. The carrying it alone."

And that was the verdict, and Coral took it like a sentence she'd been waiting for, and it was lighter than she'd feared and heavier than she'd earned, and it was theirs now, all of it, shared out like rations.

Ulyana's retraction ran in the territorial papers three weeks later—a full column, dry as a legal filing and devastating as one, withdrawing Voss & Meridian's claim on the Rivergate corridor, citing falsified incident records dating to the original washout, naming no one but Aldous Reyes, restoring his name in print where it had been buried in ash. Coral read it standing in the telegraph office in Millbrook while the clerk pretended not to watch. At the bottom, beneath the formal language, Ulyana had added a single line that could not have survived any editor worth his salt:

*The river was right. The paperwork was wrong. — U.V.*

No apology. Ulyana didn't apologize; she repaired, the way she did everything, at scale and at cost. The cost had been real—two board members resigned, the company's credit wobbled through the autumn, and there were men in gray suits who would remember. But when Coral had wired her thanks, the reply came back inside the hour, characteristically brief:

*Don't thank me. Build the small thing. It will outlast mine.*

* * *

Idris fit among them the way water fits a channel—finding the shape that was already there.

He'd shown up at the campsite in September with his boat on a cart and two of his cousins and no explanation beyond "the river said the work wasn't finished," and nobody asked him to explain further, because Idris's relationship with explanation was roughly Coral's old relationship with visibility. He knew the gravel bars by their childhood names. He knew which stone held frost first and which bank would give way in the spring pulse. When the crew argued about pier placement, they'd stopped consulting the maps first and consulted Idris first, and the maps second, and twice the maps had been wrong.

"You're surveying with memory," Coral told him one evening, watching him read the water like a ledger.

"I'm surveying with everything the water couldn't take," he said. "Paper drowns. Places don't."

He never talked about the leaking boat, though Coral knew he remembered it—the night the hull split below Widow's Bend, the storm coming down the gorge like a dropped ceiling, the crew bailing with hats and boots and bare hands while Marsh sat on the radio and let it die. They'd survived on Idris's channels, his calm voice calling depths in the dark, his hands on the sweep oar. And afterward, when they'd limped into base camp and Coral had found Ulyana reading Aldous's recovered notes by lamplight with an expression Coral had never seen on that composed face—grief arriving years late and wearing guilt's coat—afterward, everything had broken open at once, the way rivers do.

Marsh was gone. Ulyana had dismissed him before the flood even crested, and Coral suspected—though neither woman ever said it—that some of Marsh's sabotage orders had come from higher up than Marsh, and that Ulyana had burned those papers herself. Some debts you pay publicly. Some you pay by never speaking of them again.

* * *

By November the piers stood.

Four of them, mortared stone quarried from the valley's own hillsides, set on bedrock Idris had found by standing in the current with a pole and closing his eyes. The trestle bents rose above them in green timber, smelling of sap and sawdust, and the crew worked without hurry because hurry was how things failed, and this thing was not permitted to fail. Farmers brought pies down to the bank with a regularity that suggested a rota. The schoolteacher's students came on Fridays to watch, and Wren taught them to read a level, and one small girl with mud on her knees asked Coral if she was famous.

"No," Coral said, and found that the word sat easily. "I'm known. It's different. Famous is being looked at. Known is—" she searched for it, watching Fen laugh at something Okafor said across the water, watching Idris show the children how the current braided around the pier stones—"known is people knowing where to find you. And staying anyway."

The girl considered this with the gravity of the very young. "Like the river knows the stones?"

"Exactly like that."

On the last day of November they laid the deck planks, and at dusk the whole crew walked out onto the unfinished span—Fen and Okafor and Wren, Idris and his cousins, the schoolteacher, a dozen farmers who'd put in more hours than they'd ever admit—and stood in the middle over the running water, and nobody made a speech, because speeches were for things that needed justifying, and this needed nothing. Below them the Rivergate murmured over its remembering-month gravel. Above them the first stars came out over the gorge where the grand line would have gone, and did not go, and never would.

Coral touched the rail post, rough-sawn, warm from the day's sun going cool.

Aldous had wanted to build things that lasted. She understood now that lasting wasn't a property of size.

* * *

The telegram found her in December, at the survey tent they still pitched out of habit even though the crossing had a bunkhouse now.

She read it twice by lamplight. The crew gathered when they saw her face—not alarm, not anymore, but attention, the family reflex of a family that had learned what secrets cost.

*Heard about the crossing,* it began, in Ulyana's clipped hand. *Congratulations. You built the thing I was too proud to see.*

Then the second paragraph, and Coral read it aloud slowly, because it belonged to all of them:

*A commission is forming. Territorial, not corporate. They want honest surveys of the high valleys—flood mapping, crossings, the routes nobody wants. Triple pay is off the table. They can't afford it. What they offer instead is this: work done in daylight, under your own names, published in full. No sealed records anywhere in it. They asked me who to recommend. I gave them one name.*

*I told them you don't hide anymore.*

*— U.V.*

Wren laughed first, then Fen, then all of them, the bright disbelieving laughter of people being offered the exact thing they'd sworn off and discovering they'd only ever sworn off the loneliness of it.

Coral set the telegram on the drafting table, weighted it with a stone from the riverbank, and picked up her pencil.

"Well," she said, and her voice was steady, and her hands were steady, and outside the tent the river ran clear and cold and known. "Let's look at the maps."
