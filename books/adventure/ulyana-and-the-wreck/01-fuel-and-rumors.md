## 1. Fuel and Rumors

The morning the world changed — though of course I didn't know it yet, because the world never announces itself, it just shows up at your dock wearing a good coat and a bad smile — began with frost on the Beaver's struts and Dez swearing at a fuel pump in a tone he usually reserved for politicians and outboard motors.

"Third time this week," he called, voice carrying across the still water of Tachick Narrows like a curse with wings. "You hear me, Ulyana? Third time."

"I heard you the first two times," I said, setting down the crate of vaccine packs I'd been loading into the floatplane's cabin. The cold made everything crisp: the spruce along the shoreline, the smell of two-stroke and coffee, the particular silence of a lake that hadn't decided yet whether to freeze for the season. "Did it work?"

Dez emerged from under the wing, a wrench in one fist and a smear of grease across his jaw like war paint applied by someone who'd never actually seen a war. He was sixty-two years old and built like something the glacier had left behind when it retreated — squat, weathered, immovable.

"It worked," he admitted, as if this were a personal failing. "But she's complaining about it."

"She's earned the right. She's older than both of us put together."

"That's not mathematically possible."

"Neither are you, Dez. Get in."

He grinned and climbed aboard, and I finished lashing the cargo while the sun came up over the eastern ridgeline — *not* the far eastern ranges, mind you, not those, but the near ones, the polite ones, the ones that had never killed anybody I loved — and turned the water from pewter to hammered gold.

I want to say something here about how a person learns to look away from a mountain range. It's a skill, like short-field landings or reading weather on water. You don't decide once, dramatically, never to look again. You decide every single morning, in small ways. You file flight plans that curve westward when straight lines would be faster. You tell yourself the long way is safer anyway, which is even true, most days. And after ten years the detour stops feeling like a detour. It just becomes the route. The map of your life redrawn around a hole shaped like a man named Iversen.

My father flew into the eastern ranges on the fourteenth of October, ten years ago, and did not fly out. That's the whole story, if you want the short version. Nobody wants the short version. Everybody in the interior wants the long version, and they want it with details, and they want it fresh, and they have wanted it for a decade, which is why I was loading vaccines and flying west instead of east, and why the eastern horizon could go on being beautiful without my permission.

* * *

The run itself was nothing. That's the point of routine — it's the thing that holds you together while you're busy pretending you're fine.

Kettle Lake trapping camp sat ninety minutes northwest, on a horseshoe bay where old Emil Sorensen had lived alone for longer than anyone could remember, including, possibly, Emil. He ran sixty miles of trapline through country so rough the government maps still said "elevations approximate," and twice a month I brought him his mail, his flour, his diesel, and whatever the clinic in Fort Ransom had sent north — this time insulin, penicillin, and a new pair of reading glasses he would insist were worse than the old pair.

The ice had started at the edges of the bay, thin white lace creeping out from the reeds, and I set the Beaver down in the open middle with the particular tenderness that late-season water demands. A floatplane will forgive you almost anything except carelessness in October. The water is too cold to swim in, the rescue is too far away, and the ice doesn't care how many hours you have.

Emil was waiting on his dock, which meant he'd heard me coming for twenty minutes and had time to put on his good suspenders.

"You're late," he said.

"I'm early. Your watch is slow."

"My watch keeps perfect time. It's the world that's gotten fast." He took the crate I handed him and peered inside with the suspicion of a man who had been disappointed by civilization before. "Glasses?"

"Glasses."

"They'll be wrong."

"They'll be exactly your prescription."

"Then they'll be wrong." But he put them on right there on the dock, held them up to the light, squinted through them at me, and grunted the grunt that, translated from Emil, meant *thank you, these are perfect, I will never admit it.* "Coffee's on. Sit. Tell me what the world's doing."

So we drank coffee in his cabin that smelled of woodsmoke and fox musk and forty years of pipe tobacco, and I told him what the world was doing, which is to say I told him what everyone else had told me, because out here news doesn't travel by radio so much as it travels by rumor, passed pilot to trapper to bartender to priest and back to pilot, gaining detail and losing accuracy at every stop like a game of telephone played across four hundred miles of muskeg.

The gossip that week had three items.

Item one: the Hendersons' girl had got engaged to a geologist from Yellowknife, and nobody could understand it, because he was a nice enough boy but he had *teeth*, all of them, straight and white, and a man with teeth like that has never been hungry in his life and can't be trusted around a winter.

Item two: the price of marten was up, the price of wolverine was scandalous, and the trading post at Fort Ransom had started stocking actual orange juice, which Emil considered a sign of the coming apocalypse.

Item three — and this one arrived with the weight real news carries, the kind that makes a room go quiet — somebody had filed exploration permits on the eastern ranges. Staking crews moving in before freeze-up. A company nobody had ever heard of, with a name like a law firm married an insurance agency.

I felt it the way you feel a change in barometric pressure. Not a thought yet. Just a tightening behind the sternum.

"Eastern ranges," Emil repeated, watching me over his mug. His eyes were pale and quick, the eyes of a man who read sign for a living. "That'll stir things up. Old ground. Old stories."

"Old ground's all there is," I said lightly. "Everything out here is old ground."

"Not all of it's got bones in it." He said it mildly, the way he said everything, and then looked out the window at his woodpile and let me pretend I hadn't heard.

That was the other thing about the interior's gossip network. It carried grief as efficiently as it carried engagement announcements. Ten years on, people still watched me the way you watch a river in spring — respectfully, from a distance, waiting to see if the ice would hold. They knew better than to mention the wreck directly. So they mentioned it sideways, in the grammar of trappers, and I answered sideways back, and between us we maintained the fiction that we were talking about permit filings.

"Company's called Meridian something," Emil offered. "Meridian North. Or North Meridian. Fella at the post couldn't recall. Said the paperwork had more lawyers' names than coordinates."

"Lawyers don't stake claims."

"Lawyers hire men who do." He refilled my mug unasked. "Your father staked honest claims. Paper, posts, proper survey. Man could've taught the government a thing or two." A pause, delicate as snowfall. "You ever think about going back out that way? Now that there's reason?"

"No," I said.

It came out flat and fast, the way a hand comes off a hot stove, and Emil nodded slowly, satisfied, as if *no* had been the answer he was hoping to confirm. He didn't push. Emil had buried a wife and two sons in this country and understood better than any priest alive that some doors stay shut because the house behind them burned down.

I stayed another hour, helped him split kindling because his shoulder was acting up and he'd sooner die than ask, checked the insulin was stored right, and walked back to the dock through air so clean it felt like drinking it. On the way I caught myself looking east — just once, just a glance past the near ridges toward the blue wall beyond them, where the high country stood white already against the sky.

Ten years, and the habit of looking was still in me. Ten years, and the habit of looking away had only gotten stronger. Between the two of them, I managed to keep flying.

* * *

The trouble started on the flight home, which is fitting, because trouble in my experience prefers to arrive when you're tired and low on options and the light is going gold and long across the water.

We were twenty minutes out of Kettle Lake, Dez dozing in the right seat with his cap over his eyes, when the radio crackled. Fort Ransom flight service, relaying a message. That was normal — half my business came in as relayed messages, charter requests and freight quotes and Mrs. Aldous asking for the third time whether I could bring her a sewing machine part from the city.

"This one's marked personal and priority," the dispatcher said. "Charter inquiry. Search contract, sounds like. Says triple your posted rates."

Triple rates. In October. When every operator in the territory was either hauling moose meat or putting boats to bed for the winter, and the ones who weren't were lying about it.

"Who's the client?" I asked.

There was a pause on the line, the particular pause of a man reading something he finds interesting. "No name given. Letter came in with the morning mail plane. Says — hang on — 'regarding a missing aircraft, eastern sector.' Signed with a reference number only. There's a note that says the identity of the contracting party will be disclosed on acceptance of terms."

Beside me, Dez lifted his cap and looked at me with one eye.

"Eastern sector," he repeated.

"Eastern sector," I agreed, and my hands were perfectly steady on the yoke, because ten years of practice will give you that much at least.

Now, here is the thing you need to know about me, the thing the whole interior knows about me, the thing I have spent a decade making boring and reliable and unremarkable: I do not fly east. Ask anyone. Ask the bartenders, the priests, the trappers, the other pilots who quietly take the charters I decline and charge double for the privilege. Ulyana Iversen will haul your fish, your fuel, your pregnant wife, your coffin, anywhere in the territory — except there. No amount of money moves her. It's practically a landmark. People navigate by it.

A search contract for a missing aircraft in the eastern sector was therefore not a job offer. It was either a mistake or a test, and I have no patience for either.

"Tell them no," I said. "Standard refusal. No explanation required, none given."

"Ulyana," the dispatcher said carefully, "there's a P.S."

"There usually is."

"It says—" paper rustling, the sound of a man deciding how much of his day he wanted to spend on this— "it says, 'Ask her about the debt she owes Elias Hale.'"

The cabin went very quiet. Below us the lakes slid past, sheet lead and hammered copper, and somewhere ahead the sun was going down over country that had nothing to do with me.

Elias Hale. I hadn't heard that name spoken aloud in ten years, and hearing it now was like finding a splinter you'd forgotten — sudden, precise, absurdly painful for its size. Elias Hale, whose son had stood on a dock in Fort Ransom a decade ago with rain running off his jaw and blamed my father for everything. Elias Hale, whose name was in my father's expedition ledger, crossed out, disputed, fought over in letters I'd stopped reading halfway through. Elias Hale, dead five years now, according to the last rumor, which had come to me by way of a bartender, a bush pilot, and a moose hunter, and was therefore possibly wrong.

"Ulyana?" Dez said softly.

I flew for a while before answering. The Narrows came up ahead, our home water, dock lights just showing, smoke rising straight from the stovepipe of the little cabin Dez and I pretended was an office.

"Log the refusal," I said finally. "And log the message verbatim. Date, time, exact wording."

"You're going to answer it."

"I'm going to *think* about answering it. There's a difference."

"There really isn't," Dez said, but he said it gently, the way you talk to a dog standing at the edge of thin ice, and I laughed despite myself, and brought her down onto the darkening water as smooth as anything, because whatever else was true, I was still the best pilot on this side of the mountains, and some things a person gets to keep.

* * *

That night I sat in the office with the lamp on and the refusal letter half-written in front of me, and I did not write the word *declined*.

I thought about debts. My father had believed in them the way other men believe in scripture — that a favor unpaid rots, that a promise broken follows you, that the books always balance in the end whether you keep them or not. And there *was* a debt. I'd known it for ten years and told myself it had expired, the way you tell yourself a lot of things. Elias Hale had put up half the money for my father's last expedition. Half the money, gone into the mountains with the plane and the man and the truth, whatever the truth was. And whatever else Marcus Hale had shouted at me through the rain that day on the dock — and he had shouted plenty, and so, God help me, had I — the debt wasn't his fault, and it wasn't mine, and it sat there between our families like a body neither of us would dive for.

Reference number. No name. Triple rates. Eastern sector.

I looked at the letter a long time. Outside, the first real flakes of the season came down through the lamplight, fat and unhurried, settling on the Beaver's wings where she rode her lines at the dock, patient as an old dog waiting to see where we'd go next.

In the morning, I told myself, I'd refuse it properly. In the morning I'd write *declined* and be done, and the eastern ranges could keep their secrets, and the books could stay unbalanced, and I could go on being the reliable one, the safe one, the woman who never flies east.

I wrote *declined.*

Then I sat there until the lamp burned low, and sometime after midnight, without quite deciding to, I turned the page over and started a second letter — one line only, addressed to a reference number, asking a question I already dreaded the answer to:

*Who is asking?*

The reply, when it came two days later on the mail plane, was one word long, written in a hand I recognized from ten years of angry correspondence, steady and slanted and unmistakable as a voice:

*Hale.*
