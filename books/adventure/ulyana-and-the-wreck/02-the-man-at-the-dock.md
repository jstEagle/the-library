## 2. The Man at the Dock

The rain came in sideways off the lake that morning, the kind of thin, patient rain that the interior specialized in — not enough to ground you, just enough to make you question every decision you'd ever made, including building a floatplane base on a north-facing shore.

Ulyana Iversen stood under the lean-to roof of her dock shed with a mug of coffee going cold in her hand and watched the storm front chew its way down the valley. Behind her, the Beaver sat on its mooring lines like a big yellow dog tied to a fence, rocking gently and utterly indifferent to the weather. Ahead of her, forty miles of gray water and grayer sky.

She was doing the thing she did every morning now that the season had turned: reading the wind on the water, counting whitecaps, deciding whether today was a flying day or a fixing day. The wind said fixing day. The wind had been saying fixing day for three days running, and Dez was getting insufferable about it.

"You could at least pretend to be disappointed," Dez had said yesterday, sprawled under the Beaver's engine cowl with grease to his elbows. "Some of us live for this."

"Some of us," Ulyana had replied, "have a reputation to maintain."

It was true. Ten years of flying medicine and mail into camps no road would ever reach had built her a reputation as the pilot who went when nobody else would. What the reputation didn't say — what she made sure it never said — was how carefully she chose her *when*. She flew the western ranges, the southern lakes, the long river runs up to the trapping camps along the Kettle. She did not fly east. Not past the divide, not over the granite shoulders of the Carrow Range, not anywhere near the high country where the weather came off the icefields mean and sudden and where, ten years ago last September, a Norseman aircraft with two men aboard had gone into a ridge and never come out.

Her father's name was still on the memorial plaque in Fort Alder's little airport terminal. She dusted it herself twice a year. That was as close as she got.

* * *

The boat appeared out of the rain around eleven o'clock.

She heard it before she saw it — an outboard running rough, the sound of someone who either didn't know engines or didn't care. It resolved out of the murk as a black aluminum skiff with a canvas dodger, running straight across the lake toward her dock with the confidence of a man who had never once been told he couldn't moor somewhere.

Ulyana set down her coffee and picked up, without really thinking about it, the boat hook.

The skiff came alongside with a scrape of rubber against the dock timbers, and a man stepped out onto her planks before she'd even offered a hand. He was tall, maybe fifty, wearing a rain jacket that cost more than her annual fuel budget and city boots that were already learning humility on her wet decking. He had silver at his temples and the kind of jawline that suggested money had been spent maintaining it, and he looked around at her dock, her shed, her one battered airplane, with an expression she recognized instantly because she'd seen it on surveyors and government men and every other kind of man who arrived believing the interior ought to be grateful for the visit.

"Ms. Iversen." He put out his hand. "Marcus Hale."

The name landed first. The face caught up a half-second later, and then she was ten years younger, standing in a cook tent at a staging camp while men argued over maps, and a young man with an expensive watch and an expensive education had told her — kindly, reasonably, in the tone of someone explaining arithmetic to a child — that the search window was closing and further flights were a waste of company resources.

She didn't take his hand. She took the boat hook and used it to nudge his skiff's bow line loose from her cleat.

"Dock's closed," she said.

"It's raining," he said, as if that were an argument.

"It is. And you're drifting away. Good luck with that."

He looked down at the skiff, which was indeed beginning to swing out on the chop, and back at her, and something shifted in his face — not charm exactly, but the machinery of charm engaging, gears she could almost hear turning. "Ms. Iversen. I've flown four thousand miles to be here. I've been trying to reach you by radio for three weeks. I can wait out here in the rain if you prefer, but I suspect you're the kind of person who finds that more annoying than I do."

That was irritatingly accurate. She hated people who made scenes on her dock. Her dock was neutral territory, like a church or a hockey rink.

"Two minutes," she said. "Inside. You drip on my floor, you're mopping."

* * *

The shed smelled of diesel, coffee, and the particular mustiness of canvas that has been folded wet too many times. Hale looked around it the way he'd looked at the dock — cataloguing, assessing — and she watched him do the math on her operation: one plane, one mechanic, one dockhand who was mostly a dog named Biscuit currently pretending to sleep by the stove. Whatever number he arrived at, he had the sense not to say it aloud.

He accepted coffee because refusing would have been rude even by his standards, and held the mug without drinking, and began without preamble.

"I represent Meridian Survey and Exploration. We have an aircraft missing. A Cessna 208, charter survey work, last contact nineteen days ago in the eastern ranges. There were two men aboard." He recited it flatly, a report, and she noticed his hands weren't quite steady around the mug. "The company's own search was called off after six days. Weather. I'm asking you to resume it."

"The eastern ranges," Ulyana said.

"That's right."

"No."

"You haven't asked the rate."

"I don't need to ask the rate. I'm not flying the Carrows in October, Mr. Hale, for any rate. The weather window up there is a rumor this time of year. You'd be paying me to die, and I don't come cheap."

"Triple your charter rate. Plus hazard."

"Plus nothing." She leaned back against the workbench and crossed her arms. "You want to find your plane, hire out of Fort Alder. Half a dozen pilots there will take your money. They'll also probably die, so maybe spend some of it on candles."

"They won't go either. That's rather the problem." He set the mug down on her bench, on top of a parts diagram, which she noted with disfavor. "I've been told — repeatedly, and with what I can only describe as relish — that the only pilot foolish enough or good enough to search those ridges this late is Ulyana Iversen. And that she doesn't fly east."

"Your informants are well briefed."

"They are. One of them mentioned why." He said it carefully, watching her. "I know about the expedition. Ten years ago. The Norseman."

The shed got very quiet. Outside, the rain kept up its patient drumming on the roof, and Biscuit lifted his head, sensing the change in pressure the way dogs do, and put it back down.

"My father died out there," Ulyana said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her; she'd expected it to come out sharp. "Along with Tom Brandt, who was somebody's husband and somebody's father too, though I notice nobody ever puts him in the story. The company that hired them wrote them off as a weather accident inside a month and paid out settlements with the enthusiasm of men settling a bar tab. So when you sit in my shed and tell me you want me to fly back into that country looking for another one of your airplanes, you'll understand that my interest is limited."

Hale didn't flinch. She gave him credit for that much. Most men, by this point, would have found a reason to check their phone.

"You think I don't understand that," he said quietly. "You think I'm here for a mineral survey and a tax write-off. Maybe I deserve that. But I'm not asking you as Meridian's man, Ms. Iversen. I'm asking you as—" He stopped. Started again. She realized, with a small cold shock, that his hands were shaking now, genuinely, and that he'd been holding them still through sheer discipline. "My father was on that expedition too. Did you know that?"

She hadn't. Ten years ago, the expedition had been a wall of adult faces and company letterhead, and she had been twenty-four and drowning, and she had never sorted one suit from another.

"Edmund Hale," he said. "Meridian's founder. He financed the original survey. The claim staking, the core samples, all of it. He was in the staging camp when the Norseman went down." Marcus Hale looked at the floor of her shed, at the oil stains, anywhere but at her. "And whatever my father was looking for out there — whatever your father was carrying for him — it ruined us. Or saved us. I've spent ten years trying to work out which."

"Mr. Hale—"

"There's a debt," he said, and now he did look at her, and his eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "Not the kind you invoice. My father believed — believed to the end of his life, and he was not a sentimental man — that Erik Iversen died carrying something that belonged to my family, and that we owed Erik Iversen's daughter everything we had. He said it on his deathbed like a man settling accounts. I thought it was the morphine talking. Then three weeks ago I found his private papers, and I found out it wasn't."

"Found out what wasn't?"

He reached into his jacket — slowly, deliberately, the way you move around a dog you don't know — and brought out a photograph, old and creased, and laid it on the bench between them.

Two men stood in front of a floatplane on a lake she knew by heart. Her father on the left, younger than she could bear, grinning with his cap pushed back, one arm slung around the shoulder of a heavyset man in a company jacket. Edmund Hale. They looked like friends. They looked like men who had shared a hundred bad dinners in a hundred good camps.

On the back, in her father's handwriting: *E.H. — for the flight home, whenever you're ready. E.I.*

Ulyana stared at it for a long time. The rain filled the silence.

"He talked about you," Hale said. "In the letters. 'The girl reads wind on rock better than any man I've flown with.' That's a direct quote. He wanted to hire you himself, when you were older. My father didn't want much, but he wanted that."

"This proves nothing," she said, but her voice had gone rough, and they both knew it. "A photograph. A dying man's guilt. You want me to fly into country that kills experienced pilots, on the strength of a snapshot."

"I want to offer you triple rates and full salvage rights," he said. "And I want to tell you that if you say no, I'll hire someone worse and they'll probably die, and I'll carry that too, because apparently this family collects debts it can't pay." He paused. "There's one more thing. The missing plane — the Cessna — it wasn't doing survey work. I lied on the flight plan filings. It was flying a route my father marked on a map thirty years ago, into the Carrows, and it stopped reporting exactly where the Norseman went down. Same drainage. Same ridgeline. Ms. Iversen, either that's a coincidence, or whatever my father buried out there has finally decided to surface. And I need the one pilot alive who knows that country well enough to find it — and who has a reason to look that isn't money."

Ulyana looked at the photograph. At her father's grin. At the handwriting she'd know anywhere, the backward-leaning capitals, the way he crossed his t's like he was signing off on the whole world.

*For the flight home, whenever you're ready.*

Ten years she'd stayed west of the divide. Ten years of careful routes and careful weather and a hole in her map shaped exactly like a mountain. And here was a stranger on her dock telling her the hole had a bottom, and that her father might be at it.

"If I say yes," she said slowly, "it's my operation. My crew, my decisions, my call on when we fly and when we don't. You ride along as a passenger and you keep your opinions in a bag."

"Agreed."

"And if I decide it's too dangerous, we turn around, and you pay for the fuel anyway."

"Agreed."

"And you mop your own drips."

Something that was almost a smile crossed his face — tired, crooked, real. "Agreed."

She stuck out her hand. His grip was cold and strong, and she held it a beat longer than a handshake strictly required, because she wanted him to understand that this was not forgiveness. This was arithmetic. A debt was a debt, and her father had taught her that much if nothing else.

"One condition I haven't mentioned," Hale said, releasing her hand. He glanced out the shed door at the rain, at the lake, at the Beaver rocking on its lines. "We leave within the week. Before the weather closes the passes for good."

"Week's tight. I'll need a crew."

"I may be able to help there. I've already engaged a guide — a Sam Two-Rivers? Came highly recommended. Said he knew the eastern country intimately."

Ulyana went very still.

Sam Two-Rivers. Who had been on the original expedition. Who had walked out of the mountains with the survivors and never once, in ten years, said a word to her about what happened up there beyond what the reports said. Who fished off her dock every June and fixed her net winch and drank her coffee and kept his own counsel the way the mountains kept theirs.

Sam Two-Rivers, hired by Marcus Hale, before Marcus Hale had ever knocked on her door.

"When," she said, "exactly, did you engage him?"

Hale frowned. "Three weeks ago. Why?"

Outside, the rain went on falling, patient as ever, and Ulyana Iversen looked at the man she'd just agreed to fly into hell with, and felt the first cold thread of a question she couldn't yet name slide down her spine.

"No reason," she said. "Get your gear together. We leave Thursday."
