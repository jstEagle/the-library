## 3. Crew of Four

Ulyana found Dez where she always found him: flat on his back under the Beaver, one arm buried in the engine cowling to the shoulder, a wrench in his free hand and a cigarette burning down unattended on the dock plank beside his ear.

"You're going to set the lake on fire," she said.

"I'm going to finish this magneto," Dez said, "and then I'm going to die of old age, respected and solvent." He slid out, sat up, and looked at her the way he always did when she came to him with a job — first at her face, then at her hands. He'd been reading her hands for fifteen years. They told him things she didn't say out loud.

"Triple rates," she said. "Two weeks, maybe three. Survey work out east."

Dez picked up his cigarette, considered it, and put it out against the dock. That was how she knew he'd already understood. Out east. There was only one thing out east that paid triple rates, and it had a tail number Dez had once painted himself, on a different aircraft, for a different man.

"Whose money?" he asked.

"Hale's."

He whistled low, the way a man whistles when he sees weather coming over a ridge he has to cross anyway. "Marcus Hale. The Marcus Hale."

"The same."

"And you said yes."

"I said yes," Ulyana confirmed, and hated how it sounded — like a confession instead of a decision. She sat down on the dock beside him, boots dangling over the water, and gave him the shape of it: the missing survey plane, the story Hale had told, the debt her father owed and she apparently still owed after him. She left out the part where she'd nearly thrown Hale into the lake. Some details were hers alone.

Dez listened without interrupting, which was unlike him, which meant he was doing arithmetic. When she finished he said, "You know what's out there."

"I know what's out there."

"And you're going back."

"I'm going back." She watched a loon surface thirty yards off the dock and slide away like it had better business elsewhere. "Ten years I've flown around those ranges like they're made of glass. Every route east, I add forty minutes to go around. Forty minutes, Dez. Do you know how much fuel that is? How many trips?" She shook her head. "I'm tired of paying rent on a ghost."

Dez was quiet a moment. Then: "Who else?"

"That's why I'm here. I need three people I'd trust with my life, because that is literally the arrangement. You. A second pilot. And Sam Two-Rivers."

At that, Dez actually laughed — a short, disbelieving bark. "Sam. Sam who hasn't spoken about that expedition since it happened. Sam who left your father's memorial dinner before the potatoes."

"Sam who knows that country better than anyone alive, and who owes my father the same debt I do, whether he ever says so or not."

"He'll say no."

"He'll say no," Ulyana agreed. "And then he'll say yes. Watch."

* * *

Dez's shop smelled of solvent and burnt coffee, and Teodora Marchetti arrived in the middle of both, the way she arrived everywhere — slightly too fast, slightly too loud, twenty-two years old and vibrating with the specific hunger of a pilot with ninety hours and nowhere to spend them.

She'd been sweeping the hangar floor when Dez got there, which was itself a small miracle; the last two kids he'd hired had treated the broom as decorative. But it was the logbook that had sold him. Not the hours — there were almost none — but the entries. Neat columns, honest numbers, and in the remarks section of every single flight, something written in a careful schoolgirl hand: *crosswind from the west, held crab all the way down.* *Fog burned off late, waited rather than scud-run.* *Engine gauges normal, oil temp crept on climb-out, noted for next preheat.*

Dez had read that last one twice. Ninety-hour pilots did not notice oil temp creep. Ninety-hour pilots noticed whether the radio worked and whether they looked cool landing.

Now she stood in front of his workbench with her cap in her hands like she was at a job interview, which, he supposed, she was.

"Ms. Kavanagh says you'd be the one to ask," she said. "About coming along. On the charter."

"Did she now." Dez poured two coffees, handed her one, and leaned against the bench. "What did she tell you about it?"

Teodora's eyes lit up with the pure uncomplicated joy of someone about to demonstrate knowledge. "East ranges. Two weeks minimum. Triple rates. Survey support for a mineral outfit — Hale Exploration, I looked them up, they've got permits filed through Yellowknife but the registered agent traces back to—"

"Okay." Dez raised a hand. "You looked them up."

"Yes sir."

"And?"

"And their paperwork is weird," Teodora admitted, deflating slightly. "Like, lawyer-weird. Lots of holding companies. I couldn't follow it all the way down."

"No," Dez said, "you couldn't. Nobody can. That's the point of holding companies." He drank his coffee and studied her. Here was the question, and he wanted to see how she handled it. "Kid. If I told you this trip might go somewhere ugly — not dangerous-ugly, just sad-ugly, old-grave-ugly — would you still want the hours?"

Teodora didn't answer right away, and Dez liked her more for that. She turned her coffee cup in her hands, thinking, and when she looked up her face had gone serious in a way that made her look briefly older than twenty-two.

"My dad flew bush planes," she said. "Before he drove truck. He used to say every pilot ends up flying somebody's grief eventually — medevacs, repatriations, that kind of thing. He said the trick isn't avoiding it. It's being worth trusting with it." She shrugged, embarrassed. "So. Yeah. I want the hours. But I want to be worth trusting with them, too."

Dez looked at her for a long moment. Then he stuck out his hand.

"Preheat starts at five a.m.," he said. "You'll hate me by Wednesday."

"Sir, I already hate you," Teodora said, shaking his hand with both of hers, beaming, "and I haven't even started."

* * *

The problem with Sam Two-Rivers, Ulyana reflected as she guided the Cessna down toward the trapline cabin on Tazin Lake, was that he never made anything easy, including finding him. He lived half the year out here, four hundred miles from anywhere, and reached the rest of civilization on a schedule known only to himself and possibly the ravens.

She found the cabin smoking, though — a thin gray thread off the stovepipe — and touched down on the long bay with the ice-free channel running down its middle, the way Sam had taught her father to read water thirty years ago, and her father had taught her ten years after that.

Sam was splitting wood when she walked up. He was sixty-eight and swung the axe like a man settling an argument with each piece, unhurried, total. He saw her coming and finished the round he was working on before he straightened and looked at her.

"Ulyana Iversen," he said. "You look like your mother."

"Everyone keeps saying that."

"Because it keeps being true." He set the axe against the block. "Coffee's on. You didn't fly four hundred miles for coffee."

Inside, the cabin was warm and close-smelling — spruce smoke, spruce boughs, the tin of lard, the rifle over the door. Sam poured coffee into enamel cups and sat across the table from her, and for a while neither of them said anything, because that was also how Sam taught things. You let the silence get big enough that whatever you put into it mattered.

Finally Ulyana said, "There's a charter. East ranges. Hale money."

Sam drank his coffee. Said nothing.

"A survey plane went down, supposedly. Hale wants it found. Triple rates, two weeks, maybe three." She turned her cup. "I'm going, Sam. I'm asking you to come as guide. I'm asking because nobody knows that country like you, and because—" she stopped, started again, "—because you were there. And I can't do this with people who weren't."

The silence stretched. Outside, a raven said something rude and flapped off. Sam looked at her across the table with those dark, patient eyes, and Ulyana felt eleven years old again, sitting in this same cabin while the men planned the expedition that would kill her father, listening at the edge of lamplight.

"You could say no," Sam said at last. "You could keep flying around. Forty minutes extra, every trip east. Keep paying."

Her breath caught. "How did you—"

"Small country," Sam said. "Gossip travels faster than airplanes." He rose, took his cup to the basin, and stood looking out the window at the bay, at the plane riding her mooring, at the ridgelines far to the east that were just visible from here on a clear day — blue-gray teeth against the sky.

"I'll come," he said.

Ulyana let out a breath she felt in her whole chest. "Thank you. Sam, I—"

"But hear one thing first." He turned around. His voice hadn't changed at all, which somehow made it worse. "Whatever we find out there — however it looks when we find it — some things are the way they are for a reason. Your father understood that. Maybe that's the part of him you didn't inherit."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," Sam said, picking up his axe and stepping back out into the cold, "that I'll guide you to wherever you need to go. And I'll carry whatever needs carrying. But don't ask me to talk about that year. Not on the trail, not at night, not ever. You hired a guide. You didn't hire a witness."

He split another round of wood, clean through, one stroke, and Ulyana stood in the doorway with her cooling coffee and felt the first cold thread of something she couldn't yet name go down her spine — the sense, faint as woodsmoke, that everyone on this expedition knew something she didn't, and that the country ahead kept its accounts the way Sam kept his silence.

Completely. And for a reason.

* * *

They gathered at the float base on the last day of August, four of them and one stowaway-to-be whom nobody had met yet, though none of them knew that either.

Dez ran the briefing from the top of an overturned fuel drum, because he believed briefings went better when the speaker was taller than everyone. Teodora sat cross-legged on the dock taking notes in a waterproof book. Ulyana stood at the map table with the eastern ranges spread out under her hands, and if her fingers paused a half-second over one particular valley, only Sam noticed, and Sam said nothing.

"Weather window opens tomorrow, closes Thursday," Ulyana said. "We fly in on the front side, establish basecamp on the lake below the search grid, and run sweeps until Hale's money runs out or the plane turns up. Questions?"

"Yeah," said Dez. "Who's cooking?"

"You are."

"I'm the mechanic."

"You're also the only one who can make bannock without setting off the fire extinguisher. Motion carries." She rolled up the map. "One more thing. This is a professional trip. Whatever any of us thinks we know about these ranges, about that year — we leave it on the dock. We go out, we do the work, we come home. Agreed?"

Three heads nodded. Sam's nod came last and slowest, and his eyes went east, to the teeth of blue-gray ridgeline just clearing the horizon, and stayed there a moment longer than a guide's eyes needed to.

That night, long after the others had turned in, Dez sat on the dock with a cigarette he wasn't smoking, watching the Beaver rock gently at her mooring under a sky full of indifferent stars. Ten years ago he'd fueled a different plane on this same dock, for the same general heading, and shaken a young man's hand and said *bring her home safe*, and meant it, and been wrong.

Tomorrow he'd do it again.

He flicked the cigarette into the water, unlit, and went to bed.
