## 4. Weather Window

Teodora Okonkwo had been awake since four in the morning, and not because of the alarm.

She'd heard the Beaver's engine turn over in the dark — that particular cough-and-catch of a cold radial waking up — and she'd lain in her bunk at the base listening to it idle, thinking: *she's checking the engine before dawn. She's checking the engine before anyone asked her to.* It was the kind of thing Teodora had read about in books about bush pilots, the old ones with cracked spines and coffee rings on the covers, and here it was happening forty feet from her pillow. Ulyana Iversen, out on the dock in the dark with a flashlight in her teeth, loving on an airplane.

By the time Teodora got dressed and stumbled outside, breath smoking, Ulyana was already back inside the cabin, and the whole operation had shifted into that quiet, purposeful hum that Teodora was learning to recognize as *Iversen is loading for a serious trip.* Cargo netting. Tie-downs doubled. The good map — the laminated one, not the paper ones that lived rolled in a coffee can by the door.

"You're up," Ulyana said, without looking up from the tie-down she was testing. It wasn't a question or a greeting exactly. It was more like a weather report about Teodora herself.

"I heard the engine."

"Mm." Ulyana tugged the strap, satisfied, and finally looked at her. She had that way of looking at people like she was weighing them against something invisible — cargo limits, maybe. "Dez says you preflighted her yesterday."

"Twice."

"Once would've done."

"The second time was for me," Teodora said, and then felt her ears go hot, because that sounded like something a person would say when they were trying very hard to be the kind of pilot who said things like that.

But Ulyana almost smiled. Almost. It moved across her face like cloud shadow over water and was gone. "Load manifest is on the clipboard. Hale's gear goes aft of the drums, not forward. He'll want it forward because he's the one paying. He's also the one who doesn't know what weight does to a floatplane's center of gravity, so he doesn't get a vote."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And Teodora." Ulyana held her eyes now, and there was something underneath the usual dryness, something almost careful. "Today we fly east. You've never flown east of the divide."

"No, ma'am."

"There's a reason I don't fly east of the divide." She said it flat, no drama in it, the way you'd say *there's a reason I don't put diesel in the tanks.* Then she picked up the clipboard and went up the dock toward the office, and Teodora stood alone on the planks with the mist coming off the lake and understood that she had just been told something true and important and completely unexplained, which, she was beginning to learn, was how most knowledge arrived in the interior.

* * *

Marcus Hale arrived at seven sharp, which Teodora grudgingly respected. In her limited experience, money showed up late and expected the world to have waited. Hale came down the dock in a canvas jacket that had never once been rained on, carrying a leather bag and an expression of contained impatience, like a man perpetually two minutes ahead of everyone else and annoyed about the lag.

"Miss Okonkwo." He'd learned her name overnight. That was a thing rich men did; they deployed your name like a tip.

"Mr. Hale."

"Is there coffee?"

"There's coffee."

He looked at the sky while he drank it. The sky was doing something Teodora didn't love — high cloud moving in from the southwest, thin but organized, the kind of sky that had an agenda. Dez had come out onto the dock too, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime around 2019, and he was reading the same sky.

"Window's open now," Dez said. "Closes by afternoon, I'd guess. Front's moving faster than they said."

"Faster than who said?" Hale asked.

"Faster than the forecast said," Dez said, with the patience of a man explaining weather to someone who thought weather took requests. "Forecasts out here are rumors with math behind them."

Hale checked his watch. "Then let's use the window."

"We're using the window," Ulyana said, coming down the dock with the clipboard under her arm. "We leave in ten. Sam's meeting us at the fuel cache on the river — he ran ahead yesterday to stage the camp gear. Two legs today, camp by dark if the front behaves."

"And if it doesn't behave?" Hale asked.

Ulyana looked at him for a long moment. "Then we land somewhere flat and wait it out, and you learn what the interior thinks of schedules."

Something passed between them — Teodora couldn't name it, some old static, ten years deep — and Hale's jaw tightened and he said nothing, which Teodora suspected cost him something.

They loaded in silence broken only by Dez narrating weights under his breath like a prayer. Teodora got the forward seats squared away, then climbed into the co-pilot's side while Ulyana did her walk-around, touching the airplane the way other people touched horses — flat palm on the cowl, a wordless conversation about struts and fabric and the thousand small agreements between a pilot and a machine.

"Front seat's yours," Ulyana said through the door, and Teodora's whole chest went bright. *Front seat.* Right seat, technically, but in the Beaver the right seat was where you learned, and Ulyana had put her there on day three without ceremony, and Teodora had privately decided it was the single greatest honor of her twenty-three years, including the time she'd graduated flight school.

The engine caught on the third blade of the prop swing, that big throaty rumble settling into itself, and they taxied out into the middle of the lake with the mist burning off and the mountains standing around the horizon like patient old men waiting to be introduced.

"Radio check," Ulyana said, and keyed the mic, and gave her departure call, and Teodora listened to the voice — calm, unhurried, slightly bored even — and thought: *someday. Someday I want to sound like that on the radio. Like the sky is a place I've lived my whole life.*

They lifted off the water at 7:40 in the morning, and the interior opened beneath them like a book Teodora had been trying to read her entire life.

* * *

For the first hour it was perfect. Rivers braiding silver through muskeg, caribou trails stitched across the tundra like someone had spilled thread, lakes so many they stopped counting. Hale sat in the back with his maps and said nothing, which Teodora appreciated, because every time he spoke she became aware of him as a passenger, and passengers were weather of their own kind.

Ulyana flew with her whole body relaxed, one hand loose on the yoke, making tiny corrections that Teodora could barely see. She taught without teaching — a tilt of her chin toward a smoke column ("camp fire, somebody's having a slow morning"), a flick of two fingers at a ridge ("wind's coming over the top there, see the snow blowing off? That's your free weather station"). Teodora wrote none of it down and remembered all of it.

"Pass ahead," Ulyana said eventually, and her voice was the same voice, exactly the same voice, but something in Teodora's stomach tilted anyway, because the pass ahead was a notch in the eastern ranges, and the ranges were wearing cloud like a scarf pulled up to the eyes.

The high cloud from the southwest had thickened while they weren't watching. It does that, Dez always said. Cloud is patient. Cloud has nowhere else to be. And now the organized thin stuff had become organized thick stuff, and the pass — which on the map was a friendly green line between two gray masses of contour — was looking less like a doorway and more like a mail slot.

"Weather's building," Teodora said, because she wanted to be useful, and because saying it out loud made it real in a way that helped.

"It's building," Ulyana agreed. "Question is whether it's building faster than we climb." She keyed the radio and gave a position report, and her voice was still that calm radio voice, and Teodora marveled at it even as the first raindrops hit the windshield, fat and scattered, like the sky clearing its throat.

Behind them, Hale leaned forward between the seats. "Can we make it through?"

"We can make it through," Ulyana said.

"But—"

"I said we can make it through. What I didn't say is that we will enjoy it." She glanced back, and for one second Teodora saw something flicker across her face — not fear exactly, something older than fear, something that had been living in her a long time and knew its way around. "Sit down and buckle up, Mr. Hale. Turbulence over the pass is going to be honest with us."

Hale sat. The rain found its rhythm. The mountains came closer, and closer, and stopped being scenery and started being terrain.

Teodora had flown in mountains exactly twice, both times with instructors, both times in weather that would have embarrassed this. But those flights had been lessons. This was a commute. There was a difference, she was discovering, between flying *at* a mountain range and flying *into* one, and the difference was mostly in your hands.

She watched Ulyana's hands.

That was the thing she'd do later, lying awake, turning the day over like a stone. She'd watched Ulyana's hands because that's what apprentices do, that's how you steal a craft — you watch the hands. And the hands were steady through the valley approach, steady through the turn at the river bend, steady as the ridgeline rose up on their left like a wall with opinions. Steady, steady, steady.

And then, near the pass, in the last five miles where the walls narrowed and the ceiling came down to meet them and the wind began to talk in that low irregular voice it uses when it's coming over rock — the hands shook.

Not much. Not the way hands shake in movies. Just a fine tremor, a vibration in the fingers where they lay on the yoke, like a tuning fork someone had struck and pressed against the control column. Teodora saw it because she was close enough and young enough and paying attention enough, and because she had spent three weeks memorizing everything about how Ulyana Iversen did things, and this was not on the list.

Ulyana felt her looking. Teodora knew she felt it, because the chin came around, just slightly, and the gray eyes took her measure, and whatever Ulyana saw in Teodora's face made her say, evenly:

"Turbulence shakes the yoke. It's the airframe talking."

It wasn't turbulence. Teodora knew it wasn't turbulence, because the air hadn't hit them yet — the air was still ahead, still gathering itself in the pass like a held breath. The yoke was quiet. The shaking was coming from the hand, not the sky.

"Yes, ma'am," Teodora said.

And then the pass took them.

* * *

It was eleven minutes. Teodora counted, because counting was something to hold onto. Eleven minutes of the Beaver being handled by a woman who flew like the airplane was an extension of her skeleton — crabbing sideways against a crosswind that wanted to fold them into the rock, riding downdrafts that dropped them fifty feet at a gulp, threading a gap in the cloud that appeared and closed and appeared again like something breathing. Hale made one sound, early, a short syllable that might have been a prayer or a complaint, and then went silent in a way that suggested he was renegotiating his relationship with several things at once.

Ulyana never said a word. Her face never changed. Only the hands — and only if you knew to look — betrayed that some part of her was somewhere else entirely, some part of her flying this same pass ten years ago in the other direction, in better weather, with her father alive in one of these seats.

On the far side the world opened up. The cloud broke apart into ragged, sunlit pieces, the valleys ran gold and green below, and the turbulence smoothed out like a sheet being pulled tight. Ulyana set the power back, trimmed the nose, and exhaled — one long breath through the nose, controlled, budgeted, the exhale of a woman returning a borrowed calm to its shelf.

"Well," said Hale, from the back, unsteadily.

"Lake ahead on the right in six miles," Ulyana said, radio-voice restored. "That's our fuel cache. Sam should be there."

Teodora looked at her own hands. They were shaking too, but honestly, openly, the way a student's hands are allowed to shake. She flexed her fingers and thought about what she'd seen, and what it meant, and what you were supposed to do with the knowledge that your hero was human — that the steadiest pilot in the interior carried a tremor in her through certain valleys, and had built her whole life, maybe, around routing around those valleys, and that today someone with money and a secret had paid her to fly straight into the oldest one.

The lake came up silver through the broken cloud. A canoe sat on the shore beside a stack of drums, and a man stood next to the canoe, motionless, watching them land. Even from half a mile out and closing, even through a wet windshield, Teodora could feel him watching — a stillness with a shape to it.

"That's Sam," Ulyana said. Something in her voice changed on his name. Teodora heard it and filed it away with the shaking hands, in the growing drawer of things Ulyana Iversen did not explain.

Sam Two-Rivers stood on the shore with his arms at his sides and watched the Beaver settle onto the water, and he did not wave, and he did not smile, and when Ulyana shut the engine down and swung out onto the float, the two of them looked at each other across ten feet of cold lake for a long moment — two people greeting each other like survivors of something.

"Sam," Ulyana said.

"Ulyana." His voice was low and rough, like it hadn't been used in a while. His eyes went past her, up the pass they'd just come through, and stayed there a beat too long. "You came through the notch."

"Had to. Weather."

Sam nodded slowly. He looked at the mountains the way other men looked at graves. "Weather," he said, like the word tasted wrong. Then he turned and picked up a drum as if nothing had happened, and said, in a voice pitched only for her, though Teodora caught every word in the dead-calm air:

"You shouldn't have brought them here, Ulyana. Some places keep what they're given."

The wind came off the glacier above the lake and moved the water in long silver lines, and Teodora, unbuckling in the right seat with her heart still drumming from the pass, understood that the trip had just changed shape — that the weather window Dez had promised was still open overhead, blue and innocent, and that a different kind of weather entirely had just rolled into camp.
