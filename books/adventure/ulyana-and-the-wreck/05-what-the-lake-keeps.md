## 5. What the Lake Keeps

The lake had a name on the survey maps — Kettle Lake, for its round shape and the steam-fog that rose off it in September like something boiling underneath — but nobody in the interior called it anything at all. It was simply the lake below the eastern ranges, and for ten years Ulyana had treated it the way you treat a bad tooth: never let your tongue find it.

Now she stood on its shore with her boots in the gravel and her breath hanging white in front of her, and there was no avoiding it. The Beaver sat moored at the edge of the ice skin forming along the shallows, and behind her, Sam Two-Rivers was driving the last tent stake with the flat of his axe, each swing landing with the patience of a man who had done this a thousand times and would do it a thousand more.

"Ground's still soft under the moss," he said, without looking up. "Stakes'll hold unless the wind comes off the glacier."

"It'll come off the glacier," Ulyana said.

"Then we tie everything twice."

That was the whole of their conversation for the next hour, and it was enough. That was the thing about Sam that Ulyana had always been grateful for and never quite able to name: he filled silence the way ballast fills a hull, not with noise but with steadiness. He'd been on the original expedition — she knew that, Hale's file had said so, and Dez had said it plainer over coffee in Fort Reliance — but he hadn't mentioned it once since they'd lifted off, and he didn't mention it now, and some part of her understood that his silence was a kindness, and another part of her understood it wasn't only that.

Teodora came down from the ridge line at midday with her cheeks wind-burned and her eyes shining, carrying a handful of grey rock like it was treasure.

"There's talus all through the draw above us," she reported. "Big slides. If a plane went in up there, pieces could've scattered for half a mile."

"That's if it went in up there," Ulyana said. "Hale's coordinates put it over the water."

Teodora looked out at the lake, which lay flat and pewter-coloured and entirely unhelpful. "It's deep?"

"Soundings say ninety feet at the middle." She hadn't read the soundings before this week. She'd made a point of never reading them. Now she could recite them.

Dez emerged from under the Beaver's wing wiping grease on a rag that had started life as one of Priya's — no. Not Priya. Priya Raman didn't exist yet; that discovery was four days away, though none of them knew it, and Ulyana would remember later how ordinary this afternoon had felt, how the worst days always wore the clothes of ordinary ones. Dez squinted at the water.

"So we drag the fish-finder around until it beeps," he said. "You know what I caught on one of those things once? A boot. Whole boot, laces tied."

"A boot."

"Somebody's boot. I gave it a decent burial." He hefted the sonar unit, a battered portable rig he'd bolted to a plywood skiff they'd brought folded in the Beaver's belly. "Ninety feet's nothing. This thing will read a loonie at eighty."

"Then let's go find somebody's plane instead," Ulyana said.

* * *

They ran transects for three days.

It was methodical work, the kind Ulyana loved best — grid lines drawn on a chart taped inside the skiff, Dez calling depths, Teodora rowing the legs with a rhythm she pretended was casual and was actually competitive. The lake gave them weeds and drop-offs and one enormous pike that surfaced beside the boat, regarded them with ancient contempt, and sank again. It did not give them aluminum.

"Nothing on line fourteen," Teodora called on the second afternoon, and then, quieter, when Ulyana took the oars: "Do you think it's really here?"

Ulyana watched the shoreline slide past. From the water, the crash zone Hale had described was a fold of grey scree between two shoulders of rock, unremarkable, anonymous, one fold among hundreds. Ten years ago she had memorized every fold in these ranges from the air, because her father had taught her that a pilot who knows her mountains never needs a map, and then she had spent ten years carefully forgetting them, the way you forget a language by refusing to speak it.

"Hale paid for a search," she said. "We're searching."

"That's not what I asked."

Ulyana shipped the oars and let the skiff drift. "No," she said. "It isn't. But it's what I can answer today."

Teodora accepted that with the particular grace of the young, who are used to adults handing them partial truths and have learned to pocket them without complaint. Then she cocked her head. "There's Hale."

Marcus Hale stood at the basecamp shore with binoculars raised toward the ridgeline, and he had been standing there, Ulyana realized, for quite a while. He'd barely left the shore in three days. He didn't row transects, didn't haul stakes, didn't complain about the freeze-dried food the way Dez complained about it professionally and at length. He watched the mountain the way a man watches a door he's expecting someone to come through.

"He doesn't act like a man looking for a missing colleague," Teodora said.

"No," Ulyana agreed. "He acts like a man looking for something else."

She dipped the oars and pulled for shore, and told herself the cold feeling in her stomach was just the lake air coming off the water. It was a lie she was getting good at telling.

* * *

The dream came the third night, as it always came when she got this far east, as reliable as weather.

In the dream she was eleven years old and sitting on the hangar floor at Fort Reliance with a wrench too big for her hand, and the radio on the workbench crackled, and her father's voice came through it the way it had come through it that night ten years ago, calm as a man reading a grocery list:

*Kettle Base, this is Two-Bravo-Kilo. We're crossing the pass early. Weather's building faster than the forecast.*

And in the dream she answered him — in reality she had been asleep, she had learned about the call only afterward, secondhand, from the transcript she'd read so many times she could recite it like scripture — but in the dream she answered, eleven years old with her wrench, and said, *Copy, Dad. Come home the long way.*

*The long way,* he repeated, and there was something in his voice in the dream that had never been in the transcript, something almost like a smile, or an apology. *Always the long way, little star.*

Then static. Then the static changed pitch, became the sound of wind, became the sound the wind makes through torn metal, and Ulyana woke in the dark of the tent with her heart going like a failing engine and Sam's fire burned down to orange eyes outside, and she lay listening to the lake lap the gravel and thought: *he knew.* In the dream he'd known. Some animal part of her insisted the real man had known too, that night, crossing the pass early into building weather, that the calm in his voice had been the calm of a man who has already decided something.

She got up and stood outside in the cold until her hands stopped shaking, and the stars over the eastern ranges were hard and close and indifferent, and she made herself look at them, all of them, even the ones over the pass.

* * *

She found Hale's tent lit past midnight.

She hadn't meant to spy. She'd gone out again — sleep was finished for the night, the way it often was out here — meaning to check the tie-downs, and Hale's lamp threw his shadow against the nylon, big and hunched, and through the gap where the flap didn't quite close she saw the map.

Not the survey chart. Not the search grid she'd drawn and pinned in the cook tent, the honest map of their honest-seeming work. This was older, creased into softness along folds that had been folded hundreds of times, and Hale was tracing a route on it with one finger, slowly, from the lake shore up through the draws to the ridgeline — a route no search party would need, because a search party works the water first, and they had worked the water for three days and found nothing, and any competent searcher would by now be re-examining the coordinates.

Instead Hale was studying a path. A specific path. As if he already knew where the answer was and needed only to know how to walk to it.

Ulyana stepped back from the tent, quiet on the frost-stiffened gravel, and stood a long moment in the dark with her breath smoking.

Three days of sonar. Ninety feet of water. Nothing. And a client who didn't seem surprised.

She thought about the charter request she'd signed without reading closely — triple rates, a missing survey plane, a name she should have refused on principle alone. She thought about how neatly the expedition had assembled, how easily the weather window had opened, how Sam had come aboard silent as a stone dropped in a well. She thought about her father's voice saying *always the long way,* and about the fact that Marcus Hale had come to her dock knowing exactly which wound to press.

Everything fit too well. That was the trouble. Real searches were ragged things, full of wrong turns and wasted fuel and arguments about lunch. This one moved like a play with the script already written, and she couldn't tell yet whether she was an actor in it or the audience being played.

"You're up late."

Sam's voice, low, from the direction of the fire. He was sitting on a log with a tin cup, wrapped in a blanket, watching the coals. He'd known she was awake. Probably he'd known before she did.

"Couldn't sleep," she said. "Lake's noisy."

"Lakes are honest," Sam said. "That's why people don't like sleeping near them." He sipped whatever was in the cup. The firelight moved on his face. "Tomorrow you'll want to run the east transects again?"

"The east transects found nothing."

"Mm," said Sam, in a tone that held, very quietly, absolutely nothing at all, and somehow said volumes anyway. He looked at her across the coals, and for a moment — the first time since Fort Reliance — his silence seemed less like kindness than like a man standing at a door with his hand on the latch, deciding whether to open it.

"Sam," she said. "Ten years ago. You were on the—"

"I'm going to turn in," he said, rising, folding the blanket with unhurried care. "Cold gets into the bones after midnight. You should too. Big day tomorrow."

He went into his tent, and the fire settled, and Ulyana stood alone under the indifferent stars with the lake whispering against the gravel, keeping whatever it kept.

Above camp, faint in the moonlight, she could see the ridgeline where Hale's finger had traced its patient route — and she could see, or thought she could see, higher up where the scree fields began, a light. Small, brief, moving.

A lantern. Or a signal. Or her eyes playing tricks in the cold.

She watched until it didn't come again, and then she went back to her tent and did not sleep, and in the morning she drew new transects covering the east shore twice, and told no one what she'd seen in the dark — except that when Teodora launched the skiff, Ulyana stood on the beach a moment longer than she needed to, looking up at the mountain, at the fold of grey scree between two shoulders of rock, and felt it looking back.
