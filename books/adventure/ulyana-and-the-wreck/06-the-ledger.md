## 6. The Ledger

Sam Two-Rivers had a rule about secrets: you could carry one, but you had to know its weight to the ounce, the way you'd know a pack you'd be wearing for years. This one weighed about four hundred grams. He knew because he'd weighed it — actually weighed it, on the spring scale in his mother's old kitchen, the morning after he came down off the mountain with it ten years ago.

A waterproof ledger, oilcloth-wrapped, brass-clamped, found in the wreckage of a Norseman on a ridge above a lake that didn't have a name on any map he trusted. Four hundred grams of paper and metal that had been sitting against his spine in various duffel bags ever since, through two moves, one marriage, one divorce, and more nights than he cared to count where he lay awake listening to it not rustle.

He'd brought it with him on this expedition, naturally. It hadn't even been a decision. When Ulyana called — *Sam, I need a guide, east ranges, Hale's money* — his hand was already reaching for the duffel with the false bottom before his mouth finished saying yes.

That was the thing about guilt. It didn't wait for your permission. It just packed itself.

* * *

The first night at basecamp, while Dez ran diagnostics on the Beaver by lantern light and Teodora practiced knots badly, Sam walked the shoreline alone. He told himself it was to check the boat landing. He told himself a lot of things these days; telling himself things was practically a profession.

The lake lay flat as poured tin under a sky going bruise-colored at the edges. Somewhere up there — he tilted his chin toward the ridgeline, a black serration against the last light — the wreck sat exactly where he'd left it, or mostly where he'd left it. He'd put things back. He'd been careful. He'd been nineteen years younger and shaking so hard he'd dropped his gloves twice, and he'd still been careful, because Sam Two-Rivers came from people who believed the dead noticed sloppiness.

He'd gone up looking for survivors. That was the part nobody asked about, because nobody knew he'd gone up at all. The official search had been called off after nine days — weather, expense, the usual arithmetic — and Sam had gone back on his own in October, when the creeks froze enough to walk, because Eli Iversen had once pulled him out of a whiteout by the collar like a kitten and Sam did not leave debts of that size unpaid.

He'd found the plane. He'd found what the plane held. And then he'd found the strongbox wedged under the pilot's seat, latch sprung, and inside it the ledger along with core samples wrapped in burlap, and he'd stood there in the cold reading entries by the light of a candle stub until his fingers stopped working, and somewhere in that frozen hour he had made the worst decision of his life, which was to take it.

Not to steal it. That was what he told himself, anyway, all these years. To keep it safe. Because the name written across the claim transfer page — countersigned, witnessed, dated three weeks before the flight — was A. Hale, and beneath it, in a different hand, a note that read like a man shouting into a well: *Survey results verified. Original staked 1931 by Fournier party. Transfer irregular. Keeping copy.*

Eli Iversen had known. Had flown out here knowing, carrying proof that Marcus Hale's father had taken a mineral strike off a dead man's claim and laundered it through a survey company, and three weeks later Eli Iversen was a smear of aluminum and ice on a ridgeline and everyone said *weather* and everyone said *pilot error* and the Hales went on being rich and respectable, and Sam had climbed down off that mountain with four hundred grams of truth in his pack and told no one, because who was he? A twenty-four-year-old guide whose testimony would be worth less than the paper it wasn't written on. He'd told himself he'd bring it forward when the time was right.

The time had never been right. The time had developed a remarkable talent for never arriving.

* * *

"You're brooding."

Teodora dropped onto the log beside him with the gracelessness of the very young, a tin mug in each hand. She handed him one. Coffee, by the smell of it, though "coffee" was generous; Dez bought whatever was cheapest and called it character-building.

"I'm thinking," Sam said.

"That's what brooding is when old guys do it." She blew on her mug. Up close her face had that open, hungry quality he remembered from himself at that age — the wanting-to-be-somewhere-else-all-the-time quality. "Can I ask you something? You were on the original expedition. With Ulyana's dad."

Here it comes, thought Sam, who had spent ten years rehearsing for this exact conversation with approximately forty different versions of a young person, none of whom had ever actually asked.

"Everybody asks," he said. "And everybody gets the same answer."

"Which is?"

"It was a long time ago, and the mountain keeps what the mountain keeps."

Teodora made a face. "That's such a guide answer."

"It's a true answer."

"It's a *guide* answer." She sipped, winced at Dez's coffee, sipped again anyway. "She won't talk about it either. Ulyana. I asked her once why she never flies the east side if she knows it so well, and she looked at me like I'd asked about her dead husband. Which she also doesn't have. Probably."

Sam watched the firelight from camp flicker on the water. "Some country you don't fly over twice."

"But we are, though." Teodora gestured with her mug at the whole dark eastern sky. "We're flying right into it. Doesn't that seem weird to you? She took this job knowing where it was."

Yes, thought Sam. It seemed extremely weird. It seemed weird in a way that had kept him awake half the night, turning over the same question: why would Ulyana Iversen, who had avoided these ranges with the discipline of a monk avoiding sin, say yes to Marcus Hale of all people, for a search she should have refused on instinct alone?

Unless she didn't know. Unless Hale had dressed it up. Unless —

Or unless some part of her had wanted to come back and couldn't admit it, which was a thing Sam understood from the inside better than he'd ever admit aloud.

"Kid," he said, "when you've flown as long as she has, you'll learn that people take jobs for reasons they haven't finished inventing yet."

Teodora considered this with the seriousness she gave everything. "That's also kind of a guide answer."

"I'm a guide. It's what I've got." He drained the terrible coffee and stood. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we run the sonar lines, and Ulyana wants an early start, and you do not want to be the reason we're late. She forgives weather. She does not forgive sleeping in."

* * *

In his tent, by flashlight, with the zipper shut and the flap double-pinned, Sam opened the false bottom of his duffel.

The ledger sat there in its oilcloth, patient as a stone. Ten years of patience. He unwrapped it — he couldn't help it, he did this maybe six times a year, a ritual he hated and needed — and turned the stiff waterproof pages by the small circle of light.

There it was. Page fourteen. The transfer of claim 44-East from the estate of Henri Fournier, deceased without heirs, executed by A. Hale, acting for Northern Crown Survey & Development, with the survey certification signed by a man named Aldous Wren, who Sam had later learned had drowned in a boating accident the following spring that everyone agreed was very sad and nobody investigated. And below, in Eli Iversen's tight, level hand — the hand Sam would know anywhere, having watched it draw maps and mend nets and once write out a check for Sam's mother's stove when the money ran short:

*Survey results verified. Original staked 1931 by Fournier party. Transfer irregular. Keeping copy.*

Keeping copy. Eli had known someone might come looking for the original. Eli had known the flight home was dangerous — Sam had only understood that sentence fully years later, sitting with it the way you sit with a stone in your boot. *Keeping copy* meant Eli had expected the original to disappear. Meant he'd mailed or hidden duplicates. Meant he'd boarded that Norseman knowing the odds and flying anyway, because the ore samples in the strongbox were worth more than the company that stole them could afford to have walking around.

And Sam had taken the copy and sat on it for a decade while the Hales got richer and Ulyana Iversen flew circles around the one place on earth she couldn't bear to look at.

He rewrapped it. His hands were steady now, which was new. Something had shifted since the dock — since watching Hale stand there with his expensive coat and his careful grief, mentioning Eli's name like a man laying a card face-up. Sam had watched Ulyana's jaw go tight and had thought: *she doesn't know what he really wants.* And then he'd thought, with a clarity that felt almost like relief: *but I do. And I'm standing ten feet from both of them, and I'm done.*

Give it back or burn it. Those were the options he'd narrowed it to on the flight east, watching cloud swallow the ranges he'd sworn off. Give it back — to Ulyana, presumably, though God knew what she'd do with it, whether it would heal anything or just reopen the whole wound in front of witnesses. Or burn it — end it forever, let the strike stay lost, let the dead stay dead, let the Hales keep their name and Ulyana keep her sky and Sam finally sleep.

He'd packed it either way. That was the joke of it. He'd spent ten years unable to choose between giving it back and burning it, so he'd carried it, and now the carrying was over because the choosing was due, and the mountain that kept what the mountain kept had apparently decided to collect.

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when the fire had burned to orange eyes and the camp breathed in slow unison — Sam heard footsteps on the gravel and went still.

Hale. Moving quietly between the tents, not toward the boats, not toward the latrine. Toward the supply tent, where the maps were kept. Sam slipped out of his own tent low and silent, an old habit from a younger life, and watched through the gap where the supply tent's flap didn't quite meet.

Hale had a map spread on a crate, angled toward his flashlight. Not the lake charts Ulyana had marked for the sonar grid. Something older, creased to lace at the folds, and Sam's stomach dropped through the gravel, through the permafrost, all the way to China, because he knew that map. He'd seen it once before — folded in the strongbox, tucked behind the ledger, drawn in Eli Iversen's hand with the ridge route inked in blue and a small X above the lake.

An X that was nowhere near the water. An X that was up on the ridge, where no sonar line would ever sweep.

Hale studied it for a long moment, lips moving slightly, memorizing. Then he refolded it along its worn creases, slid it inside his jacket, and killed the light.

Sam stood in the cold a long time after, heart going like a drum in a parade. So Hale had a piece of it already. Maybe not the ledger, maybe not the whole truth, but enough to know the lake was theater — enough to know they weren't searching the water at all, and had let Ulyana spend her days running sonar over nothing.

Which meant one of two things. Either Marcus Hale was playing them all gently, planning to steer the search uphill when the moment suited him. Or somebody else had gotten to that strongbox before Sam ever did, and Hale was working from a copy of a copy, chasing a ghost — and the person who'd left those boot prints, whoever they were, was ahead of every single one of them.

Sam went back to his tent and lay down with the duffel's false bottom under his shoulders like a stone pillow, and did not sleep at all.

In the morning, Ulyana clapped her hands at dawn and said, "Sonar lines, people, let's move," and Sam picked up his pack with four hundred grams of other people's sins riding against his spine, and followed her down to the water.
