## 7. Sabotage

Dez Okonkwo woke to the sound of a loon doing its best impression of a man being murdered, which was normal for the lake at five in the morning. What was not normal was the smell of raw gasoline drifting through the canvas of his tent like an uninvited guest who'd let himself in and started going through the cupboards.

He lay still for a moment, cataloguing. Gasoline smell: bad. Loon: fine. The wind off the glacier: cold enough to make a man reconsider his life choices, but that was every morning this far east. He'd been a bush mechanic for nineteen years and he had a rule about mornings — you don't get out of the bag until you know what kind of day it's decided to be.

The gasoline smell got stronger.

"Okay," Dez told the tent ceiling. "Okay, okay."

He dressed in the dark by feel, boots, wool, the canvas jacket Ulyana had teased him about for six years straight because it made him look, in her words, "like a dockhand from a black-and-white movie." He liked the jacket. It had pockets for everything, including the small flashlight he now clicked on and stepped out into the grey dawn holding like a man walking into a bar fight he hadn't started.

The Beaver sat at the dock in the flat water, and even from thirty feet away Dez could see what was wrong, because there were three things wrong, stacked up like a bad joke.

One: the port float rode low. Not dramatically low — not swamped, not sinking — but sitting maybe two inches deeper in the water than it had any business sitting, the way a man sits after hearing news he didn't want.

Two: the starboard tie-down rope, the one he'd doubled and backed up himself two days ago because Sam Two-Rivers had looked at the sky and said a word in his own language that Dez had come to understand meant *weather is coming*, that rope was cut. Not frayed. Not chewed by a wolverine, though God knew the wolverines up here would try. Cut clean, both strands, the ends lying in the wet grass like something dropped.

Three: the fuel drums.

They kept four drums staged on the gravel above the high-water line, bunged and sealed, and all four were standing open. No caps. No funnels. Just open mouths to the sky, and when Dez got close he saw the gravel around them was dark and reeked, and the grass downslope was dead in a brown streak where somebody had poured — poured, deliberately, wastefully, *expensively* — roughly eighty gallons of avgas into the ground.

Dez stood there for a long moment with his breath steaming and his flashlight beam shaking slightly, which annoyed him, because his hands did not shake. His hands were the steadiest thing about him. That was the whole point of Dez.

"Uly," he called, and then louder, because camp was spread along the tree line and nobody wakes gracefully at altitude. "ULYANA."

* * *

By seven they were all standing around the drums like mourners at a very specific funeral.

Teodora had her arms crossed so tight she looked like she was hugging herself for warmth, which she might also have been doing; she was twenty-three and had packed for enthusiasm, not for mornings. Priya Raman, the stowaway journalist whom nobody had yet figured out how to be rid of, was crouched at the edge of the dead-grass streak taking photographs with the focused calm of a woman who had found exactly the story she'd smelled from the start. Hale stood apart, hands in the pockets of a coat that cost more than the Beaver's annual inspection, wearing the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. Sam said nothing, which was standard, but he was looking at the cut rope the way other men look at old scars.

And Ulyana Iversen stood with her boot toe against one of the empty drums, rocking it gently, reading it.

"Tell me," she said. She wasn't looking at Dez. She was looking at the lake, at the plane, at the treeline, in that order, the way she read weather.

"Float's holed," Dez said. "Port side, below the waterline, aft of the second strake. Somebody drove something into it — I found a punch mark, clean, deliberate. Then they left the drain plug loose so it'd take on slow and we wouldn't notice till we tried to lift off." He paused. "That's the part that gets me. It's patient work. A hole you notice right away, that's vandalism. A hole you don't notice till you're fifty feet up over trees — that's murder with extra steps."

Nobody laughed. He hadn't really expected them to, but you say these things so they don't sit in your chest.

"And the fuel?"

"Poured out. All four drums. They took the caps with them." He held one up as evidence, then remembered he'd already established there were no caps, and put it back down. "Whoever it was wanted us here and wanted us unable to leave. Those are two different jobs, and they did both."

"They wanted us stranded," Teodora said.

"They wanted us *committed*," Ulyana corrected quietly. "Stranded is an accident. This is a decision."

Dez watched her do the thing she did — stand very still while everyone else's fear moved around her like water around a piling. Ten years he'd worked for her and he'd seen her scared exactly twice, once in a cockpit with a fire warning she talked down to a landing, and once just now, briefly, before she put it away somewhere behind her face.

"So we're thinking bear?" Teodora offered, without conviction. "A bear that hates aviation?"

"A bear that unscrews bungs and pockets them," Dez said, "and carries a center punch, sure. Very handy bear. Does taxes."

Priya lowered her camera. "I checked the perimeter yesterday evening," she said. "Habit. There were no tracks then. This morning there are boot prints along the gravel bar — Vibram lug, size eleven or twelve, coming from the north shore trail and going back the same way. Single set. I photographed them before anyone walked over them, which is more than I can say for the rest of you, no offense."

"Some taken," Dez muttered.

"The north shore trail doesn't go anywhere," Sam said.

Everyone turned. It was the most words Sam had produced in three days, and they landed in the group like stones in still water.

"It goes to the old survey line," Sam went on, after letting the silence ripen the way he liked to. "Cut in '61, abandoned in '63. Nobody walks it. Nobody has a reason to."

"Except someone who knows it's there," Hale said.

"Yes," Sam agreed, and looked at Hale for exactly one second longer than was comfortable, and then looked away, and Dez, watching, felt the first cold thread of something he didn't have a name for yet.

* * *

They spent the morning doing what Dez told them to do, which was the correct division of labor in any organization containing Dez.

The float came off the strut with three of them hauling and swearing in rotation — Teodora swore in two languages, which impressed him; Hale swore in a way that suggested boarding school; Ulyana didn't swear, she just went quiet, which was worse. They rolled the float up onto the gravel and Dez got inside it with a trouble light and found the damage properly.

It was, he had to admit, good work. He hated that. The punch had gone through the aluminum at the seam where the skin was thinnest, angled so the flexing of the water landing would work it wider over time rather than tear it open at once. Whoever did it understood floats. Understood *this* float. Knew where the inspection plate was, because they'd loosened that too, gone in through it rather than leaving outside scarring.

"You'd need to know Beavers," Dez said, head and shoulders inside the hull, voice echoing tinny. "Not generally. This one specifically."

Ulyana crouched at the opening. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning our ghost has either been aboard this airplane or read its maintenance logs." He backed out, blinking in the daylight. "Or built it. Or — " he stopped, because the next option had arrived in his mouth and tasted bad.

"Or worked on it before," Ulyana finished.

The Beaver was older than all of them except Sam. It had passed through three owners before Ulyana bought it off a bankrupt outfit in Fort Nelson. Dez had gone through it rivet by rivet when she bought it, but there were years before that, years nobody had logged.

"I'll patch it," he said, changing the subject the way you change a tire, quickly and with some grunting. "I've got aluminum sheet and sealant in the kit. Two days if the weather holds. Three if it doesn't. And Uly — " he jerked his chin at the dead grass, the empty drums. "We're dry. That was our reserve. We've got what's in the tanks, maybe nine hours total, and I'm not burning any of it until I know why."

"Nine hours," Hale repeated. "To where?"

"To nowhere useful," Dez said. "Nearest strip with fuel is two hundred miles west, and that's assuming a tailwind and a friendly reception. We are, in the technical sense, marooned."

* * *

The paranoia arrived sometime after lunch, and it arrived the way weather does — not all at once but in layers, each one colder.

It started small. Teodora noticed her logbook wasn't where she'd left it, then found it under her cot, and spent twenty minutes glaring at everyone as though one of them had developed a passion for aviation paperwork. Priya announced she'd be locking her notes in her duffel from now on, and asked, sweetly, whether anyone else intended to lock anything, and got silence back that was louder than answers. Hale took to walking the perimeter alone in the evenings, which helped nothing, because a man pacing a treeline looks guilty whether he's checking for intruders or being one.

Even Dez caught himself doing it — counting tools at the end of the day, which he never bothered with normally because nobody steals a torque wrench in the middle of nowhere. But he counted them anyway, twice, and the second time he found himself wondering whether he'd miscounted the first time or whether the count had changed, and he sat down on a crate and laughed at himself, alone, in the grey light, and the laugh didn't come out right.

Because here was the arithmetic that wouldn't balance: whoever did this had walked in on a trail only a local would know, worked on their specific aircraft with knowledge of its guts, and vanished north into country that ate people. That narrowed the field to nearly nobody. And nearly nobody included, whether he liked it or not, the four men and two women currently eating beans around the same stove.

Sam sat at the edge of the firelight, as always, cleaning his rifle he hadn't fired, and Dez caught himself watching Sam's hands and hating himself for it. Twenty-two years ago Sam had carried Dez's father's gear out of a burn area when the old man's heart quit on a fire line. You don't suspect a man who carried your father out of a fire. Except Dez had also been a mechanic long enough to know that people were not machines, that they broke in ways that left no inspection record, and that the ones who loved you could still hurt you worst by keeping things from you. Sam was keeping something. Dez had known it since the dock. He didn't know what, and the not-knowing had started to itch like a cast.

Ulyana appeared beside him with two enamel mugs and handed him one. Coffee, mostly. Some coffee.

"You're counting your wrenches again," she said.

"Am I that obvious?"

"You've recounted the same box three times. If a wrench were missing, it would have filed a flight plan by now." She blew on her mug. For a while neither of them said anything, which was one of the reasons he'd stayed with her operation so long — she didn't fill silence with noise the way most bosses did.

"Talk to me, Dez. Mechanic to pilot. How bad is it, really?"

He gave her the real answer, because she'd earned it. "Float's fixable. Fuel's the problem. Even patched, we can't lift a full load, and we can't stage fuel we don't have. Best case, I fly her out half-empty to the strip, tanker up, come back — that's two days' flying and it leaves basecamp with no plane and no radio backup. Worst case, the weather closes and we walk out, which from here is a week through country that already killed one expedition." He turned the mug in his hands. "Somebody did the math on all of that, Uly. Before we ever landed. They knew how much reserve we'd carry, they knew the float design, they knew the trail. This wasn't opportunity. This was planning."

She was quiet a moment. Across the fire, Teodora was showing Priya how to feed the stove without smoking them all out, and Hale stood at the edge of the light with his back to them, talking low into the satellite phone that — Dez noted, filed it away — he'd started taking behind the tents lately instead of using it in the open.

"That's what I keep coming back to," Ulyana said finally, and her voice had gone somewhere flat and far away, the voice she used near the eastern ranges. "We were brought here. The charter, the timing, the route — I keep telling myself it's coincidence, and then I look at a holed float and four empty drums and coincidence starts looking like a plan wearing a disguise." She sipped. "Someone wanted this expedition to happen, Dez. And wanted it stuck."

"You think it's Hale."

"I think Hale lies the way other men breathe — easily, and often, and sometimes for good reasons. I think he knew more about this survey plane than he admitted on the dock. And I think," she said, slowly, "that whatever he's after, he's not the only one after it. Because Hale didn't cut that rope. Hale was asleep ten feet from me, and I heard him snore like a man with a clear conscience, which is its own suspicious talent."

Despite everything, Dez grinned. "So we've got a ghost."

"We've got a host," Ulyana said. "And we're the party." She drained her coffee, stood, and looked north across the black water toward the treeline and the invisible trail beyond it, and Dez saw her shoulders settle the way they did before she flew into something the rest of them would call impossible.

"Double the watch tonight," she said. "And Dez — check the rudder cables tomorrow. If someone knows this airplane well enough to hole a float quiet, I want to know what else they touched."

* * *

He found it at noon the next day, halfway through the rudder cables, lying in the bilge of the hull where nobody had any reason to reach except a mechanic with skinny arms and a grudge against gravity.

A center punch. Professional grade, the kind that comes in a machinist's kit, not a hardware store. Wiped clean — wiped *carefully* clean, handle and all, which takes time and cloth and patience.

But the tip still held a faint bright scar of aluminum, and wedged into the knurled grip, where the metal met the plastic collar, was a single thread of canvas. Navy blue. The same weave, Dez realized with a sensation like stepping off a stair that isn't there, as the repair patch on the knee of the coat Sam Two-Rivers had worn every single day since they'd landed on this lake.

Dez sat back on his heels in the bottom of the hull, the little tool sitting in his palm like a coal, and did not move for a long time.

Above him, through the open door, he could hear Ulyana's voice on the shore, giving orders, steady as a compass needle. He thought about calling her up. He thought about putting the punch in his pocket and saying nothing, because some knowledge, once spoken, can't be unspoken, and because Sam had carried his father out of a fire, and because a navy coat proves nothing, coats share threads, threads travel —

And then he thought about a hole punched quiet into a float, angled to kill, patient as winter, and he climbed out of the hull with the evidence closed in his fist and went to find her, and behind him the lake lay flat and silver and full of things it wasn't telling.
