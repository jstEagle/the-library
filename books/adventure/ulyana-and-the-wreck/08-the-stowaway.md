## 8. The Stowaway

Teodora found the drum on the third morning, and she would spend a long time afterward trying to decide whether she'd actually known before that — some pilot's sixth sense, the way you can feel an airplane is carrying ten pounds more than your paperwork says it should.

The drums sat at the edge of camp in a tidy row: fuel, spare oil, the food cache Dez had triple-strapped because of bears. Except one of them had been re-strapped wrong. The knot was a bowline where Dez tied everything in trucker's hitches. Teodora had spent enough hours watching him work to know his hands the way other people knew handwriting.

She stood over the drum for a full minute, telling herself it was nothing. Then she knocked on it.

The knock came back. Softly. Like someone knocking from inside a coffin and trying not to be rude about it.

Teodora did not scream, which she counted as a professional achievement. She did back up three fast steps and grab the nearest object, which turned out to be a fish filleting knife Sam had left on a stump, so her stance of armed readiness involved holding a gutting knife at a drum of aviation fuel, which in retrospect was possibly the most dangerous thing anyone did that week.

"Okay," she said, loud and steady, the way Ulyana talked to frightened passengers. "Whoever you are, come out slow."

A pause. Then, muffled: "Is the big man with the wrench nearby?"

"Dez? He's fixing the float."

"Thank God." A groan of pure suffering. "If he finds me like this I will never live it down. Give me a minute. My legs have opinions."

The lid came off and a woman unfolded out of the drum like a jack-in-the-box designed by someone with a grudge against jack-in-the-boxes. She was small, dark-haired, glasses fogged solid, wearing three layers and a look of profound regret about her life choices. She stood swaying in the gray morning light, blinking like something newly born.

"Hi," she said. "Priya Raman. Don't yell yet. Let me make my case first. It's a good case. I've been rehearsing it for two days inside a fuel drum, and honestly the rehearsal has gone better than this."

"You were in a *fuel* drum?"

"Empty fuel drum. Mostly empty. There was residual. I've been lightly marinating in Jet A for forty-eight hours and I want you to know I have thoughts about it." She peeled off her glasses and wiped them on her sleeve. "Also I need to use the facilities very badly, and then I'll make my case."

* * *

Ulyana made her case right back, and it was shorter.

"No."

They stood by the cook tent, Priya freshly returned from the trees and looking marginally more human, wrapped in the spare blanket Dez kept for shock victims. Ulyana's arms were crossed in a way Teodora had learned meant the conversation was already over; she just hadn't told the other person yet.

"I understand," Priya said. "But hear me out—"

"There's nothing to hear out. You stowed away on a private expedition into remote wilderness. You could've died. You could've gotten one of my crew killed looking for you if we'd had an emergency. You're a liability, and there's no plane here to send you home in, which means every day you're here is a day I'm responsible for you."

"That's fair," Priya said. "All true. And yet."

"And yet what?"

"And yet you haven't asked me why I came." Priya pushed the glasses up her nose. Teodora noticed her hands weren't shaking. Whatever else she was, scared wasn't on the list. "You haven't asked how I found you, or how I got into your cargo in Yellowknife without your mechanic noticing, or why I was willing to sit in a steel drum for eleven hours of flight time. People do desperate things for stories, Ms. Iversen, but nobody does *that* for a story about a missing survey plane. Nobody cares about survey planes. I care about permits."

Ulyana went very still. It was a small stillness, the kind Teodora had only seen twice — once when a bear wandered through camp, once when Hale said her father's name on the dock.

"What permits?" Ulyana said.

"Hale Holdings filed mineral exploration permits through a shell company called Northlight Ventures. Northlight's registered address is a law office in Vancouver that also registers, and I checked, four other shells, all of them tracing back through probate records to an estate with the surname Hale. The permit area covers the eastern ranges." Priya let that sit. "The same eastern ranges your father's expedition flew into ten years ago. The same ranges Marcus Hale paid you triple rates to search, while telling you he was looking for a missing aircraft."

The wind moved through the spruce. Somewhere across the lake, water lapped at the floats of the crippled Beaver.

"How do you know all that?" Teodora asked, before she could stop herself.

"Because I'm a journalist, and because Marcus Hale's family didn't lose their fortune in bad investments. They lost it in a scandal that got buried so deep even the people who profited from it won't talk about it. And because somebody at the land registry office in Yellowknife has a cousin who owes me a favor." Priya shrugged. "I traced the paper. Paper doesn't lie. People lie, and then paper catches up with them, usually somewhere cold and far from lawyers."

Ulyana looked at her for a long moment. Then she said, quietly, "Sam. Get Hale. Tell him we need to talk."

* * *

Hale took it better than Teodora expected, which was to say he only broke one thing. A tin cup. He set it down too hard on a rock and the handle snapped off, and he stared at it as if it had betrayed him personally.

"A journalist," he said. "In my charter. In my *drums*."

"In our drums," Ulyana corrected. "And she's not wrong, Marcus. Northlight Ventures. You want to tell me about that, or should we let Ms. Raman do what she does?"

Hale looked around the circle — Ulyana, arms crossed; Dez leaning against the woodpile with his arms also crossed, a man who communicated primarily through crossed arms; Sam sitting on a stump whittling something, face unreadable as lake ice; Teodora herself, hovering; and Priya, blanket-caped, taking actual notes in a waterproof notebook, which Teodora felt was a bold choice given the circumstances.

"The permit is real," Hale said finally. "It's mine. Filed under the company name because — frankly — because if I filed it under Hale, every creditor my father ever burned would come out of the woodwork. Northlight is a privacy measure, not a conspiracy."

"It's both," Priya said pleasantly. "Most things are."

"Priya." Ulyana's voice had an edge on it now. "Not helping."

"I'm being accurate. Accuracy helps eventually. It's just painful first, like physical therapy."

Hale dragged a hand down his face. Ten days in the bush had roughed him up — beard coming in gray-streaked, hands cracked, the city polish worn down to something almost human. "My father believed there was a strike in those ranges. A real one. He spent the last years of his life obsessed with it, and the last years of the family's money chasing it. If it exists, it's the only thing that puts the name back together. That's the truth. The rest of it—" He glanced at Priya's notebook. "The rest of it I'll tell when there aren't witnesses writing it down."

"Too late," Priya said. "I already have the shape of it. What I don't have is the middle, and middles are where the story lives."

"There is no story," Ulyana said. "There's a wreck, a lake, and a lot of weather. We find the plane or we don't, and then everyone goes home."

"Then you won't mind if I observe," Priya said, "and write down whatever happens to be observable."

Teodora watched Ulyana's jaw work. She was learning to read her captain the way she read weather — pressure changes, fronts building, the moment before a system breaks. Right now every line of Ulyana's body said *throw her in the lake*, except her eyes, which kept flicking toward the Beaver on its hoists, float patched but engine still half apart, and doing arithmetic Teodora could practically see: no plane, no way out, six mouths, dwindling food, and a stranger who knew things.

"We keep her," Ulyana said at last. "Under rules. One: you don't leave camp alone. Two: you don't touch anything that flies, floats, or fuels. Three—" she stepped closer, and even Teodora felt it in her chest, "—if anyone gets hurt because of you, no story on earth will save you from me. Are we clear?"

"Crystal," Priya said. "And for what it's worth — thank you. I know I'm a problem."

"You're not a problem," Ulyana said, turning away. "Problems have solutions. You're a variable."

* * *

By evening, the camp had done what camps do, which is absorb the newcomer through the twin mechanisms of chores and food. Dez put Priya on dish duty with the grim satisfaction of a man assigning penance. Sam silently handed her a second helping of stew, which Teodora understood, from long observation, was the highest honor Sam Two-Rivers bestowed on anyone short of family. Priya ate it like it was the best meal of her life, which, given the previous forty-eight hours, it may genuinely have been.

Teodora found her afterward down at the shoreline, notebook out, writing by the last flat gray light.

"You're really not scared," Teodora said. "Of any of this. Of her."

"Oh, I'm terrified of her." Priya didn't look up. "I've interviewed warlords with softer eyes. But here's the thing about fear — you can be afraid of a person and still know they're telling you the truth. When she said *variable*, she wasn't threatening me. She was classifying me. Pilots think in variables. Wind is a variable. Fuel burn is a variable. I'm weather to her." She smiled faintly. "I can work with weather."

"Why'd you really come?" Teodora sat down on a drift log. "The permits, the story — okay. But eleven hours in a drum. That's not a story. That's something else."

Priya stopped writing. For a moment she looked younger, tired around the eyes in a way the glasses usually hid.

"My first real job was covering a mining town in the territories that got sold a dream and left holding the tailings," she said. "Company went bust, executives retired comfortable, town died. I wrote it all up. Nobody cared. The pattern just kept repeating, year after year, different names, same theft. So when I saw a Hale permit crawling over ground where a whole expedition died ten years ago and nobody was ever quite able to say why —" she tapped the notebook against her knee, "— I decided this time I'd be standing there when the pattern finished, instead of reading about it after."

"And if the pattern ends with something worth killing for?"

Priya looked at her then, really looked, and Teodora realized the fear she'd claimed not to feel had just arrived, punctual as a scheduled flight.

"Then I'll have written it down," Priya said, "before it gets to me. That's the whole job, kid. Be the record. Records are harder to bury than people."

Teodora walked back up to the tents with that sentence stuck in her chest like a splinter. Above the tree line the clouds had organized themselves into something with intent — Dez had squinted at them at supper and said *two days, maybe three*, in the tone he used for forecasts he didn't like. The Beaver's engine parts lay on a tarp like a patient mid-surgery. And somewhere out past the ridgeline, in the country they'd all been circling without saying its name, was whatever it was that had brought all five of them — six, counting the woman in the drum — to the same cold shore.

She was almost asleep when she heard it: voices low by the cook tent. Ulyana and Sam, talking in the dark, too quiet to catch the words — but she caught the tone, and it was the tone people used when they'd stopped planning the search and started planning around something.

Teodora lay staring at the canvas and thought: *she knows something. Our captain knows something, and Sam knows the same something, and neither of them is telling us.*

Outside, the wind shifted, and carried the smell of snow.
