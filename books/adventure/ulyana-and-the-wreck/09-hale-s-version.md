## 9. Hale's Version

The stove in the cook tent had a temper. It burned hot at the door and cold at the pipe, so that whoever sat nearest the firebox sweated through their shirt while whoever sat at the far end hunched into their parka like a man waiting out a hockey game in an unheated arena. Marcus Hale had spent three days learning the tent's geography, and he had claimed the middle bench — close enough to be warm, far enough to watch everyone's faces.

Nobody was watching his face tonight. That was the problem.

Dez had the stove corner, mending a float strut bracket with a file and a patience that seemed geological. Teodora sat cross-legged on her sleeping bag, pretending to read a paperback she'd finished two days ago — Hale had seen her turn the same page four times. Sam Two-Rivers was outside splitting kindling in the dark, which was either industry or avoidance, and with Sam it was impossible to tell. Priya Raman, the stowaway who had somehow become furniture, sat closest to the canvas wall with her notebook open, writing something that Hale strongly suspected was about him.

And Ulyana Iversen stood at the tent flap with her back half-turned, looking out at the lake as if the water owed her money.

Three days of searching the shoreline. Three days of sonar sweeps that came back clean as a hymn. Three days of Ulyana being scrupulously, professionally civil to him, which from Ulyana Iversen was worse than being sworn at. He'd been sworn at before. He'd earned it.

He cleared his throat. "I owe you all an explanation."

The file stopped moving. The paperback stopped turning. Even the stove seemed to lean in, popping a knot of spruce in the firebox.

Ulyana didn't turn around. "We have an explanation. Missing survey plane. Missing pilot. We're looking for both."

"No," Hale said. "We don't."

* * *

He'd rehearsed this on the flight east, somewhere over the muskeg between refueling stops, composing sentences in his head the way he used to compose them for boardrooms — lead with the concession, establish stakes, control the frame. But the boardroom voice died somewhere over the pass, and what came out now was just a tired man's voice, pitched low enough that they had to stop what they were doing to hear it.

"There is no survey plane. There's no missing pilot named Kowalczyk. I invented him on the dock at Fort Simpson because I knew you wouldn't come otherwise." He looked at Ulyana's back. "I'm sorry. I've been sorry for ten years, and it hasn't once made me less of a coward, so I've decided to try the truth instead and see if it works better."

"Try it," Ulyana said, without turning.

So he did.

"My father was Edmund Hale. Some of you are old enough to remember the name. Hale Minerals. In the sixties we were nothing — my father flying a Super Cub out of Yellowknife with a Geiger counter bungeed to the strut, staking claims by feel and stubbornness. He found things. That was the one thing you could never take away from him; he could smell ore under forty feet of granite the way a dog smells a buried bone. Everyone said so, even the men he ruined."

Priya's pen had stopped. Hale took that as encouragement.

"In 1963 he flew a joint expedition into these ranges. Six men, two aircraft, a season of traverses. One of those men was a geologist named Iversen — Erik Iversen." He watched Ulyana's shoulders go still. "My father's partner. His friend, he always said. He said it so often I stopped believing it around age twelve.

"They found something. Core samples came out of that ridge that made the assayer in Yellowknife ask my father twice whether he'd mixed up his bags. Uranium, some rare earths, grades that didn't make sense for the formation. My father filed the claim. And then—" Hale spread his hands, palms up, the gesture of a man showing he holds nothing. "Then the claim disappeared. Re-filed under a company called Northcan Development. Different names on the registration. Same ground. By the time anyone looked closely, Northcan had optioned it to a consortium, the consortium had sold to a smelter contract, and the money was three moves deep in accounts nobody could subpoena. Except my father's name was on the original filing, and when the fraud surfaced — and it surfaced, there were questions in the legislature — guess whose signature was the one everybody recognized."

"The ledger," Dez said quietly, from the stove corner. He wasn't asking a question so much as naming the shape of the hole in the story.

"You know about ledgers," Hale said. "Of course you do; you're a mechanic. Everything honest in this business lives in a logbook. My father kept one. Claim stakes, sample numbers, dates, witnesses. If that book ever surfaced, it proved the re-filing was forged — and it proved whose idea the forgery was. Not his. He was the front, but he wasn't the hand. The hand belonged to the man who ran Northcan, and that man spent the next thirty years making sure no one ever connected him to any of it, while my father drank himself through two divorces and a bankruptcy and told anyone who'd listen that the proof was still out there, in the wreck, waiting."

Teodora spoke for the first time, her voice careful. "The wreck. You mean—"

"I mean the plane that went down in these ranges in 1971 carrying my father's partner and the second copy of everything." Hale finally let himself look directly at Ulyana, because a man couldn't confess to a person's shoulder blades forever. "Your father, Ulyana. Erik Iversen went down carrying my father's proof, and my father spent the rest of his life trying to find that wreck, and never did, and died in a care home in Edmonton in 1994 with a map taped inside his wardrobe door and a letter in his effects addressed to me that I didn't open until after the funeral."

"What did the letter say?" Priya asked. She'd given up pretending not to write.

Hale laughed — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it. "It said, *Marcus, if you're reading this, I'm dead and you're poor, and the only thing I ever did right was love your mother, and the only thing I ever did wrong was sign my name.* Then eleven pages of coordinates, bearings, lake elevations, and paranoid instructions about who to trust, none of whom are alive. Your father's last flight wasn't a mail run, Ulyana. Erik was flying the ledger and the core samples out for my father. They were going to go to the mining recorder together. My father swore it in the letter — swore it on my mother's grave, which knowing him was either the holiest oath available or completely meaningless, and after fifty years I still can't tell you which."

Silence. The stove ticked. Outside, Sam's axe had stopped, and Hale wondered how long it had been stopped — how much of this Sam Two-Rivers had already heard, standing in the dark with snow gathering on his shoulders.

"So the survey plane," Ulyana said at last. She turned around then, and her face was exactly what he'd expected: not fury, worse, something patient and cold, a face like the lake ice in November. "There was never a plane."

"There was never a plane."

"And the triple rate. The debt you mentioned on the dock. You said my father's name and a debt, and I got on a plane with you."

"You did," Hale said. "And I'll answer for that as long as you make me. But understand what I knew when I hired you. I knew where the wreck likely was — my father's map put it within twenty miles of here. I knew I couldn't find it alone; I'm fifty-eight years old and I get winded on stairs. And I knew that the only pilot alive who might fly these ranges hard enough and long enough to crack it was the daughter of the man who died in it." He held up a hand before she could speak. "I told myself I was sparing you. That's the lie cowards tell — that the cruelty is a kindness. I told myself you'd want to know. Maybe you would have. But I didn't give you the choice, and that's the part I can't dress up."

"Why now?" This from Dez, still working the file against the bracket, though Hale noticed the strokes had gone slow and even, the rhythm of a man listening with his hands. "Ten years, Hale. Why drag this up now?"

"Because someone else has." Hale reached into his inner pocket and drew out a photograph, worn soft at the corners, and laid it on the crate between them. "Six weeks ago I received this in the mail. No note. It's a section of my father's old map — the wardrobe map — photographed, not copied. Someone has it. Someone has been to the country recently enough to photograph it, and they mailed it to me like a calling card. Two weeks later my lawyer tells me a mineral claim has been staked on the same ground, filed by a shell company out of Vancouver. The same ground, gentlemen and ladies, that Northcan stripped in 1964 and abandoned when the politics turned bad. Somebody thinks there's enough left to come back for. And somebody wants me to know they know I know."

Priya leaned forward. "You think it's the same people."

"I think it's the same *money*. Money doesn't die; it just changes letterhead." Hale rubbed his eyes. "If that ledger surfaces, it doesn't just restore my family's name — it opens the whole grave. The theft, the cover-up, every director Northcan ever had, some of whom are sitting on boards in Toronto right now with knighthoods and endowed chairs. There are men alive today who would burn this entire valley to keep that ledger underwater. And I flew four innocent people and one journalist into the middle of it, because I wanted my father's sin off my back more than I wanted to be sensible."

He looked around the tent, at each of them in turn. Dez, unreadable. Teodora, pale. Priya, writing fast now, which should have frightened him and instead felt like absolution of a kind — at least someone was keeping the record honestly.

Ulyana hadn't moved. "Say the rest of it."

"The rest of it." He exhaled. "The rest of it is that I don't know if I'm telling you the truth to help you or to recruit you. I've spent my whole life managing people, Ulyana, and I'm good at it, and that's precisely why you shouldn't believe a word I say. Check everything. My father's letter is in my kit, sealed, and any of you can read it. The map too. I'm done being the only one holding the cards — mostly because I've finally figured out that the man who dealt them to me was cheating."

For a long moment nobody spoke. Then Ulyana crossed the tent, picked up the photograph, studied it, and set it down again with terrible gentleness, the way you'd handle something that might be loaded.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we widen the sonar grid to the north arm of the lake. And Hale — you fly with me. You can navigate."

"That's not forgiveness," Hale said.

"No," Ulyana agreed. "That's arithmetic. I need your eyes and you need mine, and neither of us trusts the other, which makes us even." She paused at the tent flap, silhouetted against the blue dark and the first flakes of the evening's snow. "One question before I sleep. This fixer your father used. Northcan's man. Does he have a name?"

Hale felt the temperature of the tent drop, though the stove hadn't changed.

"Voss," he said. "Anton Voss. Why?"

Ulyana looked out at the lake, where the snow was beginning to hiss against the water, and said, very quietly:

"Because somebody's been burning a fire on the far shore since Tuesday. And I checked the shoreline yesterday. Nobody's camped there."
