## 10. Ascent

The float patch cured overnight under a tarp and a heat lamp Dez had rigged from the camp stove, and by morning the Beaver sat on the lake looking whole again, which was more than could be said for any of us.

We left at first light because Ulyana Iversen does not believe in second light. The plan was simple: fly the short hop to the far shore where the valley narrowed, beach the plane on gravel, and climb. Sam had marked the route on the topo with a grease pencil — three switchbacks through the timberline scrub, then the scree field, then the ridge. He drew it without speaking much, the way he did everything, and when he finished he set the pencil down behind his ear like a man putting away a tool he'd need again.

"Four hours up if the weather holds," he said.

"And if it doesn't?" Teodora asked.

"Then it's five," Sam said, "and you'll wish it was four."

Dez stayed with the plane. Someone had to, after what happened to the tie-downs, and nobody argued about who. He shook each of our hands at the waterline, which was unlike him, and held mine a half-second longer than the others.

"Come back down this mountain," he said. "All four of you. I've got coffee on by dark or I'm docking your pay."

"You don't pay me," Teodora said.

"Then I'll dock something else. Your dignity. It's plentiful."

Hale came last up the gravel, breathing hard already, wearing boots that looked like they'd been bought in a city and hated ever since. He'd insisted on coming. I'd let him, over Sam's silent objection, because whatever was waiting on that ridge belonged to his family as much as to mine, though neither of us had said so out loud. Some inheritances you carry whether you want them or not.

I looked at the mountain once, all the way up, before we started. You're supposed to do that. My father taught me that too — *look at the whole animal before you put your hand on it* — and for ten years I had refused to look at this one, banking east around it, adding fuel miles, telling myself it was weather patterns, tell myself anything, Ulyana, the interior is full of excuses and they all fly.

The mountain didn't look back. Mountains never do. That's the part people get wrong about them — they think a mountain is watching you, judging, some granite god taking your measure. It isn't. A mountain is just there, older than your grief and indifferent to it, and the only thing that's watching is you.

* * *

My father taught me to read wind on rock when I was nine years old, on a ridge not so different from this one, two hundred miles west and a lifetime shallower.

We'd stopped for lunch on a slab of granite warm from the sun, and I'd been complaining — I remember complaining; I did a great deal of it at nine — about the cold gusts coming off the face above us.

"That's not cold," he said. "That's information." He took my hand and pressed it flat against the rock. "Feel that? Warm stone heats the air touching it. Hot air rises. So on a sunny day, air climbs these faces all afternoon, and when it crests the ridge it spills over the top like water over a dam. You can't see it. But you can read what it leaves behind."

"Like what?"

He pointed to a line of snow patches clinging to the lee side of a boulder field, all of them swept clean on the windward faces. "Snow tells you where the wind has been. Trees tell you where it always goes — see how they lean? Clouds tell you what it's about to do. And lichen—" he touched a crust of pale green, "—lichen tells you the truth, because lichen never lies and never hurries. It only grows on the side of the rock that gets fed."

I pressed my palm against the stone and felt nothing but cold and my own pulse, and I told him so, disgusted.

"You will," he said. "It takes ten thousand landings. But one day you'll put your hand on a rock and it'll tell you a story, and you'll believe it, and it'll keep you alive." He ruffled my hair with a hand that smelled of avgas and spruce smoke. "The country talks, little owl. Most people just never shut up long enough to hear it."

Little owl. He called me that because I'd sit up past bedtime in the cabin, wide-eyed, refusing to sleep while the midnight sun came through the window. Ten years dead now, and I still heard it every time the light went gold and low across open water.

I thought about that slab of granite all morning as we climbed, because the rock here was talking too, and I found I could still hear it, which surprised me. Grief, I had assumed, was a kind of deafness. Apparently not. Apparently it was just another frequency, and the country broadcast on all of them.

* * *

The timberline scrub gave way to scree by mid-morning, and the scree was honest work — ankle-deep shale that slid and clattered and made conversation impossible, which suited everyone. Hale struggled. To his credit, he didn't complain; he just climbed badly and steadily, like a man paying off a debt in installments. Twice I turned back to check on him and twice he waved me off with a grimace that meant *keep going, I'd rather die than be pitied*, and I understood that particular dialect fluently.

Teodora climbed like she flew — fast, hungry, twenty feet ahead and pretending she wasn't showing off. Sam moved through the talus the way water moves downhill, without apparent effort and without any decision visible, and I caught myself watching him more than once. He had been here before. He had been here with my father. And in eleven days of expedition he had not said one word about it, and the silence had weight now, real weight, like ballast strapped into the cargo bay.

At the halfway bench — a shelf of flat ground below the last headwall, where a stream ran cold and loud — we stopped to drink. Sam crouched at the water's edge and cupped a handful and didn't drink it, just looked at it.

"I fished here," he said, to the stream, or maybe to me. "Your dad caught a grayling right there, big as my forearm. Said it was a sign we'd eat well the whole trip." He let the water run through his fingers. "He was usually right about things like that."

"He was usually right about most things," I said.

Sam nodded slowly. "Not that night, though."

And that was all. He stood, shouldered his pack, and started up the headwall, and the rest of us followed, and I spent the next forty switchbacks turning that sentence over like a stone with something underneath it.

*Not that night.*

What had my father been wrong about?

* * *

The headwall was where the wind lived. We came up onto its shoulder around noon and it hit us sideways, a hard southwest flow funneling through the col, and I did what I always do in wind: I stopped, and I listened, and I read.

The snow streaks ran off the ridgeline to the northeast — steady flow, not gusting. The clouds were building over the peaks to the west, fat and white and organized, cumulus stacking like plates. By evening they'd be a problem. By tomorrow morning, worse. But we had the afternoon, and the afternoon was ours.

"Two hours to the ridge," Sam shouted over the wind. "Maybe less if we move."

Hale bent double, hands on knees. "How," he wheezed, "does anyone live out here."

"Badly," I called back, "but on purpose," and even through the wind I heard Teodora laugh, high and bright, and something in my chest unclenched a quarter turn. That girl laughed at my worst material. She was going to be either a very good pilot or a very bad influence, and knowing the interior, both.

We climbed.

* * *

Somewhere above the headwall the terrain stopped being unfamiliar, and that was the terrible part. Not the steepness, not the altitude, not the burning in my legs — those were just the mountain doing mountain things. What was terrible was the recognition. A bend in the ridge I knew. A boulder split by frost like a broken loaf. A shallow basin where the snow lingered late into summer, exactly where my memory said it would be, and standing in it I was suddenly thirty-one years old and also nineteen, and also nine, pressing my palm to warm granite, and all of those Ulyanas were climbing this same slope at once, single file, like ghosts roped together.

Because I had been here before. Not to the wreck — I'd never gone to the wreck, I'd refused the search grid, refused the coordinates, refused everything — but my father had flown this valley a hundred times before the last time, and he'd brought me along on a dozen of those flights, and a pilot learns country through glass the way a child learns a face. I knew the shape of this ridge from the air, from the left seat, from beside him. I had traced it with my eyes while he talked about fuel burns and claim stakes and the proper way to make bannock, and I had not known, could not have known, that I was memorizing the geography of the worst day of my life.

*Look at the whole animal,* he'd say, banking slightly so I could see the valley entire. *Never trust a piece of it.*

I trusted none of it now. I trusted every inch of it. Both things at once, which is, I have since decided, simply what it is to love a place that has hurt you.

Near the top the wind dropped away all at once, the way it does when you crest into the lee, and the silence rang. Below us the lake lay flat and pewter-colored, so still it held the mountains upside down, and our camp on its shore was a scatter of orange dots, and the Beaver a pale sliver at the gravel bar. From up here it all looked small and orderly and survivable. From up here you could almost believe the world was navigable.

"There." Sam's voice, quiet, pointing north along the ridgeline.

I followed his arm.

The fuselage sat in a saddle between two rises, a quarter mile off, silver skin gone dull and chalky with ten years of weather, one wing folded back along the rocks like a bird with a broken pinion. Scattered debris caught the afternoon sun — a glint here, a glint there, the wreckage field strung down the slope like beads off a snapped necklace. Even at this distance, even expecting it, even having flown away from this mountain for a decade rather than toward it, my body reacted before my mind did. My knees went soft. My breath went thin. Ten years of careful banking east, all those added fuel miles, all those polite refusals — and here it was, the whole animal, and I was looking straight at it.

Nobody spoke. Teodora had gone pale and very still, watching me, I realized, not the wreck. Hale had taken his hat off, which surprised me, and stood holding it against his chest like a man at a graveside, which was exactly what he was.

"We can camp here and go in at first light," Sam offered gently. "No sense arriving tired."

"No," I heard myself say. My voice sounded like someone else's, level and far away. "We go now."

"Ulyana—"

"She's waited ten years," I said. "I won't ask her to wait through a night too."

*I* had waited ten years. But it came out the other way, and nobody corrected me, because everyone on that ridge understood that sometimes you have to talk about yourself in the third person just to get your boots moving.

* * *

The last quarter mile took an hour. Not because of the terrain — the going was easy, gentle ground, the wreckage field spread wide enough that there was a path through it — but because of what the terrain was made of. Every step landed on something. A strut. A scrap of upholstery gone grey. A section of cowling with a paint stripe I recognized, blue and gold, the colors of Iversen Air Service, population two. Teodora drifted close to me without being asked, close enough that our shoulders bumped now and then, and I let her, and I think it helped her more than me, which was fine. That's what mentors are for, on the days when the student has to hold the teacher up.

Halfway there, I stopped at a piece of fuselage skin no bigger than a serving tray, lying flat in the scree, and knelt beside it.

There was a name painted on it, half flaked away. **...LYANA**.

He'd stenciled it himself, the year I got my license. Joked that the plane and I shared a name now so it better behave itself. I'd rolled my eyes. I'd been twenty-two and immortal and embarrassed by my father's sentimentality, God help me.

I put my hand flat on the metal, the way he'd taught me to put it on warm rock.

Cold. Of course it was cold. Cold as the lake, cold as the grave, cold as everything up here that the sun couldn't reach into. No story in it. No warmth rising. Just metal and weather and time.

But beneath my palm, faint under the frost-flaked paint, I could feel something else — a ridge of stamped lettering, the aircraft's registration, pressed into the aluminum from the factory floor decades before any of us were born. Information. The kind that keeps you alive, or the kind that doesn't. He'd have made a joke of that distinction. He'd have made a joke of nearly all of this, if he'd lived to see it, and then he'd have gone quiet, because he knew better than anyone that the country talks, and some days what it says is not for repeating.

I stood up.

"Come on," I said, and my voice cracked down the middle of the word, and I didn't fix it. "Let's go meet her."

We came over the last rise together, the four of us, and the saddle opened up, and the wreck lay ahead of us whole in its brokenness — tail intact, cockpit crushed, the mountain having caught the plane the way a hand catches a moth, gently and finally and without malice.

And that was when Sam, walking point, stopped so abruptly that Teodora walked into his pack.

"What," Hale said.

Sam didn't answer. He was staring at the ground near the fuselage's nose, at the pale gravel there, and I followed his eyes down, and the wind went out of me all over again.

There were boot prints in the scree. Dozens of them. Fresh enough that the edges hadn't crumbled.

And they were not ours.
