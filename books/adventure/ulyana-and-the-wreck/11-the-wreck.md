## 11. The Wreck

Sam Two-Rivers had been dreading this climb for ten years, and the mountain had been kind enough to let him put it off until now.

He told himself that on the switchbacks, when his lungs burned and the scree shifted under his boots like the ground itself was second-guessing him. He'd climbed worse. He'd climbed this very ridge worse, younger, carrying a pack full of survey stakes and a heart full of nothing in particular. Ten years was a long time to let a mountain grow teeth.

"You're slowing down, old man," Teodora called from above, perched on a boulder like she'd been poured there.

"I'm pacing myself," Sam said. "There's a difference. You'll learn it around forty."

Dez huffed behind him, hauling the tool roll he'd insisted on bringing because — in his words — "if we find a plane, there's going to be something on it worth fixing or worth proving broken, and either way I want my wrenches." Dez carried grief the same way he carried tools: close to the body, distributed evenly, never discussed.

Ulyana was ahead of all of them and had been since dawn. She didn't talk on the climb. That wasn't unusual — Ulyana could go a full day saying nothing but *watch your footing* and *eat something* — but today her silence had a different texture. It was the silence of a woman walking toward a door she'd nailed shut a decade ago and now had to un nail with her bare hands.

Sam knew what was at the top. He'd known for years. And every step brought him closer to having to decide, again, whether to say so.

* * *

They'd left basecamp at first light, below the lake that kept giving them nothing. Five days of sonar sweeps and Hale pacing the shoreline like a man waiting for a doctor's verdict, and the water had offered up only rocks, old logs, and one very indignant lake trout that Dez swore was big enough to have its own tail number. The plane they'd been hired to find — the missing survey aircraft, the official story — wasn't in the lake. Which meant it had never been in the lake. Which meant, though nobody had said it out loud yet, that they were looking in the wrong place entirely, or looking for the wrong thing entirely.

Sam had watched Ulyana read the sonar printouts by lamplight, her finger tracing the depth contours, and he'd seen the moment she stopped pretending. Something in her face went quiet and old, like a cabin with the lights off.

"This isn't a search," she'd said, not to anyone in particular. "This is a pilgrimage."

Hale had been standing close enough to hear it. He'd said nothing. That, more than anything, had made Sam nervous.

* * *

The ridge came up to meet them in the early afternoon, the way ridges do — gradually, then all at once. One moment they were climbing through stunted spruce with the wind talking overhead; the next the trees gave out and there was nothing between them and the sky but rock, lichen, and a wind that had opinions.

And there, fifty meters along the spine of the ridge, half-swallowed by a decade of weather: the tail.

Sam saw it before anyone spoke. The aluminum, bleached gray-white, torn open along the fuselage like a tin can opened by something patient. One wing folded back against the rock as if the mountain had tucked it in. Snow-filled hollows around it wore the smooth blue of old ice. It didn't look like a wreck anymore. It looked like a grave marker that had learned to blend in.

Nobody said anything. There was nothing you could say that wouldn't be too small.

Ulyana walked toward it. Not fast, not slow. Just forward, the way water goes downhill — no decision in it at all.

Sam hung back with Dez and Teodora. This part wasn't theirs. He'd known that since the dock, maybe since before the dock, since the night ten years ago when he'd stood on this ridge with a flashlight and a waterproof ledger in his hands and made the worst good decision of his life.

* * *

She found him under the collapsed section of the cockpit, where the airframe had folded down over the space like a hand closed in protection — or in keeping, Sam had never decided which. Her father hadn't been thrown clear. The mountain had kept him, and the winters had done what mountains do, gently, thoroughly, without malice.

Ulyana knelt there for a long time.

The rest of them stood off among the rocks, and even the wind seemed to lower its voice. Teodora had gone pale and very still, her usual chatter banked down to embers. Dez took off his hat, which for Dez was roughly equivalent to weeping. Sam watched Ulyana's shoulders and thought about all the things a body says when it thinks no one is reading it.

She stayed kneeling for twenty minutes. Maybe thirty. Then she did something none of them expected: she began, carefully, methodically, to build.

There were flat stones scattered along the ridge, frost-split and handy, and she gathered them one at a time and laid them over the place where her father lay, fitting each one like she was setting a table. Nobody helped. Nobody dared. When she ran out of stones within reach she went farther for them, and farther, until there was a cairn waist-high and honest, the kind of thing that would still be standing when everyone who remembered why was gone.

When she finally stood and turned around, her eyes were red but level, and she said, in a voice scraped clean:

"He flew it to the last. Look at the controls." She nodded at the wreckage. "He didn't bail. He rode it in trying to flatten the glide."

"Of course he did," Dez said quietly. "Iversens don't bail."

That got the ghost of a smile out of her, which Sam suspected was the entire purpose of saying it.

* * *

It was Teodora, circling the wreck with the restless curiosity of the young, who found the wrongness first.

"Um. Ulyana?" She was crouched by the aft cargo door, which hung open on a hinge rusted to lace. "These panels. Somebody's had these off."

Ulyana crossed to her. Sam followed, and his stomach had already begun its slow descent ahead of him, because he knew — he had known since the tail came into view — that this conversation was coming for him eventually, and here it came early.

"Look." Teodora pointed. "Screw heads are bright. Everything else on this airframe is gray, but these are shiny. Someone's been driving screws recently. And this—" she touched a seam along the baggage compartment, "—this gasket is newer than the airplane. Like, decades newer."

Ulyana crouched. Ran her thumb along the bright screw heads. Sam watched her do the arithmetic — aluminum doesn't shine after ten years of this altitude, this UV, this freeze-thaw — and watched the answer arrive in her face.

"Someone's been here," she said. "Recently."

"Define recently," Dez said, already pulling a panel with his fingers, gentle as a surgeon.

"Years. Not decades." Ulyana stood and turned a slow circle, scanning the ridge, the talus slopes, the tree line below. Sam tracked her gaze and felt the hair rise on his neck, because he was doing the same math from the other direction. If someone had been here within the last few years — if someone had opened the strongbox compartment, taken or checked whatever it held — then the ledger Sam had carried in his own gear for ten years might be the only copy left in the world. Or it might be worthless. Or it might be exactly what somebody wanted badly enough to come back for.

Then Ulyana stopped turning.

She was looking down at the trampled ground near the cairn she'd just built — at the patch of gravel and frost-killed moss where they'd all been walking. And Sam saw it a half-second after she did: boot prints. Not theirs. Theirs were everywhere, obviously, a whole expedition's worth of churn. But underneath, pressed into the old mud beneath the frost-heaved surface, half-filled with grit and lichen fragments that had been growing into them —

Old prints. Years old. And crossing them, sharper-edged, shallower, newer ones.

"Those are mine," Ulyana said. Her voice had gone very quiet, the way it did before weather. "From the search. Ten years ago. I came up here on foot while the others swept the lake." She pointed at the older impressions. "Nobody ever followed me up. I checked. I remember checking."

"And those?" Dez nodded at the fresher prints overlapping hers.

"Lug sole. Deep heel. Recent enough that the moss hasn't reclaimed them." She looked up, and her eyes went around the circle — Teodora, Dez, and last, longest, on Sam. "Somebody has been standing right here. Where I stood. Looking at what I looked at. More than once, by the wear pattern."

Teodora hugged her arms. "Watching us? Or watching the wreck?"

"Same question," Ulyana said.

* * *

They spread out and searched properly after that, the mood gone from mourning to hunting without anyone announcing the change. Dez worked the airframe with his wrenches and his muttering, documenting every bright screw and fresh gasket. Teodora ranged the perimeter, reading the ground the way Ulyana had taught her to read weather — patiently, suspiciously, assuming it was lying. Hale, who had hung back through all of it with an expression Sam couldn't parse and didn't trust, picked his way along the ridge crest studying the approach routes.

Sam searched the way guilty men search: thoroughly, and hoping to find nothing.

He found the camp instead. Fifty meters east, tucked behind a granite fin where it would be invisible from the lake and nearly invisible from the air — a fire ring of stacked stones, blackened inside. A flattened bedroll space, pine boughs long since rotted to brown lace. A tin cup, rusted through at the bottom, set neatly upside down on a flat stone like someone had washed it and meant to come back.

More than one visit. The fire ring had been rebuilt at least twice; you could see the older smoke-staining under the newer stones.

"Ulyana," he called, and hated how careful his voice came out.

She came. She looked at the fire ring, the cup, the rebuilt stones, and Sam watched her arrive at the same conclusion he'd been sitting on for ten minutes: whoever this was hadn't stumbled here. They'd *commuted*. They'd made themselves comfortable. They had sat on this ridge, more than once, drinking something hot, watching the lake below where a search party pretended to look for a plane that wasn't there.

"They've been watching the searches," Ulyana said. "Plural. As in, this has happened before."

"Or they're watching for the thing the searches keep almost finding," Sam said.

It was out of his mouth before he could weigh it, and Ulyana turned and looked at him — really looked, the pilot's look, the one that reads instruments — and for one long moment Sam thought she was going to ask him the question directly. *What do you know, Sam? What aren't you telling me?*

Instead, Hale's voice cut across the ridge from the crest, tight and strange.

"You should all see this."

* * *

He was standing at the high point of the ridge, where the rock dropped away north into a hanging valley, and he wasn't looking at the valley. He was looking down at a slab of granite at his feet.

Boot prints again — the lug-soled stranger's, pressed into a film of old snowmelt silt on the slab. But beside them, unmistakable, was a second set. Smaller heel. Different tread. A tread Sam recognized, because he'd spent five days at basecamp staring at the soles of the boots that made it, drying by the stove.

"Hale," Dez said slowly, coming up beside him, "those are yours."

"No," Hale said.

"They're your boots. I've patched the heels myself—"

"I know what my boots look like." Hale's voice had an edge on it now, and under the edge something that sounded almost like fear. "I have never climbed this ridge. Not once. Not ten years ago, not since. I'm telling you—" he stopped, jaw working, and started over, quieter. "I am telling you the truth. Those are not my footprints."

Sam crouched by the slab. The prints were crisp. Weeks old, maybe less. Someone had stood exactly here, in Hale's boots, at the exact spot with the best view of both the wreck and the lake.

And then, faint but distinct, drifting up from the tree line far below where the light was already failing — a sound none of them wanted to hear.

Metal. Once. Like a pot lid set down on a stove.

Everyone froze. Ulyana's head came up slowly, and Sam saw her do the range-finding without meaning to, the way pilots do, eyes narrowing on the green dark below.

"That," she said, "is somebody making dinner."

Nobody laughed. Down in the trees, comfortable and unhurried, someone had a fire going — and they'd known, Sam was suddenly certain, exactly when the four of them would top this ridge.
