## 12. Boot Prints

Dez Okafor had spent thirty years listening to machines tell him the truth. Engines didn't lie. A bearing going bad sang a little song before it seized. A fuel line wept before it failed. People, on the other hand — people would look you dead in the eye and swear a float was sound while it was taking on water like a colander.

So when Ulyana crouched at the treeline above the wreck site and said, quietly, "Dez. Come look at this," he came looking, because she used his name instead of swearing, and that was worse.

The prints were pressed into a skin of old snow in the lee of a granite slab, maybe forty meters below the fuselage. They were crisp. Not last year's crisp — *this week's* crisp, edges still sharp, no melt-freeze slumping, no squirrel tracks stitched across them.

"Somebody climbed up here," Ulyana said. "Recently."

"Could be a hiker," Teodora offered, from behind them, in the voice of someone who did not believe her own sentence.

"It's eleven miles from the lake," Sam said. "Over scree. In October." He didn't offer an alternative theory. Sam rarely did. He just stood there with his hands in his pockets looking like a man doing arithmetic he didn't enjoy.

Dez knelt and got his face close to the snow, because that was what you did — you looked at the thing itself before you looked at what it meant. The tread was distinctive. Deep lugs in a herringbone pattern, and at the heel, a worn crescent where the rubber had gone smooth. He'd seen that wear pattern before. Recently. Daily, in fact, for two weeks.

He didn't say anything. He stood up slowly, the way you stand up when your knees are telling you something and so is your gut, and he looked down the slope to where Marcus Hale was standing apart from the group, hat in hand, staring at the wreck of the plane that had killed Ulyana's father with an expression Dez couldn't file under any of the usual headings.

"Uly," Dez said, low. "Walk with me a second."

* * *

They walked far enough to be out of earshot, which on bare granite meant not very far, and Dez told her what he'd seen, because keeping it from her felt like hiding a cracked cylinder from a pilot about to fly over water.

"You're sure?" she said.

"I've resoled those boots twice. Hale asked me to glue a heel back on in camp last week. I know that tread like I know my own signature." He rubbed his jaw. "It's him or somebody wearing his boots."

"Or somebody who wants us to think it's him."

"Yeah," Dez admitted. "That too. I hate that I thought of that only after I opened my mouth."

Ulyana looked back toward the wreck. Her face did the thing it always did when grief and fury tried to occupy the same square footage — went very still, very flat, like a lake freezing from the edges in.

"Get everyone together," she said. "Not here. Down at the saddle. And Dez — don't say whose boots they are yet. Let them talk first."

"That's an interrogation technique."

"That's a *fairness* technique." She paused. "Also an interrogation technique."

* * *

They gathered at the saddle where the wind funneled through and made conversation feel urgent even when it wasn't. Priya Raman had her notebook out before anyone spoke, which Dez was learning was her version of a sidearm. Hale stood with his arms crossed against the cold. Voss — well. Voss wasn't there, because Voss hadn't come up the mountain with them, having declared himself "better used watching camp," which in hindsight Dez intended to think about very hard.

"We found boot prints below the wreck," Ulyana said. "Fresh. Someone's been up here within the last few days. Maybe hours."

Silence, except the wind doing its best wolf impression through the notch.

"Somebody want to explain that?" she said.

Teodora looked at Sam. Sam looked at the horizon. Priya wrote something down with unnecessary flourish. Hale uncrossed his arms.

"It wasn't me," Hale said.

"Nobody said it was you," Ulyana said, which was true, technically, in the way that a trap is technically a question.

"I'm saying it preemptively." Hale's voice had an edge on it now, honed rather than raised. "Because I can see where this is going, and I want it on the record: I have never been on this mountain before yesterday. I hired your crew because I couldn't find this place myself. That was the entire point."

"Then explain the prints," Dez said, and immediately wished he hadn't, because Ulyana shot him a look that said *so much for letting them talk first*, and Hale turned on him with the relief of a man handed exactly the fight he'd been bracing for.

"My boots?"

"Herringbone lug. Worn crescent at the left heel. Size twelve."

Hale looked down at his own feet as if checking they were still attached. Then he sat down on a rock, pulled off his right boot, and held it out sole-up like a man surrendering a weapon.

"Look at it."

Dez took it. He looked at it. Herringbone lug, yes — same brand, common enough in any outfitter between here and Whitehorse — but the heel was intact, freshly glued by Dez himself, and the wear pattern ran along the *outer* edge, not the inner. Ten years of walking will put a signature in a sole the way ten years of flying puts one in a logbook. This was not the foot that made those prints.

"These aren't them," Dez said.

"Thank you," Hale said, with the dignity of a man who had just removed his boot on a frozen mountainside to prove a point and intended to be insufferable about it forever.

"So somebody's wearing boots like yours," Teodora said slowly.

"Or bought boots like mine," Hale said, standing, "and walked up here ahead of us. Which raises the question of how they knew where we were going." He let that sit. His eyes went, briefly, to the saddle path leading back down toward camp. Toward Voss.

Dez watched the thought land on everyone at slightly different speeds, like flares dropping. Teodora got there first, because she was young and quick. Priya got there second, because she'd probably been there since the dock. Sam got there without moving his face at all, which was its own kind of alarming.

"The fuel drain," Dez said aloud. "The cut tie-down. The holed float. We all assumed it was one of us. Somebody inside." He shook his head. "What if it was never inside? What if somebody wanted us stranded up here — wanted us committed, deep, slow, easy to find?"

"And now we've found the wreck for them," Ulyana said quietly, "and led them straight to it."

The wind gusted through the saddle and nobody shivered, because everybody was already cold in a way that had nothing to do with weather.

* * *

They stood around the wreck after that, but differently. Before, it had been a grave and a mystery. Now it was also a crime scene, and everyone kept glancing at the treeline like the trees might be taking notes.

Hale stayed near the fuselage a long time. Dez drifted over, ostensibly to check the tie-down straps they'd rigged for the climb back, actually because he'd spent two weeks disliking Marcus Hale on principle and wanted to see if the principle still held at close range.

It was uncomfortable, discovering it might not.

"My father sent men into these ranges in '61," Hale said, without preamble, eyes on the torn aluminum. "Survey crews. One of them filed a strike report that never reached the government office. It reached my father's desk instead." He exhaled. "I grew up on money I now believe was stolen off this mountain. Off a claim that belonged to—" He stopped. Looked at the wreck. "You know whose."

"Your father's fixer is watching our camp," Dez said.

"Voss worked for my father for nine years. I haven't seen him since I was nineteen." Hale's laugh had no humor in it, just structure. "And the first time I hire a crew to find the truth, he appears at my elbow offering to 'help with logistics.' I thought it was coincidence. My family has never once been touched by coincidence."

"So why bring him?"

"Because turning him away would have told him everything. Because I thought if he was beside me, I could see what he did." Hale finally looked at Dez, and there was something raw under the polish. "Instead he's been three days ahead of me the entire time."

Dez considered this. Machines, at least, announced their failures. Men like Hale apparently failed silently for decades and then all at once.

"For what it's worth," Dez said, "I still don't trust you."

"Noted."

"But I trust you more than I did at breakfast, and breakfast was four hours ago, so the trend line's good."

That startled an actual laugh out of Hale — short, rusty, like an engine turning over after a long winter. Dez decided not to examine how pleased he was about that.

* * *

They made camp that night in the shelter of the wreck itself, which felt wrong to everyone and sensible to everyone simultaneously, which Dez supposed was what a moral dilemma felt like from the inside. The fuselage broke the wind. Her father's plane, still sheltering people. Dez found he had to go check the tie-downs twice before his hands stopped wanting something to do.

Ulyana sat apart, reading something by headlamp — papers from the strongbox, though she'd shown no one. Sam sat nearer the fire than usual, which for Sam meant within radiating distance rather than orbiting it. Priya was writing. Teodora was asleep sitting up, the way young pilots sleep, instantly and completely, like a switch thrown.

Dez settled next to Ulyana. She tilted the pages away from him, then, after a moment, tilted them back halfway. Progress, by her standards.

"Whoever made those prints," she said, "knew the route. Knew our schedule. Knew we'd find the wreck and do the searching for free."

"Voss."

"Voss, or whoever pays Voss. And whoever pays Voss has known about this site for a long time." She turned a page. Her hands were steady. Dez noticed they were steady the way you notice a pilot flying perfectly straight through turbulence — the effort invisible and enormous. "My father died carrying proof of a theft. Someone's been guarding that proof ever since. Guarding it against *us*."

"Then we're not the hunters anymore," Dez said.

"No." She closed the folder. "We're the loose ends."

Downslope, faint on the wind, came a sound Dez catalogued automatically, the way he catalogued every sound — a rock clinking against rock, somewhere below the saddle. Once. Then nothing.

Sam's head came up out of the dark like a bird dog's. He said nothing. He looked at Ulyana. Ulyana looked at Dez.

"Nobody sleeps deep tonight," she said.

Nobody argued. And far below them, patient as winter, whatever had made those prints began, very quietly, to climb.
