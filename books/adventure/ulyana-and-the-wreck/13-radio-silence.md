## 13. Radio Silence

Teodora found the satellite phone dead at six in the morning, which was the hour she had privately designated as *her* hour — the one where she got to hold the phone, check the weather feed, and pretend she was the expedition's communications officer instead of its youngest and most expendable member.

The phone sat in its padded case on the supply table in the cook tent, exactly where Dez kept it. Teodora pressed the power button. Nothing. She pressed it again, longer, the way you did when a device was sulking. Still nothing. She popped the battery out, blew on the contacts like her grandmother blowing on soup, reseated everything.

Dead.

She carried it outside into the gray half-light and held it up as if altitude might help. The sky over the ridgeline had gone the color of dishwater, and the peaks above camp had put on fresh white caps overnight — not real snow yet, just the mountain trying on winter to see how it looked.

"Dez," she called. "Dez, wake up. The phone's dead."

Dez emerged from his tent looking like a man who had been assembled hastily from spare parts. He took the phone, turned it over, and did something Teodora hadn't thought of: he looked at the antenna stub.

Then he went very still, in the way that made the hair on Teodora's arms stand up even before she understood why.

"Well," he said quietly. "That's not a malfunction."

The antenna had been cut. Not snapped, not torn off by a branch or a careless boot — cut, clean through the mounting base, with something sharp and deliberate. The severed piece wasn't even there. Whoever had done it had taken the evidence with them.

Dez crouched and examined the ground around the tent tie-out where the case usually lived. The frost was thin and old prints were mushy, but there was a scuff, a knee-print, the ghost of someone who had knelt here in the night while five people slept thirty feet away.

"He was *here*," Teodora said. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "Last night. Right here."

"Yeah." Dez stood up slowly, working his back like an old man, though he was maybe forty-five and would deny being old with violence. "Yeah, he was."

*He.* Nobody had said the name Voss aloud since the boot prints on the ridge, but everybody was saying it silently, all day long, in the pauses between sentences. Teodora could feel it sitting in camp like a fifth wheel on a four-wheeled cart.

By the time Ulyana came out of her tent, the whole crew had gathered around the supply table like mourners at a very small funeral. Ulyana took one look at the antenna stub and didn't waste breath on surprise. Teodora watched her face do something complicated — fear moving through it and then being shown the door.

"All right," Ulyana said. "So we're deaf and dumb. Options."

"The Beaver's HF set still works," Dez said. "Barely. It's a hundred-mile radio on a good day, and today is not shaping up to be a good day."

"Hale's got a personal locator beacon?"

"A PLB gets you *found*, not heard," Sam said. He'd come up so quietly that Teodora jumped; she was going to have to have a word with him about doing that. "One burst, one way, if we're dying. It's not a conversation."

"So we can shout once, at the end," Ulyana said. "Wonderful. Anything else?"

"I might have something," said Priya Raman.

Everyone turned. Priya was standing at the edge of the group holding a tin mug of coffee she'd made herself without asking, which Teodora had learned was characteristic. Since the stowaway revelation, Priya had occupied a strange social position in camp — tolerated, fed, and never quite included — but this morning she stepped right into the middle of it as if she'd been invited.

"Before anyone says anything," Priya continued, "yes, I know my presence aboard this expedition was, technically, a crime—"

"It was definitely a crime," Ulyana said.

"—but I spent three years covering resource extraction stories in places where companies make problems go away, and the first thing they always do is kill communications. So I travel with backups." She set down her mug, went to her duffel — the one she'd hidden among the cargo drums, the one that had started all of this — and began pulling things out. A multitool. A coil of coaxial cable. A small soldering iron and a brick-sized battery pack. And a length of stiff wire that she uncoiled with the flourish of a magician producing a dove.

"That's—" Dez leaned forward. "Is that a whip antenna?"

"Improvised. I built it for a story in the Tarim Basin when the local authorities confiscated our gear. It's ugly, but it talks." She glanced at Dez, and something passed between them — Teodora saw it clearly, the look of two people recognizing their own species. "Your phone takes an external antenna jack. I checked last week."

"You *checked*?" Teodora said.

Priya shrugged. "I'm a journalist. Checking is the entire job."

Ulyana pinched the bridge of her nose, but Teodora caught what happened underneath the gesture: the corner of her mouth twitching, fighting itself, losing. "Fine. Fine! Fix the phone. But Raman — if you file a single word from this mountain before we all agree on what the words are, I will personally feed you to Sam's fish."

"That's not much of a threat," Sam said. "My fish are excellent."

* * *

The repair became a production, and the production became, improbably, the best morning Teodora had had in weeks.

Dez and Priya commandeered the cook tent table, spread out the phone's guts, and fell into a shorthand that excluded everyone else within about ninety seconds. Teodora hovered, handing them tools before they asked, because hovering-and-handing was rapidly becoming her core competency on this expedition and she intended to excel at it.

"Solder that joint first," Dez said. "Ground's no good if it's cold."

"I know," Priya said. "I've done this in worse conditions than your cozy little tent."

"This is not cozy. This is luxury. You should see the tarps I've worked under."

"Oh, I'm sure the great Dez Okafor has suffered terribly in his heated hangar—"

"My hangar is *unheated*, thank you, and it smells like avgas and regret."

Teodora snorted into her sleeve. Across the tent, Sam was mending a net and pretending not to listen, but his shoulders were shaking.

The work went on through the morning while the weather closed in around them. By ten o'clock the first real squall came down off the ridge — not the mountain's final answer, just a probe, a white curtain that erased the treeline for twenty minutes and dusted the tents and then withdrew. The temperature dropped hard after it. Everyone started wearing everything they owned.

It was during the second squall, huddled in the tent listening to snow tick against the canvas like fingernails, that Hale spoke.

He'd been quiet all morning — quieter than usual, which for Hale was saying something, because his usual mode was filling silence the way other men filled ditches. Now he sat on a crate with his big hands wrapped around a cooling cup of coffee, staring at the antenna stub Dez had laid on the table like an exhibit.

"He knew where it was," Hale said. "Knew exactly. Knew which case, which tent, which side of the zipper." He looked up. "How does a man know that?"

Nobody answered, because nobody had a comfortable answer. Teodora thought about kneeling in the dark outside a stranger's tent, cutting an antenna with surgical care, and felt the squall suddenly seem very close.

"He's watching us," she said. It came out before she could stop it. "Right now. From somewhere. He's probably watched us all morning."

"Yes," Sam said simply, and went back to his net.

There was a silence with teeth in it. Then Priya, without looking up from her soldering, said: "Good. Let him watch. When this antenna works, the first thing I want is a photo of me giving the lens a wave."

"Priya," Ulyana said warningly.

"What? Fear is a resource. I'm conserving mine for emergencies."

But Teodora noticed that Priya's hands, steady enough on the iron, had gone pale at the knuckles. Everybody on this mountain was scared. Everybody was just carrying it differently — Hale in silence, Sam in stillness, Ulyana in command, Dez in jokes, Priya in bravado, and Teodora herself, apparently, in blurting.

* * *

The antenna worked at half past noon.

Teodora knew because the entire crew crowded into the cook tent to watch Priya connect the last lead, and when the phone's screen lit up — actually lit up, bars appearing like a miracle, like bread from stones — a cheer went up that Teodora suspected was audible clear to Yellowknife.

"Signal!" Dez pumped both fists. "We have signal! Eat your heart out, Voss!"

"Don't celebrate yet," Priya said, already scrolling through menus with fierce concentration. "Signal's only half the battle. We need a window to actually transmit, and—" She stopped.

Teodora saw her face change and felt the cheer die in her own chest.

"What," Ulyana said. "What is it."

Priya turned the screen around. The weather feed had updated for the first time in two days, and it was bad news delivered in cheerful meteorological formatting: a low-pressure system sliding down from the Beaufort, a storm line drawn across the map in red like a wound. Forty-eight hours, maybe less. Heavy snow. Grounding winds.

"After that," Priya said quietly, "this pass closes for the season. Anyone still on this side of it when it closes isn't flying out. They're walking out. In spring."

The tent was very quiet. Outside, the light had gone flat and sourceless, the kind of light that meant more snow deciding whether to commit.

"Then we get out before it closes," Ulyana said. "All of us. That's not a discussion point. Nobody gets left on this mountain — not for a ledger, not for ore samples, not for a story." Her eyes flicked to Priya on the last word, but there was no venom in it anymore. Something had shifted there over the past days, some grudging recalibration, and Teodora had watched it happen the way you watch ice go out on a lake — slowly, and then all at once.

"We still haven't finished at the wreck," Hale said. "The strongbox—"

"The strongbox can wait forty-eight hours or it can wait forever," Ulyana said. "People don't."

Sam set down his net. "She's right. And there's something else you should all hear." He looked around the tent, and Teodora realized with a small shock that Sam Two-Rivers, who communicated primarily in weather reports and one-word answers, was about to give a speech. "Ten years ago, seven of us went up that ridge. Five came down. What killed those two men wasn't the mountain. It was hurry. They hurried because they were scared of the weather, and the weather punished them for it." He picked the net back up. "So we don't hurry. We move smart, or we don't move."

Nobody argued with Sam. Nobody ever argued with Sam; it was like arguing with the tide.

They spent the afternoon preparing — packing loads, checking gear, Dez making one of his pilgrimages to the Beaver to mutter at the float patch. Teodora helped Priya break down the antenna rig and repack it, and found herself, without quite planning to, talking.

"Why'd you really come?" she asked. "Not the story. The *real* why. Nobody hides in a cargo drum for a story."

Priya's hands paused over the coil of wire. For a moment Teodora thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "Because three years ago I wrote a series about a mining company poisoning a river valley, and my editor killed it. Legal. Advertisers. Pick a reason." She wound the wire tighter than it needed to be. "And I let it die, because I was tired and the fight was expensive. I've regretted it every single day since. This one—" she nodded toward the ridge, toward the wreck none of them could see, "—this one doesn't get to die in a drawer. Whatever it costs."

Teodora thought about that all through supper, and afterward, standing outside the tent in the blue cold, watching the third squall of the day come down the valley like a slow white animal, she thought about it some more. Ten days ago she'd wanted nothing in the world except hours in the logbook and Ulyana Iversen's approval. Now she wanted the truth to survive the mountain, and she wanted all these strange, difficult people to survive it too, and she couldn't have told you which wanting was bigger.

Behind her, the tent flap rustled. Ulyana stepped out beside her, and for a while they just stood there together, breathing steam, watching the snow come.

"Your hands were shaking again today," Teodora said. She hadn't meant to say it. It just walked out. "On the yoke, near the pass. Last week. I saw."

Ulyana was quiet long enough that Teodora braced for the door to close. Instead: "I know you saw."

"Are you—"

"I'm fine, Teo." But Ulyana said it looking at the ridge, not at her, and the word *fine* sounded like a coat worn over something else. "Get some sleep. We climb at first light, ahead of the storm. All of us."

Teodora zipped into her bag and lay listening to the wind test the tent poles, and she was nearly asleep when she heard it — faint, from somewhere upslope, threading through the wind so briefly she almost doubted it.

A whistle. Three notes, rising. Deliberate. Human.

She sat up in the dark, heart hammering, and reached for her boots.
