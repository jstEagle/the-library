## 14. What Her Father Carried

The fuselage had been waiting for her for ten years, and it did not hurry now.

Ulyana knelt in the snow beside the wreck while the others picked their way down the talus behind her, their breath hanging in the still air like small ghosts that couldn't decide whether to follow. The old Norseman sat where it had always sat — half-buried, wings folded like a bird that had lain down to sleep and simply never gotten up. The paint was gone. The fabric on the tail had rotted to lace. But the registration number was still legible along the aft fuselage, and she read it the way you read a name carved into a headstone: slowly, twice, because once didn't seem respectful enough.

*CF-IVN.* Iversen. Her father had chosen the tail number himself, back when he'd been a man who chose things.

"Uly." Sam's voice came from somewhere above her, careful as a man walking on lake ice. "You want us to give you a minute?"

She almost said yes. She'd rehearsed saying yes for ten years, in floatplane cabins and hotel rooms and the long grey hours between fuel stops. *Give me a minute* — as if a minute were a container big enough to hold this.

"No," she said instead, and heard her own voice come out steadier than she felt. "Stay close. All of you."

Because there were boot prints over hers from years ago, and someone had been here recently enough to leave fresh ones, and whatever her father had carried out of the world, he hadn't carried it alone. She wasn't doing this alone either.

* * *

They worked the wreck the way Dez taught people to work an engine: gently, in order, narrating aloud so nobody's hands got ahead of their heads.

Teodora cleared snow from the cargo bay with a broom made from a spruce bough, chattering the whole time because Teodora chattered when she was nervous, which was all the time, which Ulyana found increasingly endearing rather than exhausting — a development she intended to examine later, when there was a later. Hale stood off at a distance that managed to be both respectful and useless, hands jammed in his pockets, watching the mountain the way a man watches a door he expects someone to come through.

It was Sam who found the strongbox.

He'd been working along the pilot's side, quiet as always, and suddenly he went quieter — which with Sam was like a radio going from low volume to dead silence. You noticed.

"Here," he said.

It was bolted under the floor panel behind the pilot's seat, a steel document case the size of a bread loaf, painted drab green, the kind survey companies used for maps and assay records. The bolts were corroded but intact. Someone had wanted it to survive the landing, which meant someone had expected the landing not to be survivable, and had planned anyway.

Ulyana's father had been many things — a poor businessman, a worse worrier, a man who named his daughter after a swan in a ballet he'd never seen — but he had never been a planner. Planning was what other men did while he flew. And yet here was a box, bolted down, positioned against the one seat in the aircraft most likely to stay in one piece.

"Dad," she murmured, "what did you do?"

Dez produced a wrench from a coat pocket full of tools the way other men produce rabbits from hats. Four bolts, forty seconds, and the box came free with a sound like a tooth leaving a jaw.

It was heavier than it looked.

They set it on a flat rock in the open, away from the wreck, and Ulyana put her hand on the latch and stopped.

"Everyone breathe," she said. "Whatever's in here has been in here ten years. It can wait another thirty seconds."

"Is that a pilot thing?" Teodora asked. "The breathing?"

"It's an Iversen thing," Sam said quietly. "Her father used to say it before every takeoff. *Everybody breathe.*"

Ulyana looked at him. He looked at the mountains. There was something in his face she'd never quite been able to read, and had always assumed was just Sam being Sam — the deep, weatherproof silence of a guide who'd seen too much country to waste words on it. Now, standing over her father's strongbox, that silence looked less like weather and more like weight.

But there was no time, and the latch was cold under her fingers, and she opened it.

* * *

The top layer was ore.

Core samples, drilled and sleeved in clear plastic, each tube labeled in her father's blocky handwriting — the handwriting she'd known from grocery lists and birthday cards and a hundred flight logs. The rock inside was unremarkable to look at: grey-green, shot through with threads of something brighter, like frost caught mid-melt.

Hale made a sound when he saw it. Not a word. A sound, low in his chest, like a man who'd been punched somewhere old.

"That's it," he said. "That's the strike. That's — my God, look at the grade."

"You can tell by looking?" Teodora asked.

"I grew up looking." His voice had gone strange. "My father had samples like these on his desk for years. He used to polish them at night. Like worry stones." He crouched, not touching, hovering. "If these are even half what they appear —"

"They're more than half," said a new voice, and everyone turned.

Priya Raman, journalist, stowaway, self-appointed expedition conscience, had been sitting on a rock twenty feet away pretending to write in a notebook. Now she stood and came closer, boots crunching, and pointed at the labels without touching them.

"I spent three weeks in the territorial archives before I came north," she said. "Assay records, company filings, the whole paper trail. Your father filed preliminary results through a broker in Whitehorse in the spring of that year. The numbers were extraordinary. Then the filings vanished, the claim was reassigned, and your father died before he could contest any of it." She looked up at Ulyana, and her eyes were kind in a way that made Ulyana trust her slightly more than she wanted to. "I'm sorry. I know how that sounds. I know what it implies."

"It implies somebody killed him for rocks," Dez said flatly.

"It implies somebody *wanted* him dead after he found them," Priya said. "Whether that's the same thing, we don't know yet."

Under the ore, wrapped in oilcloth, was the ledger.

Ulyana knew what it was before she unwrapped it — a waterproof survey ledger, the kind with the coated pages that shed rain and grease and, apparently, decades. She opened it with numb fingers and read columns of figures, dates, claim numbers, and then — on the third page — names. Her father's name. And beneath it, in the space where a transfer should have been recorded honestly, a signature that did not belong to anyone who had ever set foot on that ridge.

Aldous Hale.

Marcus made a noise like he'd been struck. When Ulyana glanced at him, he was white to the lips, staring at the page as if it were a photograph of a crime in progress.

"That's not his hand," Hale said hoarsely. "That's — it's close, but it's not. My father had a man who could do signatures. Voss. He used to say Voss could sign anything better than the person who owned it." He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "I told you I came here for proof. I didn't know it would be *this*. I thought — I hoped — maybe there was nothing. Maybe the story my mother told was just a story."

"And what did your mother say happened?" Ulyana asked. Her voice sounded far away to herself, calm, professional, a voice reading a checklist while the engine ran rough.

Hale lowered his hand. "That my father stole everything we ever had from a better man. And that the better man's name was Iversen."

The wind moved through the pass above them, carrying a fine dry sift of snow, and nobody spoke, and the mountain held its breath along with them.

* * *

It was Teodora who found the letter.

The girl had been repacking the strongbox — because of course she had; Teodora organized things when she was frightened, and everyone within a mile radius was frightened — and she went still over the bottom of the case.

"Ulyana." Her voice had changed entirely. All the chatter gone out of it. "There's — I think you should — it has your name on it."

An envelope. Heavy stock, gone soft and tea-colored at the edges, sealed with a strip of wax that had cracked but held. Across the front, in that same blocky hand:

*For Ulyana. If I don't come home.*

Not *if anything happens to me.* Not *in the event of my death*, the language of bankers and insurance men. *If I don't come home.* As if coming home were a thing he did every day, and might simply fail to do tomorrow, the way a man might fail to catch a bus.

"When was it written?" Dez asked softly.

Ulyana turned the envelope over. On the back, small, in the corner where you'd write a date:

*The night before the flight.*

Ten years ago. The last evening. Whatever her father had known about the danger he was flying into, he had known it with a full day to spare — and he had spent that evening writing to her instead of turning back.

"Take your time," Sam said. "We'll walk the perimeter."

"We'll what?" said Teodora.

"Walk around. Look at things. Be elsewhere." Sam was already moving, and one by one they followed — Dez with a last squeeze of Ulyana's shoulder, Teodora with a look of naked, unbearable sympathy, Hale with his face turned away like a man ashamed to be seen at a funeral. Even Priya went, though she hesitated, and Ulyana caught her eye and shook her head once: *not yet.*

Then she was alone with the wreck and the wind and the envelope, kneeling in the snow the way she'd knelt at a hundred gravesides she'd never been able to visit, and she broke the seal.

* * *

*My Ulyana,*

*I am bad at letters. You know this. Your mother used to say I wrote postcards like telegrams and telegrams like ransom notes. So forgive me if this comes out wrong. I have all night to get it right and I suspect I will use none of them well.*

*You are probably angry at me. Good. Anger is fuel and you will need fuel. By the time you read this you will have learned what I found on this mountain, or you will have learned nothing, and either way you will be angry, because that is what I have left you. A mess. I am sorry. I have been cleaning up messes my whole life and I have finally made one too large to clean alone.*

*Here is what matters, and I want you to read it twice:*

*The claim is real. The rock is real. There is more wealth under this ridge than either of us would know what to do with, and men have already begun to kill each other over the paperwork for it. I did not understand that when I took the contract. I understood it three days ago, when a man offered me money in Whitehorse to file the claim under another name, and when I refused, he smiled at me in a way I did not like. I have flown into weather worse than that smile. But I am fifty-four years old and I am tired, and I know how this ends for men like me — the ones who find things. We are worth more dead than silent.*

*So here is what I have done. Everything is in the box. Samples, records, copies. Enough that the truth cannot be buried unless the whole mountain is buried with it. I have told no one where it is except you, now, on paper, and I will burn this letter if I land safe and laugh at myself for an old fool.*

*And here is what I need you to promise, and I know I have no right to ask it:*

*Do not come looking for me.*

*If I go down, let the mountain keep me. Do not spend your life flying these ranges the way I flew them, hunting for wreckage. I have watched you since you were small, my girl. You love the sky the way I love it — not the machines, not the work, the sky itself, the being-in-it. Do not let them turn that into grief. Grief is a heavy load and it does not balance right and it will pull your tail down in a turn. Fly light. Fly away from here. Let the rich men eat each other over their rocks.*

*You were the best thing I ever did and I did almost nothing right, so you may imagine what that means.*

*Everybody breathe, my swan. Then fly.*

*Your father,*
*Erik*

*P.S. If Sam Two-Rivers is reading this over your shoulder, which he absolutely is, because that man has never once let you out of his sight without checking the horizon first — tell him the debt runs the other way. Tell him I know what he gave up to be on that expedition, and that I never said thank you properly, and that a man who won't say thank you out loud doesn't deserve to have his letter read aloud either. So don't read that part to him.*

Ulyana laughed.

It came out of her like something breaking loose — a short, cracked, astonished sound that startled a ptarmigan off the rocks fifty yards away — and then it turned into something else, and she knelt in the snow beside her father's grave-that-wasn't-a-grave and cried the way she hadn't cried at the funeral, or at the memorial, or in the ten years of medicine runs and mail drops and careful, deliberate routes that bent east only when the weather forced them.

She cried for the letter he'd written and burned and apparently *hadn't* burned. For the man who'd known exactly what was coming and climbed into the plane anyway, because turning back meant letting the claim be stolen without a fight, and Erik Iversen had never once in his life declined a fight he was going to lose. For the postscript, which was so perfectly, infuriatingly *him* — managing her grief, managing Sam's guilt, managing the whole world from beyond it, still giving orders, still flying the airplane from the front.

And underneath the crying, quiet as a second heartbeat, was the thing she'd spent a decade refusing to know:

He had told her not to come. And she had spent ten years not coming — and it had changed nothing, saved nothing, protected no one. The sky had taken her father and then quietly, patiently, over ten thousand hours, taken everything else too: her partnership, her savings, her willingness to look east. She had flown light, exactly as instructed, and the load had gotten heavier every year anyway.

Grief doesn't balance right, he'd written. No. It didn't. But neither did ignorance. She'd simply traded one bad loading for another and called it safety.

The wind shifted. Somewhere above the pass, the light went pewter-grey, the color the sky turned before it made up its mind about snow.

Ulyana wiped her face with the heel of her glove, folded the letter along its original creases, and put it inside her jacket, over her heart, where pilots keep the things they can't afford to lose and can't afford to feel.

Then she stood, and walked back toward the others, and found Sam standing apart from the group at the edge of the talus, facing the wreck, not looking at her — a man holding himself very carefully still, the way you hold still when you've been caught.

"Sam," she said. "Dad says the debt runs the other way."

He closed his eyes.

"And he says," she continued, stepping closer, her voice dropping to something between a question and a blade, "that you're reading over my shoulder. Which means he thought there was something you'd need to read." She stopped in front of him. Ten years of silence stood between them, and for the first time she saw it clearly — not Sam being Sam, not weather, but weight. "What are you carrying, Sam? What did you take off this mountain?"

Sam Two-Rivers opened his eyes, and looked at her, and said the four words she would remember for the rest of her life:

"I found it first."
