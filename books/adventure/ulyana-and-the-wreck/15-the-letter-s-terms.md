## 15. The Letter's Terms

Sam Two-Rivers had spent most of his life learning what not to say. His grandmother taught him first, when he was seven, that a man who talked all day heard nothing at night. The army taught him the rest. Ten years of carrying a secret the weight of a river stone had taught him more than both.

So when Ulyana Iversen came looking for him at dawn, walking along the shoreline with her boots crunching frost and a piece of paper folded into her jacket pocket like it might bite through the canvas, he knew — before she said a word — that the stone was about to change hands.

She found him at the fish racks they'd improvised below camp, checking the grayling Dez had caught two days back. Not that anyone was hungry enough to eat them fast; Priya kept picking at one and asking questions between bites, which was its own kind of appetite. But Sam liked the work of it. Scaling fish gave his hands something honest to do while his head did the dishonest arithmetic it had been doing for ten years.

"Morning," Ulyana said.

"Morning."

She didn't look at him right away. She looked east, up at the ridgeline where the wreck sat under its new dusting of snow, where yesterday they had buried what needed burying and carried down what needed carrying. Her face in the early light looked like weather coming in from a long way off.

"I need to show you something," she said. "And then I need you to tell me if I'm crazy."

"That's usually Dez's job."

"This is bigger than Dez."

* * *

They walked up the beach away from camp, past the cache, past the old moose skull somebody's grandfather had propped on a pole a lifetime ago, until the only sounds were the lake lapping at the ice shelf and a raven somewhere overhead making its opinions known. Ulyana stopped on a flat granite shelf worn smooth by ten thousand years of water and took out the paper.

"You read this," she said. "Then I want to know what we do."

It wasn't a question, exactly. It was an offering. He understood that much before he unfolded the letter and saw his father's handwriting — no. Her father's. Elias Iversen's handwriting, cramped and slanting, written with a pencil that had been sharpened with a knife because the man never carried anything as civilized as a sharpener.

*Ulyana —*

*If you're reading this, then either I'm late getting home or you've gone looking for me, and knowing you it's the second one, and I'm sorry for that, because I told you once the eastern ranges would keep. They don't keep. That's the whole trouble with them.*

*We found it. You know that by now or you'll know it soon. Copper and gold and something the assayer couldn't put a name to, enough of it to buy this whole country twice over. Hale's people have the claim papers and I have the samples and the ledger, and here is what I learned today that made me write this instead of sleeping: the claim isn't theirs. They jumped it off a Cree trapper named Baptiste Fournel, who froze to death two winters back believing his strike had been stolen fair and square. There are men in Yellowknife who will pay well for these papers and men who will pay better for them to disappear.*

*I've thought hard about which kind of man I want to be, and harder about what this mountain does to people who come up it greedy. I watched it happen to Hale's surveyor in three days flat. So here are my terms, and I want you to hold me to them:*

*One. Nobody develops this strike. Not us, not Hale, not the company, not the government. This country forgives nothing, Ulla. It doesn't forgive the mine and it won't forgive us for bringing one.*

*Two. If I don't come home, you burn the ledger and scatter the samples, and you let the mountain keep its secret. Don't trade it. Don't spend it. Don't let it make you into the thing that killed Baptiste Fournel.*

*Three. Come home anyway. Whatever happens up here, come home. Your mother used to say the sky keeps what it takes, but it gives back the flying. I believe her. I believe it even now.*

*All my love, always —*
*Dad*

Sam read it twice. The second time wasn't for the words. It was for the sound of them, the way you listen to a river twice to hear what's underneath.

"He knew," Ulyana said. Her voice was steady the way ice is steady. "The night before the flight, he knew something was wrong, and he flew anyway, and he wrote this so I'd — so someone would —"

"So someone would hold the terms," Sam said.

"Yes." She finally looked at him. "And I can't do it alone, Sam. Because there's a second copy of that ledger in the strongbox, and there's a man on this mountain who wants it, and there's a journalist at our fire who'd publish it tomorrow if we handed it to her, and every single one of those roads ends with bulldozers on this ridgeline inside five years. And I promised my father terms I've been thinking about breaking since I was nineteen years old, because breaking them would hurt Marcus Hale, and God knows I've wanted to hurt Marcus Hale for a decade."

The raven dropped out of the sky and landed on the moose skull's pole, folding itself up like a black umbrella. Sam looked at it. It looked at him. Somewhere in the back of his skull, his grandmother cleared her throat.

"There's something you should know," he said. "Something I should have told you on the dock the day you hired me. Something I've been chewing on since the ridge."

Ulyana went very still. She was good at that — stillness like a heron's, like she could stand in cold water all day waiting for the truth to swim past.

"Ten years ago," Sam said, "when they sent the recovery crew in, I was on it. You remember that."

"I remember you came back different."

"I came back because I found them. Before the official party did. I was scouting the shore approach and I cut up the gully and there it was, the tail section above the scree, and your father—" He stopped. Started again, slower. "Your father was at peace, Ulyana. I'll tell you that part straight, because you deserve it straight. He looked like a man who'd set his tools down neat at the end of the day. But the cabin of that plane was torn open, and scattered in the snow around it were pages. A ledger, waterproof stock, bound in oilcloth. Claim records. Survey notes. Correspondence between old man Hale and a land agent in Edmonton arranging the transfer of a claim that belonged to a dead Cree trapper. Signatures. Dates. Everything."

Ulyana's breath came out white and slow.

"And I took it," Sam said. "I stood there in the snow with your father ten feet away, and I picked it up, and I put it in my pack, and I walked down the mountain, and when the recovery crew asked if I'd found anything, I said no. I said no for ten years."

"Why?" It wasn't accusation. It was worse than accusation. It was genuine puzzlement, and it cut deeper.

"Because I didn't know whose side it was on," Sam said. "That's the truth, ugly as it is. If I gave it to the government, the government would open the ranges to staking, and I'd seen what staking did to the country south of here. If I gave it to the Hales, they'd burn it and keep everything. If I gave it to you, you'd have spent your twenties at war with a company that eats people like us for breakfast, and I'd watched them eat your father already. If I burned it myself—" He shook his head. "I couldn't. It was the last true thing on that mountain besides your dad. Burning it felt like burning him twice."

"So you kept it."

"I kept it. In an ammunition box under the floor of my cabin, wrapped in the same oilcloth, for ten years. I took it out some nights. I held it. I tried to figure out what a man does with a thing that's too heavy to carry and too sacred to drop." He laughed, short and without any humor in it. "You know what I figured out? Nothing. There's nothing a man can do alone. That's the answer. It takes two. Maybe it takes four. That's why I came on this expedition, Ulyana. Not for Hale's money. I came because I decided that whatever happened on this mountain, I wasn't walking away holding that box by myself ever again. Either I hand it to you, or we burn it together, or we bury it together. But not alone. Never alone again."

The raven croaked once and lifted off, and the silence it left behind was enormous.

"The letter says burn it," Ulyana said quietly. "Scatter the samples. Let the mountain keep its secret."

"I know what it says. I've had ten years to memorize those terms without ever having read them."

"Then why aren't we burning it?"

Sam turned and looked east, up at the ridgeline, where the cairn they'd built yesterday stood grey against the snowfield. Where Voss was somewhere, watching, patient as winter.

"Because of Voss," he said. "Because if we burn it and he knows we burned it, he reports back that there's nothing here worth guarding, and the company sends surveyors anyway, because they already suspect. Because a lie needs witnesses to stay dead, and we'd be leaving this mountain with none. And because—" He hesitated, then committed. "Because your father also said come home. And I think the terms and the truth have to travel together now. I think the ledger goes to the authorities, all of it, the theft and the fraud and Baptiste Fournel's name restored, and the strike gets closed by law instead of by secrecy. Secrecy is just a lock, Ulyana. Locks get picked. Law is a wall."

Ulyana was quiet for a long time. The lake breathed against the ice. Somewhere behind them, faint, came the sound of Dez swearing at the float patch, which meant the world was still turning.

"My father wanted it buried," she said at last. "He wrote it down. Terms."

"Your father also trusted you to be smarter at thirty than he was at forty-five. That's what terms are for — they're a starting place for the living, not a cage." Sam held her eyes. "But it's your call. It was always going to be your call. I just carried the box to the table."

"And if I say burn it?"

"Then tonight we burn it, both copies, and scatter the ash in the lake, and I'll sleep fine, because I'll finally not be carrying it alone."

"And if I say give it up? All of it? To the Mounties, to the press, to whoever asks?"

"Then we do that, and I'll sleep fine too. Because either way, the weight splits."

Ulyana looked down at the letter in her hands. Then she did something Sam hadn't seen her do in ten years of knowing her: she laughed. Just once, short and startled, like a bird flushing.

"You know what's funny? He wrote 'don't let it make you into the thing that killed Baptiste Fournel.' And I've spent ten years letting it make me into exactly that. Angry. Hoarding. Flying circles around the one place I couldn't look at." She folded the letter carefully along its old creases. "Okay. Here's my call, and you can argue."

"I might."

"We keep the ledger intact until we're off this mountain. We don't burn it, we don't hide it in the cairn forever, we take it out — to the RCMP, to Priya if she'll report it straight, to Baptiste Fournel's family if any are left. The strike gets killed in daylight. And the samples—" She paused, and something moved across her face like cloud-shadow. "The samples go in the lake. Tonight. Quietly. Let Voss watch us throw away the treasure. Let him report there's nothing here. The ore dies; the truth lives. My father's terms, amended by his daughter."

Sam nodded slowly. "Amended by his daughter," he agreed. "He'd have signed that. He always did say you argued better than him."

"He did not."

"He absolutely did. Told me so himself, the last time I saw him alive, at the fuel dock in Fort Providence. Said, 'That girl of mine will out-argue the whole Northwest Territories someday, God help the Territories.'" Sam smiled. "First time in ten years I've repeated something he said without it tasting like copper."

Ulyana pressed the letter flat against her sternum for a moment, the way you'd press a hand to a wound, then tucked it back inside her jacket, over her heart, where it had clearly been living since she found it.

"Tonight, then," she said. "After dark. You and me and the lake."

"Together," Sam said.

They started back toward camp, boots crunching, and the morning felt different on Sam's shoulders — lighter by one stone, heavier by one shared purpose, which was somehow the same thing. Halfway back, Ulyana stopped.

"Sam. The box under your cabin floor. When this is done — when it's all given up — what will you do with the empty box?"

He considered it honestly, the way she deserved.

"Fill it with fishing tackle," he said. "It's a good box. Deserves honest work."

That earned him a real laugh, brief and bright, and they walked down into camp together, where Dez was still fighting the float, Teodora was studying clouds like scripture, and Priya Raman was watching them come with the particular patience of a woman who already knew there was a story and was willing to wait for it.

Which was fine, Sam thought. By tonight, the lake would have the ore, and by week's end, the world would have the truth.

What nobody had counted on — what neither of them could have counted on, watching the treeline through the steam of their own breath — was that Voss had been counting on exactly this. On secrets moving at night. On two figures walking down to the water after dark.

He'd been waiting ten years for someone else to dig up the grave.

He could certainly wait one more night to see what they buried in it.
