## 16. The Stranger Descends

Marcus Hale had spent forty-seven years learning to read rooms — boardrooms, courtrooms, the cold front parlor of his father's house where men in good coats said terrible things in quiet voices. He liked to think he could smell trouble coming the way sailors smelled weather.

So it was humiliating, later, to admit that Voss walked into camp like a neighbor come to borrow a cup of sugar, and Marcus's first thought was not *danger* but *how does a man get a haircut that good at four thousand feet?*

The dog announced him first. Sam's dog — a shaggy, opinionated creature named Biscuit who had so far shown all the guard instincts of a throw pillow — lifted her head from beside the stove, pointed her nose at the tree line, and made a sound halfway between a growl and a question.

Then the trees parted and there he was.

He came down the slope toward the lake with the unhurried gait of a man who owned every step he took. Tall, silver at the temples, dressed in canvas and wool that probably cost more than Dez's truck back home. A rifle rode his shoulder the way other men carried umbrellas — casually, as an afterthought against weather. His hands were bare despite the cold, and they were the hands of a man who had never once worried about frostbite: soft-looking, well-kept, absolutely steady.

"Evening," the man called out, cheerful as a park ranger. "Hope I'm not interrupting supper. Smells like someone burned something."

"We're having chili," Teodora called back, because she was twenty-four and had not yet learned that you didn't answer strangers on mountainsides. "It's supposed to be like that."

"Is it?" The man smiled. "I'll take your word for it."

Marcus came out of his tent already knowing. That was the awful part — he knew before the man was close enough to see clearly, knew from the walk itself, that particular rolling stride his father's fixer had used crossing the office floor of Consolidated Minerals like he owned the deed to it. Twenty years since Marcus had seen him last, and the years had only improved him, the way they improve certain knives.

"Hello, Marcus," Voss said.

"Voss." His own voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "You've aged."

"You haven't. That's the tragedy of money, isn't it? It keeps the face young while everything behind it rots." Voss looked around camp with frank appraisal — the Beaver on her mooring lines, the patched float, the tents, the stove pipe smoking cheerfully against the dusk. His gaze snagged on Priya, sitting by the fire with her notebook open, and lingered there a half-second longer than politeness allowed. Then it moved on. "Cozy operation. I'd have expected more security, given what you're after."

"And what would you know about what we're after?"

"Everything," Voss said pleasantly. "That's my job."

* * *

They let him into camp because there was no sensible alternative. He was armed; they were five, and only Sam struck Marcus as genuinely comfortable with firearms, which was its own kind of comfort. And because — this was the thing Marcus kept circling back to as he watched the man settle onto a crate by the fire like an invited guest — because some old, poisoned part of him wanted to hear what Voss had to say. Wanted to hear the number. There is a particular shame in discovering that about yourself, and Marcus sat with it while Ulyana poured coffee for everyone including their guest, because hospitality was apparently hardwired into her at a level deeper than judgment.

"So," Ulyana said. She sat across the fire from Voss with her mug cradled in both hands, and Marcus watched her do the thing she did — go very still and very simple, strip herself down to essentials. "You walked two days through the bush to find us. That's expensive. Talk."

"I admire directness." Voss blew on his coffee. "Your father was direct too, Ms. Iversen. Did you know we met? Once. Fairbanks, 1979. He told me exactly what he thought of the company I worked for. Took me eleven minutes. I timed it, because I was waiting for a phone call." He smiled at some private memory. "He wasn't wrong about any of it. That's the hell of it."

"My father," Ulyana said, "is dead."

"Yes. I'm aware. I've been aware for ten years, which is rather longer than most people." Voss set down the mug. "Let me save everyone the mystery portion of the evening. Mr. Hale here hired your crew to find a missing survey plane. There is no survey plane. What's up on that ridge is the aircraft that went down in '79 carrying Elias Iversen, a set of core samples, and documentation relating to a mineral claim that has been, shall we say, contested ever since. You found the wreck yesterday. You opened the strongbox today. And now everyone in this camp knows things that would be inconvenient if they reached a courtroom or a newspaper." He nodded toward Priya without looking at her. "Or a notebook."

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Somewhere across the lake a loon made its idiot beautiful sound.

"So here's my offer," Voss continued, warm as a bank manager. "Each of you gets a number. A good number. Life-changing, for some of you — no offense intended to the mechanic, whose truck I understand is held together with prayer. In exchange: silence. Not forever. Just long enough. The claim area gets quietly acquired, the site gets professionally remediated, and in five years none of this will have happened. You'll each be richer, and the mountain will keep its secrets, which is what mountains are best at anyway."

"And if we say no?" Teodora asked.

"Then we have a much less pleasant conversation," Voss said, "which I'd genuinely rather avoid. I like camps. I like fires. This is nice." He spread his hands. "I'm offering you the boring ending. Take it. Boring endings are underrated. Ask anyone who's had an exciting one."

Marcus heard himself laugh — one short, ugly bark of a laugh that surprised even him. Everyone turned.

"Sorry," he said. "Sorry. It's just — you haven't changed at all. Twenty years, and it's still the same speech. The reasonable man in the wilderness. My father used to say you could talk a grizzly out of a salmon." He stood up. His legs felt distant, borrowed. "What's my number, Voss? Since you've clearly priced everyone else. What does the son cost?"

Voss looked at him for a long moment, and something in his face shifted — the salesman's warmth draining away like water through gravel, leaving the thing underneath, which was patient and dry and very old.

"Yours is different," Voss said. "Yours isn't money."

"No?"

"No. Yours is the ledger. Your father spent the last thirty years of his life dying of what's in that ledger, Marcus. Ruin, disgrace, the whole slow collapse — you watched it. I watched it. I drove him to the hospital the year his heart gave out, and do you know what he talked about in the ambulance? Not the family. Not you. The *claim*. So here's my offer to you specifically: the original documents come to me, and in exchange, the copies that exist — the ones in the company archive, the ones that name your father as the author of the fraud — those disappear too. All of it. Every page. Your father dies clean. Posthumously innocent. A restored name, a restored estate, and you get to visit his grave without flinching." Voss picked up his coffee again. "I can't make that offer twice."

And God help him, Marcus felt it land. Felt it slide under his ribs like a fishhook, sweet and barbed, because he had wanted exactly that for twenty years — had built his whole careful, hollow life around the hope that the record could be corrected, that the name Hale might one day mean what it had meant before. Voss hadn't offered him money. He'd offered him absolution, and he'd priced it perfectly.

"That's quite an offer," Marcus managed.

"It's a real one."

"He's lying," Ulyana said quietly.

Everyone looked at her. She hadn't moved. Steam rose off her coffee between them like a small ghost.

"The archive copies don't exist anymore," she said. "If they existed, you wouldn't need us silent. You need the originals destroyed because they're the *only* proof left. Which means the moment you walk out of this camp with them, every one of us is worth more to you dead than paid." She took a sip. "Also, you said 'remediated.' Nobody says remediated about a mountain unless they mean dynamite."

Voss regarded her with what looked, horribly, like genuine appreciation. "Mr. Hale," he said, "where did you find these people?"

"They found me," Marcus said. "Against my better judgment."

* * *

Later — after Voss had accepted a spot near the fire for the night ("Storm moving in; even I don't hike in that") and stationed himself with his rifle across his knees and his back against a boulder, radiating the relaxed menace of a cat at a mousehole — Marcus stood at the edge of the dark beyond the stove light and tried to breathe.

Ulyana found him there. Of course she did.

"You were going to say yes," she said. Not accusing. Worse — just accurate.

"For about nine seconds," Marcus said. "Maybe eleven. I time things too."

"What changed it?"

He thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty and because he was tired of being a man who curated his own answers. "My father talked about the claim in the ambulance," he said. "Not once in his life did he talk to me the way he talked about that mountain. And I realized Voss was offering to give me back a man who never existed. The innocent Elias Hale. There's no such person to restore. There's just... what he did, and what we do next." He laughed softly, without humor. "Ten years ago I sent you east because I couldn't stand what your father had and mine didn't. Now I'm standing in the dark with his fixer and I finally understand what my father actually had. Nothing. He had nothing, and he spent everything to keep it."

Ulyana was quiet a moment. "Eleven seconds," she said. "That's longer than I'd have given you credit for."

"Growth."

"Don't push it."

Across the fire, Voss had not moved. But Marcus caught it — the small turn of the man's head, tracking them through the dark, and the way his hand rested on the rifle stock not idly anymore but specifically. And past him, at the far tent, Sam Two-Rivers stood motionless in the shadows with Biscuit silent at his heel, watching Voss with an expression Marcus had last seen on men in courtrooms when the verdict was about to be read.

"Ulyana," Marcus said, low. "The strongbox contents. Where are they right now?"

"Why?"

"Because I've known that man my whole life, and I'm telling you — the offer phase is over. He asked about the box three times tonight and counted our rifles twice. Tomorrow morning, or maybe sooner, he stops buying and starts taking." He swallowed. "And I don't think he plans to leave witnesses. I think that was never on the menu. I think we were always the second item on the agenda."

Ulyana looked at the fire, at the easy circle of her people — Dez cleaning a wrench that didn't need it, Teodora talking too fast and too bright, Priya writing by headlamp with her shoulders hunched around the page like a mother over a child.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "we move first."

Behind them, soft as snowfall, Voss began to hum.
