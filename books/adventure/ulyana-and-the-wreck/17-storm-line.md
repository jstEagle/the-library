## 17. Storm Line

The storm arrived the way her father used to say the big ones did — not as weather but as a decision. One minute the ridge above them was a clean black line against a pewter sky; the next, the line was gone, and the world had been erased and redrawn in white with no horizon anywhere in it.

Ulyana stood at the mouth of the wreck-site camp — three tarps strung between wing spars and living rock, a stove ticking away on salvaged fuel — and watched the mountain disappear.

"Wind's out of the north-northwest," she said, mostly to herself.

"Thirty knots now," Dez said beside her, squinting into the white. "Forty by dark. Fifty by midnight if it's feeling ambitious."

"It's feeling ambitious."

Dez snorted. "You say that like you know the storm personally."

"I know all the storms personally. It's a long relationship." She turned back toward the camp. "Get everything tied down twice. Anything not tied down is going to be in Saskatchewan by Thursday."

The wreck site sat in a shallow bowl below the ridgeline where her father's Norseman had come to rest ten years ago, its fuselage half-swallowed by lichen and time. They had spent two days up here — cataloguing, photographing, arguing gently about what to do with what they'd found. The core samples sat wrapped in canvas inside the strongbox. The second copy of the ledger sat against Ulyana's ribs, inside her jacket, where it had lived since she'd read her father's letter alone behind the fuselage and come out a different person.

And Voss sat by the stove, drinking their tea, wearing the patient smile of a man who had all the time in the world and knew something they didn't.

* * *

Voss had walked into camp that morning out of the snow like he'd been invited. Armed, polite, unhurried. He'd introduced himself as a "land consultant," which was a phrase that meant nothing and threatened everything, and Hale had gone gray around the mouth and said, quietly, "I know you," in a voice Ulyana had never heard him use.

Now Voss held his tin cup in both hands like a churchgoer and watched the storm erase the world with an expression of mild satisfaction, as though the weather were working for him. Maybe it was. Men like Voss always seemed to have arrangements.

"Bad luck, this storm," he said conversationally, when Ulyana passed near the stove. "Traps you all up here. No radio. No plane worth flying." He sipped. "Almost as if somebody planned it."

"Storms don't plan," Ulyana said.

"No," Voss agreed pleasantly. "But people do. That's rather my point."

She looked at him. He was perhaps sixty, lean as a fence rail, with a face like a folded map and hands that had done things hands shouldn't do. His rifle leaned against a spar within reach of his chair, and everyone in camp had noticed, and nobody had said anything, because saying it would make it real.

"You should be careful with threats this far from help," Ulyana said.

"Oh, I'm not threatening anyone." He smiled. "I'm negotiating. There's a difference. Threats are for people who've run out of patience." He tipped his cup toward the strongbox. "I have enormous patience. But I would like what's in the box before this storm finishes, and I'd like everyone to go home rich and quiet. That's the whole offer. Rich and quiet. It's a good offer. Most people never get one."

"And if we're not interested?"

"Then we'll keep talking until you are." He settled back. "I'm very good at talking."

Ulyana walked away before he could see what her face did. Behind her she heard Priya, who had been pretending to inventory the medical kit, mutter under her breath: *"Rich and quiet. God, he even threatens like a press release."*

* * *

By full dark the storm had stopped being weather and become a fact of geology. The wind came off the ridge in long flat shoves that made the tarps crack like whips and drove snow sideways through every seam. The temperature fell through zero and kept falling. They huddled in the lee of the old fuselage, six people and one enemy around a stove that ate fuel like a stoker, and waited for the night to end.

It didn't end. Nights like this didn't. They just went on being night until you forgot there had ever been another kind of hour.

Teodora sat pressed against Ulyana's side, too proud to admit she was cold, her jaw set in a way Ulyana recognized from mirrors. Sam sat across the fire, methodically mending a tear in his mitt with sinew thread, his big hands steady, his eyes doing slow circuits of the camp. Dez had fallen asleep sitting up, which Dez could do anywhere, anytime, a talent Ulyana had envied for fifteen years. Hale stood at the edge of the light, looking at the storm like it owed him money.

And Voss watched the strongbox.

He thought he was subtle about it. He wasn't. Every twenty minutes, maybe less, his eyes went to the canvas-wrapped box wedged under the fuselage frame, weighed it, measured the distance to it, measured the distance to his rifle, and came back to the fire. Ulyana counted. She was good at counting. Ten years of flying alone teaches you to notice the thing that moves.

At some past midnight — or some early morning, the distinction having dissolved — Sam caught her eye across the flames. He gave the smallest shake of his head. *Not yet.* And then, a moment later, the smallest tilt of it toward the dark beyond the tarps. *Soon.*

Ulyana understood. Whatever Sam had been carrying for ten years — guilt, the ledger's twin, the whole buried weight of the expedition — tonight he intended to do something about it, and he wanted her ready.

She put another stick in the stove and waited.

* * *

The chance came at the worst possible hour, which was the only kind of chance the interior ever offered.

The wind gusted hard enough to lift the corner tarp free of its lashings, and everyone moved at once — Dez swearing awake, Teodora scrambling, Hale lunging for the flapping canvas — and in the two seconds of chaos, Voss moved too.

He didn't go for the strongbox first. He went for his rifle, and then he stood in the middle of the camp with the barrel leveled at all of them at once, snow streaming past him, and said, in the same pleasant voice:

"Now. The box. Nobody has to have a bad night."

"You're pointing a gun at four people in a whiteout," Ulyana said, keeping her voice level, keeping her body between him and Teodora without appearing to. "That's already a bad night."

"It's a bad *week* if someone makes me use it." He gestured with the muzzle. "Open it."

"The samples are ore rock," Ulyana said. "You can't spend ore rock."

"I'm not buying the rock, Ms. Iversen. I'm buying the absence of it. There's a difference, and the difference is worth more than you make in a decade." He took a step toward the fuselage. "Open it, or I start with the journalist, because journalists are replaceable and I suspect you all know it."

"Don't," Priya said, and her voice cracked only slightly, which Ulyana thought was heroic.

And then Sam moved.

Later, Ulyana would try to reconstruct exactly how it happened, and fail. Sam Two-Rivers was fifty-eight years old and built like a dock piling, and he crossed the camp faster than a man that size had any right to, and he hit Voss's gun arm with both hands and his whole weight behind them. The rifle went off — the shot swallowed instantly by the wind, a small rude cough in all that roar — and then the two men went down together into the snow, and the night became fists and breath and snow and the terrible grunting economy of two strong men trying to hurt each other.

"Sam!" Teodora screamed.

Ulyana moved. Everyone moved. Dez got there first and grabbed Voss's collar; Hale kicked the rifle away into the dark, where it vanished as completely as if the mountain had accepted it as tribute. It took all three of them to pull the men apart, and when they did, Sam stayed down on one knee, breathing wet and ragged, blood sheeting bright from his brow and mouth, one hand cradled wrong against his chest.

Voss knelt in the snow too, lip split, eyes suddenly, genuinely alive with something that wasn't patience anymore.

"That," Voss said, spitting red, "was a mistake."

"So was coming up my mountain," Sam wheezed.

They got Sam against the fuselage and Priya worked on him with the med kit while Ulyana held a compress to his brow. Two fingers broken, probably three. Ribs, likely. A face that would swell shut by dawn. Sam bore it the way he did everything, silently, watching Ulyana over Priya's shoulder with an expression that said, clearly as words: *the ledger. Now. While he's angry instead of thinking.*

Voss retrieved his rifle from wherever the snow had taken it — it took him ten minutes of blind groping, and no one helped him — and then he planted himself between the group and the strongbox, shaking snow off his coat, and said nothing at all. Which was worse than anything he'd said all day.

* * *

Ulyana couldn't hide the ledger in her jacket anymore. That was the arithmetic of it, plain and cold. Voss had seen her wear the same coat for three days. Sooner or later — probably sooner, probably tonight, probably while they slept — he would decide that patience had been tried and found insufficient, and he would search them, all of them, one at a time, at the muzzle of that rifle. And he would find it. A man like Voss always found what he was looking for, because he had never once looked for something that mattered to him.

So it couldn't be on her. It couldn't be in the strongbox. It couldn't be in the snow, because the snow would give it up in spring like a dog returning a slipper. It couldn't be burned — she'd thought about it, God knew, standing in the wind with her father's letter still fresh behind her eyes — but burning it felt like letting the theft win twice, once with the claim and once with the truth.

Which left one place.

She waited until the camp settled into its exhausted pretense of sleep. Voss sat watch by the stove with the rifle across his knees, and she let him see her get up, let him see her walk to the strongbox, kneel, and sit there a long time with her back to him, doing — as far as he could tell — nothing. Let him think she was moving the ledger, or checking it, or crying over it. Let him build whatever story suited him.

What she actually did was slide the ledger out of her jacket and into the loose fold of canvas at the bottom of the strongbox, beneath the wrapped core samples, where a searching hand would find canvas and rock and stop. And then, an hour later, when Voss's head finally dipped — even patient men slept, eventually, in storms, in warmth, after fights — she lifted the ledger out again, and she walked into the white.

The wind took her immediately. Three steps from the fire and the world was gone, just her boots and the slope and the sound like the sky tearing. She climbed by feel and memory, thirty meters up the bowl to the little shelf of rock where the cairn stood — the cairn they'd built ten years ago, or rebuilt, stone by stone, over the place where they'd laid what they could carry of her father and left what they couldn't.

She knelt in the drift beside it. Her hands, bare for only the seconds she could stand it, found the stones by shape. The flat capstone. The gap beneath it where the mountain itself had shifted and left a hollow, dry, sheltered, invisible unless you knew — and nobody knew. Nobody had ever known but her. She'd found the hollow at fourteen, hiding from her father during a game, and he'd found her there and laughed and said, *Well, now it's our spot,* and she had never told a soul, because some things you keep the way you keep a grave.

She wrapped the ledger in the spare strip of canvas from her pocket, pressed it into the hollow beneath the capstone, and stacked the stones back over it with fingers that had stopped being hands and become claws of pain.

Then she stayed kneeling a moment longer than she needed to, forehead nearly touching the frozen rock, and said — not a prayer, because she'd never learned how, but something adjacent to one:

"Hold onto this for me, Papa. You were always better at keeping secrets than I am."

The wind answered with the only answer the interior ever gave, which was more wind.

When she got back to the fire, Voss was awake again, watching her cross the snow, and his eyes went straight to her empty jacket, and something in his face loosened with satisfaction. He thought he understood. He thought she'd hidden it somewhere nearby, or given it to someone, or buried it in the snow — and tomorrow, or the next day, he would begin taking this camp apart stone by stone to find it.

Let him look, Ulyana thought, warming her ruined hands at the stove. Let him look everywhere in the world except the one place he'd never think to search, because to a man like Voss a grave was just dirt, and dirt was just ground, and ground was just something you bought.

Sam caught her eye across the dying fire. His face was a swollen ruin and he was smiling anyway, faintly, painfully, the way a man smiles when a ten-year weight finally comes off his shoulders and lands somewhere safer.

She nodded once. *Done.*

And then, over the roar of the storm, Dez — who had been quietly listening to the wind the way Dez listened to engines, head cocked, frowning — raised his voice and said:

"Wind's backing around. Northwest to west. That break in the front I was hoping for? It's coming. Twelve hours, maybe less."

Twelve hours. One crippled plane that could lift four. Seven people on this mountain, and one of them armed, and one of them lying under stones with her father.

Ulyana looked at the white wall where the sky used to be, and began, right there, in the flicker of the stove light, to count.
