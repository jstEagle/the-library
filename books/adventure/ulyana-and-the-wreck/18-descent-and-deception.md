## 18. Descent and Deception

Dez Okafor had rebuilt engines in worse places than a frozen lakeshore at four thousand feet, but he couldn't remember when. There'd been the time outside Fort Vermilion when he'd done a cylinder swap in a snowdrift using a hair dryer and prayer, and the time in Yellowknife when a bear had taken an interest in his toolbox mid-overhaul. But this — patching a holed float with epoxy, aluminum sheet, and the last of his good rivets while a storm stacked itself up against the western ridgeline like a debt collector — this was going to be the story he told at every bush pilot's bar for the rest of his life, assuming he lived to have one.

"Hand me the mallet," he said.

Teodora handed him the mallet. She'd been handing him things for six hours without complaint, which told Dez two things: she was scared, and she was too proud to say so. Both were fine qualities in a pilot, as long as they didn't arrive at the same time.

"You know," Teodora said, holding the float steady with her shoulder, "when I signed on for hours, this isn't exactly what the brochure promised."

"The brochure was Ulyana's face and a handshake."

"I'm just saying. 'Adventure in the great northern interior.' Nobody mentions the part where a man named Voss watches us from a ridge with a rifle."

Dez set the last rivet and gave it three taps, then a fourth for luck. He glanced up, casual as checking the weather, toward the notch in the ridge where Voss had appeared that morning like a bad omen with legs. The man stood there the way a crow stands on a fence post — patient, entitled, waiting for something to die.

"He's not watching us," Dez said. "He's watching the strongbox. Which means we're going to give him a very good show."

* * *

The plan had come together the night before around the stove, in the low voices people use when they're deciding something they can't take back. Ulyana had laid it out the way she laid out everything: flat, precise, no decoration. The Beaver's float was patched enough to fly — barely, once, in calm air, which meant now or never before the storm closed the lake for good. The plane could lift four people plus fuel and nothing else. Five bodies, one airplane. Somebody stayed.

And the strongbox — the core samples, the second ledger, the whole glittering reason anyone was freezing on this mountain — could never leave in Voss's hands. So it wouldn't leave at all. It would die here, loudly, convincingly, where a man with a rifle and binoculars could watch it happen.

"We sink the ore," Sam had said. It wasn't a question. Sam Two-Rivers had spent ten years carrying this secret and had apparently decided that if he was going to put it down, he was going to drop it in the deepest water available.

"The lake keeps what it's given," Ulyana said, and nobody asked her what she meant by that, because everybody already knew.

Now, in the gray morning light, Dez crouched at the edge of the ice shelf near the outflow and looked at the contraption he'd built, because of course Ulyana had turned to him and said *you're good with weights and balances, Dez*, which was mechanic-speak for *I need you to help me commit fraud against a criminal.*

It was beautiful work, honestly. A wooden crate the same dimensions as the strongbox, weighted with stones and a few pounds of scrap iron, packed with a scattering of real ore samples on top — enough glitter to read through binoculars. Inside, wrapped tight, a charge of flare compound and engine degreaser that would make a satisfying orange bloom when the crate hit open water. Not an explosion, exactly. More of a theatrical gesture. Dez thought of it as special effects.

"You realize," he told the crate, "this is the most interesting job you will ever do."

"All right," Ulyana called from the shore. She stood with her hands jammed in her parka pockets, looking smaller than usual and twice as hard, the way she did when she'd already decided something painful. "Positions. Everyone knows their part?"

They knew their parts. Hale would carry the crate down the ice with visible strain, because Voss knew Hale's build and would be watching for the right silhouette laboring under the right weight. Priya would stand at the shoreline with the camera she'd talked about since the day they'd dug her out of the cargo drums, taking pictures of nothing in particular, because a journalist photographing a salvage operation made the whole thing true. Teodora would run the winch line. Sam would handle the charge. And Dez would trigger it, because he'd built it, and because — as Ulyana had put it with the ghost of a smile — "if it fizzles, you're the only one who'll know how to swear about it properly."

Dez checked the ridge. Voss was still there. Still watching. Somewhere behind that patient silhouette was a radio, and somewhere beyond the radio was a company that had spent ten years and presumably a great deal of money making sure this mountain kept its mouth shut.

"Okay," Ulyana said quietly. "Let's bury it."

* * *

It went almost perfectly, which in Dez's experience was the most dangerous kind of going.

Hale carried the crate down the ice shelf with the strained, careful gait of a man hauling something worth more than his life, which required no acting whatsoever since Hale had spent the entire expedition looking like a man hauling something worth more than his life. Priya clicked away at the shoreline. Teodora paid out the winch line. And Dez, flat on his belly at the edge of the open water below the outflow, waited until the crate swung over the black lake and let it drop.

It went under clean. Three seconds. Four. Then the surface bloomed orange and white, a soft whump rolling across the water like the lake clearing its throat, and a scatter of debris floated up — splintered wood, a curl of smoke, and ore samples bobbing in the wreckage, quartz veins catching the light like a confession.

On the ridge, Voss raised his binoculars. Held them there a long moment. Then, slowly, he lowered them, and even at four hundred meters Dez could feel the shape of the man's satisfaction. *Good,* that posture said. *Good. Now we're done.*

"He bought it," Teodora whispered beside him.

"He bought it," Dez agreed. Then, because honesty mattered between mechanics: "He bought ninety percent of it. That last ten percent is going to depend on whether he comes down here to poke through the flotsam, and whether my fake crate floats convincingly dead."

The crate did float convincingly dead. They left it. They left the ore fragments scattered along the ice like a spill, left Priya's photographs to exist, left the whole scene arranged the way a stagehand leaves a set after the tragedy. And then they walked back up to camp with their shoulders slumped just slightly, defeated people with nothing left to guard, and Dez had to admit that Ulyana Iversen understood something about storytelling that most novelists would pay to learn.

* * *

The problem arrived at noon, wearing the face they'd all been avoiding.

"The numbers don't lie," Dez said, standing in front of the Beaver with his grease pencil and his weight-and-balance sheet, hating every word. "Full fuel, patched float, four souls. That's it. That's the airplane's whole opinion on the matter. Five of us and she won't unstick, and even if she did, that float patch is a one-flight miracle. I'm not loading her heavy over terrain like this."

Nobody spoke. The wind had begun to arrive ahead of the storm, moving through the spruce with a sound like a crowd deciding whether to riot. Snow squalls walked the far shore in gray columns.

"So someone stays," Hale said. His voice was carefully level. "Until the weather lifts and—"

"And what?" Teodora said. "There's no second plane coming. There's no rescue flight scheduled. Whoever stays walks out, or waits for the storm to pass and then walks out, and either way—" She stopped, because saying *either way they might die* out loud felt like signing it.

"I'll stay."

Everyone turned. Ulyana said it the way she announced fuel stops. Matter-of-fact. Already past it.

"No," Dez said.

"It's my expedition. My call. My—" She stopped herself, but everyone heard the word she'd swallowed. *My father's mountain.* "I know this country. I've got the tent, the stove, two weeks of rations if I ration like a trapper. The storm breaks in thirty-six hours, maybe forty-eight. I walk south to the survey cabin, follow the creek to the old trapline, and I'm at the lodge before you've finished your paperwork. I've done longer walks in worse."

"You've done them younger," Sam said quietly.

"I've done them alone, which is the relevant part." She looked around the circle, and Dez watched her do the thing she always did — turn her own fear into logistics, fold her grief into the flight plan, make sacrifice look like arithmetic. "Four seats. Priya flies out — Voss knows her face, and she's got a story to file that needs her alive. Hale goes, because if Voss figures out the box was a fake, Hale's the one he'll want to talk to, and I'd rather he do his talking in a town with a police detachment. Teo flies. Dez flies, because nobody else on God's green earth can land this airplane on that patched float without killing everyone aboard."

"And you," Dez said, "are the best pilot in this crew."

"Yes," Ulyana said simply. "Which is why I'm the only one who can afford to lose the argument."

"That's not how arguments work."

"That's exactly how arguments work when you own the airplane."

Teodora's face was doing something complicated — hero worship cracking into horror, the way it had cracked above the pass on the flight east. "You can't just—"

"I can. Watch me." But Ulyana's voice gentled, and she put a hand on Teodora's shoulder, and Dez saw her become, for one unguarded second, the mentor instead of the commander. "Listen to me. You are going to fly this airplane out, and you are going to fly it the way I taught you — slow decisions, fast hands. You'll put down at the lodge, you'll file my position with everyone who has a radio, and in three days you'll be drinking bad coffee and telling everyone how you survived a hijacking, which, by the way, you did, and I expect the full dramatic retelling when I see you. Because you will see me. That's an order from the owner of the airplane."

Teodora laughed wetly and wiped her face with her sleeve. "You're a terrible boss."

"I'm an excellent boss. Ask anyone I've ever stranded."

* * *

They loaded her out in the blue-gray light of late afternoon, working fast before the storm arrived in earnest. Dez went over the Beaver one last time with his flashlight in his teeth, though he'd gone over it four times already, because that was the deal between a mechanic and the sky: you checked until checking became superstition, and then you checked once more.

The patched float gleamed dull silver under its epoxy skin. He patted it like a horse's neck. "One flight, old girl. Make it a boring one."

Ulyana stood apart, at the tree line, with a pack she'd packed with the ruthless economy of someone who'd learned young that everything you carry is something you might have to drop while running. Tent. Stove. Rations. The .303, because she wasn't a fool. And, Dez noticed, tucked inside her jacket where the rest of them were politely pretending not to see it — a shape, flat and square and waterproof-wrapped.

The ledger. Or part of it. The real heart of the thing they'd supposedly drowned that morning.

So that was the shape of it, Dez thought. The ore went to the lake for Voss to mourn. The truth went into Ulyana's coat, walking south through a blizzard, because some things you didn't sink and you didn't surrender — you carried them out yourself, on your own back, through the worst weather the country could offer. Leadership, Dez reflected, watching her check the tie-downs one last time with those steady scarred hands, was mostly just self-erasure with good bookkeeping.

She caught him looking. Of course she did.

"You've got something to say, Dez."

"Yeah." He zipped his tool bag. "When you get to the survey cabin, check the stove pipe before you light it. Squirrels love a warm flue, and there's nothing sadder than a survival expert burning down her own shelter."

Ulyana smiled — a real one, small and crooked, the first he'd seen off her in days. "Noted."

"And Ulyana." He made himself say it plainly, because she'd earned plain. "Three days. If you're not at the lodge in three days, I'm flying back in whatever the weather's doing, patched float or no patched float, and I'll crash-land on this lake myself, and then we'll both be stranded, and it'll be very awkward."

"Understood," she said. "Nothing motivates me like sparing you embarrassment."

Hale shook her hand and held it a beat too long, and said something low that Dez didn't catch and was wise enough not to try to. Sam pressed something into her palm — a small carved thing, a loon, wing-tucked — and said only, "Old trick. The river people say the loon finds the open water." Priya hugged her hard enough to crack ribs and then pretended she hadn't. Teodora saluted, which should have been ridiculous and somehow wasn't.

Then they were aboard, and the engine caught on the third blade, coughing, settling into its grumble, and Dez ran the checks with Teodora reading back, her voice steadier than he'd expected, steadier than his own probably sounded.

The Beaver lifted off the lake at 4:17 in the afternoon, light as a promise, the patched float holding, the storm wall looming black and enormous over the western ranges like a door about to close. Dez twisted around in his seat for one last look.

Ulyana stood on the shrinking shore, one hand raised, the other holding the strap of her pack. Behind her the mountain rose into cloud. Ahead of her lay thirty miles of winter and whatever Voss decided to do next, and she stood there anyway, getting smaller, getting whiter, getting gone.

"She'll make it," Teodora said. Her knuckles were white on the yoke. "Right? She'll make it."

Dez faced forward and put his hand over hers on the throttle, steadying it the way you steady anything — not by gripping, just by being there.

"She'd better," he said. "Because in three days I'm flying back, and I am a *terrible* camper."

Behind them, the storm reached the lake, and the mountain took her out of sight.
