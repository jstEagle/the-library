## 19. Voss's Mistake

Priya Raman had spent eleven years as a journalist learning to read rooms, and she could say with professional confidence that the room they were leaving was the worst one she'd ever stood in.

Voss had walked into camp two days ago with a rifle slung casual as a fishing rod and a smile that never reached his eyes, and he'd made his offer of silence so politely that Dez had actually laughed before understanding it wasn't a joke. Now the strongbox was "destroyed," ore samples glittered somewhere at the bottom of the lake, and Voss had watched the whole performance from the ridge with binoculars like a man auditing a play he'd already read.

Which meant, Priya realized as they loaded the Beaver at first light, that Voss knew exactly how good an actor Ulyana Iversen was. And men like Voss did not leave loose ends floating on a lake.

She'd said as much to Sam while they lashed the last drum. He'd grunted — Sam's conversational register ran mostly to grunts — but his eyes had gone flat and watchful, and he'd moved his knife from his pack pocket to his belt without comment.

The plan was simple. The float patch had held overnight; the Beaver would lift four plus fuel, no more. Ulyana would stay behind, wait out the weather in the tent, walk out to the trapline cabin when the storm broke. Teodora would fly. Dez would ride shotgun beside her. Priya and Sam would take the middle seats. Hale — well, Hale had argued about staying in Ulyana's place and been overruled by a woman who did not argue so much as announce geological facts, and one of them was that Hale's knees were fifty-four years old and the walk out would kill him slower than guilt already was.

Priya settled into the middle bench, notebook inside her jacket where it wouldn't freeze, and tried not to think about how much she hated small aircraft. She'd once flown in a Cessna over the Mekong with a pilot who hummed show tunes through turbulence, and she'd decided then that her beat was trains. Trains were honest. Trains stayed on the ground out of mutual respect.

Teodora ran up the engine, and Priya watched the girl work the controls — twenty-three years old, maybe, cheeks pink, braid escaping from under her cap, hands moving over the yoke and throttles with a fluency that looked almost lazy until you understood it wasn't laziness at all. It was economy. Priya had interviewed enough surgeons and bomb-disposal techs to know what competence looked like when it stopped performing and started simply being.

"Clear!" Dez called, and the Beaver came off the lake in a long gray roar, spray sheeting off the floats, and the camp shrank beneath them — the tents, the wrecked plane on its cradle of stones, and Ulyana standing alone on the shore with one hand raised, getting smaller, getting gone.

Priya pressed her forehead near the cold window and thought: *I will come back for you.* She'd written worse promises under worse circumstances.

They climbed east toward the pass, into weather that had been threatening all morning and now committed fully. The ceiling came down to meet them like a lid closing. Teodora threaded between two ridgelines with a calm that Priya chose to interpret as skill rather than recklessness, though honestly the difference felt academic from the middle seat.

That was when Sam went rigid.

Priya felt it more than saw it — the big man's whole body changing pressure beside her, the way water changes before a rockslide. She followed his eyes down to the shoreline sliding past below them, to a figure standing on the gravel bar at the lake's outlet, where no one had any reason to be.

Even from this height she knew the posture. Shoulders square, head tilted slightly, watching them go with the patience of a man who had never once hurried in his life because everything eventually came to him.

"He can't have—" Dez started, twisting around from the right seat.

The radio crackled. Not static — a voice, dry and unhurried, riding a handheld frequency every bush pilot in the interior kept tuned for company traffic.

*"Beaver on departure, this is your friend on the ground. You're carrying something of mine. Turn back to the lake, or I'll make my point differently."*

"What point?" Dez demanded, keying the mic. "What are you—"

The point arrived thirty seconds later, visible even through the murk: a thread of black smoke rising from the direction of basecamp. Not the tents — those were stone's throw away, too far for smoke to reach quickly — but the woodpile, the fuel cache, the drum of mixed gas they'd set aside for the return leg. Burning. Burning merrily.

He hadn't burned the camp. He'd burned their margin. Their spare fuel, their retreat, their reason to believe Ulyana could sit out a week of weather and still walk out warm.

"Oh, you absolute *bastard*," Dez said, with feeling.

*"There's a strip forty miles south,"* Voss continued, voice pleasant as a man giving directions to a church picnic. *"Company staging. Wide, maintained, fuel, a phone that works. Land there and everyone has a very boring afternoon. Don't, and your captain spends the winter learning what her father learned."*

The cockpit went very quiet. Priya watched Teodora's shoulders — watched the girl take one breath, two, three, the way Ulyana did before saying something irreversible.

And then Priya saw the other thing. Saw Teodora's eyes flick to the fuel gauge, to the sky ahead, to the map clipped on Dez's knee, doing arithmetic. Priya had spent a decade watching powerful people lie, and she knew the look of someone beginning to lie right now, with their whole body, while their face stayed open and honest as a Sunday morning.

"Copy," Teodora said into the mic. Her voice was steady. Warm, even. "Staging strip. Give me a heading."

*"Two-two-five. Twenty minutes. Don't get creative."*

"No creativity," Teodora agreed, and banked south, and Priya sat back and began, quietly, methodically, to be terrified.

* * *

Here is what Priya knew, because knowing things was her entire profession and her entire personality, according to certain ex-boyfriends:

She knew the staging strip was real — she'd traced Hale's permits, which meant tracing the company's permits, which meant she'd seen the logistics documents listing that strip, its coordinates, its fuel contract. It existed. That was the problem. Everything Voss wanted existed; that was how he worked. He didn't bluff. He built situations where the truth did his threatening for him.

She also knew the weather. She'd listened to Teodora and Dez argue about the forecast at breakfast — a front pushing down from the northwest, snow squalls by midday, ceilings dropping to nothing along the southern route. Which meant the twenty-minute flight south was going to become a flight into the teeth of it.

And she knew Teodora. Three weeks of watching the girl idolize Ulyana Iversen from a respectful distance, absorbing her like rain into dry ground. And Priya had noticed something the others perhaps hadn't, because noticing was her job: Ulyana hadn't been teaching Teodora how to fly. Teodora could fly. Ulyana had been teaching her how to *decide* — when to push, when to turn back, when the mountain was lying to you, when the gauge was lying to you, when your own fear was lying to you most of all.

The question was whether any of it had stuck, or whether it would all shake loose the moment a man with a rifle started talking through a radio.

The squall line appeared ten minutes later, a gray wall stitched with white, and Priya felt the Beaver start to porpoise gently as the air went rough. Ahead, through the windshield, the world simply ended — no horizon, no definition, just cloud eating the ridgeline whole.

*"You're slowing down,"* Voss observed from the radio. He'd been silent five minutes, letting the tension do his work. *"Don't slow down."*

"Ice on the windows," Teodora said. Also true. Also irrelevant. "Dez, watch the carb heat."

"Pretend I'm not here," Dez murmured, too low for the mic, and Priya saw the mechanic's eyes flick to the same gauges Teodora was reading, and saw the exact moment Dez understood.

Priya understood a half-second later, because she'd been doing the math too, journalist-style, sloppy but sufficient: the staging strip lay beyond the squall line. To reach it they'd have to cross the front — twenty miles of deteriorating ice and turbulence in an overloaded floatplane — or turn, and turning meant Voss, and Voss meant Ulyana alone on a burning shoreline with winter coming down.

Unless the plane couldn't make it. Unless something happened. Unless the engine, say, failed.

Priya looked at Teodora's hands on the yoke. Steady. Deliberately steady, the way a liar's voice is deliberately calm. And Teodora caught her looking — caught her, held her eyes for one second in the mirror of the windshield reflection — and gave the smallest nod Priya had ever received in her life.

A nod that said: *you see it. Good. Now look away.*

Priya looked away. She turned to Sam instead, and found the old guide already awake behind his stillness, watching the girl fly with an expression Priya could only describe as recognition — the look of a man seeing someone else's ghost put on new flesh.

Sam leaned close, breath warm against her ear. "Hold on to something soft," he rumbled. "Preferably me."

"That's the least romantic thing anyone's ever said to me."

"Wait for it."

* * *

*"Five minutes out,"* Voss said. *"I can see the weather's thick. Push through it."*

"We're pushing," Teodora said.

And then, with the squall line a quarter mile ahead and rising like a curtain, she let the Beaver's nose drop two degrees, pulled the mixture lever with a delicacy Priya nearly missed, and said, in a voice pitched perfectly to carry over a radio: "Dez. Engine's running rough. Really rough."

"It is?" Dez said, catching on half a beat late and selling it badly, and Priya loved her for it.

*"What's wrong?"* All the picnic had gone out of Voss's voice.

"Rough running, losing RPM." Teodora's hands were suddenly everywhere and nowhere — feathering touches, a throttle jiggle, a flick of the boost pump that changed the engine note just enough to be audible truth. "Might be the ice. Might be contamination. I've got partial power. I'm going to try to stretch it to the strip."

*"If you land short of my strip—"*

"If I land short of your strip," Teodora repeated pleasantly, "it'll be because your fuel or your weather put me there, and you can explain that to whoever you report to. Hang on, everybody. This is going to be a landing."

It was not going to be a landing. Priya knew that the way she knew sources were lying — from the accumulation of small wrongnesses. The way Teodora's eyes weren't searching for the strip anymore. The way her descent angle steepened, committed, aimed not at the distant flat of the staging field but at the wide white expanse of the frozen bay sliding past beneath the cloud deck's ragged hem — a bay Priya remembered from the maps, shallow, sheltered, ringed low.

A bay that would stop a Beaver on floats the way a sidewalk stops a bicycle.

"Teodora," Priya said carefully, "what's under us?"

"Ice," Teodora said. "Mostly."

"*Mostly?*"

"Hold on!"

The engine coughed — genuinely coughed, Dez finally earning her keep by choking the mixture at the perfect moment — and the world tilted. Priya grabbed Sam's forearm, which was like grabbing a dock piling, and the bay came up fast, white and featureless, and the last thing she registered before impact was Teodora's voice, calm as a woman announcing dinner:

"Braces."

The floats hit first. The sound was enormous — a crack like the sky breaking, a shriek of metal, the whole airplane slamming sideways in a cartwheel of snow that somehow, impossibly, resolved into stillness. Silence. Steam of breath. The tick of cooling metal.

Priya opened her eyes. She was upside down against her harness, cheek pressed to the window, looking out at a wall of snow. Somewhere above her, blood dripped from her own nose onto the glass, one red coin at a time.

"Everybody alive?" Teodora's voice, shaking now for the first time, shaking hard.

"Define alive," Dez groaned from the front.

"Sam?"

"Soft," Sam confirmed, "as promised."

Priya laughed, and it hurt, and she laughed anyway. Then she heard the other sound — the sound that turned her laugh to ice.

A moan from the back. Not one of theirs. A wet, furious, animal moan, and the clatter of the cargo door wrenched open behind them, and Voss — who had forced his way aboard at the last fuel stop they'd faked needing, who had been crouched among the drums with his rifle the entire time, whom Priya had completely, catastrophically forgotten — dragged himself upright in the wreckage, left arm hanging wrong, face white with pain and rage.

"You," he snarled at Teodora, hauling himself forward with his good hand, rifle swinging toward the cockpit, "stupid, *dead* little—"

He got one step.

Sam Two-Rivers unfolded from the middle bench like a season changing, took the rifle barrel in one hand and Voss's collar in the other, and introduced the claim-jumper's face to the seat frame with a thoroughness that ended the conversation.

In the ringing quiet afterward, Teodora sat motionless at the yoke, hands still gripping, breath fogging, staring out at the broken white bay. Then she reached up with two fingers and touched the overhead frame — the way Priya had seen Ulyana do a hundred times, a pilot's small superstition, thank-you to the machine.

"Ulyana taught me that," she said, to no one. "Land it, then thank it."

Priya wiped her bloody nose, found her notebook miraculously intact inside her jacket, and opened it with numb fingers.

Because she'd just witnessed the story of the decade — a hijacking, a crash, a confession waiting in a broken man's mouth — and because somewhere south of here, on a burning shoreline, a woman was standing alone in the first snow of winter trusting four people to come back for her.

Priya clicked her pen.

"Teodora," she said. "Tell me everything. Start with the part where you decided to crash."
