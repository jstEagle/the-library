## 20. Controlled Descent

The gun changed everything about the sound of an engine.

Teodora had learned the Beaver's motor the way other people learned a language — by ear, by feel, by the thousand small dialects of its moods. She knew what it sounded like climbing heavy out of cold water, knew its cough on the first start of the morning, knew the particular hum it made when Dez had tuned it well and the slightly different hum when he'd tuned it after arguing with Ulyana about spark plugs. For six hundred hours she had listened to that engine and felt safe inside its conversation.

Now there was a pistol resting on Voss's thigh, pointed loosely at the space between her shoulder blades, and the engine sounded like a hostage.

"Staging strip," Voss said again, for the third time since they'd lifted off the lake. "Due east. You know it."

"I know *of* it," Teodora said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "It's a mud flat with a windsock somebody stole from a golf course."

"It has fuel and a telephone. Fly there."

She glanced at the panel. Fuel was the problem, or would be in forty minutes. The storm behind them was the bigger problem, or would be in twenty. The man with the gun was the immediate problem, or already was. She ran the arithmetic the way Ulyana had taught her — *worst thing first, then second worst, then the thing you can actually fix* — and discovered that all three problems were converging on the same patch of sky like relatives at a funeral.

Behind her, in the cabin, she could hear Priya breathing. Fast but controlled. Sam was silent, which frightened her more than the breathing; Sam's silence had been the first warning sign of this whole expedition and here it was again, dense as ballast. And Hale — Hale was somewhere back there too, and she couldn't hear him at all.

"You're doing fine," Voss said, and the gentleness in his voice was worse than shouting. "Just keep her level and point her east."

"Yes sir," Teodora said, because her father had raised her to be polite to men with guns right up until the moment she wasn't.

She looked east. The horizon over the ranges was the color of a bruise going green at the edges, and beneath it the frozen bays of the lake country lay white and featureless, snow over ice over black water, a landscape that offered nothing to a landing airplane except the opportunity to be wrong.

Ulyana would have a plan by now. That was the thought that kept surfacing, annoying as a mosquito. Ulyana would have taken one look at this situation and found the seam in it, the loose thread, the one dumb assumption Voss had made. But Ulyana was fifty miles south, stranded at the wreck site, walking out through a storm because the Beaver could only carry four plus fuel and leadership, apparently, meant being the one who stayed.

Which left Teodora. Flying. With a hijacker.

*The apprentice becomes the pilot her mentor made.* She thought of that phrase later, when people asked her about it, though at the time the only coherent thought in her head was: *I am twenty-three years old and I have never wanted anything as much as I want this man to stop talking.*

"The company will compensate you," Voss said. "Everyone in this aircraft. Generously. All you have to do is land where I say and forget my face."

"Forget your face," Teodora repeated.

"People do it all the time. Memory is cooperative, if you pay it enough."

"That's not how memory works."

"How would you know? You're a child."

And that — that stupid, casual, dismissive *child* — did something to her that fear hadn't managed. It settled her hands on the yoke. It slowed her breathing. Somewhere under her sternum a small hot coal lit up, and she recognized it from every flying lesson where Ulyana had pushed her past what she thought she could do: it was the feeling of being trusted with something too big, and deciding to be equal to it anyway.

"Sir," she said, "I need to check something on the panel. Keep your seatbelt tight."

She didn't wait for permission. She let the nose drop two degrees, just two, barely a breath of descent, and pulled the mixture control half an inch lean. The engine note thinned. To anyone who didn't know a radial engine intimately, it sounded like trouble beginning.

"What was that?" Voss said sharply.

"Running rough." She frowned at instruments she was reading perfectly well. "Happens sometimes when she's been sitting. Cold-soaked. If she quits, we put down wherever we can."

"If she quits—"

"Aircraft don't ask permission, sir. They just quit." She let the engine stumble once — a real stumble, achieved by feathering the throttle against the mixture until the prop caught air wrong and the whole airframe shuddered. Behind her someone made a small involuntary sound. Priya. Or maybe Hale. "Sorry. That's normal. Probably."

"Probably is not a word I like."

"Then you'll hate the next part."

* * *

Here is what Teodora had, and what Voss did not know she had:

She had three winters of landing the Beaver on ice, because Ulyana believed a pilot should learn on the surface most likely to kill them, so that everything afterward felt like mercy. She had Dez's voice in her head saying *the floats are the strong point, the belly is the weak point, never argue with physics, negotiate with it.* She had a map in her memory of every frozen bay on the eastern shore, drawn during long boring legs while Ulyana napped in the right seat — no, not napped, Ulyana never napped, while Ulyana *rested her eyes with total situational awareness*, which was somehow different and also somehow involved snoring.

And she had the storm. The storm was hers now. Voss thought the weather was his ally, pressing them east, hurrying them toward his little mud-flat kingdom. He hadn't done the math on what a storm does to a man in the back of a small plane with a broken arm waiting to happen.

Because that was the plan. It had assembled itself in her mind with the strange completeness of things you've known all along: she could not outfly him, could not outrun him, could not talk him down. But she could put this airplane down hard enough to end him as a threat, soft enough to keep everyone alive, and the only place in fifty miles suitable for that particular transaction was the bay directly ahead — flat white ice, a mile wide, wind quartering off the ridge at maybe fifteen knots, snow squalls drifting across it like curtains in an open window.

A frozen bay was not a runway. A frozen bay was a rumor of a runway. Ice that held a truck might not hold a Beaver's wheel-and-ski configuration; ice that held the weight might crack it sideways; and even perfect ice punished a hard landing with violence that had nowhere to go but into the airframe and everyone inside it.

But a floatplane on wheels, hitting ice at seventy miles an hour with the gear taking the shock — that was survivable. She'd seen the numbers. She'd asked Dez about it once, hypothetically, over coffee, and he'd looked at her for a long moment and said, "Why are you asking?" and she'd said, "Hypothetically," and he'd said, "Hypothetically, aim for the thickest ice near the shore, kill the engine before impact so it doesn't torque you around, and hold the yoke full back so the tail takes some of it. And then hypothetically buy me a very nice dinner, because we'd both deserve it."

She checked the ice ahead. Near the southern shore, where the shallows froze early and deep, the snow lay unbroken and blue-shadowed. Old ice. Good ice. Hypothetical ice.

"Engine's getting worse," she announced. "I'm declaring an emergency. You might want to put the gun away before the crash investigators find it."

Voss laughed. Actually laughed, one short bark. "You think I'm stupid?"

"No," Teodora said honestly. "I think you're scared, and scared men make bad guesses. Here's a good guess instead: if this plane crashes and I die, nobody flies you anywhere ever again. Your best outcome is me alive and calm. So let me be calm."

Silence from the back. Then, quietly, Priya's voice: "Teo. Whatever you're doing. Do it fast. There's a squall line coming up the valley."

"I see it."

"Do you have a plan?"

Teodora looked at the ice, at the storm, at the needle of the fuel gauge lying to her about how much time she had. "I have a hypothesis," she said.

* * *

The feigned failure became, at four hundred feet, a real performance.

She cut the mixture fully. The engine died with a wheeze and a silence so enormous it seemed to press on her eardrums, and suddenly the only sounds in the world were the wind singing in the wires and Voss saying, "What did you do. What did you *do*."

"Nothing," Teodora said. "That's the problem. Best glide is eighty-five. Everybody brace."

It was true, technically. The engine *had* quit. What Voss couldn't know was that she intended it to stay quit, that her thumb was already finding the primer lock, that the restart she'd offer him later would be a fiction with excellent paperwork. The Beaver became a glider the way a hippo becomes a swimmer — reluctantly, massively, but effectively — and the white bay swelled up toward them with terrible gentleness.

"Land on the strip," Voss snarled, and now the gun was moving, and she heard Hale's voice for the first time in ten minutes, low and gravelly: "Son, if you fire in this cabin you'll kill us all, and I promise you the crash won't be the worst part of your day."

"Shut *up*—"

"Brace," Teodora said, and flared.

She came in fast — faster than any instructor would forgive, because speed was armor on ice — and she held the yoke full back the way Dez had told her, hypothetically, over coffee, and the main gear touched first with a bang that went through the airframe like a hammer through a church bell.

The ice held. For one full second it held, and she thought, *we're going to live, we're actually—*

Then the right gear punched through a pressure ridge hidden under the snow, and the world became a centrifuge.

The Beaver slewed right, dug, cartwheeled onto its wing — slowly, horribly, the way these things happen in reality rather than in nightmares, each stage distinct and deniable — and Teodora's head met the door frame and the sky and the ice traded places twice, three times, and then there was a sound like the entire lake clearing its throat, and then stillness.

Snow drifted through the shattered windshield. The engine ticked as it cooled, though it wasn't running and wouldn't run. Somewhere water was lapping — the wing float, she thought dimly, the left float had come through the ice and was riding open water, which meant the plane was settling, which meant *get out*, which meant—

"Everybody out!" Her voice worked. That was encouraging. "Out, out, uphill side, *now*!"

She fell out her door into eighteen inches of snow and got her feet under her. The Beaver lay on its left wing like a tired bird, tail high, steam or smoke — steam, please, steam — curling from the cowling. And then the figures came out of it: Priya first, quick as a weasel, then Hale hauling himself through the broken cargo door with a dignity that survived even this, then Sam, unhurt, silent, steady, catching Priya's arm as she slipped.

Voss came last. He came badly.

He had braced wrong — braced against the crash instead of with it, gun hand locked rigid — and the impact had done what impacts do to rigid things. He rolled out into the snow clutching his right arm against his chest, and even from ten feet away Teodora could see the wrongness of the angle, the forearm bowed where a forearm doesn't bow.

"My arm," he said. Not a threat. Not a negotiation. Just a man stating a fact to the universe and expecting sympathy.

"Broken," Sam confirmed, crouching beside him with the professional detachment of a man who'd set bones in places without hospitals. "Both bones. Clean, though. You're lucky."

"Lucky," Voss repeated.

"You picked a pilot who wanted everyone alive," Sam said. "Could've gone differently."

Teodora stood in the falling snow, shaking so hard her knees knocked, and began — appallingly, uncontrollably — to laugh. It came out of her in great whooping gasps, adrenaline finding its exit, and Priya grabbed her shoulders and shook her gently and said, "Teo. Teodora. Look at me. Are you hurt?"

"I landed a Beaver on a frozen bay on purpose," Teodora said, "and it *worked*, mostly, and I'm either fine or I'm going to find out I'm fine later. Check the ELT. Check the ELT first."

Priya was already moving. The emergency beacon had armed on impact; its signal was crawling outward through the storm at the speed of light, toward satellites, toward people with helicopters. Forty minutes. Maybe less, if the weather allowed. Maybe more, if it didn't.

Voss sat in the snow holding his ruined arm and watched the wrecked airplane and understood, visibly, in real time, the shape of his mistake. "You faked the failure," he said.

"I *had* a failure," Teodora said. "It was moral."

Hale made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled by a cough. He lowered himself carefully onto the wing root, took stock of his own ribs with probing fingers, winced, and pronounced, "Bruised. Everyone bruised. Nobody dead." He looked at Teodora for a long moment, and something moved across his face that she'd never seen there before — not gratitude exactly, something older and more reluctant, the look of a man revising a ledger he'd kept closed for years. "Ulyana trained you."

"Ulyana trained me."

"She'd have put it down softer."

"She'd have put it down softer," Teodora agreed, "but she'd have been flying with a copilot and a functioning plan. I had a hypothesis and a grudge."

Sam was splinting Voss's arm with a piece of cargo strap and a ski pole, efficient and impersonal. Voss hissed but didn't fight. There was nowhere to fight to. The storm was closing around the bay like a fist, and the nearest help was a signal beacon and a prayer, and the man with the broken arm was, for the first time since he'd walked into their camp with his polite offers, simply a patient.

"We need shelter before dark," Sam said. "There's an old trapper's cache on the point, half a mile. I saw it on the way in."

"You've been here before," Priya said. Not a question.

"Everyone's been here before," Sam said. "That's the whole story, miss. Everybody comes back to this country eventually. It just depends what state they come back in."

They gathered what they could from the wreck — sleeping bags, the survival kit, a dented thermos that Dez would mourn — and Teodora took one last look at the Beaver before they left it. One wing in the water, one in the snow, propeller bent into a shape like a question mark. Ten minutes ago it had been her captor's prize and her prison. Now it was just a wounded bird on the ice, and she loved it more than she'd ever loved anything mechanical.

"You did good," Hale said quietly, beside her.

"Don't be nice to me," Teodora said. "I'm holding it together with tape."

"Then I'll say it ugly: that was the worst landing I've ever survived and the best one I've ever seen, and I don't know how both those things are true, but they are."

She nodded, because her throat had closed, and turned toward the point where Sam was already breaking trail through the snow.

Behind them, the beacon pulsed its small stubborn signal into the storm — and somewhere south, through the whiteout, a woman was walking out of the mountains carrying a secret buried in a cairn, and Teodora found herself thinking, with sudden fierce clarity, that whatever happened next, whatever the rescue brought and whatever Voss's employers sent after it, the story was no longer theirs to bury.

It was in the air now. Like everything that mattered, it was in the air.
