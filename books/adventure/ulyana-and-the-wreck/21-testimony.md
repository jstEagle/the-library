## 21. Testimony

The hospital in Yellowknife smelled like antiseptic and burnt coffee, which Ulyana decided was the honest smell of any place where people told the truth.

She'd been grounded for six days. Six days of a doctor with kind eyes telling her the ribs would knit, of nurses who kept mistaking her for someone's mother, of a phone that rang and rang until Dez threatened to throw it into Great Slave Lake. Her shoulder was strapped. Her hands, mercifully, were fine. She'd spent an hour the first night just looking at them, turning them over under the fluorescent light like a mechanic inspecting tools that had come through a crash better than expected.

Priya Raman arrived on the seventh day with a recorder the size of a cigarette pack, two coffees, and the expression of a woman who had been rehearsing restraint in the elevator.

"They're letting me have forty minutes before the RCMP get here," Priya said, setting the cups down. "Which is generous of them, considering I've been camped in your hallway since Tuesday."

"You've been sleeping in a chair."

"I've been *working* in a chair. There's a difference." She clicked the recorder on, then paused, thumb hovering. "Before we start — off the record, one question. Are you sure?"

Ulyana looked at her. Priya was thirty-something, sharp-eyed, still faintly smelling of woodsmoke from a week in the bush she'd never been invited on. Ten days ago Ulyana had wanted to put her on the first floatplane south. Now she was about to hand her the story of a decade.

"I've been sure since the cairn," Ulyana said. "I just took the long way around to saying it out loud."

"Then let's say it." Priya pressed record. "This is Priya Raman, recording at Stanton Territorial Hospital, Yellowknife. I'm speaking with Ulyana Iversen, pilot and owner-operator of Iversen Air. Ulyana — start wherever you want. Start with your father."

Ulyana had imagined this moment a hundred ways during the whiteout nights, during the walk down to the lake, during the flight out with Voss's broken arm strapped to his chest and Teodora's hands steady as bedrock on the yoke. In most versions she'd been angrier. In some she'd been crying. What actually came out was quieter than all of them, and it surprised her: she sounded like her father reading a weather report. Calm. Factual. Certain of the sky.

"In 1979 my father, Anders Iversen, flew a survey contract for Hale Minerals," she said. "Everyone in the interior knew him. He taught half the pilots on this side of the lake how to land on glass water. He found something east of the divide — a mineralized zone, copper and cobalt, rich enough that the assay sheets were worth more than the plane he died in."

"And you believe those assays were stolen."

"I don't believe it. I've held the proof." She described the ledger the way Sam must once have described it to himself, alone, for ten years: the waterproof casing, the claim-stake records in her father's hand, the second set of numbers showing the strike registered not to Iversen but to Marcus Hale's father — filed eleven days after Anders's plane went down, using survey data dated before the expedition ever launched. "My father wasn't lost," she said. "He was erased. And the company that did it built a town and a fortune on top of the erasure."

Priya didn't interrupt. That was the thing Ulyana had learned about her in the bush — she listened like a person setting stones in a wall, each fact fitted against the last.

"The cover-up?" Priya prompted gently.

"My father knew the risk. He wrote me a letter the night before the final flight. He said if anything happened, the truth would be in the strongbox, and that I should decide what the country could carry." Ulyana's voice caught, just once, on the word *decide*. "He also wrote that some ground forgives nothing. He meant that a strike like that, pulled out of that valley, would cost more than it paid. The letter asked me to choose between the claim and the land. For ten years I thought choosing meant burying it. I was wrong. Choosing means putting it in daylight and letting other people do the arithmetic."

The recorder turned over. Outside the window, a medevac King Air lifted off the strip and banked north, and Ulyana watched it go with the particular ache of a pilot watching weather she couldn't fly.

"When the RCMP arrive," she said, "I'll give them the cairn's location. Grid reference, photographs, everything. My father's remains are there, and they deserve a proper burial, not a secret. The ledger's inside the cairn. I hid it there during the storm because I thought I was protecting it. Turns out I was just keeping it warm for the law."

Priya clicked the recorder off. For a moment neither of them spoke.

"You know what happens," Priya said quietly. "Hale Minerals has lawyers who eat ministries for breakfast. You'll be sued, investigated, maybe charged for interfering with a historical site. Your operating certificate will get looked at sideways for a year."

"I know."

"And you're doing it anyway."

"Priya." Ulyana smiled, and it hurt her ribs, and she smiled anyway. "I crashed my own plane into a frozen bay to stop one man from carrying that ore out. Do you really think a subpoena frightens me?"

* * *

Marcus Hale came at dusk, when the corridor lights had gone amber and the nurses moved softer. He stood in the doorway of her room holding his hat like a man at a funeral, which, in a way, he was.

"They told me you gave the statement," he said.

"This morning. Full record. Names, dates, the works."

He nodded slowly. He'd lost weight since the mountain; the storm had carved it off him, or maybe the truth had. He sat down across from her without being asked, and for a while he studied his own hands — hands that had signed the charter, lied about the survey plane, and then, on a frozen ridge, chosen to stand between Sam Two-Rivers and a gun.

"My lawyers called," he said. "They have a strategy. It involves denying everything, discrediting Raman, and painting you as a grieving woman with a grudge. It's a good strategy. It might even work." He looked up. "I told them to find other clients."

Ulyana felt something shift in her chest that had nothing to do with the ribs.

"Hale—"

"Marcus. If we're going to do this, we may as well use Christian names. It'll annoy everyone." He almost smiled. "I'm giving testimony tomorrow. All of it. My father's files, the letters, the payments to the survey crew. There's a man named Voss who worked for him — you met his work up close. I can name where he banked, who he reported to, the whole machinery. When it's done, the Hale name will be worth exactly what it always should have been." He set his hat on his knee. "Nothing."

"That's not nothing," Ulyana said. "That's the first true thing anyone's built on it in fifty years."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "Your father offered my father a partnership once. Did you know that? 1978. Equal shares, before the assays even came back. My father laughed at him. Said a bush pilot didn't understand what ground was worth." His jaw tightened. "Turns out your father was the only one who did."

Outside, snow was starting again — light, unhurried, the kind that meant the season turning rather than the season attacking. Ulyana watched it come down past the window and thought about the valley east of the divide, closed now to claims by emergency order, its lake keeping whatever the lake keeps.

"What happens to you after tomorrow?" she asked.

"Probably ruin. Possibly charges. Definitely my mother never speaking to me again, though frankly that ship sailed decades ago." He stood, put the hat on, and paused at the door. "Iversen. When this is over — if there's anything left of me that isn't depositions — I'd like to buy you a drink. Not a charter. A drink."

"Triple rates," Ulyana said, "and I'll consider it."

This time he did laugh, briefly, like something escaping. Then he was gone down the corridor, a man walking toward his own testimony the way she'd once walked toward a ridgeline — knowing exactly what it would cost, and climbing anyway.

* * *

They released her on day nine. Dez picked her up at the hospital doors in the base truck, Teodora riding shotgun with her arm out the window despite the cold, and neither of them said anything for the first mile, which was its own kind of welcome-home.

Then Dez cleared his throat. "Beaver's a write-off. Insurance says salvage. But—" he glanced at her sidelong, "—the floats are fine. Engine's rebuildable. And there's a '52 airframe sitting in Fort Smith with low hours and an ugly paint job that I could make beautiful by spring."

"You already called them."

"I called them in the ambulance parking lot while you were getting X-rayed. Priorities, boss."

Teodora twisted around in her seat. "And I talked to the licensing office. With your hours plus mine, if you take a co-pilot through next season — officially, logged, all of it —"

"You want to fly medicine runs," Ulyana said.

"I want to fly *your* medicine runs." Teodora's chin came up, all the hunger of those first charter days still in it, but steadier now, tempered like steel that had been through a fire and a frozen bay. "You taught me to read wind on rock. Somebody should make you keep doing it."

Ulyana looked out the window at the familiar shoreline sliding past — the dock, the hangar, Sam's truck already parked crooked as ever beside it, smoke rising from the stovepipe where he'd clearly let himself in to make coffee whether anyone liked it or not. Home. Not the one she'd lost, and not the one she'd been hiding in either. A new one, still under construction, like everything worth having.

"Co-pilot," she said. "But you're cleaning the floats."

"Deal," said Teodora, so fast it made Dez laugh.

At the dock, Sam was waiting with two mugs and no speech, which was Sam's way. He handed her one and looked east, where the ranges stood blue and patient above the tree line.

"Cairn'll be good stone," he said. "I checked it twice before we left. Nothing moves that rock but spring ice."

"I know," Ulyana said. "I built it to hold something once before." She drank her coffee and watched the weather come down the lake, fair and cold and flyable, and found — to her genuine surprise — that her hands were perfectly steady. "Come spring, I'm flying back up. One last trip. You coming?"

Sam smiled into his mug. "Wouldn't miss it."

Behind them, the hangar door rattled open and Dez's voice boomed something about torque wrenches and miracles, and Teodora shouted back, and the dogs down the shore started up for no reason at all. Ulyana stood a moment longer in the middle of it, a grounded pilot with cracked ribs, a ruined reputation, a story in every paper, and not one single secret left to carry.

It was, she decided, the lightest she had felt in ten years.

Then the phone rang in the office, and Dez hollered, "Boss! Medicine run to the trapping camps — they need it up there by dark!" — and Ulyana Iversen drained her coffee, zipped her jacket, and went to work.
