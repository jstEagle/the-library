## 22. The Long Way Home

The ice went out of the lake on a Tuesday, and by Thursday morning the Beaver was back on her floats and Ulyana Iversen was doing what she had always done: hauling medicine and mail into the interior while the coffee in her thermos was still too hot to drink.

Some things, she thought, banking south over the river with the morning sun turning the water to hammered copper, were worth coming back for.

"Base to Beaver," Dez's voice crackled in her headset, lazy as a cat in a sunbeam. "You're looking good on departure. Teo says your climb-out was pretty. I told her pretty doesn't get you over a ridge."

"Tell Teodora my climb-out was *efficient*," Ulyana said.

"She says efficient is just pretty with an ego."

Ulyana laughed — actually laughed, out loud, alone in the cockpit, which would have been unthinkable a year ago. "Tell her she's grounded until she learns respect."

"Respect," Dez repeated. "Right. I'll write that down next to 'quiet' and 'tidy.'"

She let him have the last word. It cost her nothing these days, and it bought him more than he'd ever admit.

The medicine run was routine — penicillin to the Kowalski trapline, insulin to old Marta at the fish camp, a crate of dental supplies that smelled like it had been packed by someone with a grudge against dentists. The kind of flying she'd done ten thousand times. The kind of flying she'd once used as a wall between herself and everything east of the pass.

Now she flew east when the job asked it of her, and it didn't even hurt. Mostly.

"Beaver to base," she said, twenty minutes out from the first stop. "Anything else on the board?"

"Just you, me, and the ghost of Marcus Hale's legal bills," Dez said. "Oh — and Sam caught a grayling off your dock this morning bigger than anything he's pulled out of there all summer. He wants you to know he's not gloating."

"He's absolutely gloating."

"He's absolutely gloating."

* * *

The investigation had been running for six weeks now, and Ulyana had learned that government moved at roughly the speed of sediment. There were depositions, and then re-depositions, and a man from the Ministry who kept calling her "Ms. Iverson" no matter how many times she corrected him. Priya Raman's series — four parts, running under the headline *The Ledger Below* — had done more in one week than the Ministry had managed in six. The company's stock had dropped like a stone off a float dock. Two directors had resigned. Voss was in custody awaiting trial, his arm healing crooked inside a cast he complained about constantly, according to Priya, who visited him anyway because she was Priya and a source was a source.

And the ranges — all of them, the whole broken country east of the pass where her father had died — were closed to claims. Closed pending review, which everyone understood meant closed forever, or close enough. No road would go in. No drill rig would wake the lake. The ore could sleep in its mountain another ten thousand years, and the mountain would keep it.

"You did that," Teodora had said to her once, not long after they'd all gotten home, standing on the dock with her hands jammed in her pockets the way she did when a feeling got too big for her mouth.

"We did that," Ulyana had said. "All of us. Even Hale." She'd paused. "*Especially* Hale, if we're being honest about who had the most to lose."

Teodora had nodded slowly, and then, because she was nineteen and honesty was still a muscle she exercised recklessly, she'd added: "I cried during your testimony. Just so you know. In the back row. Priya saw."

"I know," Ulyana had said. "Priya told me."

"I'll deny it."

"Denied," Ulyana had agreed, and that had been the end of it, and somehow that had been enough.

Hiring Teodora full-time had been the easiest decision of the year. The girl flew like water finding its level — smooth, patient, going around obstacles instead of fighting them. Where Ulyana had spent a decade flying *away* from things, Teodora simply flew *toward*, and watching her do it had quietly taught Ulyana something she hadn't known she needed to learn.

The hard decision had been the letter. But that came later. That came at the cairn.

* * *

The first stop was the Kowalski trapline, where Mrs. Kowalski met the plane in her slippers and made Ulyana take eggs whether she wanted them or not. The second was Marta's fish camp, where the insulin was received with a grunt that Marta's family swore was practically a sonnet. By early afternoon Ulyana was empty of cargo and light as a bird, climbing back up through the scattered cumulus toward home with the sun warm on her left shoulder.

"Base to Beaver," Dez said. "You've got a visitor. He's been sitting on your dock for two hours drinking my coffee and telling fishing lies with Sam."

"Hale?"

"Hale."

Ulyana was quiet for a moment. "Is he —"

"He's fine. He looks better than fine. He looks like a man whose father's name got scrubbed clean in the newspapers and who slept eight hours for the first time in a decade." A pause. "He also brought pastries. Real ones. From the bakery in Fort Nelson. So whatever you decide about throwing him off the dock, do it after I've had my share."

"I'm landing, Dez."

"Yeah, yeah."

* * *

Marcus Hale stood up when she stepped onto the dock, which was new. Ten years ago he'd have stayed sitting just to make a point. He was thinner than he'd been in the mountains, grayer at the temples, but the hunted look was gone from around his eyes, and without it he looked almost like someone she might have liked in another life.

"Iversen," he said.

"Hale." She looked at the pastry box between them on the dock boards. "Those bribes?"

"Peace offerings."

"They can be both." She sat down across from him, cross-legged, the way she used to sit on this dock as a girl with her boots drying in the sun. Sam, three meters away at the end of the dock with his line in the water, made a great show of not listening, which was Sam's way of listening very carefully.

They ate pastries for a while. They were excellent. Of course they were; Hale had never done anything halfway, including guilt.

"The Ministry called me yesterday," he said finally. "Final review of the claim record. They wanted my testimony confirming the ledger's chain of custody." He turned his coffee cup in his hands. "I gave it. On the record. My name's on it now, right next to the part about my father. Permanent."

"How does that sit?"

He considered the question honestly, which she'd learned he could do when he wasn't performing. "Like a splinter coming out," he said. "It hurts more going out than it ever did going in. But I can put weight on it now."

Sam's reel clicked. He pulled in a small pike, looked at it with professional disdain, and put it back. "That's the third one today telling me the lake's fished out," he announced to no one. "Lake's not fished out. Fish are just smarter than me." He recast. The performance of indifference continued.

"Hale," Ulyana said. "Why are you really here?"

He looked out at the water for a long moment. "My mother kept every clipping," he said. "Ten years of clippings about the strike, the expedition, the disappearance. Boxes of them. After the story ran, she took the boxes out to the burn barrel behind the house." His jaw worked. "She burned them all except one. The one with your father's picture. She said he deserved to be remembered properly, and she'd been wrong about him, and she was too old to say sorry to anyone but a photograph." He shrugged, helpless. "So I came to say it to someone who isn't a photograph."

Ulyana looked at him. Ten years of anger was a heavy thing to carry, and she had set most of it down already, somewhere over the mountains, somewhere in the whiteout, somewhere in the moment she'd trusted him with her life and he'd held it carefully. What remained fit in her hand now, and she found she could simply open her fingers.

"He'd have accepted that," she said. "The apology. He didn't hold grudges. Drove my mother crazy."

"That tracks. You hold enough for the whole family."

"I'm working on it." She reached over and took another pastry. "Come by tomorrow. We're doing a mail run to the mission at Loon Narrows, and Teodora's flying the leg past the pass for the first time since the storm season. She could use a passenger who talks less than the cargo."

Hale blinked. Then, slowly, he smiled — and it changed his whole face, made him look briefly like a boy. "Iversen. Are you offering me a ride in your airplane?"

"I'm offering you a ride in *my* airplane, which is different, and you'll treat it accordingly."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And you're carrying the mail bags yourself."

"Naturally."

* * *

Two days later she flew north alone.

It wasn't scheduled. It wasn't necessary. Dez raised an eyebrow when she filed the flight plan and asked no questions, because Dez was a mechanic and mechanics understand that some maintenance happens on the inside.

The lake below the ridgeline lay flat and blue and utterly indifferent, holding nothing now but sky. She circled it once — just once, a slow turn, wingtip pointing at the shore where they'd made camp, where Sam had carried a secret for ten years, where Voss had watched them scatter ore into the water and believed he'd won. Then she climbed east, up the valley her father had flown the day the weather closed on him, and set the Beaver down on the gravel bar below the ridge.

The hike took two hours. Her legs knew the way. That was the strange thing — she'd made this climb exactly once before, in terror and grief, and yet her feet remembered every ledge, every scree chute, every place where the wind funneled through the rocks and sang a low note like a struck wire. Her father had taught her to read wind on rock when she was nine years old, cupping his hand behind his ear on the bluffs above their cabin. *Listen, little owl. The wind tells you what the mountain's thinking.* She heard it now, and it sounded like it was thinking something patient.

The wreck sat where it had always sat, silvered now, settling further into the lichen year by year. The Ministry had catalogued it and left it — a registered site, protected, a grave before it was ever evidence. Someone had anchored the fuselage against winter snow-creep. Small mercies from strangers.

The cairn stood above it on the bench, stones fitted tight, the way her father would have built it. Ulyana sat down beside it with her back against the largest rock and let the silence come all the way in.

She'd read the letter forty times since the mountain. She knew it by heart, but she took it out anyway, because knowing a thing by heart and touching it were different animals.

*My little owl,* it began. *If you're reading this, I was wrong about the weather, and I'm sorry, because I promised your mother I'd never be wrong about weather.*

*I found something in these hills that men will do terrible things to own. I'm not going to be one of the terrible things. Your grandfather always said a claim is just a promise you make to the ground, and I'm making mine here: nobody digs up this country for what's in it. Not while I can help it.*

*If it costs me, it costs me. But don't you dare let it cost you. Don't spend your life circling this place the way I circled it. Fly through it. Fly over it. Land where you like. The sky was always yours — the ground was only ever borrowed.*

*Wind's picking up out of the northeast. I should go.*

*All my love, always,*
*Dad*

She'd hidden the letter from the investigation — not destroyed, never destroyed, just kept — because it was hers, and because some documents aren't evidence. They're inheritance.

But she'd thought about it all summer, lying awake in the blue hours, and she'd come to understand what her father had actually written. *Don't spend your life circling this place.* And she had spent ten years doing exactly that, banking wide around the eastern ranges, keeping the memory locked in a strongbox of her own. The letter wasn't a relic. It was an instruction. And instructions weren't meant to be hoarded.

She folded it along its old creases, careful as handling a moth.

Then she lifted the top stone of the cairn — the one she'd placed herself, the one sized for exactly this, though she hadn't known it at the time — and set the letter beneath it, on top of the waterproof sleeve that held the second ledger, the one the Ministry had collected, photographed, and returned to her keeping until the review concluded. Let them rest together, the proof and the promise. Let the mountain hold what the mountain had always been willing to hold.

She replaced the stone and pressed both palms flat against the cairn, the way she used to press her hands against her father's cheeks to feel whether he was cold.

"Fly through it," she said aloud. "Copy that."

The wind came up the valley from the northeast and moved through the wreck's open door with a sound almost like breathing. She sat with it for a long time. Then she got up, brushed off her knees, and started down toward the plane, and her legs carried her lightly, like a woman leaving a visit rather than a grave.

* * *

Home again by evening, and the dock was lit gold by the low sun, and everything was exactly where it belonged.

Dez was under the Beaver's engine cowling, swearing at a fuel line with real affection. Sam was at the end of the dock with three fat grayling on a stringer and a fourth on the line, gloating openly now, having given up all pretense. Teodora sat on the rail beside him going through the next day's charts, and when she saw Ulyana step out of the trees she didn't wave or call out — she just nodded, once, pilot to pilot, the way you acknowledge someone returning from a flight you couldn't see.

"Good trip?" Sam asked, reeling in.

"Good trip."

"Catch anything?"

"As a matter of fact," Ulyana said, sitting down on the dock and pulling off her boots, "I left something where it belongs."

Sam looked at her for a moment. Something passed across his face — relief, maybe, or recognition, the look of a man setting down a pack he'd forgotten he was wearing. "About time," he said, and handed her a fishing rod instead of asking anything else, because that was Sam's way of saying everything.

They fished while the light went long and amber. Teodora abandoned her charts and joined them, catching nothing and not caring, narrating her planned route to Loon Narrows with the fierce joy of someone who has discovered that the thing she loves and the thing she's good at are the same thing. Dez emerged from the engine smelling of solvent and declared that dinner would be fish if anyone caught any and beans if they didn't, so everybody start hoping. Somewhere down the lake a loon called, and the mountains stood dark and quiet against the last of the light, keeping what they kept.

Ulyana sat among them with a rod in her hand and a line in the water and thought: *this*. Not the recovered past, not the buried truth, not the name she'd made or the one she'd lost. Just this — the dock, the crew, the work waiting at dawn, the sky overhead going slowly, generously full of stars.

Tomorrow there'd be mail to carry. Medicine for Marta. A passenger who owed her a debt and would pay it in conversation. A young pilot hungry for hours, and a pass to take her through, and weather to read, and a hundred landings yet to make on water she loved.

Her father had been right about the sky. It had been hers all along. She'd only had to stop circling and fly.

The loon called again. Sam's rod bent double.

"Don't gloat," Ulyana warned.

"I would *never*," said Sam, gloating, and the evening came down soft around them all, and held.
