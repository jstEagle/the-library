## 1. The Sandbar's Mood

The river told you things before it showed you them, if you'd learned its grammar. Xanthe Blackwood had learned it the hard way, over ten years of low water and high water, and this morning it was telling him about the sandbar ahead of the *Vesper*'s bow — a long, pale tongue of silt that hadn't been on any chart last month and wouldn't be on one next month either. The river moved its furniture the way a drunk moves through a house: constantly, carelessly, and always in the dark.

"Quarter point to port," he said, not raising his voice. Sal heard him anyway. Sal always heard him, the way old dogs hear thunder before it arrives.

The wheel answered heavy in his hands, the wood worn smooth as bone by forty years of other men's palms before his. Below them the paddle wheel churned its slow rhythm, and the *Vesper* — a hundred and twenty feet of patched steel and stubborn oak, older than Xanthe and likely to outlast him — slid west across the channel like a woman crossing a room she knew by heart.

The morning was the color of pewter. Low season. The shallows had come up out of the riverbed like bones through skin, and every captain on the water was running light cargo and lighter margins, threading channels that shrank by the hour. Xanthe had taken a contract hauling salt and lamp oil up to the mill towns, the kind of run that paid the crew and fed the boat and left nothing over, the kind of run that kept the *Vesper* alive without ever letting her live.

He didn't look at the compass.

He could feel it there anyway, the way you feel a scar. It sat in its brass cradle on the binnacle, a fine old instrument with a rosewood case and a needle that had once been famous — or would have been, if anyone but Mara had ever trusted it. It didn't point north. It hadn't pointed north in ten years. It pointed somewhere off to the southwest, steady and useless and patient, as if it were waiting for something. As if it knew something the rest of the world had forgotten.

Ten years, and he still caught himself glancing at it out of habit, still felt that small cold drop in his stomach when he remembered. Dead needle. Dead mentor. The two facts had fused somewhere inside him, so that checking the compass was like visiting a grave.

"Bar's shallower than it looks," Sal said, coming up beside him. Sal was seventy if he was a day, with forearms like dock rope and a face the river had spent a lifetime carving. He'd been first mate under Mara too, which made him the only other person aboard who remembered what the compass used to do. He never mentioned it. That was a kind of mercy, and Xanthe had never thanked him for it, because thanking him would have meant saying it aloud.

"I see it."

"You see the top of it." Sal spat over the rail, an old ritual, half navigation and half prayer. "River's dropped two hands since Tuesday. That bar's got teeth under it."

"Then we'll go around the teeth."

"Mm." Sal looked at him sideways, the look he'd been giving Xanthe for a decade, the look that said *you steer like her and you grieve like her too, and neither of those is a compliment.* "Kettle Landing by noon?"

"By noon. Tide's with us."

"Tide," Sal said, in the tone of a man who had stopped believing in tides the way other men stop believing in saints. He went forward to rouse the deckhands.

Xanthe stood alone at the wheel, which was where he preferred to be, which was also the problem, though he'd never let himself think it in those words. The *Vesper* carried a crew of nine, and he knew every one of them — knew that the cook, Bram, watered the soup on Thursdays because he bought his vegetables on credit and paid on Fridays; knew that young Petch still tied his knots the way his father had taught him, wrong, and would unlearn it only when he was ready; knew the sound each of their boots made on the deck planking, so that he could tell who was coming without turning around. He knew them and he kept them at the length of a wheel's spoke. That was the arrangement. That was the whole architecture of his life: a boat he loved, a crew he protected, a river he understood, and a silence in the middle of it shaped exactly like a woman who had drowned ten years ago with her hand on this same wheel.

The sandbar came and went beneath them, close enough that Xanthe could read its mood in the way the current wrinkled over it — a smooth, oily slick where the water ran deep over silt, a chattering roughness where it shallowed. He took the *Vesper* through the slick with two feet to spare and felt the old pride rise in him, and felt the old hollowness rise right behind it, because there was no one to show it to. Competence, he'd learned, was just another way of being alone. You got so good at the river that the river was all you had left to talk to.

The compass needle trembled. He saw it in the corner of his eye and his hands went still on the wheel.

It did that, sometimes. Not often. Maybe once a season, a small shiver, as though the needle were dreaming. He had taken the instrument apart twice in the early years, looking for a fault — a bent pivot, a fouled bearing, anything mechanical and fixable. There was nothing. The needle swung southwest and trembled occasionally, and the brass case held a faint warmth that no brass case should hold, and Xanthe had finally done what he did with everything he couldn't fix: he'd stopped looking at it.

*Southwest.* Toward the gorge. Toward the stretch of river they had both sworn off, the flooded canyon below Raven's Teeth where the water went down a hundred feet into green blackness and never gave back what it took. Toward the place where the *Mara's Luck* — her name on her own boat, God, her vanity and her glory — had gone down in a spring flood with Mara at the wheel and the river doing what the river does.

They'd found the boat. They'd never found her. The river kept her, the way it kept everything, and Xanthe had spent ten years telling himself that was grief, and ten years failing to believe it, because grief was a weight and this was something else. This was a question. The river had asked him a question the night she died, and he had spent a decade refusing to answer it.

"Land," called Petch from the bow, unnecessarily, the way young men call out land as if the rest of the boat might be worried they'd steered off the edge of the world.

Kettle Landing resolved out of the haze: a timber wharf, a rope ferry, a scatter of tin roofs, the usual congregation of barges and gossip. Xanthe brought the *Vesper* in against the current with the easy authority of long habit, lines flying, fenders squealing, and the wharf took her weight with a groan of old pilings. He stood at the wheel until the last line was made fast. He always did. A captain left the wheel last or not at all.

* * *

The trouble came aboard at two o'clock, wearing a gray suit that had been good five years ago and a smile that had never been good at all.

Xanthe saw him from the wharf and knew him before he reached the gangplank — the walk gave it away, that unhurried, proprietary stroll of a man who owns a piece of you and has come to look at his property. Debt collectors had a walk. So did undertakers. The two professions overlapped more than anyone liked to admit.

"Captain Blackwood." The man came up the plank without being invited, which told Xanthe everything else he needed to know. "Name of Corver. I'm told you're a hard man to find on the water."

"I'm not hard to find. I'm just never where people want me."

Corver smiled. He had small teeth, like a fish. "I represent certain parties in Marchetti interests."

The name went through Xanthe like cold water through a boot. He kept his face still. On the wharf behind him, Sal had stopped coiling line and was watching, and Xanthe wished he wouldn't, and was glad he was.

"Marchetti interests," Xanthe said. "That's a broad phrase. The Marchettis own half the river."

"And you owe a portion of the other half." Corver produced a ledger — a slim, black, well-kept thing, and Xanthe hated it instantly, hated the neatness of it, the way it made his whole life into columns of figures in someone else's hand. "Four hundred and twelve crowns, principal and interest, against the *Vesper* and against you personally. The note's been sold twice. My employers hold it now. They're not the patient sort."

"The last holders were patient for six years."

"The last holders weren't the Marchettis." Corver let that sit, then tapped the ledger with one finger, gently, the way a man taps a coffin lid. "I'm told you're the best pilot on this water. I'm told this boat can go places other boats can't. That's worth something. That's worth a great deal, to the right buyer, at the right moment." He closed the ledger. "Someone will be along soon with a proposal, Captain. I'd think hard about saying yes. The alternative to yes is simpler, but it's not pleasant, and it happens at a wharf like this one, in front of crews like yours."

Xanthe looked at him for a long moment. The river moved behind them both, brown and patient, carrying everything toward the sea and giving nothing back.

"Tell your employers," Xanthe said, "the *Vesper* isn't for sale."

"Everything's for sale." Corver tucked the ledger away. "The only question is whether the owner does the selling." He touched his hat brim — a mockery of courtesy — and went down the gangplank, and his gray suit shrank along the wharf until the town swallowed it.

Sal came and stood beside him. Neither of them spoke for a while. Downriver, the clouds had begun to stack up over the far hills, tall and blue-black, the first suggestion of a monsoon that was still weeks away and might, the old-timers said, come early this year.

"Marchetti," Sal said finally.

"I heard."

"Odile Marchetti's people."

"I heard that too."

Sal looked at him, and Xanthe looked back at the compass, dead needle trembling in its cradle, pointing southwest, toward the gorge, toward the water that held everything he'd lost.

"Captain," Sal said quietly, "when a shark circles, it's not because it's curious."

"I know, Sal."

"And when the river drops this low—"

"I know." He turned from the wheel. "We load and we go. We've got salt to deliver."

But that night, at anchor above the mill towns, with the crew asleep and the current murmuring against the hull like a voice he almost recognized, Xanthe took the compass out of its cradle and held it in both hands, and the needle shivered once, hard, and settled — southwest, southwest, southwest — as if something under the water were calling it home.
