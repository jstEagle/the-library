## 2. An Offer from Odile

The woman came aboard at Kettle Landing on a morning so still the river looked poured rather than flowing, a sheet of pewter stretched from bank to bank, and Xanthe knew before she set foot on his deck that she had not come to buy salt.

He watched her from the wheelhouse as her launch cut across the flat water — a black-hulled runabout with brass fittings polished bright enough to shame the sun, flying no colors at all, which on this river was its own kind of flag. The Vesper's deckhands paused in their loading to stare. Even old Sal straightened up from the cargo manifest and spat thoughtfully over the rail.

Odile Marchetti stepped onto the gangplank like a woman boarding her own parlor. She wore traveling white — actual white, on a working river, which told him everything about how little she expected the river to touch her — with a scarf of deep green silk knotted at her throat and gloves that had never gripped anything rougher than a champagne stem. Her hair was dark as wet slate and pinned up under a hat that cast her face in a deliberate half-shadow, so that when she smiled at him across the deck, only the smile itself was fully lit.

"Captain Blackwood." Her voice carried without effort, low and warm and precise, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "The famous Vesper. She's smaller than her reputation."

"Most true things are," Xanthe said.

The smile widened by a degree. He gave it nothing. Ten years of running cargo through the low-season shallows had taught him that charm was a tool like any other, and he'd learned to watch hands more than faces. Hers stayed folded at her waist, gloved and idle, which meant the work was being done somewhere else — in the voice, in the eyes, in the arithmetic running behind them.

"You're wondering why I'm here."

"I'm wondering why you didn't send one of your people. You have people. I've seen them at the landings, counting other men's cargoes."

"And yet here I am." She turned, unhurried, and let her gaze travel the length of the boat — the patched gunwale, the salt crates going aboard, the rust blooming along the stern rail like lichen. It was an inspection dressed as admiration, and they both knew it. "She was Mara Vale's boat once, wasn't she? Before she was yours."

The name landed in his chest the way cold water lands — no splash, just the shock of it, everywhere at once. He kept his face still because he had practiced keeping his face still for ten years.

"She was," he said.

"They say you were there. The night she went down." Odile's eyes came back to his, and there was nothing soft in them now, whatever the mouth was doing. "They say a lot of things about that night. Nobody says the same thing twice."

"The river keeps its own accounts, Mrs. Marchetti. I don't audit them for strangers."

"Madam Marchetti," she corrected, pleasantly. "Widow. And I'm not a stranger, Captain. I'm a customer."

* * *

She laid the papers out on the chart table in the wheelhouse, and Xanthe understood immediately that she had chosen this room deliberately — that she wanted him cornered in the small space where he did his thinking, surrounded by his own instruments, with the compass sitting in its cradle three feet from her elbow like a witness neither of them acknowledged.

"Salvage," she said. "A wreck upriver, in the gorge country. Cargo of coin and bonded goods off a Marchetti packet that foundered two seasons back. My insurers want it raised before monsoon locks the channel. Triple your standard rate, plus salvage share — fifteen percent of recovered value."

Triple rate. The number hung in the wheelhouse air like smoke. Xanthe thought of the debt collector's visit two days past, the man's soft voice and softer ledger, the way he'd tapped the page with one finger and said *Mr. Corvus sends his regards, Captain, and wonders when* — and felt the number pull at him with hands.

"Your insurers," he said. "Marchetti boats insure through Marchetti interests. You're insuring yourself."

"I'm hiring you to raise my property. The paperwork is clean."

"Papers usually are. Rivers aren't." He leaned over the chart she'd unrolled and felt the floor tilt under him before his eyes even found the coordinates.

There it was. Inked in her neat, confident hand: a bearing and a reach of water forty miles above Kettle Landing, where the river narrowed between walls of grey stone and ran fast and green over drowned timber. The Gorge Reach. The Serpent's Teeth. Locals had a dozen names for it and used none of them aloud, the way you don't say a dead man's name in front of his widow.

It was the stretch of river where Mara died.

He had known — of course he had known, from the moment the black launch appeared, from the moment Sal said *shark* — but knowing a thing in general and seeing it written in ink on a chart are different animals. His eyes fixed on those coordinates and would not move, and for a moment he was thirty years old again and standing in the bow of a different boat in driving rain, shouting a name into wind that ate it, watching a lantern light go under between two walls of stone.

"We swore off that water," he heard himself say. His voice came out level, which surprised him. "Mara and me. Years ago. After—" He stopped.

"After she drowned there," Odile finished for him, gently, horribly. "Yes. I know. That's rather the point."

He looked up. She was watching him with an expression he couldn't parse — sympathy worn like the gloves, something else underneath, patient and cold and calculating, a fisherman's expression, waiting to see if the line had gone taut.

"There's no Marchetti packet wrecked in the Gorge Reach," he said slowly. "I'd have heard. Everyone would have heard. A loss like that doesn't stay quiet."

"Perhaps we kept it quiet."

"Perhaps." He straightened. "Or perhaps there's no wreck at all, and what's actually down there is something you want found by someone who can be paid to forget finding it."

For the first time, something flickered behind her eyes — quick, quickly mastered, but real. Respect, maybe. Or recalibration. The smile returned, smaller now, more honest in its dishonesty.

"You have a suspicious mind, Captain Blackwood."

"On this river it's called a mind."

* * *

She didn't leave. That was the thing about Odile Marchetti that the river towns got wrong, he decided later — they talked about her glamour, her gowns, her late husband's fortune, and missed entirely that her real talent was *staying*. She settled into the wheelhouse visitor's chair as though it had been built for her, declined coffee, accepted tea she didn't drink, and began, with terrible patience, to take his refusal apart rivet by rivet.

"Triple rate," she said. "Fifteen percent share. Paid on delivery, not on promise — cash, here, at the landing, before you even cast off. I know about the debt, Captain."

The wheelhouse went very quiet. Out on the deck, a deckhand dropped a crate strap and cursed, and the sound seemed to come from another world.

"Do you," Xanthe said.

"Corvus holds your paper. Four hundred and eighty marks against the Vesper's hull, accruing at interest since spring. He calls it in at midsummer or takes the boat. That's eleven weeks." She sipped her untouched tea. "You've been running salt and dry goods through shoal water at starvation rates hoping to outrun the interest. You can't. Men like Corvus build their ledgers so you can't. In eleven weeks I stand on this landing and watch that boat sold off her moorings, and you take work as a pilot on someone else's deck, if anyone will have you."

Every word was true. That was the horror of her — not the threat, which was ordinary enough, but the accuracy. She had done her reading the way he read water, sounding every depth, marking every snag. He stood at his own wheel with his own chart table under his hands and realized she knew his river better than he'd ever let anyone know it, including himself.

"So you see," Odile said softly, "this isn't charity and it isn't temptation. It's arithmetic. One job. Two days' run upriver, a week's salvage in flood water, triple rate, cash in hand. Corvus paid. The Vesper stays yours." She rose, smoothed her white skirt, and gathered her papers with unhurried grace. "I'll give you until tomorrow noon. Think of it however you need to, Captain. Greed if that's easier. Desperation if it's truer. It makes no difference to me what you call the rope, so long as you take hold of it."

At the top of the gangplank she paused, one gloved hand on the rail, and looked back at him with the half-shadow falling away from her face at last, and what he saw there was not cruelty exactly. It was certainty. The look of a woman who had already counted this hand of cards and found it good.

"Oh — one more thing." Her voice dropped, almost kind. "The compass. Mara Vale's compass. They say it stopped the night she did. What a strange grief, to carry a dead instrument for ten years." She tapped the wheelhouse doorframe once, lightly, with one finger. "Bring it along, Captain. Something tells me it remembers that water better than you do."

Then she was down the plank and into her launch, and the black runabout drew a long white seam across the pewter river and was gone, and the water closed over the wake as if she had never existed at all.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat in the wheelhouse until the lamps burned low and then relit them, and the chart lay open on the table all night with those coordinates staring up at him like an eye that wouldn't blink.

Triple rate. Cash before casting off. Eleven weeks against Corvus's ledger. He turned the numbers over and over and they always came out the same way, which was the trap of numbers — they were honest, and honesty could be used against you like anything else.

And underneath the arithmetic, quieter and older, the other current: *sworn-off water.* A promise made with rain running down both their faces, Mara laughing at him even then, saying *we'll never run the Teeth again, Xanthe, some rivers keep what they take and I've paid enough* — and he had agreed, and she had gone back anyway, alone, one season later, and the river had kept her.

Why had she gone back? Ten years and he had never once let himself finish that question, because every answer ended in the same place: that she hadn't trusted him with whatever pulled her there, or that she had known he'd try to stop her. Either way it was a wound he kept by leaving it alone.

Now Odile Marchetti had put her finger directly on it, smiling.

Near dawn, Sal climbed to the wheelhouse with two cups of coffee and set one down without asking. The old man looked at the chart, then at Xanthe, and said nothing for a long time.

"She came about the Gorge Reach," Sal said finally. Not a question.

"She came about everything."

"Mm." Sal drank his coffee and stared out at the river turning from pewter to rose. "You know what my father used to say. When the price is triple, it's not because the cargo's worth triple. It's because nobody else would touch it."

Xanthe looked down at the coordinates. Forty miles upriver. Green water over drowned timber. A lantern light going out between two walls of stone.

In its cradle by the window, the brass compass sat silent as it had sat for ten years, needle dead at rest — and yet he could have sworn, in the grey first light, that it lay angled slightly southwest, toward the gorge, the way a sleeping man turns toward a sound.

"Noon," he said. "She gave me until noon."

Sal nodded slowly and drained his cup. "Then I'd best tell the crew to load extra chain," he said. "One way or the other, Captain, I don't think we're staying at this anchorage past noon."

Downriver, faint on the still air, Xanthe could almost believe he heard the sound of a black launch's engine, idling, waiting — patient as interest accruing, patient as the river itself, patient as the dead.
