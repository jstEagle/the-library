## 3. The Stowaway

Wren had hidden in worse places than a bilge. She'd spent three nights in a tannery's lime pit shed, breathing fumes that peeled the skin off her knuckles, and once a whole week in the false bottom of a coffin-maker's cart, listening to mourners gossip two feet above her head. Compared to that, the dark under the Vesper's engine room plates was almost comfortable — warm, oil-smelling, thrumming with the heartbeat of a boat that actually worked.

She'd slipped aboard at Kettle Landing in the gray hour before dawn, when the deckhands were arguing about coffee and nobody watched the gangplank. The Vesper was a riverboat of the old school: paddle-wheel aft, shallow draft, patched and repatched until she was more patch than original timber. Wren knew her type from the shipyards. Boats like this were held together by stubbornness and one person who loved them enough to lie to everyone else about their condition.

She'd chosen well. She could hear it even now, curled beneath the plates with her knees drawn up and her satchel clutched to her chest — the engine turning over with a slight hitch on the third revolution, a rhythm just shy of true. Somewhere forward, a pump was laboring harder than it should. She'd catalogued both before the sun came up, because that was what she did. Engines talked to her. They always had.

What they didn't do was stay quiet when the boat started moving.

* * *

The trouble began an hour past the landing, when the Vesper left the wide brown channel and threaded into the low-season shallows, where the river dropped its voice to a mutter over sandbars. The hull flexed. Old boats complained when they flexed, and this one complained through every seam, a chorus of creaks and groans that Wren found oddly comforting right up until the pump forward changed its tune.

She heard it stop.

Not fail — *stop*, clean, the way a machine stops when something has seized or sheared rather than merely worn. Then a man's voice, muffled through the deck above, saying something sharp, and boots hammering aft toward the engine room, and Wren pressed herself flat against the warm iron of the hull and did the arithmetic she'd been dreading since Kettle Landing.

No pump meant no water out of the bilge. No water out of the bilge meant, on a boat this age running shallows this mean, that they'd be bailing by hand within the hour or sitting on a sandbar with the river coming up around them. And if they sat, someone would come looking for spare hands, and spare hands went looking in bilges.

The hatch at the back of the engine room opened. Light fell down the ladder in a dusty column. Two pairs of boots descended — one heavy, one lighter, dragging slightly on the left side.

"The impeller's gone," said the lighter voice, an older woman's, rough as rope. "Sheared clean off. I can jury-rig a scoop chain but it'll take half a day and we'll take on water the whole—"

"Then we take on water," said the other voice, and Wren's breath caught, because she knew that voice without ever having heard it. Everyone on the river knew Captain Xanthe Blackwood's voice, or thought they did — the stories gave it gravel and thunder. In truth it was quiet. Tired. A voice that had learned not to spend itself.

"Half a day anchored in the open channel," the woman said, "with the debt collectors sniffing every landing between here and Marrow Bend? Cap'n, be reasonable."

"I'm always reasonable, Sal. That's my tragedy." A pause, and then, closer, almost to himself: "We'll hand-bail. Rig buckets off the winch."

Wren looked at the satchel against her chest. Inside it: three wrenches, a file, a coil of brass wire, a borrowed bearing ring she still hadn't decided whether to keep, and a set of apprenticeship papers with another girl's name inked over hers — papers the Harrow & Sons shipyard had sold to a canal outfit upriver, along with her, effectively, though the law preferred the word *transferred*. She'd spent four years learning engines under men who called her *girl* like it was an insult and paid her master's wages to the yard instead of to her. When she'd run, she hadn't taken money. She'd taken tools. It seemed only fair.

And now there was a broken pump one deck up, and a captain who was about to let his boat drown rather than ask for help, and Wren lying in the dark doing the thing she swore she wouldn't do again, which was care.

She climbed out of the bilge.

* * *

Later, she would try to remember exactly how it had gone, and the memory would come back to her in pieces: the look on Sal's face, weathered and wide; the wrench in Wren's own hand, raised less like a weapon than like a credential; and Xanthe Blackwood standing very still in the engine room's amber light, studying her the way he might study a chart of unfamiliar water.

"You've been aboard since Kettle Landing," he said. Not a question.

"Since before your deckhands finished their coffee," Wren said. Her voice only shook a little. "Your forward bilge pump is dead. The impeller sheared — I could hear it go. You're going to hand-bail a leaking hull through the shallows like some kind of martyr, and I'm telling you now, I didn't stow away on a sinking boat. I have standards."

Sal made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

Xanthe didn't smile. He had a face built for not smiling, all angles and shadow, with eyes the color of river water in November. But something moved behind them — recalculation, maybe. "You're a mechanic."

"I'm an engineer. Journeyman papers, four years at Harrow & Sons, steam and screw and paddle both." She lifted her chin. "The yard sold my indenture. I took it back. Technically the papers are mine twice over now, which makes me more legitimate than most captains on this river."

"That so." He glanced at Sal. Sal shrugged with her whole body, which for her was practically a speech.

"She fixed nothing yet," Sal said. "Talk's cheap. Bilges are wet."

"Show me," Xanthe said.

Wren showed him. She went forward through the boat — noting, as she went, the cargo lashed amidships, the wheelhouse with its brass fittings gone green, the compass binnacle bolted by the helm with a cloth draped over it like a shroud, which struck her as strange but wasn't strange enough to stop for — and into the pump room, where she knelt in six inches of gray water and put her hands on the pump housing the way other people touched horses.

The disassembly took forty minutes. The impeller had sheared at the hub, a fatigue failure, the kind that announced itself for weeks if anyone bothered to listen. Nobody had bothered. She said so, once, mildly, and felt the temperature of the room drop, and decided she'd said quite enough.

There was no spare impeller aboard — of course there wasn't; spare parts cost money and money was clearly a rumor on this boat — but the shaft was sound and the housing was sound, and in her satchel she had brass wire, a file, and the bearing ring. She sat cross-legged in the bilge water and built a new impeller out of wire and stubbornness, winding and soldering and filing while Sal hovered and Xanthe stood in the doorway with his arms crossed, saying nothing, watching everything.

When she reassembled the pump and cranked it over, it caught on the second turn and threw a clean arc of bilge water across her face, and Sal laughed out loud this time, a bark like a seal, and clapped Wren on the shoulder hard enough to bruise.

"Well," Wren said, spitting, "she runs."

"So do you, apparently," Xanthe said.

* * *

They anchored at dusk in a cut behind a willow island, where the current slackened and the mosquitoes rose in holy clouds. Wren sat on the cargo deck with her knees drawn up, eating salt pork and hardtack — real food, food she hadn't paid for with a day of hauling castings — and tried not to look like she was waiting for the verdict.

It came walking. Xanthe crossed the deck and stood over her, backlit by the galley lamp, and for a long moment said nothing at all, which Wren was learning was his native language.

"Rules are no stowaways," he said finally. "I've got debts that follow me landing to landing, enemies I haven't met yet, and a boat that's one bad season from being firewood. Every mouth aboard is a liability I can't afford."

"All right," Wren said.

"I'm telling you why I shouldn't say yes."

"I know. I'm letting you finish, so I can enjoy the part where you don't."

Something happened to his face then. It wasn't a smile — she'd have bet her tools it physically couldn't be — but the corners of his mouth shifted, a tectonic event, barely measurable. He lowered himself onto the hatch coaming beside her, not close, but not far either, and looked out at the willows trailing their fingers in the black water.

"Journeyman papers," he said. "Four years at Harrow's. They sold you."

"Sold my indenture to the Grand Union Canal Company. Like selling a mule, except mules get fed." She turned the hardtack over in her hands. "I ran the night before the transfer. Took the tools I'd bought with my own wages, which the yard claimed were theirs too, because everything is theirs, that's how it works. You work four years and end up owning nothing but the calluses."

"And the name on those papers isn't yours."

"Wren is mine. I picked it myself. It's the only thing on me that isn't inherited, owed, or stamped by somebody else." She glanced at him sideways. "You'd know about names, I'd guess. Half the river says Blackwood and the other half says it like a question."

He didn't answer that. But he didn't leave, either, and after a while he said, quietly, "The pump. How long will your wire impeller hold?"

"A month. Maybe six weeks if I dress it every few days. After that, buy a real one in Marrow Bend and I'll fit it in an afternoon."

"Marrow Bend's upriver." Something in his voice had changed — flattened, guarded, like a door pulled shut. "Past the gorge."

"Is that bad?"

"It's water I don't run." He stood, brushing off his coat, and the lamplight caught the lines of his face and made him look older and wearier than any captain she'd seen. "Passage to wherever you decide to jump ship. In exchange, the pumps, the engine, anything mechanical — you keep it alive. Sal works you hard and pays you in meals. If you steal, I put you off at the next landing regardless of what's in the water. Those are the terms."

Wren scrambled up and stuck out her hand. He looked at it for a moment as if it were an unfamiliar species, then took it. His grip was dry and careful, like a man handling something he was afraid of breaking.

"One more term," Wren said. "That compass on your helm. It's got a cloth over it like a dead bird. Either it's broken or it's cursed, and I fix broken things for a living, so sooner or later I'm going to ask."

For just a moment — a heartbeat, no longer — Xanthe Blackwood looked at her as if she'd spoken a word in a language he'd sworn never to speak again. The river muttered against the hull. Somewhere in the willows, a night bird called once and went silent.

"Don't ask," he said, and walked away into the dark.

Wren stood alone on the cargo deck, her hand still warm from the handshake, and told herself she'd gotten what she came for: passage, meals, a boat that needed her. It was none of her business what lay under a cloth on a stranger's binnacle.

But she was already thinking about it. She would think about it for three more nights, until the night she stopped thinking and simply picked the cloth up — and found the needle trembling, trembling, pointing not north but straight at the drowned gorge ahead.
