## 4. Sworn-Off Water

The debt collector's card sat on the chart table for three days, and Xanthe hated it more each time he looked at it. It was a small thing, cream-colored, embossed with the crest of the Kettle Landing Mercantile Bank — a heron standing in reeds, which struck him as a poor choice of bird for an institution that fed on drowning men. He'd turned it over so many times the corners had gone soft.

Three hundred crowns. That was what the Vesper owed, and three hundred crowns was what the bank would take when they took her, which they would do at the end of the month if the low season ran true and the cargo rates stayed thin as river mist.

He stood at the wheelhouse window at dawn and watched the water go by, brown and slow and patient, and he did the arithmetic he'd been doing for three days even though it never changed.

* * *

Odile Marchetti came aboard on the fourth day, uninvited, wearing a coat the color of storm water and a smile that had been fitted to her face by someone very expensive. She found him in the wheelhouse because of course she did; she'd probably bribed the harbormaster's boy to know where he'd be, or simply guessed, the way predators guess.

"Captain Blackwood." She set a folded contract on the chart table, directly atop the debt collector's card, as if she knew it was there. Perhaps she did. "I'm told you haven't signed."

"I'm told you don't take hints."

"I take payment." She let her gloved fingers rest on the paper. "Triple rate, half up front. The wreck is marked on your chart already — I had my man copy the coordinates onto it while you slept."

Xanthe crossed to the table and flipped his chart open. There they were, in a fine clerk's hand: a set of numbers that landed like a fist below his ribs. The upper gorge. The Narrows above Widow's Bend. The stretch of river where, eleven years ago, Mara Voss's boat *Kestrel* had gone down in a spring flood with Mara and two hands aboard, and where Xanthe — twenty-three years old and certain of everything except the things that mattered — had stood on the bank and watched the water close over her running light.

"You know what's there," he said quietly.

"I know there's a wreck worth salvaging before monsoon locks the channel." Odile's voice was silk over something with edges. "I know you're the best pilot on this river, and I know your boat is mortgaged to her gunwales. Sentiment is a luxury, Captain. So is pride. Neither one pays three hundred crowns."

She left the contract on the table and went down the gangplank without looking back, and Xanthe stood alone in the wheelhouse with the chart spread out before him like an accusation.

* * *

He didn't sleep that night. He sat in the wheelhouse with a lamp turned low and the compass open on the table beside the contract, the old brass thing that hadn't pointed north since the night Mara drowned. Its needle hung slack, drifting, indifferent, and he'd kept it anyway through ten years and four boats' worth of hard living because throwing it away would have meant admitting she was gone, and he wasn't ready, and maybe he never would be.

Around midnight he picked it up. The case was warm from the lamp, from his hands, from years of being held. He remembered Mara teaching him to read water by feel — *the river tells you where it doesn't want you, boy; your job is listening* — and he remembered swearing, both of them laughing over cards in the *Kestrel*'s cabin, that neither of them would ever run the upper gorge again. Some water you give back to the dead.

And now Odile Marchetti wanted him to run it towing salvage gear, with a crew who trusted him, for money.

He signed the contract at first light, when the mist was still peeling off the water and no one could see his face clearly. His hand didn't shake until after.

* * *

The crew took it the way crews take bad news: loudly, and all at once.

"Upper gorge." Sal spat over the rail, which was how old Sal expressed most emotions, including affection. "You've lost your mind, boy. Or Marchetti's bought it, which amounts to the same thing."

"It's triple rate."

"It's triple rate to drag us into a graveyard." Sal had been running the river since before Xanthe was born, and he said the next part slowly, the way he said anything that mattered. "Some wrecks are graves, not prizes. You mark me. That water up there holds things that don't want turning over."

"The *Kestrel* went down eleven years ago, Sal. Whatever's there has settled."

Sal looked at him for a long moment, and his eyes — pale as river ice — were not angry but sad, which was worse. "Has it? Has it settled in *you*?"

Xanthe didn't answer. He turned to the rest of them instead — Ferro and the twins at the deck winches, young Pim at the stern line — and gave the orders in a voice he kept flat and professional, and if any of them noticed it cost him something to say the words *we make for the upper gorge*, none of them were kind enough to say so.

They cast off mid-morning. The Vesper turned her bow upriver, against the current, the way she always did, and Xanthe felt the whole boat lean into it like a creature that knew where it was being taken and objected.

* * *

Two days upriver, the country changed.

The banks narrowed. The hills came down closer to the water, dark with pine, and the current quickened from a walk to a march, dragging at the hull with long brown arms. The channel markers grew sparse and then wrong — buoys dragged off station by spring floods, some sunk entirely, their bells silent under the surface. Xanthe worked the wheel by instinct and memory, reading the water's skin the way Mara had taught him, feeling for the pull of hidden channels, the smooth treacherous glass where a snag waited just under the surface.

Wren appeared at the wheelhouse door on the second afternoon. The stowaway-turned-engineer had a smudge of grease on her jaw and an expression of carefully rationed curiosity, and she'd learned in her week aboard that Xanthe tolerated questions better in the late afternoon than at any other hour.

"Engine's running sweet," she offered. "For whatever that's worth up here."

"It's worth plenty."

She glanced at the water ahead, then at him. "The crew's saying we're headed for the gorge. The one where—" She stopped herself, which he appreciated. Word had gotten around; word always did. "Is it true?"

"It's true."

"And you swore you'd never—"

"Wren."

She took the rebuke without flinching, but she didn't leave either. She leaned against the doorframe and looked out at the darkening water with him, and after a while she said, quietly, "My mother used to say the river keeps what you give it. Maybe it also gives back what you're ready to take."

"That's a lot of wisdom for someone who hid in a coal bunker."

"A lot of wisdom for someone who signs contracts at dawn so nobody sees his face." She said it gently, and then she was gone, back down to the engine room, leaving Xanthe alone with the wheel and the truth of it.

* * *

They reached the mouth of the gorge at dusk on the third day, and Xanthe anchored rather than run it blind in failing light, though every hand aboard knew why he anchored, and no one said it.

He climbed to the wheelhouse roof while the crew made fast below, and he looked at it.

The flood had come early this year. The river that should have been running high was running *huge* — swollen brown and muscular between walls of black rock, torn white where it broke over submerged stone, roaring low and constant like something enormous breathing in its sleep. Eleven years ago it had looked like this. Eleven years ago Mara had stood at the *Kestrel*'s helm exactly where he was standing now, and the water had taken her boat sideways into the rocks below Widow's Bend, and the last thing Xanthe had seen of her was her running light, steady and green, going under.

It was still there. That was the thing that stopped his breath. Far down in the narrows, where the current curled back on itself in a slow eddy behind a shelf of rock, a shape showed dark beneath the floodwater — a hull rib, maybe, or a piece of superstructure, breaking the surface just enough to catch the last red light of the sun. Eleven years of floods had failed to bury her. The river had kept her, the way Wren's mother might have said. Kept her, and waited.

The compass was in his pocket. He didn't remember taking it out of the case, but his hand closed around it now, and through the brass he felt it — felt it, there was no other word — a faint tremor, like a sleeping animal stirring. The dead needle had twitched. Once. Twice. And then lay still again, pointing not north, not anywhere, but *down*, toward the dark water and the wreck and whatever Odile Marchetti really wanted raised from it.

Below him, Sal's voice carried up from the deck, quiet and grim, giving the night orders. Somewhere aft, Wren's engine idled steady and true, the one clean sound in all that roaring dark.

Xanthe stood on the wheelhouse roof until full dark, watching the gorge breathe, and the compass trembled against his heart like a second pulse.

Tomorrow they would go in.
