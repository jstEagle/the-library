## 5. What the Compass Wants

Wren had never been able to sleep on a boat at anchor. A running engine was a heartbeat she could trust — she could put her palm flat against a bulkhead and feel the whole vessel alive beneath her, every pulse of the shaft telling her what was well and what was failing. But an anchored boat was a body holding its breath. The Vesper lay against the current off a willow-choked bank, her lines creaking in a slow rhythm, and Wren lay in the narrow bunk Sal had grudgingly cleared for her and listened to the river work at the hull like a jeweler deciding where to cut.

Three days upriver from Kettle Landing. Two more, if the charts were honest, to the gorge where Captain Blackwood meant to fish a wreck out of flooded water. She had seen the way he touched the chart when he thought no one was watching — not the way a man reads a map, but the way a man touches a scar.

She gave up on sleep somewhere past midnight and dressed in the dark, pulling on the too-big canvas jacket she'd fished out of the Vesper's slop chest, and went looking for something to do with her hands. That was the trouble with her, she'd been told often enough: hands that couldn't be idle, a head that couldn't be quiet. It was why the shipyard had been glad to sell her papers and why she'd been glad to steal herself back.

The engine room was warm and smelled of oil and hot iron, and she sat with it a while the way other people sat with a cup of tea. But the engine was sound. She'd been over it twice since the pump repair, and Blackwood — Xanthe, she corrected herself; the crew said Xanthe when they weren't saying Captain — had watched her work with an expression she hadn't learned to read yet. Not suspicion. Something more like a man watching weather he didn't trust.

So she wandered. That was the other trouble with her.

The wheelhouse was dark, the door standing open an inch because Sal believed a wheelhouse should breathe. Wren meant only to look — to stand where the river ran silver under a half-eaten moon and feel, for a moment, like someone with a right to be there. But the binnacle caught her eye.

It sat on the chart table, set apart from the ship's compass in its gimbals. An old brass pocket compass, its case gone soft-edged with handling, its lid open. It lay on a folded square of cloth like something laid out for burial.

She should have left it. She knew that even as she picked it up, the way she'd known she shouldn't open the foreman's ledger in the shipyard office, and had done it anyway, and that was how all of this — the fleeing, the stowing away, the pump, the bunk — had begun. Curiosity was the leak in her that nothing had ever patched.

The needle was wrong.

Not broken — she'd have felt that in the balance of it, the little tremor of a bent pivot. Wrong the way a clock is wrong. It pointed confidently, doggedly, at a heading that had nothing to do with north. Wren turned slowly in place, watching the card swing to follow, and it kept its bearing through the full turn of her body and the turn of the wheelhouse windows, holding steady on a line that ran upriver, into the dark, toward the gorge.

Her mouth went dry. She set it down. Picked it up again. Turned again. The needle swung back, patient as a dog returning to a scent.

"It's not broken," she said aloud, to nobody, because saying it made it a problem instead of a haunting, and problems she could work with.

She carried it to the chart lamp and turned the wick low, and looked at it properly for the first time. The case was brass, hand-engraved along the rim in letters worn nearly to nothing. She tilted it against the light. *M.V.* — no. Not M.V. The letters were older than that, cut deep and then worn shallow by ten years of a thumb rubbing over them. *Mara Vesper.* And beneath, in a different, finer hand: *Vesper* — the boat's name, the woman's name, the same word doing double duty.

The bezel was the thing. She saw it when she turned the compass over: a hairline gap where the bearing ring should seat, a ring of threads stripped bright where something had been forced. Someone had pried at it — or the river had, or a fall, or ten years of salt and grit working into the joint. And beneath the gap, the needle's pivot sat a half-degree off true, cocked in its jeweled bearing like a man leaning against a door that wouldn't open.

Wren sat down on the wheelhouse bench with the compass in her lap and her kit open at her feet, and forgot entirely that it wasn't hers.

* * *

The bearing ring was the hard part. A compass like this wanted a ring of nonmagnetic brass turned to a tolerance finer than anything on the Vesper, and the Vesper's stores held, as far as Wren had found in three days of cheerful piracy, rope, tar, spare pump leathers, and a coffee tin of odds and ends that Sal called the future and Xanthe called the past.

She found it in the tin. A bearing ring off a dead anemometer, of all things — she'd seen it the first night and pocketed it on instinct, the way she pocketed every small bright piece of machinery that crossed her path, the way magpies and desperate apprentices do. It was a hair too large. She closed her eyes and thought about it, and then she took the finest file in her kit and worked by lamplight, fitting, testing, filing, fitting, while the river breathed against the hull and the willows dragged their fingers along the water.

It was the most careful work she'd done since she'd left the shipyard. More careful, if she was honest, than she'd ever done there, where the work had been someone else's profit. This she did for the compass itself, for the plain stubborn dignity of a good instrument that had been left broken too long.

When the ring seated — when it *clicked*, soft as a knuckle cracking — she held her breath.

The needle shivered.

Wren watched it the way she'd once watched a governor come up to speed: the wobble, the hunting, the slow settling as the pivot found its true seat and the card swung free for the first time in — she didn't know how long. It steadied. It pointed. Not north. It pointed upriver, dead on a bearing she read off the ring with fingers that had begun to shake, and when she laid it on the chart and drew the line with a pencil stub, the line ran up the channel, past the last marked shoal, into the flooded gorge, and stopped at a bend where the chart showed nothing at all but white space and a sound depth of forty feet.

Something down there. Something specific. The compass wasn't lost. It was *waiting*, and now that someone had oiled its hinges, it was pointing.

"Give that here."

She hadn't heard him come up the stairs. Xanthe Blackwood filled the wheelhouse doorway with the lamp behind him, and his voice was quiet in the way she'd learned was worse than shouting — the quiet of a man holding something down. His eyes went from her face to the compass to the open kit to the pencil line on the chart, and she watched him read the whole story of the last two hours in about four seconds, because he was not a fool, whatever else he was.

"I wasn't stealing it," Wren said, which was true, and also the first thing every thief says.

"I know you weren't." He came forward slowly. "You were fixing it. That's worse."

"Worse?"

"Stealing, I could throw you off at the next landing." He held out his hand, and she put the compass in it, and she watched his thumb go — without looking, without any decision in it at all — to the worn engraving, the way a thumb goes to a scar. "Fixing it means it works again. And I had it arranged in my head that it didn't."

The lamp hissed. Out on the water a night bird cried once and gave up.

"It stopped," Wren said. Not a question. She'd seen enough in his face.

"The night Mara drowned." He said it flatly, the way men say things they've said to themselves so many times the words have worn smooth as river stones. "Ten years ago. In the gorge we're steaming toward. She went down with her boat, and I went down after her, and I came up alone, and when I got back aboard —" He turned the compass over. The new bearing ring caught the lamplight, bright as a coin. "It pointed there. It's pointed there every hour of every day since, and I have kept it in a case and told myself it was broken, because the alternative was to believe it was telling me the truth."

Wren sat very still. She was, by training and temperament, a person who fixed things, and every instinct in her body was screaming that the thing broken in this wheelhouse was not made of brass. There was nothing in her kit for it.

"I'm sorry," she said. "For opening it. I should have asked."

"No," Xanthe said, and something in his voice shifted, some weight sliding an inch. "You shouldn't have. And I'm glad you did." He looked at the chart, at the pencil line running into the white space, and his jaw worked. "Forty feet of water at the bend below the gorge. That's not where her boat went down. I've dived that stretch until my lungs bled. There's nothing there but silt."

"Maybe it's not pointing at her boat."

He looked at her then — really looked, the first time, she thought, that he'd looked at her as anything but a stowaway who'd turned out useful — and whatever he found in her face made him nod slowly, once.

"No," he said. "Maybe it isn't."

He closed the compass lid with a gentleness that made her chest hurt, and set it in the binnacle, and stood looking at it for a long moment with his hand flat on top of it, the way you'd quiet a horse.

"Get some sleep, Wren."

She got as far as the stairs. His voice stopped her, low, almost lost under the river.

"It's the first time it's moved in ten years. The night before we make the gorge." A pause. "That's not chance, is it."

It wasn't a question either, and she didn't answer it, because she didn't have an answer — only the memory of the needle trembling toward the dark water like a finger pointing at a grave, or a door, and the growing certainty, cold in her belly despite the warm engine room below, that whatever the compass wanted, it had waited ten years to be able to ask.

By morning the wind had swung around and the clouds were stacking up black over the western hills, and Sal stood at the rail squinting at them and said, to nobody in particular, "That's monsoon weather, and it's a week early."
