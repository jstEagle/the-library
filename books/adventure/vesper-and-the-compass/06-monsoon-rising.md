## 6. Monsoon Rising

The storm came a day early, the way debts do.

Xanthe felt it before he saw it — a pressure behind his eyes, a taste of iron in the air, the river going strange and glassy in the hour before dawn. The Vesper lay at anchor above the gorge, her cables groaning against the current, and the water beneath them had turned the color of bruised plums. Somewhere upriver, whole hillsides of rain were waiting their turn to fall.

"Barometer's dropped like a stone," Wren said, emerging from the engine hatch with grease to her elbows and a look on her face that Xanthe had learned to read as *trouble I can fix, but not for free.* "If you're thinking of waiting it out, don't. That sky's got a grudge."

"We're not waiting." He folded the chart along its worn creases. "We work through it. Odile's divers are already suited."

"Of course they are." Wren wiped her hands on a rag that was filthier than her hands. "Nothing says *salvage* like drowning in a flood for strangers' money."

He almost smiled. Ten days she'd been aboard now, officially signed on as temporary engineer, unofficially the only person on the boat who talked back to him without flinching. Sal tolerated her. The deckhands liked her. Xanthe hadn't decided yet what she was, except necessary.

The first drops hit the deck as Odile's launch came alongside, her crew moving with the brisk efficiency of people who had done this many times and never once wondered whether they should have. Odile herself stood at the rail under an oilskin hood, dry as a secret, watching the Vesper's deck with those pale eyes that never seemed to blink so much as *reappraise*.

"The storm's moved up its schedule," she called across the gap. "So have we. My divers go down at first light. Your crane holds position. Triple rate stands — double if we raise cargo before noon."

"And if the river decides otherwise?" Sal asked from beside the wheelhouse, arms crossed over his barrel chest.

Odile smiled the way a knife smiles. "Then the river keeps it, and nobody's poorer but me."

Xanthe held her gaze a moment longer than was comfortable, then nodded once. The lines went over. The cranes swung out. And the Vesper began her long, cold day of holding still above a grave while strangers picked at its bones.

* * *

By midmorning the rain had stopped being weather and become a condition of existence.

It fell in gray sheets, hammering the river flat, turning the gorge walls into waterfalls that roared down the rock like the whole cliffside trying to follow Mara into the deep. Visibility shrank to fifty yards. The divers worked in shifts, tethered to Odile's barge by lifelines that vanished into churning brown nothing, and every time one of them surfaced, gasping and half-blind, he brought word up like a diver bringing up pearls:

*Wreck's intact. Decks collapsed. Cargo hold's full.*

*Crates. Dozens of them. Sealed oak.*

*Coin. It's coin, captain — crates of it, mint-stamped.*

Each report landed on the Vesper's deck with a weight Xanthe could feel in his chest. Coin meant the wreck was what Odile claimed. Coin meant the contract would pay. Coin meant he could look the debt collector from Kettle Landing in the eye and finally be done with that particular leash.

So why did his hands want to shake?

Because he knew this stretch of water. Because ten years ago he'd stood at a wheel not forty yards from where the divers were now descending, and watched a lantern go under, and heard a compass tick its last. Because some wrecks were graves, Sal had said, and Sal had been right, and here they all were anyway, dredging one in a monsoon.

"Captain." Wren appeared at his elbow, shouting over the rain. "Pump two's cycling hot. If the bilge keeps taking water at this rate I need to shut down nonessentials."

"Do it."

"And—" She hesitated, which she almost never did. "That box Odile's crew brought up an hour ago. The iron one. They stowed it on their barge under tarp, armed guard, no talk. Doesn't that strike you odd? Everything else goes in open crates. That one gets buried."

Xanthe looked across the rain-lashed gap at Odile's barge. Under a soaked canvas, four of her men stood in a loose ring around something crate-sized and dark. One of them caught him looking and shifted his weight, and Xanthe saw the outline of a holstered sidearm under the man's slicker.

"Everything about this job strikes me odd," he said. "It has since Kettle Landing."

"You took it anyway."

"I took it anyway." He said it flat, and Wren had the grace not to press. But her eyes stayed on him a beat longer, and in them he saw the question he'd been refusing to ask himself: *what's in that box that needs guards in a flood?*

The answer came at dusk, and it came wearing his mentor's initials.

* * *

The last dive of the day nearly didn't happen. The river had risen three feet since noon, current tearing through the gorge hard enough to make the anchored barges yaw and strain, and Odile's dive master wanted to call it. Odile overruled him. One more descent, she said. One more.

The diver who came up on the final line was different from the others — younger, leaner, hauled aboard gasping and white-faced, clutching something to his chest that the other men closed around like a wound. Xanthe watched through the rain as they carried it to the tarped corner of the barge, and this time the tarp came off for just a moment, just long enough, in a flash of lightning, for him to see.

An iron box. Small. Sealed. Green with a decade of river rot except where the diver's hands had scrubbed it clean.

And stamped into the lid, deep enough to survive ten years underwater, three letters:

**M.V.B.**

Mara Vesper Blackwood.

His own name, given backward. Her name, given forward. The initials she'd punched into everything she owned — her tools, her ledgers, the compass case she'd pressed into his hands the night before she died, saying *keep it close, boy, even when it stops talking.*

The world went narrow and loud. The rain, the thunder, the groaning cables — all of it receded, and there was only the box, and the initials, and the understanding arriving in his gut like ballast dropping: whatever Odile Marchetti had hired him to find, she had known it was here. Had always known. This wasn't salvage.

It was a search. And he was the bloodhound.

"Xanthe." Sal's hand on his shoulder, heavy and warm. "You've gone gray as the water. Talk to me."

"That box." His voice came out low and steady, which surprised him. "That's hers, Sal. That's Mara's. Which means whatever else is down there, whatever Odile's paying us triple to haul up — that box is why we're here. Everything else is noise."

Sal followed his gaze across the water. The guards had re-tarped the box. Lightning lit the barge like a stage.

"Then we've got a problem," Sal rumbled, "because I've been counting their guns since morning. Six this morning. Nine now. And they ain't pointed at the river."

* * *

Night came down black and total, the storm still working itself into a fury overhead. The two vessels rode at anchor thirty yards apart, linked by a single transfer line, and between the crash of thunder Xanthe could hear Odile's crew moving on their decks — too much movement, too purposeful, boots and tackle and the metallic whisper of things being readied that had nothing to do with salvage.

He stood at the wheelhouse window with the dead compass open in his palm. It hadn't moved in ten years. Tonight, in the lightning flashes, he could have sworn the needle trembled — a shiver, a stutter, like a sleeper dreaming of waking.

"Toward the box," Wren said quietly. She'd come up beside him without him hearing, which should have worried him more than it did. "I checked earlier. When they brought it up. The needle pulled toward the barge. Just slightly. Like it remembered something."

"It's broken."

"Sure." She looked at him with those clear, unafraid eyes. "Broken things don't point at anything, Captain. Broken things just lie there. That needle's deciding."

He closed the case. His hands weren't shaking anymore; they'd gone past shaking into something harder and colder. "Get below. Check the bilge, check the engine mounts, check everything. If tonight goes wrong, I want this boat ready to run on a moment's notice."

"Run where?"

"Away," he said. "Or through. Whichever the river allows."

She held his eyes a moment, then nodded and went, and Xanthe stood alone in the wheelhouse watching the dark shape of Odile's barge swing slowly on its anchor, closer now than it had been at dusk. Thirty yards. Twenty-five. Either her cable was slipping in the flood, or someone over there was paying line out deliberately, quietly, inch by inch, the way you'd approach a sleeping animal you intended to put down.

A figure moved on Odile's afterdeck. Slim, hooded, unmistakable even at that distance. Odile raised one hand — not a wave, he realized. A signal. And along her rail, shadows detached themselves from shadows and began, very quietly, to gather at the transfer line.

Xanthe set the compass down on the chart table, open, needle trembling toward the enemy, and reached for the engine telegraph.

Below his feet, deep in the flooded dark, something unseen shifted against the Vesper's hull — a long, slow, grinding kiss of wood on wood, like the river clearing its throat.

Then the hull planking somewhere aft let out a sound he knew too well, a wet, splintering sigh, and Wren's voice came up the speaking tube, tight and fast:

"Captain — we're taking water. A lot of it. And Odile's men just cut our transfer line."
