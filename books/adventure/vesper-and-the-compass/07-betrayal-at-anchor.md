## 7. Betrayal at Anchor

Wren woke to the sound of the river changing its mind.

She had learned, in three years of shipyards and one week aboard the Vesper, that water spoke in dialects. The tick of rain on a tin roof. The slap of current against a hull at rest. The low gargle of a pump doing honest work. But this was none of those. This was a long, grinding exhale beneath the deck plates, like something enormous rolling over in its sleep — and it was getting louder.

She sat up in her bunk in the engineer's cubby, a space barely wider than her shoulders that she'd claimed by right of fixing its leaking pipe, and pressed her palm flat against the bulkhead. The vibration was wrong. Deep-water wrong. They were supposed to be anchored hard above the gorge, tied to two dead cedars and a boulder the size of a chapel, riding out the flood's first night like every sensible boat on this stretch of river.

The Vesper was moving.

She was up the ladder before she'd finished lacing her boots, barefoot on one foot because there hadn't been time for both, and out into the rain. It came down in sheets now, warm and relentless, hammering the deck into a drum. Lightning stitched the far bank white, and in that stutter of light Wren saw what her bones already knew: the cedars were behind them. The anchor lines hung slack and severed, ends whipping in the current like cut hair.

Someone had cut them loose.

Her stomach dropped through the hull and into the flood. She thought of Odile Marchetti's men, six of them camped on their own salvage barge a hundred yards upstream, their lanterns burning cozy and yellow all evening while they'd shared salt pork and lies. She thought of the iron box stamped with M.B., sitting in the Vesper's hold under a tarp, and of the crates of coin Odile wanted, and of how much simpler everything became if one leaky riverboat simply drowned in the dark with her crew asleep inside her.

"No," Wren said aloud, to no one, to the rain. "No, you don't."

She went down the companionway at a run.

* * *

Xanthe Blackwood slept like a man standing guard over his own grave — lightly, and facing the door. He was upright before Wren got two words out, and she would remember later how he didn't waste a second on questions. She said *cut loose, we're moving, we're in the flood,* and he was already pulling on his coat, already saying, "Sal. Wake Sal."

They found Sal halfway to the wheelhouse.

The old man had gotten there first — of course he had; Sal heard a river change its mind in his sleep the way a mother hears a child cry — and he'd met two of Odile's men coming down the deck the other way. What happened next took Wren a heartbeat too long to understand: a shape swinging, a lantern smashing, and then Sal going down against the rail with a gaff hook opened across his forearm, blood black as ink in the lightning-light, while Xanthe put one of the attackers over the side and sent the other running for the bow, leaping into the flood rather than face him.

"Wheelhouse," Xanthe said, hauling Sal up under the arms. "Wren — engine room. Keep us alive."

"I don't know your engine—"

"You do. You fixed her pump in an hour. Go."

She went.

* * *

The engine room in flood-rain was a cave with a heartbeat. Wren threw herself at the throttle assembly, hands finding the familiar geometry of it — Xanthe had made her walk the whole plant twice that first day, muttering, *if you're going to stow away on my boat you'll know where everything bleeds from* — and got steam up while the deck pitched under her like a living thing. Through the voice-tube, Sal's voice crackled, thin and wet: "No charts past the gorge, girl. Nobody runs this water at night. Nobody runs it at all."

"Then what do we do?" she shouted back.

There was a pause, filled with rain and the groan of straining timber. Then Xanthe's voice, calm in a way that frightened her more than panic would have: "We do what Mara did. We listen to the compass."

* * *

Later — much later, when her hands finally stopped shaking — Wren would try to reconstruct those hours and find they wouldn't hold still in memory. Fragments, mostly:

Xanthe at the wheel with the brass compass open on the binnacle before him, its needle trembling not north but *downriver*, steady as a plumb line, pointing at something under the flood no chart had ever drawn.

Him calling depths to her through the tube — *starboard, give me starboard, there's a channel hugging the bluff* — and her feeding the engine just enough, never enough, the old Vesper groaning up to speeds she'd been built for and never asked to give.

The gorge walls rising black on either side, so close she could hear the flood tearing at the rock, spray coming over the rails in warm sheets, and the compass needle never wavering, never once wavering, as though whatever hand had set it was still holding it.

A snag — a whole drowned tree, root-crown like a fist — appearing out of the dark dead ahead, and Xanthe putting the wheel over so hard the boat stood on her beam-ends, and Wren lying flat on the deck plates listening to her own heart and the engine's arguing about which would quit first.

And through all of it, Sal, propped in the wheelhouse corner with his arm bound in a shirt soaked through red, refusing to lie down, calling soundings from memory in a voice gone thin: "Deep water port side — trust it — I fished this bend when your captain was in swaddling—"

"We're past the bend, Sal."

"Then trust it anyway."

* * *

Dawn found them below the gorge, in water gone suddenly gentle, drifting in a wide brown reach where the flood spread itself thin among drowned willows. The rain had softened to a gray whisper. Mist stood on the river in columns.

Wren climbed up from the engine room smelling of oil and sweat and found Xanthe at the rail, the compass open in his cupped hands. Sal sat wrapped in blankets on the wheelhouse bench, gray-faced, alive, his bandaged arm cradled like something precious. The Vesper leaked, but only the way an old woman complains — steadily, manageably. Wren had already made a list.

"They'll think we sank," she said.

Xanthe didn't look up from the compass. "Odile will know better by noon. She'll count our lanterns missing off that wreck and she'll know exactly what kind of trouble she failed to drown." He closed the lid with a soft click. "She has the coin. We have the box."

"The box she wanted at the bottom of the river with us attached to it."

"Yes." He turned then, and in the gray light Wren saw something in his face she hadn't seen in the week she'd known him — not the weary competence, not the grief worn smooth as river stone. Something harder and brighter underneath, like ore showing through rock. "Mara kept a ledger. Whatever's in that box, Odile Marchetti has spent her whole life making sure it stayed sunk. And last night she spent two good men and my boat's moorings trying to keep it that way." His jaw tightened. "Which means it's worth more than gold. Which means we're taking it downriver."

"To the council?"

"To everyone." He looked past her, upriver, toward the gorge and the wreck and the woman who'd tried to bury them both. "Every town on this water. Every captain she's ever squeezed. Everyone who ever wondered why the Marchetti name sits on half the licenses between here and the delta."

Wren leaned on the rail beside him. Below, the river carried them along, patient and enormous, toward places none of them could see yet. Her hands were still trembling. She noticed she didn't much care.

"You know," she said, "when I stowed away, I figured I'd ride as far as Kettle Landing and jump ship."

"And now?"

"Now I want to see her face," Wren said, "when you show her what the river kept."

Xanthe almost smiled. Behind them, Sal coughed and said, "First thing's first. Somebody get me dry bandages and somebody get this girl a real bunk — she's crew now, whether she signed or not."

"I'll sign," Wren said quickly.

"You'll sign after breakfast," Sal said. "Nobody signs articles bleeding and starving. That's how bad contracts happen."

Ahead, the mist was lifting off the water, and far down the reach — a smudge at first, then unmistakable — a boat lay athwart the channel, lanterns still lit against the dawn, blocking the only deep passage south.

Odile Marchetti had not waited for them to come to her.
