## 8. Mara's Ledger

The rain had thinned to a gray whisper by the time they got the iron box aboard, but the Vesper's deck still ran with water, and Xanthe's hands still shook — not from cold, though the monsoon had wrung every degree of warmth out of the gorge. He sat in the wheelhouse with the box between his boots and did not touch it for a long while.

Sal lay below decks with a splinted arm and a poultice Wren had improvised from engine rags and river mud, which was either medicine or infection waiting to happen; she'd promised to argue about it later. The engine ticked beneath them, wounded but willing, and outside the fogged glass the gorge walls rose dripping into cloud, green stone going black where the water stained it. Somewhere upriver, Odile Marchetti's boat was hunting for them or pretending they were already dead.

Xanthe looked at the box.

It was smaller than he'd imagined all these years, though he'd never once admitted to imagining it. Iron gone the color of old lead, furred with rust along the seams, stamped on the lid with two letters worked deep into the metal before it was cast: **M.B.**

Mara Blackwood. He carried her name like a coat that didn't fit, had carried it since he was nineteen years old and too cowardly to keep wearing his own. Ten years he'd told himself the compass was broken, that the river had simply taken her the way rivers take people, that there was nothing under the water at Gallow's Reach but silt and sorrow. And here was the proof that he'd been lying to himself in the most convenient possible way: the thing she'd died for had been sitting in the dark thirty feet down, patient as bone, waiting for him to stop being afraid of it.

He pried the latches with a flathead screwdriver Wren handed him without being asked. She'd appeared in the wheelhouse doorway an hour ago and hadn't left, just settled onto the chart bench with her knees drawn up, watching him with those quick dark eyes that missed nothing. She'd earned the right to be here. She'd kept the engine alive through rapids no chart acknowledged, run barefoot through floodwater to cut loose a fouled line while bullets — no, not bullets, he corrected himself, not yet — while grappling hooks sang off the rail. She'd stayed when staying meant drowning alongside him.

The latches gave. The hinge screamed. The smell that came out was river-bottom and iron and something older, something like a sealed room.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, lay books.

Three of them. Ledgers, bound in green leather that had held its color against a decade of black water better than anything else in the box deserved to. Beneath them, a packet of letters tied with string, and a folded document sealed with wax that had cracked but held its impression: a salvage charter, countersigned twice.

Xanthe unwrapped the top ledger with hands that had stopped shaking now, because some part of him had finally understood what this was and had gone very quiet, the way the river goes quiet before it drops over a ledge.

Mara's hand. He knew it instantly, the strong slanted script, the way she crossed her sevens, the little hook she put on the tail of every *y* because she'd learned penmanship from a river pilot who'd learned it from a forger, and she'd always laughed when she said that part. *Numbers don't lie, boy, but the people who write them down will, so you learn to read both.*

The ledger opened on a date twenty-three years old. Salvage rights, filed and paid, for the stretch of river from Kettle Landing clear up past Gallow's Reach. Her name. Beside it, in the same confident hand, entries that made his blood slow and heavy in his veins: cargo recovered from the *Meridian*, a Marchetti packet boat that had foundered with a fortune in specie and silk aboard. Recovered legally, under charter, with the Marchetti family's own signature agreeing to a one-fifth recovery fee.

And then, page after page, the record of what actually happened. The one-fifth paid in full, witnessed, notarized. The Marchettis' refusal to honor it. The second charter Mara had been forced to sign — new terms, "revised," the word underlined twice in ink pressed hard enough to tear the paper. The third. Each revision taking more, until the entries stopped being about money and started being about silence. A payment recorded simply as *for the partner's discretion*. Another: *to keep the widow quiet re: Meridian manifest.* And then, dated eleven days before the entry stopped forever, a final line in handwriting that had changed — shakier, faster, written by someone who knew she might not get another chance:

*If I go under at the Reach, it is no accident. E.M. has said as much to my face. Everything is in the green book and the letters. The compass knows where I've hidden the rest. Tell my boy the truth costs more than gold but it's the only coin worth carrying.*

*Tell my boy.*

Xanthe set the ledger down on the chart table with exaggerated care, because if he didn't control his hands they would do something he couldn't take back, and there was no one left from that night to take it back against except a woman three miles upriver who had hired him, smiling, to carry her family's sins to the bottom of the same water.

"Xanthe." Wren's voice, low. "You've gone white as chart paper."

"She didn't drown." He heard his own voice come out flat and strange, like someone else's through a bulkhead. "She was run down. Or holed. Or boarded and cut loose — " His jaw tightened. "Same trick they used on us last night. It's a family recipe."

Wren came off the bench and stood beside him, not touching, just near, the way she'd learned to be near him — close enough to steady, far enough to let him pretend he didn't need it. "E.M.," she said. "That's — "

"Elias Marchetti. Odile's father." He turned pages with a gentleness that surprised him. Here was the murdered partner's name — Tomás Reyes, drowned two years before Mara, officially an accident, unofficially the first draft of the same story. Here were the letters, dozens of them, Mara's correspondence with a magistrate in Corvane who had apparently gotten cold feet, each reply more careful than the last, the final one returning her documents unopened with a note: *I have a family. You should consider yours.* She'd had a family. She'd had a nineteen-year-old boy who worshipped her and a boat named after evening prayer, and the Marchettis had taken both, or tried to.

The compass sat in its case on the shelf above the wheel, brass worn bright at the edges from ten years of Xanthe's thumb. It wasn't pointing north. It hadn't pointed north since the night she died. All this time he'd thought it broken, thought himself cursed, thought the river had swallowed the only true thing he owned.

It had been pointing here. For ten years. Through every run, every season, every port, the needle trembling off true by a few degrees, faithful as grief, saying *downstream, downstream, downstream* — and last night, running rapids blind in a flood, it had led them through channels no chart showed because Mara had charted them herself and taught the water to remember her. The compass hadn't been broken. It had been waiting for him to be ready. Or perhaps it had simply been leading him home the long way, the way a mother walks slowly so a child can keep up.

"Odile knew," he said. The rage was there — he could feel it banked in his chest like a fire shut in a stove, hot enough to warp iron — but it wasn't wild. That was the strange thing. He'd spent ten years imagining this moment, and in every version he'd imagined breaking things. But standing here with her handwriting warm under his eyes, Mara sounded less like a ghost demanding blood and more like a pilot reading water: *don't fight the current, boy. Read it. Use it.* "She knew the box might exist. She couldn't find it herself — her divers have worked the Reach for years, that's why the wreck was 'lost,' that's why the charts show open water. But she needed to know if the evidence survived. So she hires the one captain crazy enough to work the Reach, the son of the woman who hid it, and she lets me do the diving." He laughed once, without any humor in it at all. "I confirmed it for her. I pulled it up and handed it to the flood. If we'd sunk last night, the ledger goes down with us, and the Marchetti fortune stays clean forever, and nobody ever has to know their grandfather signed away a fifth of a salvage and then killed the woman who asked for it."

"And instead," Wren said, and there was a fierce little smile starting at the corner of her mouth, "you sank her rudder and stole her thunder."

"I stole her grandmother's crimes, is what I did." He closed the ledger. The green leather was cool under his palm. "This goes to the council at Corvane. Every town on the river, every license desk, every pilot who ever lost a charter to a Marchetti 'revision.' Let them read it aloud if they want. Let them read it in the market square."

Wren studied him. "And Odile?"

Downriver, through the rain-haze, the gorge mouth waited, and somewhere beyond it Odile Marchetti would be regrouping, counting her losses, deciding whether the Vesper and everyone aboard were witnesses or problems. Xanthe looked at the compass, needle steady now, pointing not at treasure and not at revenge but straight down the channel toward home.

"Odile gets what my mother would've given her," he said. "The truth, in front of everyone, and the chance to live small with it." He picked up the helm. "Now let's go introduce her to the river towns."

Behind them, the engine caught on Wren's first try, and the Vesper turned her bow downriver into the rain.
