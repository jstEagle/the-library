## 9. The Gorge in Flood

The rain had stopped being weather and become a fact of the world, the way gravity was a fact, the way grief was. Xanthe stood at the wheel with his coat plastered to his shoulders and watched Odile Marchetti's boat come out of the gray wall ahead like something surfacing from a bad dream.

She'd chosen her ground well. The Marchetti steamer — *Corvina*, all black hull and brass arrogance — sat square across the only navigable exit below the gorge, engines idling, floodlights cutting cones through the downpour. Behind her, the river narrowed to a throat of white water between rock walls that no chart in existence marked as anything but cliff.

"Sal," Xanthe said quietly, "how does she look?"

Sal stood at his shoulder, arm bound and slung from the night's fighting, face gray under the rain hood. "Like a door slammed shut." He spat over the rail. "She's not here to talk, boy."

No. She wasn't. Odile had cut them loose to drown, and when that hadn't worked — when the compass had walked them through channels no map admitted to, when Wren had kept a wounded engine breathing through sheer stubbornness and prayer — she'd simply moved her door downstream. Patient as a debt collector.

Xanthe felt the old pull in his chest, the one that said *ram her, take her, make her pay for Sal's arm, for the crates she meant to steal, for Mara*. Ten years of that pull, coiled like a spring in him. He let it coil. He didn't let it fire.

"Wren," he called down the engine hatch.

Her head appeared, streaked with grease and rain, eyes bright as coins. "She'll hold," Wren said before he could ask. "Barely. Don't ask for more than barely."

"I'm going to ask for everything."

He laid it out fast, one hand on the wheel, the other tracing lines on the wet chart board though the lines existed nowhere but in his head — in the compass's trembling insistence, in the memory of what it had shown them these past two nights. The *Corvina* blocked the main channel because the main channel was the only channel anyone believed in. But rivers lie to people who only read them. The flood had opened something else: a side race behind the eastern bluff, a slot of water maybe forty feet wide where the gorge spilled over an old shelf and dropped into the lower river. In ordinary seasons it was gravel and deadfall. In this flood, it was a chute — violent, uncharted, and utterly invisible from where Odile sat.

"She can't see us run it," Xanthe said. "The spray off the shelf will blind her lights. We drop behind her in her own shadow."

"And if we're wrong about the depth?" Sal asked.

"Then we won't be wrong for long."

* * *

They came at the chute bow-first into the rain, and the compass lay open on the binnacle before him, its needle no longer wandering but locked, steady as a heartbeat, pointing at the gap in the bluff that his eyes could barely find. Ten years silent. Now it pulled like a living thing. *This way,* it said. *I always meant this way.*

"Mara," he murmured, "if you're steering, don't show off."

The Vesper swung toward the rocks and every instinct Xanthe owned screamed against it. The bluff rose up wet and black, and just when the bow should have shattered, the channel opened — a seam in the stone he'd have sworn wasn't there, flooded now to a depth the river alone had measured. White water took them.

It was not running rapids so much as being swallowed by them. The Vesper pitched, rolled, righted; cargo shifted below with a sound like furniture in a falling house. Water came over the rails in solid green sheets. Xanthe steered by the needle and by feel, hands reading the wheel the way a blind man reads a face, feeling the current shove the stern left and correcting before his mind caught up. Somewhere below, Wren fought the engine — he could hear her voice rising through the deck plates, wordless, defiant, the throttle answering her hand like a trained animal.

The shelf came. He felt it more than saw it, a long grinding shudder through the keel as they crossed the drowned ledge, close enough to scrape paint, close enough to end them — and then the bow dropped, and the world fell away, and the Vesper rode the falling water down into the lower river like a leaf going over a millwheel.

They surfaced in Odile's shadow.

The *Corvina*'s floodlights blazed fifty yards ahead, pointed upstream at nothing. Her crew stood along the rail watching the empty gorge, waiting for a boat that would never come back. Rain sheeted between the two vessels. Nobody had seen them yet.

"Sal," Xanthe breathed. "The rudder."

Sal was already moving, calling two of the deckhands, pointing to the long boat hook and the boarding axes wrapped in canvas. "Not the hull," Xanthe said, catching his eye. "Nobody drowns tonight. Not even hers. Rudder and rudder alone. I want her helpless, not bleeding."

Sal looked at him a long moment — looked at him the way he had ten years ago, when Xanthe had been a boy learning knots on this same deck. Then he nodded once. "Aye. Mercy it is."

The Vesper slid forward on the flood, quiet as theft, using the *Corvina*'s own wake-noise to cover their engines. Closer. Closer. Xanthe could see the rivets in her stern, see the great brass rudder swinging lazy at its stock, see the man at her taffrail lighting a cigarette under an awning, cupping the match against wind that didn't exist yet.

Odile stepped out onto her stern gallery.

She was in a dry coat, dry hair, immaculate as a verdict, and she was looking upstream with the patience of someone who had already counted money that wasn't hers. Xanthe watched her for three full seconds — the woman whose family had built its fortune on Mara's stolen work, on a murdered partner, on a ledger sunk in iron — and he found, to his surprise, that the rage had changed shape. It wasn't a knife anymore. It was a rudder. It had somewhere to go.

"Now," he said.

The Vesper surged. They came alongside the *Corvina*'s quarter so fast the impact rang like a bell, fenders screaming, and Sal's men were over the rail before Odile's crew understood there was anything to understand. The cigarette man got a boat hook across the chest and sat down hard. Two others reached for weapons and found deckhands' fists instead. It was ugly and brief and nobody died, which was the whole design of it, and then Sal's axe bit twice into the rudder stock's collar and the great blade hung loose, spinning free, useless.

The *Corvina* began, gently, majestically, to swing sideways in the flood.

Odile turned. Found him. For a moment neither captain spoke, and the rain filled the silence between the ships like a held breath.

"You were supposed to sink," she said. Not angry. Almost curious.

"The river had other plans." Xanthe kept his voice level. "Or maybe just an older debt than yours."

"My family built this river's trade—"

"Your family stole it." He let the words out slowly, and they cost him less than he expected. "Mara kept the proof. You sent me to confirm it existed and die with it. That's done now, Odile. All of it's done except you deciding how the rest of your night goes."

Her crew was surrendering in ones and twos — hands up, eyes flicking between captains, nobody eager to drown for Marchetti pride in a flood. Odile looked at them, and at her hanging rudder, and at the black water carrying both boats helplessly downriver together, and something in her face closed like a ledger balancing to zero.

"You'll ruin us," she said.

"No," Xanthe said. "You did that yourself. I'm just delivering the arithmetic."

* * *

They drifted down together through the last miles of the gorge, the crippled *Corvina* taken in tow, her crew aboard the Vesper drinking hot coffee out of the galley's good mugs because Wren insisted drowning-adjacent people deserved decent coffee. Sal sat wrapped in blankets by the stove, laughing at something, arm and all. The rain thinned. Ahead, far downriver, the lamps of Kettle Landing glowed through the mist like embers in ash.

Wren climbed up beside him at the wheel, grease-streaked, soaked, grinning like the flood itself.

"She held," Wren said. "Told you. Barely."

"Barely's my favorite speed." Xanthe glanced down at the binnacle. The compass needle had gone quiet again — not dead, not silent the way it had been for ten years, but resting, the way a dog rests after a long run home. He covered it with the case lid, gentle as closing a book.

"What happens at Kettle Landing?" Wren asked.

Xanthe looked at the iron box lashed under the pilothouse bench, Mara's initials dark under the lamplight, and thought of the council hall, the charts, the name that would be carved back into the river's official memory after a decade of erasure.

"We land," he said, "and we tell the truth for once. All of it. Starting with hers."

The Vesper ran on toward the lights, towing her enemy home.
