## 10. Inheritance

The council hall at Graywater smelled of lamp oil, wet wool, and forty years of arguments that had never quite been settled. Wren stood at the back of it with her arms crossed over her patched shirt, watching Xanthe carry the iron box down the center aisle as though it were made of glass instead of rust.

He set it on the long table before the council — seven elders behind a scarred plank, the river towns' whole government in one room with a leaking roof. The box's lid had been pried open the night before, by lamplight, by all four of them together: Xanthe, Sal with his arm in a sling, Wren with a pry bar she'd sharpened herself, and old Sal's dog Biscuit supervising from the corner. Inside had been no gold. Only paper — pages and pages of Mara Vesper's small, level handwriting, ink gone brown but every figure legible, every date exact.

The eldest councilor, a woman named Harkness with hands like tree roots, turned the ledger's pages slowly. The room held its breath. Wren found herself holding hers too, though none of this was hers to grieve or claim. She was an engineer who'd been aboard eleven days. She had no stake here except the ones she'd chosen.

But she'd read enough of those pages by lamplight to feel them in her chest. Salvage rights bought for nothing and sold for fortunes. A partner's name crossed out of the company rolls three days after he drowned. And at the bottom of the last page, in a hand that shook only slightly: *If I do not come back from the gorge, let whoever finds this know the Marchettis did not build their house on stone. They built it on my partner's lungs full of river.*

Harkness looked up. "Captain Blackwood. This is your mentor's hand?"

"It is." Xanthe's voice was steady, but Wren saw what the councilors didn't — the way his thumb pressed white against the table's edge. "Mara Vesper kept better books than any bank on the river. She kept them because she knew someday someone would need to read them."

"And you bring them freely? You could sell these to the Marchettis for more than your boat is worth."

Xanthe almost smiled. "I could. But then I'd have to look at myself in the engine room mirror every morning, and I don't much like what's there already."

A sound went through the hall — not quite laughter, not quite relief. Something loosening.

The vote took less time than Wren expected. Revocation of the Marchetti salvage licenses, all of them, every town from Graywater to the sea. Restoration of the Vesper name to the charts — not just the boat, but the woman. *Vesper Reach*, the new survey would say, where the old charts had said Marchetti Bend. Harkness read the motion aloud twice so there could be no mistake about whose water it honored.

Outside afterward, the rain had stopped for the first time in four days. The whole town steamed in the sun like a kettle taken off the fire. Wren walked beside Xanthe down the muddy main street, past shopfronts where people were already pinning up handbills with the ledger's worst figures copied out in charcoal, and neither of them spoke until they reached the levee.

"They'll want to make you something," Wren said. "A magistrate. A harbor master. Some kind of title."

"I know."

"You hate titles."

"I hate meetings more." He looked out at the Vesper moored below, her hull patched with Wren's welding showing bright as new scars, her wheelhouse window replaced with glass that didn't quite match. "She looks terrible."

"She looks like a boat that survived a flood," Wren said. "That's the best thing a boat can look."

* * *

Odile Marchetti left Graywater on foot.

Xanthe watched it happen from the deck of the Vesper, arms folded on the rail, saying nothing. She came down the levee road alone — no trunks, no guards, no boat of her own anymore, seized along with everything else. Her traveling dress was still fine; that was the last fine thing about her. She walked the way people walk when they've decided dignity is all they're permitted to keep.

At the bottom of the gangplank road she stopped. Looked up at him across thirty feet of gray water.

He should have felt triumphant. He'd imagined this moment for ten years without knowing it — Odile diminished, the debt between their families settled in public, Mara's name spoken in council halls instead of drowned in a gorge. But standing there, watching her count her remaining coins on the levee road, he mostly felt tired. Old tired. River tired.

"Your mother's partner," Odile called up, and her voice carried clean across the water, "drowned because he tried to renegotiate terms my father considered final. I was nine years old when I learned that's how business is done. Nobody taught me another way." She paused. "You could have let me drown in the flood. You had every reason."

"The river does enough killing," Xanthe said. "It doesn't need my help."

"No," she agreed quietly. "It doesn't." She adjusted the strap of her single bag. "For what it's worth, Captain — I never intended the wreck for you specifically. I intended it for anyone who could confirm the box existed. It simply happened to be you. The river has a sense of humor."

"So I've been told."

She stood a moment longer, and something moved behind her eyes that might have been the ghost of respect, or grief, or just the wind off the water. Then she turned and walked east along the levee, getting smaller, until the road bent and took her.

Sal appeared at Xanthe's shoulder, chewing on the unlit pipe he chewed whenever smoking was forbidden. "You going to feel bad about that?"

"No."

"Good. Because I'm not going to." Sal spat thoughtfully over the rail. "Some wrecks are graves. She dug 'em."

* * *

The debt collector from Kettle Landing arrived two days later, and Wren — who had appointed herself unofficial guardian of the Vesper's peace — stationed herself in the engine room hatch with a wrench the size of her forearm, just in case.

She needn't have bothered. Xanthe counted out the payment in front of the man on the wheelhouse desk: salvage shares, council witness fees, even a bonus Harkness had insisted on. The collector weighed the purse in his hand, checked it twice against his book, and for the first time since Wren had seen him board at Kettle Landing, smiled.

"Pleasure doing business, Captain." He tipped his hat at the door. "Word of advice — stay clear of Kettle Landing for a season. Marchetti men drink angry."

"Marchetti men are looking for work," Xanthe said. "Like everyone else."

After he'd gone, Xanthe sat at the desk a long moment, looking at the space where the debt had lived inside his head for ten years. It was strange, Wren thought, watching him through the open door. He looked lighter and lost at once, like a man setting down a load so heavy he'd forgotten how to stand without it.

Then he opened the drawer and took out the compass.

* * *

They gathered in the wheelhouse at dusk — all of them, though nobody had called a meeting. Sal in the doorway with Biscuit at his boots. Wren perched on the chart table because chairs were for passengers. Xanthe at the binnacle, the brass compass open in his hands, its needle resting easy now, pointing wherever it pleased.

"It worked," Wren said, into the quiet. "In the rapids. It knew channels that aren't on any chart. I keep thinking about that. A needle doesn't know anything. It just answers a question somebody asked it."

"Mara asked it plenty of questions," Sal said around his pipe. "Forty years' worth. Maybe it got in the habit of answering."

Xanthe turned the compass over. On the case's underside, worn nearly smooth by decades of thumbs, the engraving: *M.V.* — and beneath it, smaller, added later in different tooling, letters Wren hadn't noticed before: *for whoever comes after.*

His breath caught. Wren saw it catch.

"She knew," he said. Not to anyone. To the compass, maybe. To the river. "All that time it sat dead in my drawer, and she'd already written me into it."

"Engineers sign their work," Wren said softly. "It's the first rule."

Xanthe laughed — a short, surprised sound, half swallowed. Then he closed the compass, set it in its velvet-lined case, and latched it shut. He placed it on the shelf above the binnacle, where the river light would find it every morning, and left it there.

"Its work is done," he said. "Ours isn't."

* * *

The contract was signed at the Kettle Landing agent's office a week later, and Wren read it three times before she believed it. Permanent engineer, the *Vesper*, full crew share, room and board, and — written into the margin in Xanthe's blunt handwriting — *apprenticeship papers recognized and countersigned, X. Blackwood, master.*

Her real papers. The ones the shipyard had sold out from under her. Made legitimate by a captain who'd known her eleven days.

"You could have gotten someone cheaper," she said, when she came back aboard. "Someone who didn't weld a hole in your deck plate on day two."

"That hole saved us in the gorge and you know it." He was coiling line at the bow, not looking at her, which was how he talked about things that mattered. "Besides. Crew's not a cost. Crew's what keeps a boat from being cargo."

Wren leaned on the rail beside him and looked downriver, toward home waters, toward whatever came next. The monsoon had passed and left the channel high and fast and clean. Somewhere ahead were cargoes and sandbars and towns that would want to shake the captain's hand forever, and somewhere behind them was a gorge with a new name on the charts, and a woman's grave that finally had its story told.

"Where to, Captain?" she asked.

Xanthe Blackwood — no longer hiding, no longer owing, no longer steering by a dead needle — took the wheel and rang the engine bell twice.

"Downstream," he said. "Home first. Then anywhere the water goes."

And the *Vesper*, patched and proud and pointed true, slid out into the current with a full crew and a clear name, carrying them all toward morning.
