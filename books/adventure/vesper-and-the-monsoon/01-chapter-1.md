## 1. Chapter 1

The river gave up its dead slowly, and Jonah Coldharbour had learned to be patient with it.

He went down at first light, before the navy patrol boats made their morning circuit, when the water was still the color of old pewter and the cold hadn't yet worked its way through his suit into his bones. The wreck sat forty feet down on the eastern shelf of the channel, a river steamer that had gone down twenty years ago with her hull stove in and her name burned off her stern. The charts called the stretch of water nothing at all — an unnamed bend in the Vell, marked only by depth soundings and a warning about submerged obstructions.

Jonah had been diving her for three weeks.

The routine was simple enough that his hands could do it while his mind went elsewhere, which was the only way he'd survived this long in the trade. Weight belt. Knife on the right hip, lamp clipped to the left strap. Check the air gauge twice, because Teo's regulators were beautiful pieces of work but Teo himself was a gambler with other people's lives and only ever careless with his own. Then over the side, feet first, letting the cold close over his head like a door.

Forty feet down, the world narrowed to the circle of his lamp.

The steamer lay on her port side, half-buried in silt, her superstructure collapsed into a tangle of iron ribs and rotted decking. Fish moved through her in slow silver schools. A curtain of weed hung from what used to be the wheelhouse, swaying in the current like something breathing. Twenty years underwater and she was still recognizably a ship — you could see where the passengers had stood, where the cargo had been lashed, where someone had painted a stripe along the rail that the river had never quite managed to strip away.

She was also picked clean. Every fitting worth a coin had been salvaged years ago, by crews with better equipment and better permits, or no permits at all. What remained was scrap: copper pipe, brass valves, the occasional porthole if you knew how to work one out of a corroded frame without cracking the glass. Enough to keep a man fed if he worked every day and wanted nothing else out of life.

Jonah wanted something else out of life. He just couldn't have said what, anymore.

He worked the forward hold that morning, prying at a bulkhead plate with the short crowbar he kept for the purpose, working it loose an inch at a time while the silt billowed around him and settled again. His breathing echoed inside the helmet, steady and slow, four counts in, six counts out. Below him, the deck plating disappeared into mud that had swallowed whole sections of the ship. Above him, faint as memory, the surface shimmered.

Somewhere up there, a patrol boat would be coming around the bend within the hour. He had maybe ninety minutes of good bottom time, and he intended to use all of them.

* * *

The debt was eleven years old, and he never talked about it.

Teo knew there was one. Everyone who worked with Jonah long enough figured out that a certain portion of everything he earned went somewhere — a payment, sent quarterly, to an address in the floodplain district that Jonah had never once explained. Teo had guessed at gambling debts, at a wife, at a bastard child. Sefa, when she'd joined them two winters back, had assumed blackmail; she'd seen enough of it in the service to recognize the shape of a man paying regularly and unhappily.

They were all wrong, and Jonah let them be wrong, because the truth was worse than any of their guesses and none of their business besides.

The truth was a name on a ledger in a flooded city, and a signature under it that read *Coldharbour*.

He hauled the bulkhead plate free and hooked it to the lift line, then paused, lamp sweeping across the hold. Something glinted near the base of the collapsed stairway — not the dull orange of rusted iron but a deeper yellow, brass or bronze, half-buried where the mud had crept in through a sprung seam. He dropped to his knees on the tilted deck and dug with gloved fingers, working the object loose with the patience of a man who had pulled ten thousand things out of rivers and knew better than to rush any of them.

It was a fitting of some kind, hand-span wide, cast in heavy bronze. Decorative. A ship's ornament, maybe, or part of a railing mount. He turned it in the lamplight, thumbing off the silt.

And stopped breathing.

There was a seal pressed into the metal. Not stamped — cast, part of the original piece, which meant it had been put there deliberately, meant to be seen, meant to last. A design he knew the way every kid who'd grown up on the Vell knew it: from forbidden songs, from the hushed stories old boatmen told after the third drink, from the one page in his grandfather's atlas that had been pasted over with newer paper that didn't quite match.

A wave beneath a seven-pointed star.

Vesper.

* * *

Twenty years ago, the nation of Vesper had existed. It sat astride the lower Vell delta, rich from river trade and mountain silver, proud enough to build a fleet and foolish enough to think a fleet would save it. Then came the war, and the winter of the sinking, and one night in deep fog the entire Vesperan fleet — warships and grain barges and passenger steamers, everything that could float — went to the bottom of the estuary within a single tide. Torpedoed, mined, scuttled; the histories disagreed, because the histories had been written afterward, by the victors, and the victors had decided that Vesper had never really been a nation at all. Just a region. Just a rebellion. Just a regrettable incident, now closed.

The maps were redrawn. The names were changed. The survivors scattered inland, and the government paid them a small indemnity and required them to sign a paper renouncing any claim to nationhood, and most of them signed because their children were hungry.

Jonah's grandfather had owned one of the old atlases. Jonah, at nine years old, had peeled back the pasted-over page and seen the country that wasn't there anymore, all its towns printed in fine confident ink: *Vesperport. Callowmere. The Seven Sisters.* And beneath the map, in his grandfather's handwriting, a single line: *Ask no questions about this.*

So he hadn't asked. For thirty years he hadn't asked. And now here was the star and the wave, cast in bronze, sitting in the mud of a wreck the navy patrolled daily and never once salvaged.

Because they weren't guarding the wreck. He'd understood that for years, in the way you understand things without letting yourself know them. Patrol boats don't guard wrecks. They guard *secrets*. And secrets, unlike wrecks, don't get picked clean by salvage crews — which meant whatever was left aboard this ship, the navy wanted left aboard.

His air gauge said he had time. His hands, he noticed distantly, had gone very steady, the way they did when the rest of him was afraid.

He turned the fitting over. On the reverse, half worn away, was a courier's mark — a small stamped cartouche, the kind registered dispatch riders used to seal official pouches against tampering. Jonah had seen the modern version on navy mail. This one was older, the lettering archaic, but the function was the same.

This ship had carried government freight. Sealed, registered, important enough to mark.

Important enough to erase.

* * *

He should have pocketed the fitting and gone back to scraping copper. That was the smart move, the move that kept a diver alive: take the scrap, sell the scrap, pay the debt, stay small, stay invisible, stay alive. Thirty years of diving had taught him that the river rewarded exactly one virtue, and it was obedience.

Instead he knelt on the tilted deck of a drowned ship and looked down the length of the hold, into the dark beyond his lamp's reach, where the mud had buried the aft compartments deepest.

The patrol boats never salvaged her. In twenty years. While every other wreck on the Vell had been stripped to her bones.

He thought about his grandfather's line of ink. *Ask no questions.*

He thought about the quarterly payments, and the address in the floodplain, and the woman who received them, and the way she had looked at him the last time — not grateful, not warm, just *measuring*, the way you measure timber before you commit it to a load-bearing wall.

"Damn it," he said, and the word bloomed silver in his helmet, absurd and alone.

He swam aft.

The mud thickened as he went, the current dying away into dead water where twenty years of silt had settled undisturbed. His lamp carved a tunnel through the murk. Deck plates gave way to open frame, frame to collapsed bulkhead, and the darkness pressed closer, and somewhere in the back of his mind a voice that sounded like every dive master he'd ever had was reciting the rules about silty enclosed spaces and the divers who died in them.

He found the compartment behind the engine room, where the hull had folded inward around some old catastrophic blow, leaving a gap barely wide enough for a man in hard kit. He should have turned back. The gap was a throat, and the dark beyond it was a stomach, and the river had eaten better men than him for less reason.

He went through anyway.

Inside, the compartment was small and intact, a pocket the collapse had sealed like a fist. And the mud here was different — deeper, finer, undisturbed, except—

Except for a rectangle. Faint, almost invisible, a ghost of straight lines in the soft floor of silt. The kind of impression left by something heavy that had rested there a long time and then been removed.

Or by something heavy that had shifted, sunk deeper, and waited.

Jonah swept the lamp low and saw the corner of it just breaking the surface of the mud, down where the compartment floor met the hull. An edge. Straight-edged, deliberate, banded in metal that the river hadn't managed to stain.

He dug. Silt clouded everything; he worked blind, by feel, fingers walking along the edge until he had its dimensions — chest-sized, longer than it was wide, heavier than anything he could lift alone. Lead-sheathed. He knew lead when he felt it, the soft dead weight of it, and he knew why anyone would sheathe a chest in lead, and the knowing went through him like cold water through a torn suit.

Lead keeps out water. Lead keeps out prying eyes. Lead is what you wrap around something when you intend it to sink and stay sunk and tell no one where.

His air was getting short. The voice in his head had stopped reciting rules and started shouting. He got a lifting sling around the thing anyway — it took three tries, arms burning, lungs hammering — and rigged it to the main lift line, and hammered twice on the hull plating: the signal for Teo, topside, to start the winch easy.

Then he hung onto the line as the chest broke free of the mud with a slow reluctant sigh, and the silt closed over the space where it had lain for twenty years, and Jonah Coldharbour rose through the dark water of the Vell toward the thin bright shiver of the surface, hauling behind him the thing the navy had drowned a nation to bury.

Above him, faintly, he could already hear the engines of the morning patrol.
