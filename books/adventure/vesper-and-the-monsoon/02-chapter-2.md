## 2. Chapter 2

The chest came up the way dead things come up — slow, reluctant, trailing silt like a shroud it wasn't ready to leave behind.

Jonah had it on the winch line at forty feet, and he knew before he saw it that this was no engine block, no brass fitting, no scrap worth its weight in bread. The line went taut wrong. Scrap shifts, tumbles, fights you in small stupid ways. This hung steady, balanced, patient, as if it had been waiting twenty years for someone strong enough and foolish enough to pull.

He worked the lift alone because Teo was topside running the compressor and Jonah had told him to stay there. The river was green-black at this depth, cold enough to ache through the neoprene even in high summer, and his lamp made a small trembling circle in all that dark. The wreck beneath him was a paddle steamer, or had been — hull ribs fanned open like the carcass of some enormous fish, cargo hold collapsed into itself, everything furred with a century of mussels and rust. The navy charts called this stretch of the Vell dredged and empty. The navy patrols passed over it twice a week with sonar and never stopped. Jonah had wondered about that for three years. He'd stopped wondering out loud after the second time a patrol boat slowed to watch him dive and then moved on without a word.

The chest sat half-buried in the mud where the hold's floor had given way, wedged against a rib of the keel. Lead sheathing, he could see that much even through the grime — dull grey, soft-edged, the kind of metal you lined a box with when what was inside mattered more than the box. It was the size of a sea chest, big enough to hold a man's folded clothes or a fortune in coin or nothing at all. Four iron rings, one at each corner, for rigging. And on the lid, where the silt had been scoured away by the current funneling through the broken hull, a seal pressed into the lead itself.

A circle. Inside the circle, a wave curling over a mountain peak. Beneath them, a single word in an alphabet Jonah didn't read but recognized anyway, the way you recognize a face from a photograph you were never supposed to see.

Vesper.

His first thought, absurdly, was of the debt. Numbers moved behind his eyes — the ledger Maren kept in her head, the sum he owed, the years left to pay it. A chest like this, lead-lined, sealed, salvaged clean off a wreck the navy pretended not to see — a private buyer would pay for that without asking questions. A careful buyer. The kind who paid in untraceable weight and never gave a name. Enough to clear what he owed and walk away whole.

His second thought arrived slower and colder: nobody lines a chest in lead to protect what's inside from water. You line it in lead to keep what's inside from being found. By sonar. By surveyors. By anyone sweeping a river with instruments, looking for exactly this.

The navy hadn't forgotten this wreck. They were watching it.

Jonah hung in the black water above the seal of a drowned nation and felt the current push gently against him, like a hand on his shoulder saying *go back up, boy, go back up now.* Twenty years ago the maps had carried Vesper — a river nation, mountain-fed, proud, gone under in a single season of floods that everyone agreed was terrible and nobody ever explained. Twenty years ago the atlases were reprinted and the word was scraped out like a stain. He'd been nine years old. His father had come home from that last season of work on the upper rivers quieter than he'd left, and had stayed quiet for the rest of his short life, and had died owing money to a woman in a floodplain tenement whose family had come from somewhere the maps no longer showed.

*Go back up.*

Jonah hooked the lift straps through two of the four rings, checked them twice, and thumbed the winch signal twice — *take the strain slow.* Above him, the world brightened by degrees. The chest rose out of the mud with a sigh of displaced silt, and the river closed over the place where it had lain like a mouth.

* * *

Teo met him at the gunwale with the tag line already in hand, and Jonah watched his partner's face do three things in quick succession: relief that Jonah was up, confusion at the shape coming over the side, and then recognition — because everyone recognized that seal, even if they couldn't read the letters under it. It was stamped on old coins that collectors weren't allowed to collect. It was carved, once, on harbor stones in cities that had since renamed their harbors.

"No," Teo said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You're going to say something. I can see you composing it." Teo braced as the winch swung the chest inboard and set it down on the deck plates with a boom that Jonah felt through his boots. Water sheeted off the lead. "No."

"It might be nothing."

"It's not nothing. Nothing doesn't get lined in lead. Nothing doesn't get sunk in a wreck the navy babysits." Teo crouched next to it, not touching, the way you crouch near a snake you've decided not to kill yet. He was a small man built entirely of angles and opinions, with hands that could rebuild a compressor blindfolded and a face that did all his talking for him. Right now the face said *I am begging you.* "Jonah. Look at me. We melt it down."

"Melt it—"

"The lead. Today. Tonight. We cut the seal off, we break the lead into ingots, we sell it as scrap along with everything else, and whatever's inside goes in the crucible too if it'll burn, or the deep hole if it won't. Nobody ever sees it. Nobody ever knows. We make maybe two hundred marks and we live."

"And the debt?"

"The debt keeps!" Teo threw his hands up. "The debt is survivable! That's the whole genius of debts, they let you keep living! You know what isn't survivable? Being the diver who pulled up Vesper's seal while a navy patrol boat sits at anchor four miles downstream!"

Jonah looked downstream. The patrol boat was a grey smudge against the evening glare, right where it always was, right where it had been every week for three years. Watching a dredged and empty stretch of river.

"Three years," Jonah said. "They've sat on this spot for three years and never once dropped a hook. Why keep a guard on nothing?"

"Because it's not nothing! That's my entire point, that has been my point since you signaled me to take the strain—" Teo stopped himself, breathed, dragged both hands down his face. When he spoke again his voice had dropped to the low register he saved for genuine emergencies, the one he'd used the night the compressor flooded and the night the revenue men boarded them. "Jonah. Please. I've been on this boat with you six years. I've never asked you what the debt is, who Maren is, why a grown man pays a quarter of everything he earns to an old woman in a tenement with a leaking roof. Never asked. That was the deal and I've kept it. But I'm asking now. Is whatever you owe her worth dying for? Because that's what's sitting on our deck. That thing is not money. That thing is a reason somebody sinks a whole country and scrubs the maps."

The river slapped against the hull. Somewhere far off, a patrol engine turned over.

Jonah knelt beside the chest. Up close, out of the water, he could see how deliberate everything about it was — the riveted seams, the corner rings sized for standard naval lift gear, the lock plate under a hinged lead flap so even the keyhole couldn't be seen. Someone had built this to survive. Someone had loaded it onto a steamer twenty years ago expecting it to matter forever.

And someone else had put that steamer on the bottom of the Vell with everything aboard.

"Help me get it below," Jonah said.

"Jonah."

"Below decks, out of sight, before the patrol makes its evening run. Then we talk. I'm not melting anything until I know what I'd be melting."

"That's how it starts," Teo muttered, but he took the other corner, because six years of partnership meant his hands obeyed before his mouth finished arguing. Between them they wrestled the chest down the companionway into the hold, which smelled of diesel and bilge and old rope, and set it on deck boards between the fuel drums. Jonah hung the work lamp from a hook. Yellow light fell across the seal, and the wave curled over the mountain, and the drowned word sat in its circle like a held breath.

Teo stood at the foot of the ladder with his arms crossed, guarding the exit more than blocking it. "Ten minutes," he said. "Then I start cutting lead either way. I mean it, Jonah. I will physically begin destroying evidence of a national crime while you stand there, and I'll sleep fine, because I'd rather be a small criminal than a dead honest one."

"Fair terms."

Jonah examined the lock. It was good work — salt-water bronze, though this was fresh water, which told him the chest had traveled; it had crossed the sea before it ever came up this river. The mechanism was a three-lever design, older style, the kind that yielded to patience rather than force. He'd picked worse with less light. But he didn't touch it yet. Instead he ran his palm flat over the lead sheathing, feeling for seams, and found what he was looking for near the rear edge: a courier's plate, small as a playing card, screwed flush into the metal. Registration marks. A route code worn nearly smooth. And a number — *Courier Registry, 4th District, No. 11.*

Every nation had couriers once. Vesper had made a religion of them — sworn carriers, bonded to their cargo, the only people allowed to move sealed freight across borders without inspection. When Vesper drowned, the registries burned, or were said to have burned. Jonah's father had once, drunk past caution, mentioned knowing a courier. Just once. Then never again.

No. 11. If any registry survived anywhere — in exile, in hiding, in a floodplain tenement owned by a woman his father had owed — then a registered chest was not salvage. It was mail. Undelivered mail, twenty years late, addressed to whoever was still alive to receive it.

And you didn't melt down someone's mail. Every code Jonah had ever lived by, dockside and underwater, agreed on that much.

"Teo," he said quietly. "What do you know about Maren?"

Teo blinked. "Your landlady of the soul? Old woman, floodplain, collects your money like the tide?" He paused. Something shifted in his face. "Why?"

"What district does she come from? Before the floodplain. Where was her family from?"

"I don't— Jonah, why are you asking me this?"

"Because her accent comes out sometimes. Late, after the money's counted. A vowel she holds too long. I heard it my whole childhood and I never knew where it was from." He looked down at the seal. "I know where it's from now."

The lamp buzzed. In the hold's shadows the chest seemed larger than it had on deck, the way important things do.

"If I open this," Jonah said, "and it's coins, we sell the coins, we pay the debt, we're done, and you can tell me you told me so for the rest of our lives. If it's papers—" He stopped. Started again. "If it's papers, then it belongs to whoever sent it. Or whoever it was sent to. And I think I know a woman who might be both."

"And the navy?"

"The navy sank the ship it was on. Whatever's in here, they already decided it should be at the bottom of the Vell. Which means it's worth more to them down there than anything we could get for the lead." He met Teo's eyes. "Which means it's worth more up here, too. To somebody."

Teo was silent a long moment. Outside, faint through the hull, the patrol engine note rose and fell, rising, falling — closer than usual tonight, or maybe just the wind. He looked at the chest the way a man looks at a door he knows opens onto a long drop.

"You're going to open it," he said. Not a question.

"I'm going to open it."

"At least do it smart. Cut the hinges, don't force the lock — if it's registered mail, breaking the lock breaks your claim to it, whatever claim means. Keep the seal intact. And Jonah—" Teo crouched, finally touching the lead, laying his palm flat beside the wave-and-mountain as if taking its temperature. "Whatever's in there. We look at it once. Once. Then we decide with clear heads whether we're couriers or corpses. Deal?"

"Deal."

Jonah got the hacksaw from the tool locker. The blade bit into the first hinge with a sound like a whispered argument, fine sparks drifting down into the bilge shadow, and Teo held the lamp steady and didn't say *no* again, which was its own kind of loyalty. The hinge gave. The second one fought longer. And as Jonah sawed, working by lamplight in the belly of his own boat, he understood with perfect clarity that he was passing a line he'd been approaching his whole life — that his father had stood on some similar deck twenty years ago making some similar calculation, and had chosen silence, and had paid for it slowly ever after.

The lid came free with a groan of tired metal.

Inside, packed in oiled wool gone stiff as bark, lay documents. Bundles of them. Dozens. Sealed with wax, tagged with courier numbers, wrapped against water the way you wrap things you expect to fight for. On the topmost bundle, under a thin film of wool, a cover page showed through — a printed heading, formal, official, the kind of heading that belonged to fleets and ministries.

And beneath the heading, a date. Three days before Vesper drowned.

Teo made a sound low in his throat. "Jonah," he whispered. "That's not mail. That's a record."

Above them, muffled by hull and water, the patrol engine note changed — steadied, slowed, began to turn.

Toward them.
