## 3. Chapter 3

The courier markings took Jonah three days to trace, and every hour of it felt like walking down a hallway with someone following.

They were etched into the chest's lead skin, fine as spider silk, visible only when he held the lamp at an angle and let the light rake across the metal. A chain of small glyphs running from the lock plate to the base: a river bar, a mountain notch, a bell, a bell with a line through it, and then a string of numbers that Teo swore weren't numbers at all.

"Route marks," Teo had said, the first night, turning his magnifying lens over the glyphs until his eye watered. "Courier routes. This is a delivery manifest, Jonah. Whoever sealed this thing expected it to travel. Expected it to be *carried*."

"Carried where?"

"That's the part I don't like." Teo had tapped the bell with the line through it. "This one means the route's dead. Terminated. Which means the last courier on this chain either delivered and died, or died and never delivered."

Jonah had said nothing to that. He'd been thinking about the bell for two days now, and about the debt, and about the letter he'd received six weeks ago on paper so thin it was nearly translucent, with an address written in a hand that shook.

*You know what you owe. Come before the frost.*

No signature. None needed.

* * *

Maren lived where the river forgot itself — the floodplain district south of the salvage yards, where the tenements stood on stilts above water that rose and fell with the seasons and the government's whims, and where nobody official ever came because officially nobody lived there. The navy had stopped patrolling the floodplain years ago. Too poor to tax, too wet to burn.

Jonah went alone. He told Teo he was going to sell scrap. Teo looked at him with the particular patience of a man who had partnered with Jonah Coldharbour for nine years and had stopped expecting the truth around the edges of things.

The walkway to Maren's tenement bowed under his weight, boards gray and soft as old bread. The stair was a ladder, more or less. The door was a curtain of oiled canvas, and behind it the room smelled of lamp oil, dried fish, and something older — camphor, maybe, the smell of things being preserved against time.

She was smaller than he remembered, and he'd only met her once, eleven years ago, at a funeral he still couldn't talk about without his chest tightening. She'd been upright then. Fierce. A woman with river-gray hair and hands that looked like they could crack walnuts, or skulls. Now she was propped in a chair by the window like laundry someone had hung out to dry and forgotten, a blanket over legs that had stopped cooperating sometime around the last thaw. Her eyes were still fierce. That was the terrible thing. The body had quit on her and the eyes hadn't gotten the message.

"You took your time," she said.

Her voice was gravel in a paper bag. Jonah stood in the doorway, water dripping off his coat hem onto her floor, and found he had nothing prepared. He'd had three days on the river to prepare something. A greeting. An explanation. A lie.

"You sent for me," he said finally.

"I sent for you in the spring. You didn't come."

"I came when I could."

"You came when the navy's patrol rotation moved off the middle channel. Don't insult me, Coldharbour. I was reading navy schedules before you were weaned." She gestured with one knuckled hand at the chair across from her. "Sit. You're dripping."

He sat. The chair complained. Through the window behind her he could see the floodplain stretching gray to the horizon, water and reeds and the skeletons of boats, and beyond it, faint as a pencil line, the Kestrel Range with its first dusting of white.

"You have it," she said. Not a question.

"How do you know that?"

"Because you came." She smiled, and it was not a kind smile. It was the smile of someone who had set a trap years ago and was only now watching it close. "A man with a clear conscience doesn't cross the floodplain in October. A man with a clear conscience sells his scrap and goes home. You came because you found the chest, and because you found the chest, you went looking for the markings, and because you went looking for the markings, you're sitting in my parlor dripping river water on my floor. Courier logic, Coldharbour. It's older than you and it doesn't fail."

"The seal's intact," Jonah said. "The lock's water-damaged but the seal's intact. Lead-lined. Whatever's inside is dry."

"I know what's inside."

"I don't."

"No," Maren said. "You don't. And that's the only reason you're still alive, so let's keep it that way a while longer." She turned her head toward the window, toward the mountains, and for a long moment the only sound was the water lapping at the stilts beneath them, patient as a debt. "Twenty years ago there were four hundred registered couriers in the Vesperan service. Runners, riders, divers. We carried everything — letters, ledgers, contracts, confessions. A nation runs on paper, Coldharbour. Kill the paper and you kill the nation twice."

"Vesper's been dead twenty years."

"Vesper's been *drowned* twenty years. There's a difference, and you of all people should appreciate it. You make your living down there." She looked back at him. "I'm the last registered courier. Do you understand what that means? It means somewhere in a burned archive there's a ledger with my name in it, and a line through it, and the line is waiting for one final entry. Delivery confirmed. Or delivery failed."

"And the delivery is—"

"The chest." She said it simply, the way you'd say *the tide* or *the winter*. "It's been trying to get home for twenty years. Every courier who carried it is dead. Do you know how they died? Not in battle. Not in storms. One by one, quietly, over years. A drowning here. A fire there. A fever that came on too fast and too conveniently. The government didn't just sink Vesper, Coldharbour. It's been *mopping up* ever since. And the last name on that ledger before mine is a name you know."

The room got very small.

"Whose name," Jonah said. His voice came out flat, careful, the voice he used underwater when the air gauge started lying.

Maren studied him for a long time. Outside, a heron stalked the shallows with the murderous patience of all herons.

"Your father's," she said.

* * *

Jonah didn't remember standing up. He was at the window with his hands on the sill, looking out at the gray water, and Maren was talking behind him in that gravel voice, unhurried, letting each word find its depth.

"Corren Coldharbour. Registered courier, third class, route seven. The mountain route. Kestrel Pass, and the high valley beyond it." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "He was good. Not as good as me, but good. He carried the chest twice before the sinking. Once north, once back. He knew the route better than any man alive, which is why they killed him second-to-last, and why they left me for last — an old woman in a stilt house, what threat could I be? They wanted to leave one name in the ledger. One loose thread they could cut whenever it suited them."

"My father drowned," Jonah said. Still not turning around. "When I was fourteen. Salvage accident, the middle channel. Everyone said—"

"Everyone said the boat was overloaded. Everyone said he was greedy, took a risk, paid the price. Everyone said." Maren's voice hardened. "I read the incident report eleven years ago, Coldharbour, when you first came to me after his memorial, asking what your father had been to me. I pulled strings I shouldn't have had. The boat wasn't overloaded. The boat was *scuttled*. A seacock opened from inside the hull, and your father was the only one aboard who could have closed it, and he was in the water by the time it mattered. The navy called it an accident because the navy writes the reports. But the navy also writes the ledger, and your father's name was in it, and loose threads get cut."

Jonah's hands were white on the sill. The floodplain lay out there, flat and gray and indifferent, and somewhere under it, under all the water he'd ever worked, his father's boat sat in the mud with a seacock open, and he had dived wrecks within a mile of it. He had dived *over* it, maybe. He had never known.

"Eleven years," he said. "You've known for eleven years. You watched me dive. You watched me take his boat, his gear, his trade—"

"I watched you pay his debts," Maren said. "Every coin you earned, half of it gone before you touched it. You thought I didn't know where it went? You thought I didn't know about the payments to the harbor office, the 'fees,' the 'levies,' the quiet money that kept your salvage license alive when by every right it should have been revoked? Someone has been bleeding you for your father's sins for eleven years, Coldharbour. And you paid, and paid, and never asked why, because asking why is dangerous, and your father taught you better than that, didn't he. *Don't ask, don't look, keep your head down and your hands busy.*"

"Stop."

"He taught you to dive in water he knew was full of—"

"*Stop.*"

The word cracked off the walls. The heron outside lifted and beat away low across the reeds. Jonah stood there breathing, and Maren let him breathe, because she was old and patient and she had spent twenty years learning exactly how long a man needs.

When he turned around, she was watching him with those unkillable eyes.

"What do you want," he said.

"Ah. There it is. The diver's question. What's the depth, what's the current, what's the price." She pulled the blanket higher on her legs. "Here's what I want. Here's the debt, itemized, so there's no confusion. Your father carried for Vesper. Your father died for carrying. The government took his name and his boat and his son's livelihood and called it weather. You owe Vesper, Coldharbour. Not me — Vesper. And Vesper's price is simple."

She lifted one finger. It trembled, and she let it.

"Take the chest across the Kestrel Range. Before the snows close the passes. There are people in the high valley — descendants, survivors, children of the drowned — who have waited twenty years for what's in that lead box. They have a right to it. The dead have a right to it. And you—" the finger swung toward him, "—you are the last name that can carry it, because the last name before yours on the courier ledger is your father's, and blood closes accounts."

"The passes close in six weeks. Maybe five."

"Then you'd best stop standing in my parlor."

"It's not that simple. The navy's crawling over the river towns. There's a commander — Hale. She's executing boatmen over rumors of salvage. If they catch that chest on me—"

"They'll kill you," Maren agreed pleasantly. "Slowly, and in an office, with paperwork. That's how they do it now. But here is what you're not seeing, because grief has you by the throat. You don't have a choice. You think you do. You're standing there calculating — the risk, the reward, whether you can melt the thing down and pay your debts and forget my face. But you already carried it out of the water. You already went looking for the markings. You already came here. A man who found a Vesperan chest and *didn't* come to the last Vesperan courier — that man I'd worry about. That man would already be dead, or already theirs." She leaned forward, and the effort cost her something; her breath went thin and wheezing. "You came because some cold, stubborn, drowned part of you has known for eleven years what you owe. I'm just the paperwork, Coldharbour. I'm just the ledger catching up."

Jonah looked at her for a long time. The trap of it was beautiful, he had to give her that. No threats. No chains. Just the truth, arranged in a circle around him, with one door leading at the mountains.

"If I say no," he said.

"Then you'll dive scrap until your lungs rot, and pay your harbor levies, and one morning a navy cutter will come alongside your boat, and a polite officer will ask you a few questions about a wreck in the middle channel, and that will be the last morning of your life. Because they know the chest is up, Coldharbour. Why do you think Hale is burning the river towns? They don't know *where* it is. They only know it's *found*. And the only man who could have found it is the son of the last courier on route seven." She settled back, exhausted by the speech, but her eyes never wavered. "You're already in the water, Coldharbour. I'm just telling you which way the current runs."

* * *

He stayed until dark. He didn't mean to. But she gave him tea that tasted of camphor and pine, and she talked — about his father, mostly, and about route seven, and about the high valley where the chest had to go. She talked the way dying people talk, in no particular order, everything of equal weight, and Jonah sat in the lamplight and let it wash over him and understood, with a diver's cold clarity, that he was being fitted for something. A harness. A load. The weight was already on him; she was just showing him where the straps went.

"The lock is damaged," he said, when the lamp had burned low. "Water got into the mechanism. I can't open it, and I'm not sure I want to."

"Don't open it." Maren's voice had gone quiet, almost tender. "The seal is the proof. A broken seal is just paper. A sealed chest is a nation's testimony. Carry it sealed. Let the people in the valley break it — they've earned the right to be the first hands on it in twenty years." She closed her eyes. "There's a boy in the river city. A lockpicker, they say. Young. Vesperan blood, if the rumors are true — and rumors about Vesperan blood are usually true, because we were everywhere before they drowned us. Find him. You'll need hands that aren't yours. You'll need more than hands. You'll need a crew, and you'll need to move within the fortnight, and you'll need to trust people, Coldharbour, which I know is the heaviest load of all for a man like you."

"A man like me."

"A man who's been paying for his father in the dark for eleven years." She opened her eyes. "It ends at the top of the range. Whatever's in that chest, whatever your father's name is worth — it ends there. Delivery confirmed. The ledger closes. You'll be free, Coldharbour. Free or dead, but free or dead *honestly*, and I think you've forgotten there's a difference."

He stood to go. At the curtain he stopped, because there was one more question, the one he'd been carrying since she said his father's name, and it came out before he could weigh it.

"Did he know? My father. About what was in the chest. Did he know what they'd done?"

Maren was quiet for a long time. The water lapped the stilts. The lamp hissed.

"He suspected," she said at last. "That's why he kept carrying. A man who knows nothing can walk away. A man who suspects — he carries it to the grave, and then he passes the weight to his son, and hopes the son is strong enough." She looked at Jonah, and for one moment the fierceness in her eyes cracked, and underneath it was something old and tired and grieving. "He hoped right. Now go. And Coldharbour — the frost is early this year. The mountain won't wait for your grief to settle. Nothing up there ever waits."

* * *

The walkway back across the floodplain was black and slick, and Jonah moved along it without a lamp, letting his feet find the boards the way they found a wreck in silt — by feel, by weight, by the old knowledge in his hands. Behind him the tenement window glowed, one small square of light in all that gray water, and he knew without looking that Maren was watching him go, timing him against the frost.

The Kestrel Range stood against the stars, white already above the tree line. Six weeks. Maybe five. A chest full of a nation's testimony, a dead father's name on a courier ledger, and a commander named Hale sealing the southern roads like a hand closing around a throat.

He needed a crew. He needed an engineer, a fighter, a smuggler with mules, and a boy who could open anything.

He needed to move tonight.

Jonah stood at the end of the walkway where the boards met solid ground, and for the first time in eleven years, the debt in his chest had a shape and a direction, and it pointed up — into the cold, into the passes, into whatever waited in the high valley for a sealed box and the last name on a drowned ledger.

He turned toward the river city and its lamplight, and started walking.
