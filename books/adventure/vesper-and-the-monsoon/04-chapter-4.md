## 4. Chapter 4

Wren had been watching the door of the Saltglass Tavern for two hours before Jonah Coldharbour finally walked through it.

She knew his walk by now — that heavy, deliberate tread of a man whose body remembered the weight of water even on dry land. He moved like he was always carrying something up from the bottom. She'd followed him for three days since she'd seen him in the chandlery with the lead-lined chest strapped under oilcloth, and in three days she'd learned everything a person could learn from shadows: he drank little, tipped fair, checked every room's exits before he sat down, and paid his debts in coin that came from somewhere he didn't talk about.

She also knew what was stamped into the lead beneath that oilcloth, because she'd gotten close enough to see it while he argued with a farrier about mule shoes.

The seal of Vesper. The nine-pointed star inside the wave.

Her grandmother used to draw it in the ash at the bottom of the stove when she thought Wren wasn't looking, then smear it out fast, like erasing evidence.

Wren pulled her cap lower over her ears and slipped out the back before Coldharbour could clock her face again. He'd noticed her once already, outside the rope-walk, with that flat diver's stare that made her feel weighed and found wanting. She couldn't afford to be found wanting. She couldn't afford to be found at all.

But she could afford to be useful. Useful people got taken along.

* * *

The engineer was easy. Teo Marisol worked out of a lean-to behind the clockmaker's shop, a broad-shouldered woman with burn-scarred forearms who could apparently fix anything except her own habit of talking to machines like they were stubborn children. Wren watched her through the window for an afternoon, saw her take apart a broken pump and put it back together better than new, and decided she'd do.

Jonah did it differently. He just walked in, set a corroded brass lock from the chest on her bench, and said, "Can you open this without breaking it?"

Teo turned it over twice, held it to the light, and frowned. "This is Vesperan work."

"It is."

"Vesper's been underwater twenty years."

"So has this lock. Can you open it?"

Teo looked at him for a long moment, and Wren — wedged in the alley between a rain barrel and a wall that smelled of fish guts — saw something pass over the engineer's face that wasn't greed or fear. It was hunger. The specific hunger of someone who'd spent her whole life fixing things other people threw away.

"Yes," Teo said. "When do we leave?"

Wren nearly fell into the rain barrel. That was it? No haggling? No *what's in it for me*?

Later, much later, she would understand: everyone Jonah recruited that week was someone the world had already thrown away. You didn't haggle with a man offering you a reason.

* * *

The deserter was harder.

Sefa Renn lived above a dye-house in the Tanners' Quarter, alone, keeping to herself, which was exactly what you'd expect from a woman who'd walked away from the navy with her service tattoo half-burned off her wrist. Wren knew about the tattoo because Wren knew how to get into rooms nobody invited her into, and she'd been in Sefa's room on the second night, crouched behind a wardrobe, reading the woman's life off its surfaces like a courier reads seals.

A folded uniform, navy grey, kept clean. A knife honed to a mirror edge. A single letter, opened and refolded so many times the creases were going soft, with the Admiralty's black wax broken on it. And on the windowsill, a row of nine small stones, river-smoothed, arranged in a star pattern that Wren's chest went tight looking at, because her grandmother had arranged river stones the same way.

Nine points. A wave. Vesper.

Wren hadn't touched anything. She'd climbed back out the window feeling like she'd robbed a grave.

So when Jonah knocked on Sefa's door three days later, Wren was already in the stairwell, listening, telling herself it was reconnaissance and not eavesdropping and definitely not cowardice about knocking herself.

"I know what you are," Jonah said through the door.

Silence. Then: "You have about four seconds before I demonstrate what I am."

"I need someone who knows how Hale thinks. How his patrols move. How he'll come after us." A pause. "Because he will come after us."

More silence. Then the sound of a bolt drawn back, and Sefa Renn stood in the doorway — tall, close-cropped hair gone grey early, eyes like a winter harbor. "Commander Hale," she said, "doesn't chase salvage thieves. He erases them. Whatever you've got in that chest, diver, it isn't worth your life."

"It isn't mine to weigh," Jonah said. "I made a promise."

"To who?"

"A dying woman in the floodplain. To a nation that doesn't exist anymore."

Sefa looked at him the way you look at a man who's said something either very stupid or very true, and Wren saw her hand drift to her wrist, to the ruined place where the tattoo used to be.

"I burned my papers," Sefa said. "I'm no one."

"No," Jonah said. "You're the only one of us who knows exactly what we're running from. That makes you the most valuable person in this city."

Wren heard the long quiet that followed, and then, quietly, almost angrily, as if the words were being extracted against their will: "How many mules?"

* * *

The smuggler was the one who worried her.

Not because Ilo Vasq was dangerous — though he was, in the way of men who smiled too easily — but because Wren had grown up around his kind, and his kind sold you out the moment the price cleared the cost of the betrayal. He ran a mule train out of the stockyards, six animals and a reputation for getting cargo anywhere the roads were bad enough that honest freight wouldn't go. He wore a good coat with patched elbows and laughed with his whole face, and none of it quite matched his eyes, which were always doing arithmetic.

Wren tailed his mule train for a day and a half, learning his routes, his stops, the names of his drivers. On the second evening she got ahead of him on the Kestrel road and simply stepped out of the trees onto the path in front of his lead mule, which was either very brave or very stupid, and she still wasn't sure which.

Ilo reined up and looked down at her — a skinny kid in a borrowed cap, sixteen years old and all elbows — and grinned. "Well. The mountain's grown a child. Are you lost, little sprout, or are you a toll?"

"I hear you move cargo," Wren said. "Quietly."

"I hear lots of things. Most of them cost money to confirm."

"There's a job. Over the range. Before snowfall."

Something flickered behind the grin — quick calculation, the arithmetic starting up. "Over the Kestrels? In autumn? Nobody moves cargo over the Kestrels in autumn. The passes eat caravans."

"The pay says otherwise."

"Mm." He swung down from the saddle, unhurried, and crouched to her level, which she hated, because it was a trick, making himself friendly-sized. "Here's my problem, sprout. Jobs that pay well enough to cross mountains out of season are jobs where the cargo is worth more than the carriers. And jobs like that end with the carriers at the bottom of a ravine wondering why they weren't told. So before we talk numbers — what am I carrying?"

Wren hesitated. It was the hesitation that did it, she realized later. If she'd lied smoothly, he'd have walked. But she looked at him — really looked — and said, "The truth. About Vesper."

The grin died. Not slowly. All at once, like a lamp snuffed.

For a moment Ilo Vasq wasn't smiling at all, and what was underneath was older and wearier and, strangely, more honest. "My mother sang Vesperan lullabies," he said quietly. "Wouldn't tell me where she learned them. Wouldn't ever say the word twice." He stood up, and the grin came back, but it fit differently now, like a coat returned to its right owner. "All right, sprout. Now I'm listening."

"You'll have to talk to Coldharbour."

"Then let's go find the diver." He paused, glancing back at her. "You're not his crew yet, are you? Because if you're negotiating for him, kid, we should discuss your cut."

"I don't want a cut."

"Everyone wants a cut."

"I want to come *with*," Wren said, and heard her own voice crack on it, and hated herself for it.

Ilo looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw, he didn't laugh at. "Yeah," he said. "I know how that is."

* * *

Which left the fourth one. The one nobody had recruited, because nobody knew she existed.

Wren spent the night before the crew's departure sitting on the roof of the Saltglass Tavern, watching the chimney smoke and rehearsing. She'd been rehearsing for years, actually — since her grandmother died and left her nothing but a name spoken once, into a pillow, like a confession: *we were Vesseri, child, and the sea remembers.* Since she'd taught herself locks because doors kept closing. Since she'd learned that the world had a hole in it shaped exactly like a drowned country, and that nobody else seemed to notice the draft coming through.

At dawn she went to Jonah.

He was loading the mules in the yard behind the tavern, working beside Teo, who was rigging the false-bottomed panniers, and Sefa, who was checking sightlines with a soldier's economy, and Ilo, who was arguing with a mule named Duchess and losing. A family, almost, though none of them knew it yet. They moved around each other like strangers in a market — careful, watchful, leaving space.

Wren walked straight up to Jonah Coldharbour and said, "Take me with you."

He looked down at her. Up close he was older than she'd guessed, weathered like dock timber, with diver's pale eyes that didn't miss much. "No."

"You don't even know what I can do."

"I know what you are. You've been following me for eleven days. You were in the alley behind Teo's shop. You were in the stairwell at Sefa's." He said it without heat, the way you report the tide. "And you went through Ilo's ledgers last Tuesday, which he hasn't noticed yet, but he will."

Behind him, Ilo's head came up sharply. "You *what*—"

"I also know," Wren pressed on, heart hammering, "that your chest has a Vesperan courier seal on it, and that courier seals mean registered couriers, and registered couriers mean there's a registry, and I know things about registries that—" She stopped. Steadied. Her grandmother's voice, low and certain, in the dark of a hundred cold rooms: *we were Vesseri, child.*

"My grandmother drew the star in the stove ash," Wren said. "Every night. And wiped it away before morning, like it was a crime. She never told me why until the end, and by then she could barely speak, and all she got out was *the record has to come home*. I think—" Her voice did the cracking thing again and this time she let it. "I think your chest might be what she meant. And I am not staying behind. I'm done staying behind things."

The yard was silent except for Duchess the mule blowing air through her lips.

Sefa spoke first. "She picked my window lock without tripping the bell wire I rigged myself," she said, and there was something in her voice that might have been respect or might have been alarm. "That's not nothing."

"She read my ledgers and left them in order," Ilo said. "Also not nothing. Also slightly terrifying."

Teo just looked at Wren with those machine-tinker's eyes, the ones that assessed everything for what it could become. "Can you keep pace on the trail?"

"I've been living on rooftops since I was twelve. I can keep pace on anything."

Jonah hadn't moved. He was studying her the way he must have studied wrecks — patiently, thoroughly, checking for structural failure before committing to the dive. Wren made herself stand in it. Made herself not fidget. *Weigh me,* she thought. *Find me sound.*

"Why Vesper?" he asked finally. "Why does it matter to you? You're what, sixteen? The country drowned before you could walk."

"Because they took it away," Wren said. "Not just the country. The word. You can't say it in school. It's not on maps. My grandmother died pretending to be from somewhere else, and I watched her do it, and I decided—" She stopped, surprised by the thing rising in her, old and hot. "I decided I'd rather die saying it than live erasing it. Somebody has to carry the word. That's what a courier *is*, isn't it? Somebody who carries."

Nobody answered. The wind came down off the Kestrel Range, early and cold, smelling of frost to come, and rattled the empty panniers.

Then Jonah reached into his coat and tossed her something small. She caught it against her chest before she could think.

A key. Old brass, Vesperan workmanship, the wave-and-star worked into the bow.

"That's from the chest's outer hasp," Teo said, frowning. "I haven't even—"

"I know," Jonah said. He was still looking at Wren. "Consider it a test. If you can pick the lock it belongs to before we clear the city walls, you're crew. If you can't—"

He didn't finish, because Wren was already turning the key over in her fingers, laughing — actually laughing, a sound she hadn't made in months — because she'd seen this lock, hadn't she, in the chandlery, and she'd already worked its mechanism out in her head from the sound of the tumblers through the oilcloth, and it was going to take her maybe ninety seconds.

"Deal," she said.

Ilo snorted and swung up onto his mule. Sefa rolled her eyes at the sky. Teo muttered something fond and exasperated at a buckle. And Jonah Coldharbour almost — almost — smiled, the way stone almost softens.

They went out through the south gate in ones and twos, strangers with a secret, five people and six mules and a lead-lined chest full of a dead nation's testimony. Wren rode at the rear on a borrowed pony, the brass key warm in her fist, and watched the river city shrink behind them — the roofs, the cranes, the navy patrol boats crawling the grey water like beetles.

Somewhere behind them, word was already moving toward Commander Hale. Somewhere ahead, the passes waited, white and patient.

But for one mile — just one mile, with the frost smoking off the fields and Ilo singing something in a language his mother had pretended not to speak and Sefa grudgingly correcting his pronunciation — Wren let herself feel it.

Not hope, exactly. Something rougher and more reliable than hope.

The feeling of a lock giving way. Of a door opening from the wrong side, the side you'd picked your way to, and finding people standing there holding a lantern for you.

Then Duchess the mule stumbled, Teo swore, Sefa snapped an order, and the family-that-didn't-know-it-yet lurched forward into the hills, and Wren spurred her pony to catch up, because that was what couriers did.

They carried things. And they didn't stop.
