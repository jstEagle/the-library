## 5. Chapter 5

Sefa had learned to read Commander Hale the way sailors read weather — not by what he said, but by what he stopped doing.

In the years she'd served under him aboard the *Corvane*, he'd been a man of small economies: no wasted words, no wasted gestures, no wasted men. He ate the same ration biscuit as his crew. He mended his own coat. He remembered the name of every rating on his deck and the name of every prisoner in his hold, and he used both with the same flat courtesy, which was somehow worse than cruelty. Cruelty you could brace against. Courtesy from Hale was a door closing quietly behind you.

Now she stood at the back of a crowd of maybe forty riverfolk in the market square of Lowmarsh, hood up, hands in her pockets, watching that same quiet economy at work three years after she'd deserted it.

The navy had come into town at dawn — two gunboats moored at the ferry landing, a column of marines in grey marching up the mud street with their boots making a sound like slow applause. By midmorning they'd cleared the square and set up a table, and behind the table sat Hale himself, which told Sefa everything about how badly they wanted whatever it was they were looking for. Commanders did not leave their ships for salvage rumors. Commanders left their ships for things that could end governments.

"Your town sits on the Coldharbour Reach," Hale was saying to the assembled boatmen, and his voice carried without effort, the way it always did, pitched precisely so the back row heard every word. "Three weeks ago a wreck on this reach was disturbed. Diving lamps were seen below the waterline at night. Salvage gear was purchased in this market." He let that sit. "I am not interested in punishing salvage. The river belongs to whoever works it. I am interested in one item raised from that wreck. A chest. Lead-lined, courier-sealed, older than most of you."

He turned a page on the table before him. Sefa's stomach tightened. She knew that ledger. She'd seen him use it in ports from here to the Salt Straits — a list of names, boats, debts, habits. Hale never arrived anywhere uninformed. The interrogation was theater; the file was the weapon.

"Doran Vetch," he read. "Boatman. Operates a punt out of the eastern staithe. Purchased lamp oil and two coils of new rope on the fourteenth." He looked up and found the man in the crowd without searching for him, the way a hawk finds a vole. "Master Vetch. Step forward, please."

A big man in an oilskin went, moving like a man walking to his own funeral. Sefa recognized him distantly — everyone who worked the Reach knew Doran Vetch, a decent sort who ferried kids across to the reed islands in summer and never charged them.

"You sold rope and oil," Hale said, "to a diver."

"I sell rope and oil to half the river," Vetch said. His voice cracked on *river* and steadied itself. "I don't keep a book of faces."

"No," Hale agreed mildly. "You don't. But your daughter does. She keeps accounts for the chandlery. Very neat hand." He turned another page. "On the fourteenth she recorded a sale to a party of two. One older man, grey, heavy build. One younger, dark-haired, described as quick-moving. Paid in mixed coin, including two pieces struck in Vesperan mint."

The square went very still. Twenty years since Vesper drowned, twenty years since its name came off every chart and treaty and gravestone, and the word still landed in a crowd like a dropped anchor.

"I can't help what coin comes into my till," Vetch said.

"Of course not." Hale closed the ledger. And then, in the tone of a man discussing cargo weights, he said: "Where did the diver go?"

"I don't know."

"Where is the chest?"

"I don't know that either."

Hale nodded slowly, as if this were a reasonable answer, as if he'd expected nothing else. He looked past Vetch at the marines flanking the table. "Sergeant. The boats."

Two marines took Vetch by the arms — gently, Sefa noted, gently, because Hale's men were always gentle right up until they weren't — and walked him down toward the landing where the gunboats lay. The crowd didn't understand yet. Sefa did. Her mouth had gone dry before Vetch even reached the water.

*He's going to make an example,* she thought, and the thought arrived with the old cold weight settling behind her sternum, the feeling she used to get on the *Corvane*'s quarterdeck when Hale would pause too long over a sentence. *He's going to do it in front of everyone, and he's going to be polite about it.*

"Commander—" someone in the crowd started, and Hale raised one hand, and the voice died.

At the landing, the marines put Vetch in a punt. Not a gunboat. A punt, his own punt, the one with the faded blue paint and the cushion his wife had sewn. They tied his hands in front of him — considerately, so he could hold something — and set him adrift on the slack brown water ten yards off the staithe.

Then a marine with a firelock stepped to the edge of the dock and shot him twice through the chest.

The sound rolled up the Reach and came back off the bluffs. Vetch fell sideways into the bilge water and the punt drifted, turning slowly, and the crowd made a sound Sefa had heard only once before, in a port called Greywater, after a similar morning — not screaming, not yet, just a long collective exhale, forty people breathing in at once and forgetting to let it out.

"The chest was raised from a wreck the navy has records of," Hale said to the square, conversationally, as though resuming a lecture interrupted by a minor noise. "It contains documents belonging to the Crown. Anyone who has seen it, held it, moved it, or knows where it lies will report to my officers before nightfall. Anyone who does not—" he glanced at the drifting punt, and corrected course slightly, "—will have made a choice, and choices have costs. That is all. Thank you for your time."

He gathered his ledger. He thanked the harbormaster by name. He complimented the bread at the market stall where he'd bought his breakfast, and paid for it, and tipped.

Sefa walked away before her legs decided for her.

* * *

She found the rest of them that evening in the back room of the Drowned Bell, a tavern on the canal side where the ceiling leaked tannin-stained water onto a floor nobody mopped. Jonah sat with his forearms on the table and his eyes somewhere in the middle distance. Teo was taking apart a lock mechanism with the focus of a man pretending the world contained nothing but pins and springs. Wren sat cross-legged on the bench, very still, in the way teenagers go still when they're trying not to look scared. Ilo was counting money he clearly wasn't seeing.

They'd heard. Of course they'd heard. Half the city had heard; the other half had heard it wrong.

"They executed a boatman," Sefa said, sitting down hard. "Doran Vetch. In front of his neighbors. For selling rope."

"We know," Jonah said quietly.

"Do you?" She heard her own voice come out sharper than she meant, and didn't pull it back. "Because I've watched this before. Three years on his ship. Greywater, I told some of you. You think that was weather? That was Hale clearing a smuggling route. Eleven dead, and he wrote letters of condolence afterward. In his own hand. To the families." She pressed her knuckles against the table. "He doesn't kill because he's angry. He kills because it's efficient, and then he's kind to you, and the kindness is worse, because it tells you he's already filed you under *acceptable losses* and moved on."

Teo's hands had gone still over the lock parts.

"He's not hunting a chest," Sefa said. "Not really. Chests sink. Papers rot. Whatever's in that thing, he needs it gone in a way that leaves no copies, no witnesses, no story. Which means—" she looked at Jonah, and made herself say it, "—which means we're not carrying evidence anymore. We're carrying the reason he exists. Men like Hale are built by the things they helped bury."

Jonah looked at her for a long moment. Then he reached under the table, unlocked the false bottom of the crate they'd bolted there, and lifted out the chest.

Up close, in lamplight, the courier seal of Vesper caught the flame and threw it back dull gold. Sefa had seen that seal once before, years ago, on a dispatch case in the *Corvane*'s signal room — a case that had come aboard sealed, and gone into the captain's safe, and whose contents she'd never been cleared to know. She'd told herself for three years that she hadn't wanted to know.

"Teo got the inner compartment open last night," Jonah said. "Ilo's seen it. Now you should too. All of you. If we're doing this, we do it knowing."

He opened the lid.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, lay papers. Dozens of them. Fleet manifests. Coal requisitions. Crew rosters — Sefa saw the words *muster roll, passenger manifest* and felt the floor tilt, because passenger manifests did not belong with warship paperwork, not unless civilians had been aboard. On top of the stack, a single sheet, heavier stock, bearing the Crown's own cipher at the head and a signature at the bottom that even Wren recognized, because the name was on coins.

Teo read it aloud, because someone had to, in a voice that started steady and didn't stay that way.

*"By order of the Admiralty, acting under authority of the Crown: the vessels of the Vesperan evacuation fleet, being compromised, are to be sunk at their moorings with all souls aboard. No rescue operations are to be mounted. No survivors are to be received. This order is to be executed with discretion and expunged from record following completion."*

Silence. The tannin water dripped somewhere above them, patient as a clock.

"With all souls aboard," Wren repeated. Her voice was very small. "That was — those were the evacuation ships. My grandmother talked about the evacuation fleet. Everyone's family was on those ships. That was how you got out." She looked up, and her face had gone white and fierce at once. "There wasn't a storm. There wasn't a plague. They sank the lifeboats."

"They sank the nation," Ilo said. He'd gone grey. "And then they erased it, and then they spent twenty years making sure nobody went digging. And now a diver pulled the receipt out of the river." He laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "No wonder they sent Hale."

Sefa stared at the signature. Somewhere behind her eyes, unasked-for, rose a memory: Hale on the quarterdeck of the *Corvane*, three years ago, reading orders by lamplight with that same flat courtesy, then burning the pages in the brazier and telling her, kindly, *Some cargoes are better not logged, Lieutenant.* She'd obeyed. She'd told herself obedience wasn't complicity. She'd deserted anyway, and told herself desertion settled it.

It hadn't settled anything. It had just moved her from one side of the water to the other.

"That's why the boatman died," she said finally. "Not for the rope. Because Hale needed the town afraid enough that nobody remembers faces. Nobody remembers a grey-haired diver and a girl with quick hands. He's erasing us the way they erased Vesper. Same method. Smaller scale." She looked around the table. "So we get smaller than his scale. We move at night, we split the route, we leave no pattern. And we move fast, because he doesn't do slow sieges. He'll seal the southern roads within days, and when he does, the only way over the Kestrels will be the high passes — and the snows are early this year."

Jonah was already nodding, already reaching for the chart tube against the wall. But it was Wren who spoke next, and her voice had changed — steadied, hardened, fourteen years old and suddenly ancient.

"My grandmother used to say the truth about Vesper was like seeds in floodwater," she said. "You can drown the field. The seeds wait." She touched the lid of the chest, lightly, the way you touch a coffin. "We're the floodwater going the other direction."

Outside, rain began on the canal, soft and steady, and somewhere south of the city — Sefa was sure of it, the way you're sure of weather in your bones — riders were already on the roads, carrying Hale's lists, checking names, closing doors.

They had perhaps three days. Maybe less.

"Pack tonight," Jonah said. "We leave at dawn."
