## 6. Chapter 6

The city of Greywater smelled different before dawn — coal smoke and river rot and the sour sweetness of the distilleries along the quay — and Jonah had learned to read it like a chart. This morning it told him two things: the tide would turn in an hour, and someone had been burning paper.

He stood in the doorway of Ilo's stable yard with a cup of chicory going cold in his hand and watched the smoke rise from the customs house district. Not a chimney fire. Too much of it, too black, too steady. Burning paper made a particular column, greasy and reluctant to climb, and there were three such columns standing over the administrative quarter like bad omens.

"They're clearing the ledgers," Sefa said beside him. She'd come out without being called; she slept like a cat aboard ship, one ear always open. "When Hale seals a port, he burns the harbor manifests first. Nothing in writing that says who came or went."

"You'd know."

"I'd know." She didn't flinch from it anymore. That was new, and Jonah wasn't sure yet whether it was strength or just exhaustion with pretending.

Below them, in the yard, Ilo was cinching the last strap on a mule named Biscuit, who had opinions about being loaded at four in the morning and expressed them by trying to bite Teo's hat off his head. The false load was Ilo's masterpiece: forty pounds of salted fish packed around a lead-lined chest wrapped in oilcloth, the whole arrangement built into a double-bottomed pannier that would pass any casual inspection short of taking the mule apart with tools. Teo had wanted to weld it shut. Wren had pointed out, reasonably, that if they ever needed to get at the contents quickly, they'd be doing it while someone was shooting at them, and maybe welding was a bad idea. Teo had sulked about it for a day, which for Teo was practically a religious observance.

Jonah drained the chicory and went down to help.

* * *

They moved out as the sky went from iron to pewter, five people, six mules, and one chest that could hang every one of them. Ilo led, because Ilo knew the back ways out of Greywater the way other men knew their own kitchens. He took them down the tannery lane where the stink covered everything, across the drainage flats where the city's filth ran seaward, and onto the levee road heading south, all without passing a single lantern that mattered.

Wren rode rear guard on the last mule, which was against every rule Ilo had about children and rearguards, and which Wren had ignored with the serene confidence of someone who had never once been fourteen and wrong about anything. She kept glancing back at the city. At the columns of smoke.

"What are they burning?" she asked when Jonah dropped back to check her girth strap.

"Paper," he said.

"I know it's paper. What's *on* the paper?"

He tightened the strap a half-turn he didn't need to tighten. "Names," he said. "Dates. Shipments. The ordinary machinery of running a country." He met her eyes. "And once it's ash, none of it ever happened."

She was quiet for a hundred yards. Then: "That's what they did to Vesper, isn't it. Not just the sinking. The erasing after."

Jonah looked ahead at the road, gray and empty and running south between flooded fields. "That's what they do," he said. "It's cheaper than admitting anything."

* * *

The southern roads were the problem, and everyone knew it.

Greywater sat at the knee of the Kestrel River, and from it three roads fanned out: the coast road east, the lowland road south through the hill country, and the old mining road west into the foothills of the range itself. Any sane fugitive took the coast road — flat, fast, full of smugglers' coves and friends of Ilo's. Any sane navy commander sealed the coast road first.

By midmorning they learned that Hale had done both, and more.

Ilo's cousin Dree ran a chandlery at the last crossroads before the marshes, a woman with forearms like hawser rope and a talent for hearing things. She met them at her back door with flour on her hands and bad news in her mouth.

"Coast road's got a checkpoint at Saltmere Bridge since yesterday noon. Not the usual toll boys — actual navy, blue coats, and they're turning everyone back and logging names." She wiped her hands. "Lowland road's worse. They've posted a notice. Salvage embargo, whole river district, reward for information regarding unlicensed recovery operations. There's a description on it."

"Of what?" Jonah asked, though he knew.

"A chest," Dree said. "Lead-lined, courier-marked. And a diver. Big fellow, scarred hands, answers to Coldharbour." She looked at him without pity, which he appreciated. Pity would have been worse. "Reward's enough to buy a boatyard. You know what that means."

"It means every man who owes me money is now worth something by selling me instead."

"It means that," she agreed. "West road's still open as of last night. But Jonah — west is the mountains. West is snow inside a month, and the passes close, and nobody comes over the Kestrels after the closes. Nobody. Not smugglers, not pilgrims, not fools."

"We're aware," Ilo said.

"Are you?" Dree looked at each of them in turn, and lingered on Wren. "Because I've sold supplies to mountain parties for twenty years, and the ones who come back are the ones who turned around early."

Jonah bought salt, lamp oil, and a second coil of rope, and paid with money he couldn't account for honestly, and thanked her, and they went west.

* * *

The lowland road south would have been the smart route even with the notice posted — notices were paper, and paper could be walked past. But at the second crossroads they found the proof that Hale wasn't running this like paper.

A troop of cavalry, eight riders, watering horses at the ford. Blue coats. And behind them, roped to a post outside the tollhouse, a man Jonah recognized distantly — a barge hand off the *Kindly Ann*, a quiet fellow who'd once sold Jonah eels. He was alive. For now. A sergeant was reading him questions off a board while the troopers watched with the boredom of men who had done this eleven times today already.

They lay in the drainage ditch a quarter mile off, mules' noses held, and watched.

"He'll talk," Sefa said quietly. "Not because he's weak. Because Hale's interrogators ask questions in an order designed so that by the fourth question you've already answered the fifth without noticing. Everyone talks. The only variable is how much useful noise they make first."

"So we go around?"

"There's no around. Look at the ground." She was right — the ford sat in a bowl of open pasture, sight lines clear to the horizon in every direction. "We go away. We go west, up the mining road, and we put altitude between us and that man before he finishes with the eel seller and starts on the crossroads."

They backed out of the ditch and went west, and Jonah did not look back at the tollhouse, and hated himself a little for how easy not looking was.

* * *

The mining road climbed.

That was its whole character — it climbed, and it kept climbing, switchbacking up through terraced fields gone wild since the mines closed, through stands of birch turning gold at the edges, past the rusting headframes of shafts that had given out a generation ago. The air changed within the first hour. It thinned and cooled and sharpened, and by afternoon Jonah could see his breath, and the mules were blowing hard, and the river valley below had become a silver thread in a green-brown quilt.

Nobody spoke much. Climbing ate words.

But Jonah felt it anyway, and he could see the others feeling it too — that specific lightness that comes when the decision is finally behind you. For weeks there had been the choice, hovering: take the chest or melt it, find Maren or run, assemble a crew or go alone. Every hour of choosing had been its own small weight. Now the choices were spent. They were committed. The road went up, and the snows were coming, and Commander Hale owned every road behind them, and there was nothing left to decide except to keep walking.

It should have felt like terror. Mostly it did. But underneath the terror, shamelessly, was something that felt almost like joy.

He caught Teo grinning at nothing, wiping his hands on a rag, and knew the engineer felt it too. Teo had spent his whole life fixing other people's machines in other people's yards, competent and invisible. Now he was the keeper of a secret that could crack a nation, walking into mountains with people who needed him. "Don't look so happy," Jonah said.

"Can't help it." Teo shrugged. "Best job I ever had."

"You haven't been shot at yet."

"That's what I keep telling myself to stay humble."

* * *

They camped that night in the shell of a mine office, stone walls, no roof, a floor of slate chips. Ilo set pickets on a rotation — himself, Sefa, Jonah, and Wren, who had argued her way onto the list by pointing out correctly that she slept least of any of them anyway.

Jonah took the middle watch, midnight to three, and sat with his back to the cold wall watching the stars wheel over the ridge line. Below, far off, a light moved along the valley road. Then another. Lanterns, or lamps, moving in formation, unhurried, methodical. Not searching the mountains. Searching the roads. Sealing them one by one, the way a man closes a net not by chasing the fish but by lifting the edges.

Sefa relieved him at three and followed his glance down the valley. "He's not hurrying," she said. "That's the thing people get wrong about Hale. They expect a hammer. He's not a hammer. He's water. He finds the low ground and fills it, and eventually you're swimming whether you meant to be or not."

"You served under him a long time."

"Six years." She settled onto the watch stone, rifle across her knees. "Get some sleep, Coldharbour. Tomorrow we cross the tree line, and after that it's just weather and rock, and I'm told your family has history with both."

He paused. "What's that supposed to mean?"

"Wren talks when she thinks people are asleep," Sefa said. "Go to bed."

* * *

The next morning the mining road ended where the mines had, at a collapsed adit and a shrine some long-dead foreman had carved into the rock face — a rough-hewn figure with a lamp, offerings of rusted nails and bird skulls still wedged in the cracks. Beyond it the road became a track, and the track became a suggestion, and the suggestion went up into country where the birch gave way to pine and the pine gave way to nothing at all.

Ilo stood at the tree line looking up at the bare slopes above, at the white already dusting the high shoulders of the range, and for once the smuggler had nothing clever to say.

"Well," he said finally. "No refunds."

They went up.

And it was somewhere in that first hour above the trees, lungs burning, the whole grey world of the river country spread out below them like a map of everything they'd escaped, that Wren — riding point now, because she read trails the way Teo read engines — stopped so abruptly that Biscuit walked into her.

"Jonah." Her voice was strange. Flat. "You need to look at this."

He climbed up to her. She was pointing at a cairn beside the track, one of the hundreds of waymarkers the old miners had stacked, except this one had been restacked recently. Fresh-turned stones. And wedged under the capstone, folded once, a piece of paper weighted against the wind.

Jonah unfolded it. The handwriting was careful, an old woman's handwriting, letters formed like someone who had learned penmanship as a discipline and never let it lapse.

*If you are reading this, you chose the mountains, which means you are either brave or cornered, and with Coldharbour it is usually both.* A pause in the ink, then: *The fast pass is watched. The slow pass may already be closed. There is a third way, but it is not a way — it is a crossing, and you will know it when the river speaks. Trust the child with the maps. Trust the deserter with the gun. Trust the smuggler, but count the silver.*

At the bottom, unsigned, a single mark: the courier's knot, the same seal that was wrapped in oilcloth forty pounds deep in Biscuit's pannier.

Maren. Dead a week, buried in a floodplain tenement, and still ahead of them on the trail, leaving instructions in stones.

"She knew," Wren whispered. "She knew which way we'd have to go. Before any of it."

Jonah looked up the slope, at the white shoulders of the Kestrel Range, at the third way hiding somewhere among them that wasn't a way at all but a crossing, whatever that meant. Behind him, far below, the valley lights were still closing roads. Ahead of him, the river waited, and apparently it intended to speak.

"Break camp's over," he said, folding the note into his coat. "We're not following her map anymore. We're following her ghost. Move up — and somebody tell Ilo to count the silver."
