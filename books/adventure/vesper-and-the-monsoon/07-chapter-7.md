## 7. Chapter 7

The river at Corren's Ford was the color of old pewter, and it smelled like iron and rotting reeds, and Wren hated it on sight.

Not the river itself. Rivers were fine. Rivers were honest — they went where they went, and if you fell in one you drowned, simple as that, no lies about it. What Wren hated was the crossing. Crossings meant people, and people meant questions, and questions were the thing she'd been dodging since she was nine years old, when a harbor watchman in Saltmere had grabbed her wrist and asked her name and she'd learned, very quickly, that a lie told fast enough could be truer than the truth.

"Two coppers a head," said the boy minding the toll rope. "Four for anything with legs that eats."

Ilo counted out coins from his sleeve with the bored air of a man paying for nothing in particular. The mules stood hip-deep in the shallows, ears twitching at the midges, the false load on the third one — the one with the chest sewn into its panniers under forty pounds of dried fish — swaying gently like it weighed exactly what it was supposed to weigh. Wren had sewn those seams herself, double-stitched, waxed thread, and she'd checked them three times before dawn, but her eyes kept going back to them anyway. Checking. Always checking.

"That's the last ford before the hill country," Sefa murmured beside her. She'd pulled her hood forward so the navy cut of her jaw was mostly shadow. "After this, we're in shepherd land for six days. No roads worth the name."

"And no ferries," Jonah said. He stood at the water's edge with his boots off, trousers rolled to the knee, looking across at the far bank like he was measuring something no one else could see. He did that a lot. Wren had decided it wasn't showing off; it was just how his mind worked, always sounding depths other people didn't know were there.

The ferry itself was a flat barge on a greased cable, big enough for two mules and six people if nobody breathed wrong. The ferryman came down from his hut above the bank while they loaded, and Wren clocked him the way she clocked everyone: fifty-ish, heavy through the shoulders, hands like knotted rope, a gray beard trimmed short and a walk that favored the left leg. Old soldier or old laborer, hard to say. A wool coat gone shiny at the elbows. And around his neck, on a leather cord, half-hidden by his collar —

Wren's stomach dropped.

She knew that pendant. Everyone who'd ever worked the river docks of Saltmere knew that pendant, because Vesperan river-folk had worn them for luck before there was any such thing as luck to be had, a little stamped disc of tin or silver with a wave curling over a keel. Half the drowned nation's survivors had melted theirs down for bread money in the bad years. This man hadn't. This man wore his where anyone could see it, which meant either he didn't care who knew what he was, or he wanted someone to ask.

Nobody asked. That was the rule now. Twenty years since Vesper drowned, twenty years since the maps got redrawn and the histories got rewritten and the word *Vesperan* became something you said quietly, if you said it at all.

Wren kept her mouth shut and helped load the mules.

* * *

The crossing started fine.

The cable sang, the barge slid, the water slid faster underneath, brown-green and cold. Teo sat on the gunwale with his arms wrapped around his knees, still greenish from three days of bad food, and Ilo fed the mules dried apple slices to keep them calm, and Jonah stood at the bow watching the far bank come closer, and Wren let herself think, for maybe ten whole seconds, *we're going to make this crossing, easy, boring, done.*

Then the ferryman looked at the third mule.

He wasn't staring at the false load — Wren's stitching was good enough that even a suspicious eye saw fish. He was staring lower, at the drag strap, at the brass buckle Wren had bought secondhand because new brass drew eyes. Secondhand brass from a Saltmere junk stall. Brass that had come, apparently, off a Vesperan boat, because stamped into the buckle's face, worn nearly smooth but not quite, was the same mark as the ferryman's pendant: the wave over the keel.

The ferryman stopped hauling the hand-over-hand line. The barge drifted, groaning against the cable, and the current took hold and swung the nose downstream, and nobody said anything for the length of three heartbeats.

"Where," the ferryman said, in a voice like gravel poured slow, "did you get that buckle."

"Flea market," Wren said, fast, easy, the lie already dressed and walking. "Saltmere. Guy wanted four coppers, I gave him three—"

"I know every maker's stamp on this river." He turned. His eyes were pale gray, the color of the water, and they went from the buckle to Wren's face and stayed there. "That's Halven & Sons, Corren town, closed nineteen years. They only ever sold to river families." He stepped toward the mule. "Off my cousin's boat. Off my cousin's boat that went down with everything else."

"Friend," Ilo said, all warmth and open palms, "if we'd known it was sentimental—"

"It's not sentimental." The ferryman's hand closed on the pannier frame, and Wren saw his knuckles whiten, and she understood, with the cold clarity of someone who'd spent her life reading strangers, that this man was not angry. He was shaking. "It's mine. It's ours. Everything that went down in that flood belongs to us, and you're carrying it across my river like it's cargo."

Jonah moved — not fast, not threatening, just there, between the ferryman and the mule, the way a wall is between you and weather. "It's dried fish," he said. "We're haulers. We're sorry about your cousin. Truly. But we're on a schedule, and we paid our toll."

"You paid to cross." The ferryman didn't raise his voice. Somehow that was worse. "You didn't pay for what you're carrying."

And then he reached past Jonah — quick, stronger than he looked — and tore the pannier flap open, and the false seam Wren had double-stitched with waxed thread parted along the top like it was nothing, because of course it was nothing, because everything comes apart eventually, that was practically the family motto of the whole world.

The chest sat there in its nest of sacking. Lead-lined, river-scoured, and on its lid, plain as a shout, the seal of Vesper.

* * *

Nobody moved.

The current pushed the barge against the cable and the cable groaned and the midges whined and the water went on being water, indifferent, ancient. On the near bank the toll boy had stopped chewing whatever he was chewing. Teo had gone the color of the river foam. Sefa's hand had drifted, casual as breathing, to the knife at her belt, and Wren saw it and put her own hand over Sefa's wrist — gently, barely touching — and Sefa's fingers uncurled.

Because here was the thing Wren understood, standing on a drifting barge with a stranger's grief coming off him like heat off a stove: you couldn't fight this. You could fight soldiers, thieves, tax men, even Hale's navy, in the sense that fighting them was possible and sometimes survivable. But you couldn't fight a man whose whole family was at the bottom of a flooded valley, holding the proof of it in his fists.

"This is mine too," the ferryman said. His voice cracked on *mine*, and he hated that it cracked, Wren could see him hating it. "My mother. My brother. My brother's wife and the baby. Forty-one thousand people, and the government says there was never any such place. Says it was a dam failure. Says we're liars if we remember." He laughed, one dry note with no humor anywhere in it. "Twenty years I've run this ferry. Every year fewer people cross who remember. Every year I think, soon there'll be nobody left to argue with them, and then they'll have won without ever firing a shot."

"We know," Wren said.

Everyone looked at her. She hadn't planned to speak. Her mouth had just gone ahead on its own, the way it did.

"We know what it says," she said. "We've seen inside it."

"Wren." Jonah's voice, low. A warning or a question; with Jonah it was usually both.

"No, look at him." She turned to face the crew, and her heart was going like a trapped bird, but her voice came out steady, and she was almost proud of that. "Look at all of you pretending we can pass through the world with this thing like it's a box of nails. He's not going to report us. He's not going to sell us. He's the one person on this whole river who won't."

"He could be working for Hale," Sefa said quietly. "Grief makes good bait."

"Grief makes good *people*," Wren said. "Mostly."

The ferryman hadn't moved. His hands were still on the chest, flat now, the way you'd rest them on a coffin. "Who are you," he said to Wren, "to talk about my grief?"

And there it was. The question she'd been dodging since Saltmere, since age nine, since forever. The lie was right there, dressed and ready: *nobody, sir, just a hired pickpocket, just cargo.* Easy. Fast. Truer than the truth, almost.

"My grandmother came down the Vessa when the fleet burned," Wren said. "She was fourteen. She never talked about it except once a year, when she'd cook this fish soup with too much pepper and cry into it, and she'd tell me the names of streets in a city that isn't on any map anymore. Marlow Street. The Lantern Steps. She made me memorize them like they were real places, because—" Wren's voice caught, and she let it, for once, instead of swallowing it. "Because they were. They are. She died two winters ago, and I'm the only one left who knows those street names, and I don't even have blood claim to them, I'm just — I'm what's left. So yeah. I know a little bit about carrying things that aren't mine."

The river ran. The cable groaned. Somewhere upstream a bird cried out, harsh and lonely.

"Vessa," the ferryman said softly. Not *Vesper*. The old word, the river-word, the one the survivors used among themselves. He said it the way you'd say a dead person's name.

"Vessa," Wren agreed.

He looked at her for a long time. Then at Jonah, at Sefa with her hidden knife and her deserter's eyes, at Teo shivering on the gunwale, at Ilo, who had gone uncharacteristically quiet, which from Ilo was practically a confession of feeling.

"Forty-one thousand," the ferryman said again. "You know how I keep count? My mother kept a ledger. Every family on the Vessa, every boat, every shop. She was the one who wrote things down — that was our trade, writing things down, carrying them. Couriers. My mother's mother was a courier on the river run." His thumb moved over the seal on the chest's lid, tracing the wave, the keel. "When the water came up, the ledger went down with her. So now the count lives in my head and nowhere else, and some nights I can't sleep wondering what happens to it when I die."

"Then don't let it live in your head," Wren said. "Come with us."

"What?"

"The chest is going over the Kestrel Range. To people who'll read it out loud. All of it — the order, the names, the ships, whoever signed it. If your count's in there, if your mother's ledger matters, if any of it matters, it stops being a secret in three weeks." She swallowed. "Or you can take the chest off us right now, dump it in your hut, and sit on it alone until Hale's men find you, because they *will* find you, because they're sealing every road south of the hills, and a man wearing that pendant with a Vesperan chest in his house is the easiest catch they'll ever make."

"Wren," Jonah said again, sharper now.

"He deserves to know the odds," Wren said. "Everybody keeps treating grief like it's fragile. It's not fragile. It's the strongest thing on this barge."

* * *

They tied up at the far bank, under a willow gone gold with early frost, and the ferryman — Brannic, his name was, though he took a long time deciding whether to give it — made tea on a little clay stove while the mules drank and the crew stood around in the awkward silence of people who'd nearly fought each other an hour ago and didn't know what to do with their hands now.

He didn't come with them. Wren had known he wouldn't before she asked; some men keep their post the way other men keep their vows. But he sat with the chest between his boots for a long while, drinking his tea, and finally he said, "There's a road up the east side of the hills. Shepherds' track. Navy maps show it fading out at the tree line, so they don't bother watching it — they think nothing with wheels goes there. Nothing with wheels doesn't. Mules manage."

"You know this how?" Sefa asked.

"Because I ferry their wool down every autumn, and their salt and lamp oil up. Or I did." He looked into his cup. "This autumn they stopped coming down. Word is Hale's outriders are on the passes early this year. Word is he's looking for something."

Nobody said anything. The wind moved through the willow.

Brannic got up, went to his hut, and came back with a strip of oilcloth and a stub of charcoal pencil, and he wrote — Wren watched over his shoulder, and the handwriting was beautiful, a courier's hand, loops and slants like water — three lines and a name and a mark that was the wave over the keel, drawn small and precise.

"Show this at the shepherd stations. Aldith at the first station, her sister at the second. They're my wife's kin. They'll feed you, point you true, and keep their mouths shut, because they owe me and because they're Vessan and because that's reason enough." He held the oilcloth out — then pulled it back an inch. "One condition."

"Name it," Jonah said.

"When it's read out loud, wherever it's read — my family's names get said. All of them. Mother kept the ledger; somebody has to keep the reading." His gray eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "Say them loud. Say them like they're street names in a city that exists."

Wren took the letter. "Marlow Street," she said. "The Lantern Steps."

Something moved through his face — pain and relief tangled up together, indistinguishable. "You really did memorize them."

"She made me. Twice a year. I used to hate it."

"She was keeping you," Brannic said simply. "That's what remembering is. It's how the dead keep us."

He shook Jonah's hand, and Sefa's, and clapped Ilo on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him, and when he got to Wren he didn't shake her hand at all. He pressed the tin pendant from around his own neck into her palm and folded her fingers over it, and his hands were warm and huge and shaking slightly, and neither of them said anything, because there wasn't anything that would fit.

Then they loaded the mules, and Wren re-sewed the pannier seam with fresh waxed thread, better this time, and they took the east track up into the hills as the light went long and amber behind them.

It was Teo, walking beside her, who finally said what they were all thinking. "Hale's outriders are already on the passes. Early. Looking for something."

"Yeah," Wren said. Her thumb found the tin pendant in her pocket, the wave, the keel, worn smooth by twenty years of another family's grief. "And we're three days from the foothills with a chest full of the only truth anybody's ever going to get."

Ahead of them the Kestrel Range stood up against the sky, white-capped already, too early, too much snow — and on the lowest shoulder of the nearest pass, small with distance but unmistakable, something glinted. Metal. Moving. A line of it, patient as a noose, working its way along the ridge.

Wren counted the glints twice, hoping she was wrong.

She wasn't wrong.
