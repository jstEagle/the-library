## 8. Chapter 8

Sefa heard the checkpoint before she saw it — the particular rhythm of a navy picket doing its job badly: too much talking, not enough watching. She held up a fist and the mule train stopped on the switchback below the pass road, breath steaming in the gray morning.

"What is it?" Jonah asked, coming up beside her.

"Smoke from cook fires. Too many." She counted again by habit, the way she'd been trained to count mastheads. "Six, maybe seven. That's a full picket, not a toll post."

Ilo swung down from the lead mule and squinted up the road. "The Kestrel toll station. It's been run by two old men and a dog for as long as I've hauled through here."

"Then someone told them to stop being two old men and a dog," Sefa said.

She felt it settle over her then, that cold familiar weight — the sense of being inside a machine she knew too well. Hale didn't scatter patrols at random. He built nets: a picket here, a rider line there, each one placed so that whatever slipped through the first gap would walk into the second. She had helped him build such nets for six years. She had stood at checkpoints exactly like this one, checking papers with one hand while her other hand rested near her sidearm, and she had been good at it. That was the part that never stopped surprising her, how good she'd been.

"We go around," Teo said. "There's a shepherd track above the tree line—"

"In this frost? With four mules and a chest?" Ilo shook his head. "We'd lose two days and probably a mule, and the chest goes into a ravine."

"So we talk our way through," Wren said. She was crouched by the false-bottomed load, checking the straps out of pure nervousness. "I'm good at talking."

"You're good at lying to customs men who are bored," Ilo said. "These are soldiers looking for something specific. Different animal."

Jonah looked at Sefa. He didn't say anything. He just looked, the way he did — patient, unhurried, like a man waiting at the bottom of a dive for you to make your own decision about coming down.

"I can get us through," she said.

* * *

They staged it half a mile below the station, behind a rockfall that hid them from the road. Sefa pulled off her oilskin coat and took out the bundle she hadn't opened since the night she deserted: her dress uniform jacket, folded tight, wrapped in waxed canvas against the river damp. The navy blue had gone dull but the braid was intact. Lieutenant's bars. Her name tape. The crest of the Riverine Command on the shoulder.

Nobody spoke while she put it on. Wren stared. Teo looked away, which was its own kind of courtesy.

"You kept it," Jonah said finally.

"I kept everything. That was the problem." She buttoned the collar. Her fingers remembered the sequence without her asking them to. "My credentials are real. My authorization codes are real. As far as any clerk at that station knows, Lieutenant Sefa Vane of Riverine Command is conducting a requisition inspection of cargo moving north under commercial cover. It's even true, if you squint."

"And when they radio it up the chain?" Ilo asked.

"They won't. Pickets don't confirm routine inspections upward; they log them downward. By the time anyone reads the entry, we'll be through the col and the weather will have closed the passes behind us." She said it steadily, because saying it steadily was how you made it true. But she could feel the flaw in it the way you feel a hairline crack in a hull — invisible, load-bearing, fatal at depth. "There's one cost. Once my name goes into their log attached to this convoy, I'm not a deserter anymore. I'm a deserter operating openly. There'll be a warrant with my face on it by nightfall. No more walking into a garrison town. No more navy hospitals if I take a bullet."

"That was already true," Jonah said quietly.

"It was probable. Now it's certain." She shrugged the jacket straight. "Certainty is cleaner."

Wren stepped forward. "Give me your cap. If you're playing an officer, you need a runner — some kid carrying your dispatch case. Officers travel with runners. Everyone knows that."

Sefa looked at her. Fifteen years old, all elbows and nerve, claiming blood she might or might not have. "You understand what you'd be walking into?"

"I understand I'm the only one of us who looks harmless." Wren grinned, and it was such a young grin that Sefa's chest hurt.

"All right," she said. "Runner."

* * *

The Kestrel toll station sat where the road pinched between a granite wall and a drop into the white water of the Vail. It had been built as a customs house a century ago and never improved: stone walls, a plank gate across the road, a lean-to stable, and now seven soldiers in navy gray who had clearly arrived recently enough that their cook fire still smoked wet wood.

The sentry at the gate saw the uniform before he saw her face, and Sefa watched the change come over him — shoulders back, chin level, the whole body reorganizing itself around authority. She had worn that effect like a coat for six years. Putting it back on fit worse than the jacket did.

"Lieutenant," the sentry said. "Didn't expect Riverine this far up."

"Riverine goes where the river does, corporal." She let her eyes move past him, cataloguing with deliberate slowness: two soldiers at the gate, three at the fire, one on the roof with a signal flag case, one holding horses in the stable yard. Seven. A lieutenant inspecting cargo rated exactly this much attention and no more. "Who commands?"

"Sergeant Brakkis, ma'am." He hesitated. "We're under orders to inspect all northbound freight. Papers and cargo both."

"Then you'll be pleased to see me, because I'm here to do it properly instead of badly." She held out her credential wallet. The corporal opened it, read her name, and something flickered across his face — recognition, maybe, or just the reflexive unease of a man meeting a rank he couldn't argue with. "Convoy of five, four mules, commercial salvage goods moving north under contract. You'll log the inspection under my authority. Sergeant Brakkis can countersign."

"Yes, ma'am." He turned and bellowed, "Sergeant! Officer up the road!"

While they waited, Sefa made herself look at the soldiers. Young. Cold. One of them had a Vesperan coin strung on a cord around his neck — a drowned-nation penny, sold in river towns as a luck charm to people who no longer remembered why it was unlucky. She thought of the smuggler ship, the *Cormorant's Daughter*, and made herself keep looking.

Brakkis came out wiping his hands on his coat. He was forty-odd, heavy through the shoulders, with the flat competent eyes of a man who had done garrison work his whole career and resented every posting. He read her orders twice.

"Requisition inspection," he said. "Northbound cargo. You have manifest authority?"

"I have field authority, sergeant, which covers manifests." She let a fraction of ice into her voice. "If you'd prefer to hold the convoy while a written request travels down to Command and a reply travels back — four days, in this weather — I'm happy to stand here with you and watch the snow close the pass for both of us."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not suspicion exactly. Calculation. And underneath it, faintly, the thing she'd been afraid of: a soldier who had been briefed. "What's the cargo?" he asked.

"Salvage scrap and sealed freight. Lead-lined cases, shipping manifests intact." She said it evenly. "You're welcome to open the freight cases yourself, sergeant, if you want to explain to the quartermaster corps why a bonded shipment got broken open at a toll station by a garrison detail acting outside its mandate."

That landed. She watched it land. Brakkis was a career man, and career men feared paperwork more than bullets.

"The mule with the false bottom," he said. "Open it."

Her pulse went once, hard, and settled. "That's the bonded case. Seal's intact. Break it and the whole shipment's void, and the loss comes out of the nearest officer's record." She paused. "Yours outranks mine locally, sergeant. Your call."

Behind her, Wren shifted the dispatch case from one hand to the other, a small restless sound, perfectly ordinary.

Brakkis looked at the mule. Looked at the seal — Teo had forged it beautifully, a wax disc stamped with a shipping house's mark, unbroken. Looked at her bars, her braid, her name tape. Then he stepped back and waved the gate open.

"Log it," he told the corporal. "Lieutenant Vane, Riverine Command, inspected and passed. Countersigned." He glanced at her. "Weather's turning, ma'am. I'd move fast."

"We intend to."

She walked the convoy through the gate at a pace that was brisk but not fleeing, past the cook fire, past the boy with the coin necklace, past the plank wall where a notice had been nailed up fresh — paper still stiff, ink barely dry. She read it without turning her head, the way you read things peripherally, a skill drilled into every officer:

**BY ORDER OF COMMANDER HALE, RIVERINE COMMAND: SEIZURE OF UNREGISTERED SALVAGE. PERSONS ASSISTING CONCEALMENT TO BE HELD AS ACCESSORIES. REWARD FOR INFORMATION LEADING TO RECOVERY OF STOLEN NAVY PROPERTY.**

Stolen navy property. So Hale had filed the chest as navy property. Which meant somewhere in the archives there was a ledger entry proving the navy had always known what lay in that wreck — had known, and patrolled it for twenty years, and salvaged nothing, and erased the records. You didn't guard stolen property for two decades unless the theft was yours.

She kept walking. Behind her, the gate planks rattled shut.

* * *

They didn't speak until the road bent out of sight of the station, another mile, and then another, until the granite wall fell away and the pines closed overhead and the sound of the Vail faded under mule hooves. Sefa called a halt at a stream crossing, mostly because her legs wanted a reason to stop.

Wren handed back the dispatch case and looked at her with wide, careful eyes. "You were incredible."

"I was a liar in a costume." She stripped off the jacket. Her hands were shaking now, delayed, the way they always did after — after inspections, after musters, after the *Cormorant's Daughter*. She folded the jacket along its old creases and wrapped it in waxed canvas, and the crew stood around her in a loose half-circle, and she understood that they were waiting, politely, unbearably, for her to explain herself.

So she did.

"I was Riverine Command for six years," she said. "Enlisted at seventeen because the navy fed you and taught you to dive, and I was good at both. Commissioned at twenty-three. By twenty-five I was running interdiction details under Commander Hale — smuggling suppression, they called it. Unregistered cargo, unregistered crews." She tied the canvas cord. "Three years ago we intercepted a ship called the *Cormorant's Daughter* off the Silt Reaches. Smuggler vessel, thirty-one souls aboard, running medicine and tools to the floodplain settlements. No arms. We boarded, found nothing actionable, and Hale ordered us to scuttle her anyway. Make an example. He said the floodplain settlements were supply lines for Vesperan remnant networks, which was a lie, and everyone on that deck knew it was a lie, and we did it anyway."

The stream made its small cold noise. Nobody moved.

"We took their boats. We put them in the water eight miles offshore." Her voice stayed level. She had practiced level for years; it was the one uniform that never wore out. "Twenty-nine of them drowned. Two were pulled out by fishermen and disappeared inland. I stood watch on the deck and I listened to it happen, and I wrote the log entry afterward that recorded it as a navigational casualty. Storm damage. My handwriting. My signature."

Teo sat down slowly on a rock, like a man whose knees had made the decision for him.

"When I deserted, eleven months ago, I told myself I was leaving because of the executions. Hale shot a boatman in Corrin's Landing last month — you've heard. Before that there were others. I told myself it was the accumulation." She looked up at them, at Jonah, at Wren, at Ilo leaning against a mule with his arms crossed and his face unreadable. "It wasn't. It was the log entry. Every night since, I write that entry again in my head, and every night I sign it again. I joined this crew because you're carrying proof of what was done to Vesper. I stay because of the *Cormorant's Daughter*. I am not a good person hauling your chest over a mountain. I'm a person trying to stop signing."

For a long moment nobody said anything. Sefa had imagined this confession a hundred times on sleepless watches, and in every version the crew recoiled, or pitied her, or quietly began planning how to be rid of her before she became a liability. She braced for the recoil.

Wren crossed the space between them and hugged her, quick and fierce, the way teenagers hug — as if the moment might escape. "Twenty-nine people get to matter now," Wren said into her shoulder. "Because you're here."

Sefa held still. Then, carefully, she put one hand on the girl's back.

Jonah crouched by the stream and splashed water on his face, and when he straightened he said, "Maren's letter says the truth belongs to the drowned. You're the only one of us who knows exactly what that costs to carry. That makes you the most valuable person on this mountain, not the least trustworthy."

"She's right, though," Ilo said. Everyone looked at him. He shrugged. "About herself. She's not a good person. Good people don't exist; I've checked the market thoroughly." He pushed off the mule and started tightening a girth strap. "But useful people who know what they're ashamed of — those are rarer than honest ones, and twice as reliable. They've got nowhere better to be."

"That's the ugliest compliment I've ever received," Sefa said.

"I specialize."

Teo laughed — a short, cracked sound, surprise more than humor — and the knot in Sefa's chest loosened by a single turn. Not gone. She suspected it would never be gone, and she had stopped wanting it to be. Some weights were ballast. They kept you upright in bad water.

They moved out. The road climbed through the pines toward the col, and the light went thin and silver, and far behind them, down at the toll station, a signal flag would be going up a pole and a log entry with her name in it would begin its slow journey down the chain of command toward Commander Hale's desk. Let it travel, she thought. Let him read it. For six years her name had appeared in his files as a tool. Today it appeared as a declaration.

Near dusk, Ilo rode back down the trail from a scouting loop with his collar up and his expression wrong. Not frightened — Ilo didn't frighten — but tight, controlled, a man carrying something fragile he didn't want to set down.

"Problem," he said, reining in beside Jonah and Sefa. "Back at the station, Brakkis wasn't deciding whether to believe you. He'd already been told to expect a navy officer traveling north with a bonded shipment. Our inspection was on his list." He paused. "Somebody gave Hale our route, our cover story, and our timetable. Somebody inside this crew."
