## 9. Chapter 9

Ilo had been selling secrets since he was fourteen years old, and in all that time he had learned one rule above every other: never feel anything about the merchandise.

The chest was merchandise. The crew were colleagues. The debt to Maren was a contract like any other. He repeated this to himself on the third night out of the river city, while the others slept around a banked fire under a shelf of rock and the mules dozed with their ears flat against the cold. Then he took the signal lamp from his pack, checked that Wren was truly asleep — she wasn't, she never was, but she had the good grace to keep her breathing even — and walked fifty yards down into the dark of the river bend until the firelight couldn't reach him.

The syndicate called itself the Ledger. Everyone else called it the Ledger too, because smugglers are not a creative people when it comes to names, only when it comes to routes. Ilo had carried freight for them for nine years. They had bought his silence, sold his protection, and once — in a bad winter he tried not to think about — they had left him bleeding in a ditch because the price of retrieving him exceeded the price of replacing him. He held no grudge. That was the trade. You were worth what you cost, and no more.

He uncovered the lamp, fitted the amber shutter, and began the old pattern: three long, two short, three long. *Market open. Seller present.* The signal would carry eight miles downriver to a relay post above the ferry crossing, where some half-paid fisherman with a telescope would note it in a book and pass it up the chain. By morning, a buyer would know there was something worth buying moving through the hill country.

He told himself it was insurance. That was the honest word for it, or as close to honest as Ilo ever got. If the navy caught them in the passes — and Hale's patrols were sealing roads like a man stitching a wound — then the Ledger was the only door out. Sell the chest's location now, negotiate safe passage later. The syndicate didn't care about drowned nations or dying couriers. They cared about margin. Margin was a language Ilo spoke fluently, and in that language, what he was doing wasn't betrayal. It was hedging.

Everyone hedges. Only fools bet everything on one horse.

He closed the shutter and stood a moment longer in the dark, listening to the river go by, cold and patient, carrying everything downstream eventually.

* * *

The answer came faster than it should have.

Two nights later, at a waystation below the tree line — a lean-to of blackened timber where Ilo had watered mules a dozen times over the years — he found a chalk mark on the north post. A circle with a slash through it, and beneath it a number: **7**. Seven meant a meet at the seventh milestone marker on the old drover road, one rider, unarmed, before dawn.

Ilo knew the code. What bothered him was the speed. The Ledger moved at the speed of profit, which was to say slowly, through intermediaries, with three confirmations and a cut for everyone in the chain. This mark had been made within hours of his signal. Someone had been waiting for him to call. Someone had known he would.

He went anyway. Curiosity was the other thing that had kept him alive all these years, right after greed.

The seventh milestone was a leaning stone furred with lichen, half-swallowed by broom scrub. The rider was already there when Ilo came up the drover road, a grey figure on a grey horse in the grey hour before light, and everything about the figure was wrong. The Ledger sent brokers in good coats who complained about the cold and haggled before saying hello. This person sat still as a fencepost, and the horse was navy-bred — Ilo could tell by the set of the hocks, the deep chest, the kind of animal you couldn't buy, only requisition.

"You're not Ledger," Ilo said, stopping ten paces out.

"No," the rider said. A woman's voice, level, unbothered. She turned in the saddle just enough for the last of the starlight to catch her face. "But we've met. Ferry crossing. You were loading mules."

Sefa had described her well enough that Ilo recognized her before she finished speaking: Lieutenant Brannoch, Hale's outrider commander. The one Sefa said could find a footprint on granite.

Ilo did the arithmetic fast and found the sum ugly. His signal hadn't gone to the Ledger. Or it had gone to the Ledger first, and the Ledger had sold it upward within the hour — which meant the Ledger was already working for Hale, had maybe been working for Hale for years, its whole network of relays and chalk marks and half-paid fishermen converted into a navy listening apparatus without anyone bothering to tell the people feeding it.

"Your broker took my offer," Ilo said carefully.

"Our broker accepted your offer," Brannoch corrected. "That's how it works now. Everything flows one direction." She dismounted, unhurried, and let the reins hang. Unarmed, she'd said in the mark. She was armed; he could see the pistol butt under her coat. But she made no move toward it, and somehow that was worse. "You have something Commander Hale wants. We know what it is. We know where you got it. And now we know about your friends — the engineer, the deserter, the child with quick hands. Oh, and the smuggler. That's you."

"I know who I am."

"Do you?" She crouched by the milestone, traced the chalk circle with one finger, and smeared it out. "Nine years in the Ledger's books, Vosk. Good numbers, mostly. One bad winter." She glanced up. "We read your file. All of it. The ditch, too."

The cold went through him then, the old cold, the ditch cold. He kept his face still. Nine years of practice.

"Here's the shape of it," Brannoch said. "You give us the chest's route — the passes you're taking, the timing, where the girl sleeps. In exchange: passage out. Any port, any border. Your debts cleared, your name struck from our lists, and a purse that sets you up wherever the map ends. No one chases you. No one remembers you. That's the whole offer. It doesn't expire politely."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you're a man standing next to four people the navy is going to find anyway, and you'll have chosen to be found with them." She stood, brushed off her knee, and remounted with an ease that looked like contempt for the ground. "You don't need to answer tonight. You need to notice that you already know how this ends. Men like us always know."

She turned the horse. Then paused.

"One more thing, since you'll wonder. The old woman, Maren. She's dead — did they tell you? Died the week after your diver visited her." Brannoch said it lightly, testing the weight of it on him. "So whatever debt you think you're carrying, there's no one left to collect it. Debts to dead women are just stories men tell themselves. You seem like a man who prefers accounts."

She rode south. Ilo stood by the milestone for a long time, watching the smear of chalk where the circle had been, doing the thing he was best at in the world, which was counting.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat up with the mules and ran the numbers, because numbers were safe, numbers never lied, and if he stopped running them he would have to feel something instead.

Option one: take the deal. Passage, purse, erasure. He had spent his whole life trying to become unfindable; here was the navy offering to make him officially nothing. It was, objectively, the smart play. Any broker in the Ledger would tell him so. Any broker in the Ledger *had* told him so, in effect, by selling him.

Option two: refuse, warn the crew, run. Bad odds. Brannoch hadn't followed him here alone — there'd been a second horse's prints on the drover road, shod heavy, hanging back. They were watched. Probably had been since the river city.

Option three, which arrived uninvited somewhere past midnight and refused to leave: stay. Warn the crew. Change the route. Feed the navy a false line and put himself between the chest and the noose, for no payment whatsoever, in service of a debt owed to a dead woman he had met exactly twice.

That one made no sense. He examined it from every angle, looking for the hidden profit, the angle, the long game where Ilo comes out ahead. There wasn't one. That was the horrifying part. Somewhere between the river city and the foothills, between Teo humming over the mule harnesses and Wren stealing his knife sharpener and Sefa teaching her navy card games and Jonah saying *thank you* in that gravel voice like he meant it — somewhere in all that, Ilo had acquired people. Not colleagues. People. And people, he was discovering, were the one asset class you couldn't hedge.

Debts to dead women are just stories, Brannoch had said.

Maybe. But Ilo had grown up on stories, and he knew how they worked: you didn't get to choose which ones stuck. Maren had pressed his hand at the tenement door with fingers like paper and said, *the chest goes home*, and he had nodded the way you nod at old women, agreeing to nothing, and apparently his whole body had taken notes.

Dawn came up grey behind the eastern ridges. He heard the camp wake — Teo coughing, Wren complaining about the cold in two languages, the mules stamping. Sounds he had, without noticing, started to need.

He walked back up the drover road, and he did the hardest thing he had done in nine years of profitable cowardice.

He told them everything. The lamp signals. The chalk mark. Brannoch, the offer, the second horse. The Ledger sold to Hale. All of it, laid out flat, no spin, because he understood suddenly that spin was worthless here — that the only currency he had left with these people was the naked, unprofitable truth.

Silence around the fire. Wren staring at him. Sefa's hand drifting, unconsciously, toward a weapon. Jonah watching him with those diver's eyes that were used to seeing things at the bottom of dark water.

"The Ledger's theirs," Ilo finished. "Every relay, every mark, every ear I ever used. Which means they know we're coming, and they know roughly where. And I'm telling you this because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because I picked my side. Late, and badly, and after taking money from both teams, which is very on brand for me. But I picked it."

Jonah looked at him a long moment. Then at Sefa. Something passed between them that Ilo couldn't read.

"The fast pass is watched," Jonah said quietly. "It always was. Now we know by whom, and how." He stood, and threw Ilo a coil of rope — the gesture casual, the meaning enormous. "Congratulations, Vosk. You just volunteered to scout it."
