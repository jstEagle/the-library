## 10. Chapter 10

The frost came in the night, and by morning it had written its name on everything.

Teo woke to a world gone white and brittle — the grass stiff as wire, the mules' breath hanging in plumes, the canvas of Ilo's pack crackling when he shifted it. He lay in his bedroll a long moment, listening to the camp come alive around him: Wren's grumbling, Sefa's boots on frozen ground, Jonah saying nothing at all, which was how Jonah said most things.

*First frost.* His grandmother used to say it like a sentence. *First frost means the mountain has started counting.*

He got up, stamped feeling into his feet, and went to check the chest.

It rode in the false-bottomed pannier on the lead mule, wrapped in oilcloth and rope, looking for all the world like a crate of blacksmith's stock. Teo unwrapped just enough to see it — he did this every morning now, a ritual none of them commented on but all of them needed. The lead lining showed dull silver where the cloth gapped. The seal of Vesper sat on the lid like a closed eye: three waves under a falling star.

The lock was the problem.

He'd known since the river. Twenty years underwater, then the shock of air and cold, and the mechanism had seized with corrosion and silt packed into every seam. Maren's letter had been clear — the chest was to be opened only at journey's end, before witnesses, and not before. But a lock that couldn't open at all was worse than a lock that shouldn't. If they reached the valley and the thing wouldn't give, twenty years of waiting would end with Jonah prying at it with a crowbar while the documents inside turned to pulp.

So each night, in whatever light they had, Teo worked the lock.

He worked it now, kneeling beside the mule with his tool roll unrolled on a flat stone, fingers already aching from the cold. Ilo wandered over with two cups of something hot and bitter and set one down within reach without being asked. That was Ilo's whole vocabulary of kindness, and Teo had learned to read it fluently.

"Still fighting it?"

"Winning," Teo said. "Slowly."

"You said that Tuesday."

"I say a lot of optimistic things. It's my best quality."

Ilo snorted and went to see to the mule train, and Teo bent back over the lock.

It was beautiful work, underneath the ruin. Vesperan — of course it was; the whole nation had been built by people who treated engineering as a form of prayer. A seven-lever mechanism where any decent lockmaker would have used four, each lever cut to a different depth, each spring wound in opposition so that temperature changes that swelled one plate shrank another. Someone had designed this chest to survive exactly what it had survived. Someone had designed it to be opened by a patient hand after twenty years of drowning.

Teo thought about that person sometimes. Dead now, most likely, or scattered to wherever survivors scatter. But their hands were still here, in the geometry of the levers, still doing their one job across two decades and a drowned country. He found that he cared about them the way you care about a colleague you'll never meet.

By midmorning the third lever moved freely for the first time. By noon, when they stopped to water the mules at a stream skimmed with ice, the fourth came loose with a click he felt in his teeth.

"One more day," he told Jonah, who had appeared at his shoulder the way Jonah did, silent as weather.

Jonah looked at the lock a long moment. "Don't force anything."

"I never force anything. I persuade."

"Mm." Jonah's eyes stayed on the chest. There was something in his face Teo had learned to notice and never comment on — a kind of bracing, like a man watching someone else defuse a mine he'd buried himself. "When it opens, we do it together. All of us. Not you alone with it at midnight."

"I'm offended you'd think—"

"I'm not thinking anything. I'm asking." And that was Jonah: he didn't demand, he asked, and somehow the asking weighed more than any order.

"Together," Teo agreed. "All of us."

They climbed through the afternoon. The foothills came up to meet them in long folds of pine and granite, the trail narrowing to a shelf above a river that ran green and furious below. The frost held through the day in the shadows, melting only where the sun struck directly, so the mountainside was striped white and dark like something half-erased. Wren ranged ahead the way she always did, coming back with reports of hoofprints, old campsites, a shrine with offerings left so long ago the fruit had turned to leather. Sefa walked the rear, checking backtrail every quarter hour with a discipline Teo recognized as military and knew better than to name.

At dusk they made camp in a hollow beneath an overhang, out of the wind, and Teo built a small fire because small fires could be hidden and no fire could not be survived. They ate. Ilo dealt cards and lost gracefully to Wren, who cheated, and lost less gracefully to Sefa, who didn't need to. And when the others settled toward sleep, Teo took out the tools again, because the fifth lever had been mocking him all day and he was not a man who let locks mock him.

The hollow was quiet. Just wind in the pines, the mules shifting, the river far below talking to itself. He lit the lamp low, shielded it with his body, and set the chest on his knees.

The fifth lever was fouled with something that wasn't rust. He probed it carefully — wax, old wax, candle wax, deliberately poured into the mechanism. A sealant. His heart picked up. Wax meant intention; wax meant someone had wanted this lock untouched until a specific moment. He warmed it gently with the lamp, softened it, picked it out grain by grain with a needle probe, working by feel more than sight, the way his grandmother had taught him to work clockwork in the dark of a power cut.

The lever gave.

And beneath it, where the fifth lever should have seated against the bolt, there was no bolt at all. There was a second chamber — a slim brass sleeve running the length of the lock case, sealed at both ends, and inside it, when he eased the cap off with a jeweler's screwdriver and tipped the case, a rolled tube of paper slid into his palm.

Teo sat very still.

The paper was wrapped in waxed silk, tied with thread gone brittle as hair. It was dry. Whatever water had gotten into the chest in twenty years on the riverbed, it had never touched this — the sleeve was machined to tolerances that made his engineer's heart ache even as his stomach began to turn over. This wasn't a document kept safe by luck. This was a document kept safe by design, by someone who had assumed the main compartment might be compromised, might be seized, might be drowned forever — and had built a fallback into the bones of the lock itself.

*Open me last,* the design said. *Or open me never.*

He should wake Jonah. Jonah had said together, all of us, and Teo was not a man who broke agreements, especially not agreements made with Jonah Coldharbour's quiet, load-bearing voice.

But his hands were already moving. Not opening the silk — just holding it, weighing it, feeling the faint stiffness of the paper within. It was a strip, narrow, maybe a handspan long. A fragment. A note. Something small enough to hide in a lock, which meant something important enough that its maker had feared losing everything else.

*Just look at the outside,* he told himself. *That's not opening it. That's inventory.*

The silk was stitched along one edge with tiny characters. Vesperan script — he'd seen it on the courier markings, on Maren's letter, and Wren had been teaching him scraps of it on the trail, laughing at his accent. He knew maybe thirty words. Enough to read a street sign. Enough, as it happened, to read a label.

The stitching said: *For the opener. Read alone or read aloud — but read.*

His mouth had gone dry. He looked over at the others. Jonah lay on his side facing the fire, awake — Teo could tell by the quality of the stillness; Jonah slept like a plank, motionless, and this was a different kind of motionless. Watching. Waiting to see what Teo would do.

*I'm asking,* Jonah had said. *Not demanding.*

Teo held up the tube. "There's a compartment in the lock. There's paper in it."

Jonah sat up without a word. One by one the others surfaced — Sefa first, instantly alert, hand finding her knife before her eyes fully opened; then Wren, then Ilo, drawn by the lamplight and the wrongness in the air. They gathered close, a circle of shoulders against the cold, and Teo felt the strange gravity of it, five people orbiting a strip of paper smaller than his hand.

"Read it," Wren whispered.

Teo untied the thread. The silk fell open. Inside was a strip of heavy rag paper, dense with ink, the handwriting small and fierce, the letters of a man writing fast and knowing it might be the last thing he ever wrote.

Teo read it silently first. He couldn't help it; his eyes simply took it in, the way they took in a schematic, whole and instant.

Then he wished he hadn't.

*To whoever opens this: the fleet sailed on the 14th of the Harvest Moon with eleven hundred souls aboard, passengers and crew, and the order that sent it down came not from Vesper but from the Admiralty of the Crown, signed under seal. I have seen the order. I have copied it. My name is Edran Vail, First Clerk of the Naval Registry, and if you are reading this, I am dead, and the copy is in the chest, and the signature is real. Do not let them tell you it was a storm. Do not let them tell you it was war. Eleven hundred people were put into the sea by their own government because they had begun to count, and governments cannot abide being counted. Burn nothing. Trust no office. Carry it to people, not to power.*

Below the words, a date. Below the date, a signature — a small, precise, bureaucratic hand, the signature of a man whose entire life had been ledgers.

And beneath the clerk's signature, countersigned, witnessed, official, a second name. A name Teo had heard spoken once, quietly, on a night on the river when Jonah thought everyone was asleep, when he'd said it like a man spitting out a splinter:

*Coldharbour.*

Teo's hands were shaking. He became aware of it distantly, the way you become aware of weather. The paper trembled in the lamplight, and the names trembled with it, and nobody spoke.

"Read it aloud," Sefa said at last, very quietly. "All of it. We don't get to have parts of this."

So he read it aloud. His voice cracked on *eleven hundred* and he steadied it and kept going, because Sefa was right — they didn't get to have parts of this. When he reached the countersignature he almost stopped. He felt Jonah go rigid beside him, felt it like a change in air pressure.

Wren read the name first. Her voice came out very young. "Coldharbour. Like — your Coldharbour?"

"My father's," Jonah said. Flat. Careful. Each word placed like a stone on a cairn. "Countersigned. Witnessed. Official." A breath. "He signed the order, Teo. Or he signed off on it. Either way, his name is on the same page as eleven hundred drowned people, and it's been sitting in my hands for weeks."

The fire popped. Somewhere up the slope a branch cracked under the frost, loud as a shot, and nobody flinched because nobody had anywhere to put the flinch except into silence.

"That's why Maren chose you," Sefa said slowly. "Not despite the name. Because of it."

"She said the record wasn't finished," Jonah said. "She said a Coldharbour signature belongs on it too. I thought she meant—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought she meant something smaller than this."

Teo looked down at the paper. Wonder curdling into horror — that was the exact shape of it, he could feel the curdling happening, the beautiful machine in his hands becoming evidence, the elegant lock becoming a coffin nail, the clerk's fierce small handwriting becoming the voice of a dead man saying *do not let them tell you it was a storm.*

"There are ships' names in here," he said, because someone had to say something concrete, and concrete was his language. "In the main compartment, once we open it — the clerk says he copied the order. Ships, dates, tonnage, passenger manifests. And a signature." He looked up. "We're not carrying a chest anymore. We're carrying a confession. Signed. Dated. Countersigned by a Coldharbour."

Ilo laughed — one short, unhappy bark. "No wonder Hale wants it. It's not evidence. It's a loaded weapon pointed at the whole government."

"It's eleven hundred people," Wren said, and her voice shook, and she didn't try to steady it. "It's eleven hundred people who were told it was a storm."

Nobody answered that, because there was nothing to answer it with.

Jonah held out his hand, palm up, and Teo gave him the strip of paper, and Jonah read it twice, lips moving slightly, and then folded it along its original creases with a precision that Teo understood completely — the precision of a man holding himself together with procedure.

"We open the chest tomorrow," Jonah said. "Daylight. Together. And then we move faster, because as of tonight we know exactly what Hale is afraid of, and men who are afraid do not negotiate." He looked around the circle, firelight moving on his face. "Get some sleep. All of you. Tomorrow we find out everything."

They broke apart slowly, reluctantly, the way people leave a room where something enormous has been said. Teo resealed the brass sleeve, empty now, and set the lock's cap back in place, and found his hands were still trembling and made them stop through sheer professional will.

He was rolling up his tools when Wren appeared beside him, crouching, her eyes red-rimmed in the firelight.

"The man who wrote it," she said. "Edran Vail. He hid it in the lock because he figured the chest might get seized, right? So he built a way for the truth to survive even if the truth got captured."

"That's the design, yes."

She nodded slowly. "Twenty years underwater, and it waited for you." She looked at him with an expression he couldn't parse — awe and accusation in equal measure. "You fixed a dead man's lock, Teo. You finished his work. Don't you get what that makes you?"

"Tired," Teo said.

But she was already walking away, and he sat alone by the small fire a while longer, looking at the chest with its closed eye of a seal, listening to the frost tick and spread through the rocks around them like something counting.

Up-slope, in the darkness beyond the firelight, a mule lifted its head and blew softly through its nostrils at a scent none of the humans had caught yet.

Sefa caught it anyway, ten minutes later, standing the last watch. She stood very still in the cold, reading the dark, and then she woke Jonah with a hand on his shoulder and one word:

"Riders."
