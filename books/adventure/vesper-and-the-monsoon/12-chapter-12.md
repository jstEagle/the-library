## 12. Chapter 12

The fork came at the end of the third day of climbing, where the trail split like a river deciding its fate.

Jonah stopped first, because Jonah always stopped first. He'd learned that habit in the water — you never let the current choose for you. The left-hand path climbed steep and narrow toward the Kestrel's fast pass, a knife-cut between two granite shoulders that would put them over the range in two days if the weather held. The right-hand path bent south and west, wandering through the high sheep country toward the slow pass: three extra days, lower ground, safer ice.

Or it had been, twenty years ago, when anyone had last bothered to map it.

He stood with his hands on his hips and his breath steaming and let the others catch up to him. Behind him the mule train jingled to a halt, Ilo's animals stamping and blowing in the thin cold air. Wren came up on Teo's sled-runner, one hand steadying the engineer's shoulder. Teo was walking again, barely, his bandaged leg wrapped in layers of wool against the altitude chill, but every step cost him something Jonah could see even from behind.

Sefa ranged ahead along the ridgeline the way she always did, moving like she'd been born to this country instead of to a navy deck. She dropped back down after ten minutes, her face saying what her mouth wouldn't yet.

"Well?" Ilo asked.

"Smoke," Sefa said. "Two fires, maybe three, in the trees below the fast pass. Cook fires, spaced like a picket line." She looked at Jonah. "Outriders. Hale's. He's not chasing us anymore. He's waiting."

The word settled over the group like a change in pressure. Waiting meant he knew where they were going. Waiting meant he'd guessed the fork before they reached it, which meant someone had told him, or he simply understood the mountains the way Jonah understood rivers — by asking where the water had no choice but to go.

"Slow pass, then," Teo said. His voice was rough from the cold and the fever he kept insisting he didn't have. "It's three days longer but—"

"The slow pass might be shut." Ilo said it flatly, without drama, the way he said everything now. Since the night they'd found out about his signal to the syndicate — since the night Jonah had stood in front of the crew and said *he stays* — Ilo had stopped dressing his words up. It was penance, maybe. Or just honesty arriving late. "First frost came early this year. Everyone on the mountain's been saying it. If the snowline's already down to four thousand feet, the slow pass is a tomb. We walk into it and we die tired instead of quick."

"So we die quick instead," Wren said.

"We don't die at all," Jonah said. "We think."

They made camp in a hollow below the fork, a wind-shadow of boulders with room for the mules and a fire small enough to hide. While Sefa set watches and Wren fed the animals, Jonah sat with the maps — real ones, bought from a hill-country trader who hadn't asked questions — and studied both routes until the fire burned low.

The fast pass was watched. That was certain.

The slow pass was a gamble against weather. That was nearly as certain.

There was no third route. There was never a third route; that was the mountain's whole joke.

* * *

They argued around the fire after dark, voices low, the way people argue when sound carries and death listens.

"Three days is nothing," Sefa said. "We've got food for five if we're careful. Six if we eat the mules."

"You'll eat my mules over my dead body," Ilo said.

"That can be arranged."

"I'm serious about the snow, though." Teo had his bad leg stretched toward the fire, his tools spread on a cloth beside him because his hands couldn't stay idle even when the rest of him failed. "I grew up in hill country. My grandmother read weather the way priests read scripture. The clouds today were mare's tails — high, streaky, moving east to west. That's a front coming in behind us. Two days, maybe three. If it hits while we're on the slow side, we're in open sheep country with no shelter and a man who can't walk."

Everyone looked at Teo. Teo looked at the fire.

"I'm not saying don't try it," he added. "I'm saying know what you're betting."

"And the fast pass has soldiers in it," Wren said. "Soldiers with rifles. I counted the smoke like Sefa said — three fires. That's six men minimum, probably eight. Outriders travel in pairs."

"Eight men who expect us to come blundering up the trail," Sefa said. "Not eight men expecting what we could send them instead."

Jonah listened. This was the part of command nobody warned you about — not the deciding, but the listening, holding everyone's fear in your hands at once and feeling how heavy each one was. Wren's fear was young and sharp-edged. Sefa's was old and disciplined, the fear of a woman who'd already died once in a uniform and hated the fit. Teo's fear was arithmetic. Ilo's—

Ilo's fear was quiet. Quieter than it had been a week ago. A man who'd sold them once and been forgiven anyway carried a strange lightness, Jonah thought. Like a man who'd already lost everything worth losing and found the ground beneath him solid after all.

"What are you thinking?" Jonah asked him.

Ilo shrugged. "I'm thinking the fast pass is watched by men watching a trail. Nobody watches the rock above the trail."

Sefa's head came up slowly. "You're talking about the goat paths."

"I'm talking about the fact that I've run this range for eleven years, and there isn't a pass in it I haven't crossed some way other than the way on the map. The outriders are sitting on the trail because the trail is the only thing a soldier can guard. Soldiers don't climb." He smiled thinly. "Smugglers do."

"It's winter climbing," Sefa said. "Above the tree line. With a chest we can't drop and a man who can't—"

"I know what it is." Ilo's voice stayed level. "I'm not saying it's good. I'm saying it's the only version where we all arrive."

Teo was shaking his head before Ilo finished. "My leg won't make that climb. You all know it. I'll slow you to a crawl on scree, and on ice I'm dead weight, and I won't be dead weight, I'll be the reason—"

"You'll be none of those things," Jonah said, "because we haven't decided anything yet."

But they all knew the shape of the decision now. It sat in the firelight between them like the chest itself: heavy, sealed, unavoidable.

* * *

Later, when the others had rolled into their bedrolls, Jonah walked the picket line, checking the mules' hobbles, checking the sky. The stars were hard and close. To the west, over the Kestrel's spine, he could see the front Teo had named — a faint smudge of cloud, organized, patient, coming on like something with an appointment.

Maren's voice lived in his head these days more than it had when she was alive. *A debt is not a weight, Jonah. It's a rope. The question is always what's on the other end.*

He heard boots behind him and didn't turn. Only one person in camp moved that quietly besides himself.

"Ilo."

"Jonah." Ilo came and stood beside him at the picket line, hands jammed in his coat. For a while neither spoke. The mules breathed steam into the dark.

"I want to scout the fast pass," Ilo said finally. "Tonight. Alone. I can be up to the saddle and back before midmorning. I'll count the outriders, find their blind spots, mark the goat path. Then you decide with real numbers instead of smoke-counting."

"No."

Ilo turned to look at him. In the starlight his face was unreadable, which was its own kind of answer — a smuggler's face, worn like armor. "No?"

"If they take you, they take everything you know. Routes, numbers, where we sleep. And they'll take it gently, Ilo. Hale doesn't need to torture men like you. He needs to offer them things." Jonah kept his eyes on the ridge. "You taught me that. First week out of the river city, remember? You said every man has a price and the smart ones know their own."

"I said that before I found mine."

Jonah looked at him then. "Did you?"

"Yes." No hesitation. No smuggler's slide. Just the flat truth of a man standing on ground he'd chosen. "My price was staying alive and comfortable. I paid it once already — I sent that signal, and I've been paying ever since. What's left of me is cheap, Jonah. Cheaper than the chest, cheaper than the kid, cheaper than Teo. Send me up the mountain. If I'm taken, you lose the least."

"That's not how this works anymore."

"Then tell me how it works."

Jonah was silent for a long moment. Below them the valley breathed cold. Above them the pass waited with its fires and its rifles.

"It works like this," he said finally. "Nobody goes alone. Nobody pays alone. That's what Maren built, that's what she asked for — not a courier, a *crew*. Because a lone courier can be caught, but a crew—" He shook his head. "A crew has to be broken, and breaking takes time, and time is the one thing Hale doesn't have either. The front comes in behind us same as it comes in behind him."

Ilo was quiet. Then: "So you're saying yes."

"I'm saying yes, and Sefa goes with you to the base of the ridge, and Wren goes halfway as far as Sefa, and if you're not back by midmorning we take the slow pass and you find your own way home." He held out his hand. "That's trust, Ilo. Not the feeling. The terms."

Ilo took his hand. His grip was dry and strong and steady, and Jonah thought: *this is what it costs. Not the trusting. The signing of the terms knowing full well what they might buy.*

* * *

They left before dawn, in the blue-black hour when the cold is honest. Ilo wore his lightest gear and carried no fire kit — nothing to signal with except a mirror, nothing to identify him if taken except his own name, which he'd already spent once. Sefa walked him to the treeline and stood there while he climbed, and Jonah watched her watch him go, and understood that whatever she felt about navy men and traitors and forgiveness, she'd decided to spend it on this.

Wren appeared at Jonah's elbow as the light came up, gray and gradual, like the mountain was being developed in a darkroom.

"He'll come back," Wren said.

"You don't know that."

"No. But I decided it anyway." Wren glanced up at him. "Is that stupid?"

Jonah thought about Maren. About debts and ropes and what hung on the other end. About a father whose name belonged on a record of shame, and a son carrying paper across a mountain to balance it.

"It's the whole job," he said. "Deciding things you can't know. Everything else is just walking."

They waited through the morning. Teo worked on the chest's lock fittings with numb fingers and refused tea twice. Sefa cleaned her rifle with the patience of ritual. The sun climbed, and the shadow of the western ridge shortened, and Jonah marked the hours the way a diver marks air — not with panic, but with the cold knowledge of exactly how much remained.

Midmorning came. Passed.

At half past, Wren said, quietly, "Slow pass, then."

And Jonah was opening his mouth to say yes — to say the terms held, to say trust meant honoring the loss too — when Sefa's hand closed hard on his arm.

She was looking up-slope, toward the notch of the fast pass, where the morning light lay white on stone.

Three figures were descending. Moving fast. Moving in the loose, rolling gait of men who knew the mountain.

And the one in front, limping slightly, waving his arms like a madman, was Ilo.

Behind him, flanking him, were two men Jonah didn't know — ragged, armed, wearing no uniform at all.

Sefa saw them at the same instant and her rifle came up. "Those aren't outriders."

"No," Jonah said slowly. His heart was doing something complicated in his chest. "They're not."

Ilo reached them breathing hard, grinning like a man who'd cheated at cards and gotten away with it, and jerked a thumb at the strangers behind him.

"Fast pass is clear," he said. "Hale's outriders pulled out at dawn — all of them, gone south, moving fast. Something spooked them." He paused, and the grin faded into something else, something older and stranger. "These two say they know why. They say they've been waiting in the high country for twenty years." He looked at Jonah, and his voice dropped. "They say they're Vesperan. And they say the chest isn't going where we think it's going."
