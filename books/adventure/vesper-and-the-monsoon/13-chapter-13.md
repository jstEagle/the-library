## 13. Chapter 13

The col came at them like a held breath finally let out.

Sefa had been counting the switchbacks since dawn — eleven, twelve, thirteen — and each one higher and colder than the last. The mules picked their way along a shelf of gray stone no wider than a cart track, with the valley falling away on their left in long blue folds of cloud. Above them the pass narrowed to a notch between two shoulders of rock, and through that notch the sky showed white and hard, like the eye of something watching.

"Wind's turning," Ilo said beside her, his voice pitched low under the creak of harness. He'd been quiet since the fork, quieter than a man should be who'd been forgiven for what he'd done. "Coming down off the glacier. You can smell it."

Sefa could. Iron and snow and something underneath, the cold mineral stink of high stone. She'd smelled it before, years ago, on a different range, during a different operation she still couldn't say aloud without her hands shaking. She adjusted the sling of her rifle and said nothing.

Jonah was twenty yards ahead with Wren, both of them bent into the wind. The chest rode mid-train in Ilo's best false load, wrapped in oilcloth and packed around with dried fish that stank even through the canvas — Teo's idea, camouflage by offense. Teo himself walked at the rear with the spare mule, which was wrong, because Teo belonged at the front where he could worry about everyone's boots and rope lengths and the exact angle of every load, and Sefa had told him so twice since morning.

"Somebody has to watch our backs," he'd said, cheerful as ever, and she'd let it go, because arguing with Teo about safety was like arguing with a river about flowing downhill.

Now she stopped on the twelfth switchback and looked back down the trail, and the feeling hit her all at once, whole and cold: they were being watched from behind.

Not seen. Watched. There was a difference, and every soldier knew it. Being seen was an accident. Being watched was a decision someone was making about you.

"Ilo." She kept her voice flat. "How far back is the last village?"

"Half a day down. Why?"

"How many men did you see when we watered there?"

He frowned. The wind pulled at his scarf. "Three. Maybe four. Farmers."

"Farmers don't stand watch rotations on a well platform."

Ilo went very still. Then, without another word, he turned and looked up the trail toward Jonah, and Sefa saw him do the arithmetic she'd already done — the timing of the patrols below, the way the southern roads had been sealed too neatly, the fact that Hale's outriders hadn't shown themselves once in six days of climbing, which wasn't absence. It was patience.

Hale didn't chase. Hale waited until the ground did the work for him, and then he took away every route but one, and then he stood at the end of that one route with his hands folded, almost courteous, and let you walk into him.

She'd watched him do it to a smuggler crew off the Salt Coast, four years ago. She'd been standing in his shadow when it happened. She'd told herself the thing every deserter tells herself: *I didn't fire. I just stood there.* As if standing were neutral. As if the ground didn't remember who stood on it.

"Sefa!" Jonah's voice came down the trail, thin with distance. "What do you see?"

"Get off the skyline," she called back. "All of you. Now."

For one second nobody moved — the frozen half-second where people wait to be told the danger is real — and then Wren dropped off the trail's edge onto the uphill side, dragging Jonah by the sleeve, and Ilo was shouting at the mules, hauling the lead rope leftward toward a fold of rock, and Sefa turned fully around with her rifle up and scanned the ridgeline above the notch.

There. Not movement exactly. A shimmer where the snowfield met bare rock, the kind of distortion heat makes over a fire except this was cold air over warm stone, and it resolved, as she watched, into three shapes that had been lying flat among the boulders and now weren't lying anymore.

"Contact!" she shouted. "Ridge, above the pass! Down, everybody down—"

The first shot cracked across the col and took the lead mule through the neck.

It didn't fall so much as fold, legs going sideways, and the load shifted and the animal slid toward the outer edge of the shelf and Ilo threw himself on the lead rope with both hands and dug his heels in and screamed something wordless, and the mule went over anyway, taking forty pounds of dried fish and, God, Sefa realized a heartbeat later, *the chest* — no. No, the chest was mid-train. The falling load was decoy. Teo's decoy. The fish had been the point all along, or part of it, and there was no time to admire the engineering because the ridge above them bloomed with muzzle flash, three rifles firing in rhythm, disciplined, spaced, *military*, and the second volley came down the shelf like hail thrown by an angry god.

Stone chips stung her cheek. She went to one knee behind a boulder the size of a sleeping ox and fired twice at the shimmer, not aiming so much as answering, putting rounds into the rocks above the shooters to keep their heads down. Beside her Ilo had gotten the train moving again, hauling the mules into the rock fold, and Wren was doing something impossible and calm, cutting the false load free of the fallen mule's tangled harness with a knife while stones sang off the rock around her head.

"The chest!" Jonah's voice, raw. "Wren, leave it—"

"It's *fine*, it's cut—" And it was, the whole wrapped bundle sliding free, and Wren got her arms around it and staggered upright under the weight, this girl who weighed less than the bundle, and started moving uphill with it bent double against her spine.

A fourth shot. Different angle. Lower.

Sefa knew before she turned. She knew from the sound of it, flatter, closer, from the flank — there'd been a second position, of course there'd been a second position, Hale never used one blade when two would do — and she turned and saw Teo at the rear of the train, halfway through swinging the spare mule around, and she saw the moment the round took him high in the chest, a little punch of red mist and then the sound arriving late, and she saw him look down at himself with genuine surprise, the engineer confronted with a failure he hadn't diagnosed, and then his knees went and he sat down hard on the trail as if he'd simply decided to rest.

"*TEO!*"

Her voice tore out of her before she knew it was hers. She was running before she finished the word, straight across open ground she'd have ordered anyone else shot for crossing, and the flank shooter fired again and the round sparked off stone a hand's width from her boot, and she didn't care, some part of her brain noting with clinical disgust that she didn't care, that fear had been burned out of her somewhere between the first volley and the sight of Teo sitting in the trail looking puzzled at his own blood.

She got hands under his arms. His pack dragged. His blood was on her wrists immediately, shockingly warm in all that cold.

"Hey. Hey. Engineer." She hauled him backward toward the rock fold, his boots plowing furrows in the scree. "You stay with me. You tell me what's broken. That's your job, right? Tell me what's broken."

His mouth moved. Blood at the corner of it. "Lung," he managed, and laughed, a horrible wet bubble of a laugh, because of course Teo would diagnose himself. "Left. It's — it's filling."

"Then we get you down the mountain and we drain it. Ilo! *ILO!*"

The smuggler appeared out of the smoke-haze of gunpowder breath, face gray, and together they got Teo behind the boulder, and Wren arrived seconds later with the chest still strapped to her back, hair loose and wild, eyes enormous and absolutely steady.

"Flankers," Wren said. "Two more, coming up the switchbacks below. I heard them on the gravel."

Of course. Three on the ridge, two below. A closing fist. Hale's geometry, exact as always.

"We can't hold here," Sefa said, and heard her own voice come out level, cold, the voice of a woman she'd spent four years trying to bury. "We can't stay, we can't go back, and the pass is covered. So we go *through*."

"Through?" Ilo stared at her. "Through *what*? Through their line?"

"Through the notch, low and fast, while they're repositioning. They expect us to break downhill or hug the wall. Nobody crosses open ground under fire unless they're insane."

"I've met you," Ilo said. "You might be."

"Wren." Sefa turned to the girl. "The chest stays on your back. Can you run with it?"

Wren rolled her shoulders once, testing, and something passed over her face — grief, fury, both compressed into a single hard line — and she said, "I've run from worse than bullets."

"Jonah's on point. Ilo drags the two live mules. I carry Teo. We move on my count, thirty seconds apart, and nobody stops for anything, including me." She ripped Teo's shirt open, jammed the wad of bandage hard against the wound, and tied it with her teeth and one hand. His blood soaked through before she finished the knot. "Teo. Look at me."

His eyes found hers. They were still clear. That was the worst part, how clear they were.

"If I slow you down," he whispered, "you leave me. Take the chest and—"

"Shut up. You're cargo now. Cargo doesn't get a vote." She got her arms under him, lifted, and felt the terrible lightness of him, how much of him was already gone. "Count of my hand. Go."

They went.

The world became noise and motion and the taste of copper. Sefa ran bent double with Teo across her shoulders and the shots came down around them in that patient military rhythm, and she counted strides because counting was a thing her body knew how to do when everything else was burning. Twenty strides. Forty. Wren ahead of her, flying over the broken ground with the chest bouncing against her spine, small and huge at once. Jonah at the notch, waving them through, screaming words the wind ate.

At sixty strides the flankers below opened up and a round clipped the boulder beside her and a fragment of stone opened a line of fire along her jaw, and she didn't stop, didn't slow, put her head down and *ran*, and somewhere in the middle of it Teo stopped weighing anything at all, went slack and heavy in the bad way, and she knew, the way you know weather coming, the way you know ice thin underfoot.

She crossed the notch. She kept running another fifty paces down the far side, into the wind-shadow of the shoulder, where the shooting suddenly couldn't reach, and there she knelt and laid him down in the snow with a gentleness that contradicted everything else in her body.

"Teo."

His eyes were open. Fixed on the white sky. His breath came in short, wet pulls, each one costing more than the last.

"Drain it," he whispered. "There's a — third rib, there's a space, you can — I showed you the kit, I *showed* you—"

"You showed me everything," Sefa said. Her hands were already moving, pulling the kit, the little brass tube he'd made her practice with a dozen times on the river journey, laughing at her terrible technique. "You never shut up about anything. Hold on to that."

She did it badly. She knew she did it badly even as she did it, hands shaking, wind screaming, blood on everything, and a little fluid came and not enough, and his breathing didn't ease, and Wren knelt on his other side and took his hand and Ilo stood over them both with his body angled uphill like a wall, watching the notch, rifle ready, guarding them from the world.

"Jonah," Teo said. It was barely a sound.

Jonah knelt. Took his other hand.

"Melt it down," Teo whispered, and smiled, and it was the old smile, the workshop smile, the one that meant *I'm joking, but also I'm not.* "Or don't. Don't. Finish it. Finish the — the job, Jonah. Somebody has to check everyone's boots."

And then, between one wet pull of breath and the next, he stopped.

The wind kept going. That was the unbearable thing. The wind didn't care; it poured over the shoulder of the mountain and filled the silence he left behind, and Sefa knelt in it with her hands still pressed to his chest, holding pressure on a heart that had no more use for it, and something in her that had been cracking for four years — since the Salt Coast, since the smuggler ship, since *I just stood there* — finished breaking and reformed into something new.

Not softer. Harder. Colder. A tool, finally, sharpened to its purpose.

She closed his eyes. She took his tools — the little brass tube, the folding knife, the coil of wire he'd never be without — and she put them in her own pockets, because they were going over that notch again if she had to crawl, and outriders' bodies were going to answer for this, and after the outriders, if it came to it, the man who sent them.

"Sefa." Jonah's voice, hoarse. "We have to move. They'll come down."

"I know." She rose. Wiped her face with the back of her wrist and left a smear of Teo's blood there, and decided to leave it. Let it dry there. Let it ride with her into whatever came next. "We bury him when we're past them. Not before. He'd hate a shallow grave."

Jonah looked at her a long moment — at the blood, at the stillness in her, at whatever had changed behind her eyes — and nodded slowly, and said nothing, because he understood. Some griefs aren't for sharing. Some are for carrying, like a load, up a mountain, at altitude, in winter.

They moved. Wren carried the chest. Ilo led the mules. Jonah broke trail, and Sefa took the rear, walking backward half the time, rifle up, watching the notch they'd come through, daring it.

Behind them, faint in the wind, she could hear the outriders reaching the col, and voices, and then — clearer than she wanted — a whistle. Two notes, rising. Hale's signal for *regroup and pursue*. She'd heard it a thousand times across four years and two lives.

But mixed in with it, thinner, farther down the southern slope, came another sound. Voices. Many voices, moving fast, not in Hale's clipped cadence but in the loose, overlapping call-and-response of hill people — and with them, a dog, baying.

Ilo caught it too. He turned, frowning. "That's not the navy."

"No," Sefa said. Her hand found the brass tube in her pocket and closed around it, cold and certain. "That's the ferryman's country. That's the letter of passage."

The voices were coming toward them — and so, she realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the wind, was the storm, a white wall of it already swallowing the peaks above the pass.

* * *

They buried Teo two hours later, above the tree line, in ground Wren chose because the stone there stood in a ring like the ones in the old Vesperan songs she'd grown up humming without knowing why. There was no time for a proper cairn. There was only time for what they had: a shallow cut in the frozen earth, broken by the picks, lined with the oilcloth that had wrapped the chest, and Teo laid in it with his tools replaced in his pockets where Sefa had first taken them from — because she couldn't keep them, not all of them, not after seeing his face when he talked about his work.

Jonah spoke. Just a few words, half of them lost to the wind. Wren put a river stone on his chest, the way you'd mark a diver's grave. Ilo stood at the head and said nothing at all, which from Ilo was a eulogy.

Sefa stood apart, at the edge of the ring of stones, and did not pray. She made a list instead. Names, positions, timings. The ridge shooters' spacing. The flankers' angle of approach. The whistle signal. The direction the reinforcements would come from. She built the fight again in her head, piece by piece, the way Teo would have built a machine, and she found the flaw in it — theirs, not Hale's — and she marked it, and she would not make it twice.

When they turned to go, she looked back once at the mound of stones, already whitening with the first flakes of the storm.

"You checked everyone's boots," she said quietly. "Every single day. Now I'll check yours."

Then she turned uphill, into the wind, and followed the others toward the whiteout, toward the pass, toward the man whose whistle was still hanging in the air behind them like a debt of its own.
