## 15. Chapter 15

The ledge was barely wide enough for four people and a mule, and it had to hold five people and no mule at all.

Wren pressed her back against the rock and tried not to look down. The drop went on longer than her eyes wanted to believe — the river below was a gray thread stitched through white forest, and the wind coming up off it smelled like ice and old stone. Somewhere above them, maybe an hour behind, maybe less, Hale's outriders were picking their way along the trail they'd abandoned in the dark.

"They'll reach the col by midmorning," Sefa said. She'd wedged herself against the cliff face with Teo's arm over her shoulders, holding most of his weight. His bandage had soaked through again during the night's scramble, and his skin had gone the color of the fog. "If they see smoke—"

"That's rather the point of smoke," Ilo said.

He was already unpacking the spare gear. Everything they couldn't carry: the second tent, the oilskin groundsheet, two of the mule panniers, the coil of rope too short to be useful, Teo's spare coat because he couldn't wear two coats and wouldn't say so out loud. Ilo stacked it all against the rock wall at the far end of the ledge, where the overhang would shelter it from snow, and worked with the brisk cheer of a man building something instead of burning it.

"It won't fool them long," Jonah said.

"Doesn't need to." Ilo struck flint to steel. "It needs to fool them for exactly as long as it takes us to get off this mountain face. A camp burned in the night says *they panicked, they dropped everything, they went down the valley trail.* Nobody looks for climbers when there's a perfectly good trail going down."

"And if they send someone to check the ledge?"

Ilo paused, flint hovering. "Then we're already gone, and I'll have died having saved us several hours, which is more than most men manage."

Wren watched him set the fire. There was something strange about seeing it — Ilo, who counted every coin twice and mourned every burned ration like a death in the family, feeding good gear into the flames without hesitation. He fed it slowly, though. Piece by piece, so it would burn for hours instead of minutes. A slow fire read as a camp that had settled in for the night. A fast one read as a signal or a flight.

"You've done this before," she said.

"I've done many things before, little lockpick." He didn't look up from the fire. "That's the tragedy of me."

The flames caught the overhang and threw orange light across the cliff face, and for a moment the whole ledge glowed like a lantern. Wren thought about how visible they were. How the light would spill out over the valley like a shout. Then she looked at the shape of the overhang and understood — from below, from any distance, it would just be a smudge of warmth in a crack in the mountain. Shepherds burned fires in cracks all the time.

*Trust the plan,* she told herself. *Trust the man who made it.*

She was getting better at that. It still cost her something every time.

* * *

Jonah crouched at the eastern end of the ledge, where the rock fell away into the waterfall route, and studied it the way he studied wrecks — patiently, without hope, looking only for what was true.

The falls came down the cliff in three steps. In summer they'd be a thundering white ribbon; now, in deep frost, they were a staircase of blue ice, veined and bulging, running with thin water in the warm hours and freezing again at night. Where each fall met the next, the water had carved shelves and pockets in the rock, and twenty years of freeze-thaw had left ledges, chockstones, places where a root or a rusted iron pin might still hold.

Only Wren could read it. That was what Sefa had said, half-accusing, when Wren had pointed it out last night. And it was true, and Wren didn't entirely know why. Her mother had taught her locks and letters both, and had taught her that stone was just a lock you climbed instead of picked — every crack a keyhole, every hold a tumbler waiting to be set. But her mother had never seen these falls. Nobody had shown Wren this route. She simply looked at the ice and the rock and knew, the way she knew which key fit which ward, that the third shelf would hold weight and the fourth would not. That the black streak of water under the second fall meant thin ice over moving water, and the white bulge beside it meant solid.

"You're sure," Jonah said. Not really a question. He asked it anyway, every time, because asking was how he carried other people's fear for them.

"The line goes left at the first step, right at the second," Wren said. "There's a cave behind the middle fall — big enough for all of us, dry enough to rest. Then down the chimney to the tree line. Two hours. Three with Teo."

"With me," Teo said, from Sefa's shoulder, "you can round up."

His voice was a ruin. The fever had him now, properly — Wren could hear it in the way he talked slightly past them, as if the words were arriving from somewhere else. But his eyes were still Teo's eyes, still doing arithmetic. "Rope," he said. "How much rope do we have?"

"Forty feet of the good line," Jonah said. "The rest is burning."

"Forty feet." Teo closed his eyes. "So we anchor at each step, climb down the length, re-anchor. Six anchors. Every anchor costs us ten minutes and every minute costs us—" He stopped himself. They all heard what he hadn't said. *Every minute costs us the distance between us and Hale.*

"We don't re-anchor," Wren said.

They all looked at her.

"We free-climb the steps and use the rope only for the bad parts. For hauling Teo." She pointed with her chin at the route. "See the shelf? You could walk a goat along it. And there—" the pale seam angling down-left, "—that's a ramp, not a wall. My mam took me on worse in the city walls when I was nine."

"Your mother," Sefa said carefully, "taught you to climb sea cliffs."

"She taught me to climb things I wasn't supposed to be able to climb. Same thing."

Jonah was quiet a moment. Wind gusted up the falls and scattered snow-smoke off the ice. Below them the world hung open and enormous.

"All right," he said. "But we go in order. Me first, then Wren reading holds to me, then Teo roped between Sefa and me, then Ilo last with the chest."

"The chest," Ilo said, "goes nowhere near my back on vertical ice. It goes on the rope, lowered step by step, and I follow it down like a nursemaid." He patted the lead-lined box, wrapped now in sacking and lashed tight. "It has survived drowning, gunfire, and my company. It will survive a waterfall."

Wren felt something loosen in her chest — not relief exactly, but the specific loosening of hearing competent people agree on a plan. That was the thing nobody told you about terror, she was learning. Terror got smaller when it was shared out. Alone, it filled the whole sky. Divided among five people who each held a piece of it, it was just weather. Bad weather. Weather you moved through.

"Then we go now," Jonah said, "while the ice is hard and the fire is young."

* * *

The first step was the worst, because it was the first.

Wren went second, a body-length above Jonah, calling holds down to him even though he could see them — because saying them aloud steadied her own hands, and because Jonah had asked her to, and because the sound of her own voice describing *left hand, blue ice, trust it* was proof that she was still thinking and not merely clinging.

The cold came through her gloves in seconds. The rock bit through her sleeves. Twice the hold she called turned soft under Jonah's boot and he shifted without complaint, finding the alternative she'd named a breath later, and each time she felt the correction travel up through the rope like a word spoken in a language only the two of them spoke.

Below, faintly, impossibly far below, the river whispered.

*Don't look down. Look at the next hold. Only ever the next hold.* Her mother's voice, worn smooth from repetition. *A climber who looks at the bottom is already falling.*

At the first shelf they gathered, breathing hard, and hauled the chest up after them on the rope, Ilo walking it down the ramp with his boots planted wide. Teo came last, half-carried, Sefa behind him whispering the count of his movements — *weight on your left, now the right, good, good* — in the flat calm voice of someone who had once talked wounded sailors through damage control while the deck tilted.

The second step was steeper, and here the rope mattered. Jonah drove an iron piton into a crack Wren found for him — a hairline seam hidden under an apron of ice, which she located by sound, tapping with a knuckle until the rock rang hollow and then solid, the way she tapped a safe's door to find the plate behind it. The piton went in with a shriek. Jonah hung his whole weight on it. It held.

"One," he said. "Teo, you're tied in. We lower you to the shelf."

They lowered him. It took forever. The rope ran through Jonah's gloved hands in slow, controlled burns, and Teo swung gently against the ice like a plumb bob, and Wren watched Sefa's face while she paid out the line — watched the deserter's jaw set against some memory of ropes and dropping things, watched her master it, watched her count under her breath.

Teo reached the shelf. Sefa went down after him. Then the chest, swaying, patient, obscene in its ordinariness — a box full of paper that had already killed one nation and was now dragging five people down a frozen waterfall.

Then Ilo, and then Wren, last but for Jonah, because Jonah always went last. She understood that now. It wasn't heroics. It was bookkeeping. He put himself at the point where any loss would reach him first.

Halfway down the second step, her foot skated.

It happened the way it always happens — no warning, no drama, just the world suddenly tilting and her stomach rising into her throat and her fingers slamming onto ice that squealed under her nails. She slid a body-length before the rope snapped taut, and then she was hanging, swinging, staring at the drop with her heart hammering somewhere up around her teeth.

"Wren." Jonah's voice, from above. Calm. Absolutely calm. "Look at me."

She couldn't. She was looking down.

"Wren. Look at me."

She dragged her eyes up. He was braced against the slope above her, rope wrapped twice around his forearm, and his face was the steadiest thing in the whole white world.

"There's a chockstone at your left knee," he said. "Yellowish. Your mam taught you to find those?"

Her breath came back in pieces. "Find the — the pocket behind it."

"There you go."

She found the pocket. She got her boot into it. She stood on it, shaking, and climbed, and Jonah's hand closed on her collar at the top and set her on the shelf like she weighed nothing at all.

"Everyone slips," he said quietly. "The ones who die are the ones who stop listening for the next hold."

She nodded, not trusting her voice. Behind them, high above, the glow of their own decoy fire flickered off the clouds like a false dawn.

* * *

The cave behind the middle fall was real. Wren had promised it and it was real — a hollow in the cliff the size of a chapel, curtained by falling water gone to lacework ice, floored with dry gravel. They collapsed into it gasping, and for a few minutes nobody did anything but breathe.

Then Sefa lit the smallest candle she owned, cupped in her palms, and they saw each other's faces: Jonah's gray with effort, Ilo's uncharacteristically silent, Teo's flushed bright with fever, her own probably no better.

"Rest," Jonah said. "Ten minutes. Then the chimney."

Wren sat against the wall and let the shivering run its course. Through the ice curtain the outside world was smeared into ribbons of gray and white, and somewhere beyond it, downhill and behind, men with rifles were following the smell of their burned gear toward an empty ledge.

"They'll know by noon," Sefa said. "Someone will climb up. They'll find ash and a cold camp and no tracks going down the valley."

"Then they'll come to the falls," Ilo said.

"They'll look at the falls and think no one could be mad enough."

"Commander Hale," Sefa said, "has never once in his life assumed that about anyone. It's how he caught the smugglers off Corvo Shoal. Everyone assumed no one would sail the reef channel at night." She fed a splinter of wood to the candle flame, watching it catch. "He'll send climbers. Two, maybe three. Light ones."

"Then we're ahead of them," Jonah said, "and we stay ahead of them."

Wren looked at Teo. He'd gone quiet in a way that frightened her more than the fever flush did. His head was tipped back against the stone, eyes half-closed, lips moving faintly — counting, she realized. He was always counting. Rations, rope lengths, days, debts. Now his lips moved and no numbers came.

"Teo." She crawled over. "Hey. Engineer."

His eyes focused on her slowly, like a lamp being trimmed. "Wren."

"When we get down, you're going to fix the chest lock properly. The real repair. Not the field job."

Something kindled faintly behind the fever. "The tumbler assembly's corroded. I'd need vinegar, fine wire, six hours—"

"Six hours in a warm room," she said. "With tea. Down in the trees there'll be a shepherd's hut or a charcoal burner's camp, there always is. You'll have your six hours."

He smiled, small and crooked. "You're learning to lie like a smuggler."

"I'm learning to promise like one. Different skill."

Ilo snorted from across the cave. Sefa almost smiled. Even Jonah's mouth twitched, and Wren felt the moment settle over the five of them like a blanket — brief, thin, but real. A family, huddled behind a frozen waterfall, promising each other warm rooms none of them could guarantee.

The candle guttered. Ten minutes were gone.

* * *

The chimney was the part Wren hadn't told them about in detail, because telling them in detail would have been cruelty.

It was a crack in the cliff behind the lowest fall, wide enough to stand in, narrowing as it descended, choked with ice and old rubble. It went down perhaps eighty feet to a sloping fan of frozen scree, and from the scree it was a scramble through boulders to the tree line, and from the tree line the world became ordinary again — trails, huts, distance, survival.

But the chimney itself was dark, and the ice inside it was old ice, and old ice lied.

"Rules," Wren said, at the lip. Her voice echoed strangely in the shaft. "Nobody climbs on their own judgment. I call every hold. If I don't call it, you don't touch it. Teo goes first, lowered on the rope, because he can't brace. Sefa follows him. Then the chest. Then Ilo. I go second-to-last. Jonah last, same as always."

"And if you call a hold wrong?" Ilo asked. Not cruelly. Honestly.

"Then we all find out together." She met his eyes. "Same as everything else on this mountain."

He held her gaze a moment, then nodded once, and she understood that something had been decided between them — not forgiveness, not yet, but a truce ratified in ice. *I will trust your hands. You will trust mine.*

They lowered Teo into the dark. His boots scraped. His breath echoed up to them in ragged bursts. Sefa went down the rope beside him, hand over hand, talking to him the whole way, a low continuous murmur that Wren couldn't make out words from and didn't need to. It wasn't the words. It was the sound of a voice that refused to stop.

Then the chest, bumping gently down the shaft on two lines.

Then Wren.

Inside the chimney the world was blue-black and close. Ice glazed the walls like the inside of a bottle. She climbed down by feel and by memory of the thirty seconds of daylight in which she'd memorized the upper section, calling holds upward to Jonah in a voice she kept level by main force — *right boot, wedge, good; left hand, rock not ice, rock; stop, stop, move nothing, listen* — and once she stopped them all for a full minute because she'd heard water running somewhere behind the ice, moving water, the killer's whisper, and they waited until it seemed to fade and went on.

Above her, Jonah's weight came down the shaft steady as tide. Below her, the gray oval of the exit grew, and with it the light, and with the light the sound of wind in trees.

Wren came out of the chimney into falling snow.

Not the dry powder of the heights — real snow, fat flakes, the kind that meant the storm everyone had been dreading had finally stopped dreading them and started arriving. It was already dusting the scree fan, filling their tracks as fast as they made them. She stood in it and felt it land on her eyelashes and understood what it meant: the passes would close. All of them. Whatever lay ahead, they would cross it inside the winter's fist.

Jonah emerged last, coiling the rope with numb fingers. He looked up at the sky, then east, toward the final pass, then down at all of them standing in the thickening white like ghosts at their own funeral.

"Hale's column will turn back in this," Sefa said. "Regular troops will."

"Regular troops," Jonah agreed. "Hale isn't regular troops."

Downslope, half-buried already, Wren saw it: a roofline. Charcoal-black timber, a curl of long-dead smoke from a cold chimney. A hut. Maybe the very one she'd promised Teo.

And beside it, half-hidden under new snow, something that was not a hut and should not have been there.

A sledge runner. Steel, Army pattern, navy-stamped.

Fresh tracks leading away from it — and fresh tracks leading back.

"Jonah," Wren said quietly. "We have a problem."
