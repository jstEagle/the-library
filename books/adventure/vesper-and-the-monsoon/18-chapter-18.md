## 18. Chapter 18

The fever came back with the altitude.

Teo felt it first in his hands, which had never lied to him — hands that could feel a misaligned gear through three layers of glove, that could read the grain of a lock the way other people read print. Now they felt distant, wrapped in wool, as if they belonged to someone else and were only lent to him on sufferance. Then the cold came, the wrong kind of cold, the kind that starts in the marrow instead of the skin, and he knew.

"Jonah." His voice came out as a rasp. He had to try again. "Jonah. I'm burning up."

Ahead on the rope line, Jonah turned. Even through the white chaos Teo could read his face the way he read machines: the quick inventory, the calculation running beneath the stillness. *How bad. How far to the pass. What it costs.*

"How bad?" Jonah asked, which meant he'd already done the arithmetic.

"Bad enough that I'm telling you instead of hiding it."

That got a ghost of a smile. Twenty years of friendship, most of it spent in engine rooms and river mud, and they had long ago agreed that the only currency worth anything between them was the unvarnished truth. Teo had watched Jonah lie beautifully to strangers, to navy officers, to himself — but never to him.

"Can you walk?"

Teo tested the question honestly. His legs answered with a long, wavering static, the signal of a system running past its tolerances. The wound in his side from the col — the outriders' bullet that had torn through flesh and missed everything that mattered by the width of a prayer — had gone hot and silent, which was the worst thing a wound could do. Hot meant infection. Silent meant it was working on him in the dark.

"I can walk," he said. "For a while."

"Then walk. And when you can't, you tell me, and we deal with it. Deal?"

"Deal."

The storm had no interest in their deal.

It had come down off the peaks an hour after dawn, or what they guessed was dawn — the sky and the snow had merged into one seething white nothing, and the world had shrunk to the length of a rope. Sefa led on the long line, reading the slope by the drag of her boots and some instinct she refused to explain. Wren came second, roped to Ilo, who had come back from scouting the fast pass two days ago with frostbitten cheeks and a story about outriders that had made the decision for them: the slow pass, the one that might already be snowed shut. It wasn't shut. It was worse than shut. It was open and howling and trying to kill them one degree at a time.

The chest rode in the middle of the line, lashed to the last mule — the one mule Ilo had managed to keep alive through the col, a sour-faced gray beast named Turnip, whom Teo privately considered the most reliable member of the expedition.

And Teo walked at the rear, roped to Jonah, counting his steps the way he counted gear teeth, because counting was the only thing keeping the fever's tide from coming all the way in.

*One. Two. Three.* The wind took the count away and he started over. *One. Two.*

He thought about the documents. He couldn't help it. They lived in his head now the way a half-built engine lived there, all its parts spread out on the workbench of his mind, demanding assembly. The ships' names — *Cormorant, Bright Meridian, Saltwife* — the dates, the tonnages, the neat columns of coal and cordite requisitioned from Vesper's own navy yards. And the signature. He had touched it through the protective oilcloth with a fingertip, the way you touch a hot stove to confirm it's real.

The signature of the man who was, right now, somewhere behind them in that white hell, bringing a column of soldiers up their trail.

*One. Two. Three.*

"Teo." Jonah's voice, close, right at his shoulder. When had Jonah gotten so close? "You stopped counting out loud."

"I wasn't counting out loud."

"You were. You always do. It's the most annoying thing about you and I need it right now. Count."

So Teo counted, out loud, into the wind, and the numbers went off into the white like coins dropped down a well, and he walked.

* * *

They stopped at midday, or what Sefa judged was midday, in the lee of a rock spine that rose out of the slope like the rib of a drowned ship. They got the tent half up before the wind found a seam and tried to peel it off the mountain. Wren swore at it in three languages, two of which Teo was fairly sure she'd invented.

He sat with his back against the rock and let them work around him, and hated it, and understood it. There was a particular shame in being the one who sat. He had spent his whole life being the one who fixed things — who could take apart a waterlogged lock or a broken winch or a crew's morale and put it back together better than before. Now he was the broken thing, and the fixing was out of his hands, which were, he discovered, shaking.

Sefa crouched in front of him. She had cut the bandage open with her knife, and her face did something careful and controlled when she saw the wound.

"How does it look?" Teo asked.

"Like a wound," Sefa said.

"Sefa."

"Like a wound that's been walking uphill for two days in a fever." She dug in the med kit — their med kit, which had started the journey well-stocked and now contained a roll of linen, a tin of grease, and a bottle of grain alcohol Ilo had been saving for a celebration that was clearly not going to happen. "It needs cleaning, proper stitching, and a month of rest. We have alcohol, thread, and a pass. So."

She worked without ceremony. Teo watched the sky instead, because watching Sefa's hands shake — Sefa, whose hands never shook — was worse than the pain. The alcohol went in and he made a sound he was not proud of and Wren, nearby, said loudly to Ilo, "So the syndicate, they really had a whole ledger of everyone who owed them? Even the—"

"—even the harbormaster, yes, and the harbormaster's *wife*—" Ilo picked up the thread without missing a beat, his voice pitched to fill the space, to give Teo something to be inside of instead of the pain. Teo could have kissed him.

When it was done, Sefa packed the wound and looked at him for a long moment.

"Teo. Listen to me. Fever does arithmetic on you. It subtracts. You'll want to be brave and quiet and not be a burden. Don't. Every gram you can't carry, you hand to us, and we carry it, and that's not charity, that's just how a crew works."

"Since when are you a crew philosopher?"

"Since I deserted from an organization that thought people were equipment." She stood. "Equipment gets left when it breaks. Crew doesn't. So don't be equipment."

* * *

By the time they moved again, the wind had risen to something with intent in it. It didn't gust anymore; it *leaned*, a continuous shove against the chest and shoulders, and the snow stopped falling from the sky and started moving sideways, a horizontal flood of white that erased the slope, the horizon, the distinction between ground and air.

Teo lasted another hour.

He knew it was over when he caught himself walking into the rope, leaning on it, letting it take his weight, and not remembering deciding to. The counting had stopped. He tried to restart it and found the numbers wouldn't hold still — they slid around like parts on a greased bench.

"Jonah," he said. "I'm done walking."

He expected argument. He got a hand on his shoulder, turning him, easing him down against Turnip's flank, and Jonah's face above him, close and calm in a way that Teo knew cost Jonah something enormous.

"Okay," Jonah said. "Okay. We carry you."

"You can't. The chest—"

"The chest weighs less than you do and it can't tell me when I'm being an idiot." Jonah was already uncoiling rope. "Ilo! Skis. Give me your skis."

Ilo's skis were good skis, smuggler's skis, hickory and birch, and he surrendered them with a face like a man donating a kidney. Jonah and Ilo lashed them into rails, side by side, and rigged the rope into a harness — a litter, a sledge, something between the two. Wren contributed her spare parka, folded under where his hip would bear. Sefa re-rigged the rope line so the litter could be hauled in relay.

They loaded him on it like cargo, and the last thing his pride managed before the fever finally dragged him under was: "If you drop me off a cornice, I'm haunting every one of you."

"Only if there's anything left to haunt," Ilo said. "Hold on, engineer."

Then the mountain tilted and slid, and Teo let go.

* * *

He surfaced and sank and surfaced. The fever was an ocean, and he was a piece of wreckage on it, and sometimes he rose high enough to see the world.

He saw Jonah hauling the litter, bent double into the wind, taking the rope across his shoulder the way he'd take a dive line, with that terrible patient strength, and heard him counting too — *one, two, three* — and understood, dimly, that Jonah was counting for him, keeping the rhythm Teo had lost.

He saw Wren walking beside the litter for hours with one hand on his arm, talking to him — telling him about the Vesperan words her grandmother had taught her, words for snow, eleven different words, and how the old language had one that meant *snow that remembers what it covers*, and Teo thought, in the ocean's grip, *that's the one, that's the one falling on me.*

He saw Sefa come back along the line at some gray interval to check the bandage, and put her bare hand against his forehead, and say nothing at all, which from Sefa was a scream.

He saw, once, something that might have been a dream: a light far up the slope behind them, a thread of lantern-glow in the white, moving the way a column moves — many lights, ordered, spaced — and heard Ilo say, very quietly, "They're still coming. In *this*." And heard Sefa answer, "Hale doesn't believe in weather. He believes in orders. Orders don't freeze."

And then the ocean took him again.

But under the fever, under the shaking and the fire, there was a floor. And on the floor of him, something sat that the fever could not reach. It was the same thing that had lived in his hands his whole life — the thing that looked at a seized lock and saw not the seizure but the mechanism, the way through. It had no words. It was simpler than words. It was: *not finished yet.*

The documents weren't delivered. The signature hadn't been read aloud where it needed to be read. The crew — his ridiculous, impossible, assembled-by-accident crew — was still hauling him uphill through the teeth of a storm, and every one of them was spending themselves on him, and a man didn't get to quit while people were spending themselves on him. That wasn't bravery. That was just arithmetic. The fever could do its subtraction. Teo had always been better at arithmetic than the fever.

*Not finished yet,* said the thing on the floor of him.

*One,* said the thing. *Two. Three.*

* * *

He came all the way back at night — real night, or the mountain's version of it, the white gone to a deep gray-blue — because the litter stopped moving and cold air hit his face and someone was shouting.

"—can't be sure in this! Could be wind on the ice, could be a cornice letting go—"

"Then we find out," Jonah said. "Carefully."

Teo opened his eyes to a world of swirling dark and lantern-glow. He was still on the litter. Above him, the slope reared up into nothing, and somewhere up in that nothing there was a sound — a deep, grinding groan, like a ship's hull working in heavy seas, like ten thousand tons of ice deciding something.

"The pass," Sefa said. She had her lantern up, and its light showed her face, tight and pale. "It's the serac field above the notch. The storm's loading it. If it comes down while we're in the throat—"

"Then we go through before it does," Jonah said. "Or we sit here and Hale's column walks up behind us and we find out what he does to witnesses."

Nobody said anything. The wind filled the silence. Teo, from his litter, felt the strange clarity of the fever-burned — too weak to act, therefore free to see. He looked at his crew: Ilo with his frostbitten face and his donated skis; Wren with her hand still on his arm; Sefa with her lantern like a held breath; Jonah with the rope already in his hands, already turning toward the dark throat of the pass.

And behind them, far below, faint as a memory: lights. A column of them. Coming on, in weather that would kill any sane commander's pursuit, because the man leading it did not believe the world could refuse him.

"Teo." Jonah crouched by the litter. Snow crusted his beard. His eyes were clear and exhausted and absolutely certain. "Can you hold on through the notch? It's maybe an hour, maybe two. Rough going. I need to know if we rope the litter to the line or leave it and carry you."

Teo looked up at the grinding dark where the pass waited, and behind it, somewhere, the valley where everything they carried was supposed to arrive. He thought about the signature. He thought about the eleven words for snow.

"Rope the litter," he said. "I'm not finished yet."

Jonah held his eyes a moment, then nodded once, and stood, and turned to the others.

"Rope the litter," he said. "We cross tonight — before the ice up there finishes deciding. Sefa, you lead. Ilo, Wren, relay on the litter. Move fast, move quiet, and whatever you hear behind us—" he glanced down the slope, at the distant crawling lights, "—don't stop to look."

They roped up. The line went taut, one body to the next, Teo's litter lashed into the middle of it like a knot that everyone's lives ran through. And as they moved into the throat of the pass, under the groaning seracs, into the white dark where the mountain was making up its mind, Teo heard it — faint, far down the trail, carried on a lull in the wind:

Voices. Many voices. The cadence of a column closing ranks.

Hale had entered the storm.
