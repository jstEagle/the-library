## 1. Six Tides

The wreck sat in forty meters of green silence, and Marguerite Marchetti had been arguing with it for three days.

"Stubborn old girl," she murmured into her regulator, running a gloved hand along the hull's barnacled shoulder. The *Corvina* — or whatever name she'd worn before the navy painted over it — lay canted on her port side like a drunk asleep against a wall, and every hatch, every companionway, every interesting-looking seam had been welded shut with the particular thoroughness of people hiding something. Not sealed for safety. Sealed for embarrassment.

Marguerite had made a living off other people's embarrassments for eleven years. Cargo manifests that didn't match their holds. Insurance claims that didn't match their fires. She'd learned to read a ship the way her mother read faces — by what it was trying not to say.

But today the ship was winning.

Her air gauge read a third remaining. Above her, filtered through the water, the *Pelican's Rest* rocked on its anchor chain, and Suri's voice came down the comm line, bright and impatient as a gull.

"Bosun says you've been down long enough to grow gills, Captain. Also there's coffee. Also also, Dev tried to make fish curry and we may need to evacuate."

"Evacuate the boat or the kitchen?"

"Yes," Suri said cheerfully.

Marguerite laughed into her regulator, bubbles streaming up past her face like a string of silver beads, and decided the navy could keep its secrets one more day. She kicked for the surface, rising through the thermocline where warm water met cold and the world went briefly blurry, like looking through an old window.

She broke the surface into late-afternoon gold. The atoll's lagoon stretched flat as hammered tin, and somewhere beyond the reef the first trade winds of the season were gathering their breath. Monsoon soon. Everyone in the archipelago could feel it coming, the way old people felt weather in their knees.

Dev hauled her aboard with the easy strength of a man who complained about everything except work. He was fifty-three, built like a barrel with opinions, and he handed her a towel and a mug of coffee in the same motion.

"Navy still not talking?"

"Navy still not talking. The liaison boat comes by twice a day to remind us our permit is provisional and our presence is tolerated."

"Tolerated," Dev repeated, spitting over the side with ceremonial disgust. "We found this wreck. We mapped this wreck. We're the ones who told them where it was, and now we need permission to look at our own discovery."

"They said salvage rights pending investigation."

"Investigation of what? It's been four years. Whatever they're investigating, they're investigating it very slowly and from a beach bar, if the liaison officer's sunburn is anything to go by."

Suri appeared from below decks, seventeen years old, salt-blond hair in a perpetual state of rebellion, holding a clipboard she used mostly as a prop for feeling important. "Captain, tide table's updated. We lose our working window in two days unless we move to the outer anchorage. Also the curry situation has been resolved. Mostly. There's a smell."

"There's always a smell on this boat. I've contributed to it myself." Marguerite settled against the rail, letting the wind dry her hair, and allowed herself the small luxury of being tired. Three days of diving a locked box. Three days of watching a navy patrol boat circle like a dog around a bone it couldn't be bothered to chew but refused to share.

It should have been enough to walk away from. Wrecks were like lovers; the stubborn ones taught you nothing except how much time you'd wasted.

Then the radio crackled.

"*Pelican's Rest*, *Pelican's Rest*, incoming vessel, east channel, no registry broadcast. You seeing this, Captain?"

"I see it," Marguerite said, because she did — a white-hulled fast launch cutting the lagoon's mirror into ribbons, running too fast for a channel full of coral heads, which meant either a fool or someone who knew these waters better than the charts did.

Both, as it turned out.

* * *

Tomas Reyes stepped aboard without asking permission, which was so perfectly him that Marguerite almost laughed. Eleven years since they'd spoken in anything but insults relayed through harbormasters, and he still boarded her boat like he owned the dock beneath it.

He'd gone gray at the temples. He'd gotten broader through the shoulders, softer through the middle, and his hands — those careful, clever hands she remembered from dive school, hands that could tie a bowline blindfolded underwater — had a new scar across the knuckles. But the smile hadn't changed. That smile had always been a lockpick.

"Marchetti."

"Reyes. You're trespassing."

"It's a public anchorage." He looked around the deck with theatrical appraisal. "Still running a crew of misfits and orphans, I see. Hello, Dev. Still cooking like a grudge?"

"Still swimming like a rock, Reyes?" Dev said pleasantly, and went back to his lines.

Suri stared at Tomas with open fascination, the way teenagers stare at famous people or dangerous animals, categories Tomas occupied simultaneously.

"You have six days before monsoon landfall," Tomas said. "I have a job. It pays triple your navy permit rate, it's legal — mostly — and it requires a diver who can read a wreck the way you do. Which is you. Still, after all these years, you."

Marguerite crossed her arms. "You ran me aground in the Vasse Strait in '19 and blamed my charts."

"Your charts were wrong."

"My charts were fine. Your ego was wrong, and it weighed more than my boat."

He had the grace to wince. "Fair. I've been paying for that ego for years, mostly in lawyers." He produced, from inside his jacket, a document in a heavy sealed envelope — actual wax, actual ribbon, the kind of contract people used when they wanted a signature to feel like a ceremony. "Before you throw it overboard, look at the coordinates."

And that was the moment the afternoon changed temperature.

Because she already had. Her eyes had gone to the envelope's corner the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth. Six digits. A latitude. A longitude. And her body knew them before her mind consented to — the way you know a song from two notes, the way you know a staircase in the dark.

Eight years ago, in that square of ocean, her brother Luca had gone down on a working dive and not come back. Eight years ago, Marguerite had hung up her fins for a year, then picked them up again because grief doesn't pay harbor fees, and because the sea was the only place she could hear him — the memory of him laughing through his regulator, telling her she dove like their grandmother danced, all elbows and joy.

The coordinates matched. Not approximately. Exactly.

"Where did you get these?" she asked, and her voice came out level, which cost her something.

"A client," Tomas said. "A good client, with deep pockets and a short fuse and a deadline measured in tides. Six tides, Marchetti. After that, the monsoon sits on this whole coast for five months, and whatever's down there belongs to the storm until spring."

"What's down there?"

"That's the job. Find out." He held out the envelope. "Sealed terms. You sign, you read. You don't sign, you never see the numbers again."

"That's not how contracts work."

"It's how *my* contracts work." His eyes flicked toward the horizon, toward the east channel, quick and involuntary — a man checking whether he was being watched. "Look. I know what you think of me. Most of it's earned. But I'm offering you triple rate, first refusal on salvage, and a dive nobody else in this archipelago can make safely. In six days. Take it or leave it."

Behind him, Dev had stopped working. Suri had stopped pretending to work. The whole boat had gone quiet in that particular way crews go quiet when they can smell a bad idea coming, the way dogs smell rain.

"Captain," Dev said carefully, "a word."

"In a minute."

"Captain. *Now*, preferably."

Marguerite took the envelope. She didn't open it. She held it the way you hold something that might be a gift or might be a hook, and she looked at Tomas Reyes, and eight years of grief stood up inside her chest and put its hand on the scale.

Six tides. The sea would rise and fall six times between now and the storm, and somewhere in that arithmetic was either a payday or an answer — maybe both, maybe the same thing.

"I'll give you my answer at dawn," she said.

Tomas smiled his lockpick smile. "Dawn works." He stepped back onto his launch, threw Dev a salute that Dev did not return, and ran his engine too fast out of the channel, because some men never learn and some men never want to.

* * *

The crew meeting happened in the galley, which smelled faintly of Dev's defeated curry, under the single swinging lamp, with rain starting to tick against the portholes like fingers testing glass.

"Say it plainly, Dev."

"He boards your boat after eleven years, waves money, and won't tell you what the job is until you sign?" Dev turned his mug in his thick hands. "Captain, I've run cargo with smugglers and salvaged for insurance sharks, and even the sharks showed you the goods first."

"The seal's wax, not law. I can break it and walk away."

"And he knows that?"

"He knows I'm curious. Curiosity's the whole design."

Suri, curled on the bench with her knees up, spoke quietly. "I looked him up. After he left. Harbor records, incident reports, the gossip at the fuel dock." She hesitated. "There's a pattern, Captain. Every job he's run in the last eight years, somebody got hurt. Not him. Somebody working for him. A rigger in Port Anselm. Two divers off Ceylon Deep. Always the same story: equipment failure, tragic accident, settlement signed quietly."

The lamp swung. Rain ticked. Marguerite thought of Luca — of the last thing he ever said to her on a boat deck, which was that she worried too much, and the second-to-last, which was that Reyes's outfit paid double and double was worth a little risk.

Luca had died on a Reyes-adjacent job. She'd never been able to prove the connection. She'd never stopped trying.

"Here's what I keep circling," Marguerite said slowly. "Those coordinates. He didn't stumble on them. He didn't buy them from a chart house. He gave me the exact spot where my brother died, wrapped in a contract, timed to a deadline, wearing a smile. Either that's coincidence — and I stopped believing in maritime coincidence around the time I started reading wrecks — or he chose that number on purpose. And if he chose it, he wanted me to recognize it."

"Why would he want that?"

"Either to wound me, or to warn me, or because he thinks grief makes me predictable." She set the envelope on the table between them, wax seal up, unbroken. "Or because he needs a diver who has a reason to go down there that has nothing to do with money. Grief is the best motive in the world, Dev. It doesn't ask questions until it's already at forty meters."

Dev rubbed his jaw. "So we vote. All hands. That's how this boat works."

"We vote." Marguerite looked around the galley — Dev, solid as ballast; Suri, sharp-eyed and loyal past the point of sense; old Fen the engineer, who'd said nothing yet and whose silences historically preceded mutiny or wisdom. "And whatever we decide, we decide tonight. Because dawn's coming, and Tomas Reyes will be standing in my wheelhouse at first light with that smile, and I intend to know exactly what I'm going to do before he does."

Fen finally spoke, from the shadows by the engine-room door, in his gravel voice. "One question, Captain. Before we vote."

"Ask it."

"If you break that seal tonight and read those terms alone — no crew, no witnesses — will you still be able to walk away tomorrow? Or will the coordinates have you already?"

The lamp swung. Nobody answered, because nobody needed to.

Marguerite picked up the envelope, carried it to her cabin, and sat with it in her lap while the rain thickened against the hull. She thought about Luca at sixteen, teaching her to clear a flooded mask, patient and laughing. She thought about the navy's welded hatches, forty meters down, keeping secrets with government patience. She thought about six tides, counting themselves down somewhere beyond the reef, each one pulling the monsoon closer like a curtain.

Then she broke the seal.

The terms were clean — cleaner than Tomas's contracts had any right to be. Triple rate. Salvage first-refusal. A confidentiality clause with teeth. And a target description, typed in bland bureaucratic language, that described a vessel lost with all hands eight years ago in precisely that square of ocean, a vessel whose official record ended in three words:

*Sunk. Cause unknown.*

Marguerite read those three words until the lamp burned low, and understood, with the cold certainty of a woman reading her own chart, that the cause was not unknown. The cause was down there, behind the welds, waiting in the dark with her brother.

At dawn, when Tomas Reyes stepped into her wheelhouse, she slid the signed contract across the chart table and said only: "Six tides. Let's not waste one."

His smile flickered — just once, just slightly, like a compass needle near iron — and Marguerite Marchetti, who had spent eight years learning to read wrecks, read it perfectly.

Behind her, on deck, Dev began casting off the lines without being asked, and Suri stood at the rail watching the eastern sky, where the first clouds of the monsoon were stacking up like a fleet nobody had chartered.
