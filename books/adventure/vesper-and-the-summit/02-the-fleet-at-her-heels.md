## 2. The Fleet at Her Heels

The *Vesper* ran south through the reef channels with her engine singing the only song Marguerite trusted — the low, honest complaint of a diesel that had been maintained by someone who loved it, which meant Dev, which meant it complained the way family complains: constantly, and out of devotion.

"Three knots off the tide," Dev called from the engine hatch, his voice carrying up through the deck plates with the smell of warm oil. "If we hold this line we make the outer passage before the water drops."

"We'll hold the line," Marguerite said. "Hold your own head under the hatch and check the cooling intake again."

"Already checked it."

"Check it again. I like knowing where you are when I'm nervous, and I am always nervous."

He laughed and went back under, and Marguerite turned to the chart table where Tomas stood with his sleeves rolled, tracing the channel with one blunt finger. Eight years since they'd shared a deck, and he still stood at charts like a man reading a love letter he wasn't sure was addressed to him.

"The fleet left Port Calloway at midnight," he said. "Three hulls. The *Iron Meridian* leads them — Okonjo's outfit. They'll run the deep water around the atoll and lose half a day on fueling. We cut the reef."

"We cut the reef and lose a propeller if your coordinates are wrong by a hundred meters."

"My coordinates aren't wrong."

"Your coordinates got me to sign a contract in the dark," Marguerite said. "That's a different kind of wrong."

He looked at her then — that steady, patient look that had always made her feel like a specimen under glass — and said nothing, which was its own kind of answer.

At the bow, Suri was coiling lines with the fierce precision of someone who had decided that if the world was going to be untrustworthy, at least her knots would not be.

* * *

Suri had been a deckhand for four years, and in four years she had learned three things about Marguerite Marchetti: she never ran an engine harder than the sea allowed, she never asked a diver to go where she wouldn't go first, and she never, ever talked about her brother.

So when the new charter came through — sealed, navy-adjacent, paid in advance by a man with a rival's eyes — Suri did what she always did with things that didn't add up. She counted them.

One: the coordinates. She'd seen the chart. She'd also seen Marguerite's face when she'd seen the chart, and Marguerite's face had gone the color of old rope.

Two: the timeline. Six tides. Nobody paid that kind of money for six tides unless the thing at the bottom was worth more than the boat, the crew, and possibly the law.

Three: Tomas Reyes. Suri had been on deck the night, three years back, when Marguerite had gotten word that Reyes was diving the *Cormorant* wreck and had said, very quietly, into her coffee, "I hope he drowns." She had not said it with humor.

Suri finished her coils, went below, and did the thing she was not supposed to do, which was open the chart locker where Marguerite kept the old files.

She found the manifest at the bottom, under eight years of dust and a dried-out marker. It was a cargo declaration for a naval transport — the *AS Vantage*, hull number half-scratched out, the paper water-stained but legible. Suri read it twice, because the first time she was sure she'd misread it.

The *Vantage* had gone down, according to every registry, in a storm. Loss of hull integrity. Tragedy, weather, God's will.

The manifest said otherwise. It listed cargo loaded at Port Calloway — crates, sealed, manifest weight four hundred tons — and then, in a different hand, stamped across the page in red: **OFFLOAD COMPLETE — SCUTTLE CHARGE AUTHORIZED — SEE ORDER 114**.

Scuttle charge. Authorized. Somebody had sunk that ship on purpose, and then somebody had made sure the paperwork sank with her.

Suri sat down on the chart locker's edge and held the paper very still, the way you hold still when you've stepped on something that might be a stick or might be a sea snake.

Above her, through the deck, she could hear Marguerite's voice calling depth soundings, steady as a metronome, and Suri thought: *she knows. She's known since the wheelhouse. That's why her face did that.*

She folded the manifest into her shirt and went back up to coil lines that were already perfect, and to watch the eastern sky, where the monsoon clouds had begun to organize themselves into something with intent.

* * *

The reef channel narrowed at midday to a throat of coral and green water, and Marguerite took the wheel herself, because the *Vesper* deserved a hand that had grown up on these waters and because she did not trust her own attention to anything else right now.

"Contact," Dev said from the bow, binoculars up. "Stern light. Two points off starboard. Running dark otherwise."

Marguerite's stomach dropped. "The *Meridian*?"

"Too small. Tender, maybe. Scout boat for the fleet."

"They're not waiting to fuel, then." She felt it like a hand between her shoulder blades — the fleet at her heels, exactly as promised. "Tomas. Your friends are ahead of schedule."

"They're not my friends."

"Your employers, then. Even worse."

The scout boat was fast and knew the channel. It ran the throat a half-mile ahead of them, and Marguerite watched it swing wide around the marker rock and thought, with a diver's instinct, *that line's wrong, the current's pushing the channel east this season* — and then the scout boat proved her right by grinding, audibly, even at this distance, on something that was not on any chart.

The sound carried over the water like a scream.

"They're holed," Dev said. "Taking the rocks."

"Then they're someone else's problem."

"Skipper." Dev lowered the binoculars. "There's a diver in the water."

Marguerite saw it then — a splash of orange, a head, an arm waving. The scout boat had put a man over the side to read the channel by eye, and the current had taken him, and the current was taking him east, toward the outer reef, toward the long gray comb-over of breakers where boats went to die and divers went to disappear.

She had the *Vesper* swung onto the new heading before she'd decided to.

"Marguerite." Tomas's voice, careful. "The tide window—"

"I know the tide window."

"The fleet—"

"I *know the tide window*, Tomas." She throttled up. "Get a line ready. Dev, fenders and a heaving line. Suri, my gear — I'll go in if I have to."

"You won't have to," Suri said, already hauling the rescue canister, and there was something in her voice Marguerite couldn't read, and didn't have time to.

* * *

The man in the water was a boy, really — nineteen, maybe twenty, fleet coveralls, terror coming off him in waves you could almost see. By the time the *Vesper* reached him he'd gone under once already, and when he came up the second time his arms were windmilling wrong, the drowning-man's climb, and Marguerite knew they had seconds, not minutes.

The swell was running six feet through the throat. A throw line wouldn't reach. A boat alongside in that water would crush him against the hull.

So she did the thing she'd sworn off doing. She looked at Tomas.

"Your gear fits me," she said.

His jaw tightened. "The current will drag you both onto the reef shelf."

"Then I'll need someone on the line who doesn't panic." She was already stripping off her shirt. "Can I trust you?"

The question hung there, eight years deep, full of everything she wasn't saying.

Tomas met her eyes. "You always could," he said. "That was never the problem."

She didn't ask what the problem had been, then. There was no time, and there was a boy dying, and there was a reef coming up fast and gray.

The water hit her like a wall of cold glass. The current took her instantly — a great muscular hand shoving her east — and she swam the angle, hard, counting strokes, until the boy's orange shoulder broke the surface ten feet ahead. She got the rescue canister under his arms. He grabbed her, of course, the way drowning people do, and she took the classic hold, locked him against her chest, and shouted into his ear the oldest words in the trade:

"I've got you. Stop fighting me. *I've got you.*"

The line came taut. Above, on the deck, she could see Tomas hauling, hand over hand, Dev braced behind him, Suri on the winch, and the whole boat leaning into it, the *Vesper* herself pulling, and Marguerite thought, absurdly, in the middle of the chaos, with salt in her teeth and a stranger's heartbeat hammering against her collarbone: *he didn't let go of the line. Watch him the whole way in — he didn't let go.*

They came over the rail in a tangle of limbs and seawater, and the boy vomited half the reef channel onto the deck, and Dev wrapped him in blankets, and Marguerite lay on her back gasping like a landed fish while Tomas crouched over her, dripping, his hand hovering an inch above her shoulder as if he'd wanted to touch it and thought better of it.

"You cut it fine," he said.

"I cut everything fine," she said. "That's my whole reputation."

He laughed — a real one, startled out of him — and for one moment, one tide, one breath, it was eight years ago and they were young and nothing had happened yet.

Then the moment passed, the way moments do, and Marguerite remembered the chart in the wheelhouse, and the coordinates, and the red-stamped word she hadn't seen yet but was already, somewhere in her bones, beginning to dread.

* * *

The boy's name was Pell, and he slept in the galley under two blankets, and Suri sat across from him with the fleet's scout boat's dropped satchel — recovered from the rocks, Dev's idea, salvage law being salvage law — and went through it with a deckhand's thoroughness.

Rations. A signal lamp. Charts.

And a folder, stamped with the fleet's house mark, containing a working summary of the *Vantage* wreck site. Depth, hull condition, cargo hold locations. And one line, underlined twice in someone's impatient hand:

*Priority: recover manifest documentation from the safe before the navy dive team arrives. Nothing else matters. Nothing else surfaces.*

Suri read it three times.

*Nothing else surfaces.*

She looked up through the galley porthole at the darkening sky, then down at the folded paper pressing warm against her ribs — the old manifest, the red stamp, the scuttle order — and she understood that she was now carrying two halves of the same lie, and that the man who'd brought them here was sitting ten feet above her head, keeping watch at the rail, keeping watch over all of them.

She went up on deck. Marguerite was at the wheel. Tomas was at the rail, watching the fleet's lights gather on the southern horizon like a slow, patient storm.

"Skipper," Suri said quietly. "When we make anchorage — I need to show you something I found."

Marguerite glanced at her, read her face the way she read water, and nodded once.

Behind them, unnoticed, the monsoon's first thunder rolled down off the eastern clouds, and ahead of them, six fathoms down and forty years dark, the *Vantage* waited with everything the water had kept.

Suri put her hand flat against her shirt, over the folded paper, over the truth, and held her course.
