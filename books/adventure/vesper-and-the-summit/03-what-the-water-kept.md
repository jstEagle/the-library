## 3. What the Water Kept

The wreck opened for Marguerite the way old houses open for people who once lived in them: reluctantly, with a groan, exhaling silt and memory.

She went down alone. Tomas had argued for a paired dive, protocol, safety in numbers — his voice crackling through her earpiece with that particular patience he used when he thought she was being reckless. She'd switched it off at forty meters. Some conversations you needed to have without an interpreter.

*Vesper* had gone down on her side, half-buried in coral sand off the shoal, her hull furred with nineteen years of growth. The navy's seal was still there — a lead strip across the aft hatch, stamped with numbers nobody would explain. Marguerite ran her glove along it and felt, absurdly, like a burglar in a cathedral.

Inside, the dark was total until her lamp found the walls. Cabins collapsed into themselves. A mess table bolted to the deck, tilted at an angle that made her stomach tilt with it. And everywhere the small archaeology of panic: a boot, a coffee mug fused to the metal by rust, a child's shoe that made no sense at all until she remembered some crews brought families on long hauls.

Her brother's name was Emile. Everyone called him Milo. He'd been twenty-six when *Vesper* took him, and the inquiry had called it equipment failure, and their mother had died believing it, and Marguerite had spent nineteen years diving other people's wrecks because she could not stop looking for the truth of this one.

Tomas had known. That was the thing sitting cold in her chest as she swam the corridor. He'd known the coordinates were hers before he ever slid the sealed contract across the bar in Puerto Aliso. He'd watched her read them and said nothing, and she had signed anyway, because ambition is just dread wearing a better coat.

* * *

The captain's cabin was aft, past a bulkhead door wedged open by something soft. Her lamp caught it: a canvas dive bag, silt-stiffened, its strap rotted through. She worked it free gently, the way you handle anything that has waited a long time.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job against nineteen years of saltwater, was a logbook.

Milo's handwriting jumped at her like a face across a crowded room. *Day 4 — viz poor but holding. T. says the manifest doesn't add up. Cargo listed as machine parts weighs like munitions.* Her heart began a slow, heavy percussion. *Day 6 — T. wants to log a discrepancy with the company. R. says no. Argument in the galley. R. says some cargo is better lost.*

R. The first mate, Rafael Ostrova, dead these nineteen years, drowned with everyone else. Convenient, a dead man to blame.

Then, tucked in the back cover, a plastic sleeve. Photographs. And a page torn from someone else's log — Tomas Reyes's own, from the year before *Vesper*, when he and Milo had trained together and been, briefly, friends. On it, in Tomas's neat draftsman's hand, was a diagram of a quick-release harness clip. Annotated. *Modified per M.M.'s request — releases under load. Do not use for tethered work.*

Releases under load.

Marguerite hung in the black water and read it three times, because the water seemed to be pushing the words around, rearranging them, refusing to let them mean what they meant.

On the last dive, Milo had been tethered to the salvage line when the current surged. The inquiry report said his harness failed. His line parted clean, no fraying, no defect — *parted*, the report said, as if lines simply decided things. And here was Tomas's own hand, years earlier, describing a clip that released under load, modified at someone's request, and a warning not to use it tethered.

And beneath the diagram, one more line, added later, the ink different:

*M. asked again. I should have said no twice.*

M. Marguerite. She had asked. God help her, she remembered asking — young, greedy, wanting a quick-release rig like the commercial divers had, never thinking, never once thinking —

No. No, that wasn't it either. She stared at the sleeve until her eyes burned behind the mask. Someone had requested the modification. The initials were hers. But Milo had written *T. says* in his own log, and Tomas had kept this page, carried it, hidden it inside her brother's book like a confession he couldn't say aloud.

Or like evidence he'd kept in case he ever needed leverage.

The bulkhead groaned. Somewhere above, the sea changed its mind about the weather.

* * *

She surfaced into the first spit of the squall, rain needling the swell, and Suri hauled her over the gunwale with hands that shook.

"You're bleeding air," Suri said. "Your tank. It's reading low and it shouldn't be."

Marguerite looked at the gauge, then at the gear rack, then at the crew — Dele coiling line too carefully, old Ferro not meeting anyone's eyes, the whole boat wearing the tight faces of people who had started counting who benefits.

"Someone's been in my kit," she said quietly.

Nobody answered, which was an answer.

Tomas came up from the wheelhouse, squinting at the western sky where the monsoon had begun assembling itself out of bruised cloud. "We've got maybe two hours before this turns ugly. Tell me you found something worth dying for."

"I found your handwriting," Marguerite said.

Something crossed his face — fast, but not fast enough. Not for her. Nineteen years of reading water had taught her to read men too, and Tomas Reyes had just flinched like a fish on a hook.

"We'll talk below," he said. "When we're clear of the shoal—"

"The fleet's coming," Dele called from the bow. Three sets of running lights, threading the reef channel, coming on fast and arrogant.

Tomas made his choice then, and it told her everything. "We go down together, now, while they're still outside the channel. We finish the job and we outrun the weather." He was already pulling his gear, already not looking at her. "You wanted the truth, Marchetti. It's still down there. All of it."

* * *

They went down together, which was the last honest thing either of them did for an hour.

The squall arrived overhead like a fist on a drum, and the wreck felt it — every surge translating down through sixty feet of green water into shudders along the hull. Sediment bloomed. Their lamps carved tunnels through soup.

Marguerite led him to the captain's cabin and held the logbook up between them, the plastic sleeve pressed flat against the oilcloth so his own words faced him in her lamplight.

*Releases under load.*

Tomas's regulator hissed. Behind the mask plate, his eyes did arithmetic — denial, calculation, surrender — and landed somewhere she hadn't expected: exhaustion.

"It wasn't me," he said, bubbles streaming. He pulled the mouthpiece aside. "I built the clip, yes. For her. For *you*. You wanted what the commercial rigs had, and I was twenty-three and stupid and in love with you, and I built it, and I told Milo never to let it near a tethered line." His voice cracked across the frequency. "But I didn't cut anything, Marguerite. I've spent nineteen years trying to prove I didn't."

"Then why seal the contract? Why the coordinates? Why now?"

"Because the fleet's owner is Rafael Ostrova's daughter, and she's spent nineteen years and a fortune making sure *Vesper* stays closed. Because your brother figured out the scuttling and the cargo and the man who ordered it, and that man's family is still powerful, and the only proof left in the world is in this hull." He grabbed her arm, gloves squeaking on neoprene. "I hired you because you're the only diver alive stubborn enough to get it, and because I owed Milo a debt I can only pay in truth."

Above them, the hull screamed.

It happened the way disasters happen — all at once, after waiting forever. The aft section, weakened by nineteen years and the storm surge hammering it, gave way with a sound like a whale dying. The corridor behind them folded. Black water came through the bulkhead door not as a flood but as a decision, instant and total, and Tomas shoved her toward the forward hatch and turned back for the logbook bag —

— and the deck dropped out from under him.

A deck plate, corroded through, took his weight and surrendered. He went down into the machinery space below with a cry that came through the comm as pure animal fear, and a cable reel came down after him, and then the water, and then the dark.

"Tomas!" Her own voice, ragged in her ears.

His lamp, fallen, lit the space below like a stage. She saw him pinned at the leg under the reel frame, saw the water rising around him, saw him wrench his mask off because it had flooded, saw his face — the face of the boy who'd built her a clip because he loved her, the face of the man she'd hated for nineteen years — looking up at her through the rising water.

"Marchetti." Coughing. "The code. You know the code. You never leave anyone below."

The diver's code. Older than any contract, older than any grief. The first thing her father ever taught her, before he taught her to swim: *the person below is yours, no matter what they've done.*

The hull groaned again. Above, faintly, through sixty feet of storm-churned sea, she could feel the fleet arriving — engines idling over the shoal, patient as sharks.

The water reached Tomas's chest.

"Please," he said.
