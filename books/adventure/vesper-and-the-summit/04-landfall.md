## 4. Landfall

The line went taut in my hands and I pulled, and pulled, and the sea gave him up the way the sea gives up everything — grudgingly, in pieces, wearing the face of what it took.

Tomas Reyes came through the hatch of the flooding hull like a drowned man being born, coughing half the Andaman Sea back out of his lungs, his mask gone, his regulator hanging by its hose like a severed limb. I got my arm under his and kicked for the surface, and the storm that had been growling on the horizon all morning finally made good on its promise. Rain came down in ropes. The swell picked us up and set us down again like a hand testing fruit it wasn't sure it wanted.

He was sobbing by the time we broke through. I want to be honest about that, because I spent three days hating him and the hating had made him into something enormous, a monster built out of my brother's last bubbles. Sobbing, clinging to my spare line, saying my name over and over — *Marguerite, Marguerite* — as if it were a rope too, as if it might hold.

"You cut his line." I said it into his ear, calm as a woman reading a tide table. The squall was screaming around us, but my voice came out level. I'd surprised myself with it. "You cut his line, and you've been paying me salvage money for six years like it was interest on a debt."

"I didn't mean—" He gagged on seawater and the rest of it. "The manifest. He found the manifest. He was going to report it, and the company — Marguerite, they would have ruined me, they would have—"

"You cut his line."

"Yes." The word came out of him the way the wreck had given him up: grudgingly, in pieces. "Yes. God forgive me. Yes."

Above us, the *Vesper's* rail appeared through the rain, and Suri's face with it, and four more hands leaning over, and a heaving line snaking down. I caught it. I held on. The diver's code says you never leave anyone below, and I hadn't, and I want you to know that the code held even when I wanted it to drown.

That is the whole of what happened in the water. The rest happened on deck, in the harbor, in the weeks after, and I will tell it plainly because plainness is what I owe him now.

* * *

We limped into harbor with the monsoon chewing at our heels, the *Vesper* riding low and groaning, her hold full of the evidence nobody had paid us to find. The navy-sealed manifest, water-stained and legible. The scuttling charges' wiring, photographed in place. And the recording — because Tomas, in his desperation, had talked the whole way up, and the helmet cam I'd switched on before the dive had heard every word.

The crew gathered in the galley that night while the storm worked itself into a fury outside. Rain hit the portholes like thrown gravel. Nobody sat down. That was the strangest part — five people standing in a rolling room, watching me, while the payday they'd bled for sat in a sealed case on the table between us.

"You could sell it back to him," Suri said. Not accusing. Just laying it on the table next to the case, the way she laid everything, flat and honest. "The recording. He'd pay triple to make it disappear. We'd all eat for a year."

"We could," I said.

"Or you could take it to the inquiry." She turned her mug in her hands. "And the company lawyers will drag us through the mud for two years, and the navy will seal half the testimony, and Tomas Reyes will maybe see eighteen months in a comfortable facility with a view of the water."

"Yes."

"And your brother will still be dead."

"Yes." I looked around at them — old Dev with his bad knee and his worse opinions, the twins who hadn't spoken to each other since the reef channel, little Ferro who'd signed on for one season and stayed for six. "I spent six years thinking the water killed him. Six years being afraid of it and loving it and not knowing which was the lie. The truth costs everything, Suri. But it's the only thing that buys you back."

Dev snorted — Dev snorts at everything, including sunrises — and then he sat down, which was his way of voting. One by one they sat, until only Suri was standing, and then she sat too, and reached over, and put her hand flat on the sealed case.

"Take it in tomorrow," she said. "Before he can buy it back. Before we can talk ourselves out of it."

* * *

The maritime inquiry building smelled of mildew and old paper, and the officer who took my deposition had the patient, tired face of a man who had heard every kind of confession the sea could produce. I gave him the recording. I gave him the manifest. I gave him my brother's dive log, its pages soft as cloth from six years in a drowned hull, and I watched the officer read the last entry and then look up at me with something that was not pity, which I was grateful for, because I had decided I could not survive pity that morning.

Tomas was already in custody — he'd turned himself in, the harbor master told me, two hours after we docked. "Said he couldn't sleep," the man said, shrugging. "Said the water kept talking to him."

It does that. I could have told him. It talks to all of us. The only question is what you do when it says your name.

* * *

The storm broke on the third day, the way storms do — all at once, like a door slamming, the sky scrubbed clean and blue and indifferent. The harbor went glass-calm. The *Vesper* sat at her mooring looking, for the first time since I'd known her, like she'd been forgiven for something.

I took the dinghy out alone at dawn. In the bag between my feet were my brother's effects, the ones I'd carried from port to port for six years like ballast I couldn't name: his dive knife, his lucky coin with the hole drilled through it, the terrible harmonica he could not play and refused to stop playing, the photograph of the two of us at fourteen, squinting into a sun that hadn't yet learned to be cruel.

I said a few words. I won't repeat them; they were between me and him and the water. Then I let them go, one by one, and watched the sea take them gently, without argument, as if it had only been holding them for me all along.

When I rowed back, Suri was on the dock with two cups of coffee and the expression of a woman who has been awake since four and intends to make it everyone's problem.

"Well?" she said.

"Well what?"

"Are you going to stand there looking peaceful all morning, or are you going to help me convince Dev that the *Vesper* needs her hull re-coppered before the season turns? Because he says she doesn't, and he's wrong, and you're the only person he's afraid of."

I took the coffee. It was terrible. It was the best thing I'd ever tasted.

"Re-coppering," I said, stepping onto the dock, into the ordinary bright morning, into the rest of my life. "How much is it going to cost us?"

Suri grinned. "More than the truth did."

She wasn't wrong. But for the first time in six years, I could pay it.
