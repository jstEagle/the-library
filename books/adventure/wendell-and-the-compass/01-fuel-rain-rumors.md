## 1. Fuel, Rain, Rumors

The rain found them twenty minutes before the strip did.

Lena Thornbury felt it first as a change in the engine's voice — a thickening, a wetness in the note that meant water was getting where water shouldn't. She eased the stick forward and dropped the old transport out of the cloud deck into a world gone green and dripping: flooded paddies stitched together by mud roads, a river running high and brown as tea left too long, and there, cutting through the treeline at an angle that only a drunk or a desperate man would have chosen, the strip.

It wasn't much of a strip. It never was, this far upcountry. Four hundred meters of graded clay that turned to porridge every monsoon, hemmed in on one side by banana palms and on the other by a drainage ditch that had ambitions of becoming a canal. A windsock hung from a bamboo pole, soaked to a dark rag, pointing stubbornly northeast. Someone had painted a faded number on a fuel drum near the tree line — *7* — which either marked the drum or the year it had been placed there. With this outfit, it could have been both.

"Strip's soft," Sefa said from the navigator's seat. She'd been watching it through the rain-streaked window with her chin propped on her fist, the way she watched everything — like she had all the time in the world and intended to use none of it. "Left wheel's going to sink past the hub if we're not quick."

"Then we'll be quick." Lena lined up short over the palms, letting the ground rush up to meet her instead of chasing it down. The trick to a soft strip wasn't gentleness; it was commitment. You touched down exactly where you meant to and you didn't change your mind, because the clay would punish hesitation worse than it punished weight.

The wheels hit. The plane slewed left, caught, plowed a fan of red-brown water off the strip and into the ditch, and rolled to a stop forty meters from the fuel drums with the engine ticking and the rain hammering the fuselage like applause from an audience that wanted more.

Lena sat with her hands on the yoke for a moment, listening to the weather work. Then she unbuckled.

"Pip," she called back toward the hold. "Crates."

A scramble, a clatter, and Pip appeared between the seats — sixteen years old, all elbows and grin, hair cut short with what looked like garden shears and a machete already slung across his back for no reason anyone had ever gotten him to explain. "Six crates, four to unload, two stay aboard. The fever medicine goes to the mission first, right? Before the fuel?"

"The medicine always goes first."

"That's what I said." He was already hauling the door open, letting the rain in sideways.

"It's what you asked," Lena said. "Asking isn't saying. Saying is when you know."

Pip grinned wider, unbothered, and dropped out onto the strip into water ankle-deep. Lena followed, and the monsoon closed over her like a second skin.

* * *

The mission sat at the far end of the settlement, a long low building with a tin roof that turned the rain into constant percussion. Sister Anselme met them at the door with two novices and a hand truck, and if she noticed how quickly Lena worked — crates offloaded, manifest signed, no lingering, no tea offered or accepted — she had the grace not to say it aloud. She'd flown with Lena once, years ago, back when Lena still stayed for tea.

"You came in ahead of the storm front," the sister said, watching Pip and Sefa wrestle the last crate up the steps. "That was good flying."

"That was arithmetic," Lena said. "The storm was coming whether I flew well or badly. Flying well just meant arriving before it."

Sister Anselme smiled the way old women smile at answers they've heard a hundred versions of. "And yet you won't come inside while we count the crates. You'll stand in the rain like a woman with somewhere better to be."

"I do have somewhere better to be."

"Nowhere is better than here in the rain, child. That's the whole secret nobody tells you." But she let it go, signing the manifest with a pencil stub, pressing it flat against her forearm because the clipboard had warped years ago. "You'll take payment in fuel credit again?"

"And the rest in cash."

"Of course." The sister counted bills from a tin that had once held biscuits, and Lena folded them away without counting them back, which was its own kind of courtesy in a place like this. "One thing before you go." The sister's voice shifted — not heavier, exactly, but more careful, the way voices get when they've rehearsed something. "There was a man here yesterday. Asking after you."

Lena kept her face still. It was a skill she'd practiced so long it had stopped being effort. "What kind of man?"

"A city man. Soft hands. He said he was a broker, buying quinine futures." Sister Anselme's mouth went dry and thin around the words. "Nobody buys quinine futures at a mission dispensary on a clay strip in the middle of the monsoon. He asked who flew my supplies. I told him the church arranged everything through channels he wouldn't understand, and he smiled as though I'd told him something useful anyway."

"What did he look like?"

"Tall. Gray at the temples, but young through the eyes. A scar—" she touched her own jaw, tracing a line toward the ear. "Here. And he wore boots too clean for this country. He knew your name, I think. Or part of it. He said, *the Thornbury crew*, like he was reading it off a list."

The rain filled the silence. Out on the strip, Lena could see Pip perched on the wing root, talking to Dez about something, both of them gesturing at the engine cowling. Sefa stood in the open door of the plane, watching the treeline the way she always watched treelines, which was to say like they owed her money.

"How many people has he asked?" Lena said.

"He was at the trading post two days ago. The ferryman says he was at Kalter's Landing before that." Sister Anselme paused. "He's moving upriver ahead of you, child. Whatever he wants, he wants to be standing where you're going to land."

Lena took the manifest copy, folded it once, and put it away. "Thank you, Sister."

"You could wait out the storm here," the sister said, and there was nothing casual in it. "Two days. Three. No one finds anyone in monsoon rain unless they want to be found very badly."

"We don't wait anywhere. That's the whole arrangement."

"Yes." Sister Anselme looked at her for a long moment, and behind her eyes was the particular patience of women who bury people younger than themselves. "Your brother used to say something like that. Right before he stopped saying anything at all."

Lena felt it land the way she always felt Tomas's name land — not like a blow anymore, after three years, but like a hand pressed flat against a bruise she'd stopped noticing until someone touched it. "Tomas waited places," she said. "That was his mistake. Not mine."

"No," the sister agreed quietly. "I expect his mistake was believing the river would forgive him for knowing things." She tucked the biscuit tin under her arm. "Fly safe, Lena Thornbury. And if a soft-handed man asks me about you again, I'll tell him the church flies its own supplies and God handles the scheduling."

Lena almost smiled. "He won't believe you."

"No. But it will annoy him, and annoyance makes careless men." The sister turned back toward the door, then paused. "One more thing. He paid for information. Real money, not promises. Do you know what kind of man pays cash upriver?"

Lena knew. Cash upriver meant someone downstream with more cash than sense, or more fear than patience. Cash upriver meant the question mattered to somebody important enough to fund it.

"Yeah," she said. "I know what kind."

* * *

They fueled in the rain, because waiting for the rain to stop was a luxury for people whose faces weren't on lists.

Dez ran the pump while Lena held the grounding wire and watched the water rise in the ditch beside the strip — slowly, steadily, the way trouble rose, imperceptible minute to minute and then suddenly over your boots. Dez was a broad, quiet man built like something assembled rather than born, with forearms scarred from a decade of engine burns and a way of speaking that treated every sentence as a budget: nothing wasted, nothing extra.

"Cass Merrow," he said, without looking up from the hose.

Lena kept her voice level. "Who told you that name?"

"Sister Anselme told you. You've had that look since you came back from the mission." Dez screwed the cap down on the first drum and moved to the second. "Cass Merrow runs freight and favors out of Port Calloway. Half legitimate, half not, and the half that isn't pays better than the half that is. Word was he got squeezed hard two seasons ago when somebody fed a stack of ledgers to the customs office. Somebody died around it, too. Pilot, I heard." He glanced up then, rain streaming off the brim of his cap. "You'd know more about that than me."

"I'd know exactly as much as I know," Lena said, "which is that we're fueled in ten minutes and gone in twelve."

"Lena." Dez set the hose down. He rarely used her name, and when he did it was usually a warning shot. "If Merrow's asking after us, he's not asking out of curiosity. Men like that ask because they've already decided. The asking is just them checking the price."

"Then we make ourselves expensive to find."

"We've been expensive to find for three years. We're also broke, and the plane needs a compressor, and Pip needs new boots, and I need—" He stopped himself, which was unlike him, and Lena filed the stop away without comment. "We can't outrun a name forever. Names travel faster than planes."

"Not in this weather."

Dez almost smiled. Almost. He picked the hose back up.

Pip appeared out of the rain, jogging along the wing with his collar up and his grin intact, because Pip's grin survived everything short of actual drowning. "Fuel's paid, sister's blessed us, and the ferryman's wife gave me bread. Also there's a rumor."

"There's always a rumor."

"This one's about us." He hopped up onto the wheel strut, dripping, delighted in the way he got delighted by things that should have frightened him. "Ferryman says there's a broker working the river offering fifty credits for word on where the Thornbury crew lands next. Fifty! For just *talking*. That's more than I made in six months back home."

"That's the point," Lena said. "Back home, talking about us cost you nothing. Here it pays. Everyone becomes a seller eventually. It's just a question of price."

"So what's our price?"

"Our price is that we're not here." She checked her watch, then the sky, then the western horizon where the storm front sat like a wall of wet slate. They had maybe ninety minutes before it arrived, and the strip would be unusable after, and every hour they lingered was another hour for fifty-credit rumors to grow teeth. "Pip, aboard. Stow the tie-downs. Sefa—"

But Sefa was already standing at the edge of the strip, looking down the muddy road that led into the settlement, and she wasn't watching the road at all. She was watching a man on it.

He was perhaps three hundred meters out, walking with the unhurried gait of someone who has paid for the right to be unhurried. Tall. Umbrella despite the rain being warm. Even at that distance, even through the gray curtain of the monsoon, Lena could see the boots were wrong for the country — dark leather, polished, each step placed like a signature.

She felt the moment settle over her, cold and familiar. This was the feeling she'd built her whole life around avoiding: the moment when the distance between you and the thing hunting you stops shrinking gradually and starts shrinking fast.

"That's him," Sefa said. Not loudly. She didn't need to.

"Everyone aboard," Lena said. "Now. Calmly."

Pip slid off the strut and moved. Dez pulled the chocks without being told. And Lena stood one second longer, watching the tall man watch her — because he had stopped now, she realized, and he was looking straight down the strip at the plane, at her, and even through the rain she could see him raise one hand. Not a wave. Something smaller. A gesture of acknowledgment, almost courteous, like a chess player touching a piece to say *your move*.

Then he turned aside, unhurried, and stepped beneath the awning of the trading post, and was gone from the rain.

Lena climbed aboard, slammed the door, and dropped into her seat. Her hands were steady on the checks. Her heart was doing something less professional.

"He saw us," Sefa said, buckling in. "He wanted us to see him seeing us."

"I know."

"That's a message."

"I know that too."

Dez pulled himself into the jump seat behind them, wiping rain off his face with a forearm. "So what's the message say?"

Lena ran the engines up, watching the gauges climb, feeling the old transport shudder awake around her like a large animal deciding to cooperate. Through the propeller blur she could see the trading post, the awning, the shape of a tall man standing beneath it, not moving, not needing to move.

"It says he knows where we are," she said, "and he's not in any hurry. Which means he doesn't think we can run far enough to matter." She released the brakes. "Let's go prove him wrong."

The wheels tore free of the clay with a sound like a kiss being taken back, and the strip fell away behind them into the rain, and Lena climbed toward the storm front with the rumor riding shotgun in the seat beside her: *Cass Merrow knows your name, and he's paying cash.*

Behind them, the tall man stepped back out into the rain and watched them go, unhurried, the way a man watches something he has already bought.
