## 2. The Crew That Keeps Moving

The rain found me before the town did.

It came slantwise off the hills in gray sheets, turning the road into a river of its own, and I drove the last three kilometers with the wipers losing their argument against the water and my eyes on the mirror more than the road. Old habit. You learn to watch your back before you watch your way forward, when you've been running as long as we have.

Kessel's Landing wasn't much to look at even in dry season — a strip of corrugated roofs hunched along a brown river, a fuel depot, a market that smelled of diesel and overripe fruit and the particular damp rot of a place that had given up fighting the monsoon years ago. In wet season it was worse. In wet season it was perfect, because nobody important ever came here, and nobody important was exactly what I wanted.

I parked the truck behind the fish cannery where the mud would swallow the tire tracks inside an hour, pulled my hood up, and walked into town carrying nothing but cash and a list.

*Medicine for the interior clinics. Fuel filters. Dez's spark plugs — the good ones, not the ones that'll foul by Thursday. Food that isn't rice. Coffee if God is kind.*

And underneath all of it, written so lightly you'd miss it if you didn't know my handwriting: *Don't be seen. Don't be remembered. Don't stay.*

That was the rule. It had been the rule since Tomas died, though back then it hadn't been a rule so much as a reflex — the whole crew flinching away from every fixed point like a hand pulled back from a hot stove. Four years later the flinch had hardened into doctrine. We kept moving because moving was the only shape safety came in.

I was halfway through arguing with the pharmacist about the price of amoxicillin — he wanted triple because of the roads being out, and I wanted him to remember that I was the only pilot fool enough to fly them in this time of year at all — when the boy came in from the rain.

He was maybe nineteen, slick-haired, wearing a jacket too nice for Kessel's Landing and shoes that had never touched mud before today. He went to the counter, bought a bottle of water he didn't drink, and stood there looking at the bulletin board with the studied boredom of a man memorizing a room. His eyes passed over me once. Came back. Passed on again.

I paid the pharmacist without another word about the price, took my parcels, and walked out into the rain at an unhurried pace, because hurrying tells people they've won.

Then I cut through the alley behind the cannery, ran the two blocks to the truck, and got us out of Kessel's Landing eleven minutes ahead of whatever that boy had come to find.

Some days the whole job is just that: leaving well.

* * *

Dez was under the Cessna's engine cowling when I got back to the strip, which was where Dez was always to be found, a smear of grease from jaw to elbow, talking to the radial engine in a low voice like it was a horse that spooked easy.

"Tell me you got the plugs," she said, without looking up.

"Got the plugs."

"Tell me you didn't get robbed."

"Paid triple for the antibiotics."

"So robbed." She slid out on the creeper, sat up, and pushed her goggles onto her forehead, leaving a pale band across her brow like a reverse tan line. Dez Okafor was thirty-one and looked forty after a bad week and twenty after a good one, and the week we'd been having was neither. "Sefa says the number three cylinder's eating oil. I say Sefa worries. But I also say we're not crossing the range on a cylinder that eats oil, so somebody's flying to Torval for parts, and guess who speaks Torvali."

"You do."

"I do," she agreed grimly, "because I'm indispensable, which is a curse."

"Where is Sefa?"

"Chart room. Arguing with the weather."

"Sefa argues with everything."

"Sefa wins, mostly." Dez wiped her hands on a rag that was less rag now than grease holding itself together, and something in her face shifted — the joke draining out of it, the way it did whenever she had to say a thing straight. "Lena. The boy from town. He came by the strip first. Before the pharmacy. Asked the fuel man who owned the plane parked on the apron."

My stomach did the slow cold drop it always did, like an elevator between floors. "What'd the fuel man say?"

"What fuel men say. Nothing, for money." She held up her palm, and I saw the coin she'd pressed into it — the fuel man's fee for silence, standard rate. "But the boy wrote something down anyway. And then he walked toward the telegraph office."

The rain hammered the hangar roof, filling the silence for me, which was kind of it.

"Collector?" I said.

"Who else wears shoes like that into Kessel's Landing? Somebody's paper on somebody. And since none of us has borrowed money in four years—" She stopped herself. I watched her stop herself. There was a half-second there, a hitch in her voice like a gear tooth skipping, and I filed it away the way you file away a new rattle in an engine: not urgent, but noted.

"Find Sefa," I said. "We're wheels up by six."

"It's four hours' flying to Torval in this soup."

"Then we'd best start now."

* * *

Sefa had the chart room lit by a single lamp and the whole eastern seaboard of the country spread across the table in overlapping sheets, weighted down with coffee mugs and a wrench and, inexplicably, one of Pip's boots. Sefa Vunivalu stood over it all with a pencil behind each ear and a third in his hand, drawing lines that the weather would ignore completely.

He was a big man, Sefa, island-born, built like the hull of a boat, with a face that gave away nothing except when he was worried, at which point it gave away everything. He was worried now.

"You heard," I said.

"Heard what?"

"Boy in town. Collector's runner. Headed for the telegraph office."

Sefa set down the pencil. "Whose debt?"

"That's the question, isn't it."

"Because it isn't mine." He said it fast, too fast, and then made himself say it again slower, which was Sefa all over — honest to the bone but needing to check the bone twice. "It isn't mine, Lena. I haven't signed anything since Suva."

"I know."

"And Dez—"

"Is Dez." I looked at the charts instead of him. The rain ticked against the window. Somewhere out on the apron, Dez's wrench rang off the cowling, one bright note. "Four years ago we swore we were done owing anybody anything. We pay cash, we sleep in the plane, we don't sign, we don't stay. So either the rule broke somewhere, or it's old paper come looking, or it's not paper at all."

"Or someone's using a collector to flush us," Sefa said quietly. "Someone who knows we run from collectors the way horses run from fire."

I hadn't let myself think that yet, and hearing it out loud made it real, made it take up space in the room. Because there was exactly one person from our past clever enough and mean enough to think of it, and I had spent four years telling myself she'd forgotten us.

"Cass Merrow doesn't know where we are," I said.

"No," Sefa agreed. "She just happens to have runners in every port town between here and the delta, asking after a blue-and-white Cessna with a mechanic woman and a big islander navigator. Which is what the fuel man told me she has, when I bought his silence this morning. Same price Dez paid. Man's making a living off our nerves."

The floor seemed to tilt very slightly, the way it does when a deck shifts under swell. I put my hand flat on the chart table until it stopped.

"When?" I managed.

"This morning. Yesterday, maybe, in the next town over. Asking around. Not threatening. Just asking." Sefa picked up his pencil and put it down again. "Lena, if it's her — if she's actually hunting us after all this time — what does she want? We don't have anything she wants. We never did. That was always the point of us."

"We have a plane," I said. "We have licenses. And we have the one thing Cass Merrow has never been able to buy, which is pilots stupid enough to fly the interior in monsoon season."

Outside, thunder rolled down off the hills, long and low, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"Pip's not back yet," Sefa said.

Pip.

Of course. Of course Pip wasn't back yet. Pip was fourteen going on fifteen and possessed of the unshakable conviction that being small and quick and charming constituted actual armor, and Pip had gone into town an hour ago to buy food that wasn't rice, and Pip was currently somewhere between the market and the strip while a collector's runner worked the same muddy streets.

I was moving before I finished deciding to move. Sefa was already reaching for the lantern.

* * *

We found Pip at the edge of the market square, backed up against a shuttered fruit stall, with the nice-jacketed boy standing over them and two larger shapes loitering at the mouth of the alley behind.

"—told you, I don't know any Okafor," Pip was saying, chin up, arms crossed, market bags dangling from one fist. The bags contained, I would later learn, three tins of condensed milk, a sack of limes, and a paperback novel with the cover torn off. Priorities. "I work for nobody. I'm a tourist."

"You're a tourist," the boy repeated, without inflection.

"A bird-watcher."

"In monsoon season."

"The birds are very dedicated."

The boy sighed, reached into his jacket, and I didn't wait to see whether it was a notebook or something worse. Sefa simply arrived — that was the thing about Sefa, he moved quiet for a big man, like weather coming in — and put one hand flat on the boy's shoulder and turned him around with the gentle inexorability of a tide.

The boy looked up. Way up. He did the arithmetic boys like him do, quick and cold, and decided that whatever commission he was chasing wasn't worth the hospital bill.

"Wrong person," he said, stepping back, hands open. "Happens all the time."

"Does," Sefa agreed pleasantly. "Rain's heavy. Be a shame to slip."

The two shapes in the alley melted away, professionals enough not to test it. The boy tipped an imaginary hat — to Pip, of all of us, which I suspected Pip would be insufferable about for a week — and walked off into the rain, unhurried, already writing something down.

Pip watched him go with their heart in their throat; I could see it beating there. Then they turned to me, held up the market bags, and said, with a fourteen-year-old's flawless instinct for the wrong thing to say:

"Got the condensed milk."

* * *

We flew out at dusk, in weather no sane pilot would call flyable, because staying the night in Kessel's Landing meant waking up to more than one visitor, and visitors compound interest.

Dez flew first leg while I nursed the number-three cylinder's oil pressure and Sefa navigated by the seat of his pants through cloud that had no business being navigated, calling out ridgelines from memory like a man reciting poetry in the dark. Pip rode in the jump seat between the crates of medicine, soaked to the skin, eating condensed milk with two fingers out of the tin, and narrating the storm clouds for us like a tour guide, because Pip processed fear by talking and we had all learned to let them.

"They look like mountains," Pip shouted over the engines. "Angry ones. Like the hills grew up and decided to fight the sky."

"Less poetry," Dez called back, "more watching for the tower lights at Torval."

"There aren't tower lights at Torval."

"Then watch harder for the absence of them."

I sat in the right seat and let them fill the cabin, all three of them, their voices braided together over the drone of the engines, and I thought about how strange a thing a crew is. Not family — family is a word for people you're given. A crew is a thing you build, rivet by rivet, out of people who were broken in compatible ways. Dez, who'd lost her own workshop and her name in a bankruptcy some bank had engineered for profit. Sefa, whose charts of his home waters had been used, once, by men he'd trusted, to move cargo he still couldn't talk about. Pip, who we'd found stowing away in Moresby at twelve years old and who had never once, in nearly three years, asked us where we were going — only whether there was room.

There was room. There was always room. That was the whole secret of us, the thing no collector or rival or telegraphed description could touch: we weren't running *from* anything anymore, not really. We were running *together*, and the together had become the point.

Tomas used to say that. Before. He used to say, *Lena, one day you'll stop running and find out what you were running toward.* And I'd laughed at him, because Tomas was the serious one, the believer, the one who thought ledgers could be weapons and testimony could be armor and that the law, given enough evidence, would eventually do what it said on the tin.

Tomas was four years dead, and the law had done nothing of the sort, and the man he'd died trying to expose still slept in a dry bed every night.

So we flew. Through the dark and the rain, toward Torval and its absent tower lights, with a cylinder drinking oil and a runner's pencil scratching our description into some ledger of his own. We flew because that was the prayer we knew.

It was near midnight, Torval's single runway lamp finally swimming up out of the murk beneath us, when Sefa leaned over from the nav table and spoke close to my ear, under the engine noise, just for me.

"Fuel man sent a message after we left. Cost me double." He paused. "A woman came through Kessel's Landing this afternoon. Well-dressed. Chartered the mail boat upriver. Left this for whoever was asking after the blue-and-white Cessna."

He put a card in my hand. Heavy stock, expensive, dry despite the weather — she'd have kept it inside her coat, because Cass Merrow understood presentation the way other people understood breathing.

One line, in handwriting I'd have known anywhere, sharp and slanted and sure of itself:

*Lena. One run. Triple rates. Ask for me at the Bell & Anchor, Torval. — C.M.*

I read it three times while the runway lights rose to meet us, and outside the rain kept falling, patient as a debt, and somewhere behind us in the dark the whole flooded country lay waiting, full of rivers and old names and everything we had sworn we'd never touch again.
