## 3. An Old Rival's Offer

The rain came in sideways off the estuary, and with it came Cass Merrow.

Lena saw the seaplane first — a long-winged Pelican, white paint gone chalky, riding the chop outside the breakwater like it owned the water. She knew that plane. She'd helped patch its port float once, six years ago, in a hangar in Corvane, back when she and Cass Merrow had still been friendly enough to share tools and insults in equal measure. Back before everything.

She was halfway through a crate of penicillin vials in the lean-to behind the airfield office when Sefa appeared at her shoulder, rain streaming off the brim of her cap.

"White Pelican," Sefa said. "Just landed."

"I see it."

"Cass Merrow just stepped out of it."

"I see it," Lena said again, and set down the vial she was holding before her hands could shake. "Nobody's home. We're not here."

Sefa gave her the look — the flat, patient look of a navigator who has heard every version of *we're not here* and never once seen one work. "She's walking this way."

"Then we're not here *loudly*."

But there was nowhere to go. The strip at Tallow's Reach was mud and gravel and three buildings, and their own plane sat in the open with its cowling off, Dez elbow-deep in the number two engine, Pip napping under the wing in a hammock strung between the strut and the fuel drum. Running would mean leaving the aircraft, and they did not leave the aircraft. That was the one rule older than all the others.

Cass Merrow crossed the airfield like weather. She wore a slicker the color of wet slate and carried nothing — no case, no papers, no weapon visible, which from Cass meant less than nothing. Her hair was shorter than Lena remembered, silver coming in at the temples, and the years had carved her face into something harder and more interesting than the pretty, sharp-featured rival Lena had raced cargo against across half the coast.

"Lena Thornbury," Cass said, stopping an arm's length away, rain beading on her collar. "Still hiding behind other people's airplanes."

"It's my airplane."

"It's a Kestrel Mk III with a cracked engine mount and a debt on its tail." Cass smiled without warmth. "I do my homework."

"You always did." Lena wiped her hands on a rag that only spread the grease thinner. "What do you want, Cass?"

"To hire you."

The word landed wrong, like a crate shifted in transit. Lena laughed, short and disbelieving. "No."

"You haven't heard the terms."

"Don't need to. The answer's no. It was no when you were asking about us in Vell's Landing, and it was no in Corvane, and it's no now, in the rain, at Tallow's Reach, which by the way is a long way to fly for a no."

Cass didn't flinch. That was the thing about her; Lena remembered it from every race, every negotiation, every night they'd drunk each other under tables in a dozen river towns. Cass Merrow absorbed a blow the way a hull absorbs water — quietly, and with a plan for pumping it out later.

"One run," Cass said. "Upriver to Karst Head. Triple your going rate, paid half up front, plus a bonus if you beat the monsoon crest. In and out. You'll be rich enough to stop running, if that's what you're doing — and we both know it's what you're doing, because I've watched you do it for four years, Lena. You move like something's chasing you. I'm offering to pay you enough that nothing has to chase you ever again."

Somewhere under the wing, Pip's hammock creaked. Lena kept her eyes on Cass and her voice level. "You've been following us."

"I've been *aware* of you. There's a difference, and the difference is that I haven't done anything about it until now." Cass tilted her head toward the lean-to, the crates, the medicine stacked under oilcloth. "You fly relief runs for mission money and sleep in your plane. You're the best pilot on this coast and you're living like a fugitive. I'm the only person offering you a way out. Doesn't that strike you as worth five minutes?"

Lena looked past her, out at the gray water, at the white Pelican rocking at anchor. Six years ago she'd have taken this meeting over drinks and argued Cass down to double pay and first pick of the route. Six years ago Cass hadn't been asking favors of pilots who flew medicine into places the law forgot.

Six years ago Tomas was alive.

"No," Lena said. "Get off my field."

* * *

Cass didn't leave. That was the second thing Lena had forgotten about her: she treated refusal as an opening bid.

She waited out the worst of the rain in the airfield office, drinking terrible coffee the keeper offered her because Cass Merrow had a reputation for tipping well, and by the time the sky lightened to the color of dirty tin, she'd made herself pleasant to everyone within reach. She complimented Sefa's charts — "river work, is it? You don't see good river navigation anymore" — and admired the Kestrel's engine work at a volume calculated to reach Dez under the cowling, and bought Pip a meat pie from the cart by the gate without asking whether the kid wanted one, which of course Pip wanted one, because Pip was nineteen and hungry and had never met a stranger with food.

By evening the whole crew had talked to Cass Merrow except Lena, and Cass had talked to none of them about the contract. She'd just been *nice*. It was, Lena thought grimly, the most dangerous thing she could have done.

They held the argument in the Kestrel's cabin that night, door shut, rain drumming the fuselage like fingers. Lamplight. Four bodies and a map spread across the chart table.

"Triple rate," Dez said. He said it carefully, the way he handled anything fragile, and he wouldn't quite meet Lena's eyes. "Half up front. That's — Lena, that's eight months of what we make now. Maybe ten."

"We don't need ten months."

"We don't need it," Dez agreed. "We *need* four."

The word sat in the cabin like a third engine, heavy and humming. Sefa looked at Dez. Pip looked at the floor. Lena felt the temperature of the room change.

"Four months of what?" she asked, though she already knew. She'd known since Vell's Landing, maybe, or since the night in Corvane when Dez had gone out alone and come back two hours late with his sleeves wrung and his smile bolted on. She'd let it go, because letting things go was how this crew survived, and because she owed him the same mercy she asked for herself. But mercy had a cost, and tonight the bill had arrived wearing a slate-gray slicker.

Dez exhaled through his nose. "There's a man named Ovid Crane. Lends money to mechanics. I borrowed against the overhaul last winter when the Kestrel's compressor went, because we couldn't fly relief runs with no compressor, and the interest is—" He stopped. Started again. "It compounds monthly. I've been paying it down out of my share. I'm down to owing four months' worth, and he's found me twice now, and the second time wasn't friendly."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because you'd have sold your watch, and then your seat, and then whatever else, and I wasn't going to be the reason this crew shrank by one more person." His jaw worked. "One more person. Yeah. I know how that sounds."

Silence. Rain. Pip, very quietly, from the bunk: "Crane works for people who work for the river syndicates."

Everyone looked at him.

"He does," Pip said, sitting up. "I hear things. That's my whole job, hearing things. Ovid Crane collects for holdings companies that are shells, and the shells roll up to—" He hesitated, then finished, because Pip had never learned the trick of not finishing sentences. "The same network Tomas was mapping. The same one."

The name filled the cabin. Nobody said it again. Nobody had to.

"That's rumor," Dez said, but his voice had gone careful again, and his hands, which could rebuild a carburetor blind, lay flat and still on the chart table.

"It's a pattern," Sefa said. She'd been quiet the whole meeting, which meant she'd been thinking the whole meeting, which meant trouble. She turned the lamp slightly so its light fell on the map, on the blue thread of the river running up into the flooded country toward Karst Head. "Look at it straight, Lena. A rival we haven't spoken to in six years tracks us across four hundred miles of coast. Offers triple pay for one run, upriver, in monsoon season, when no sane operator flies. Half up front — cash, which pays exactly one debt we know of. And the destination is the heart of the country where the smuggling routes run." She looked up. "Either Cass Merrow doesn't know what she's carrying, or she knows exactly, and neither answer is good."

"So we refuse," Dez said. "We refuse and I figure out Crane another way—"

"There is no another way," Sefa said gently. "Not in four months. Not flying medicine at cost. You know it. I know it. And Cass knows it, which is why she's sitting in that office right now being charming instead of negotiating. She doesn't need to negotiate. She needs to wait."

Lena stood at the cabin door with her hand on the latch and listened to the rain and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like calm, that the vote had already happened. That it had happened the moment Dez said *four*, and the moment Pip said *the same one*, and the moment Sefa laid the map out flat instead of arguing. Her crew — her brilliant, stubborn, loyal crew, the family she'd built out of the wreckage of the last one — was going to take the job, and they were going to look at her while they did it, and she was going to have to decide what kind of captain she was going to be about it.

She thought about Tomas. She thought about the oath, spoken over turned earth in a graveyard above Corvane with the rain coming down just like this: *never again, not for any price, not for anyone.* She thought about how oaths are easy when the dead are fresh and impossible when the living are hungry.

"Vote," she said. "All in favor of taking Merrow's contract."

Dez raised his hand immediately, and then, painfully, kept it raised. Pip raised his after a moment, apologetic as an apology. Sefa raised hers last, slow, watching Lena the whole time.

Three to one. Lena kept her own hand down and her face still.

"Against," she said, for the record. "Motion carries." She rolled the map closed with more force than it needed. "We take the job. One run. Upriver and back, and not one mile farther, and if I find so much as a rifle barrel in those crates, we dump the lot in the river and fly home on fumes. Is that understood?"

"Yes, Captain," Sefa said, and there was something in it that wasn't surrender — something more like a hand extended across a table.

Dez sagged with relief so plain it was almost embarrassing, and Pip grinned, and just like that the cabin warmed, the way a room warms when a fire catches. Lena hated how much she loved them. It was the most dangerous thing about her, and Cass Merrow had counted on it, and been right.

* * *

She went out into the rain afterward, alone, and walked the length of the strip to clear her head, boots sucking in the mud. At the office window, lamplight showed Cass Merrow awake, writing something, unhurried, a woman whose plan was proceeding.

Lena stopped in the dark and looked at her for a long moment. Then she pushed the door open.

Cass glanced up, unsurprised. "Captain."

"The answer was no," Lena said. "My crew outvoted me. So understand this clearly, because I'll only say it once. If this run has anything in it but what your manifest says — if I find one gram of anything that touches the river trade, the trade my brother died pulling into the light — I will burn your cargo on the water and fly home, and I will spend the rest of my life making sure every pilot on this coast knows what you tried to move through me. Are we understood?"

For a heartbeat something moved behind Cass's eyes — something quick and unreadable, there and gone, too fast to name. Then she smiled, warm as banked coals, and slid a folded contract across the desk.

"Perfectly," she said. "Half payment's in the envelope. We load tomorrow at dawn."

Lena took the contract and the envelope and walked back out into the rain, and it was only halfway down the steps that she realized what had flickered across Cass's face in that unguarded instant.

It hadn't been guilt.

It had been fear — quickly hidden, badly, by a woman who never hid anything badly — and Lena lay awake in the Kestrel's cabin listening to the monsoon build, turning it over and over, wondering what Cass Merrow was afraid of, and why she needed *them* — of all the crews on this coast — to carry it.
