## 4. Sworn Off

Sefa heard the argument before she reached the galley — Dez's voice pitched high and defensive, Lena's low in the way that meant she was already losing.

She stopped at the top of the companionway ladder and stood there a moment, one hand on the rail, listening the way you listen to weather coming. The *Kestrel's Wing* was moored at the fuel dock on the Verrin River, rain drumming the canvas awning they'd rigged over the aft deck, and the whole boat had the feel of a held breath.

"—triple pay, Sefa." Dez had turned, caught her in the doorway. He was still holding the contract flimsy, creased where he'd been worrying it between his fingers for the last hour. "Triple. You know what that does to what I owe."

"I know what it costs," Sefa said.

"It's one run."

"That's what they all say."

Lena sat at the chart table with her arms folded, not looking at either of them. She'd been sitting like that since Cass Merrow walked off the boat an hour ago, since her voice had gone flat and careful when she said *I told him no* — and then Dez had said *we should vote*, and everything had changed temperature.

Pip was curled in the corner bunk with a paperback he wasn't reading, watching them all sideways. Sixteen years old and already fluent in the language of adult silences. Sefa wished, not for the first time, that the kid had learned to read anything else as well.

"Vote," Dez said again. "That's fair. That's how we do things."

"We've never voted on something like this," Sefa said. "Because we never needed to. We swore."

Dez flinched. It was small — a twitch at the corner of his mouth, a half-blink — but Sefa saw it, because she'd been seeing it for six years, every time Tomas's name came near the conversation like weather.

"You can't hold us to that forever," Dez said quietly.

"No," Sefa agreed. "You can't. But I can remind you what we were holding when we made it."

The rain thickened against the awning. Downriver, somewhere past the fuel lights and the mud flats, thunder rolled along the monsoon front like something heavy being dragged across the sky.

"Tell it," Pip said from the bunk. His voice cracked halfway through, sixteen going on seventeen. "If we're voting, tell it right. All of it."

Sefa looked at Lena. Lena looked at the chart table, at her own hands lying flat on the old salt-stained wood, and after a long moment she nodded once — permission, or surrender, hard to say which.

So Sefa pulled out the second stool and sat down, and told it.

* * *

It started, the way these things always start, with money that seemed clean.

Six years ago, in the dry season, when the Kestrel was younger and so were all of them. A broker named Halloran — respectable, gray-suited, offices above the customs house in Pell's Landing — had come to them with a cargo run: sealed crates, upriver to a mining camp, double rate, no questions. They'd been three months behind on the engine refit. Dez had been walking around with that same look he had tonight, that arithmetic of debt behind his eyes, and Lena had been twenty-four and hungry and her brother Tomas had just signed on as their cargo handler, fresh off a bad year of his own, grinning like the whole river belonged to him.

Sefa remembered that grin. She made herself remember it now, saying it out loud in the galley while the rain came down, because remembering it was the price of the telling and she paid it every time.

"The crates were wrong from the start," she said. "Too heavy for medical supplies. Too well-sealed. Tomas noticed before any of us — he used to tap the crates, did I ever tell you that? Knocked on them like a doctor listening to a chest. He said cargo talks if you know how to listen."

"He knocked on everything," Pip said softly. "Crates, hulls, my head."

"He knocked on this one and his face went strange. And instead of saying something to us, he started asking questions on his own. Quiet ones. He found out the crates weren't going to any mining camp. They were going through it — into a transfer network that moved product and money and people up the whole length of the Verrin basin. And the name at the top of that network—" Sefa paused. Even now, even after everything, the name sat in her throat like a stone. "—was Cass Merrow."

Dez set the contract flimsy down on the table very carefully, the way you put down something that might go off.

"Tomas went to the magistrate," Sefa said. "He'd copied what he could from the manifests. He thought the law would do what laws are supposed to do. What he didn't know — what none of us knew — was that Merrow owned half the men he was reporting to. The tip went straight back up the chain. Three days later we got hired for another run, same route, and we took it because we needed the money, and at Blackwater Bend—"

Her voice snagged. Lena's hands hadn't moved on the table, but her knuckles had gone white, and Sefa took a breath and kept going, because stopping was worse.

"At Blackwater Bend there were men waiting who weren't supposed to be there. They boarded us. They wanted to know which one of us had been talking. And Tomas—" She looked at the bulkhead, at nothing. "Tomas stepped forward before anyone could answer for him. Said it was him alone. Said the crew knew nothing. Bought us our lives with the truth and they killed him anyway, right there on the deck, and threw the evidence overboard, and left us alive because dead witnesses draw magistrates and scared ones don't."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere forward, the mooring line creaked against the piling.

"There was no investigation," Sefa said. "There was a filing clerk who lost the report. There was a magistrate who recused himself. There was a funeral in a river town called Marrow's Bend, actually, if you can believe the river's sense of humor, and there were five people standing over the grave, and four of us swore."

"And the fifth?" Dez asked, though he knew.

"The fifth was too busy swearing a different oath," Sefa said, and nodded at Lena. "Hers was to keep the rest of us breathing. Mine was that we'd never carry Merrow's freight, never touch his money, never take a job we couldn't open and look inside. We swore it over the dirt. All of us. You put your hand on the grave, Dez. I watched you do it."

"I was different then," Dez said. "We all were. Broke is broke, but I'm past broke now, Sefa. I'm under it. Those debts aren't going to forgive themselves, and Merrow's money will clear every one of them in a single payment."

"Merrow's money has never once cleared without collecting interest," Sefa said. "You know what the interest was last time? It was Tomas."

* * *

Afterward — after the telling, after the long wet quiet — Lena finally spoke.

"Sefa's leaving out the part where I decided we'd run," she said. Her voice was steady in the way of someone holding something heavy at arm's length. "After the funeral. I sold the house Mama left me in Pell's Landing to buy the engine refit, and I told everyone we'd keep moving. New port every week. Never long enough to be found. I built the whole life we live now out of that one decision, and I've spent six years telling myself it was for the crew." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "It was for me. Running felt like doing something. Staying still felt like drowning. So we ran, and it worked, mostly, and I let myself believe that motion was the same thing as safety."

"It kept us alive," Dez said.

"It kept us *moving*," Lena said. "Those aren't the same thing, Dez. We've got no home, no harbor rights, no friends close enough to call at two in the morning. We've got a boat and each other and a rule about never staying anywhere, and I need you to understand that rule isn't wisdom. It's scar tissue."

"So you're saying vote no," Dez said.

"I'm saying I already told Merrow no," Lena said. "And then you put our finances on the table in front of everyone like a hand of cards, and now we're voting, because that's how we do things. Because if I just override you, then the oath doesn't mean anything either — oaths only count when they cost."

Pip had closed the unread book. "Can I say something?"

"You're crew," Sefa said. "Say it."

"I don't remember Tomas." Pip's voice was careful, like he was carrying water across a moving deck. "I was ten. I remember somebody knocking on my head and calling it a hull inspection, and I remember everybody crying at a funeral and nobody telling me why. Everything I know about him, I know from you two telling it. And here's what I've figured out." He swallowed. "You tell it like a warning. Every time. Don't stop moving, don't trust brokers, don't knock on crates. But the way Dez looks when you tell it — that's not a warning face. That's a *missing him* face. Both things are true, right? The job is poison AND we miss him. And I think maybe we've been so busy not-dying that we forgot the other true thing."

Nobody answered that. The rain did.

* * *

They voted at midnight, huddled under the awning while lightning stitched the western sky. Sefa no. Lena no. Pip, after a long look at Dez's face, a reluctant yes — "because if we don't, Dez drowns in debt and I lose him anyway, and I'd rather have everybody scared than anybody gone." Which left Dez, whose yes was a whisper and whose hands wouldn't stay still.

Two to two. Deadlock, until Dez looked at Lena and said, "Your call. You're captain. That's why we gave you the title."

And Sefa watched her captain sit with it — watched Lena Thornbury weigh her brother's grave against her mechanic's ruin, six years of running against one more run, the oath against the family that was still alive and needed keeping — and saw her arrive somewhere she hated.

"One run," Lena said at last. "Upriver and back. Triple pay, and Merrow's contract in writing with the delivery terms spelled out so tight he can't bend them. And we inspect every crate ourselves before it's sealed. If I find one thing in that manifest that smells like the old network, we walk, debts or no debts, and I'll sell my boots to cover the fuel."

Dez exhaled like a man surfacing. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." Lena stood, pulled the contract flimsy off the table, and stared at it the way you stare at a door you're afraid to open. "Merrow wanted this run badly enough to triple the rate during monsoon season. Nothing about that adds up. Cargo that urgent, that expensive, that secret — I want to know what we're actually carrying before it's aboard, not after."

"You think he's lying to us?" Pip asked.

"I think men like Cass Merrow only tell the truth by accident," Lena said. "First light tomorrow, we crack those crates before they're loaded. Every single one."

Sefa stayed on deck after the others went below. The storm front was closer now; she could smell the iron in it. Downriver, the fuel dock lights blurred in the rain, and beyond them the black water ran north toward the interior, toward Blackwater Bend, toward everything they had sworn off and were somehow sailing toward anyway.

She pressed her palm flat against the rail — the same rail, repaired twice since, that Tomas had leaned on the night before he died — and made herself a private promise beneath the public one.

Whatever was in those crates, whatever game Cass Merrow was playing, this time they would look inside *before* it cost them.

This time, the knocking would come first.
