## 5. Loading the Lie

The vote had gone the way Lena had known it would the moment she saw Dez's face, and that was the worst part of it — not losing, but knowing she'd lost before the argument started. Three hands to one. Sefa had raised hers slowly, like it weighed something, and Pip had raised his because Pip always raised his, and Dez had raised his with his eyes fixed on the table because he couldn't look at her while he did it.

So now they were loading Cass Merrow's crates at first light, in the rain, on a strip of mud the locals called a dock, and Lena was smiling at Cass's people and counting everything.

"Six crates of penicillin, four of saline, two of sutures and field dressings," said the man Cass had sent to supervise the transfer. His name was Voss, and he had the flat, patient voice of someone who had said the same lie many times and never tired of it. "Manifest's in your copy. Standard cold-chain on the antibiotics — you'll want the generator running on the river leg."

"We know how to move medicine," Lena said. "We've been doing it since before your boss knew this river had a name."

Voss smiled the way a door closes. "I'm sure you have, Miss Thornbury. Cass speaks highly of you."

"Cass and I have never exchanged a kind word in our lives. He can stop speaking highly of me."

She turned back to the work before he could answer, and let the work take her. That was the discipline of it, the thing she'd learned the hard way across eleven years of keeping a crew alive: you could be furious, you could be afraid, you could be grieving, and the crates still had to be weighed and the straps still had to be set and the weight distribution still had to be right or the plane would go into the trees with all of you inside it. Grief did not excuse bad loading. Nothing excused bad loading.

The crates were good ones. That was the first thing she noticed, and the first thing that itched. Solid pine, proper battens, stenciled shipping marks in fresh paint — the kind of crates that came from a real medical supplier, the kind Cass had no honest reason to be buying. Cass Merrow did not deal in penicillin. Cass Merrow dealt in the things that traveled *inside* shipments of penicillin, in the gaps between legitimate goods, in the gray water where the river trade did its real business. Everyone up and down the waterway knew it and no one could prove it, and Tomas had died trying to prove it, and here was Cass's money buying their plane's fuel and Dez's debts and Sefa's silence and Lena's own tired, traitorous hope that one run would buy them all a year of running.

*One run,* she'd told them. *Then we're gone, further upcountry, somewhere he can't reach.*

She'd said it like she believed it. Maybe she did. Maybe that was the lie she was loading along with the crates.

"Pip." She kept her voice at working volume, under the rain. "Left side, third crate. Check the stenciling against the manifest. I want the batch numbers exact."

Pip was seventeen and could read a lie faster than he could read a map, which was saying something, because Pip read maps like other people read novels. He crouched by the crate, rain streaming off the brim of his cap, and ran his finger along the painted numbers. "Batch 4471-C," he read. "Matches."

"Weight?"

He put his palm flat on the crate's top slat, then — casual as scratching an itch — pressed his shoulder against the corner and rocked it. Pip had grown up hauling cargo before he'd grown up to anything else; he could weigh a crate with his body the way other boys weighed fish in their hands. He frowned.

"Heavy," he said. "Heavy for saline. Saline's mostly water, but it's bagged light — you feel it slosh. This doesn't slosh."

"Could be glass bottles instead of bags. Some suppliers still bottle it."

"Could be." Pip didn't believe it and didn't pretend to. He looked up at her with rain on his lashes and that terrible young directness he hadn't learned to hide yet. "You want me to open one?"

Lena glanced down the dock. Voss stood at the far end under an awning, talking to one of Cass's rivermen, both of them watching the plane but not the crates. Dez was in the plane's cargo bay, re-rigging a tie-down point, his back to all of them. Sefa was up on the flight deck running her pre-checks, visible through the windshield, her silhouette bent over the panel.

Everyone accounted for. Everyone watching something else.

"Not here," Lena said. "Not yet. Tonight, at the mooring, when Voss and his friends are drunk on Cass's own money. I want to know what we're carrying before we're carrying it into the interior — and I want to know it without Cass knowing we know. Understood?"

Pip nodded, and there was something in his face that was older than seventeen and colder, and it made her chest hurt, because she'd put it there. Eleven years of running had raised that boy as much as any of them had. He'd learned suspicion the way river kids learned swimming — early, and because the alternative was drowning.

They finished loading by mid-morning. The rain never stopped; it only changed its mind about how hard it meant to fall. Voss counted the crates twice, made Lena sign the manifest in duplicate, and shook her hand with a grip like a manacle.

"Cass expects delivery at Blackwater Bend within the week," he said. "Weather's his problem and yours, but the deadline's just yours. He was very clear."

"Cass is always very clear. It's the only honest thing about him."

Voss laughed — a short, genuine bark that surprised her — and then his face went flat again, and he touched the brim of his hat and walked off down the dock into the rain, and Lena stood there feeling the shape of the thing she'd signed.

*Blackwater Bend.* She knew the name. Everyone on the river knew the name. It was a trading post and a fuel depot and, if even half the stories were true, the place where Cass's cargo changed hands on its way to wherever it went. Tomas's last letters had mentioned it three times. She'd read those letters so many times the paper had gone soft at the folds, and she could not read the words *Blackwater Bend* without hearing his voice saying them, quick and bright and certain, *Lena, I've almost got it, I can almost see the whole shape of it—*

She had almost seen the shape of it too. That was the shape of a grave.

* * *

They moored that night in a backwater Sefa had found, a cut in the bank where the plane could sit on its beaching gear under overhanging trees and the boat — the old river launch they'd bought two seasons back for exactly this kind of run, plane for the open stretches, boat for the flooded narrows — could lie at anchor in the dark. Cass's money had bought them the launch's fuel too, and its provisions, and that was another small humiliation Lena swallowed like a stone.

They ate rice and dried fish under the plane's wing, out of the rain, and nobody talked much. Dez cleaned his tools he'd already cleaned. Sefa charted tomorrow's stretch of river she'd already charted twice. Pip watched the water. This was what a crew looked like when it had made a decision it didn't believe in: everyone very busy with things that didn't need doing.

"Voss's men went into town," Sefa said at last, to no one in particular. "All four. Took the skiff about an hour ago. I'd give them till midnight, maybe later. Cass's per diem buys a lot of local rum."

Lena looked at her. Sefa looked back, mild as a lake, and Lena understood that her navigator had been counting Cass's men the way she counted everything — headings, hours, debts — and had been waiting for Lena to say the word.

"Dez," Lena said.

He didn't look up from his tools. "I know what you're going to ask."

"Then you know I have to ask it anyway. If Voss comes back early, if anyone's watching the crates, I want the honest answer about how much noise we can afford to make."

Dez set down his wrench. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with hands that could coax a dead engine back to life and a face that had never once successfully lied to her, which was why he was bad at cards and worse at hiding debts. He'd hidden these, though. He'd hidden them well enough that she'd found out from a creditor instead of from him, and that fact sat between them at every meal like an uninvited guest.

"Nobody's watching," he said. "Voss thinks he bought us. That's the whole beauty of it from his side — why would you spend men guarding people who already took the job? He's not guarding cargo. He's *delivering* it." He paused. "And I'm not going to pretend that doesn't turn my stomach, Lena. Taking the job was one thing. Opening the man's crates is another. If he finds out—"

"If he finds out, we'll know what we're dealing with before we're mid-river with no fuel and no friends. If he doesn't find out, and the crates are what the manifest says they are, then I'll apologize to his memory and we'll fly medicine like honest people." She stood. "But I've watched that man's name buy silence in four towns, and I've buried a brother who chased him, and I am not carrying six hundred pounds of anything of Cass Merrow's into the interior on faith. Faith is for people who haven't read his manifest. Get the crowbar."

* * *

They did it in the launch's hold, because the launch sat lower than the plane and the trees hid it better, and because if you were going to do a dishonest thing you might as well do it in the most dishonest room available. Pip kept watch on the bank with the night glasses. Sefa stood at the hatch with a lamp shuttered to a slit. Dez worked the crowbar, and Lena watched, and the rain came down on the canvas deck above them with a sound like slow applause.

The first crate was exactly what it claimed to be. Penicillin, bottled, packed in straw, batch numbers matching, cold packs still faintly cool in their wrapping. Dez repacked it with the tenderness he showed all working machinery and they looked at each other and nobody said *maybe I was wrong*, because they all knew better than to spend that luck on crate one.

The second crate was saline, bagged, correct. The third was sutures and dressings, correct. By then the work had settled into a rhythm and the dread had settled into Lena's chest like ballast, because three honest crates meant nothing except that Cass could count to three, and the itch behind her eyes had only gotten sharper.

The fourth crate was heavier than the manifest said.

Not by much — twenty, thirty pounds, the kind of discrepancy a busy dockhand would wave through and a tired pilot would never catch. But Pip had felt it with his shoulder that morning and Lena felt it now, tipping the crate before Dez pried it, feeling the weight sit low and dense and silent. No slosh. No rattle. The weight of paper, she thought. The weight of metal. The weight of things that do not want to be found.

"Careful," she said, though Dez was already being careful. He was always careful. It was one of the three reasons she kept him.

The lid came up with a groan of nails. Straw packing, on top — a good six inches of it, loose and theatrical, the kind of packing that exists to be seen and not to protect anything. Dez lifted it out in armfuls, and underneath, wrapped in oilcloth and bound with twine, lay flat bundles. Not bottles. Not bags. Not sutures.

Lena crouched and cut the twine on the nearest bundle with her pocketknife, and unfolded the oilcloth, and the lamp's slit of light fell across it, and for a long moment she didn't understand what she was looking at, and then she did, and the understanding went through her like cold water through a hull.

Ledgers.

Dozens of them. Bound ledgers, shipping manifests, customs duplicates, correspondence in bundled files — years of it, by the look of the bindings, some of the books swollen with damp and some crisp and recent. And interleaved through everything, in sealed envelopes marked with a customs stamp she recognized from Tomas's letters, were the kind of documents that existed in exactly one copy each: certifications, waivers, clearance papers. Signatures. Dates. Names.

"Saline," Dez said flatly. "It says saline."

"It says saline," Lena agreed. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her, because her hands were not. She lifted one ledger and opened it to a random page and the lamp light showed her columns of figures and initials she didn't recognize and one she did — a stamp, an official stamp, the regional customs authority, the same authority that had spent three years officially concluding that there was insufficient evidence to pursue any investigation into river smuggling, the same conclusion that had arrived six weeks after they'd buried her brother.

The law hadn't failed to touch Cass Merrow. Looking at this crate, Lena understood with a clarity that felt like falling, the law had been *carrying* him. These were the records of it. This was the proof of it. And it was packed in straw under a false manifest, in the rain, in her boat, headed upriver.

"To where?" Sefa asked quietly. She'd come down from the hatch without Lena noticing, and her face in the lamplight was the face she wore in bad weather — calm, total, awake. "Blackwater Bend, Voss said. But this isn't cargo for a bend. This is cargo for a fire. Nobody ships this much paper unless they're moving it somewhere to burn it."

"Monsoon season," Lena said. The words came out of her like something another person was saying. "Flooded rivers, washed-out roads, every official station in the interior cut off for weeks. If records disappear during the monsoon — a warehouse flood, a fire, anything — there's no one to audit it until the water drops. By then it's ash and river mud and Cass Merrow has been clean for five years instead of five."

She kept turning pages. Her brother's face kept rising in front of her, unbidden — Tomas at nineteen, at twenty-two, at twenty-six, quick and bright and certain, *I've almost got it, Lena* — and she made herself keep looking at the columns, because this was what he'd almost got, this was the whole shape of it, and it had been sitting in a crate in her boat all day while she signed manifests in duplicate.

"There's more," Pip said from the hatch. He'd come down silent as he always did, and he was crouched at the far end of the crate, and he had one of the sealed envelopes in his hands, and his voice had gone strange. "Lena. This one's got a name on the outside."

She took it. The envelope was heavy paper, sealed with the customs stamp, and across the front in a clerk's careful hand was written a name and a date, and the name was *T. Thornbury*, and the date was eleven days before her brother's boat was found burned to the waterline on the lower river.

The rain applauded on the canvas. Nobody moved. Somewhere out in the dark, an owl called across the flooded trees, and the water slapped the launch's hull, and Lena Thornbury knelt in a dishonest room holding an envelope with her dead brother's name on it, and understood that Cass Merrow had not hired her crew despite who she was.

He had hired them *because* of it. Because the last people in the world who would open these crates and understand what they were looking at — and who would, out of grief, out of pride, out of eleven years of running — still take his money and carry his firewood for him. Because the joke was the best part, and Cass Merrow had always struck Lena as a man who enjoyed a joke.

She put the envelope in her coat, inside, against her ribs, and stood up.

"Repack it," she said. "All of it. Exactly as it was. Nails, straw, twine — Dez, I want it indistinguishable, because tomorrow we fly this upriver and we deliver it, and Voss and his rum-soaked friends are going to watch us do it, and not one of them is going to guess that we've read every page." She looked around the lamplit hold at her crew — Dez with the crowbar, Sefa with the lamp, Pip with the rain running off him — and heard her own voice go low and hard, a voice she hadn't used since the graveside. "Because we're not carrying Cass's cargo anymore. We're carrying Tomas's case. And Cass is about to spend a week escorting his own evidence straight into the hands of the only people on this river who know what to do with it."

Sefa's lamp didn't waver. "The magistrate's station at Fort Calloway is cut off till the water drops," she said. "You said it yourself."

"Then we find another way. There's always another way. Tomas found one, and they killed him for it, and they missed this." She touched her coat, over the envelope. "They missed it because they were in too much of a hurry to burn the rest. We won't be in a hurry. We'll be *punctual*. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference."

Dez drove the last nail home at a quarter past two in the morning, and the crate sat in the dark of the hold looking exactly like six hundred pounds of saline, and out on the black water the rain kept falling, and forty miles upriver at Blackwater Bend a man who thought he had bought them was sleeping the sleep of the well-served — and Lena lay awake in the plane's cabin listening to the water and did not sleep at all, because she had opened the envelope, just the corner, just far enough to see the first line of the clerk's covering note, and the first line said:

*Re: subject Thornbury — final disposition of materials seized, per instruction, prior to hearing.*
