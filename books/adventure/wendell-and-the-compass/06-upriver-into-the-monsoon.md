## 6. Upriver Into the Monsoon

The storm found them an hour out of Merrow's field, and it did not knock first.

Dez felt it in the yoke before she saw it — a shudder through the airframe like a dog shaking off water, the old transport's wings flexing in air that had gone soft and wrong. She'd been flying since she was sixteen, mostly in machines that should have been scrapped, and she had a superstition she'd never said aloud: the sky always told you what kind of day it meant to be, if you listened with your hands.

Her hands were telling her to turn around.

"Cloud's building fast off the ridge," Sefa said from the nav seat, not looking up from her charts. She said it flat, the way she reported everything, but Dez caught the tension underneath. "That's not monsoon fringe. That's the leading edge."

"Merrow said the front wasn't due until tomorrow night," Pip called from the back, where he'd wedged himself between two crates because sitting still made him itch.

"Merrow said triple pay for a milk run," Dez said. "Merrow says a lot of things."

Lena was in the cockpit door, one hand on the frame, watching the horizon go the color of wet slate. She hadn't said much since they'd loaded. That in itself was information. Lena talked when she was deciding; she went quiet when she'd decided and didn't like it.

"We're committed," she said now, to no one in particular, or maybe to herself.

Dez pulled the mixture a hair richer and climbed two hundred feet, hunting smoother air. The engine note changed, smoothed out, then stuttered — once, twice — and her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

"There it is," she muttered. "Come on, sweetheart. Not today."

She nursed the throttle. The stutter came again, longer this time, and behind her she heard Pip stop moving entirely. Everyone on this crew knew that sound. It was the sound of a fuel system picking up something that wasn't fuel.

"Water in the lines?" Sefa asked.

"Or sediment. Merrow's field jockey filled us from drums that've been sitting in the rain since the last administration." Dez worked the primer, watched the pressure gauge wobble. "I'll drain the sumps at the river transfer. If we make the transfer."

The clouds ahead were no longer ahead. They were all around, a gray cathedral with no roof, and the first rain hit the windshield like flung gravel. Dez flipped on the wipers; one of them worked. She'd fixed the other three times, and each time the part she needed existed only in her imagination.

"Visibility's going," Sefa said. "I'm giving you the river line. Follow it east-northeast, and the bend at Kessel's Landing should come up on your left inside twenty minutes."

"Should?"

"Should."

* * *

Kessel's Landing was where the flying ended and the lying began — that was how Dez thought of it, anyway. The manifest Cass Merrow had signed listed their cargo as medical supplies bound for the mission station at Upper Varrow, which sat at the end of a road that hadn't existed in forty years. In reality, the crates went onto a boat, and the boat went upriver into flooded country where no magistrate's clerk would ever ask a question, and every mile of that journey was a lie stacked on top of another lie.

They came down through the rain in a descent Dez would remember for a long time — instruments unreliable, the strip a brown smear between drowned paddies, crosswind shoving the tail around like a drunk friend. She landed long, hard, and crooked, and when the wheels stopped complaining she sat for a moment with her hands shaking on the yoke and let nobody see.

"Nice landing," Pip said over the intercom.

"Liar."

"Terrible landing. Ten out of ten."

The transfer was chaos in the way transfers always were: men in oilskins hauling crates down a ramp slick as soap, the boat waiting in brown water the color of strong tea, rain coming down so hard the far bank was a rumor. Dez supervised the loading with her hood up and her teeth set, checking each crate against Sefa's list while the dockhands moved too fast and cared too little.

It was crate eleven that stopped her.

Not because of anything visible. Because of weight. She'd hauled enough medical crates to know what penicillin and plasma and suture kits weighed, the way a carpenter knows wood by its knock. Crate eleven weighed wrong. Heavier than its manifest entry by a good margin, and when the dockhand swung it onto the deck, something inside shifted — a dense, sliding movement, nothing like glass vials settling.

She put her palm flat on the lid. Rain drummed against her knuckles.

"Problem?" One of Merrow's men stood at the foot of the ramp, a big soft-spoken fellow named Brannic who smiled like a man who'd never once been surprised. He'd been friendly the whole morning. His friendliness had a floor under it, though, and Dez could feel it.

"Just counting," she said.

"They're counted."

"I count twice. Habit." She smiled back, and made herself step away, and marked the crate number in her head where she kept things she couldn't say yet: *eleven, fourteen, nineteen.* Three crates too heavy. Three crates that shifted wrong.

Sefa appeared at her elbow, ostensibly to argue about stowage. "You felt it too," she murmured.

"Eleven, fourteen, nineteen."

"I have the same three." Sefa's eyes stayed on the ramp. "We're past the point where it matters what's in them. We're taking them either way."

"That's exactly what worries me."

* * *

The boat was called the *Magpie*, and she was older than everyone aboard except possibly the river itself.

Dez fell in love with her immediately and against her better judgment. Forty feet of riveted hull and patched planking, a wheelhouse barely big enough for two, an engine — a genuine antique, some pre-war diesel that someone had loved and neglected and loved again across decades — that turned over on the fourth try and settled into a rhythm like a tired man climbing stairs. Her owner, a leathery woman named Odile who took Merrow's money without counting it, gave them a tour that consisted of pointing at things and naming the ways they'd fail.

"Bilge pump works. Mostly. Spare's under the bench. Don't run her above half throttle in the current — the shaft bearing sings, and when it stops singing, you've bought yourself a swim." Odile looked at the sky, then at Lena. "You're running up to Blackwater in this?"

"That's the plan," Lena said.

Odile laughed, short and humorless. "Plans are cheap in monsoon season." She shook hands all around, held Pip's shoulder a moment longer than necessary — *come back, that gesture said, though they'd known her ten minutes* — and stepped off onto the landing without looking back.

Dez took the engine room. It was a coffin-sized space aft, hot and loud and smelling of diesel and bilgewater, and within an hour she knew the *Magpie*'s heartbeat better than her own. There was comfort in that. Engines didn't lie. An engine told you exactly what it was and what it needed, and if you listened, it never betrayed you. People — employers, rivals, brothers-in-law of dead friends — were another matter entirely.

The river widened as they left the landing behind, and the rain settled in for the duration, a gray curtain that erased the world beyond fifty yards. Sefa ran the wheel with Lena beside her, calling depths from memory and chart and instinct, because the channel markers had stopped being maintained years ago and the flood had rearranged everything else. Pip stood lookout in the bow under an oilskin tarp, shouting depth soundings like a boy playing at being a sailor, which, Dez reflected, he technically was.

For two hours, it almost felt like an ordinary run.

Then the engine coughed.

Dez's head came up from the workbench where she'd been re-packing a water pump seal. She waited. The cough came again — a hitch in the rhythm, a missed beat, then another, then a roughness that crept into the whole note like rot into fruit.

"No," she told it. "No, no, no."

She killed the fuel flow to the primary filter and opened the drain cock over a tin cup. What came out was diesel the color of iced tea, with a fine gray sediment swirling in it, and floating on top, unmistakably, water.

"Contaminated load," she called forward, already reaching for the spare filters. "Same stuff as the plane. Merrow's fuel is wet all the way down the chain. I can filter as we go, but I need to slow her, and I need somewhere dry to do a full tank purge, or we're going to be doing this every hour until the injectors give up."

"How long can you hold at reduced power?" Lena appeared in the engine room doorway, ducking under the low frame, rain streaming off her oilskin.

"At this rate? Six hours. Maybe eight if I keep swapping filters and pray a lot. After that we're drifting."

Lena looked at the cup of filthy fuel in Dez's hand for a long moment. Water ran off her hood and pooled on the deck plates. Somewhere overhead, thunder rolled down the valley like furniture being moved upstairs.

"The mission station has fuel storage," Dez said. "If it's any good. And there's the trading post at Two Sisters Bend, but that adds four hours—"

"No detours." Lena said it quickly. Too quickly. "We stay on schedule. Filter as we go. We'll purge at Blackwater."

Dez studied her. Lena's face in the dim light of the engine room was closed and set, the face she wore when a decision had been made that she didn't want examined. Dez had flown with her six years. She knew every version of that face.

"You've seen something," Dez said. Not a question.

"I've seen weather." Lena turned for the door. "Keep her alive, Dez."

"Always do."

But after Lena was gone, Dez sat on her stool with the bad fuel cupping her palms, and let herself think the thought she'd been refusing since the landing: *we are committed.* Committed to a schedule Lena wouldn't bend. Committed to cargo that weighed wrong. Committed up a flooding river, in a leaking antique, on fuel full of water, for a woman who paid triple and smiled too easily, on a route that ended where the law didn't bother going.

Tomas had died on a route like this. On *this* route, near enough. Everybody on the boat knew it and nobody had said it, the way a family doesn't say the name of the disease.

Dez drained the filter, fitted a fresh one, and reopened the fuel flow. The engine stumbled, caught, steadied. She patted the warm block the way she'd pat a horse.

"Just us, then," she said. "Do your job and I'll do mine."

* * *

Night came early under the storm, the way it does in monsoon country — not a sunset but a dimming, the gray going darker gray, then black, the rain a constant hiss on the water. Sefa anchored them in a cut behind a willow island for three hours while a squall line walked across the river throwing lightning like a juggler, and Dez used the time to swap filters and bail the bilge and discover, with a sinking professional horror, that the *Magpie*'s hull had a seam weeping just above the waterline on the port side, slow but honest, the kind of leak that gets worse exactly when you need it not to.

She hammered in a wooden plug wrapped in canvas and told herself it would hold. Told herself a lot of things.

At midnight they ran on. The river rose as they went, brown water climbing the banks, whole trees sailing past in the current like exhausted swimmers. Pip fell asleep in the wheelhouse chair and slid sideways and woke and pretended he hadn't. Sefa drank tea and navigated by lightning flash, reading the water's texture in half-second exposures — Dez watched her do it and marveled, the way she always did, at the navigator's ability to hold a map of an invisible world inside her head.

Near dawn, the engine coughed again, and this time the cough had a metallic edge to it, a sound like a coin in a grinder, and Dez's blood went cold in a way the rain never managed.

She shut her down to idle and climbed into the wheelhouse, dripping, grimy, hands black to the wrist.

"Talk to me," Lena said.

"Injectors are picking up grit. I've filtered everything I can filter. If I don't do a full tank drain and flush, we lose the engine inside two hours, and I cannot rebuild injectors on the water." Dez wiped her face with her forearm and only made it worse. "There's a fuel dump at the old logging camp above Serpent Rapids. Odile mentioned it. If it's stocked, if it's clean, if the access channel isn't silted shut — three ifs."

"And if it's not?"

"Then we're a very well-decorated island."

Silence, except the rain and the engine idling below like a patient animal. Lightning flickered downstream, silent, a rumor of worse weather coming up the valley toward them.

Lena stood at the wheelhouse window, and Dez watched her look north up the drowned river, toward Blackwater Bend, toward whatever it was she wasn't saying. Six years of flying together had taught Dez that Lena Thornbury's silences were load-bearing walls. You didn't knock them down. But you could lean on them.

"Cass Merrow's fuel is wet," Dez said quietly. "Her crates weigh wrong. Her schedule doesn't bend. And we're committed." She held up her black hands. "I'm not asking you to tell me anything, Captain. I'm just saying it out loud once, so it's been said."

Lena didn't answer right away. When she did, her voice was low and even.

"I know what it sounds like. All of it." A muscle worked in her jaw. "Get us to that fuel dump, Dez. Whatever's true, we deal with it better with a working engine."

"That's not a no."

"It's not a yes, either. It's a *keep us alive.*"

Dez nodded slowly and turned to go back down into the heat and noise of the engine room, where at least everything lied to her face instead of behind it. She had one foot on the ladder when Sefa spoke from the chart table, very quietly, in the voice she saved for things that mattered.

"Dez. Before you go down." She was staring at the river ahead, at the gray wall of rain where the channel bent north. "The water level's wrong. It's come up a foot since midnight, and the current's shifted. The flood crest is moving faster than the forecasts said." She looked up, and in the lamplight her face was calm the way deep water is calm. "Whatever we're going to do on this river, we should do it soon. The country's closing behind us."

Dez looked back the way they'd come — at the drowned banks, the sailing trees, the rain erasing the world — and understood that Sefa was right in a way that had nothing to do with charts. The landing was hours behind. The plane was hours behind. Everything was behind them now except the river, and the river only went one direction.

No turning back. There hadn't really been a turning back since Kessel's Landing, maybe since the vote, maybe since the day they'd sworn an oath over a grave and then spent six years pretending distance was the same thing as peace.

"Fuel dump first," Dez said. "Then whatever's waiting at Blackwater."

She went down the ladder into the dark, put her hands on the old engine, and began, gently, to bring her back up to speed — into the rising water, into the wall of rain, into whatever the crates held and whatever Cass Merrow wanted with them, committed now past any argument, running upriver into the monsoon with the dead man's river pulling them north.
