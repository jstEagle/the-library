## 7. What the Crates Hold

Pip had a rule about other people's cargo: don't ask, don't touch, don't count what you're carrying unless you're paid to count it. It was one of Lena's rules, actually, but Pip had adopted it the way he adopted most of the crew's rules — quickly, and without fully understanding why they mattered until they did.

The rule lasted four hours into the monsoon leg.

They were holed up in a river shack that smelled of fish oil and mildew, waiting out a squall that turned the sky the color of wet slate. The plane was tied down on the mudbank above the waterline, tarped and shivering in the wind. The boat — Cass Merrow's boat, loaned generous as a knife between the ribs — sat lower in the water beside it, riding heavy with eleven crates that were supposed to be medicine.

Pip wasn't supposed to be alone with them. That was the thing. Sefa had gone up the slope to argue with the shack's owner about mooring fees, Dez was elbow-deep in the plane's port engine swearing at a fuel line, and Lena had walked into the trees with the satellite phone held high, hunting for a signal bar like a woman trying to catch rain in her fist. Which left Pip, seventeen years old, wet to the knees, and alone in a floating box of secrets with nothing to do but think.

Thinking was dangerous. Thinking was how you got caught.

He told himself he'd just look. Not open — *look*. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole point of rules, wasn't it? Rules drew lines so you knew exactly how far over the line you'd stepped when somebody asked.

The crate nearest the stern had taken water. Not much — a dark bloom across its lid where rain had found a seam — but enough that when Pip pressed his palm flat against the wood, he felt the give of swelling grain, and beneath it something that didn't feel like medicine bottles packed in straw. Medicine crates had a certain sound when you knocked them. A hollow glassy clink, bottles kissing each other through their padding. Pip had hauled enough of them to know it the way he knew his own heartbeat.

This crate went *thud*. Dense. Dead.

He knocked again, softer, glancing up the slope. Dez's voice drifted down through the rain, arguing with an engine in a language only engines understood. Nobody came.

"Okay," Pip said out loud, because saying things out loud made them real and real things could be stopped. "Just looking."

The lid was nailed down with short galvanized nails, the cheap kind that bent instead of driving true. Two of them near the corner hadn't gone in flush; they stood proud of the wood like little metal teeth. Pip worked his thumbnail under one, then gave up on thumbnails and used the flat of his belt buckle, which was the tool he used for everything from prying floorboards to opening coconuts. The nail groaned. Gave. Came out with a squeal that sounded, in the hush between gusts of rain, loud enough to raise the dead.

He froze. Counted to thirty. Dez swore at the engine. The rain kept falling.

Three more nails. Then the lid lifted with a suck of damp air, and Pip looked down into the crate, and everything he thought he knew about this run tilted sideways and slid into the river.

No bottles. No straw. No white boxes stenciled with red crosses.

Bundles. Dozens of them, wrapped in oilcloth and bound with waxed twine, stacked in neat rows like loaves of bread. And on top, face-up, a ledger — a real ledger, cloth-bound, its corners worn soft with handling, swollen fat with years of entries. Water had touched the edge of the cover and left a tide mark across someone's careful handwriting.

Pip's mouth went dry even though the air was wet enough to drink.

He should have put the lid back. He knew that with the same cold clarity he knew the sound of medicine crates. Put the lid back, hammer the nail halfway home so nobody'd notice, spend the rest of the run being very good at not knowing anything.

Instead he picked up the ledger.

It was heavier than it looked. The pages inside were dense with columns — dates, names, sums, route numbers. Some of the names had been struck through with a single hard line, the ink pressed deep enough to emboss the page beneath. Others were circled. In the margins, in a different hand than the entries, small annotations in pencil: *confirmed*, *paid off*, *moved*, and once, chillingly plain, *handled*.

Pip didn't understand all of what he was reading. He understood enough. He'd grown up around the edges of the river trade, running messages for men who paid in crumpled notes and never looked you in the eye. He knew what ledgers like this were. They were the whole ugly machine written down — every payment, every shipment, every official bought and every witness priced. Ledgers like this got people killed. Ledgers like this were why you never stayed anywhere long enough to be found.

And there was a name in the margin notes that made his hands go still on the paper.

*Thornbury.*

Not Lena. He checked twice, heart slamming, before he saw it was *T.* Thornbury. Tomas Thornbury. Lena's brother. The one whose grave the crew had stood around in the rain six years ago while Sefa spoke the oath — the oath Pip hadn't been there for, having joined after, but which he'd heard about often enough to know its weight. *We carry no cargo for them. We fly no errands for them. We forget nothing.*

Tomas's name was all through these margins. Not as a buyer or a seller. As a problem. Page after page, the pencil hand tracking him: *asking questions at Kesswick landing*, *spoke to the customs clerk*, *has copies?*, and then, on a page dated three weeks before the grave, the last entry in that cramped pencil script:

*T. handled.*

Pip sat down hard on the gunwale. The boat rocked. Rain drummed the tarp overhead like fingers on a door.

The crew didn't talk about Tomas much. Lena least of all. Pip had pieced him together from fragments — a photograph in Sefa's navigation case, a joke Dez still half-told before catching himself, the way Lena flew low over rivers she didn't need to fly low over, like she was looking for something on the banks. A man who'd chased the smuggling ring the law never touched. A man who'd died for it. Pip had always understood it as finished. Grief, like a flight plan, filed and closed.

But here was Tomas's handwriting — or close enough to it, the pencil annotations matching the looping capitals Pip had seen on that old chart in Sefa's case — still moving through the world, still asking questions, still unfinished. Bound for somewhere downstream in the hold of a boat owned by the man who'd hired them. Hired *them*, specifically, the crew that had sworn off exactly this.

Bound for destruction, Pip realized, and the thought arrived whole and terrible, like weather. You didn't ship records like this upstream during monsoon season at triple pay to *use* them. You shipped them somewhere they could be burned, sunk, buried. Cass Merrow wasn't paying them to deliver medicine. He was paying them to make sure the past stayed dead.

And Tomas Thornbury was in those crates. Not his body — his work. His evidence. Everything he'd gathered before they handled him, sealed in oilcloth, riding in the belly of a boat Pip was sitting in.

Footsteps on the mud above. Pip's whole body went rigid.

"Pip!" Sefa's voice, cutting through the rain. "You alive down there?"

"Yeah," Pip called back. His voice cracked on the second syllable. He cleared his throat. "Yeah. Checking the tie-offs."

A pause. Long enough to be suspicious, short enough to be rain and hurry. "Don't fall in. Lena wants us loaded and ready — window in the weather's coming."

"Right." Pip looked down at the ledger in his lap, at the tide-marked cover, at the name struck through and circled and tracked across a hundred pages by a pencil that belonged to a dead man's enemy. "Right. Ready."

Sefa's footsteps faded up the slope.

* * *

Pip put the lid back on. Mostly.

He couldn't make himself nail it shut. His hands wouldn't do it — some stubborn animal part of him refusing to seal the evidence away again, to help bury it a second time. So he set the lid in place, loose, and dragged a coil of rope and a tarp corner over the crate until it looked like ordinary clutter, and then he climbed the muddy slope with his heart going like a two-stroke engine and found the others under the lean-to behind the shack.

Lena was there, phone finally surrendered, shaking rain off her jacket. Dez sat on an upturned crate working a rag down a wrench. Sefa was counting coins into the shack owner's palm. Ordinary. Safe. The kind of scene where a person could keep a secret forever if they wanted to.

Pip opened his mouth to say something about the weather window and heard himself say, instead: "I looked in a crate."

Everything stopped. Dez's rag froze mid-stroke. Sefa's coins went quiet. Lena turned, and her eyes did the thing they did — went from surprise to assessment to something older and colder, the look of a woman recalculating every mile of a route she'd already flown.

"You *what*?"

"The stern crate took water. I heard it — it doesn't sound right, it doesn't sound like medicine, and I—" Pip's words were tumbling now, tripping over each other. "It's not medicine. It's ledgers. Bundles of records, wrapped up, dozens of them. Shipping manifests, payments, names. Names of officials. And—" He swallowed. This was the part that mattered, the part that had followed him up the slope like a dog. "There are notes in the margins. Somebody tracking a man through the whole book. T. Thornbury. Your brother, Lena. And the last note says—"

"*Handled*," Lena said.

Her voice was flat. Not surprised. That was the worst part — Pip had expected shock, denial, anger at him for opening the crate. Instead she said it like a word she'd been carrying for six years, set down now in front of everyone, and her face didn't change at all except around the eyes, where something tightened like a knot drawn fast.

"You knew," Dez said quietly. He'd set the wrench down. "You suspected."

"I inspected the crates before we loaded," Lena said. "In the dark, at Cass's dock, like I inspect everything. The weights were wrong for medical supplies. Too dense, too uniform. I told myself it could be anything — contraband liquor, untaxed goods, the usual. Something we could finish, deliver, and never speak of." She looked out at the rain, at the grey river sliding past the mooring. "I didn't let myself think it might be *that*. The ring. His ring."

"So we turn around," Sefa said. It wasn't a question, but it wasn't quite a statement either. She was already doing the arithmetic Pip could see happening behind her eyes — fuel, distance, the flooded channels, the weather window closing like a fist. "We take the crates back downriver, dump them in the deepest hole we can find, and we're gone before anyone knows."

"And Cass sends the next crew," Lena said. "Or comes himself. And the next time, there's no curious kid with a belt buckle to open the lid first. The records burn, and everything Tomas bled for burns with them, and the man who wrote *handled* next to my brother's name keeps his river." She turned back to them. Rain ran off the lean-to's edge in a beaded curtain between her and the boat. "That's the choice. That's what's actually in those crates. Not cargo. Him. The unfinished version of him."

Nobody spoke. The rain filled the silence.

Pip felt it land in his chest like ballast — the weight of what he'd found settling into place. Six years ago he'd been a dock kid running messages, too young for the grave, too young for the oath. Now the oath's reason was sitting in the stern of their borrowed boat, wrapped in oilcloth, waiting for him to decide what happened next along with everybody else.

"We could sink them ourselves," Dez offered. "Here. No delivery, no Cass, no—"

"No," Pip said.

Everyone looked at him. He hadn't meant to say it out loud. But it was out now, standing in the rain with the rest of them, and it refused to take itself back.

"They tried to kill those records once already," Pip said. His voice shook and he let it. "When they killed Tomas. And it didn't work, because he hid copies, or sent them places, or — I don't know, but the ledger says they weren't sure. *Has copies?* It's written right there, they *weren't sure*. And now the originals are in our boat, and if we sink them, we finish the job for the people who killed him. We do what they couldn't." He looked at Lena. "You always say we don't stay long enough to be found. But we've already been found. Cass found us. He hired us. He put his brother-killing cargo in our hands because he thinks we're the kind of crew that doesn't look."

He took a breath.

"I looked."

The rain came down. Somewhere upriver, thunder rolled along the valley like a barrel.

Lena studied him for a long moment — the way she studied weather, charts, the faces of strangers at fuel stops. Then something in her shoulders shifted, a degree of angle, a decision arriving.

"Nobody opens another crate without me there," she said. "And nobody talks about this where the walls are thinner than the rain. We run the window like planned, like honest couriers, all the way to the transfer point." Her jaw set. "After that, we decide what Cass Merrow's medicine is really worth — and to whom."

Sefa exhaled slowly through her nose. Dez picked his wrench back up and put it down again. And Pip climbed back down the muddy slope toward the boat, toward the loose-lidded crate and the dead man's handwriting, knowing — the way you know a storm's bearing, the way you know the sound a crate makes when it isn't medicine — that whatever waited for them at Blackwater Bend, it had been waiting since the day Tomas Thornbury died.

And now, at last, it was awake.
