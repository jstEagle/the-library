## 8. Betrayal at Blackwater Bend

The rain had been falling for two days without once deciding to be serious about it — a gray, patient drizzle that soaked everything and proved nothing. Lena stood at the wheel of the *Marrow's Gift*, the old river boat they'd transferred to at Kessel's Landing, and watched Blackwater Bend come up out of the mist like a rumor.

She didn't like it. She hadn't liked anything about this run since the crates.

"Current's fast through here," Sefa said beside her, one hand on the rail, eyes on the water. The bend itself was a wide S-curve where the river doubled back on itself around a bluff of black stone, and in monsoon season the whole channel turned into something with opinions. "We'll want to hug the inside line or we'll get shoved into the cutbank."

"I know the line."

"You know the line in dry season." Sefa said it without heat, which was worse. Sefa never used heat when she was right.

Lena adjusted anyway. The boat answered sluggishly — she was riding low, heavier than her manifest claimed, and every hour of rain had added weight to the canvas covers and the deck planking. Somewhere below, Pip was keeping watch on the cargo hold, because none of them trusted what they were carrying anymore, and Dez was in the engine room pretending the port engine wasn't making a sound engines shouldn't make.

Three days upriver from Kessel's Landing. One more day to the transfer point at Harrow Flats, where Cass Merrow's people would take the crates off their hands and pay them triple rate for a job that should have been refused.

Lena had spent those three days doing arithmetic she hated. The manifest said medical supplies: plasma expanders, antibiotics, surgical kits bound for the mission station at Harrow Flats. The crates said otherwise. She'd pried one open at midnight outside Vell's Reach while the others slept, and found beneath a convincing top layer of sealed saline bags a false floor, and beneath the false floor — ledgers. Dozens of them. Bound in oilcloth, wax-sealed, packed with the kind of care people only gave things they intended to survive a long journey intact.

She knew ledgers like that. She'd seen them once before, six years ago, spread across a magistrate's table by a man named Tomas Thornbury who believed the law would do what the law was for.

The law hadn't. And Tomas was under a cairn on a hill above Corvane with his name spelled wrong on the marker because nobody who loved him could afford a stonecutter who could spell.

*You swore off,* she told herself, hands steady on the wheel. *You took the job anyway. So finish it, take the money, and get clear.* That was the rule. That had always been the rule. Never stay long enough to be found, never carry anything worth killing for longer than you had to, never let the crew become the kind of thing that grief could use as a weapon against you.

The river bent. The black bluff slid past on their starboard side, streaming with water, and ahead the channel narrowed between drowned willows whose crowns stuck up out of the flood like the antlers of some submerged herd.

That was when she saw the boats.

Two of them, sitting low and dark in the lee of the bluff where the current slackened — gunmetal-gray hulls, tarped superstructures, no lights. They'd been there a while. Waiting. As the *Marrow's Gift* came abeam, the tarps came off, and Lena saw the mounts on the bow decks and the men behind them, and one of the men raised a hand — not a weapon, just a hand, palm out, almost friendly.

"Sefa," Lena said quietly. "Kill the engine."

Sefa was already moving. The diesel coughed, staggered, died, and the silence that followed was enormous — just rain on the canopy and the river muttering against the hull as their momentum carried them forward, helpless, into the space between the two gray boats.

"Don't," Lena called out, loud enough to carry. "Nobody touches anything."

Dez came up the companionway wiping his hands on a rag, took one look, and went pale in a way that had nothing to do with fear of guns. "That's Merrow's livery," he said. "That's — Lena, that's Cass's own boats. Why would he—"

"Because he already has what he sent us for," Lena said. "Or he wants us to think he doesn't."

A voice carried across the water, amplified by a cheap megaphone and the shape of the bluff. "*Lena Thornbury.* Bring your boat alongside the near hull. You'll be paid at Harrow Flats as contracted. This is a security check. Nothing more."

Security check. In the middle of Blackwater Bend, in monsoon season, with two armed boats positioned exactly where the current gave her no options. Her brother had died chasing men who talked like that — smooth procedural language wrapped around violence like gauze around a blade.

"We're contracted through to Harrow Flats," she called back. "Contract says delivery at the flats. I don't stop mid-river for anyone."

The pause that followed lasted four seconds. She counted them. Then the megaphone again, and the friendliness had drained out of the voice entirely.

"*Ms. Thornbury. My employer is a patient man, but the river is not. Come alongside, or we will come to you, and your boat is old and the water is high. Your crew does not have to be part of this.*"

There it was. The knife inside the gauze. *Your crew does not have to be part of this* — meaning: give me a reason and I'll make them part of it.

Lena looked at Sefa. Sefa's face was unreadable, but her hand had drifted to the signal lamp housing, and her eyes flicked toward the stern, toward the skiff lashed there, toward the flooded willow country on the far bank where a small boat might — might — lose itself among the drowned trees if the shooters were slow and the gods were generous.

Pip appeared at the hatch, sixteen years old and trying very hard to look like someone who'd been in a standoff before. "What's happening?"

"Nothing," Lena said. "Go below."

"Lena—"

"*Below, Pip.*"

Pip went. Good kid. Smart enough to be scared, obedient enough to live.

Lena stepped to the rail. "All right," she called. "We come alongside. Nobody boards with weapons drawn. That's my condition."

"Agreed," said the megaphone, too quickly.

*Too quickly,* Lena thought. *Everything about this is too quick.*

They drifted in. The gray hull loomed up on the port side, and lines went across, and men in wet oilskins stepped aboard the *Marrow's Gift* with the unhurried confidence of people who had done this many times and never once been sorry. Six of them. Two stayed on their own boat covering the deck; four fanned out across her ship with their slings loose but their hands ready, and the one in charge — a broad, gray-templed man with a scar through his eyebrow whom Lena would later learn was called Voss — walked straight past her to the cargo hatch as if he already knew the way.

"You're in violation of the contract," Lena said to his back.

Voss didn't turn around. "The contract was between you and Mr. Merrow. I'm not party to it." He nodded at two of his men. "Open them. All of them. Start aft."

They worked fast and professional, slicing the canvas ties, popping the seals, peeling back the oilcloth. Lena watched the first crate come open — saline bags on top, and beneath them, exposed now to the rain and to everyone, the ledger bundles in their wax-sealed wrapping. Voss lifted one out, weighed it in his hand, and smiled at it the way a man smiles at money.

"There it is," he said softly. Then, louder: "Count them. All accounted?"

"Thirty-one," one of his men called back.

"Thirty-two shipped," Voss said, and turned at last to look at Lena, and his smile widened by a degree that made her skin crawl. "One short, Ms. Thornbury. Where is it?"

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"No?" He set the ledger down on an empty crate, deliberately, within her sightline. "My employer is very thorough. Thirty-two bundles left Corvane. Thirty-one are here. Someone opened a crate somewhere between Vell's Reach and here, and someone removed one bundle, and I am going to need you to tell me where it is, because the man who pays me becomes extremely unpleasant about missing inventory."

Cold moved down Lena's spine and settled in her stomach. Pip. Pip had pried a crate open three nights ago — she'd caught the boy at it, seen the evidence spilling out under a lantern, heard the whispered argument afterward. They'd resealed it. They'd thought they'd resealed it well enough.

And Pip still had a bundle hidden in the hold, wrapped in a spare shirt, because Pip couldn't let go of it — because Pip had read enough of it, in one night of forbidden lamplight, to understand that these pages were the ghost of Tomas Thornbury, walking.

"They're all there," Lena said. "Count again."

"Oh, we'll count again." Voss sighed, theatrical, regretful. "But counting takes time, and time is the one thing this river won't sell us. So here's what happens instead." He gestured, casually, at the boat around them. "This vessel takes on water. Tragic. Old hull, monsoon season, these things happen. You and your crew will have ample time to save yourselves. We'll take the cargo from here — Mr. Merrow's own launch is faster anyway — and you can consider your contract fulfilled upon your eventual arrival at Harrow Flats, alive or otherwise. Payment to follow. Eventually. To your next of kin, if it comes to that."

"You're joking," Dez said. It burst out of him. "You're — the manifest was medical supplies, there's a mission station waiting on—"

"The mission station got its supplies," Voss said. "Top layer. Check it yourself, if you live long enough." He snapped his fingers at his men. "Below. Scupper plugs. Both of them. And the seacocks — open them a quarter turn, no more. Let it come in slow. I'm not a monster."

He said it like he wanted credit for it.

They found the scupper plugs in ninety seconds. Lena watched them do it — watched the rubber plugs pulled from the drain channels in the bilge, watched the quarter-turn of the raw-water seacocks, watched the first thin threads of river water begin to thread across the bilge plates like veins under skin. It was methodical. It was practiced. These men had sunk boats before.

"Voss," Lena said, and the name stopped him at the rail, halfway back to his own hull. "Cass Merrow killed a man over these ledgers six years ago. A pilot named Tomas Thornbury. Did he ever tell you that? Because the ledgers say so. Page forty, if you can read."

Something flickered behind the scarred eyebrow. Not guilt. Recognition — the flicker of a man filing information away for later, deciding whether it was useful or dangerous.

"Everyone's dead over something, Ms. Thornbury," Voss said. "That's what makes the living so valuable." He stepped across onto the gray hull. "Lines off. Good luck. The willows start a mile downriver — if you make the bank, the fishing camps at Serpent's Tooth sometimes take strangers in. If you're feeling charitable toward us, tell them Merrow's men were here and were very sorry about it."

The lines came off. The gray boat's engine turned over, a deep animal sound, and it pulled away into the rain with thirty-one bundles of her brother's unfinished work aboard, and Lena stood at the rail of her dying ship and did not scream, because screaming was a luxury and she'd given up luxuries years ago.

*Never stay long enough to be found,* she thought. *Never carry anything worth killing for.*

Every rule she had, broken in a single contract. Every fear she'd ever fed, confirmed in a single morning. Stopping cost you. Belonging cost you. Trusting an old rival's money cost you — and now the bill was coming due in cold river water rising inch by inch beneath her feet.

* * *

They worked.

There was nothing else to do, so they worked. Dez got the port engine running again — the sound it had been making turned out to be a cracked mount, irrelevant now, since the engine's only job was to push them toward the bank before the river decided their course for them. Sefa took the wheel and threaded them downstream through the drowned willows with the hull settling by degrees, listing to starboard, the bilge pump running flat-out and losing ground anyway.

"How bad?" Lena asked, coming down into the hold, where the water was ankle-deep and rising and cold as a confession.

"Seacocks are quarter-open, which means maybe twenty liters a minute, plus whatever's coming through the seams now that the hull's working." Dez was elbow-deep in the bilge, wrenching at something. "I can close the seacocks if I can reach them, but they're under the water line now, which means somebody's going swimming in bilge soup to do it."

"I'll swim."

"You'll command," Dez said, without looking up. "Pip's smaller than you. Pip fits."

"Pip stays out of the water."

"Lena." Dez finally looked up, and there was rain and bilge-grime on his face and something underneath both that she recognized from six years ago, from the graveside, from every bad night since. "We're all going in the water eventually today. The only question is whether we choose our turns."

In the end it was Pip who went under — of course it was, because Pip was sixteen and indestructible and had something to prove — and Pip came up gasping with the seacock key in one fist and a grin that fooled nobody. They closed the intake. They slowed the bleeding. They did not stop it.

By late afternoon the *Marrow's Gift* grounded herself, mercifully, on a sandbar shelf a half-mile short of the true bank, wedged in the shallows with the flood combing over her foredeck and the drowned forest standing all around them like a congregation of the drowned themselves. The willow crowns broke the current into a thousand small eddies. Rain fell. Dusk came down gray and total.

They sat in the wheelhouse, the four of them, soaked through, and took inventory out loud because saying numbers made them real and manageable.

"One boat, aground, holed, unfixable without a dry dock we don't have," Sefa said. "One skiff, twelve feet, holds three uncomfortably. Four crew. No radio — the aerial went with the mast when we grounded. Half a case of ration bars, two jerry cans of water, one flare pistol with three flares."

"And thirty-two bundles of evidence," Pip said quietly, "minus thirty-one."

Everyone looked at Pip. Pip looked at the deck.

"I took one," Pip said. "The night at Vell's Reach. I put it back — I thought I put it back right, I resealed the wax, I — I read some of it, Lena. Some of it's in his handwriting. Your brother's. There are notes in the margins, dates, names, and I thought—" The voice cracked and steadied, the way young voices do when they've decided to be older than they are. "I thought if we just delivered them like Cass wanted, they'd burn. Everything he died for would burn, and we'd have helped carry it to the fire. I couldn't. I hid it in the skiff's locker under the tarp kit. It's still there. It's the only reason we still have any of it."

The rain filled the silence. Lena found she wasn't angry. She'd expected to be angry — had rehearsed anger on the walk down to the hold — but what arrived instead was something older and stranger. Tomas had been twenty-six and certain and impossible to stop, and Pip was sixteen and certain and impossible to stop, and the world kept producing people like this no matter how many times it killed them.

"You did wrong," Lena said. "You nearly got us all killed."

"Yes."

"And you saved the one thing on this river worth saving." She held out her hand. "Give it to me. Not because I don't trust you. Because if Voss comes back looking, it needs to be on me."

Pip fetched it. The oilcloth bundle was damp at one corner but the wax seals had held, and inside, when Lena peeled a fold back by lantern light, the ledger paper was dry and dense with columns of figures — shipments, routes, payments, officials' names — and in the margins, cramped and slanting, a handwriting she would have known blindfolded, underwater, at the bottom of the river.

*T. — confirm with the harbormaster before the 14th. He lies about the second channel.*

Her brother's voice, six years dead, giving her directions.

Sefa leaned over her shoulder. "That's not just records. That's a map of the whole operation. Names, dates, payees. This is enough to hang Merrow if it reached anyone with authority and a spine."

"Authority we can find," Lena said slowly. "A spine is harder. The last magistrate who saw pages like this sold them back to Merrow for a fishing lodge, and the courts closed the file, and the man who collected the evidence got dead in a boating accident." She folded the oilcloth closed and pressed her palm flat on it, the way you'd press a wound. "So we don't hand it to the first uniform we see. We get it to someone who's already proven they can't be bought. And until then, it doesn't leave my body."

"That's dramatic," Dez said.

"So is drowning."

Outside, full dark now, the rain thickening from drizzle toward something with intent — the barometer Sefa kept nailed by the wheel had been falling all day, and she kept glancing at it the way you glance at a dog that's gone quiet. The monsoon wasn't finished with them. The monsoon, Lena suspected, was just clearing its throat.

She stood in the wheelhouse doorway and looked out at the black water sliding past the sandbar, and let herself feel it, briefly, honestly: the fear. Not of the water. Of the stopping. Of what happened to people who planted themselves in front of men like Cass Merrow and refused to move. Tomas had stopped. Tomas had planted himself. And the river had taken him, or Merrow had, depending on which version of the story you believed, and neither version had ever been made to answer for it.

*Running kept us alive,* she reminded herself. *Running is the only reason there's still a crew.*

But running hadn't saved Tomas. Running hadn't saved the towns downriver that bought their medicine and whispered about Merrow and looked away. Running had just moved the grave markers further apart.

"Lena." Sefa's voice, low. "Look. Upstream. At the bend."

Lena raised the lantern.

Far upriver, back the way they'd come, two sets of running lights had appeared through the rain — moving slow, sweeping the banks, searching. Gray hulls. Coming back downriver, working the channels methodically, the way you search when you're not sure the body sank.

Voss had remembered something. Or Cass had asked a question Voss hadn't liked. Either way, they were coming to finish a job they'd left half-done, and the only thing between the crew and the searching lights was a mile of drowned willows, a twelve-foot skiff, and the dark.

"Skiff," Lena said. "Now. Everything we can carry and nothing we can't afford to lose."

"What about the boat?" Pip asked.

Lena looked back at the *Marrow's Gift*, settled on her sandbar like a tired animal lying down in the rain, and felt the cost of the run tally itself one line higher.

"She's done," Lena said. "Leave her. We were never meant to keep anything anyway."
