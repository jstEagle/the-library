## 9. Taking On Water

The first thing Sefa learned about the boat Cass left them was that it lied.

It sat in the black water with the smugness of a thing that had already won — a flat-bottomed river barge gone soft with rot, its gunwales green with moss, its single mast snapped and lashed back at some earlier indignity. From the outside it looked like a boat. From the inside, where Sefa knelt in six inches of cold water by lantern light, it looked like a grave that hadn't finished digging itself.

"Tell me again how bad," Dez said from the stern, both hands pressed to a seam as if he could hold the river out through sheer stubbornness.

"Bad." Sefa swept her palm along the hull's inner skin and felt the give of wood gone punky, the whisper of water finding new paths every time the river breathed against them. "She's not leaking. She's *permeable*. There's a difference. A leak you can find. This is the whole hull deciding it's tired."

Pip stood in the bow holding the lantern high, and the light made a small trembling island around them — three people, one dying boat, and forty kilometers of flooded country on every side, black and featureless except for the crowns of drowned trees standing up out of the water like the masts of other, unluckier ships.

"They could have just shot us," Pip said. It wasn't a complaint. It was an observation, the way Pip said most things now, having learned somewhere between Blackwater Bend and here that the world ran on arithmetic nobody taught children.

"They wanted us to do it ourselves," Sefa said. "Cass Merrow doesn't waste bullets on people the monsoon will kill for free."

* * *

They had been set adrift two hours past dusk.

Sefa kept replaying it, because her mind did that — it took the worst hour of her life and turned it over and over like a stone in a riverbed until she'd worn all the sharp edges off and could hold it without bleeding. Cass's men had come alongside in a low gray skiff, engines muffled, rain hoods up, polite as tax collectors. Four of them. One had held a shotgun across his knees with the casualness of a man holding a lunch pail, and another had stepped aboard, checked the crates they'd recovered — no, not recovered; *allowed to keep*, Sefa understood now, because empty crates would have raised questions and full ones bought them their hour's head start — and then smiled at Lena with something that wasn't quite pity.

"Cass says you can keep the boat," the man had said. "He says you always were good at staying afloat."

Then they'd cut the towline, and the current had taken them sideways into the dark, and the last thing Sefa saw of the gunboat was its running lights shrinking away upriver toward whatever place Cass Merrow went to count his money and sleep soundly.

Lena hadn't spoken for the first hour. She'd stood at the stern with the rain running off her chin, watching the lights go, and Sefa had watched her instead of the water, because someone had to know where the captain was even if the captain didn't want to be found. When Lena finally spoke, it was one word — *"Bail"* — and the crew had moved, because that was the whole architecture of their lives: Lena decided, and they survived it.

Now the bailing was rhythm-work, and rhythm was the only prayer Sefa trusted. She'd grown up on boats before she'd ever flown one; her grandmother used to say the sea doesn't hate you, it just doesn't know you're there, and rivers were worse, because rivers knew exactly where you were and had opinions about it. She worked the bail bucket in a steady arc — scoop, swing, dump, scoop — while Dez moved along the seams with tarred canvas and the boat's own broken pride, hammering wedges into the worst gaps, caulking what could be caulked, pressing his palms flat against the hull like a doctor listening for a heartbeat.

"How long?" Lena asked. She'd taken the second bucket. Of course she had. Lena Thornbury never asked anyone to do work she wouldn't do herself, which was either leadership or martyrdom depending on the hour.

"If we keep ahead of it? Till morning." Sefa dumped another load over the side. "If we stop bailing? Twenty minutes. Maybe less if the wind swings."

"And if the wind swings?"

Sefa didn't answer. The wind swung. That was what monsoon wind did. Somewhere out in the drowned forest, a tree groaned and let go, and the splash rolled across the water like distant artillery.

* * *

The rain came down harder around midnight, if midnight was a thing that still existed. Time had gone soft since Blackwater Bend. There was only wet and cold and the lantern's shrinking circle and the endless arithmetic of the buckets: water in, water out, and the question underneath everything, unspoken but constant — *which side is winning tonight?*

Dez found the leak that mattered at half past twelve. He found it the hard way, by putting his arm shoulder-deep into the bilge and following the cold tongue of inflow back to its source: a seam near the keel where the planking had opened like a split lip, wide enough to fit two fingers into, fed by the pressure of the river itself.

"There," he said, and his voice had gone flat and careful, the voice he used when the news was bad enough that shouting would only make it worse. "I can't get to it from inside. Not properly. I need to fother the hull — canvas over the outside, weighted down, let the river hold the patch on for us."

"You'll have to go under," Sefa said. It wasn't a question. Fothering meant diving in black floodwater, in the rain, beside a boat that could shift or roll or simply suck him under when the patch pulled tight.

"I'll have to go under," Dez agreed. He was already stripping off his jacket, moving with the economy of a man who'd done dangerous things in bad places so many times that fear had become a tool he picked up and put down as needed. "Tie me off. Short line. If I yank twice, haul."

"I'll haul," Pip said.

"No," Sefa and Lena said together.

"Yes," Pip said, and planted those narrow feet on the wet deck, chin up, lantern light catching the stubborn set of a jaw that was going to belong to somebody formidable someday and was already practicing. "You're bailing. Both of you. If we stop bailing while Dez is in the water, we all go down together and the line doesn't matter. I'm the only one who can hold the rope and watch the water at the same time. I've got the best eyes. You said so yourself, Sefa. In Kessari. You said I spotted the sandbar before you did."

Sefa had said that. She'd said it eight months ago, in sunlight, as a joke. She hadn't known she was writing a contract.

"Twice means haul," she said. "One tug means hold. And Pip — if the line goes slack, you don't come looking. You shout, and we come to you. Promise me."

Pip promised. Children promised easily; that was the terror of them. Sefa tied the line around Dez's chest herself, checked it twice, and then held his face in her hands for a moment — mechanic, friend, the man who'd kept their engines alive through eleven countries and one funeral — and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the knot didn't say better.

Dez went over the side without a splash. He'd always been like that, even as a boy in the engine yards — quiet entry, no wasted motion, trouble met on its own terms.

For ninety seconds there was only rain and the bucket-work and the line running out through Sefa's fist, and Sefa counted, because counting was what navigators did when there was nothing left to navigate: *one, two, three,* the river breathing against the hull, *ten, eleven, twelve,* the lantern guttering, *twenty, twenty-one* —

The line jerked once. Hold. Then again, hard, twice, and Dez's head broke the surface gasping, one arm thrown over the gunwale, and behind him, streaming water and moonlight-less dark, the canvas patch clung to the hull like a bandage on a wound that wanted to be fatal.

"It's holding," Dez coughed. "It's — get me up, it's holding—"

They hauled him over the rail and he lay on the deck shivering and grinning, and Sefa realized she was laughing, high and cracked, the laugh that lived next door to crying, and that Lena had stopped bailing for exactly four seconds to press her forehead to Dez's soaked hair before picking the bucket back up. Four seconds. That was the budget for relief on this boat tonight.

* * *

It was nearly two in the morning when the river tried to take Pip.

Later, Sefa would assemble it piece by piece, the way she assembled charts — this fix, that bearing, the truth emerging only from overlap. Here was what she knew: the wind had backed around to the northwest, driving a short ugly chop across the flooded plain, and the boat, riding light after Cass's men took the cargo, had begun to yaw at anchor. Here was what she knew: Pip had been sent forward to check the bow anchor line, because the bow anchor was all that stood between them and the open water to the east, where the drowned forest thinned out and the current ran like a freight train. Here was what she knew: the line had chafed.

She heard it before she understood it — a crack like a knuckle, too small for a tree, too sharp for thunder — and then the boat's whole posture changed, the bow swinging, the deck tilting, and Pip's lantern went spinning across the planks and gutted out, and in the sudden dark Sefa heard the one sound she would hear for the rest of her life whenever she closed her eyes:

A splash. Small. Child-sized.

"Pip!" Lena was moving before the word left Sefa's mouth, and Sefa was moving too, and Dez was up off the deck like a man electrocuted, and none of them had a light, none of them had a line rigged, none of them had anything but the fact that the river does not negotiate and does not wait.

"Throw line!" Sefa shouted, tearing the coil off the stern cleat, and Lena caught it and paid it out toward the sound, toward nothing, toward the black water where a child had stopped being visible. "Pip! Shout! SHOUT!"

For three seconds — Sefa counted them, she counted everything, it was her curse and her craft — there was silence, and in that silence Sefa's mind offered her, with terrible clarity, the image of Tomas. Tomas going under in different water, years ago, in the job they'd sworn off, the brother who had shouted and shouted until the river closed over the sound. *Not again. Not this boat. Not this family.*

Then, from ten meters off the port bow, small and furious and alive:

"HERE! I'm HERE — something's got my — my foot's STUCK—"

Not drowning. Tangled. The chafed anchor line had whipped out with the swinging bow and wrapped Pip's ankle, and now the child hung in the black water at the end of it, held up by a rope and dragged down by the same rope, the river pulling the boat one way and the anchor dragging the other, and Pip was the knot in between.

"Hold the line, don't pull!" Dez was already at the rail, knife in his teeth, second knife in his hand. "Lena — weight the stern, NOW, or the swing takes them under!"

Lena threw her whole body against the stern ballast and Sefa threw hers beside her, two women leaning into dead weight while the boat groaned and the angle changed by degrees, and Dez went over the rail feet-first into the water with the knife, and Sefa heard him working — heard Pip sobbing and cursing at once, which was somehow the best sound of the night — and then the tension on the throw-line changed, and Dez's voice came up out of the dark, ragged:

"Got them. Haul. HAUL."

They hauled. Pip came over the gunwale like a landed fish, streaming, shaking, lips blue, ankle rope-burned and bleeding, and Dez followed on the tether, and for a moment the four of them lay in a heap on the wet deck, a tangle of arms and rope and breath, nobody speaking, everybody holding on to everybody, the boat rocking beneath them like something alive that had decided, for tonight, to let them stay.

"We re-anchor," Lena said into the dark. Her voice was wrecked and level at the same time, which Sefa thought might be the definition of the woman. "Bow and stern both. Nothing on this boat is trusted again. Nothing."

"Aye, Captain," Dez whispered.

"Sefa. Fix our position. However you have to. Stars, soundings, smell — I don't care. I need to know where we are by dawn."

Sefa crawled to the shattered compass binnacle, cupped her hands around the one dry match she had left, and read the card by the flame's last breath. Then she sat back in the rain and listened — really listened, the old way, her grandmother's way — to the water talking against the hull, to the far-off grind of current through a channel she remembered from a chart she'd memorized five years ago and never expected to need.

"Ilya's sandbar," she said slowly. "Or close to it. Southeast, maybe six kilometers, if the chart in my head isn't lying. There was a pilot living out there. Off-grid. People said he knew the flooded country like his own kitchen."

Lena was quiet a moment. Rain drummed the deck. Below them, faint but steady, the patched hull held.

"Anyone who lives alone on a sandbar in monsoon season," Lena said at last, "is hiding from something. Or hunting something."

"Maybe both," Sefa said.

"Either way." Lena picked up her bucket. "We're not picky anymore. Bail till dawn. At first light, we limp southeast — and we pray the man on the sandbar likes visitors worse than he likes being alone."

Sefa scooped, swung, dumped. The river breathed against the patched hull and, for now, stayed outside where it belonged. Around them the drowned forest stood silent, patient, full of things they couldn't see — and somewhere out in that darkness, six kilometers and one desperate morning away, a stranger waited who knew these waters better than any map, and whose reasons, Sefa suspected, were about to become their own.
