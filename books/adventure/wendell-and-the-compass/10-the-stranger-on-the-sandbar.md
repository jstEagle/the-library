## 10. The Stranger on the Sandbar

The river gave them up at dawn, spitting the boat onto a sandbar like something it had chewed all night and found wanting.

Lena felt the keel grind and shudder beneath her, felt the whole hull shiver through her boots, and for one long moment nobody moved. Sefa was still bailing. Pip was asleep sitting up, arms wrapped around a bucket, cheek pressed to the gunwale, mouth open. Dez stood at the tiller with both hands white-knuckled on it, steering them onto the bar as deliberately as a woman walking into a room to end an argument.

"Ground," Dez said. Her voice was sandpaper. She hadn't slept either — none of them had — but she'd taken the last watch because Lena's hands had started shaking somewhere around the third hour of the night, and Dez had simply lifted them off the bail line without a word and pointed her toward the stern.

"Ground," Lena agreed.

She looked back downriver, the way they'd come. The monsoon was still out there, a gray wall of rain marching up the valley, but here in its lee the world was almost gentle: mist lifting off the water, birds working the shallows, the drowned forest standing hip-deep on either side of the channel with its leaves dripping. It was obscene, how beautiful it could look after what it had tried to do to them.

They'd lost everything else. The plane was back at the fuel strip under Cass Merrow's contract, which meant under Cass Merrow's thumb. The crates were gone — seized at Blackwater Bend by men who hadn't even bothered to hide their faces. The boat they were stranded on was taking water through a seam that canvas and prayer had slowed but not stopped. And Cass had left them alive, which was worse than if he hadn't. Alive meant witnesses who could be found later. Alive meant loose ends.

*He wants us to die out here,* Lena thought, looking at the gray water sliding past the bow. *And he wants plausible deniability while we do it.*

"Everyone out," she said. "We need to get her light enough to see the damage."

Pip woke when the boat touched bottom properly, jolting upright with the bucket still clutched to his chest like a teddy bear. He blinked at the sandbar, at the trees, at the sky going pale behind the mist. "Are we dead?" he asked seriously.

"Not yet," said Sefa.

"Oh." Pip considered this. "Good. I'm hungry."

* * *

The boat sat tilted on the bar with her belly exposed, and the damage was ugly. A long split along the port strake where the interceptors had rammed them, and below that, where the planking had flexed all night, two seams had opened like mouths. Dez lay half in the bilge with a hammer and a lump of pitch, swearing steadily and creatively, while Sefa and Pip hauled their remaining gear onto the sand: one crate of medicine they'd kept back for themselves, a tarp, two coils of rope, a dented kettle, and the clothes they stood in. Everything else had gone into the river or into Cass's hold.

Lena walked the bar while they worked, because if she stopped moving she would have to think, and thinking led straight back to Blackwater Bend — to the moment the other boat had swung across their bow and men with rifles had come over the rail casual as dockworkers, and Cass's foreman, Voss, had smiled at her with real regret and said, *Nothing personal, Miss Thornbury. Just business being business.* To the moment the current took their lines and the last thing she saw of the evidence — Tomas's evidence, the reason he was dead — was a crate lid nailed shut over it, bound for wherever things went to be destroyed.

Her brother had spent two years copying those ledgers. Had died with copies sewn into his coat lining. And she had carried the originals upriver in a crate marked MEDICAL SUPPLIES because Cass Merrow had paid triple rate and Dez owed money to people who broke fingers, and Lena had told herself it was just one run, just this once, just until they got clear.

*Never stay long enough to be found. Never take a job you can't walk away from.* She'd broken both rules in a single week, and now here she was, found, stranded, and walking away from nothing.

The sandbar curved maybe two hundred meters before it merged with a stand of flooded casuarina. At the far end of it, half-hidden by the trees, there was smoke.

Not forest smoke — too thin, too steady, too deliberate. A cook fire, banked and tended.

Lena stopped. Stood very still. Listened past the drip of the leaves and the lap of the water.

A man stepped out of the treeline.

He was tall and lean in the way of people who worked rivers for a living, browned to the color of old rope, somewhere between forty and sixty with a face weathered past guessing. Gray stubble. Eyes the flat pale green of bottle glass. He carried no weapon that she could see, which meant nothing at all, and he walked toward her without hurry, boots sinking slightly in the wet sand, and stopped a respectful ten paces off.

"You're leaking," he said. His voice was low and rough, an accent she couldn't place — somewhere upriver, maybe, or nowhere at all.

"We noticed."

"That hull's been patched twice already. Canvas over the forward seam." He nodded at the boat. "You did that yourselves?"

"Last night."

"In the dark. In the rain." Something flickered in his eyes that might have been approval. "You'll do."

"I'm sorry," Lena said, "do I know you?"

"No." He looked at her a moment longer than was comfortable, then past her, at the boat again, and his jaw tightened in a way that had nothing to do with her. "But I know her. That's the *Kestrel*. Built in Harrow Bend, 1911, by a man named Oduya. Thirty-foot river freighter, shallow draft, twin bilge pumps — well, one working pump, now." His gaze came back to hers. "She belonged to a friend of mine. Name of Anselm Reyes. Ran cargo out of Millbrook Landing for eleven years. Never missed a payment, never ran anything he shouldn't. Then about four years ago his papers started coming up wrong at every checkpoint between here and the delta. Fines. Seizures. A cargo impounded that turned up later in a warehouse owned by a man whose name I won't say on open water."

Lena felt the cold start in her stomach. "Cass Merrow."

The man's expression didn't change, but something behind his eyes did — a door opening a crack. "You know him."

"He hired us. Three days ago. Triple rate, monsoon run, medical supplies." She heard her own voice go flat and hard. "Then his men took the cargo at Blackwater Bend, holed our boat, and left us for the flood."

For a long moment the stranger said nothing. Mist drifted between them. Somewhere behind her, Dez's hammer rang against the hull, steady as a heartbeat.

"And what was in the crates," the man said quietly, "that a man like Merrow would burn three boats' worth of goodwill to take back?"

"Ledgers. Records. Evidence against his smuggling operation." Lena held his gaze. "My brother died collecting it. Six years ago. The courts never touched Merrow — not for that, not for Anselm Reyes, not for anyone. So yes. I know him."

The stranger studied her the way he might study water before crossing it — reading the current underneath. Then he turned and whistled, two notes, low.

Out of the trees came a dog. Big, brindled, gray-muzzled, moving with the unhurried confidence of an animal that had never once been afraid. It trotted to the man's side and sat, and regarded Lena with the same flat green assessment as its owner.

"Breakfast," the man said. "You and your crew. There's fish and there's rice and there's coffee that tastes like tar, and then we should talk, because you're not getting that boat off this bar without timber and pitch, and you're not getting timber and pitch without me."

"Why would you help us?"

"Because Merrow ruined my friend," he said simply. "Anselm drank himself into the grave two years after they took his boat. And because—" he paused, and for the first time something raw moved across his face and was gone, "—because the man who signed the falsified charges that put my partner in prison works for Merrow too. So." He shrugged, a small economical motion. "You have a reason. I have a reason. The river has room for both."

Lena didn't move. Every instinct she had — six years of instincts, six years of keeping her crew moving, of never trusting help that arrived unasked — was telling her to say thank you, we'll manage, and get back to the hull.

But they couldn't manage. They needed timber. They needed someone who knew these channels. And the alternative to accepting help was drifting, leaking, into a country that wanted them dead.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Ilya."

"Lena Thornbury." She hesitated, then stuck out her hand. His grip was dry and strong, calloused across the palm in the pattern of a tiller man. "Ilya — I don't trust easy. My crew doesn't either. We've been burned by helpful strangers before."

"Good," said Ilya. "Trust is for people with time. You don't have any. Come eat."

* * *

The camp was a lean-to of driftwood and tarpaulin tucked into the casuarinas, so neat it was nearly invisible from the water — Lena noted the skill of that, the years of practice in it. A man who lived off-grid built to be unseen. There was a fire pit lined with stones, a rack of split fish smoking, a coil of net, and a dugout canoe pulled up above the high-water line, its hull scarred but sound.

Ilya fed them without ceremony: rice and smoked fish and the promised tar-coffee, and Pip ate enough for two boys and fell asleep again almost immediately afterward, mid-sentence, sitting up, which made Sefa laugh for the first time since Blackwater Bend — a short, cracked sound, but a laugh.

Dez ate slowly, watching Ilya the whole time. When the plates were cleared she said, "You knew the *Kestrel*'s build year. You knew Oduya built her. You knew she had twin pumps." She set down her cup. "That's not river gossip. That's ownership knowledge."

"It is," Ilya agreed.

"So how."

"Because I helped Anselm fit those pumps. Because I've piloted this stretch of river for twenty-two years, and for eleven of them I ran ahead of the *Kestrel* on dark nights, finding channels for her when the markers washed out." He turned his cup in his hands. "And because when Anselm died, the boat was supposed to be sold to pay his debts. Instead it disappeared. I've wondered for four years where she went." His eyes came up. "Now I know. Merrow's people have been using her as a courier ever since. Running her dirty so that if she's ever stopped, she stops looking like a smuggler's boat. Clever. Cruel. Very much his style."

Sefa had gone very still. "So when his men rammed us—"

"They put bullets and a boarding party into a boat they own," Ilya said. "Which tells you how little they feared questions. Out here, there are none. The magistrate's station at Port Lachlan hasn't sent a patrol upriver in three years. Everyone knows why." He looked at Lena. "Your brother learned that lesson too, I think."

"Yes," Lena said. "He did."

The fire popped. Down on the bar, the *Kestrel* leaned on her wounded side, and the river slid past her, indifferent, carrying on toward the sea with everything it had taken.

"Here is what I can give you," Ilya said. "Timber — there's good casuarina deadfall back in the trees, and I have tools. Pitch. Two days' work and your seams will hold for calm water, maybe even for weather if Dez knows what she's doing." Dez made a sound of offended pride. "And I can give you the channels. The flooded country east of here is a maze — drowned forest, old logging cuts, sandbars that move every season. Merrow's men will run the main channel because their boat draws too much to go anywhere else. You, in the *Kestrel*, drawing what she draws, can go where they can't follow. If you're trying to reach Port Lachlan—"

"We are," Lena said. "There's a regional magistrate there. Corrupt or not, it's the only law on this river."

"He's corrupt," Ilya said flatly. "Magistrate Enfield takes Merrow's money same as everyone. But—" and here he paused, and Lena saw him choose his words with care, "—there are records offices in Port Lachlan. There are clerks. There is a system, rotten as it is, and rotten systems sometimes have one honest beam left holding up the roof. And there are people. River towns remember, Miss Thornbury. They remember longer than courts do."

Lena looked at him for a long time. "What's the price?"

"I told you. I come along."

"That's not a price, that's a passenger."

"It's a price," Ilya said, "because you don't want me. I can see it in your face. You want to say yes to the timber and no to the man, and you can't, because the timber comes with the man." He smiled faintly, without humor. "So. The price is that you carry a stranger into your business, a stranger with his own reasons, his own grief, and his own ideas about what happens to Cass Merrow at the end of this river. Maybe my ideas match yours. Maybe they don't. That's the risk you take."

Silence. Dez and Sefa exchanged a glance over the fire — the crew's old language, the one that said *your call, Captain*.

Lena thought about Tomas. Thought about the oath over his grave — *we keep moving, we keep clear, we owe the dead nothing but distance* — and how completely, how catastrophically, they had already broken it. They were past the country of careful rules. The only question left was who stood beside them in the new one.

"If you come with us," she said slowly, "you follow my orders on my boat. No freelance justice. Whatever happens to Merrow happens in daylight, in front of witnesses, in a way that sticks. I didn't lose my brother to watch some river pilot put a bullet in the man and call it done. It has to *count*, Ilya. It has to be the kind of finished that can't be undone by lawyers."

Something shifted in Ilya's face — a loosening, almost, like ice going out of a channel in spring. "Daylight," he repeated. "Witnesses. Yes. I can live with that. Better than I can live with the dark way, though I've thought about it often enough." He reached out and fed a stick to the fire. "Your brother sounds like he was a stubborn man."

"He was the worst kind of stubborn. The kind that was right."

"Then we'll be stubborn together." Ilya stood, brushing sand from his knees, and nodded toward the boat. "Come. Tide turns in an hour and we'll want the *Kestrel* light and high on the bar when it does. Timber first, talk later. And Miss Thornbury—" he paused, and the bottle-green eyes caught the firelight, "—one more thing you should know before you decide you've made a bargain."

"Go on."

"The men who boarded you at Blackwater Bend. Did any of them wear a red scarf? Silk. Tied at the throat."

Lena went cold. "Voss. Cass's foreman."

"Voss." Ilya said the name the way a man says the name of a disease. "Then listen carefully. Voss keeps a list. Everyone who's ever crossed Merrow and lived — he keeps their names, their ports, their families. Your names are on it now. Which means before you reach Port Lachlan, word will travel faster than you do." He picked up his axe from beside the lean-to and slung it over his shoulder. "So we work fast, we sail quiet, and we take the channels Merrow's maps don't show. Because the only thing more dangerous than what's chasing you—"

"—is what's waiting ahead," Lena finished.

Ilya nodded once, grimly, and handed her a second axe.

"Welcome to the flooded country," he said.
