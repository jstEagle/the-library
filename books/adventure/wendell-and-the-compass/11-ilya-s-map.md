## 11. Ilya's Map

The rain had a sound Ilya knew better than his own heartbeat. It came in two voices — the soft one that meant the river was only being patient, and the hard one that meant it had decided to take something. Tonight it spoke both at once, drumming on the tin roof of his stilt house while the water beneath climbed the pilings like something with fingers.

He sat with the lamp turned low and spread the map across the table, weighting its corners with four brass shells he'd never fired. The paper was old — older than the war, older than some of the towns it named — but the river it described hadn't changed. Rivers never changed. They just moved their furniture.

*Here,* he thought, tracing a line west of Blackwater Bend. *This is where they'll have to slow down.*

The flooded country didn't appear on any chart worth trusting. During the wet season the whole western basin drowned — forest becoming archipelago, ridgelines becoming reefs, old roads running thirty feet under brown water where catfish patrolled the steeples of submerged chapels. The government maps showed a single channel through it, wide and honest-looking. That channel was a lie. It silted shut every year by June, and anyone who trusted it found out the way you find out most things out here: too late, and all at once.

But there were other ways through. Ways that looked like nothing. A cut between two drowned hills that was actually an old logging canal. A "marsh" that was really a flooded quarry with a channel down its middle deep enough to float a barge. A stretch of dead-straight water that followed the bed of a rail line, the ties still down there, the rails still rusting, close enough to the surface to read like braille if you knew what your keel was telling you.

Ilya knew them all. He'd spent eleven years learning them, first for money and then for spite, and spite was the better teacher. Money taught you the safe way. Spite taught you every way.

He heard the boat before he saw the light — the particular laboring cough of an engine running rich on bad fuel, which meant strangers, because everyone local either had good fuel or no fuel. He blew out the lamp and stood in the dark doorway with the rain coming off the roof in a silver curtain, and watched the *Wren's Errand* feel her way up his channel with a patched hull and a boy up front swinging a lantern like he was trying to signal the moon.

They'd made it. Part of him had bet against it. The river took bets like that every night and mostly won.

He put the lamp back on and stepped out onto the deck, arms crossed, and let them see him standing there unafraid, because the first lesson of dealing with desperate people was to look like the calmest thing in sight.

* * *

They came aboard dripping and hollow-eyed, and he fed them before he talked to them, because that was also a lesson: hungry people make bad decisions, and he needed their decisions to be good ones. Rice and dried fish and strong tea, served on his one good table while the storm worked itself into a fury outside and the pilings groaned like old men.

The girl — Pip, the others called her, though he suspected it wasn't her name so much as her job description — ate like someone who'd recently been reminded that food existed. The mechanic, Dez, kept glancing toward the door whenever the wind slammed it, cataloguing sounds, already halfway to fixing things that weren't broken yet. The navigator, Sefa, said almost nothing but watched everything, and Ilya marked her as the dangerous one. Desperate people with nothing left were predictable. Desperate people who *observed* were not.

And Lena Thornbury sat across from him with her hands wrapped around the tea bowl, and looked at him the way he imagined he looked at maps: reading, measuring, deciding how much of what she saw she could trust.

"You said you knew this boat," she said finally.

"I said I recognized it." He turned his cup slowly on the table. "It belonged to Marisol Vane. She ran cargo up this stretch for nine years. Best pilot on the water, and I include myself in that assessment reluctantly."

"What happened to her?"

"Cass Merrow happened to her." The name came out of him flat and cold, a stone dropped on a table. "Three years ago she carried a shipment for him without knowing what was in it. When the law started asking questions, Merrow told them exactly where to look. Her boat. Her manifest. Her handwriting on the false papers — forged, but convincing enough. She's in Kestrel Bay prison now. Eleven years."

Sefa's eyes came up sharply. "Falsified charges."

"Forged documents, a bought magistrate, and a woman who trusted the wrong man's money." Ilya shrugged, as if the shape of it didn't sit in his chest like a swallowed anchor. "Merrow didn't just ruin her. He used her. She was the proof he needed that anyone who got close to his operation could be burned and blamed, and after her, nobody on the river would carry for him except men who were too scared or too stupid to ask questions. Which was, I suspect, the point."

Lena set down her tea. "You want to use us to get to him."

"I want to use you the way you're planning to use me," Ilya said. "Which is to say: mutually, and with our eyes open. You have evidence that can convict him. I have the only map of the flooded country that isn't a lie, and hands that know these channels in the dark, in the rain, with a holed boat. You need me. I need what you're carrying to be worth the trip."

"And if we hand it to the magistrate and the magistrate is bought like Marisol's was?"

"Then we'll know that too, and I'll still have my map and you'll still have your lives, and Merrow will have won again but slower." He leaned forward. "But I've watched Kestrel Bay's new magistrate for two years. She transferred in from the coast after the last one retired very suddenly and very wealthy. She's refused three bribes that I know of, because the men who offered them are now doing road work in chains. She's not clean because she's honest. She's clean because she's *stubborn*, and stubborn is the only kind of clean that lasts out here."

Silence, except the rain. Pip had stopped eating. Dez had stopped pretending not to listen.

"Why help us?" Lena asked. "Really. Not the justice speech. The real reason."

Ilya looked at her for a long moment. Then he got up, went to the shelf above his bunk, and brought back a photograph gone soft and gray at the edges from years of handling. He laid it on the table between them.

Two people on the deck of a boat — the same boat, before the patchwork, before the leaks. A heavyset woman with her arm slung around a younger man's shoulders, both laughing at something outside the frame. Marisol Vane and her brother Denny, who'd been her engineer, who'd taken the fall alongside her because he'd signed the same forged manifest, because family signs what family asks you to sign.

"Denny taught me engines," Dez said quietly.

"He taught half the river engines," Ilya said. "That was the kind of man he was. Marisol is the kind of woman who will come out of that prison in eight years with nothing — no boat, no name, no river — unless somebody burns Merrow's operation down to the waterline first. So yes. Justice. But justice is just the word we use for the part of grief that's still moving."

Lena looked at the photograph a long time. Something moved behind her face — he'd seen that movement before, in mirrors, in other people who carried a name like a wound. When she spoke, her voice was careful.

"My brother died chasing the same operation."

"I know," Ilya said. "Tomas Thornbury. The river towns remember him. They remember that the law didn't."

Her jaw tightened. "Then you know why I don't trust words like *justice* anymore."

"Good," Ilya said. "Trust the map instead. Maps don't lie. People do. And right now, the map says Cass Merrow's gunboat draws too much water for the eastern channels once the flood peaks tomorrow night, which means he has to take the long way around through Sallow Reach, which buys us eighteen hours. After that, we're all swimming in the same lie of a river, and the fastest liar wins."

* * *

He unrolled the map properly after that, and it became a different kind of conversation — the kind Ilya loved best, the geometry of escape, the mathematics of water.

"The main channel here—" his finger traced the broad blue line the government charts showed, "—is silted to two feet in places. Nobody's used it since the war. Merrow's pilots know that; they'll assume you'll hug the edges looking for depth, which means they'll watch the edges. So we don't take the channel at all."

"We take the quarry," Sefa said.

Ilya glanced at her, and felt the small cold pleasure of finding a mind shaped like his own. "You know it."

"Sefa ran stone barges for two seasons," Dez said. "Before."

"The quarry floods to fourteen feet at peak," Sefa went on, leaning over the map, her finger finding the drowned pit west of the ruins of Kilnmoor. "If the flood's peaked, there's a clear line down the middle where they blasted deepest. The entrance is narrow — maybe twenty feet — and there's a collapsed crane arm just under the surface on the north side. Everyone avoids it because everyone hit the crane. But if you come in tight to the south wall instead—"

"—you miss the crane and find the deep line," Ilya finished. "Exactly. From the quarry we cut northwest through the drowned forest along the old logging canal. It runs shallow in three places, but shallow *predictably* — I've sounded it every season for six years. Then the rail cut. Then we come out at the mouth of the Vessel River two miles below the magistrate's station at Harrow Landing, having traveled a route Merrow's charts say doesn't exist."

"And if he figures out we didn't take the main channel?" Lena asked.

"Then he sends men into the flooded country after us, and they slow down, and they get angry, and angry men on unfamiliar water make mistakes." Ilya smiled, and knew it wasn't a warm smile. "The flooded country punishes arrogance faster than any magistrate. Every stump under that brown water is a verdict waiting to be delivered."

Dez was frowning at the boat visible through the window, riding low on her mooring lines. "She'll make it? With the patches?"

"She'll make it if we don't push her past twelve knots in open water and if we baby her through the canal. Canvas and pitch will hold in slack water. In rapids, canvas and pitch hold about as long as a promise from Cass Merrow." He tapped the rail-cut section of the map. "There's current through here — the old riverbed funnels the flood flow. We'll need someone on the bow reading the water and someone aft on the pump, and everyone else praying in whatever language they've got."

"I don't pray," Sefa said.

"Then you'll have extra time to bail."

Pip laughed — a short, startled sound, the laugh of someone surprised to find humor still living in them — and the tension in the room loosened by a degree Ilya could measure. Good. Fear was fuel, but only in small doses. Too much and it fouled the engine of a crew the way bad fuel fouled Dez's beloved motor.

Lena studied him across the table. "You've done this before. Run people through there."

"Once." He held her gaze. "Marisol. The night before the law came for her. If I'd run her farther — out of the territory entirely, past the border stations — she might have gotten clear. Instead I put her ashore at Kilnmoor because she wanted to stand and fight for her name, and the law was waiting there, because someone sold her route." He let that sit. "So understand what you're buying when you buy my help. I'm not a lucky man. I'm a prepared one. Luck ran out on me years ago and I've been making do with preparation ever since."

"And tonight?" Pip asked softly. "Is this luck or preparation?"

Ilya looked at the girl — young, sharp, still carrying the unarmored eyes of someone who hadn't yet learned to expect betrayal from every handshake. He envied that and pitied it in the same breath.

"Tonight," he said, "it's both. The river sent you to my sandbar leaking, which is luck. That I was here, with a map, with a grudge, with dry rice and a dry roof — that's preparation. Take both and thank neither. The river doesn't like being thanked. It gets superstitious."

* * *

They slept in shifts, and Ilya took the last watch alone, as he always did, sitting in the doorway with the rain easing toward that soft, patient voice and the floodwater lapping at the third plank below his feet.

Tomorrow night the basin would peak. Tomorrow night the quarry would be full and the logging canal would be passable and the rail cut would run fast and mean. Tomorrow night, somewhere south, Cass Merrow's gunboat would be grinding up the long channel to Sallow Reach, burning fuel and patience, believing the flooded country was a wall instead of a door.

Ilya turned the brass shells over in his palm, one by one, an old habit from a war he didn't talk about. Four shells. He'd carried them since before Marisol, since before the grudge had a name. He'd never fired them. Some nights he told himself that meant something — that he was a man of maps and channels, not bullets. Other nights he wondered what he'd become if the map failed and the shells were all that was left.

He thought about Denny Vane's laugh in the photograph. He thought about Marisol's voice on the last night, ashore at Kilnmoor, saying *go, Ilya, go, one of us should stay free* — and how he'd obeyed her, and how obedience had tasted every day since.

Eighteen hours. By this time tomorrow they'd be inside the quarry cut, committed, past the point where the flooded country would let them turn around even if they wanted to. He'd seen that moment on passengers' faces a hundred times — the instant the banks narrowed and the sky shrank and every soul aboard understood, all at once, that the only way out was through.

He found he was looking forward to it.

From the back room came the sound of Lena Thornbury turning over in her sleep, restless, dreaming whatever dreams a woman dreamed who'd spent years outrunning a brother's ghost and had finally, somewhere over the last two days, stopped running and started walking toward it.

Ilya pulled the map back across the table and weighted it down again, corner by corner, shell by shell.

Outside, far to the south, thunder rolled — or maybe it was engines, or maybe it was the river clearing its throat. Eighteen hours, and then the flooded country would open its drowned green door, and everyone who walked through it would come out changed, if they came out at all.

He checked the lamp. He checked the mooring lines. He listened to the rain turn soft, and patient, and hungry.

Then he went to wake Sefa for her watch, because the tide waited for no one's grief, and the flood would peak whether they were ready or not — and somewhere out in the dark, he was almost certain, Cass Merrow was already moving.
