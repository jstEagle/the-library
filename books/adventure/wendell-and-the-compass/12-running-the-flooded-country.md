## 12. Running the Flooded Country

The rain came down like the sky had given up on being sky.

Dez stood at the engine housing in the stern of Ilya's boat and listened to it the way other people listened to music — every knock, every rattle, every note a half-step flat. The engine was a forty-year-old diesel that some mad genius had rebuilt with parts from three different manufacturers, and it was running rough because the fuel line was sucking air somewhere between the tank and the pump, and Dez could not fix it while it ran, and they could not stop while it leaked.

So she did what she always did. She made friends with the problem.

"Talk to me," she murmured, hand flat on the housing, feeling the vibration stutter through her palm. "Come on. Talk to me."

Behind her, Sefa held the wheel with both hands, eyes on the drowned forest sliding past on either side of the channel. It wasn't really a river anymore, not since the monsoon had climbed out of its banks and swallowed the lowland whole. Trees stood to their shoulders in brown water, their crowns still green, so that from a distance it looked like a forest walking. The current moved through them in slow, muscular pulses, and where the old channels crossed, the water braided into rapids that hissed over submerged fences, roads, the roofs of barns.

"You're drifting left," Pip called from the bow, where he crouched with a boat hook, fending off floating debris. A fence post spun past, then something white and bloated he chose not to identify out loud.

"I'm compensating for the current," Sefa said.

"You're drifting left."

"I know what I'm doing, Pip."

Dez tuned them out. She'd learned years ago that the sound of her crew arguing was the sound of things being normal, and normal was a resource you rationed like fuel.

The leak was the real enemy. They'd taken on water ever since Blackwater Bend — a slow, patient seep along a seam below the waterline where Cass's men had put a gaff through the hull, or maybe just age and bad luck had finally collected their debt. Either way, the bilge pump ran more than the engine idled, and every hour the seam gave up a little more ground. Ilya had stuffed it with oakum and tallow that morning, a pilot's trick, but tallow softened in warm water and the water here wasn't cold.

Dez wiped rain off her face and looked at the gauge. Fuel: two-thirds. Bilge: cycling every four minutes now instead of six. Math you didn't need Sefa for.

"How far to the cut?" she shouted forward.

Ilya stood at the bow beside Pip, one hand on the rail, reading the water the way Lena read weather charts. He hadn't sat down once in six hours. He pointed left, toward a gap in the trees where the water ran fast and green-white.

"Through there," he called back. "Half a klick of rapids, then the old logging canal. Flat water after that if the dam hasn't gone."

"And if the dam's gone?"

"Then we find out why they call it the canal."

* * *

They went through there.

There was no better way to say it later, and Dez thought about that afterward — how the worst moments of your life compress into plain words. The trees closed overhead, dripping, and the channel narrowed until the branches scraped both gunwales at once, and then the bottom dropped away and the boat began to run.

Sefa flew it. That was the only word. She'd navigated air through monsoon cells and mountain passes with instruments failing, and now she flew the boat through standing timber, reading the current's shoulders, finding the deep lanes by the color of the water alone — brown meant shallow, green meant deep, white meant pray. Dez braced against the engine housing and kept one hand on the fuel line fitting, ready to clamp it if the vibration shook it loose, and watched her friend do impossible steering with her jaw set like a woman carrying something heavy up a long stair.

A log came at them out of the murk, whole tree, root ball spinning. Pip screamed a warning and shoved off with the boat hook and it wasn't enough, and the root ball caught the port bow and slewed them sideways into the current, and for three full seconds Dez felt the hull sliding broadside toward a submerged roof — she saw the ridge line under the water, tiles furred with silt — and then Sefa did something with the wheel and the throttle together that no manual would ever recommend, and the boat bit, and swung, and threaded the gap between the roof and a deadfall with a hand's width on either side.

Nobody cheered. Nobody had breath to cheer with.

Then the trees opened, and the water flattened, and they were through.

Dez became aware of sounds returning to the world one at a time: the engine, laboring; the bilge pump, cycling; Pip laughing high and shaky at the bow; rain, constant, indifferent. She looked down and realized she'd been clamping the fuel fitting so hard her fingers had gone white, and she made herself let go, joint by joint.

"Canal," Ilya said quietly. "Dam's holding."

For about nine minutes, everything was almost fine.

* * *

The seam let go an hour later, in flat water, without drama — which somehow made it worse. One minute the bilge pump was cycling every four minutes; the next, Dez heard the pitch change and knew before she looked that they were losing.

She got the inspection hatch open and shone the work lamp down into the bilge and watched brown water come through the hull in a steady, muscular pulse, in time with the current outside, as though the river had found a heartbeat in their boat and was using it.

"Sefa! Hard aground, anywhere, now!"

"We're in the middle of a flooded—"

"NOW!"

Sefa didn't argue. That was the thing about Sefa; she argued about drift and headings and whose turn it was to cook, but when Dez used that voice she turned the wheel like a woman obeying a fire alarm. The boat nosed toward a stand of dead trees rising from a shallow shelf, and Ilya guided them in with small hand signals, and Pip got lines around two trunks before the current could change its mind.

They grounded with a grinding shudder, settled, tilted, and held.

Dez already had the tool bag open. "Pip — pump. Keep it ahead. Sefa, I need the canvas roll and the pot of pitch from under the bench. Ilya—" She looked up and found him already kneeling beside her at the hatch, sleeves rolled, and something in his face told her he'd done this before, more than once, in worse places.

"Tell me what you need," he said.

"A miracle and forty minutes." She stripped to the waist in the rain, because dry clothes were a luxury for people whose boats didn't leak, and looked at the hole. It was worse than she'd hoped. The seam had opened along two feet of planking, the caulking rotted to black string, and the wood around it was soft as bread. Patching it from inside was a bandage on a wound that needed stitches. But stitches required a haul-out and a yard and three days, and they had none of those things.

Outside, then. Underwater, then.

"I'm going over the side," she said.

"In this current?" Sefa's voice cracked upward.

"I'll tie off. The trees are holding us. Pip, give me the long line and the spare canvas — the heavy stuff, not the deck awning. And the pitch pot, hot as you can get it."

They built the rig in five minutes: a line around her waist, anchored to a trunk; a second line for tools; the canvas cut into a patch big enough to lap six inches past the seam on every side. Ilya heated the pitch over the little stove in the cabin, sheltering the flame with his body, and when Dez slipped over the gunwale into the brown water the warmth of it hit her like a slap — warmer than the air, warmer than blood almost, monsoon-warm, river-warm, alive.

She went down the hull hand over hand, following the anchor line, and found the seam by feel. Her world contracted to the size of her arms. Current pulled at her legs. Debris bumped past in the dark. Somewhere above, muffled by water, she could hear Pip counting the pump cycles out loud, keeping time like a drummer, keeping them all honest.

First pass: clean the seam. She dug the rotten caulking out with a hooked pick, working blind, fingers reading the wood. Second pass: stuff it. Oakum, driven in with a mallet she'd lashed to her wrist — she'd learned that lesson the hard way once, watching a good wrench vanish into the Mekong like it had been recalled. Third pass: the patch. She pressed the canvas against the hull and held it there with one forearm while her free hand hauled the pitch pot down on its own line, and Ilya's voice came faintly through the hull, talking her through the pour, telling her when the pitch was at temperature, telling her to breathe.

She breathed. She poured. The pitch spread across the canvas in a black bloom, sealing cloth to wood, and the water coming through the seam slowed, and slowed, and stopped.

Dez hung there in the warm brown dark, cheek against the hull, and laughed — a short, ugly, relieved laugh that nobody above could hear.

* * *

It took three hours and two more patches, because the first patch held but the second seam — the one she'd flagged as suspect back at the landing, the one they hadn't had time for — decided to introduce itself. By the time she climbed back aboard, shaking, lips blue despite the warm water, night was coming down and the rain had found a second gear.

They ate cold rice and canned fish in the cabin, all four of them plus Ilya packed shoulder to shoulder under the one dry corner, the stove going, the bilge pump cycling every ten minutes now, which Dez had decided to think of as a victory.

"Patches will hold," she said, mouth full. "Two days. Maybe three if we're gentle."

"We are never gentle," Pip said.

"Then two days."

Sefa was studying the chart by lamplight, tracing the route Ilya had drawn in pencil — backchannels, cutoffs, a smuggler's path through country the maps had mostly given up on. "The magistrate's station is two days' run from here," she said. "If the patches hold."

"If," said Pip.

"Everything is if," Sefa said. "If the storm holds off. If Cass's gunboat doesn't pick up our trail. If the dam holds. We've been living on if since Blackwater Bend. It's a perfectly good diet."

Dez looked around the cabin — Sefa bent over the chart, Pip curled against the bulkhead already half asleep, Ilya sitting very still with his hands around a tin cup, watching the rain with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. And she thought about Tomas, the way everyone on this boat thought about Tomas at least once a day whether they admitted it or not. He'd have loved this, she thought. Not the danger exactly. The competence. The four of them doing the thing nobody else could do, in the place nobody else would go.

Her chest did something complicated. She put it away.

"Get some sleep," she said aloud. "All of you. I've got first watch on the pump."

"You patched a boat underwater for three hours," Sefa said. "You sleep."

"I'll sleep when the boat does."

"That's never, then," Pip mumbled, already gone.

Ilya rose without a word and took the watch stool, and when Dez started to object he shook his head once, and she read it correctly: this was the part he knew how to do. Watching water. Waiting. She lay down on the bench next to Sefa, boots on, jacket for a pillow, and stared at the ceiling beams and listened to the rain and the pump and the river breathing against the hull, and for a while she let herself hope — carefully, the way you carry a full cup through a moving boat.

Fragile. But full.

* * *

She woke to silence, which on a boat is the loudest alarm there is.

No engine. No rain on the cabin roof. Just the pump, cycling, and Ilya's voice, low, from the bow.

Dez was up and out before her head finished clearing. The clouds had torn open in the east and the first grey light lay across the flooded forest, and Ilya stood at the rail looking back the way they'd come, down the long canal, toward a smudge on the horizon that was not cloud.

Smoke? No. Wake. A long white wake, drawn straight across the flat water behind a boat moving fast, and even at this distance Dez could see the silhouette — low, armored, a gun mount forward.

Cass's gunboat. Hours behind them, maybe less. Running the same canal, because there was only one canal.

"Everyone up," Dez said, and her voice came out calm and flat and much older than she felt. "Sefa — engine. Now. I don't care if it's smooth, I care if it's loud. Pip — get every scrap of canvas and pitch we've got left onto that aft seam, because we are about to ask this boat for everything it has."

She looked back at the wake, closing slow and certain, and felt the fragile cup of hope tilt in her hands.

"They've found our trail."
