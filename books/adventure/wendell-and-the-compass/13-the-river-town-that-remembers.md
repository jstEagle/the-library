## 13. The River Town That Remembers

The town announced itself before we saw it — a smell of woodsmoke and wet rot riding the current, then the sound: hammers on tin, a dog losing its mind at nothing, a generator coughing somewhere behind the tree line like an old man clearing his throat. Sefa stood at the wheel with the rain sheeting off her shoulders and watched the river bend open into a wide brown reach where the flooded country finally let go of its grip.

Karu Landing. She'd marked it on charts ten years ago and never once stopped there. Nobody did, not if they could help it. It sat halfway between nowhere and worse, a scatter of stilt houses leaning over water the color of tea left too long, a fuel dock that was more memory than structure, and a single street of mud so deep it swallowed boots to the ankle and occasionally the whole leg.

But the boat needed diesel, and Dez needed bandages, and Pip needed food that hadn't come out of a can, and they needed all of it without being seen, which in Karu Landing meant being seen by exactly the right people and no one else.

"Slow approach," Sefa said. "Idle in past the fish traps. Don't run up to the dock like we own the place."

Dez, propped against the engine housing with his arm strapped tight across his chest and his face the color of old candle wax, managed a crooked grin. "You mean don't announce ourselves with the throttle like certain people I could name."

"I mean," Sefa said, "that towns like this remember who came in loud. And right now we are four strangers with a bullet-holed boat and no story worth believing."

Pip appeared at her elbow, dripping, eyes bright with the particular hunger of someone who'd been eating crackers for three days. "Is it true there's a woman here who sells fried plantain?"

"It's true there's a town," Sefa said. "Everything else is negotiable."

They came in slow, past the fish traps — bamboo weirs standing in the shallows like the ribs of something enormous — and tied off at the downstream end of the dock, away from the one other boat moored there, a flat-bottomed barge flying no colors at all, which in Sefa's experience meant it flew every color depending on who was asking.

Ilya went first. He always went first now; it had become the shape of things without anyone deciding it. He stepped onto the planks, stood a moment in the rain with his hood back and his face bare, and let himself be seen. That was the trick of it, Sefa realized. In a town that feared Cass Merrow, the only safe stranger was one the town already knew.

An old man mending a net under the eave of the fuel shed looked up. Looked longer. Then he said something in the river dialect, low, and spat over the edge of the dock, and whatever he said traveled down the street faster than the rain ran off the roofs.

By the time Sefa got Pip and the jerry cans onto the dock, half the town had arranged itself into the appearance of not watching them. A woman beat a mat that didn't need beating. Two boys stopped throwing stones at a heron and simply stared. A door closed softly somewhere up the street, which was its own kind of message.

"They know the boat," Ilya said quietly. "It belonged to Renn Oduya. My partner's brother built it. Half this river has seen it go by."

"And they know what happened to Renn," Sefa said.

Ilya's jaw tightened. "They know what happens to people who cross Cass."

* * *

The fuel pump worked the way everything in Karu Landing worked — reluctantly, with help. The old net-mender turned out to be the pump operator, the mechanic, and the harbormaster, all in one stooped body, and he took Sefa's coin without counting it and filled the tanks without being asked twice, all while conducting a conversation that consisted mostly of silences with meaning packed into them.

Sefa had grown up around silence. Her mother's people measured words like money, spent them carefully, saved the rest. But this was a different silence. This was the silence of a town holding its breath.

"You're running through the flood country," the old man said at last, in trade speech accented thick as river mud. "In monsoon season. With that hull."

"We're aware of the hull," Sefa said.

He snorted. It might have been laughter. "Cass Merrow's men came through two weeks ago. Bought fuel. Took more than they paid for." His eyes flicked toward the barge at the far end of the dock, then away. "Took a girl, too. Ferryman's daughter. Sixteen. Said she'd seen something on the water she shouldn't have. Her father went to the magistrate at Fort Calloway. The magistrate sent him home with a warning about making false reports."

Sefa kept her face still. Inside, something cold turned over. *Two weeks ago.* The timeline fit. This was the wake they were traveling in — the evidence from Blackwater Bend, the ledgers, the girl who'd maybe seen the handoff and been disappeared for it.

"How many men?" she asked.

"Six. Guns open-carried, which nobody does unless they've been told they can." The old man finished filling the tank and capped it with exaggerated care. "They asked about you, you know. Or about a boat heading downriver fast with holes in it. The ferryman told them he hadn't seen anything. Brave or foolish, hard to say which. Maybe both. Most days they're the same thing out here."

"Where did they go?"

"Upstream. Toward the chapel reach." He handed back the cap wrench. "You didn't hear that from me."

"I haven't heard anything from you," Sefa said. "I've heard weather."

He almost smiled. Then the smile died and he leaned close, close enough that she could smell the pipe smoke soaked into his coat, and said one more thing, very quietly: "There's a woman on the hill road. Mama Edele. Blue shutters. If your business is what I think it is — and I think it is, because grief travels faster than boats — you should see her before dark."

"Why?"

"Because she's been waiting," he said. "Three years, she's been waiting. And waiting people, when the thing they wait for finally comes, they don't always check whether it's the thing they wanted."

* * *

They bought what they could carry without looking like they were provisioning an expedition: rice, dried fish, plantains hot enough to burn fingers through paper, a bottle of fever tonic that Pip pronounced disgusting and drank anyway. Dez sat on the dock steps under an oilcloth with his arm in a sling, supervising nothing, and the children of the town drifted toward him the way children drift toward anything broken and interesting. Within twenty minutes he had an audience of five and was teaching them a card game involving cheating, which he insisted was the entire point of the game.

"He's going to be insufferable when that arm heals," Sefa muttered.

"He's insufferable now," Pip said, mouth full of plantain. "Imagine him with a fan base."

Sefa laughed despite herself, and the laugh felt strange in her chest, rusty, like a hinge in the rain. She'd forgotten what laughing cost. For nine days — since Blackwater Bend, since the gunboat, since Dez went down with a bullet through the meat of his shoulder — she had been running on the flat, gray discipline of survival, and survival didn't have room for laughing. But here, in a town that fed strangers and lied to gunmen for them, something in her chest unclenched a quarter turn.

That was the thing about places the law forgot, she thought. They didn't forget each other.

She found Mama Edele's house by the blue shutters, which were less blue than they had once been, faded to the color of a sky remembered rather than seen. The house stood on the hill road above the flood line, built on posts driven deep, with a veranda that commanded the whole bend of the river. From that veranda, Sefa understood, a person could watch everything come and nothing leave unnoticed.

Mama Edele was small and round and iron-backed, wrapped in cloth the color of dried chilies, and she was sitting on her veranda shelling beans into a bowl as though she had all the time the river had ever wasted. She looked up at Sefa and Pip standing at the bottom of her steps, and her hands did not stop moving.

"You're the ones in Renn Oduya's boat," she said. Not a question.

"Yes," Sefa said.

"You're the ones Cass Merrow shot at." Also not a question.

"Yes."

Her hands paused. Just for a breath. Then resumed. "And you're the ones carrying Tomas Thornbury's work."

Sefa felt the ground tilt under her, though the steps were solid and dry. "How —"

"Mama Edele," Pip started, but the old woman raised one bean-laden hand and Pip stopped as if a switch had been thrown.

"Sit down," Mama Edele said. "Both of you. Beans don't shell themselves and neither do stories."

* * *

They sat. Rain dripped off the veranda roof in a beaded curtain between them and the river. Mama Edele shelled beans and talked, and the talking had the unhurried cadence of someone who had decided long ago exactly how much truth to spend and on whom.

"I knew Tomas," she said. "Not well. Well enough. He came through here three years back, the season the water ate the east road. He was asking questions nobody asks. About cargo. About who signs for what. About a warehouse at Blackwater Bend that burns medicine and calls it spoilage." She shook her head slowly. "A young man, polite, tired-looking, with that way of listening like your answer mattered more than his question. People liked him. People trusted him. That was dangerous for them and they knew it, some of them, and they helped him anyway."

"Did he find what he was looking for?" Sefa asked.

"He found part of it. Enough to frighten the right people." Her voice didn't rise or fall. It stayed level as the floodwater. "He stayed three nights in this house. Slept in the back room. Ate at my table. On the last morning he gave me a letter and asked me to hold it until someone came asking after him — someone specific, someone who'd come looking not because they were curious but because they loved him. He said, *if they come asking because they loved me, give it to them. If they come asking because they're paid to, tell them I never stopped here.*"

Sefa's throat closed. She thought of Lena — of what Lena had been doing three years ago, flying circuits, keeping the crew moving, keeping grief at the speed of distance. Of how long it had taken any of them to stop running long enough to come looking.

"He mailed a copy," Mama Edele continued. "From the post office here, before he went on upriver. He made a show of it — loud, public, letting the clerk see him seal it and stamp it. He wanted it known that a letter existed. You don't kill a man quickly when a letter exists. You follow him first. You find out where the letter goes and who reads it." Her hands stilled entirely now, resting on the bowl. "It bought him time. It wasn't enough time. But it was time, and he used every hour of it."

"The letter you held," Sefa said. "The one for whoever loved him."

Mama Edele nodded toward the house. "Inside. Where it's been three years, in oilcloth, under the floor of the back room, beneath the box where I keep my winter blankets, which no one in this climate has ever needed, which is why I chose it."

Pip made a small sound, half awe, half wanting to cry and refusing to do it in front of a stranger.

"You waited," Sefa said. "Three years."

"I waited," Mama Edele agreed. "People told me to stop. People told me he was dead and letters to dead men are just paper. I told them paper keeps. Paper is patient. Paper doesn't rot in the flood the way promises do." She set down the bowl. "Then two weeks ago, Cass Merrow's men came through asking their questions, and I knew the waiting was nearly done — one way or another. Either his people would come, or his enemies would come, and I would know which by how they asked."

"And how did we ask?"

"You knocked," she said simply. "His enemies kick doors."

* * *

The letter was exactly where she said it would be, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, and Sefa carried it out to the veranda and held it on her knees and did not open it, because it wasn't hers. It had a name on it in a hand she recognized — the same careful, slightly cramped handwriting that covered the ledger pages Pip had pulled from the crate at Blackwater Bend. *Lena.*

"She should read it first," Sefa said. "Alone, if she wants. Or not alone, if she doesn't. That's her call."

Mama Edele studied her for a long moment, then nodded, satisfied, as though Sefa had passed some test whose questions were never spoken aloud. "You're the navigator," she said. "The one who keeps the courses. I can always tell navigators. They're the ones who ask where before they ask why."

"Usually the reverse gets you killed out here," Sefa said.

"Usually the reverse gets everyone killed everywhere," Mama Edele said. "Out here we've just had more practice noticing."

She fed them — beans and smoked fish and a porridge sweetened with wild honey, eaten on the veranda while the rain came down harder and the light went bronze and then gray. Other people came, quietly, one and two at a time. The ferryman whose daughter was taken, a broad quiet man with hands like anchor chain, who sat and said almost nothing except one sentence: "If you get to Fort Calloway, there are people who will talk. They wouldn't talk before. They'll talk if it's you." The postmaster's widow, who confirmed the mailing, who remembered the date because it was her husband's birthday. A boy of maybe nineteen who had ferried Tomas upriver to the chapel reach and had never told anyone, and who drew the channels in spilled honey on the tabletop with one steady finger while the adults pretended not to memorize it.

Nobody asked for anything. Nobody wanted payment. What they wanted, Sefa realized, watching the honey-channels gleam and fade, was what every forgotten community wants: to matter to somebody with reach. To have what they knew count. Three years of knowing and swallowing it, and now four strangers in a shot-up boat had walked into town and the knowledge had somewhere to go.

When the light failed, Mama Edele pressed the letter into Sefa's hands and closed Sefa's fingers over it with both of her own, small and strong as trap-springs.

"One more thing," she said. "Tomas told me something that last morning. I've kept it like I kept the letter. He said —" and here her voice changed, softened, became for a moment younger, as though repeating it required her to borrow his — "*Tell my sister the compass isn't broken. She just hasn't stopped moving long enough to read it.*"

Sefa sat very still. Out on the black river, lightning walked along the far hills, silent, counting something.

"She'll understand," Mama Edele said. "Or she won't yet, and she will later. Both are fine. That's how the dead talk to us — on delay."

* * *

Back at the dock, the others were loading the last of the stores by lamplight, rain hammering the tarpaulins. Dez was arguing with Ilya about whether the patched seam would hold above twelve knots, which meant Dez was feeling better, which meant the fever tonic was working or the audience of children had restored his will to live. Pip ran ahead to announce them, and Sefa saw Lena look up from the cargo manifest she was studying by lantern, saw her register Pip's expression — that specific brightness Pip got when carrying something fragile and enormous — and start toward the gangplank before Pip reached it.

Sefa climbed aboard. Rain ran off her hood. She held out the oilcloth bundle, water-spotted, three years patient.

"This is yours," she said. "From Karu Landing. From a woman named Edele, who kept it the way you keep a course — through everything." She hesitated, then gave Lena the rest of it straight, because Lena deserved straight. "Tomas wrote it. Before he died. He mailed a copy to buy himself time, and he left this one hidden with instructions — only for the person who came looking because they loved him."

Lena took it the way you take something that might break, or might detonate, or might be both. She didn't open it on the deck. She carried it aft, into the cabin, and lit the lamp, and sat down at the little table with the chart drawers, and Sefa stood in the doorway and did not follow, because some doors you guard instead of entering.

Through the rain-streaked window Sefa watched her sister-in-crew — watched Lena's thumb trace the handwriting on the envelope, once, twice, like reading braille. Watched her break the seal. Watched her read the first lines and go absolutely still, so still the lamp flame seemed to move more than she did.

Then her shoulders began to shake — not sobbing, something quieter and worse, the shaking of a person receiving a transmission three years late and finding it addressed directly to the wound. Sefa moved inside and sat beside her, not touching, just present, the way you stand watch.

Lena read to the end. Then she read it again. Then she looked up, eyes wet and burning, and said the words that turned the whole night on its axis:

"Sefa. He says he hid the originals. The real ledgers — the ones that can convict Cass, not copies, *originals*, signed, dated, sealed. He hid them where the law would never think to look." Her hand flattened on the letter. "And he drew a map."

Outside, thunder rolled up the valley like a door closing behind them, and somewhere upstream, in the drowned dark toward the chapel reach, six armed men were waiting for a boat that was no longer coming — and had no idea what was already in its hold.
