## 14. Tomas's Last Letter

The letter was four pages, written in the cramped, slanting hand Lena had spent six years trying not to remember. The paper was soft with damp, the ink feathered where the river town's humidity had gotten into it, but every word was legible — Tomas had always pressed hard when he wrote, as if he didn't trust the words to stay on the page unless he forced them there.

She read it aloud because she couldn't not. Because Sefa and Pip and Dez were crowded around the oil lamp in the old woman's back room, and because a letter that had waited this long to be read deserved more than one pair of eyes.

*"Lena,"* it began. *"If you're reading this, then either I'm dead or I'm gone somewhere you couldn't follow, and knowing you, you've already decided it's your fault. Don't. I mean that. Put the guilt down before you pick up anything else I've left you."*

Her voice caught on *put the guilt down*. She kept reading.

*"You were right about Merrow. You told me for years — don't take his contracts, don't fly his cargo, don't stand close enough to him to smell the money. And I did it anyway, because the pay was good and because I thought I could see the whole board from where I sat. I couldn't. Nobody can. That's how he stays untouchable."*

Sefa shifted against the wall. Pip had stopped fidgeting entirely, which for Pip was its own kind of prayer.

*"Here's what I found, so listen carefully, because I'm only going to say it once and I may not have time to say it twice."*

Lena's hands had begun to shake. She steadied them against her knee and went on.

* * *

The letter laid it out in Tomas's methodical way — he'd been the navigator between the two of them, the one who loved a clean sequence of numbers — and what it laid out was worse than anything the crew had guessed, even after everything they'd survived.

Cass Merrow hadn't been running contraband upriver. Not primarily. The medicine runs, the gun crates, the untaxed fuel — those were real, but they were the *cover*, the visible traffic that inspectors learned to expect and eventually stopped inspecting. Underneath them, folded into manifests across a dozen front companies, moved something quieter: bribes. Payments. A ledger of everyone on the take — magistrates' clerks, harbor officers, two names out of the regional court itself — recorded in Cass's own hand, initialed by his own factor, dated across nine years.

Nine years of corruption, itemized. Enough to hang half the officialdom of the interior if it ever reached honest eyes.

And that was exactly why it would never reach honest eyes. Every copy Cass kept was under his roof. Every witness who'd seen the ledgers whole was either bought or buried. Tomas had been the exception — a pilot who'd ferried sealed strongboxes often enough to grow curious, who'd copied pages in the dark of a layover while the factor slept, who'd understood too late what he was holding and smarter, just barely, than the men sent to collect him.

*"They know I have copies,"* the letter said. *"I don't think they know I have the originals."*

Lena looked up. "He doesn't say how he got the originals."

"Keep reading," Sefa said quietly.

*"The night I took them, the factor was drunk and the strongbox seal was already broken — someone inside Merrow's own operation had cracked it first and put it back wrong. I never learned who. Maybe someone with a conscience. Maybe someone setting a trap. Either way, I signed the manifest, flew the box like it was still sealed, and walked away with nine years of Merrow's sins in a document case under my chart bag."*

Dez made a low sound, almost a laugh, almost not. "That's why they killed him."

"That's why they killed him," Lena said. Her voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's.

*"I meant to take it to the magistrate at Corren's Landing months ago. I meant to be brave in daylight, the way you always were. But every road to an honest official runs through country Merrow owns, and I kept telling myself next run, next run."*

She had to stop there. The lamp hissed. Rain worked at the shutters. In the next room the old woman — Maren, she'd said her name was, Maren Oduya, who had known Tomas for eleven years and fed him more meals than his own sister had — moved quietly around her kitchen, pretending not to listen, giving them the room the way you give a family room at a graveside.

Pip touched her elbow. Just once. Lena breathed, and read on.

* * *

*"So here is what I've done instead, and here is where you come in — though God knows I never wanted you anywhere near this."*

Tomas had hidden the originals. Not in a bank vault, not with a lawyer, not anywhere a man with money could buy access. He'd hidden them the way river people hide things: in plain sight, in water, in a place so obvious it became invisible.

The drowned chapel.

Everyone on the upper reach knew the chapel — the old mission church that had slid into the flood channel thirty years back when the river changed course, its bell tower still breaking the surface in dry season like a black tooth. Boats tied to it. Kids dove off it. Fishermen sheltered under its collapsed nave when storms caught them mid-channel. It was a landmark, a joke, a hazard on the charts. Nobody guarded a landmark.

*"Under the third step of the bell tower stair,"* the letter read, *"there's a cavity the masons left when they rebuilt after the slide. I've sealed the originals in waxed canvas and tarred linen, weighted, wedged in behind the stone. Dry as a sermon. I checked it twice this season."*

"And we flew past it," Dez said hoarsely. "On the way upriver. We flew right over the bell tower."

"I know," Lena said.

*"I'm writing this down because I might not get the chance to tell you. If I make it to Corren's Landing myself, none of this matters — burn the letter, forget the chapel, let me do it the loud way. But if I don't, then you need to understand what you're holding, because it isn't just paper. It's the only thing in the world Merrow can't buy back, can't burn, and can't afford to let exist. He will spend everything he has to destroy it. Everything. Understand that before you decide to go after it. Understand that he'll spend it on you, too."*

Lena set the letter down on the table, very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

For a long moment nobody spoke.

Then Pip, who was seventeen and had grown up on boats and bad decisions and other people's wars, said what all of them were thinking:

"He knew. He knew they'd come for him, and he wrote it down anyway, and mailed it, and got on the plane anyway."

"He wasn't going to run," Lena said. Her throat was tight. "That was the whole point of him. He wasn't going to be another person who saw it and looked away. Not even at the end."

* * *

Maren came in with tea nobody had asked for and stood over them while they drank it, and it was Maren who filled in the rest, in the flat practical voice of a woman who had watched the river take things for sixty years and learned to narrate loss without drowning in it.

"He came through in the spring," she said. "Thinner than when you'd last seen him, I'd guess. Nervous. Kept looking at the road. But he sat where you're sitting and he ate two bowls of my fish stew and he asked me — this is the part you'll want — he asked me whether the chapel stairs were still sound. Whether anyone had been doing work on the tower. I told him no one touches that ruin; the mission sold the land and the diocese won't spend a coin on it. He nodded like that was the answer he'd hoped for." She refilled Lena's cup unasked. "Three weeks later the constable came asking questions about him. I told the constable nothing, because the constable drinks at Merrow's table. Half this town does. The other half is afraid to."

"And now?" Sefa asked. "Which half is bigger tonight?"

Maren smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "Tonight? Tonight a boat full of strangers came up my stairs carrying Merrow's stolen evidence and a gun wound in their mechanic, and I gave them my dead friend's sister's tea. Word of that will be all over the landing by morning. And every soul who hears it will have to choose which half they belong to." She set the pot down. "I chose mine eleven years ago, girl. When your brother first sat in that chair."

Lena looked at the chair. Looked at the letter. Something in her chest that had been a closed fist for six years — grief clenched so hard around itself it had become indistinguishable from habit — began, very slowly, to open into something else.

Not peace. Nothing like peace.

Purpose. Which hurt more, and was infinitely more useful.

* * *

They planned at the table until the lamp burned low, and then Dez lit the backup lantern and they planned past that, maps spread over the letter, over the crate-lid where Pip had copied out the key passages in pencil.

The geography was brutal and simple. Corren's Landing — the magistrate's station, the only court in five hundred miles that Cass hadn't yet bought, or hadn't confirmed buying, which amounted to the same gamble — lay two days downstream by river in good weather. This was not good weather. The monsoon's leading edge had come through that evening and the worst of it was still stacked up over the highlands, meaning two days could become four, could become a week, could become never.

And between here and there lay the chapel reach.

"Cass's men are already watching the water between us and the chapel," Sefa said, tapping the chart. "Six of them at least, maybe more by now. They lost our trail at Blackwater Bend, but they'll sweep downstream. It's the only direction that makes sense — they'll assume we're running for the magistrate too."

"So we can't outrun them," Dez said. His shoulder was bound and strapped and his color was bad, but his mind ran as clean as ever. "We can't outrun a gunboat in a patched hull. We can't outrun them in the plane either, not in this weather, not loaded."

"No," Lena said. "But we don't have to outrun them. We have to arrive."

She spread the chart flat and put her finger on the drowned chapel, that little printed cross and the notation *OBSTRUCTION, BELL TOWER SUBMERGED IN FLOOD* that some cartographer decades ago had inked without ever imagining what would sleep beneath it.

"They're hunting a boat carrying evidence. Fine. Let them hunt it. We go down at night, darkened, on the flooded backchannel Ilya described — slow, quiet, no lights. We stop at the chapel. Ten minutes, maybe fifteen, to work the third step loose and get the case out. Then we run for Corren's Landing straight down the main channel, in the open, fast as she'll go, and we dare them to stop us."

"Dare them," Dez repeated. "With what?"

"With witnesses." Lena looked around the table. "That's the thing Tomas understood and I've been too scared to learn for six years. Cass's power isn't guns. Guns are just what his power buys. His power is that everybody along this river believes nobody can touch him — that testifying is suicide, that the law is his, that keeping your head down is the same as staying safe. The moment we walk into that magistrate's station with originals in hand, every person he's ever leaned on gets to find out the fear was optional. He can't shoot a courtroom. He can't bribe nine years of his own handwriting."

"It's a hell of a theory," Sefa said. "It requires us to survive the next three days."

"Everything worth doing does."

Pip had been quiet a long time, chewing the end of the pencil. Now she looked up. "Lena. The letter says he'll spend everything. What if — what if getting the originals out of the chapel is exactly what he wants? What if he knows about the hiding place somehow, and the men on the water aren't hunting us at all. They're waiting at the chapel."

The room went very still.

Lena considered it honestly, because Pip had earned honesty. "Then we're dead," she said. "Or we're careful enough that we're not. Ilya knows that channel better than the men on those gunboats know their own bunks. We go in silent, we send no one ahead, and if there's a watch posted on the tower, we see them before they see us and we come back and think again. But Pip — Tomas hid those papers three years ago and they've sat there untouched ever since. If Cass knew where they were, they'd have been ash within the week. He doesn't know. He's guessing, same as we are. The difference is he thinks he's hunting a courier, and we know we're retrieving a dead man's work." She flattened her palm on the letter one more time. "That's worth more than any gun."

* * *

Near dawn the rain thinned to a gray whisper and Lena went out alone onto Maren's porch, the letter folded into her jacket, over her heart, which was a sentimental gesture and she knew it and did it anyway.

The river ran high and brown and endless below the town, carrying the monsoon's leavings — branches, blossoms, a broken canoe, the whole drowned country sliding seaward. Somewhere down that water lay a bell tower with its third step waiting, and a brother's voice in waxed canvas, patient as stone.

"You always said I was the brave one," she said quietly, to no one, to him. "You were wrong. I was just faster."

A gull cried somewhere upstream. The current pulled everything it touched toward the sea, indifferent and enormous, and Lena Thornbury stood on the porch of a woman who remembered her brother and felt the last of the running go out of her legs.

They would leave at first light. Down the flooded backchannel, darkened, silent, into the reach where six armed men were sweeping the water for them.

And halfway down that reach, rising black out of the flood, the bell tower was already waiting — and so, though none of them knew it yet, was the gunboat, anchored in the shadow of the mission ruins with its engine cold and its watch turned toward the main channel, while the backchannel lay open behind it like a held breath.
