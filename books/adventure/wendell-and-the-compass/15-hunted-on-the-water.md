## 15. Hunted on the Water

Pip heard the gunboat before anyone else — a sound like a wasp trapped in a tin cup, carried flat across the water by the wind that had finally stopped raining for an hour.

He was up in the bow, standing on the crate that held the last of their food, one hand on the stay, squinting into the gray-green maze of the flooded country. That was his job now, unofficially. Eyes. He was smallest and lightest and he could stand on things without the deck complaining, and after the night they'd spent bailing, after the night Pip had nearly gone into the black water and Sefa had hauled him back by the collar of his coat, nobody argued about what his job was.

"What is it?" Dez called from the engine housing, where she'd been elbow-deep since dawn, coaxing the old boat's heart to keep beating with canvas patches and prayer and a truly obscene vocabulary.

"Smoke," Pip said. Then, because it wasn't smoke: "No. Engine. High-pitched. Fast."

The boat they'd taken from the sandbar — Ilya's friend's boat, the *Maren's Gift*, Ilya said her name was, said it the way you'd say a dead person's name — had a sound of her own. A deep, laboring chug, waterlogged and honest. This new sound was nothing like that. This new sound was lean and hungry and getting closer.

Ilya came up from the stern where he'd been reading the water the way Sefa read charts, and Pip saw him go very still, head cocked, like a heron deciding whether the fish was worth the strike.

"Gunboat," Ilya said. "Cass's. The *Verity*." He almost smiled when he said the name. "He always did like his jokes."

"That's a joke?" Pip asked.

"His boats are all named for things he isn't." Ilya turned, and the humor was gone from his face, and what was left under it made Pip's stomach drop. "There's a channel splits ahead. Left goes wide and slow. Right goes narrow, fast, and through drowned forest. He'll expect us to take the wide one, because the narrow one will kill anyone who doesn't know it."

"You know it," Pip said.

"I grew up in it." Ilya was already moving to the wheel. "Tell your people to hold on to something. And tell the big one — Dez — I'll need everything the engine has, and I'll need her to not ask me questions for the next twenty minutes."

* * *

Pip scrambled aft, feet slipping on wet planking, and relayed it. Dez wiped her hands on a rag that had started life white and was now the color of the river, and looked at Ilya at the wheel with an expression Pip had learned to read as *I have opinions and I am choosing to die before I share them.*

"Everything she has," Dez said, "is about sixty percent of what an engine should be. But it's hers." She dropped the hatch and disappeared into the engine space, and a moment later the *Maren's Gift* surged forward with a shudder and a cough and then, grudgingly, a kind of roar.

Sefa was at the chart table, though the charts were nearly useless in the flooded country — the water had swallowed the banks weeks ago, and the landmarks were all underwater or wearing new shapes. She was navigating by Ilya's pointing finger and her own memory of the map he'd drawn on the back of a supply manifest in charcoal, and her knuckles were white on the table's edge.

"Pip." She didn't look up. "Go below. Now."

"I'm the lookout—"

"You nearly went in the water last night. If we take a hard turn, you're the lightest thing on this boat and the water will take you and I will *not* be able to—" She stopped. Breathed. Sefa never stopped in the middle of a sentence; that was how Pip knew how scared she was. "Below, Pip. That's not a request."

Lena came out of the cabin then, and Pip saw her look at Sefa, at Ilya, at the gray line of forest ahead, and Pip saw her do the arithmetic she was always doing — the arithmetic of what a thing would cost. She'd been doing it since Tomas died, Pip figured. Since before. Maybe it was the only sum she knew.

"Let him stay," Lena said quietly. "Strapped in. He's got the best eyes, and I want to see what's behind us." She looked at Pip, and there was something in her face that wasn't in it usually — something that might have been apology. "Strapped in, Pip. Sefa's rope, Sefa's knots."

So Pip got lashed to the forward stay with a length of line Sefa tied in about four seconds flat, standing on his crate with the wind and the spray hitting him in the face, and honestly? It was the safest he'd felt in days. Tied to the boat. Part of the boat. If the boat lived, he lived.

The channel forked ahead. Left: wide, brown, calm as bathwater, the drowned fields stretching away on either side like a flooded sky. Right: a slot between two walls of half-submerged forest, black water threaded with the pale ghosts of drowned trees, branches hanging low enough to comb your hair.

Ilya took them right.

* * *

The first ten minutes Pip would remember all his life, though he couldn't have said afterward what happened in them, only the sensation: the world narrowing to a corridor of green and black, the engine screaming behind him, branches whipping past close enough to take his head off, Dez's voice rising from below deck in a continuous stream of encouragement that Pip was fairly sure was not encouragement. Ilya steered like a man who had grown up inside this river, who had it memorized the way you memorize your own house in the dark — every snag, every sunken fence line, every place the current made a decision for you.

"Hard left!" Ilya shouted, and the boat heeled, and Pip's stomach went with it, and a dead tree slid past so close he could have touched it, could have counted the rings.

And then, behind them, through the trees, the wasp-in-a-cup sound again. Closer. The *Verity* hadn't taken the wide channel after all — or she'd cut across some open water Pip couldn't see, some shortcut on Cass Merrow's charts, because of course Cass Merrow had charts. Cass Merrow had everything. Cass Merrow had taken the ledgers, the evidence, Tomas's unfinished work, and left them on a leaking boat to die, and now he was going to finish the job.

"They're in the channel behind us," Pip shouted. "Maybe two minutes. Maybe less."

"Two minutes of this channel puts us at the Narrows," Ilya called back. "After the Narrows, open water to the cache. If they catch us before the Narrows, they catch us."

"And if they catch us *at* the Narrows?" Dez's voice, from below.

"Then it gets interesting," Ilya said, and Pip heard — actually heard — Dez laugh, one short bark, and thought: *that's what we are now. People who laugh at that.*

The gunboat's sound swelled. Pip twisted against his rope and saw it, or a piece of it — a gray shape sliding through the green behind them, maybe two hundred meters back, maybe less, a bow gun mounted on the foredeck with a man standing behind it, and the sight of that man, that casual standing posture, like he was waiting for a bus, made Pip's mouth go dry.

"They've got a gun. An actual gun. On the front."

"Everyone down as low as the boat allows," Lena said, calm in the way Pip knew was her most frightened voice. "Ilya. How much farther?"

"Eighty seconds. Maybe ninety."

"Then we're going to spend those ninety seconds being shot at."

"Probably," Ilya agreed.

* * *

The first shot went wide — a geyser of river a boat-length off the port side, the crack of it arriving a half-second after the water jumped. Pip flinched so hard he nearly came off his crate, rope snapping him back, and then made himself look, made himself watch, because that was the job. Eyes.

"Left side, low," he called. "They're testing range."

The second shot was not a test. It hit the *Maren's Gift* somewhere aft with a sound like a hammer on a bell, and the whole boat shuddered, and Dez's voice rose up through the deck in words Pip's mother would have washed his mouth out for. The engine note changed — dropped, stuttered, caught again.

"We're hit!" Dez bellowed. "Above the waterline, we're hit but we're *fine*, keep her moving!"

Third shot. Fourth. Pip stopped counting and started watching the water, watching the trees, watching for the thing that mattered: the Narrows. Ilya had described them at dawn in his flat, unhurried way — *the channel pinches to less than the boat's width, current runs through it like a millrace, and there's a rock in the middle that the water has been arguing with for a hundred years. You go through left of the rock, at speed, and you don't touch the wheel.* Pip had thought he was exaggerating. Pip no longer thought that.

The forest walls began to lean in. The water speeded up — Pip could see it, could see the current bunching and whitening ahead, could hear it under the engine noise, a low rushing growl like something waking up.

"Two hundred meters!" Ilya shouted. "When I say so, everyone down and *hold*."

The gunboat fired again and this time Pip heard it hit something that wasn't the hull — heard the crack of wood, the whip-crack of a branch, and then a sound he would hear in his sleep for years afterward: Dez's voice, cut off mid-word. Not a scream. Worse than a scream. A *stop*, a silence where a voice had been.

"*Dez!*" Sefa was moving before the echo faded, dropping through the hatch, and Pip heard the sounds from below — Dez's breathing, ragged and wrong, Sefa's voice low and fast, and then Dez again, weaker: *"Engine's fine. I'm fine. Drive the boat."*

She wasn't fine. Pip knew it the way you know things in your body. But the engine note held, ragged but holding, and Ilya held the wheel, and the Narrows came at them like a door closing.

"NOW!" Ilya roared, and the world became water.

* * *

Pip had been in rapids once, years ago, a river crossing in the old country, before Lena's crew, before any of it. He'd thought that was fast water. This was a different substance entirely. The *Maren's Gift* hit the millrace and the river took her the way a hand takes a paper boat — completely, carelessly, with total power. The rock was there, exactly where Ilya said, black and glistening, water exploding off it in two white wings, and they went through the left wing so close Pip could have spat on the stone, and the boat spun a quarter-turn and slammed through standing waves that came over the bow and hit him in the chest like fists, and then —

— and then open water. Gray-green and wide and rain-softened, the forest falling away behind, the gunboat's sound suddenly distant, muffled, cheated.

Ilya eased the wheel. The engine coughed, stuttered — and kept going. Pip realized he was shaking, realized his hands were welded to the stay rope, realized his face was wet and it wasn't all river.

"Dez," he said. It wasn't even a word, really. It was just the name, thrown aft like a rope.

* * *

They found her in the engine space, sitting on the deck plates with her back against the housing, one hand pressed hard against her left side, just below the ribs. Sefa was kneeling over her with the medical kit open and her face doing the thing it did — the flat, controlled, absolutely-terrified face she wore when things were worst.

"Through and through," Sefa was saying, in the voice she used for reporting facts. "Left side, low. Missed anything that matters, I think — she's lucky, it's a graze-and-groove, the bullet—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "It's bleeding a lot but it's bleeding *out*, which is better than the alternative."

"I told you," Dez said. Her voice was gray. Her face was grayer. She grinned at Pip anyway, because Dez always grinned, that was the whole architecture of Dez, the grin was the load-bearing wall. "Told you I'd get shot before I let this engine die. Now nobody gets to say I don't sacrifice for my work."

"Shut up," Pip said, and his voice cracked, and he didn't care. "Shut up, you're — you're supposed to be *fine*—"

"I am fine. I'm shot, not dead. There's a difference and the difference is I intend to complain about it for weeks." But her hand was shaking against her side, and when Sefa packed the wound and tied it off, Dez made a sound she tried very hard to turn into a laugh and didn't quite manage.

Lena crouched beside her. Pip watched Lena's face do the arithmetic again — he could almost see the numbers moving behind her eyes, the cost tally, the running total this river was keeping on all of them. Tomas on one side of the ledger. Now Dez, bleeding on the deck plates of a dead friend's boat, on the other.

"We stop at the cache," Lena said. Not a question. "We stop, we patch her, we patch the boat, and we don't move again until she can stand."

"The *Verity* will—"

"The *Verity*," Ilya said from the wheel, "lost us at the Narrows. Cass knows these waters, but he doesn't know them like I do, and he won't risk the rock at speed with a gunner who shoots like that one does. We have hours. Maybe." He looked back at them, and his face was the river's face, unreadable and moving underneath. "But we should not spend them arguing. Get her below. Properly below. And someone bail — we took water over the bow in the Narrows, and I think we took a second hit below the waterline that I don't like the sound of."

Pip listened. Under the engine's ragged chug, under the hiss of the river along the hull, there it was: a new sound. A knock. Water, where water shouldn't be, arriving on its own schedule.

* * *

They ran another hour through the drowned country, Dez below with Sefa's hand pressed over the dressing and Sefa's other hand on the chart table because Sefa refused to do only one job when two needed doing, and Pip back up on his crate, strapped in, watching the gray horizon for the wasp sound.

The cache, when Ilya finally turned toward it, was nothing. That was the point. A drowned church spire rising out of the floodwater like a finger, a bend in a vanished creek line, and behind it a gap in the standing trees where the water went dark and still. Ilya took them in so slowly the engine was barely breathing, threading between submerged fences that showed only as brown shadows, and then there was a roofline under an awning of vines, half-sunk, and a loading dock that had once belonged to a world that no longer existed.

"Smugglers' cache," Ilya said. "Old one. Cass used it before he got respectable." He almost smiled. "He thinks it's forgotten. It isn't. Nothing on this river is forgotten. That's the problem with rivers, and the gift."

They tied off in the green silence. Dez made it up the ladder on Sefa's shoulder and her own fury, and lay down on the dock planks in the gray light, gray-faced, still grinning, still insisting she was fine while Sefa changed the dressing and her blood soaked through the bandage fast enough that Pip's stomach turned over.

And Pip stood watch at the mouth of the creek with the rope still looped around his wrist, listening to the water knock against the inside of the hull — faster now, he was almost sure, faster than an hour ago — and watching the wide gray water beyond for the shape that would mean the *Verity* had found them after all.

The engine note, when Dez had last coaxed it, had been dying. The hull was leaking worse. Dez couldn't stand. And somewhere out there on the flooded country, Cass Merrow was moving, and he knew this river too.

Pip put his hand flat against the hull, felt the water knocking, and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water in his lungs, that the *Maren's Gift* was not going to make it to the magistrate's station.

Whatever came next, they were going to have to carry it — the ledgers, the evidence, Tomas's unfinished work, Dez herself — on something other than this boat. And they had nothing else.

Behind him, deep in the flooded forest, a bird went up in a clatter of wings. Something had startled it.

Something was coming through the trees.
