## 16. Where the Law Doesn't Go

The cache was older than Ilya's grief and half as tidy.

He'd found it the way he found everything on this river — by feel, by the way the current bent wrong around a drowned snag, by the smell of creosote and old canvas that no honest structure ever carried. The entrance was a seam in a bluff face, hidden behind a curtain of hanging vines that had grown thick enough to hide a gunboat, and inside it opened into a hollow the smugglers of three generations had carved out of the limestone. Shelves cut into rock. Barrels with false bottoms. A rusted stove whose chimney vented through a crack in the stone so the smoke came out cold and scattered somewhere above the canopy. Somebody had thought about this place for a long time before they built it, and Ilya had always suspected he knew who.

His partner, Danya, had known it too. That was the trouble.

"Everyone in," Ilya said, holding the vine curtain aside while Dez brought the boat's nose against the mud shelf with a touch so gentle it barely made a sound. "Pole her back another meter. If anyone runs this bend at speed, I want us invisible."

"Invisible," Dez repeated, but she did it, walking the pole along the bottom with her wounded arm held tight against her ribs, sweat standing on her forehead even in the chill of the cave air. The bullet had gone through the meat below her shoulder two days ago, and the wound had gone from angry to ugly, and she hadn't said one word about either.

Pip came up the bank last, hauling the oilcloth bundle like it was a sleeping child, and Sefa took it from him and set it down on the flattest shelf with both hands, careful, ceremonial. Inside were the ledgers. Tomas Thornbury's ledgers, pulled from the flooded chapel an hour before Cass Merrow's men had come down the hill with lanterns and rifles, close enough behind them that Ilya could still hear the splash of their boots in his ears.

They were here. They had them. And now, Ilya thought, sitting down heavily on a barrel that groaned under him, came the part nobody planned for, because nobody ever plans for what happens after you get the thing you're willing to die for.

* * *

Sefa lit the stove because the alternative was sitting in the dark listening to the river, and the river tonight sounded like it was chewing on something. Rain hammered the bluff above them and came down through the crack in a thin, cold thread that pooled in a natural basin some long-dead smuggler had chiseled to catch exactly that. Everything in this place had been built by people who expected to be hunted. Ilya felt at home in it and hated feeling at home in it.

Dez cleaned her wound by stove light while Pip watched with the particular horror of the young who have just learned that bodies are not reliable machines. She poured spirits into the channel, hissed once through her teeth, and said, "Somebody talk. The quiet is worse than the rain."

"The magistrate at Vell's Landing," Sefa said. She had the ledgers open in front of her, pages fanned out, and she'd been reading them the way she read charts — fast, total, filing everything. "That's still the plan, yes? We run downriver, we put these in front of the regional magistrate, we let the law do what the law does."

"And the law is?" Dez asked.

"Honest," Pip said, too quickly.

Nobody answered him. That was the answer.

Ilya got up and crossed to the shelf where Sefa sat, and he turned one of the ledger pages with a finger that left a wet print on the old paper. Names. Dates. Tonnages moved, fees paid, officials paid, officials *kept*. He'd spent six years looking at fragments of this machine from the outside — a seized shipment here, a jailed friend there — and seeing it whole, in Tomas Thornbury's cramped and furious hand, was like seeing the entire river laid out on one chart. Every tributary. Every lock. Every man paid to look away.

"This page," Ilya said. "Third column. You know what that is?"

Sefa looked. Her jaw tightened.

"That's the Vell's Landing customs house," she said.

"That's the magistrate's own brother-in-law," Ilya said. "Drawing a salary from Cass Merrow twice a month for eleven years. And four lines down—" he tapped again, "—that's the fee schedule for the magistrate himself. Not bribes. *Fees.* Merrow pays him a retainer the way a shipping company pays for mooring rights. It's not corruption you can catch a man doing. It's corruption as infrastructure."

"So we don't go to Vell's Landing," Pip said.

"We were never going to make it to Vell's Landing," Dez said flatly. "Not with that gunboat behind us. Not with my arm leaking. Be honest with yourselves."

"I'm being honest," Sefa said, and there was something rising in her voice that Ilya recognized, because he'd heard it in his own chest for six years. "That's why I'm saying we take it to someone outside the river. Fly it out. Lena has a plane. We fly these to the capital, we hand them to the federal prosecutors, the ones who don't drink Merrow's water—"

"And how do they prove anything?" Ilya asked. He kept his voice level. He'd learned level. Level was the only way to say hard things without them turning into screaming. "Paper travels badly, Sefa. A courier gets delayed. A clerk loses a box. An office burns — and offices do burn, on this river, when the right people need them to. You put these ledgers in a government building and in six months they're ash, and everyone who touched them is dead, and Merrow is standing at his landing waving at boats."

"You don't know that."

"I know *Danya*," Ilya said.

The name dropped into the room like a stone into still water. Pip looked up. Dez stopped moving, cloth in hand, halfway through tying off the bandage.

Ilya hadn't told them much. He'd told Lena, mostly, and even then only the shape of it: a partner, falsified charges, a cell. But the stove was warm and the rain was loud and the truth had been sitting in his chest so long it had fossilized, and he heard himself say the rest before he decided to.

"Danya kept records too. Smaller ones. Fuel purchases, crew lists, the names of the men who ran Merrow's enforcement boats. Danya believed in paper the way you believe in it, Sefa. Believed if you wrote the truth down carefully enough, somebody honest would eventually read it." He turned another page. His finger wasn't steady anymore, and he let them see that. "So Danya mailed copies. To the port authority. To the provincial court. To a journalist in the capital who wrote one article and then retracted it and left the country. And eight weeks after the first letter went out, Danya was arrested at our own dock for smuggling — cargo planted in our hold while we slept, witnessed by two constables who signed statements they didn't write. Sentenced on paper that was *perfect*, Sefa. Every form stamped. Every procedure followed. The law worked exactly as designed, and what it was designed to do was bury my partner."

The thread of water coming through the ceiling crack ticked into the basin. Somewhere far above, thunder rolled along the ridge like a barrel being moved.

"So when you say *the law*," Ilya finished, "I hear a word for a thing that already ate the last person who trusted it."

* * *

For a long time nobody spoke, and Ilya let the silence do its work. He'd learned that too, on the river: you don't fight a current, you wait for it to tell you where it's going.

It was Dez who broke it, because Dez always broke silences, usually by bleeding or swearing, and tonight she managed the second one.

"Options," she said, holding up fingers on her good hand. "One: Vell's Landing magistrate. Owned. Two: capital prosecutors. Slow, leaky, maybe ash in six months, and we don't survive long enough to see it either way, because that gunboat will find us first. Three—" she wiggled the third finger, "—we don't hand it to anybody. We use it."

"Use it how?" Pip asked.

"Merrow's whole empire runs on fear, right? Fear works because everybody thinks they're alone in knowing things. Every dockhand who took hush money thinks he's the only one who'd hang for it. Every town that looks away thinks every other town would sell them out." Dez leaned forward, and even hurt and gray-faced she had the look she got around engines — total attention, hands wanting to be busy. "But this book says otherwise. This book says the customs man at Vell's Landing, the harbor master at Skell, the ferry operator at Two Rivers — it names them all in one place. You show a town its own page, and suddenly they're not alone anymore. Suddenly there are forty people in the room who all did the same crime for the same man, and the only person who can hang them all is the man they're all afraid of."

"You're talking about starting a fire," Sefa said quietly.

"I'm talking about showing people they've already been burned," Dez said. "There's a difference."

"It's not justice," Sefa said. "It's revenge with extra steps. You tear Merrow down in the street and what fills the hole? The next man with guns and money, and no ledgers to catch him with. I buried a captain because of this river's idea of justice. I'm not going to build him a successor."

"You buried a *brother*," Dez said, gentler than Ilya had ever heard her. "Lena buried a brother. And I've watched her carry those ledgers for two days like they weigh more than the plane. So maybe ask her instead of deciding for her."

"She's asleep."

"Then we wait for her to wake up," Dez said, "and until then nobody decides anything, because that's how crews die — somebody makes a call alone in the dark and the rest of you find out at the funeral."

Ilya almost smiled. Almost. There was something in the argument he recognized from the inside: Sefa wanted the system to work because if it couldn't, then nothing was safe, anywhere, ever, and that was a weight no navigator should have to steer by. Dez wanted to burn the thing down because burning was faster than waiting and she'd been running out of patience since before Ilya met her. And Pip—

Pip was sitting apart from all of them, knees drawn up, staring at the ledgers with an expression Ilya had seen on young faces in river towns his whole life. Not fear. Recognition. The look of someone realizing that the adults' world is made of the same rot as the water it floats on.

"What about the towns?" Pip said.

They all looked at him.

"The river towns," Pip said. "The woman who gave us Tomas's letter. All those people who *remembered*. They didn't forget Merrow. They're just — waiting. For proof. For permission. For somebody to go first." He swallowed. "If we hand this to a magistrate, we're asking one man to be brave. If we use it ourselves, we're asking forty towns. Which one of those sounds more likely?"

Ilya stared at the boy and felt something shift in his chest, some old ballast sliding. Six years he'd thought about justice as a transaction — evidence delivered, sentence served, balance restored, a clean entry in a clean book. He'd never once thought about it as weather. Something that moves across a whole landscape at once and doesn't need anyone's permission.

"The chapel woman," he said slowly. "She gave you the letter. Did she ask for anything?"

"No," Pip said. "She said Tomas bought bread from her stall every market day for two years, and nobody had ever told her what happened to him, and she wanted to know who to grieve properly."

"*Who to grieve properly,*" Ilya repeated. "That's the river, boy. That's every town on it. They're not corrupt. They're ungrieved. Nobody ever told them the truth about their own dead, so they keep their heads down and their mouths shut, and Merrow calls that loyalty." He stood up. His knee cracked; he ignored it. "You show them the pages with their own towns' names. You tell them what happened to Tomas Thornbury and to Danya Volkov and to every other body the current took. Then it isn't our justice anymore. It's theirs. And Merrow can't buy off a whole river."

* * *

The curtain of vines moved.

Every one of them froze — Dez's hand going for the rifle she'd propped against the barrel, Sefa rising silent, Ilya stepping between the sound and the ledgers without deciding to, his body older and smarter than his head.

Then the vines parted and Lena came in out of the rain, soaked to the skin, hair plastered flat, eyes going around the cave in one sweep — Dez's arm, the ledgers, the rifle, the positions of everyone standing — cataloguing damage the way she catalogued everything, fast and complete.

"You were supposed to sleep," Dez said.

"I did. Four hours. On a boat full of holes, in a storm, with a gunboat somewhere behind us." Lena wrung out her sleeve and came toward the stove. "That counts."

"There's something you need to hear," Sefa started. "We've been arguing about—"

"I heard most of it." Lena crouched at the mouth of the passage, where the rock funneled sound up from the water. "Sound carries strangely off the bluff. I've been awake for the last twenty minutes." She straightened and looked at each of them in turn, and last of all at Ilya, and what was in her face wasn't the wariness he'd seen when they'd met, or the grief, or the iron discipline of a woman who never stayed anywhere long enough to be found. It was the thing underneath all of those, the engine itself. "My brother wrote those books. He died writing them. And I have spent years making sure I was never anywhere long enough for the past to catch me, and it caught me anyway, mid-river, in monsoon season, because that's what the past does — it doesn't knock, it just arrives."

She picked up the top ledger and held it against her chest.

"Sefa's right that the courts might fail us. Dez is right that handing this over means losing control of it. Ilya is right that the law on this river is Merrow's second-favorite weapon." Her voice didn't rise at all, which somehow made it worse, the way deep water is worse than waves. "And Pip is right that there are forty towns out there waiting for someone to go first. So here's what I think we do. We don't choose one. We do all of it. Copies to the towns as we pass — pages, not the originals. The originals go to the magistrate at Vell's Landing, because if we die getting them there, at least they'll be in a government building when it burns, and burning buildings leave witnesses. And everything else — the story, the names, the truth about Tomas and Danya and every soul in these columns — goes out loud, in every market square and fuel dock between here and the Landing, until Merrow's name tastes like poison in his own mouth."

Silence again, but a different kind. The kind that comes after a decision, when everyone is measuring whether they can live inside it.

"And if the magistrate burns them anyway?" Sefa asked.

"Then the towns will already know," Lena said. "You can't burn a story out of forty thousand people. Merrow taught me that without meaning to. He built his empire on everyone keeping quiet — which means the whole river is already primed. He just never figured anyone would hand them matches."

Ilya looked at the boy who'd started it, at the mechanic with her ruined arm, at the navigator who'd finally stopped arguing, at the woman holding her dead brother's handwriting against her heart — and he thought about Danya, in a cell, believing in paper, and understood at last that Danya hadn't been wrong. Just early. Paper needed people to carry it, and the river had never had enough carriers.

Until now.

"One problem," Dez said. She nodded toward the entrance, toward the rain and the dark water beyond it. "Vell's Landing is two days downstream in good weather. In this, it's four. And that gunboat knows this stretch better than we do."

"No," Ilya said, and felt the old knowledge rise in him like water finding its level. "It knows the main channel better than we do. It doesn't know the flooded country. The drowned forest past Blackwater Bend, where the mapmakers gave up and the water goes wherever it wants. Merrow's pilots won't risk it in monsoon season." He allowed himself the smallest grim smile he'd owned in six years. "I will. My partner is in a cell because men like Merrow own the safe routes. So we take the unsafe one."

Outside, the storm thickened, and the river rose against the bluff, and somewhere downstream a gunboat patrolled a channel that was about to be very empty indeed.

"Pip," Lena said, rolling the ledgers back into their oilcloth, "get the copying ink ready. We start with the chapel town's page — we owe that woman a visit on the way. Dez, that arm needs stitching before we move. Sefa, chart me every side channel off the drowned forest that Ilya's memory can't cover." She looked up, and for the first time since Ilya had known her, she wasn't scanning the horizon for the fastest way out.

She was looking downriver.

"And Ilya," she said. "Show us the way in."
