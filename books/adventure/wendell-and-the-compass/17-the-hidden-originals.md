## 17. The Hidden Originals

The letter had said *where the water keeps the bell.*

Lena read it again by lamplight in the smugglers' cache, her thumb tracing the ink that had faded to the color of old tea. Tomas's handwriting — she'd know it anywhere, that forward lean like a man always hurrying toward something. She had spent three years learning to look at his handwriting without her chest caving in, and she still wasn't very good at it.

*Where the water keeps the bell,* he'd written. *You always laughed at me for memorizing the river instead of the roads. Laugh now, Lennie. It's all I could trust.*

Below it, a sketch. A chapel with a squat tower, drawn from memory and badly — the proportions wrong, the windows too tall — but recognizable enough. And beneath the sketch, three words: *San Elpidio. Third pier.*

"Third pier of what?" Dez asked, leaning over her shoulder. His arm was bound tight against his ribs where the bullet had torn through, and he'd gone gray around the mouth, but he was standing, because Dez didn't know how to do anything else.

"The drowned village," Sefa said quietly.

Everyone looked at her. She was crouched by the cache's single window, watching the rain come down off the hills in gray sheets, and she didn't turn around when she spoke.

"There's a village up past the Narrows that the dam company flooded twenty years ago. They moved everyone out, raised the waterline, and the whole valley went under. Except the flood never came up as high as they promised, and it never went down either. The church tower still sticks out of the water some seasons. The locals call it the Drowned Chapel." She turned then, and her face in the lamplight was grim. "Pilots use the tower as a range marker. Nobody goes inside."

"Why not?" Pip asked.

"Because it's underwater, Pip."

"Not all of it," Lena said. Her heart was going hard and strange against her ribs. "Not in dry season. And we're at the tail end of monsoon — the rivers are high, but the reservoir behind the old dam..." She looked at Ilya.

He was sitting apart from them, as he always did, cleaning a pistol with the unhurried patience of a man who had done nothing else for years. He looked up. "The reservoir's down two meters since the rains started easing. I've run past San Elpidio four times this month." A pause. "The tower's fully clear. The nave roof is just under the surface. You can see the ridge line through the water on a calm day."

"So the nave might be wadeable," Dez said. "If the water dropped far enough."

"If," Sefa said.

"It's always if," Lena said. She folded the letter along its worn creases and put it inside her jacket, over her heart, which felt less like sentiment than armor. "Cass thinks the evidence burned with the copies he seized at Blackwater Bend. That's the only reason we're still breathing. The moment he learns there are originals —"

"He already knows," Ilya said flatly.

The room went quiet. Rain drummed on the corrugated roof.

"You saw the gunboat's search pattern yesterday," Ilya went on. "They weren't sweeping channels. They were quartering the banks. Grid work. That's not a man looking for a leaking boat. That's a man who knows exactly what you're looking for and wants to be standing on it when you arrive." He set the pistol down. "Tomas mailed that letter from Harrow Bend. Postmasters talk. Cass has paid postmasters before."

Lena felt the floor tilt under her, the way it did sometimes when grief came back around wearing fear's clothes. *He already knows.* Which meant every hour they sat here was an hour Cass's men spent getting to San Elpidio first — or waiting there, patient as crocodiles.

"How far?" she asked.

Ilya almost smiled. It was not a comforting sight. "By the backchannels? Six hours, if the water behaves. Four, if it doesn't."

"Then we go now," Lena said, "while it's dark and raining and nobody sane is on the river."

"I'm not sane," Ilya agreed, and began loading the pistol.

* * *

They ran blacked-out through the flooded country, and Lena stood at the bow with rain hammering her shoulders and let the darkness be a kind of mercy.

She couldn't see the drowned forest sliding past — she could only hear it, the wet slap of branches against the hull, the occasional groan of a submerged trunk somewhere beneath them that made Sefa, at the wheel, flinch and correct. Ilya knelt beside her calling depths from memory, his voice low and even, a litany: *Shoal left. Deadfall right, give it room. Current picks up here — hold center.* He knew this drowned world the way other men knew their own kitchens, and Lena understood suddenly that he hadn't learned it for love of the water. He'd learned it because the water was the last place that belonged to him.

*Two kinds of justice meeting,* she thought, and then stopped thinking, because ahead of them the channel narrowed and the rain came sideways and there was no room in her head for anything but the next hundred meters.

They passed through the Narrows at dawn — or what passed for dawn, a bruised gray light that made the whole world look like it was underwater too. And then the channel opened, and the trees fell back, and there it was.

San Elpidio.

The reservoir spread out flat and pewter-colored to the far hills, and out of its middle rose the chapel tower, black stone streaked white with bird droppings, its bell arch empty, its cross long since fallen. Around it, just breaking the surface here and there, were the ridgelines of roofs — a whole village lying beneath the water like a sleeping congregation. A fence post. The peak of a chimney. The top rail of what had once been someone's gate.

Nobody spoke. Even the rain seemed to fall more softly.

"My grandmother came from a place like this," Sefa said at last. "Flooded for a dam that never got built. She used to say the government drowned the town twice — once with water, once with paperwork." She cut the engine and let them drift. "Tomas picked a good hiding spot. Nobody robs a graveyard."

"Tomas wasn't hiding it from robbers," Lena said. "He was hiding it from Cass. And Cass doesn't dive." She shrugged off her jacket, wrapped the letter in oilcloth, and stowed it in the waterproof bag lashed under the bow seat. "Dez, you're on the boat. No arguments — you're shot and someone has to keep us afloat and get us out fast. Pip, you're with me. Sefa—"

"I'm diving with you," Sefa said.

"You don't have to."

"Lena." Sefa's voice was gentle and absolutely immovable. "Your brother died chasing these ledgers. You're going to want to carry them out yourself, and if the current takes you, or the ceiling comes down, or your leg gets caught on a pew, you are going to drown rather than let go. I've watched you let go of nothing for three years." She was already pulling on the borrowed wetsuit, the one from the cache, patched at both knees. "I'm coming so that when the moment comes, someone whose head isn't full of ghosts can make the smart choice."

Lena wanted to argue. She found she couldn't. "Fine," she said, and heard her own voice crack on it, and pretended she hadn't.

* * *

The water was cold in a way the monsoon rivers weren't — deep-water cold, held-down cold, the cold of things that had been submerged a long time and meant to stay that way.

Lena went in first, off the boat's stern, and the shock of it closed around her like a hand. Below her boots the village descended in stages: the gate rail, a street corner where two stone walls met at a drowned right angle, a window frame gaping with dark. Fish scattered from her shadow. Light came down through the surface in trembling columns and broke apart on the rooftops below.

The nave roof was two meters down. Ilya had been right — the ridge line showed faintly through the water, and where a section of roofing had collapsed years ago, there was a gap, a ragged skylight into the dark of the church itself.

She dove.

The world went green-black and silent. She swam down along the ridge tiles, found the gap, and slipped through into the nave.

Inside, the chapel was a cathedral of suspended sediment. Her lamp beam swung across pews furred with silt, an altar draped in algae like a green altar cloth, stained glass windows that had somehow survived — their colors muted, glowing dully, saints and angels swimming in the gloom. An entire congregation of glass saints presiding over a church full of water and silence.

*Where the water keeps the bell.* But the bell was gone from the tower; she'd seen the empty arch. So where —

Then she saw it, and her heart slammed so hard she nearly lost her regulator.

Hanging from the rood beam above the altar, where a sanctuary bell would hang in a small country church, was a bell. Not the tower bell — a handbell, bronze, green with age, suspended from an iron bracket by a chain that had corroded to lace. And bolted to the underside of the beam, directly beneath it, a wooden box the size of a bread crate, tarred and strapped, with a lead seal stamped with a mark she knew better than her own name.

Her brother's mark. The little river-compass he used to doodle on everything — schoolbooks, letters, the margins of charts.

*Tomas.*

She was moving before she'd decided to, kicking down the aisle between the pews, silt billowing up around her, the saints watching from their windows. The box was heavier than it looked — she got her arms around it and pulled and it barely shifted, the straps fused to the beam by twenty years of mineral scale. She worked at the buckles. They wouldn't move. Her air ticked down. Somewhere above her, faint through the water, she heard the muffled sound of Sefa's fins at the roof gap.

And then, through the green murk, another sound.

Engines.

Muffled, distant, but unmistakable — and not one engine. Three. Coming up the reservoir from the south, from the direction of Cass's territory, and slowing.

Lena's blood went to ice water colder than the reservoir. She looked up through the broken roof at the wavering silver ceiling of the surface, thirty feet of drowned church between her and the sky, and understood with perfect clarity what Ilya had told her: *he wants to be standing on it when you arrive.*

They hadn't beaten Cass's men here.

They'd led them.

* * *

She surfaced in the tower's shadow, gasping, and Sefa hauled her over the gunwale, and Lena got the words out between breaths: "Three boats. South shore. They're here."

Dez was already at the engine cover, one-handed, cursing. Pip stood in the bow with binoculars, and his voice came back thin and fast: "Two gunboats holding off the south shallows. Third one's circling the tower. There's divers' flags on it, Lena. They're putting divers in the water."

"They'll find the box," Sefa said. "Or they'll find the bell and figure out the rest."

"No," Lena said. Her mind had gone strangely, perfectly clear — the clarity she'd only ever felt twice before, once the night Tomas died and once the night she'd sworn the oath over his grave. Both times it had felt like him lending her his calm. "They won't. Because the box isn't coming up that way." She pointed at the tower. "Look at the construction. Old churches like this — the tower base is solid stone, separate footing from the nave. There's a crawl space under the altar floor, Tomas told me once, used for storage when the village flooded seasonally. If the box is strapped to the rood beam, the original hiding spot was probably the crawl space, and he moved it up where divers would have to go through the roof gap — one way in, no cover." She was pulling her fins back on as she talked. "But the crawl space connects to the tower base. Dry footing. Maybe."

"Maybe," Dez repeated.

"Maybe is what we have." She looked at each of them — Dez gray and stubborn, Sefa steady, Pip terrified and trying not to show it, Ilya unreadable at the wheel with his hand resting on the throttle like it lived there. "They think we're running. Every boat they've got is watching the channels north, because that's the way out. So we don't go out." She nodded at the tower rising black against the rain-gray sky. "We go in. Down through the tower, into the crawl space, and we take my brother's box out through the front door of his own church while they're watching the roof."

Silence. Rain on the water. On the third gunboat, a diver's flag climbed its halyard.

"And if the crawl space is flooded?" Pip asked quietly.

Lena settled her mask on her forehead and picked up the pry bar.

"Then Tomas picked a bad hiding spot," she said, "and I'll tell him so myself. Ilya — put us against the tower, north side, out of their sightline. We're going home."

The engine woke. The boat swung toward the drowned chapel, and above them, in the empty bell arch, the wind moved through the tower with a sound almost — almost — like ringing.
