## 18. Storm Front

Dez felt the storm before she heard it — a pressure change in her bad knee, the one she'd wrecked falling out of a Cessna's wheel well at nineteen, back when she still thought her body was replaceable. The knee had never lied to her since. It ached now like a struck bell.

"Front's coming," she said, and didn't bother raising her voice. On the boat, nobody needed to. "Big one. The kind that eats rivers."

Sefa looked up from the chart table, where Ilya's hand-drawn map lay weighted down with four brass shells and a coffee mug. Outside the patched windows the flooded country stretched gray-green to every horizon, drowned forest standing in six feet of water like a congregation waiting for a sermon that was never going to come.

"How long?"

Dez chewed on it. Her hands were already moving without her — checking the engine housing, wiping oil off the dipstick, doing the small compulsive arithmetic of a mechanic who has learned that machines fail in weather and people fail faster. "Two hours to the leading edge. Maybe three before it's on top of us."

"And the magistrate's station?"

Ilya stood in the doorway with rain already beading in his beard, come up from checking the bilge. He'd been down there half the night; the canvas patch over the hole the gunboat had torn in them was holding, but holding the way a promise holds — technically, and only if nobody asked too much of it.

"Four hours," he said. "Five, if we take the safe channel."

"Safe channel's flooded out," Sefa said. "You said so yourself."

"I said *probably* flooded out." Ilya's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "There's a difference between probably dead and certainly late."

Dez wiped her hands on a rag that had been black for three years and looked at the map. Ilya drew like a man who thought in water — channels as veins, sandbars as knuckles, the whole drowned delta rendered in confident pencil strokes that Dez would have trusted over any government survey ever printed. The magistrate's station sat at the top right corner, a small square marked with a flag symbol, tucked behind a levee town called Harrow's Landing. Between them and it: eleven miles of open floodwater, two channels, one shortcut through what Ilya had labeled, with no apparent irony, *the Throat*.

And somewhere ahead — ahead of them, planted there like a hook in the river's jaw — Cass Merrow.

They all knew it now. The fisherman at the resupply dock had told them without being asked, the way people in river towns tell you things: eyes down, voice low, glancing at the water like it might be listening. *Merrow's got men at Harrow's Landing. Bought the levee crew, bought the ferryman, bought the constable who isn't supposed to be for sale.* Cass wasn't chasing them anymore. Chasing cost fuel and patience, and Cass Merrow had learned from Blackwater Bend that this crew did not sink easy. So he'd done the smarter thing. He'd gone to the one place they couldn't avoid, and he'd sat down to wait.

"He knows we're coming," Pip said from the stern, where she'd been keeping watch with a pair of binoculars held together with electrical tape. She was nineteen and had the eyes of a hawk and the judgment of a golden retriever, and Dez loved her like a sister she'd have killed for. "He's not even pretending otherwise. That's what scares me."

"It should," Lena said.

Dez hadn't heard her come into the wheelhouse. She never did. Lena moved quiet as ballast shifting, and she had the look she got before landings in bad country — calm on the surface, everything underneath running hot. She stood at the window and watched the western sky, where the clouds had organized themselves into something with intent.

"Tomas used to say the monsoon doesn't punish you," Lena went on. "It just tells you the truth about your boat."

Dez snorted. "Your brother was a poet and a liar. The monsoon punishes you plenty. It just does it honestly."

That got the smallest flicker of a smile out of Lena, which these days was worth more than triple pay. Then the smile went away, because everything went away on Lena's face eventually, folded up and put in a drawer, and she said: "We go now. Ahead of the front. If we're tied up at the magistrate's dock when it hits, we're dry, we're inside, and we've got witnesses. If we're caught on open water—"

"We're a leaky boat full of evidence in a hurricane," Dez finished. "Yeah. I did that math too. It comes out ugly either way, but one ugly has a roof."

Sefa was already rolling the map closed. "The Throat, then?"

Ilya was quiet a moment. Dez watched him look at the water — really look, the way he always did, like he was reading mail addressed specifically to him. When he spoke, his voice had the flat certainty she'd learned to trust more than her own instruments.

"The Throat. And we don't stop for anything. Not debris, not trouble, not each other." He caught himself, glanced at Pip. "Except each other. You know what I mean."

"I know what you mean," Pip said solemnly, and Dez had to turn away so nobody saw her face do something soft.

* * *

They ran the first three miles hard and clean, and Dez spent them with her head inside the engine compartment instead of watching the water, because that was the deal she'd made with herself years ago: she kept the machine alive so the machine could keep the people alive, and she didn't waste attention on things she couldn't fix. The engine was an old marine diesel some genius had salvaged off a fishing trawler, underpowered and overworked, and it ran rough in the humid air, coughing at intervals like a smoker climbing stairs.

She talked to it while she worked. She always talked to it. *Come on, sweetheart. Not today. Today you're a hero.*

The hull groaned beneath her — the patch taking strain, the frame flexing in ways frames weren't meant to flex. Every sound came up through the deck plates and into her spine, and she'd gotten so fluent in the boat's language that she could translate: *that groan is tired, that knock is angry, that high whine is afraid.* Right now the boat sounded like all of them.

The sky went from gray to bruise-colored. The wind arrived in gusts first, testing them, shoving the bow sideways and letting go, then shoving again harder, a bully learning what they could take. Rain came in sheets that moved sideways, that hit like thrown gravel. Dez crawled out onto the deck twice to lash down the last of the crates — Tomas's ledgers, wrapped in oilcloth, triple-bagged, riding in the driest spot they had like a sleeping child — and both times the water tried to take her feet out from under her, and both times she came back through the wheelhouse door soaked to the skin and grinning, because there was a version of her that had always loved this, the fight, the noise, the world trying its best and her best being better.

"You're enjoying this," Sefa accused, hauling the door shut behind her.

"I contain multitudes."

Ahead, the Throat opened up between two walls of drowned forest — a channel maybe forty meters wide where the current funneled through like water through a fire hose, whitecapped even before the storm proper hit. Ilya brought them to the entrance and held there, throttle balanced against current, reading the water the way Dez read engines.

"Once we commit," he said, "there's no backing out. The current won't allow it. There are snags — trees under the surface, whole houses, God knows what the flood pulled loose upstream. I'll call them. You steer what I call."

"Copy," Sefa said, hands on the wheel.

"Dez." Ilya turned. "If we ship water, I need your pump and I need it yesterday."

"Pump's primed, spare's rigged, and I've got a bucket with my name on it." She cinched her rain jacket tighter. "Go."

He went.

The Throat took them the way a throat takes a pill — fast, involuntary, with a lot of gagging. The current grabbed the bow and swung it and Sefa wrestled it straight, and then they were in it, doing nine knots over ground in a boat that topped out at ten, the drowned forest whipping past on both sides close enough to touch, spray coming off the wave crests in ropes. Ilya stood at the bow rail with one arm around a stanchion and pointed and shouted and Sefa steered and Dez listened to the hull and hated every sound it made.

*"Snag! Port side, ten meters!"*

Sefa hauled starboard. Something dark slid past below the surface — a whole tree, root ball up, big enough to stove them in like a tin can — and Dez's heart did something acrobatic and illegal.

*"Harder! Come harder right!"*

The boat heeled. Cargo shifted. Somewhere aft, Pip swore with impressive creativity for someone raised by missionaries. They cleared the snag by what Dez would later estimate, generously, as four feet, and then the next call came and the next, and time went strange the way it did in extremis, stretching and compressing, ten minutes that felt like a shift and a lifetime.

Then the rain went from heavy to biblical.

It didn't fall so much as arrive, all at once, a wall of water dropped on them from a height, and visibility went from fifty meters to nothing. Dez lost the forest walls entirely. She lost the horizon. She lost, briefly, the bow of her own boat. The world became sound — engine, wind, rain hammering the deck like applause from an enormous hostile audience — and Ilya's voice cutting through it, calling depths and snags from memory now, because memory was all anyone had left.

*"Trust me! Left of the dead oak — trust me—"*

Sefa trusted him. Dez held her breath and the pump handle both, and the boat threaded whatever needle Ilya saw in his head, and then the current eased, and the walls of forest reappeared out of the murk like a curtain lifting, and they were through.

Nobody cheered. Nobody had the breath. But Pip put her hand flat on the deck planking, and Dez saw her do it, and understood it completely: *thank you, old girl. Keep us together a little longer.*

* * *

The pump ran for the last hour. Not frantically — steadily, the way a heart runs during a hard climb rather than a panic. Water was coming in around the patch, slow and patient, and Dez bailed and pumped and listened to the hull and made her peace with the fact that the boat was dying. It had been dying since Blackwater Bend. Everything since had been hospice care.

She found she minded less than she expected. Boats were like people that way — you could love them knowing the ending, and the loving wasn't wasted by it.

Harrow's Landing appeared out of the rain at 3:40 in the afternoon, twenty minutes ahead of the worst of the front, and Dez's first coherent thought was that it looked wrong.

Not damaged-wrong. *Arranged*-wrong.

The levee town sat on a rise above the flood line, a scatter of tin roofs and warehouse sheds and a stone customs house left over from some empire's better days, and the magistrate's station was the building with the flagpole, exactly where Ilya's map promised. But the docks — the docks were empty. A river town in monsoon season should have been chaos: barges rafted up, ferrymen cursing, cargo lashed and covered, everyone fighting the weather together because that's what river towns did. Instead every working boat had been moved to the far bank or pulled out of the water entirely, and the main dock stood bare in the rain like a stage between acts.

"Somebody cleared the harbor," Sefa said quietly.

"Cass bought the ferryman," Dez said. "Fisherman told us. Bought the levee crew, bought the constable." She looked at the empty dock and felt the cold arrive in her gut, the specific cold of a mechanic who recognizes a setup. "He didn't clear the harbor to keep us out, Sefa. He cleared it so there'd be nothing in our way. He wants us to come alongside."

Lena stood at the window with the oilcloth bundle held against her chest, and Dez watched her look at the magistrate's station — at the light burning in its windows, warm and yellow and ordinary — and Dez knew exactly what she was thinking, because they were all thinking it. Behind those windows was the law. Behind those windows was the end of eight years of running, the grave finally paid its due, Tomas's work finished. And between the dock and those windows stood whatever Cass Merrow had arranged in the empty space he'd made.

"There," Pip said suddenly, binoculars up. "On the station roof. Look at the flagpole base."

Dez borrowed the glasses. Two men, crouched behind the parapet, rifles wrapped against the rain. And across the street from the customs house, parked casual as Sunday, a black-hulled gunboat with its engines idling — Cass's other boat, the twin of the one that had holed them at Blackwater Bend, sitting in the lee of the levee where the storm couldn't touch it, waiting with the patience of money.

"He's not hiding," Dez said. She handed the glasses back. "He wants us to see."

"Of course he does." Lena's voice was very level, the levelness of a woman holding something down with both hands. "He wants us to understand that the law is his. That we can fly all night and outrun the monsoon and survive the Throat, and it won't matter, because the door we're rowing to is a door he owns."

Rain hammered the roof. The wind screamed down the empty harbor. And Dez, who fixed things for a living, looked at the impossible arithmetic in front of them — a dying boat, a guarded dock, a storm arriving in minutes, evidence that had to reach that yellow-lit window or all of it, all of them, everyone who'd helped, everyone who'd died, was for nothing — and did what she always did with impossible arithmetic.

She started looking for the part nobody else had checked.

"The levee," she said slowly. "The fisherman said Cass bought the levee crew. What does a levee crew actually *do*, Ilya? In a storm like this?"

Ilya turned from the window, and Dez watched the same thought land on him that had landed on her — watched it hit like the rain, sudden and total.

"Floodgates," he said. "There's a sluice gate under the east levee. Drainage, for when the river gets high. It lets the water out of the harbor channel into the fields beyond." His eyes had gone bright and hard. "If it's open—"

"The harbor channel drains," Dez finished. "Drops what — a meter? Two?"

"Two, near the dock. Fast." Ilya was already moving for the map, already pulling the pencil stub from behind his ear. "A gunboat drawing Cass's draft can't follow anyone through a draining channel. And a dock approach with a two-meter drop—" He stopped. Smiled, and it was not a friendly smile. "Cass bought the levee crew to keep the gate shut. He wanted the harbor deep for his own boat."

"So we make it shallow for everybody," Pip said, catching up three steps behind everyone else as usual and somehow arriving first.

Lena looked at the magistrate's windows. At the gunboat. At the sky, which had gone the color of a bruise going green. When she spoke, she sounded like a woman setting down a weight she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it was there.

"We come in fast, we come in loud, and we give them one chance to do the right thing in front of witnesses." She lifted the oilcloth bundle. "And if they don't — Dez, how much engine do we have left?"

Dez put her hand flat on the deck, felt the old diesel hammering away below like a heart refusing instructions, and grinned through the rain still dripping off her chin.

"Honey," she said, "you have no idea."
