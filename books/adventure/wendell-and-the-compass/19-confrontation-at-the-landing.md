## 19. Confrontation at the Landing

The storm broke open the sky an hour before we reached the landing, and I took it as a warning. Rain came down in ropes, thick enough to drink, hammering the canvas patch Dez had sewn over the hole in our hull until the whole boat sounded like a drum being beaten by an angry god. Through it all, Ilya stood at the tiller with his collar up and his eyes half-closed, reading water none of the rest of us could see.

"Magistrate's station is around that point," he said, nodding toward a black shoulder of land where the river bent hard left. "You can hear the generator before you see the lights."

He was right. Under the rain's roar there was a low mechanical growl, and beyond it, a smudge of yellow where floodlamps burned through the downpour. The landing itself was a shelf of concrete built into the bank — a government afterthought from some decade when the interior mattered to somebody — with a corrugated-roofed station house behind it and a radio mast leaning like a drunk against the sky.

I should have felt relief. Instead my hands had gone cold on the gunwale, because Ilya had gone quiet in the wrong way.

"What," I said.

He didn't answer at first. He brought us around the point slow, engine idling low, and let the current carry us so we drifted rather than arrived. That was when I saw what he'd already seen: a gunboat tied up at the far end of the landing, riding high and mean, its searchlight sweeping the water in lazy arcs. Cass Merrow's boat. Cass Merrow's men on the dock under oilskins, four of them, rifles slung but hands near.

"He got here ahead of us," Sefa said, coming forward out of the wheelhouse, hair plastered flat. "How?"

"Backchannel's flooded this time of year," Ilya said. "He'd have run the main channel. Faster if you don't mind being seen." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "He doesn't mind being seen. He never has."

Pip appeared beside Sefa, small and soaked and clutching the waterproof satchel to their chest like a second heartbeat. Inside it were Tomas's ledgers — the originals we'd pulled from the drowned chapel two days ago, wrapped in oilcloth, dry as bone. Everything Tomas had died for. Everything Cass had spent years trying to drown.

"We could go around," Pip said. "There has to be another way in."

"There isn't," Ilya and I said at once.

I looked at the kid. Rain ran off the brim of the hat Dez had given them back in the river town, and underneath it their face was pale but set — the set of someone who'd stopped asking whether they were scared and started deciding what to do about it. I knew that face. I'd worn it for six years, ever since a muddy strip outside Kessarin where a constable handed me my brother's effects in a plastic bag.

"No," I said. "We're not going around. We've been going around things my whole life. Around towns, around debts, around the truth." I turned to face all of them — Sefa with her jaw tight, Dez leaning in the wheelhouse doorway with one arm pressed to the bandage on his side, Ilya motionless as driftwood. "Tomas didn't die going around. He died walking straight at it. We finish it his way."

Dez grunted. "Straight at four rifles. That's a hell of a way."

"You have a better one?"

"I have a worse one," Ilya said quietly. "Which might be better."

* * *

He laid it out while we idled behind a screen of drowned willows a hundred meters short of the landing, rain drumming the canopy overhead. His plan was simple the way all desperate plans are simple.

"Cass wants the ledgers. He thinks he wants them destroyed, but more than that he wants *you* — because as long as you're alive and talking, the ledgers exist somewhere. So he'll want to take you whole. That means talk first, guns later. Men like him always talk first." Ilya's eyes stayed on the sweep of the searchlight. "He'll invite you onto the dock. He'll be reasonable. He'll offer you money, then threats. While he does that, his best people will be looking at your faces, not the water."

"And the water?" Sefa asked.

"The water is mine." He pointed upstream, to where the river bent around the point in a long green curve. "That channel runs shallow along the north bank — sandbar under two feet of flood, moving every season. His pilot doesn't know it moved last spring. His pilot is steering by charts three years old, because Cass is too cheap to buy new ones." Now the smile came, thin and real. "When I give the word, we come in fast on the south side, past his boat, and I put us against the magistrate's steps. And when his gunboat tries to swing after us—"

"It grounds," Dez said slowly. "On the bar."

"It grounds. Hard enough to crack something, maybe. Hard enough to cost him ten minutes getting off, which is ten minutes longer than we need."

"And if he just shoots us before we get there?" Pip asked.

"He won't. Not in front of the station." Ilya glanced at me. "There are two constables inside who owe him nothing and a magistrate who owes him less. Cass's power lives in places where nobody's watching. He won't do murder under floodlights. He'll do it later, quiet, the way he did everything else." His voice went flat. "The way he did to Renna."

Renna. His partner. The name sat between us like a stone. Six years in a cell on falsified charges while Cass ran their route, their contacts, their life.

"So we walk into his mouth," Sefa said, "and trust a sandbar."

"I've trusted worse," Dez said. "I've married worse."

Despite everything, someone laughed — Pip, a short cracked sound, quickly swallowed. But it broke the tension enough that we could move.

I took the satchel from Pip. They resisted for a second, fingers white on the strap, then let go. "You carry the truth better than I do," they said, which was the kind of thing kids say without knowing it's a knife.

I buckled it under my coat, against my chest, where Tomas used to carry his letters home.

"Engine hot," Ilya said. "On my word."

* * *

Cass Merrow hadn't changed, except in the ways that mattered.

He stood at the center of the landing under a wide black umbrella held by one of his men, and he looked exactly like the man I remembered from the years before everything — broad, silver-templed, dressed too well for the rain, with the easy posture of someone who has never once doubted that a room belongs to him. When our boat swung in fast along the south wall and ground up against the concrete steps, he didn't flinch. He smiled.

"Lena Thornbury." His voice carried easily over the rain, warm as brandy. "Six years, and you still fly like you're angry at the sky."

"Cass." I came up the steps with my crew fanned behind me — Sefa on my left, Dez favoring his side, Pip small and watchful, Ilya last, hood up, hands loose. "You remember how to say my brother's name, or has practice worn it smooth?"

Something flickered behind the warmth. There it was. I'd spent six years imagining this moment, and in every version I'd shouted, or broken, or begged. In none of them had I been this calm, and the calm frightened me more than the rifles did.

"Tomas was a tragedy," Cass said, spreading his free hand. "A tragedy I mourned. But grief makes people careless with blame, Lena. You know what he was chasing. You know what happens to people who chase it." His eyes moved past me, counting my crew, pricing them. "You're wet, exhausted, and holding cargo that's worth nothing except trouble. Hand it over. Walk away rich. Every one of you. Triple what I paid, and no debts — Dez, I'll even burn those notes of yours myself."

Beside me, Dez went very still. Money had been the hook the first time, too. I watched him look at it — really look — and then look at Pip, and turn his head slowly side to side.

"You came all this way," Cass continued, "through my river, in my season, to deliver paper to a magistrate who takes my money twice a year. You think he'll read it? You think anyone down here reads anything?" He stepped closer, umbrella tilting to shed a sheet of water. "Or I can make this simple. Give me the satchel, and everyone walks. Refuse—" he sighed, genuinely regretful, a performance he'd rehearsed in mirrors, "—and I'm afraid the river's been very hungry this monsoon. Boats sink. People vanish. It's practically tradition."

"You're right," I said. "It is tradition."

His smile thinned. "Excuse me?"

"Tomas wrote it all down." I kept my voice level, pitched to carry — not just to Cass but to the station house, to the lit windows where shapes had begun to gather behind glass. "Every shipment, every payment, every official on your list. He mailed copies ahead of himself because he knew he might not make it. And he hid the originals somewhere even you couldn't buy your way to. You've spent six years burning everything he touched, Cass. You missed the chapel."

For the first time, the umbrella tilted. Just slightly. Just enough for the rain to hit his face, and for me to see it land.

"You don't have anything," he said softly.

"I have his handwriting. I have dates and names and amounts, including the payments to the constable who filed the report saying Tomas's death was an accident. Including the payments to the judge who jailed Ilya Vetrov's partner on charges you wrote yourself." I heard Ilya shift behind me, silent as rising water. "You didn't ruin a stranger, Cass. You ruined a river pilot named Renna Osei, who taught half the pilots on this water how to read a flood. Ask any of them whose route you stole. They remember. Rivers remember. That's the problem with rivers — everything floats downstream eventually."

"Enough." The warmth was gone now, stripped clean off his voice, and beneath it was the thing I'd always known lived there. He lifted two fingers, a small bored gesture, and his men began to spread along the dock. "Last chance. The satchel."

"Come take it."

And that was when Ilya gave the word.

* * *

It happened fast, the way water happens.

Behind us, our engine roared to life — Dez had never shut it down, had been standing at the throttle this entire time with his hand on it, bleeding patience. Our boat surged off the steps and swung out into the channel, empty, a decoy running dark toward the bend. Cass's men spun toward it, rifles coming up, and the gunboat's searchlight snapped away from us to chase it—

—and the gunboat's own engines screamed to full reverse as her pilot, following three-year-old charts, cut across the north channel to intercept.

The river rose up and took her.

I heard it more than saw it: the long grinding shriek of a hull meeting sand at speed, the lurch, the shouts, the mast of the searchlight whipping sideways and painting the rain gold as the boat slewed hard aground, listing, stuck fast on a bar that wasn't supposed to be there. Her pilot's voice carried across the water, raw with disbelief: *"Bar! Bar, she's on a bar—"*

"Get them inside!" someone shouted from the station house, and the door banged open — two constables, rain-slicked, rifles out, suddenly very interested in the scene. Behind them, a tall gray figure in a magistrate's coat descended the steps into the light, unhurried, watching.

Cass turned back to me, and for one naked moment there was nothing on his face but calculation failing. Four men on the dock. Two constables. A magistrate. A grounded gunboat screaming behind him. The arithmetic collapsing in real time.

"Lena," he said, low, only for me. "Whatever he told you about Renna, whatever paper you think you have — hand it to me and I disappear. You'll never see me again. That's worth more than a trial. Trials lose papers. Trials lose witnesses. You know how this country works."

"I used to," I said. "Then I stopped running."

I pulled the satchel from under my coat and held it out — not to him. Past him. To the tall figure coming down the steps through the rain.

"Magistrate Okonjo," I said. "My name is Lena Thornbury. These are the original ledgers of the Merrow line, copied by my brother Tomas Thornbury before he was murdered for them. There are witnesses waiting downriver in towns you've never been sent to protect. And there's a pilot standing behind me who can tell you exactly who wrote the charges against Renna Osei."

Cass moved. I'll give him that — he was fast for a man his age, and six years of fear made me flinch, made my hand start to rise, made the world narrow to the space between us—

But Sefa was faster, and Dez's boathook was longer, and Ilya simply stepped into his path like a fact of geography, and Cass Merrow found himself held at the bottom of the magistrate's steps by three people who had every reason to hate him and none left to fear him.

The constables took his arms. He didn't struggle. He looked at me over their shoulders, and the last thing he said was almost conversational, almost admiring:

"Your brother would be proud. That's the worst part, isn't it? That he'd be proud of *this.*"

"Yes," I said. "It is."

They walked him up the steps into the light, and the rain kept falling, and the satchel passed out of my hands for the first time in six years, and I discovered that my knees had been locked the whole time because when I finally let them bend, they nearly went.

Sefa caught my elbow. Pip caught the other side. Between them, they walked me to the top of the landing, where the floodlights made the rain look like falling stars.

Behind us, out in the dark, the gunboat groaned on its sandbar like something dying.

Ahead, through the station house window, I could see the ledgers open on a desk, the magistrate's lamp bent over them, page after page after page turning.

And turning.

And turning.
