## 20. Delivering the Dead Man's Evidence

The storm broke at dawn, and Sefa had never been so grateful for anything in her life.

She stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel's Ghost* — Ilya's boat, Ilya's name, though by now it belonged to all of them — and watched the rain thin from a solid gray wall into long silver curtains, and then into nothing but a dripping hush. The river ran high and brown and fast beneath them, swollen with everything the monsoon had dumped into it over three days, carrying branches and roof tin and one entire floating outhouse past them toward the delta. The sky above the eastern hills was the color of a bruise going yellow at the edge.

Behind her, in the stern, Lena slept sitting up against the mast step with Tomas's letter folded open on her knee. She'd read it four times through the night. Sefa knew because she'd counted — counted the way she counted everything now, channels and sandbars and hours since Dez had last been conscious, hours since they'd pulled the canvas patch tight enough to hold. Counting was how she kept the fear in its box.

Dez lay under the tarp amidships, feverish, the wound in his shoulder wrapped in boiled cloth that Pip changed every two hours whether he needed it or not. He'd woken once, near midnight, when the lightning turned the whole world white, and asked in a slurred voice if they were winning. Pip had told him yes. It wasn't even a lie.

"Magistrate's station is forty minutes," Ilya said, coming forward to stand beside her at the wheel. He smelled of engine oil and river water and he hadn't slept either; nobody had. "Past the bend you can see the tower. Red roof."

"I see it in my head already," Sefa said. "I've seen it every hour since Blackwater Bend."

"That's navigation." Almost a smile. His face didn't do much else; grief had carved him down to essentials years ago, same as it had done to Lena. "Navigation is seeing a place before you get there."

"What's it called, then? Seeing a place after?"

He considered this with the seriousness he gave everything. "Home," he said.

Sefa looked at him. He looked back at the river, unbothered, as if he hadn't just said something enormous, and reached past her to adjust the throttle a quarter inch, easing them out of the main current where the floodwater ran like a freight train.

They came around the bend at half past six, and there it was: the magistrate's station, a squat stone building on a bluff above the water, red-roofed, flying the regional pennant gone dark and heavy with rain. A dock below it. Two government skiffs tied up, and a third — sleeker, black-hulled, riding low —

Sefa's stomach dropped before her mind caught up.

"Cass," she said.

Lena was awake instantly. She came forward without a word, took one look at the black boat, and Sefa watched her do the thing she did — the thing she'd done since Tomas died, the thing that had kept them alive and moving and alone for seven years. The calculation. Every exit weighed. The river ahead, the shore behind, the storm-swollen channels where a smaller boat might lose a gunboat in the flooded forest.

"No," Sefa said quietly.

Lena looked at her.

"You're done calculating," Sefa said. Her voice shook and she let it. "We're all done. That's the whole point of what we're doing here. You taught us that — no, don't look surprised, you did. Seven years of running, and you finally picked a direction. This is it. We don't turn around at the last hundred meters because a boat makes you nervous."

"He has men," Lena said. "He has guns. And we have ledgers and a wounded mechanic and—" She stopped. Pressed her hand flat against the wheel housing, against the boat, as if steadying herself on something that couldn't leave. "I promised Tomas I'd keep you all alive."

"Tomas is dead," Sefa said, and hated how it sounded, and said it anyway, because it was true and truth was the cargo now. "And keeping us alive meant running until running was all we were. He didn't die so we could spend our whole lives being ghosts. He died because somebody had to say stop. So we're saying it. All of us. Ask them."

Lena looked aft. Pip stood at the rail, soaked, hollow-eyed, holding the oilcloth bundle of ledgers against their chest like a child. Dez was asleep again, or unconscious; but his good hand had found the bundle's corner in the night and hadn't let go, even in fever, even now.

"They already voted," Pip said. "Back at the cache. You just weren't listening."

For a long moment Lena said nothing. Rain dripped off the canopy. The black boat sat at the dock below the station, and men moved on its deck, unhurried, loading crates — Cass Merrow tying off loose ends, getting ready to run now that the storm had passed and the evidence hadn't arrived.

Then Lena laughed. It was a small, cracked sound, nothing like her usual dry humor, and she wiped her face with the back of her hand and said, "Fine. Fine. Bring us in, Ilya. Dockside, port side to. If anyone shoots at my crew, run them down."

"Yes, captain," Ilya said, and this time he did smile.

* * *

It went almost wrong immediately, which Sefa supposed was the most honest kind of almost.

They came alongside the dock at a quarter to seven, and Cass Merrow was waiting at the top of the ramp with four men and the easy, expensive smile Sefa remembered from a lifetime ago, from contract negotiations and riverside handshakes and the year before everything. He wore dry clothes. Of course he did. Cass always wore dry clothes while everyone else drowned.

"Lena Thornbury," he called down, warm as brandy. "You made it. I was starting to worry — monsoon season, these rivers, anything could happen to a crew out here. Anything at all." His eyes flicked to the oilcloth bundle in Pip's arms, and the warmth didn't change at all, which was how Sefa knew exactly how cold he was underneath. "Come inside. Dry off. We'll settle accounts."

"We settled accounts at Blackwater Bend," Lena said, climbing the ramp with her chin up and her hands empty and visible. Sefa flanked her left, Ilya right, Pip behind with the bundle. "You sank our boat. You left a child and a wounded man to drown in a monsoon. That was your settlement."

Cass sighed, theatrical, pained. "There's been a misunderstanding—"

"There's been a ledger," Lena said. "Several. Originals, copied, cross-referenced, witnessed. Everything your operation burned in the towns you own, everything the courts never saw because the witnesses drowned or disappeared or got bought — it's all in this bundle, Cass. And it's about to be in that building." She nodded past him, toward the station doors. "So you have a choice. Step aside, or make this the day you finally give the law a reason it can't ignore."

Something shifted in Cass's face. Not fear — Sefa had expected fear and was disappointed — but recalculation, fast and fluid, a trader repricing goods. His men shifted too, hands drifting toward jackets.

And that was when the dock started filling up.

They came out of the town the way water comes through a broken levee — quietly at first, then all at once. An old woman with a market basket. Two fishermen still wringing out their nets. A boy Sefa recognized from the resupply town, the one who'd carried their fuel cans. Then more. A dozen. Thirty. People who had lost boats to Cass's "tolls," people who had lost family to his routes, people who had simply watched for years and said nothing because saying nothing was how you stayed breathing. They lined the rail of the dock and the path to the station and they didn't shout. They just stood there, in the dripping morning, looking at Cass Merrow with faces that had stopped being afraid.

The old woman from the river town — Marta, the one who'd given them Tomas's letter — stood at the front. She looked small and immovable as an anchor stone.

"The magistrate sent word downriver after the storm," she said, in the tone of someone reporting weather. "Asked if anyone had business with the court. First time in nineteen years anybody official asked." She looked at Cass. "We have business."

Cass's smile held, but Sefa watched his eyes move along the crowd, counting, pricing, finding the arithmetic had turned against him. His men looked at each other. Guns are for crowds that scatter; nobody had told these people, and they weren't scattering.

"You'll regret this," Cass said to Lena, quietly, and for the first time the brandy warmth was gone and the voice underneath was just a voice, medium-sized, mortal. "Whatever you think you've won—"

"I won nothing," Lena said. "Tomas won it. Ilya's partner won it. Marta's son won it. I'm just the delivery service." She stepped past him, up the ramp, and Cass's men parted because thirty people were watching and some things are heavier than paychecks. "Magistrate's expecting us."

* * *

Inside, the station smelled of wet stone and old paper. The magistrate himself was younger than Sefa expected — forty maybe, tired, with ink-stained fingers and the wary look of a man who'd taken this posting knowing what it was: exile to the edge of the map, where the law was a rumor and the rumor was owned. He'd cleared his desk. There was tea, absurdly, actually tea, and he poured it himself with hands that weren't quite steady.

"Merrow's men tried to file a complaint an hour ago," he said. "Claiming piracy. Against your vessel." He glanced at the bundle Pip set down on his desk with the reverence of an offering. "I told them I'd hear all complaints in order of arrival. Yours appears to be first."

"It's not a complaint," Lena said. "It's evidence. Ledgers covering eleven years of Merrow's shipping operation — tolls, protection payments, cargo manifests for contraband, payments to officials. Originals, hidden by my brother, Tomas Thornbury, who was killed gathering them. Copies were made and distributed to three locations before we ever set foot on your dock. Destroy these, and the copies surface anyway." She laid the letter beside the ledgers, unfolded to the last page, where Tomas's handwriting marched in neat, stubborn lines. "His instructions. His witnesses' names. He planned for you, Magistrate. He just died before he could knock on your door."

The magistrate opened the first ledger and was quiet for a long time. Outside, through the shuttered window, Sefa could see the crowd on the dock, unmoving, growing. Word traveled fast in river towns. Word had been traveling for eleven years, with nowhere to land. Now it had somewhere to land.

"Names in here," the magistrate said at last, turning a page. His voice had changed — steadier, harder, the ink-stained fingers suddenly purposeful. "Customs officers. A harbor master. Two names from the regional capital." He looked up. "Do you understand what happens next? Because I will tell you honestly: I am one magistrate with four constables, and this man has owned this river longer than I've held a commission."

"Look outside," Sefa said.

He looked. The crowd had spilled off the dock onto the bluff now, and more were coming down the road — Sefa saw carts, saw the glint of rain gear, saw faces she knew from a dozen stops up and down the water. The fisherman whose boat Cass's men had scuttled. The ferry widow. The boy's whole family, apparently.

"Nineteen years," the magistrate said softly. "And it takes a dead man's ledgers and a monsoon." He closed the ledger and squared it on his desk like a man squaring accounts. "Constables! Witness statements, starting now. And send two men to the dock to take Mr. Merrow into custody — pending charges of smuggling, extortion, bribery, and the murder of Tomas Thornbury."

The words went through Sefa like current through water. Murder. Said aloud, official, in a room that mattered. Seven years of a wound she'd carried secondhand — Lena's wound, really, worn so long it had become the crew's — and someone in authority had finally spoken its name.

Outside, they heard it happen. No fight. Cass Merrow, who had sunk boats and bought magistrates and left children to drown, was taken off his own black boat between two constables with his hands cuffed in front of him, arguing in a voice that got higher as it went. The crowd didn't cheer. River people don't waste breath on cheering. They just watched, every one of them, making sure, the way you watch a rope hold.

Marta stood closest. When they brought him up the bluff road past her, she spat at his feet, precisely, without anger, and walked away.

* * *

By noon the station had become something none of them had ever seen: a courtroom where the river got to talk.

Witnesses came in a slow, steady procession — the fisherman, the ferry widow, a barge hand who'd signed false manifests under threat, a customs clerk who arrived trembling with a satchel of receipts he'd kept hidden in his mother's floor for six years. The magistrate wrote until his pen ran dry and borrowed another. Statements stacked up. Names in the ledgers matched names in the testimony, over and over, the way a key matches a lock, until the lock gave.

Sefa stood on the veranda most of the afternoon with Ilya, watching the river run gold in the post-storm light. Dez was upstairs in the infirmary, awake at last, complaining about the bandages, which everyone agreed was the healthiest sign yet. Pip had fallen asleep in a chair in the witness room, still holding the empty oilcloth, unwilling to surrender it even emptied.

"Your partner," Sefa said. "The one they jailed on falsified charges. With Cass in custody, the charges come apart. The magistrate can reopen it."

Ilya was quiet a moment. "Rosa. Yes." He turned his cup in his hands. "Eleven years I've been telling myself the river would handle it. The river handles everything except the things that matter." He looked out at the water, brown and high and glittering. "I'll go to the capital when the roads open. File everything. Take the boat up to fetch her myself, if they release her."

"She'll want to see the boat."

"She'll want to yell at me for letting it leak." Something eased in his face, briefly, like sun through cloud. "That's fair. I did let it leak."

Inside, through the open window, they heard the magistrate reading a charge aloud, and a voice answering, and the scratch of the pen. Imperfect, Sefa thought. Slow. One magistrate, four constables, a river full of wounds, and trials still to come, appeals, money, lawyers from the capital. Cass Merrow would fight. Men like him always fought longest in the paperwork.

But the ledgers were real. The witnesses were real. The dead man's handwriting was real, and it was everywhere now, in triplicate, in official hands, unstoppable as floodwater.

Justice, Sefa thought, watching the light move on the river. It doesn't arrive like weather. It arrives like a river — late, muddy, carrying everything with it, taking forever, and then, all at once, it's here.

Lena came out onto the veranda as the sun dropped low. She looked lighter than Sefa had seen her in seven years — not happy, not yet, maybe not for a long time, but unburdened in some structural way, like a boat that had finally put down ballast it no longer needed. She leaned on the rail beside them and watched the water together, the three of them, saying nothing, because nothing needed saying.

Then Pip burst through the door behind them, wide awake, waving a telegram flimsy with one hand and grinning like the monsoon had never happened.

"You have to see this," Pip said. "Dez is reading it out loud and he keeps laughing and it's scaring the constables."

Lena raised an eyebrow. "What is it?"

Pip handed her the flimsy, breathless. "It's from the harbormaster at Millbrook. About the strip. The lease."

Lena read it. Read it again. And then she did something Sefa had never once seen her do in all the years of running — she folded the paper carefully, put it in her pocket, and smiled like a woman with somewhere to be.

"Well," Lena said. "Everyone in the boat."
