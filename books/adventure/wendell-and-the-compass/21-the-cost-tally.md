## 21. The Cost Tally

The rain had stopped.

That was the first strange thing Pip noticed, three days after the magistrate's men took Cass Merrow away in irons — the rain had actually stopped, and the sky over the river town had gone a pale washed blue, like something scrubbed clean and hung out to dry. For weeks the monsoon had been the whole world: water falling from the sky, water rising from the banks, water seeping through everything they owned. Now the sun came through in long slanting bars, and steam rose off the landing boards, and Pip stood on the dock with a mug of tea going cold in her hands and didn't quite know what to do with all that light.

She'd gotten used to the dark. That was the thing nobody warned you about — that you could get used to anything, even the thing that was trying to kill you.

Below her, at the waterline, the *Kestrel's Grace* sat half-sunk against the pilings, held up by ropes and stubbornness. She was done. Everyone knew it. Dez had spent two days down in her belly anyway, poking at the wound near the stern where the gunboat's wake and the flooded country's rocks had finished what Cass's men started, and this morning he'd finally climbed out, wiped his hands on a rag that was more grease than rag, and said the words out loud.

"She's not worth fixing."

Nobody had argued. That was the strangest part of all of it, maybe. The *Grace* had been theirs for six years — longer than Pip had been crew, though she'd been aboard since she was fourteen and now she was seventeen and she'd grown up on those decks the way other kids grew up in houses. The boat had a name painted on the transom in Sefa's careful hand, and a dent in the port rail from the time Dez misjudged a lock gate, and a burn mark on the galley table from the night Tomas had tried to teach Pip to cook and set the onions on fire instead. Every plank of her was a memory, and now she was a wreck with a rope around her bow, and nobody argued, because arguing wouldn't change the arithmetic.

Pip drank her cold tea and did the arithmetic herself, because that was what she did now. Somebody had to keep the tally. It might as well be the youngest.

**Dez:** one shoulder torn open by shrapnel when the gunboat's first shot went wide and hit the engine housing instead of the engine, which Dez maintained was the boat taking the bullet for him. Stitched twice, once by Sefa with fishing line because the town doctor was drunk, once properly two days later. He couldn't lift his right arm above his chest yet. He kept trying anyway, and Sefa kept smacking his hand down, and it had become a kind of ritual between them, almost affectionate.

**Sefa:** nothing you could stitch. She'd navigated them through the drowned forest at night with a map drawn from memory and Ilya's shouted corrections, and she'd done it perfectly, and then she'd stood on the magistrate's landing while witnesses came forward out of the crowd — dockworkers, ferrymen, an old woman named Amara who'd given them Tomas's letter — and heard them say things about Cass Merrow that Sefa had known in her bones for years but never expected anyone to say out loud. Pip had watched her stand there very still, listening, like a woman hearing a language she'd forgotten she spoke. Since then Sefa had been quiet in a new way. Not the old quiet, the flight-quiet. Something else. Pip thought it might be the sound of a person setting down a weight.

**Ilya:** he didn't talk about costs. He talked about currents. But his partner Renn was still in jail two hundred miles south, and the magistrate's clerks moved at the speed of wet paper, and every morning Ilya walked to the magistrate's station to ask, and every morning he came back and said "soon" in a voice that meant he didn't believe it yet. His boat — the one they'd limped ashore on, the one that used to belong to his friend Dario whom Cass had ruined — was holed below the waterline too. Two wrecks tied to the same pilings, like the river collecting its debts from everyone at once.

**Lena:** Pip saved her for last, because Lena was hardest to count. On the surface, nothing. She flew the mail run yesterday, short hop downriver and back, first time since the storm, and landed smooth as silk and smiled at the dockhands. But Pip had found her at dawn twice now, sitting in the *Grace*'s cabin — you could still get in if you climbed over the rail and the floor tilted enough that you had to brace your feet — reading Tomas's letter. The same letter. Over and over. Pip knew because she'd read it too, once, and once was enough; some things you carry better folded than unfolded. Lena read it like a woman doing sums. *What did it cost you, Tom? What did it cost us? What do we owe now?*

And **Pip**, herself, own line of the ledger: she'd nearly gone overboard at Blackwater Bend, and Sefa had caught her wrist so hard the bruise was still there, yellow-green now, fading into the shape of fingers. She kept touching it. Not because it hurt. Because it was proof. She'd been on the crew four years and this was the first time she'd understood, all the way down, what "crew" meant — that it meant someone's hand closing around your wrist in the dark with the river trying to take you, and not letting go even when it would've been easier to let go.

She was still doing the sums when Dez came up the dock stairs, moving carefully, his arm in a sling Sefa had made from an old chart.

"You're up early," he said.

"Couldn't sleep." Pip looked at the wreck below them. "It's weird without the engine noise. I keep hearing it anyway. Like a phantom limb."

Dez grunted and lowered himself onto the dock edge beside her, wincing. "Forty-one days," he said. "That's how long I've been on that boat. Six years total, but forty-one days without stopping, counting the flooded country. You know what I thought about? A bed. Just a bed. A real mattress in a room that doesn't move." He shook his head slowly. "And now there's beds everywhere and I can't sleep either. Too quiet."

"Too quiet," Pip agreed.

They sat with that. Downriver a barge horn sounded, low and mournful, and somewhere a dog started up answering it, and the ordinary noises of a morning that wasn't trying to kill them filled in around the silence the way water fills a hull.

"Debts are settled," Dez said after a while. It wasn't casual. Pip heard what was under it — the reason he'd voted yes on Cass's contract, the hidden debts, the shame he'd carried alone for months because he couldn't bear to tell Lena that he'd borrowed against the *Grace* herself, against the engine, against their whole floating life, from a lender in Port Callow who'd have taken the boat out from under them. "Magistrate's clerk confirmed it this morning. The forfeiture papers got voided when Merrow's operation got seized. Turns out my lender was on Merrow's payroll. Whole thing just—" he made a gesture with his good hand, opening his fingers, letting something invisible go, "—dissolved."

"That's good," Pip said. "That's really good."

"Yeah." He didn't sound good. He sounded hollowed. "You know what the worst part is? Not the debt. The lying. Four months, looking Lena in the eye every morning, knowing I'd voted us into that run because I was scared. And it turns out the run was a trap, and my debts were bait, and I handed them the hook myself." His jaw worked. "Lena says it doesn't matter. Says Merrow would've found another way in. Maybe true. But I'll know. I'll always know I was the open door."

"Dez." Pip turned to face him. She was seventeen and he was thirty-eight and she'd never once talked to him like an equal, but the river had rearranged things; the flood had flattened everybody to the same level. "You patched that hull in the middle of the night with canvas and prayer while we took on water. You got shot and kept the engine running until the engine literally couldn't anymore. If you're an open door, you're also the guy who nailed it shut behind us. Both things are true. That's the tally."

Dez looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed — a short, cracked, surprised laugh, the first one Pip had heard out of him since before Blackwater Bend. "Canvas and prayer," he repeated. "Yeah. Lot of prayer in that engine room. God and I aren't speaking currently, but we had a good run during the monsoon."

Down the dock, Sefa appeared with two more mugs of tea and handed them out without being asked, because Sefa always knew, somehow, when people needed tea and never needed to be told. She sat down on Pip's other side and looked at the wreck for a while.

"I called the salvage yard," she said. "They'll give us eighty for the hull. Eighty." She said it flatly, the way you'd report a death. "Six years of our lives. Eighty."

"Take it?" Pip asked.

"Take it." Sefa blew on her tea. "A grave shouldn't cost money to dig. Let them pay *us*." She sipped. "But I'm keeping the name plate. And the bell. And the compass — the actual brass one, the one Tomas used. That comes off before the yard touches her tomorrow."

"The compass," Pip said softly. The title of it all, almost — *Wendell and the Compass* was a story Sefa told sometimes, late at night, about a man named Wendell who sailed with a broken compass that pointed not north but toward whatever he'd lost, and how that was either a curse or the only honest instrument ever made. Pip had always thought it was just a story. Now she wasn't sure. Her own compass felt broken lately, pointing at everything they'd lost instead of anywhere forward.

"Lena signed something yesterday," Sefa said, changing course the way she changed course, sudden and sure. "At the magistrate's station. You should hear it from her, but I'll tell you it's not leaving. Whatever it is, it's not another contract and it's not a bill of sale."

Pip's stomach did something complicated. Leaving. The word had been the crew's whole religion for four years — *never stay long enough to be found* — and now here was Sefa saying *not leaving* like it was weather, like it was simply a fact arriving on schedule.

"Is that good?" Pip asked.

"I think," Sefa said slowly, "it's the first thing in six years that might be."

They found Lena at midday, up at the airstrip — or what passed for one, a mowed field above the town with a windsock faded to pink and a fuel drum on a wooden cradle. She was standing next to the plane talking to a surveyor type with a clipboard, and when she saw the three of them coming up the path she dismissed him with a nod and met them halfway.

Her face was doing something Pip had never seen it do. It took a second to identify it, because it was so unfamiliar: Lena Thornbury was relaxed. Not the performing kind of relaxed she did in towns, the charm that kept creditors and collectors at a friendly distance. Actually relaxed. Shoulders down. Eyes soft.

"So Sefa told you," Lena said.

"Told us what?" Dez asked, though his tone said he already suspected.

"I leased the strip." Lena jerked her thumb at the mowed field, the windsock, the fuel drum. "Five-year lease. There's a hangar that needs a roof and a bunkhouse that needs everything, and the rent is cheap because nobody else wants land that floods every spring." She smiled — small, real. "We'd know how to handle that, I figure."

Nobody said anything for a second. Pip watched Dez's face do the same thing hers felt like it was doing — hope and grief fighting for the same square inch.

"Roots," Dez said finally. "You want us to put down roots. You. Lena Thornbury. The woman who taught me the rule — *never stay long enough to be found.*"

"I'm revising the rule." Lena looked out at the field, the river beyond it, the wreck tied at the pilings below. "Tomas died running toward something instead of away from it, and I've spent six years getting that backwards. Running away from everything. You don't get to choose what finds you if you never stop moving. You just stay lucky until you're not." She turned back to them. "Cass is in custody. The ledgers are with the magistrate. The towns along this river know our names now, and for the first time in my life that feels like safety instead of exposure. These people fed us. Hid us. Gave us a dead man's letter. I'm not flying out on them. I'm done flying out on people."

"And if somebody comes for us?" Pip asked quietly. "Somebody always comes."

"Then they'll find us home," Lena said, "with friends. That's the whole difference, Pip. That's the entire difference in the world."

Dez put out his good hand and Lena took it, and then Sefa, and then Pip, and it wasn't a speech and it wasn't an oath — they'd sworn oaths over a grave once and Pip didn't think any of them wanted another — it was just hands, calloused and greasy and stitched and bruised, holding on for a second longer than a handshake needs.

That evening they stripped the *Grace*. It was awful and it was right. Dez, one-armed, directed from a deck chair like a dying admiral while Pip and Sefa and Ilya carried things ashore: tools, charts, the galley kettle, the tin star Pip had nailed over her bunk at fourteen. The bell came off last, and Sefa rang it once — one clear note rolling across the water in the gold evening light —
