## 22. Anchored

The rain stopped on a Tuesday.

Lena noticed it the way you notice a toothache ending — a slow, disbelieving easing of pressure she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it wasn't part of her body. She stood in the doorway of the magistrate station's guest quarters with a tin cup of coffee going cold in her hands and watched the clouds tear open over the river, and for the first time in eleven days the water ran silver instead of brown.

Behind her, Sefa was asleep sitting up, which was a trick only navigators and new mothers could manage. Pip was asleep everywhere at once, folded into a chair like laundry. Dez had been moved to the infirmary two nights ago, his shoulder sewn and splinted, complaining about the thread count of the sheets until the painkillers took him under mid-sentence.

And out front, in the flooded yard where the magistrate's men had strung lanterns against the dark, Cass Merrow sat in the back of a prison launch under guard, wrists cuffed, watching the same sky. He hadn't said a word since they'd brought him in. Lena had walked past him once, close enough to touch, and he'd looked at her with an expression she'd spent years trying to imagine and now found she couldn't have guessed in a hundred tries. It wasn't hatred. It was something smaller than that — the look of a man doing arithmetic he kept losing.

She didn't give him the satisfaction of stopping. Tomas would have wanted her to say something cutting, probably. But Tomas had also died believing the courts would never reach a man like Cass Merrow, and here was the proof that he'd been wrong about one thing, at least, and it seemed kinder to let him be wrong about the other too.

The coffee was terrible. She drank it anyway, standing in the doorway, letting the clean air move through the room like it owned the place.

* * *

They flew out three days later, but not far.

The regional strip at Kalvero was a scar of crushed coral and mud half a mile from the river landing, and the *Wren* — what was left of the boat Ilya had steered through the drowned country with canvas on her ribs and prayer in her engine — sat beached on the bank like something the river had coughed up and regretted. Dez stood over her with his arm in a sling and pronounced her dead with the flat authority of a doctor who has tried everything.

"Ribs are gone. Keel's cracked in four places. She took more water than she ever held fuel." He patted the hull anyway, gently, the way you'd touch a coffin. "She got us here. That's the whole obituary."

"She deserves better than an obituary," Pip said. "She deserves a plaque."

"She deserves to be stripped for parts," Dez said, "and every rivet of her is going into whatever we build next."

That word — *next* — hung in the air longer than it should have. Nobody picked it up. They all heard it land somewhere soft and stayed quiet around it, the way you stay quiet around a bird that might startle.

Because *next* had always meant *away*. Next strip, next contract, next town where nobody knew their faces. Eleven years of *next*, ever since Tomas died and the crew had sworn over his grave that they'd keep moving until moving was all they were. Lena had built the rule herself: never stay long enough to be found, never land twice in the same place in a season, never let a place grow roots around you because roots are how grief gets its hooks in. They'd flown medicine and gossip across half the interior on that rule, and it had worked, if you called survival working, if you called a life measured in departures a life.

But the rule had a flaw she'd only just seen, standing in the wreckage of everything: running hadn't saved Tomas. Running hadn't saved them from Cass. Running had just made sure that when the blow finally came, they'd take it alone, on open water, with no one ashore who knew their names well enough to come looking.

The river towns had come looking anyway. That was the thing she kept turning over at night. The old woman in the resupply town who'd pressed Tomas's last letter into Sefa's hands — she'd kept it eleven years for strangers she'd never met. The fishermen who'd rowed out through the tail of the storm to point the magistrate's men toward Blackwater Bend. The witnesses who'd walked into the station one by one, farmers and ferrymen and a woman who ran a fuel dock, each of them saying Cass Merrow's name out loud for the first time in their lives because a battered crew of fliers had shown them it could be done. The law hadn't reached this country. But the people had. The people had been reaching for each other the whole time, and Lena's crew had spent a decade flying above it all, untouchable, unattached, alone.

She was tired of being untouchable. It turned out untouchable was just another word for unheld.

* * *

The strip came up for lease on a Thursday, which felt like the kind of joke the universe told when it thought it was being subtle.

"It's not much," said the land agent, a leathery woman named Okonjo who'd flown supply runs back when the interior strips were carved by hand, and who looked at Lena's ship — patched, salt-stained, one prop blade replaced with a mismatched spare — with the frank appraisal of someone reading a biography off the airframe. "Two hangars, one of them honest. A bunkhouse that leaks in exactly one corner. Fuel storage that'll need recertifying before you trust it. And the best approach light in three hundred miles of river, because the old man who built it loved this strip more than his own children."

"Why's it available?"

Okonjo shrugged. "He died. His kids live coastside and think the interior is a rumor." She handed over the paperwork without ceremony. "Month to month to start. I'm not sentimental about tenants. Stay or go, the strip doesn't care."

But Lena cared. That was the whole revelation, arriving on cheap government paper with a carbon copy underneath. She read the terms twice, then a third time, and her hand hovered over the signature line the way it used to hover over departure checklists — except this was the opposite of a checklist. This was the list of reasons to stop.

She signed it in the shade of the good hangar while the monsoon's last ragged clouds burned off the eastern hills, and her signature came out steadier than she expected, as if some part of her had signed weeks ago and the rest of her was only catching up.

Then she went to find her crew, because a lease was just paper until the people you loved agreed to be anchored by it.

* * *

She found Dez first, naturally, because Dez never left the machines. He was elbow-deep in the *Wren*'s dead engine, salvaging, and he didn't look up when Lena sat down on the cowling beside him.

"I heard about the strip," he said.

"Okonjo talks fast."

"Okonjo talks to everyone. Half the river will know by sundown." He pulled something free — a fuel pump, still good — and set it on the wing with the tenderness he showed only machinery. "You want my opinion."

"I want your vote. Same as the job with Cass. Majority rules."

Dez wiped his hands slowly on a rag that had started life white sometime in the previous decade. "When we voted to take that run, I voted yes because of my debts. You knew that. Everyone knew that. And I've been thinking — that's the first time in eleven years I voted scared instead of loyal, and look where it landed us." He flexed the shoulder inside the sling, wincing. "So here's my vote, and it's not scared. I'm tired of rebuilding engines in rented hangars where the landlord changes the locks. I'm tired of knowing every bolt I tighten is coming apart again in six months behind me. I want a floor that stays put under my feet." He looked at her then, and there was something raw under the grease and the gruffness. "I want to build something that lasts long enough to break my heart when it breaks. You know?"

"I know," Lena said, and meant it down to the bone.

"Sefa'll say yes but make you wait a day, so it doesn't look easy. Pip—" He almost smiled. "Pip's already unpacked. Kid's been sleeping in the bunkhouse for two nights. Did she tell you?"

"She did not."

"Kid's smarter than both of us."

* * *

Sefa made her wait two days, exactly as predicted, and then found Lena at dawn on the riverbank, where Lena had taken to walking the length of the landing with her coffee, learning the water the way she'd once learned weather charts — by attention, by repetition, by love.

"The strip's a bad idea," Sefa said, settling onto the piling beside her.

"Probably."

"No cover from the east wind. Approach over open water in flood season. Nearest real medical facility is a day's flight." She ticked the faults off like waypoints. "It's a terrible idea."

"You've made your case very thoroughly."

"I have." Sefa laced her fingers around one knee and watched the current slide past, brown-gold in the early light. "My grandmother had a saying. *A navigator who loves the sea too much will drown; one who loves it too little will never leave port.* I've been loving it too little for eleven years, Lena. Flying over everything. Charting exits out of every place before I've charted anything else." She turned, and her eyes were bright and steady. "I want to learn this river. All of it. Every sandbar, every bend, every channel Ilya knows. I want to draw the map properly for once — not a map of ways out. A map of home."

Lena looked at the water. When she spoke, her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Tomas used to talk about that. Staying somewhere long enough to know it."

"Tomas talked about a lot of things we weren't brave enough to do yet." Sefa bumped her shoulder gently against Lena's. "We're brave enough now. Apparently it only took a monsoon, a betrayal, a gunboat, and a dead man's ledgers."

"We're slow learners."

"The best ones always are."

* * *

Ilya took the longest, which surprised no one, least of all Lena.

He lived on his own boat still, moored at the downstream end of the landing, and he came and went like weather — present, useful, impossible to pin down. He'd helped them haul the *Wren* clear, helped Okonjo's crew restring the approach lights, helped the magistrate's clerks sort the ledgers into evidence bundles with a patience that startled everyone who'd seen him handle a throttle. But he kept his gear packed. Lena noticed. She'd spent eleven years noticing packed gear; it was a language she was fluent in.

She found him one evening on his deck, mending a net he clearly didn't need, and sat down across from him without asking. He glanced up, said nothing, went back to work. That was Ilya — conversation as a shared silence, offered freely.

"There's work here," Lena said eventually. "Real work. Medicine runs, mail, survey flights for the flood mapping the magistrate wants done. Sefa's already charting the channels. Dez needs hands he trusts in the hangar, and he trusts maybe three people alive, and you're two of them."

Ilya tied off a knot. "I don't stay places."

"I noticed."

"My partner sat in a cell for six years because I taught myself not to stay. Because I was always moving, always one bend ahead of whoever wanted to hurt us, and when they came for him, I wasn't there, and after, staying anywhere felt like painting a target." He set the net down. Out on the water, the evening birds were coming in low over the reeds. "Cass is in custody. The trial will happen, or it won't. Either way, the man who took six years from him can't take any more. So the reason I had for running is finished." He looked at her, and there was something almost amused in the weathered lines of his face. "Which leaves me with no reason to run and no reason to stay, and I find I don't know how to choose between nothing and nothing."

"Then don't choose," Lena said. "Stay badly. Stay the way we're all going to do it — badly, one month at a time, ready to change our minds. Okonjo's lease is month to month. Nobody's chaining anyone to a piling." She paused. "But the bunkhouse leaks in one corner, and Dez says you're the only person whose patching he'd sign off on, so consider yourself conscripted regardless."

Ilya laughed — short, rusty, like an engine turning over after a long winter. "Conscripted."

"That's how belonging works. Nobody asks. They just hand you tools."

He was quiet a moment, looking at the river, at the strip lights beginning to glow amber down the bank, at the hangar where lamplight showed Dez's silhouette bent over the *Wren*'s bones. Then he nodded, once, the smallest possible commitment, and began coiling the net away.

"I'll need my own berth," he said. "For the boat."

"Done."

"And no one touches my charts."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

* * *

They raised the roof beam on the second hangar in the first week of the dry season, all five of them, hauling on ropes in the gold morning light while Okonjo supervised from a folding chair with the expression of a woman who had seen many crews promise to stay and was allowing herself, cautiously, to believe this one.

Pip painted the name on the new boat before it even had an engine — *Wren II*, in letters that leaned drunkenly to starboard, because Pip painted the way she flew, fast and confident and slightly crooked. Underneath, smaller, she added a line that made Sefa laugh and made Lena turn away quickly and pretend to inspect the rudder:

*She got us here.*

The bunkhouse leak got fixed. The fuel tanks got recertified. The first medicine run went out on schedule with Sefa navigating channels she'd drawn herself and Ilya riding along pointing out sandbars that had moved overnight, arguing cheerfully with her about every one of them. Dez rebuilt the *Wren*'s engine into the new boat, rivet by salvaged rivet, and swore the compression had never been better, and dared anyone to disagree.

And on an evening in late dry season, with the strip lights warming and the river running calm and silver, Lena stood at the edge of the landing where she'd once stood counting reasons to leave, and counted something else instead.

One hangar, honest. One bunkhouse, watertight. One mechanic with a floor that stayed put. One navigator drawing maps of home. One runner who'd stopped packing her bag at night. One pilot who'd traded his packed gear for a berth and a set of charts with a future in them. One brother, avenged, whose letter lay in a drawer in Lena's room, read so many times the creases had gone soft — and whose voice, in the last line, she could finally hear without flinching: *Come find the place worth staying for, Lee. It exists. I checked.*

She'd spent eleven years believing the moving was the price of keeping the people she loved safe. Standing there, with the smell of engine oil and river water and someone — Pip, unmistakably — burning dinner in the bunkhouse, Lena understood at last that it had been the opposite. The moving had been the cost. This — the staying, the leaking roofs, the arguments about sandbars, the burnt dinners, the names on the lease — this was what all those miles had been buying.

Footsteps came up the bank behind her. She didn't turn. She didn't need to. After everything, she knew the weight of every one of them by sound alone.

"Dinner's ruined," Sefa announced, arriving at her shoulder.

"Pip's cooking. Ruined was the forecast."

"Dez says if we eat it anyway, it builds character."

"Ilya's already eating it, isn't he."

"He says it builds character."

Lena laughed, and the sound went out over the water unafraid, and she turned at last from the river — not toward the sky, not toward the next strip, not toward anywhere at all — and walked home.
