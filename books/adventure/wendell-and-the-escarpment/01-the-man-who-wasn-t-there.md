## 1. The Man Who Wasn't There

The town was called Ferrow's Landing, though no one living could say who Ferrow had been or what he'd landed for. It sat at the base of the Escarpment the way a stone sits at the foot of a wall—unnoticed, permanent, slightly damp. The river ran through it brown and patient, carrying down silt and pine needles and, in the spring, things better not named. Kestrel Nakamura had lived there eleven months under the name Daniel Reyes, and in that time he had learned three things about being invisible: keep your eyes down, keep your hands busy, and never, ever let anyone see you look north.

The Escarpment filled the sky above the town like a held breath. On clear mornings its limestone face caught the first light and burned gold, and the whole valley seemed to tilt toward it, the way flowers tilt toward sun. Kestrel made a point of facing south when he drank his coffee on the boarding house porch. He read the weather off the backs of his hands instead. Old habit. The mountain had taken enough from him without getting to watch him flinch.

He worked now for Ovid Brann, who ran a boatyard and a fuel dock and a small empire of small favors. Kestrel patched hulls, hauled engines, cleared brush from the winter-drowned slips. The work asked nothing of him but his shoulders, which was exactly what he could afford to give. Once a tourist had watched him re-seat a propeller shaft with an improvised puller and said, *You're wasted here, man—you should be guiding.* Kestrel had smiled the smile he kept for such moments, flat as a coin, and said he got seasick.

That night he'd stood at the edge of the dark water behind the yard and listened to the river talk to itself, and he'd thought: *eleven months*. Eleven months since the inquiry. Eleven months since the photographs of the empty ledge, since the widow's face in the hearing room, since the word *abandoned* was spoken aloud by a man in a gray suit who had never been above the treeline in his life. Eleven months of being no one, which was a kind of punishment and also, if he was honest, a kind of mercy. A man who is no one cannot fail anyone else.

The rain came early that October, a fine gray sift that turned the sawdust paths to porridge. Kestrel spent the morning under the tarp shed, scraping antifouling off a dory while the radio played static and gospel through a speaker gone soft with damp. He liked the scraping. There was a rightness to it—the old paint coming away in long curls, the bare wood underneath honest and pale. Most things in his life felt like layers over something rotten. Here, at least, you could get down to the truth of a boat in an afternoon.

Brann came out around noon, wiping his hands on a rag that had once been white. "Fella asking after you," he said, too casually.

Kestrel's scraper stopped moving. Only for a half second, but Brann caught it; the old man missed nothing that happened on his water.

"Tourist?"

"Woman." Brann squinted out at the rain. "Came in on the morning bus, which nobody does. Asked at the store, asked at the church, asked at my office whether I knew a boatman named Reyes who'd worked mountains before." He looked at Kestrel then, level and unhurried. "I told her I didn't discuss my people."

Kestrel set the scraper down. His heart was doing something slow and cold, like a stone rolling over in deep water. "What did she look like?"

"Young. Thirties maybe. City coat, wrong boots for this mud. Carrying a canvas pack like it mattered more than her luggage." Brann folded his rag into quarters, unfolded it. "And she had a compass hanging off her wrist. Brass case, worn to shine. The kind of thing a man carries forty years before it looks like that."

The rain ticked against the tarp. Somewhere downriver a loon called, that sound like grief remembering itself.

"I don't know any woman," Kestrel said.

"No," Brann agreed. "I expect you don't." He turned to go, then paused. "For what it's worth, Reyes—I've hired men running from worse than whatever you're running from. But nobody runs forever along a river. Rivers all lead somewhere."

He went back inside. Kestrel stood a long time with his hand on the dory's gunwale, feeling the wood breathe under his palm, and did not look north.

* * *

She found him at dusk, because of course she did. He should have left that afternoon—he'd even packed the go-bag in his head, itemized it the way you do, sleeping bag, cash, the false papers taped under the floorboard—but his feet had refused the motion, some stubborn animal part of him tired of running from women with questions. So he was still at the yard when the light went blue and the rain thickened, and she came down the plank walk with a flashlight she wasn't using, as if she wanted to be seen coming.

Up close she was thinner than Brann's description, with the hollowed look of someone who hadn't slept properly in weeks. City coat, wrong boots, mud to the knee anyway. She stopped six feet away, out of arm's reach, which told him she'd thought about reach.

"Daniel Reyes," she said.

"That's me."

"My name is Mara Voss. My father is Aldous Voss. He's a surveyor." She lifted her wrist so the compass swung free, catching the last gray light. "This is his. He never goes anywhere without it. It came back to me four days ago in a parcel with no return address."

Kestrel wiped his hands slowly on his thighs. "I'm sorry to hear that. But I'm a boat mechanic."

"He sent it to me," she said, ignoring this, "because someone taught him how to get a parcel out of the high country fast and quiet. Someone who knew the routes, the caches, the people. Someone whose name came up once in my father's office years ago, before—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "Before whatever it was that made you disappear."

The rain filled the silence between them, drumming on the tarp, ticking off the hulls. Kestrel became aware of every exit at once: the plank walk, the slip road, the water itself. Eleven months of practice said *deny, deflect, vanish*. But there was something in her voice under the fear—not desperation exactly. Precision. She had come a long way on a thin thread, and she knew it was thin, and she was holding it anyway.

"Whoever your father is," Kestrel said, "he should have sent that parcel to the police."

"The police called off the official search nine days after the team went missing." Her jaw tightened. "Nine days. In monsoon country. They said weather risk. They said the passes were closing. My father has been going up that mountain for thirty years and they gave him nine days."

"Searches cost money. Insurance companies get nervous."

"This wasn't insurance." She stepped closer, boots sucking in the mud, and her voice dropped low and hard. "A man came to our house after the search was called off. Nice suit. Very polite. He told my mother it would be 'kindest to let the mountain keep them.' Those were his words. *Let the mountain keep them.* Like they were already dead. Like it was settled." Her hand closed around the compass until the knuckles whitened. "My father isn't dead, Mr. Reyes. Dead men don't mail parcels."

Kestrel looked at the compass. Brass, worn to shine, the bezel scarred where it had been dropped on rock a hundred times and forgiven. He knew that make. He knew the weight of it in a pocket, the small tug of it toward true north, the way a good instrument becomes an extension of the hand that trusts it. And he knew what it meant that Aldous Voss had sent it home: not a message of love. A message of intent. Surveyors send home their instruments when they mean to come back for them—or when they want someone to know where they've been.

"What was your father surveying?" he asked, and hated himself for asking, because asking was the first step onto a road he'd sworn off.

Mara exhaled, and some of the iron went out of her, and he saw how exhausted she actually was. "Boundary work. Upper Ashen valley. Corporate contract—we don't know whose, exactly. He took a team of five up three weeks ago. Two came down early with altitude sickness. Then nothing. No radio contact. The firm says equipment failure." She laughed, short and bitter. "Three radios failing at once."

The rain came harder. Out on the black water, the current pushed past the pilings with a low continuous rush, the sound of something enormous and indifferent going somewhere. Kestrel thought about the upper river. He had not let himself think about it in almost a year, and now it rose in him whole: the smell of cold spray in the gorge, the particular blue of glacial ice at dawn, the cairns the valley people built that were not cairns at all but something older. He thought about a ledge below a cornice and a rope he should have fixed and a client's voice fading into wind, and he shut the door on it, the way he did every night, and the door didn't hold the way it usually did.

"I can't help you," he said quietly.

"I haven't asked you to yet."

"You were about to."

"Yes." She reached into her coat and brought out an envelope, thick, and set it on the gunwale between them, weighted against the wind with a stone from the path. "That's five thousand dollars. It's most of what I have. I'm not asking you to climb anything. I'm asking you to go up the lower river as far as the gorge mouth and look. Look for signs of the team, their camps, their gear. Talk to whoever will talk. Three days, maybe four. If you find nothing, you come back and tell me the mountain keeps them, and I'll believe you, and I'll stop."

"And if I find something?"

Her eyes came up to his, and in the blue dusk they were very steady. "Then you'll know why a man in a nice suit told my mother to let the mountain keep them. And you can decide what kind of man lets that stand."

Kestrel looked at the envelope. Five thousand dollars was escape money. Five thousand dollars was a bus ticket south and a new name and another eleven months of scraped hulls and southward-facing porches. Everything in him that had survived the past year leaned away from it like a plant from shade.

But the compass lay on her wrist, needle swinging, finding north even in the dark, even in the rain, because that was what it was made to do. And beneath the drumming of the water he could hear, faint and absurd, the memory of his own voice from another life, telling a client: *the mountain doesn't hide anyone. People just stop looking.*

"Three days," he heard himself say. "Lower river only. I don't go above the gorge mouth. That's not negotiable."

Mara Voss nodded once, and for the first time since she'd walked down the plank walk, she looked less like a woman begging and more like a daughter certain of her father. "He'll be waiting," she said. "Whoever finds him."

Kestrel picked up the envelope. It was heavier than paper should be, or lighter—it was hard to tell anymore. Behind her, far up the valley, the clouds had torn open for a moment, and the Escarpment showed itself: vast, gilded by the last light, indifferent as God.

He turned his back on it and went to pack.
