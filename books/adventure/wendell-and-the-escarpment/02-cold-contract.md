## 2. Cold Contract

I didn't sleep after she left. That was the first cost, and I knew it would not be the last.

The woman—Mara Voss—had sat across my table for the better part of an hour with her father's compass between us, and I had said almost nothing worth saying. Now the lamp burned low and the river spoke through the floorboards of my shack, that constant gray murmur that had become, over three years, the only voice I trusted. I turned the compass over in my hands. Brass, dented at the bezel, a hairline crack across the crystal like a frozen lightning strike. On the back, scratched deep enough to survive decades of pockets: *A. VOSS — SURVEY DIVISION — PROPERTY OF THE CROWN.*

Property of the Crown. The words had a museum smell. Nobody surveyed for the Crown anymore; they surveyed for corporations with names like Meridian and Vantage Terra, firms that filed their maps in Singapore and their consciences nowhere at all. Aldous Voss had been surveying since before I was born, if the wear on this instrument meant anything. A man did not carry the same compass for thirty years unless he believed in what it pointed toward.

That was either very good or very bad news for whoever had gone missing with him.

I set it down and made myself think like the man I used to be—the one who read weather and water and human beings for a living, before I'd learned that the last of those was the one that killed you.

*The official search lasted nine days.* She'd said it flatly, the way people say things they've repeated until the grief wears smooth. Nine days for four men in high country above the Escarpment, in late season, with the monsoon six weeks out. I'd run searches. Nine days was what you gave a lost day-hiker with a cell signal. For a survey team working remote drainages, nine days was a press release, not an effort. Somebody had spent just enough money to be able to say money had been spent.

And then there was the way she'd found me. Not through the guide registry—I wasn't on it, hadn't been since the Tavan expedition, and the blacklist made sure no registered outfit would touch my name. Not through the town, where I was nobody, a quiet man who fixed generators and hauled freight and looked at his boots. She'd found me because someone had told her about me. Someone who knew what I'd been, and what I'd done, and decided I was still the right tool.

People don't hire disgraced guides because they want competence. They hire them because they want someone with nothing left to lose.

I lay down without undressing and watched the roof beams until the lamp died, and somewhere in the dark I admitted the thing I'd been refusing to admit since she walked in: I was going to say yes. Not because of her father. Not even because of her. Because of the compass needle, which had trembled once, when she first set it on the table, and settled pointing upstream—the way everything in my life lately seemed to point, whether I consented or not.

* * *

Morning came up the color of dishwater. I found her at the dock café, the only place in Corren's Landing with more than one kind of tea, sitting with her back to the wall the way people do when they've learned to expect trouble from doorways. She'd dressed for the country this time—boots still stiff with newness, a pack that had never been heavier than an airport carousel. But her eyes were the same: red-rimmed, level, and absolutely unwilling to cry in front of a stranger twice.

"Sit down," she said, before I'd fully committed to approaching. "You have questions. I could see you building them all night."

"I have one." I sat. "Who told you about me?"

She didn't flinch, which I noted and resented. "A pilot named Dessa Rook. She flies supply runs into the upper valleys. She said if my father was anywhere along the Ashen drainage, there was exactly one man who knew that country better than the maps do—and that the maps were wrong anyway, because he'd helped draw half of them before—" She stopped herself.

"Before I got people killed," I finished. "You can say it. Everyone else does."

"I wasn't going to say that."

"You were thinking it. That's fine. Thinking it is free." I leaned forward. "Here's how this works, Ms. Voss. If we do this, I need you to understand what you're buying. I'm not a rescue anymore. I'm not licensed, bonded, insured, or forgiven. What I am is a man who can walk up a river and tell you what he sees. That's all. And I need you to answer questions honestly, because right now your story has holes in it a marmot could den in."

"Ask."

"The search. Nine days. Who called it off?"

She hesitated. That hesitation was itself an answer, but I let her find the words. "The regional authority. There was a—a procedural review. Weather window closing, resource allocation. The official language was 'suspended pending conditions.'"

"Suspended pending conditions," I repeated. "And who funded the search?"

"The survey firm. Voss and Halloway—it's my father's company, he founded it. They paid for the helicopters."

"They paid for the search for their own missing founder." I watched her face. "Did that seem normal to you? At the time?"

"It seemed generous." Her jaw tightened. "It seems different now. Yes. I've had time to think. Three months of thinking." She pulled a folded printout from her jacket—a news clipping, soft at the creases from handling. *SURVEY TEAM MISSING IN ESCARPMENT BACKCOUNTRY; SEARCH STOOD DOWN.* Below it, a smaller item I'd missed the first night: the regional authority's director of operations had taken a position with a land-development consortium two weeks after standing the search down. Two weeks. Men don't fall upward into those jobs by accident. They're paid to land there.

"Your father's team," I said slowly. "Four men. Who were they?"

"Aldous. His senior surveyor, Tomas Reyes. Two field assistants, brothers, the Okonkwo boys. And—" she stopped.

"And," I prompted.

"And a fifth name on the flight manifest who never boarded. A man named Emeric Salle. He was supposed to be the independent verifier. Every boundary survey has to have one—an outside check, someone with no stake in the line being drawn." She met my eyes. "He resigned by email eleven days before the flight. No reason given. My father called him fourteen times. I have the phone log."

A verifier who quit quietly right before deployment. A search stood down fast and cheap. An official who landed softly in development money. None of these things proved anything. Together they made a shape, and the shape had teeth.

"Here's what I'll do," I said. "I'll scout the lower river. The first thirty kilometers of the Ashen drainage, below the gorge. Looking for sign—camps, caches, anything the official search walked past or didn't bother walking to. That's it. That's the contract. I'm not promising to find anyone. I'm not promising to go past the gorge, and I'm sure as hell not promising to get anyone out. You pay me for days worked, whether I find your father or find nothing."

"That's it? Money?"

"That's it. Money." I said it harder than I needed to, because the alternative reasons were lined up behind the word like passengers at a closed gate, and I wasn't ready to open it. "I work alone. You stay in Corren's Landing. If I find something, I come back and we talk. If I don't, you take your compass home and hire a helicopter like a normal grieving person."

She studied me for a long moment. Outside, a barge horn moaned on the river, and rain began ticking against the café windows, fine and cold, the first taste of a season that would eventually swallow the high country whole.

"My father hated helicopters," she said quietly. "He said you couldn't hear the country from one." She pulled an envelope from her jacket and set it on the table, thick and uncounted. "Half now. Half when you report back. Whatever you find—or don't."

I took it. My hand was steadier than I deserved.

* * *

I left before dawn two days later, because dawn is when the river lies least about itself.

The lower Ashen ran brown-green and muscular with early melt, loud in its gorge, quiet in its bends, and I moved along it the way I'd moved along rivers for twenty years—reading the banks the way other men read newspapers. Old flood lines. Bear sign, fresh. The bleached ribs of a boat some fool had tried to run the narrows in. And then, eleven kilometers up, tucked under a leaning slab of granite where the trail pinched against the water: canvas.

Not old canvas. Old canvas grays and rots and smells of mice. This was taut, waxed, sun-faded on one face only—the south face—which meant it hadn't been there through a winter. I circled it the long way, downwind, the way you approach anything in the bush that might have teeth, and found the cache staked under the slab with surveyor's neatness: aluminum cases, a tripod sleeve, a sealed drum of instruments, all lashed and covered against weather by someone who expected to come back for them.

Someone who hadn't come back.

I knelt and cut the lashings. The first case held a theodolite wrapped in fleece, pristine. The second held notebooks in oilcloth—four of them, and my chest went tight, because four men had gone up that river and here were four men's books, abandoned together, which meant they'd stopped moving as a team and started moving as a decision. The third case—

The third case had holes in it.

Three of them, clean and small, punched through the aluminum lid in a tight cluster, and the exit tears on the underside were ragged and bright. Fresh metal showing at every edge, unoxidized. I'd seen bullet holes in game and in doors and once, badly, in a man. These were weeks old at most. Maybe days.

I sat back on my heels and listened. The river went on talking its gray talk. A dipper worked the shallows. Nothing else. But the sound of the water had changed for me, the way a familiar room changes when you notice the window's been open all along.

Four notebooks. Bullet-holed gear. A cache hidden by careful hands and never reclaimed. And upstream, past the gorge, in country I was banned from entering and had promised a woman I wouldn't enter—the source of the Ashen, and whatever truth had walked up there to die or to wait.

I opened the top notebook. The handwriting was small and exact, a surveyor's hand, and the first entry was dated five days before the team went missing. The last entry was dated the day they vanished, and it was a single line:

*The line on the map is a lie. We are going to the source to prove it.*

I closed the book and looked upriver, into the narrowing walls of the gorge, where the water came down cold and white from a country that remembered my name—and not kindly.

Thirty kilometers, I'd told her. Below the gorge. Money only.

The river, of course, had already decided otherwise.
