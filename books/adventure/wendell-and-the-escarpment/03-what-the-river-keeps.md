## 3. What the River Keeps

The river above the last bridge had no name that appeared on any map Kestrel trusted, which was most of them. The surveyors called it the Voss Line, he'd learned from Mara's notes, because her grandfather had first charted it, and names like that stuck to water the way silt sticks to boots—quietly, permanently, whether you wanted it or not.

He moved upstream alone. That was the arrangement: money for scouting, no promises beyond the lower gorge, no contact until he came back down or didn't. Mara had argued with him about it at the trailhead, standing in the gray light with her father's compass cupped in both hands like something rescued from a fire.

"You said you were banned from this country," she'd said. "Banned by whom?"

"By people who sign papers." He'd shouldered his pack. "If I'm not back in six days, don't come looking."

"And if you are?"

"Then I'll have found nothing worth finding."

She hadn't liked that answer. He could still see her face as he crossed the bridge—a woman doing arithmetic with hope, subtracting what she couldn't afford to lose.

Now the arithmetic was all around him. The river ran high with early melt, green-white and muscular, and the canyon walls closed in until the sky was a seam of hammered tin. He walked the old herding path when there was one, and when there wasn't, he walked the water itself, reading it the way other men read faces. A river tells you everything if you listen: where it has been, what it carries, what it intends to do to anything foolish enough to stand in its way.

On the second day he found the first thing.

It was caught in an eddy behind a fallen spruce—a dry bag, scuffed gray, half-inflated with trapped air. He fished it out with a stick, working slowly, because things in rivers are sometimes bait and sometimes just grief. Inside was survey equipment wrapped in a fleece jacket: a total station in its case, a GPS receiver, batteries gone flat. And stitched into the webbing of the shoulder strap, a name tag in careful block letters.

*R. OKONKWO.*

Kestrel sat on the log for a long time with the tag under his thumb. Then he turned the bag over and saw the holes. Three of them, punched clean through the fabric near the base, the kind of holes that don't come from rocks. Rocks tear. These were punctures, round and deliberate, spaced like someone had fired twice and corrected their aim.

He repacked the bag exactly as he'd found it, minus the GPS unit, which he buried in his own pack beneath the rope. Not evidence yet. Just weight.

* * *

Mara found the key to her father's office taped inside the housing of an old desk lamp, which told her two things at once: that Aldous Voss had expected someone might search his office, and that he trusted lamps more than locks. She sat in the dark of the house on Corliss Street with the lamp disassembled in her lap and felt the specific loneliness of learning your parent is a stranger.

The office itself looked ordinary. Survey firm, third generation, brass instruments on the shelves, framed certificates of licensure going back sixty years. Her grandfather's face in one photograph, her father's in another, both men squinting into some middle distance where property lines lived.

She started with the filing cabinets because they were boring, and boring places hide things well. Deeds, easements, correspondence about drainage easements along the lower Voss. It took her four hours to reach the folder labeled *ASHEN VALLEY — BOUNDARY REVISION — 2011*, and when she opened it, the first page was a memo in her father's handwriting, brief and businesslike:

*Per client instruction, monument positions adjusted to reflect agreed alignment. Original field data archived. Do not circulate.*

*Agreed alignment.* She read the phrase five times. Her father was many things—remote, exacting, a man who corrected your grammar mid-sentence—but he was not vague. Vagueness in Aldous Voss was a locked door.

She pulled the original field data. It was all there, boxed and dated: the 2011 survey of the Ashen valley boundary, done properly, with photographs of stone markers that predated every map the government had ever printed. Markers set by the valley community generations before anyone thought to ask whose land it was. And then a second set of documents, typed on letterhead, showing the same boundary shifted—moved east by nearly four hundred meters, enough to put the spring pastures, the burial ground, the whole eastern terrace of the valley inside a parcel flagged for mineral lease.

There was no client signature on the adjustment order. There was a reference number, and when she traced the reference number through three folders, it led to a holding company, and the holding company led to a name she recognized from the financial pages: Meridian Development Group.

Mara sat down on the floor among the paper and understood, with a cold spreading patience, that her father had known. Had known for thirteen years. Had drawn the false line himself, or signed off on it, and filed the truth away in a cabinet like a confession he couldn't bring himself to burn.

And then, eleven days ago, he had taken a team upstream toward the Ashen valley in monsoon season, telling the firm he was doing a routine resurvey.

Telling the firm.

She looked at the memo again. *Do not circulate.* Her father's handwriting, steady as always. But underneath it, faint, pressed hard enough to emboss the next page, was a second line he'd written and scratched out. She held the sheet up to the window light and tilted it until the shadow filled the grooves.

*They will know we know.*

* * *

On the fourth day, Kestrel found the cache.

It was hidden the way old guides hide things—in plain sight, in a feature so obvious no one looks at it. A split boulder above the river's north bank, its crack packed with stones that didn't match the local rock. He almost walked past it. What stopped him was the lichen: the packing stones were clean-edged, recently placed, while everything around them wore forty years of gray.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, were notebooks. Three of them, waterproof paper, the handwriting small and disciplined. R. Okonkwo's, by the initials. And tucked between the pages, folded into quarters, a single sheet torn from an official government map—the 1:50,000 series, the Ashen quadrangle—with the boundary line inked in red running east of where any honest line could run, and beside it, in fresh pencil, a correction.

Okonkwo had been meticulous. The notebooks laid it out day by day: the team's route upstream, their instrument readings, and then, beginning on the fifth page, something else. Readings taken not from the official monuments but from stones in the forest—stones with carvings worn shallow, stones Okonkwo described with growing unease.

*Day 6. Crossed to the east terrace. Found marker stones the 2011 survey does not mention. Carved. Old. If these are the true boundary, the published line is wrong by 380 meters, and wrong in the direction of money.*

*Day 7. Voss says we continue. He keeps looking at the stones like a man visiting a grave he dug.*

*Day 9. T. wants to turn back. Says the "client" will hear about our readings regardless of what we file. Voss gave him the satellite phone and told him to go downriver if he was afraid. T. went. Nobody has said his name since.*

*Day 11. We are keeping the originals separate now. Voss has written something he calls a confession. I asked him who it's for. He said: whoever finds us.*

Kestrel closed the notebook and listened to the river. The sound filled the gorge the way dread fills a chest—not loud, exactly, but total.

A corrected line. A confession. A man named T. who went downriver and vanished. Bullet-holed gear in an eddy. And somewhere above him, in country getting narrower and colder, either survivors or graves.

He thought about turning back. He was good at turning back; it was the one skill his disgrace had left him in surplus. Six days, he'd promised. Money only. Scout the lower river, report, walk away.

But the notebooks weighed more than paper weighs. He thought of Mara doing her own arithmetic with hope, and of a valley somewhere ahead full of people whose inheritance had been redrawn by men with reference numbers, and of a client he had once left on a mountain because the weather turned and his nerve turned with it. He had spent nine years telling himself the mountain killed that client. The mountain had only finished the job. Kestrel had started it, the moment he decided his own skin was the fixed point everything else was measured from.

He wrapped the notebooks in the oilcloth again and put them inside his jacket, against his spine, where a man carries the things he can't afford to lose and can't afford to be found holding.

Then he looked up the gorge, where the walls pinched together and the river went white over unseen drops, and saw—faint, half a kilometer up, on the far bank—smoke. Thin. Deliberate. The kind of smoke made by people who want to be seen by friends and not by anyone else.

Or the kind made as bait.

Kestrel stood very still in the failing light, the map fragment a stiff rectangle against his ribs, and did the only arithmetic that mattered anymore: whatever waited upriver, he was already carrying it with him.

He started walking.
