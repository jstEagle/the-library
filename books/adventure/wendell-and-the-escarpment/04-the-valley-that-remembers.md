## 4. The Valley That Remembers

The river changed its mind three times between the survey cache and the valley, and each time Kestrel followed it without argument. That was the old discipline: the water knows where it's going even when you don't. He'd learned that from a man named Oyate, years ago, in another life with another name stitched on his sleeve.

By the third morning the walls of the gorge began to lean back, opening like a hand uncurling, and the air came down to meet him smelling of woodsmoke and wet barley and something floral he couldn't name—some high-altitude bloom that only grew where the ground had never been plowed by anyone's greed. Terraces stepped up the flanks of the valley in long green shelves. Above them, the Escarpment raised its gray head into cloud, indifferent as a judge.

He stopped at the edge of the first field and stood there longer than he needed to.

There were places in the mountains that remembered being hunted. You could feel it in how the paths bent away from sightlines, in how the villages sat below the ridgelines instead of crowning them, in the way dogs announced you a full quarter mile before your boots touched stone. This valley didn't feel hunted. It felt *kept*. Fences mended. Water channeled in channels older than any map he'd ever carried. A child's jacket drying on a fencepost, sleeves hanging empty, patient as prayer flags.

Kestrel adjusted his pack and walked in openly, hands visible, because that was the only currency worth anything here.

They saw him coming, of course. They always did. By the time he reached the lower terraces there were three men leaning on hoes who hadn't been leaning on anything a moment before, watching him with the unhurried attention of people who had outlasted every kind of visitor the lowlands could send. He stopped at the boundary stone—a flat slab taller than his waist, carved with spirals worn nearly smooth—and waited to be invited across.

No one invited him across.

"Looking for the survey team," he said. "Four of them. Came upriver maybe two weeks back."

The men said nothing. One of them spat thoughtfully into the dirt, which was not an answer but was also not nothing.

"I'm not with Meridian," Kestrel added. "I'm not with anyone."

That got him a flicker of something—not warmth, but recognition that he understood the question underneath the silence. The oldest man among the three jerked his chin toward the village above, and Kestrel took it for what it probably wasn't: permission.

* * *

They let him sit in the shade of a granary while the valley decided what to do with him. Tea arrived, bitter and hot, set down by a girl of perhaps ten who looked at him the way children look at weather. He drank it. He waited. Around him the village went about its afternoon with the studied indifference of people who had learned that strangers reveal themselves fastest when they think no one is watching.

What they saw, presumably, was a big weathered man in good boots gone bad, carrying a guide's frame pack with the frame cracked and rewelded, sitting very still, watching the high passes the way a man watches a door he's afraid will open.

What they couldn't see was the insignia. It rode inside his collar, sewn there against regulations and every reasonable judgment: the silver thread mountain-and-river mark of the High Corridor Guides, the guild that had certified him once upon a time and then erased him so thoroughly that his name no longer appeared in their rolls. He kept it because cutting it out would have meant admitting it was over. He kept it the way you keep a scar.

Near dusk, she came.

She was small and straight-backed and moved like the terrain owed her money, and she was old in the way that stones are old—not diminished, just finished with pretending otherwise. She crossed the yard without hurrying, sat down on the granary step an arm's length from him, and studied his face with eyes the color of creek ice.

"You watch the north wall," she said. Her accent was thick as rope. "All afternoon. Why?"

"Storm sign," Kestrel said. "Clouds building wrong over the Escarpment. Early monsoon this year."

"We know the monsoon comes." She said it mildly, but there was iron under it. "We have always known. I ask why *you* watch."

Because I'm going to have to bring someone down through that country in weather that kills professionals, he didn't say. Because I've already decided to do the thing I swore I wouldn't do, and I'm pricing it. He said, "Habit."

"Hm." She held out her hand—not to shake, palm up, imperious. "Collar."

He should have refused. Every instinct honed over four years of invisibility told him to refuse. Instead he heard himself say, "You won't find what you're looking for," and turned the collar out with two fingers.

Her fingers found the silver thread. She traced the mountain-and-river mark slowly, twice, and then her whole face changed—not softening exactly, but settling, like snow finding its angle of repose.

"This stitch," she said. "Double-locked at the horn. Only one teacher made her students sew it that way." Her ice-colored eyes came up. "Old Tomas Oyate. My brother."

The name went through Kestrel like a piton driven too deep. "Tomas Oyate taught half the guides on the corridor."

"He taught the ones worth teaching. Most washed out." She released his collar and sat back. "Suri Oyate. And you are the one whose name is not spoken in the guild hall anymore. The one who left a client on the mountain."

The words landed where she meant them to. Kestrel looked at the ground between his boots. "Yes."

"Good," Suri Oyate said. "Then we do not waste time pretending you are honest. Sit. Tell me about the four."

* * *

He told her everything he had—the bullet-holed gear, the cache, the notebooks describing a corrected boundary line, the torn map page. She listened without interrupting, which he recognized as a professional courtesy; guides listened that way too, cataloguing, weighing, deciding whether the story would hold weight.

When he finished, she was quiet for a long moment. Then: "Show me the page you tore from the notebook. The one with the line drawn on it."

He fished the folded paper from the inner pocket where it had lived against his ribs since the cache. She unfolded it on her knee and studied the red line that slashed across the upper valley—the line that, if it stood, would run straight through these terraces, these fences, this granary, this ground where her people had buried their dead for longer than the lowland government had existed.

"They drew this in an office," she said at last. "Three hundred kilometers from here, by men who have never smelled our barley." She folded the page along its existing creases and handed it back with great care, as if returning something borrowed. "But the land does not read their maps. Come. Walk with me."

* * *

She led him up past the last terrace onto a shoulder of grassland where the valley opened toward the glacier-fed headwaters, and the wind came off the ice fields cold enough to make his eyes water. There were cairns up here. Dozens of them, small and unremarkable, scattered across the slope in a pattern that looked like accident.

"Their surveyors came in spring," Suri said. "Two of them. Polite men with instruments. They asked to see our boundary markers, and we showed them the ones near the trail, because those are the ones visitors are shown." Her mouth curved, dry as scree. "These others—" she gestured at the cairns "—they did not show. Each one sits on true bedrock. Under the flat capstone of each is a bronze plate, cast before the lowlanders had a word for 'survey.' The plates carry the old marks. The old agreement lines. My grandmother's grandmother watched them set."

Kestrel crouched beside the nearest cairn. She nodded, and he lifted the capstone with both hands, grunting at the weight. Beneath it, seated in a socket cut with astonishing precision, lay a disc of green-patinaed bronze, engraved with spirals and a boundary sigil identical in structure to the spiral on the village boundary stone.

"Their falsified line runs forty meters east of this marker," Suri said. "Forty meters. Enough to take the spring pasture, the high barley, the burial ground. Enough to make us tenants on our own inheritance." She watched him replace the stone. "Your surveyors found these. All of them. We watched them walk the line for six days. One of them—wept, standing here. A young woman. She photographed every plate. She said—" Suri paused, choosing the words with care, translating more than language "—she said the map was wrong on purpose. She said she could not sign her name under it. She said her father built the lie and she intended to end it."

The wind moved through the grass in a long sigh. Kestrel stayed crouched, one hand still on the capstone, feeling the cold of the bronze ghosting up through his palm.

"Aldous Voss," he said quietly. "And his daughter thinks he's dead."

"Four came up," Suri said. "We fed them here, the last night. Then they went up the gorge toward the source, all four, though the oldest one argued to stay. Two days later, aircraft we did not know came over the pass, low, twice. After that—no one." Her ice eyes fixed on him. "You are going up there."

It wasn't a question. He didn't insult her by making it one.

"The monsoon is early," he said instead. "When it breaks, the gorge becomes a chute. Whatever's up there gets sealed in until autumn—or washed down."

"And whoever goes up after them?"

"Goes up knowing the way down may not exist."

Suri Oyate considered him for a long moment, the wind pulling at her shawl. Then she did something he hadn't earned and couldn't refuse: she pressed her closed fist briefly against his chest, over the hidden insignia, the way you greet family at a graveside.

"Tomas wrote to me about a student," she said. "Brilliant in the broken country. Careless with promises. He said the mountain would either teach him or take him." Her voice gentled by a degree. "It has taken much from you already, I think. Perhaps now it teaches."

She turned and started down toward the terraces, and the light was failing, and behind her the first clouds of the early monsoon were stacking themselves against the Escarpment like a debt come to collect.

"One more thing," she said over her shoulder, not stopping. "The young woman who wept—she gave my granddaughter a radio frequency. Said if ever someone came asking honest questions, we should pass it on." She recited seven numbers without looking at him. "She said her name was Wren. And she said, if the man who comes is wearing a guide's mark inside his collar—he'll know what to do with it."

Kestrel stood alone on the shoulder of the world, the frequency burning in his memory, the storm building in the north, and understood that his invisibility had died somewhere back down the river, and that the only thing left to decide was what would rise in its place.

Below, in the last light, a lamp was lit in the granary window—for him, or for the dead, or for whatever came next.

He started down.
