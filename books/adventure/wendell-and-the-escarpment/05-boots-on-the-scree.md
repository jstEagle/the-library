## 5. Boots on the Scree

I heard them before I saw them, which was how I knew they weren't valley people.

The Ashen moved through their own country like water finding its level—no wasted sound, no argument with the terrain. What came up the trail that morning announced itself: boot heels striking scree with the confidence of men who had never been told *no* by a landscape, the metallic whisper of gear straps, a radio coughing static into air that had never carried one. I was mending a net downriver of Suri Oyate's village when the sound reached me, and my hands went still on the twine the way an animal goes still when it smells smoke.

Three days I had been in the valley. Three days of Suri's testing silence, of learning where the true markers stood—stones older than any survey, carved with spirals that predated every map Aldous Voss's firm had ever inked. Three days of eating what the herders offered and sleeping badly under a roof I hadn't earned. And three days of watching the high passes, because I knew what followed me the way I knew what followed weather.

Meridian had come.

I left the net and climbed the goat path above the river bend, moving low through the juniper until I could see the trail where it switchbacked up from the southern col. They were four hours out still—a file of six, maybe seven figures in gray composite armor, moving with the unhurried economy of professionals. Not soldiers exactly. Soldiers carry themselves like they expect to be shot at. These men carried themselves like they expected to be *obeyed*. Meridian's hunters. I'd crossed paths with their kind once before, years ago, escorting a mining delegation through country they had no reverence for, and I remembered thinking then that they walked as if the ground owed them passage.

The lead figure raised a hand and the column stopped. Even at this distance I could read the geometry of it: he was studying the valley the way a man studies a ledger, looking for discrepancies. Looking for people who shouldn't be there.

Looking for me.

I should have gone to Suri immediately. That was the sensible move, the move the old Kestrel Nakamura—the guide with the insignia, the man whose judgment clients trusted with their lives—would have made. Warn them, organize, get the elders and the children up the hidden trails toward the high summer pastures before the contractors arrived.

Instead I lay in the juniper with my heart going like a trapped bird and did the arithmetic of my own cowardice. If I warned them, I became part of it. My face, my name, my presence—all of it entered the record. Meridian would learn within the hour that Kestrel Nakamura had been seen in the Ashen valley, and everything I had built these two years—the false name, the rented room in the river town, the careful smallness of my life—would burn with the first cabin they torched.

If I stayed quiet, I stayed invisible. And if I stayed invisible while they did what they had come to do, then I was no longer a witness. I was furniture.

The column moved again, angling not toward the village but toward the outlying herding stations—the stone shelters where the young herders lived through the grazing season, far from the elders' protection. Of course. You don't start with the fortified place. You start with the isolated ones, the ones no one will miss for a day, the ones you can question without an audience. I had run enough expeditions to know how professionals work a problem, and the problem they'd been given, written in some boardroom a thousand miles away, was: *find the survey team's records, find the whistleblower, leave nothing that talks.*

They reached the first station—an hour later, a thin thread of smoke rose from its chimney, ordinary and domestic, and I watched through the field glasses I'd taken from the surveyors' cache as the gray figures spread around it with the smooth coordination of men who had done this in other valleys, other countries, other lives.

What happened next took less than four minutes.

* * *

They didn't storm the station. That was almost worse. The lead man—I would come to think of him as the Surveyor, though his name, I learned later, was Dain—knocked politely. The door opened. A young herder stepped out, and I recognized him even at distance: Tam, the one who had laughed at my clumsy grip on the milking stool my second night, the one who had shown me where the ibex crossed the river at dawn. Nineteen, maybe twenty. He came out with his hands loose at his sides and his chin up, because he had done nothing wrong and did not yet know that this meant nothing.

Two of the hunters went inside. Through the glasses I saw them moving through the single room with methodical contempt—opening the grain chest, pulling the blankets apart, tipping over the little altar where Tam kept his grandmother's beads. One of them found something. I couldn't see what. But he held it up, and the Surveyor crossed to him, and there was a moment of stillness that I recognized from the worst moments of my old life: the stillness of a man reading a document that decides someone else's fate.

Then they brought Tam outside, and the polite part ended.

I won't write down everything I saw through those glasses. Some of it I've tried to forget and failed; some of it I keep precisely because forgetting would be a second betrayal. They wanted to know where the strangers had gone—the survey team, the ones who had passed through the valley weeks ago heading upstream. Tam told them the truth, which was that he didn't know, and they declined to believe him, because men like that are paid not to believe anyone. They walked him to the edge of the station yard. One of them struck him behind the knees so he knelt on the scree. And the Surveyor crouched in front of him, patient as winter, and asked his questions again.

The third hunter went back inside with a jerrican.

I understood then what the burning cabin in my mind's eye had always been—not a memory but a forecast. Two years ago, on the Karst expedition, I had stood at the mouth of an ice cave and made a calculation about a client named Ellery Vance, a calculation about odds and exposure and the limits of what one man could carry, and I had chosen the numbers over the person, and the numbers had been wrong, and Ellery had died alone in the dark listening to my boots recede. Every day since, I had lived inside that choice like a man living inside a collapsed tent. Invisibility wasn't peace. It was just the collapse, maintained.

The jerrican splashed against the station's south wall. The smell of fuel reached me even across the wind gap, sharp and obscene among the smells of juniper and dung-smoke and cold stone.

Tam spat at the Surveyor. Small defiance, enormous courage. The Surveyor didn't flinch. He nodded to the man with the lighter.

I was moving before I finished deciding.

That's the truth of it, and I hold onto it because it's the only clean thing in the whole dirty ledger of that morning: there was no decision, or rather the decision had been made years ago in the ice cave and I had simply been too broken to honor it until now. My body went down the goat path the way water goes down a chute, fast and total, and every skill I had spent two years trying to bury woke up at once—the silent footfall, the reading of wind, the counting of sightlines. There were six of them. Six was too many for a fight. So I didn't fight.

The station sat below a slope of loose talus, and the Ashen country had taught me in three days what it takes most men years to learn: that the mountain is a participant, not a backdrop. Above the station, wedged in the gully, was a shelf of granite the size of a truck, held by nothing but habit and friction, the way half the mountainside was held. Herders don't build below such things by accident—they read the slopes the way sailors read sky. The hunters, I noted, had positioned themselves with their backs to it.

I climbed the gully to the east, wide and loud in my movements, deliberately careless, and dislodged a cascade of stones. Heads turned. Voices snapped on radios. Two of the hunters started toward my noise, which was the point, which pulled them off the yard and pointed their eyes uphill while their backs stayed to the mountain.

Then I put my shoulder to the wedge.

It took everything. Rock groans in a language older than words, and for one black instant it didn't move and I thought *this is how it ends, crushed stupid under a stone for a herder boy*, and I found I didn't care, and in the not-caring the strength came. The slab let go with a sound like the valley clearing its throat. It went down the talus in a long thundering rush—not at the hunters, I wasn't aiming to kill, I had killed enough by omission—but across the yard's edge, between the men and the station, a curtain of dust and bouncing boulders that turned discipline into panic. Radios shrieked. Someone fired twice at nothing. The fuel-soaked wall caught anyway—a spark, a lick of flame climbing the dry timber—and I came down through the smoke with a drawn skinning knife and cut the strap holding Tam's wrists and put us both into the willow brake along the stream before the dust settled.

We ran the way the ibex run, which is to say we ran like people who belong to the ground. Behind us the station burned properly now, orange light through the smoke, and the hunters' voices cracked across the yard—angry, disorganized, and then, worse, organized again. The Surveyor's voice. Calm. Giving orders I couldn't hear but could imagine: *two men on the stream, sweep the willows, he can't have gone far.*

But he had gone far, because I knew the stream, and more than that I knew the thing the hunters' maps didn't show: the limestone sink beneath the third bend where the water drops underground for forty meters and comes up singing on the other side. I pulled Tam into the sink's shadow, into cold wet dark that smelled of ancient stone, and we stood hip-deep in the current with the firelight flickering orange on the ceiling above us, and we listened to boots pass overhead, close enough to touch, and pass on.

Tam was shaking. I put my hand on the back of his neck, the way you steady a client on exposed ground, and felt the bruise already rising where they'd hit him.

"They asked about the surveyors," he whispered. "I told them nothing. Kestrel—I told them nothing."

"I know," I said. "I saw."

"You came." He said it like a fact he was checking against the world, making sure it held. "Suri said you would. She said the man who wears that insignia used to come back for people."

The insignia. The old guide's mark stitched into the jacket I'd worn into this valley like a ghost wearing its own headstone. I had thought it was shame I carried in. Standing in the dark under the mountain, with a burned station smoking above us and my false life burning with it, I understood it had been something else all along. A debt instrument. A promise someone had made on my behalf before I was born, that I was only now being called to pay.

Above us, the last light failed, and the hunters' radios went quiet in the way that means a search has become a hunt. Somewhere downstream, Suri Oyate's village would be seeing the glow of the fire and understanding what had entered their valley. And somewhere upstream, past the gorge, past the source, Aldous Voss was alive or dead with the papers that had bought all this fire.

"We go up tonight," I told Tam. "Before the rain. Before they seal the passes."

"Up to where?"

"To the source," I said. "To finish what your valley started."

And far to the south, faint as a pulse, I heard the first thunder of the monsoon walking toward us over the Escarpment.
