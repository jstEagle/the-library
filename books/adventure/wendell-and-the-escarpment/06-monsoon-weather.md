## 6. Monsoon Weather

The charter pilot told her twice.

"Storm cell's building over the divide," he said, tapping the radar screen with a blunt finger. "You want to land on that strip below the valley, you land in the next six hours or not at all. After that, nobody flies. Nobody walks out either, for a week, maybe ten days."

Mara looked at the screen. The storm bloomed there like a red flower opening, petals of rain spreading across the high country where the river came down from the Escarpment's shoulder. Somewhere under that weather was a man who had once carried her on his shoulders through a museum in another life, before the surveys and the silence and the long slow estrangement of a father who was married to his work. Somewhere under that weather, if he was alive at all, Aldous Voss was waiting—or hiding—or dying.

"Then we'd better be quick," she said.

The pilot shrugged the shrug of a man paid to ferry fools into mountains. "Your funeral."

The Cessna bucked and wallowed through the last hour like a boat in chop, and Mara pressed her forehead to the cold window and watched the world below change. The low country fell away—river towns, terraced fields, the brown ribbon of the water she had been tracing on maps for three weeks—and then the land stood up. The Escarpment did not look like geography from the air. It looked like intention. A thousand meters of gray limestone lifted straight out of the green, seamed with shadow, crowned with cloud, and the river entered it through a gorge so narrow it seemed less carved than split, as if the mountain had been axed open and never healed.

She had grown up with that gorge on the walls of her father's study. He kept an old survey photograph of it framed above his desk, taken from the air forty years ago, and as a child she had asked him what lived up there and he had said, *people*, and when she asked what people, he had gone quiet in a way she had never understood until now.

Now she understood. Now she had read the files.

* * *

The strip was a scar of gravel at the valley's mouth, and the plane was gone before its engine noise faded, the pilot clearly unwilling to spend one more minute on the ground than the contract required. Mara stood with her pack in a wind that smelled of wet stone and woodsmoke, alone except for a boy of perhaps ten who watched her from beside a pen of goats with the frank, unblinking stare of children everywhere.

"You are the flyer woman," he said, in the trade tongue.

"I'm looking for Kestrel Nakamura."

"He is at Suri Oyate's fire." The boy turned and walked, apparently satisfied that this settled everything, and after a moment Mara followed, because there was nothing else to follow.

The village sat on a shelf above the river: dry-stone houses with sod roofs, corrals, drying racks hung with fish, and a long low hall whose door stood open to the weather. Inside, a fire burned despite the hour, and around it moved people who stopped what they were doing when she ducked beneath the lintel—not hostile, exactly, but watchful, the way a household watches a stranger.

Kestrel Nakamura rose from the shadows near the hearth.

He was thinner than the photographs on the search-and-rescue forums, grayer, with a beard he hadn't bothered with for the camera. But she knew him instantly—the set of the shoulders, the way he stood slightly angled toward the door even indoors, a man who had spent years making sure no room could hold him against his will. His eyes went to her face first, then to her pack, then to the satellite phone clipped to her belt, and something in them closed like a shutter.

"You flew in," he said. Not a question. An accusation.

"The passes close tomorrow."

"They were already closing." He crossed the room, took her elbow—not gently—and steered her back out into the wind, away from the fire, away from the ears of the hall. Behind them an old woman watched them go with an expression Mara couldn't read. "Do you have any idea what's been happening here? Do you know what I've seen in the last four days?"

"Burned cabins," Mara said. "Men with Meridian patches. A herder they worked over by the second bridge. Yes. I know. I've known since before I hired you."

That stopped him. They stood in the lee of the hall's stone wall, wind tearing at their clothes, and he studied her the way he must have studied weather—as a force to be read, respected, survived.

"Then say what you came to say," he said. "Fast."

"My father's firm drew the boundary line." She made herself hold his eyes. "Voss Geomatics. Nineteen years ago. The original survey that moved the valley markers two kilometers east—that was my father's signature on the certification page. I found the field notebooks in his office. I found the draft that didn't match the final. He falsified it, Kestrel. He sold these people's land for a mining concession, and he's been carrying it for nineteen years, and I think—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, hated herself for it, pushed through. "I think that's why he came back. I think the missing team wasn't lost. I think he brought them upstream to find the true markers and undo it. To confess. And someone found out, and now they're burying him along with the evidence."

Kestrel said nothing for a long moment. Above the Escarpment the clouds had gone the color of wet slate, and the light in the valley was failing in that strange green way that meant the sky was loading itself like a weapon.

"You knew," he said finally. "When you hired me. You knew what your father did."

"I suspected. Then I confirmed. While you were upstream finding his cache, I was in his office finding mine." She lifted her chin. "You want to judge me? Go ahead. But you took money from me knowing more than you told me too. Don't pretend we're not the same kind of animal."

Something moved behind his eyes—not anger. Recognition, maybe. Or relief, which was worse.

"No," he said quietly. "We're not the same kind. I'll tell you why." He looked up at the ridge, at the coming storm, and when he spoke again his voice had changed, flattened, gone somewhere else entirely. "Three years ago I had a client on the north face of Mount Corren. His name was Daniel Reyes. First-time alpinist, rich, insistent, wanted the summit for his fiftieth birthday. Weather window collapsed on descent. Whiteout, wind to eighty knots. We were two hours from the rappel anchors and Daniel was hypoxic and slowing, and I made a judgment call." He paused. "I left him."

Mara felt the words land like stones dropped into still water.

"He couldn't move fast enough. My other client was deteriorating faster—I told myself. The arithmetic of survival, I called it later, in the inquiry. One life weighted against two. And the board bought it, mostly. The family didn't. The press didn't. They pulled my certification, blacklisted me from every permitted range on the continent, and they were right to. Because here's what I didn't tell the inquiry, what I've never told anyone until this minute." He turned and looked at her directly, and his eyes in the failing light were the color of the river in flood. "Daniel Reyes was still alive when I left him. They found his body three days later, sitting upright against the anchor, rope still coiled. He'd built a snow shelter. He'd waited. He trusted me to come back with help, and I didn't come back, because coming back meant admitting the call was wrong, and I chose my reputation over a man's life. That's the animal I am, Mara. So no. We're not the same. You lied to protect a search. I lied to protect myself, and a man froze to death holding a rope I should have been on the other end of."

The wind gusted, hard, and somewhere far up-valley a sound rolled down the gorge—not thunder. Rockfall. The mountain shifting its weight ahead of the rain.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked. "Why now?"

"Because you're going to ask me to go back up that gorge tomorrow. Into the teeth of whatever Meridian sent. And you deserve to know what you'd be tying yourself to." He shook his head slowly. "I spent three years learning to be invisible. No name, no history, no trail. And then your compass showed up and I told myself it was just money, just a scout of the lower river, and every day since I've watched myself do it again—make the small safe call, the call that keeps me hidden. Burning that cabin's ashes off my boots cost me more than you know. Going further costs everything. If I go up there and it goes wrong, if I have to make an arithmetic-of-survival call with your father's life in the balance—" His jaw tightened. "You should know who you're hiring."

Mara stood in the wind and thought about her father's study, the framed photograph of the gorge, the word *people*. She thought about nineteen years of Sunday phone calls that got shorter every year, the way he never met her eyes at Christmas, the locked drawer she'd broken open with a screwdriver at two in the morning while her own hands shook. She had spent her whole life telling herself her father was simply a cold man, a distracted man, and the truth was so much simpler and so much worse: he was a guilty man, and guilt is a kind of coldness, and it had radiated off him all those years like heat off stone, and she had never once thought to ask what was burning underneath.

"We're both liars," she said. "Fine. Then let's do it honestly from here." She pulled the folded papers from inside her jacket—the draft survey, the certification page, the letter she'd found in the locked drawer, unsent, addressed to the Land Registry, dated eleven years ago and never mailed. "He tried once before. Eleven years ago. He wrote the confession and he couldn't send it. Whatever happened to him up there now, he went back to finish it. And I'm going up after him, with or without you, because he's the only father I have and these—" she held up the papers, wind snapping at them, "—are the only proof that matters, and I am done being careful."

Above them, the sky finally broke.

Not gradually. All at once, the way a dam breaks—a wall of rain falling out of the darkening air with a sound like applause, like static, like the end of something. In seconds the dust of the village square turned to mud, the fire in the hall hissed, and the river below the shelf visibly began to rise, browning, thickening, gathering itself.

Kestrel watched the water come down the gorge and Mara watched him watch it, and she saw the exact moment the decision finished making itself in him—saw the shuttered look leave his eyes and something older and harder take its place, something that might once have been called a guide's calm.

"First rain's early," he said, half to himself. "A week early. That changes everything." He turned to her, shouting now over the roar of it. "Suri Oyate says the true markers are three days' climb up the gorge, past the cataract. Your father's camp will be above that. Meridian's men are between us and him—they burned the upper cabin yesterday, they're working their way down. Which means we go up through the flood, past them, fast, in weather that kills experienced climbers every single year." He held out his hand for the papers, and she gave them to him, and he sealed them inside his jacket against his chest. "And if I make a call up there that you don't understand, you follow it without arguing. Can you do that?"

"Yes," Mara said.

"It might be the wrong call. It's been wrong before."

"Then it's wrong with witnesses," she said. "Both of us, this time. No more arithmetic."

For a moment—just a moment—something that was almost a smile touched the ruined country of his face. Then he turned toward the hall, toward the old woman in the doorway who had been listening to all of it, and raised his voice in her language, and Suri Oyate answered with a single nod and a stream of instructions that sent half the village moving into the rain.

"What did you ask her?" Mara shouted.

Kestrel pulled up his hood and looked up the gorge, where the river was already swallowing its own banks, where her father was waiting somewhere in the drowning dark with a confession in his hands.

"I asked her how fast her fastest people can climb in a storm," he said. "And she told me to stop wasting time talking to you."

* * *

They left within the hour.

The boy with the goats watched them go from under the eaves of the drying shed, and behind him the whole village stood in doorways with lamps lit against the noon-dark storm, watching the two strangers and their guide start up the flooded trail. Mara looked back once, at the top of the first switchback, and saw the fires of the hall blurred behind sheets of rain, and Suri Oyate standing apart from everyone else, one hand raised—not waving, Mara realized. Pointing. Upstream. Toward the source.

Toward whatever the river had been keeping all these years.

The trail climbed. The rain fell harder. And somewhere above them, in the white roaring throat of the gorge, the mountain waited with everything it had decided to give them.
