## 7. The Forbidden Source

**Kestrel**

The gorge narrowed until the river was less a river than a held breath—a green-black thread compressed between walls of granite so close that Kestrel could have touched both sides with a spread of his arms. The water ran cold and fast and utterly silent in the way that deep water is silent, its noise swallowed by stone. Above them, a slit of sky the color of old pewter.

"Nobody comes up here," Mara said behind him. Her voice came flattened by the rock, stripped of everything but fact.

"That's the point." He tested the next hold with his boot heel—wet schist, flaking at the edges—and moved on. "Meridian's people think in maps. Maps end where the contour lines give up. This doesn't exist for them."

"And it exists for us?"

He didn't answer that. Three days since the burning cabin, three days since he'd pulled the herder Toma out of Meridian's hands and watched his own careful invisibility go up with the smoke. Now there was no version of this story where he walked away clean. There was only upstream.

They climbed through the morning, through a country that grew stranger as it rose. Moss hung from seeps in the rock like green hair. Water fell in threads from heights they couldn't see, and once a whole hillside of scree shifted under their feet with a sound like slow applause. Kestrel read the gorge the way he'd once read it for clients—the flood lines scratched into the stone above his head, dark with old silt, telling him exactly how high the river could rise when the monsoon finally broke. The highest line was over his head by two body-lengths. He didn't mention it to Mara. She'd seen it too; she had her father's eyes, and her father had been a man who read terrain for a living, whatever else he'd done with that gift.

Near midday the gorge opened without warning into a hanging valley, a bowl of stone cradling a lake the color of bottle glass, fed by snowfields that clung to the cirque above like dirty lace. The source. The river began here, and here, on a shelf of flat ground above the shoreline, stood three tents gone gray with weather, guy-lines slack, one of them collapsed into itself like a dead animal.

Kestrel put his hand out and stopped her.

"Wait."

He circled the camp the way Suri Oyate had taught him to circle anything unknown—downwind, wide, patient. No movement. No smell of cooking. But no smell of death either, not yet, though the cold slowed everything up here, even rot. He found the first body inside the collapsed tent: a young man, Asian features, frost-stiffened hands still curled around a notebook he'd died holding. The second lay outside, near a fire ring long gone to ash, an older woman with gray braids, a wound high on her chest that had been bound and rebound and bound again with strips of shirt. She'd fought. She'd lasted days.

Mara made a sound behind him, small and terrible, and knelt by the older woman and touched her shoulder the way you'd touch someone sleeping.

"I know her," she whispered. "That's Dr. Ferro. She taught my second-year field course. She—" Her voice broke off. She pressed her fist against her mouth.

Kestrel crouched beside the young man and eased the notebook from his fingers. It was water-swollen and half-frozen, and the last legible entry was dated eleven days ago:

*Voss says we stay. Says running makes us evidence of nothing. R. wants to push down tonight. I'm staying with A.*

*We corrected the line. God help us, we corrected the line, and now we know why nobody wanted it corrected.*

He looked up. "There's a third tent."

It was pitched apart from the others, back against a boulder, and it was not collapsed. As they crossed the shelf toward it, a voice came from inside—cracked, furious, alive:

"If you're Meridian's, I've got nothing left worth taking but my opinion of you, and you can't carry that downhill."

Kestrel stopped. Beside him, Mara had gone white.

"Dad," she said.

* * *

**Mara**

The man who crawled out of the tent was not her father. That was her first thought, obscene and instant, and then it wasn't true at all—it was her father exactly, reduced, the way a negative is the exact image of a photograph with all the light reversed. Aldous Voss had been a big man, deliberate, silver at the temples and vain about it. This man was gaunt, bearded, one leg wrapped in filthy splints, both hands shaking around the pistol he aimed at them with the grim competence of a man who'd learned to shoot late and hated it.

Then his eyes found her face and the pistol dropped six inches, and he made a sound she'd never heard him make, not at her mother's funeral, not ever.

"Mara." He said it like a word in a language he'd forgotten. "No. No, you can't be—you were supposed to be in—" He stopped himself, and she watched him do the arithmetic, watch him understand what her presence meant: that she'd come looking, which meant the search had failed, which meant the world above had already written them off. His face did something complicated and settled on grief.

"You shouldn't have come," he said.

"You should see the other options." She heard her own voice shake. She crossed the distance between them and then didn't know what to do with her arms, because he was thinner than her memory of him and he smelled of woodsmoke and infection, and when she finally embraced him he gripped her hard enough to hurt, his face buried in her shoulder, shaking.

Behind them, Kestrel Nakamura stood watching the lake shore with his whole body turned outward, listening to the country. She'd learned to read that posture in the past week. It meant: *we are not safe, and I am counting the seconds until you believe me.*

"Dad. Your leg."

"Fell in the icefall coming down from the pass. Set it myself, which I don't recommend." He laughed, a dry rustle. "Ferro knew more medicine than I do. She kept me alive for—" His voice caught on the name. "She went out for water nine days ago and never came back. And Danny—" He looked at the collapsed tent and couldn't finish.

"We found them," Mara said softly. "Both of them."

He nodded, jaw working. Then, abruptly, businesslike in the way she knew was armor: "Nakamura. They said you were finished. Blacklisted."

"They said right."

"My daughter hired you anyway."

"She did."

Aldous Voss studied Kestrel for a long moment, and Mara saw something pass between the two men—not warmth, exactly, but recognition. Two men whose names had been ruined, one justly and one unjustly, or perhaps both justly, depending on who told it.

"Then come inside before the light goes," her father said. "I have something to show you, and after that, I suspect, very little time."

* * *

**Kestrel**

Inside the tent, by the light of a candle stub, Aldous Voss showed them why four people had died at the top of a river no map acknowledged.

The documents were wrapped in oilcloth and had been slept on, literally, for eleven days. Kestrel laid them out on the sleeping bag while Mara held the candle. There was the original survey—the one bearing Aldous's firm's seal, the one that drew the boundary along the low ridge and cut the Ashen valley out of its own watershed. And beneath it, page by page, the correction: new measurements, new markers, photographs of stones Suri Oyate's people had tended for generations, cross-referenced against colonial-era records that predated the firm's involvement by eighty years.

"The original was falsified," Aldous said. Flatly, the way a man confesses to murder. "Not sloppy. Falsified. I signed it. I took the fee, and I signed it, and I told myself the client's data was probably fine." He tapped the corrected pages with a trembling finger. "Two years I sat on it. Then Ferro came to my office uninvited—she'd been doing independent work in the valley, with Suri's people—and she put the truth on my desk and asked me if I was the kind of man who could live with it. And I discovered I wasn't. So I assembled a team and came up to re-survey properly, on my own money, and somebody at the client's office found out."

"Meridian," Kestrel said.

"Their security arm. We spotted their scouts on day four. Danny wanted to run for the low passes; Ferro argued we'd never outrun them carrying instruments, that our only value was the data surviving. So we split the risk—Danny carried duplicates downriver, Ferro and I stayed as—" He smiled without any humor in it. "As decoys, I suppose. Bait. It worked better than I'd planned."

Mara's hand found her father's. Kestrel looked away, out through the tent flap at the lake, and let them have the moment, and used the moment to listen.

The wind had changed. It had been at their backs all day, out of the south, warm and wet and smelling of lowland rain. Now it came down off the snowfield, cold, and beneath the cold he caught it—that smell he knew from the worst year of his life, the year the river took Elena Marsh. Wet stone. Vegetation rotting fast. The sky over the cirque had thickened from pewter to slate, and the slate was moving.

"The monsoon breaks tonight," he said. "Maybe tomorrow. When it does, this gorge becomes a pipe. Everything that falls on forty square kilometers of snowfield and rain comes down that slot at once."

"Then we leave now," Mara said.

"Your father doesn't walk out of here. Not on that leg. Not down the gorge."

"Then we carry him."

"With Meridian behind us?" Kestrel shook his head slowly. "Carrying a grown man down wet schist, we move at a quarter speed. They'll walk us down like animals." He turned back to Aldous. "Is there another way?"

"There's the old traverse," Aldous said. "North wall. Suri's people used it before the road—sheep tracks, ledges. It stays above flood line almost the whole way. But it's exposed, and I'm not crossing it on this leg, and neither are your documents if I'm carrying them."

"So we split again," Mara said. "Like you and Ferro."

"No." Aldous's voice hardened. "Absolutely not. I am not sending my daughter—"

"You're not sending me anywhere." Mara knelt and began repacking the oilcloth with quick, fierce hands. "You're staying. Suri Oyate's people will take you—I'll carry word to them. Dad. Look at me." She waited until he did. "You spent two years being the wrong kind of man. This is how you stop. You let the evidence go where it has to go, and you live, and you testify. That's the whole job now."

For a long moment the only sound was the candle flame breathing. Then Aldous Voss laughed—broken, relieved, defeated—and pressed the oilcloth bundle into his daughter's hands, and over her shoulder he looked at Kestrel.

"I know what they say about you," he said quietly. "Elena Marsh. Four years ago, the ford below Serpent Bend."

Kestrel felt the name land in his chest like a thrown stone. "You've done your reading."

"I read everything about everyone I hire. Or that my daughter hires." The old man's eyes were steady. "They say you misread the river. They also say you went back into it four times. I'm asking you to misread nothing today, and to go back in as many times as it takes. Can you get her down?"

Kestrel looked at Mara—at the set of her jaw, so much her father's—and thought about the gorge below, the flood lines on the stone, the smell of the coming rain.

"I can get her down," he said.

Outside, the first drop hit the lake like a rifle shot. Then another. Then the sky simply opened, and the monsoon came down the cirque in a gray roaring curtain, and somewhere far below them, faint under the rain, Kestrel heard the thing he'd been dreading: voices on the lower trail, and the metallic click of a radio.

Meridian had found the gorge.
