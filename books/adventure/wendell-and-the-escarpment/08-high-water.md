## 8. High Water

The rain came the way Suri Oyate had promised it would—not as weather but as a decision. One hour the sky was a bruised grey lid over the gorge; the next it opened like a wound, and the world became water.

I stood in the doorway of Aldous Voss's shelter and watched the river change its mind about everything. The Ashen Fork, which for six days had been a green ribbon threading between stones, went brown and then white and then something that was neither color at all—just motion, just mass, a freight train of mountain running downhill with no brakes. Boulders I'd used as stepping stones yesterday were gone. Not moved. *Gone*, swallowed into the churn.

Behind me, Aldous coughed, and the sound pulled me back from the window of rain.

He was propped against a rolled sleeping pad, his broken leg splinted with survey staves and strapping tape, his face the grey of old ash. Three days in the final camp with two dead men and a confession he'd written by headlamp while the cold climbed into his chest—that kind of thing doesn't wash out. He'd held on because holding on was all he had left to do. Now the holding-on was nearly finished too.

"You're not leaving today," he said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't quite an argument. His voice had the flat calm of a man who has already argued with himself and lost.

"The gorge is a death trap in this," I said. "By tomorrow the water drops. We move fast, we move quiet—"

"Kestrel." He said my name the way you'd set down something heavy. "Look at me. Look at my leg. Look at my hands." He held them up. They trembled like leaves, and not from fear. "Meridian's men found our cache below the falls this morning. You told me yourself—their tracks on the west bank, four of them, moving up. If you wait for the water to drop, you wait for them."

Mara stood at the far wall, arms wrapped around herself, her father's compass open in her palm like a small dark eye. She hadn't spoken since dawn. She'd spent the night reading his confession twice through, and whatever she'd found in those pages had rearranged her face into something older.

"He's right," she said quietly. "Dad's right."

"I'm not carrying him out through a flood gorge," I said. "And I'm not leaving him."

"Neither are we asking." Suri Oyate filled the doorway behind me—I hadn't heard her come through the rain, which should have frightened me more than it did. The old woman was soaked to the skin and utterly unbothered, as if water had simply decided to pass around her dignity. "The high caves above the hot springs have held our people through nine monsoons. My grandmother wintered in them during the war years. Your surveyor will be warm, dry, fed, and hidden until the passes clear. That is not a promise I make lightly, Aldous Voss. Understand what it costs us if your hunters find the caves."

"They won't find them," Aldous said. "Not if these—" he touched the documents stacked beside him, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord— "are somewhere else entirely."

* * *

We did it in the space between one thunderclap and the next.

Aldous made me read the confession aloud first—all eleven pages of it, his handwriting degrading from careful architect's script to a scrawl that leaned hard left, as though the man writing it were being pushed. The falsified boundary line, drawn in 2011 to shift the valley's eastern watershed forty meters onto Meridian's parent company's claim. The bribes. The signatures bought. And at the end, the corrected survey—the true line, re-measured by his own team at cost of two lives, witnessed by the whistleblower Denali Cho whose body we'd wrapped in her own tent three days ago—and his signature, dated last week, renouncing everything.

"Say it back to me," he said when I finished.

I said it back. Every number. Every name. I'd carried route descriptions in my head for twenty years; I could carry this.

"Now the papers go with you, and the truth goes with both of us." He pressed the oilcloth bundle into my hands. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything. "If I die in those caves, it changes nothing. If they kill me, it changes nothing. The document exists now outside my reach, and that's the only safety either of us has ever really had."

Mara knelt and took his face in her hands. I turned away to give them the moment, and instead found myself looking at the storm, and thought about another storm, seven years ago, when I'd looked away from a client named Tomas Reyes for exactly ninety seconds and the mountain had used every one of them. Some debts don't get paid. They just get refinanced, at worse rates, forever.

When Mara stood, her eyes were red but level. "Come back down when the water drops," she told her father. "Both of us will meet you at the trailhead."

"I'll be there," Aldous said, and lied beautifully, and everyone let him.

Suri Oyate put her hand on my shoulder—dry suddenly, impossibly, as if the rain respected rank. "Nakamura." She'd been using my real name since the day she recognized the old guide's insignia stitched inside my jacket, the one I'd never bothered to remove because I'd stopped believing anyone would look. "The gorge will try to keep you. It keeps what hesitates. Don't hesitate."

* * *

We dropped into the gorge at noon, or what would have been noon under any sky other than that churning grey ceiling.

The trail was gone. Not washed out—*gone*, the whole cut bench of it sloughed into the river, so we traveled the rock itself, traversing the gorge wall on ledges slick as oiled glass, roped together, thirty feet above a current that had stopped being a river and become a verdict. The spray hit us sideways. The noise was total—a continuous roar with voices in it, if you listened wrong, and I made myself listen wrong only once, because in that roar I could hear exactly how a person sounds when the water takes them, and I had heard it before, once, from across a crevasse field, and I did not need the reminder.

Mara climbed well. That surprised me, and then didn't—she was her father's daughter, and Aldous Voss had spent a week at eight thousand feet with a shattered leg keeping himself alive through pure stubborn bookkeeping. She moved the way he wrote: precisely, without wasted motion, terrified and doing it anyway.

"Talk to me!" she shouted over the water, and I realized I'd gone silent, gone inward, gone to the bad place.

"Left traverse, ten meters!" I shouted back. "Trust the rock, not your eyes—the spray lies about depth!"

She trusted the rock. Ten meters later she was beside me, breathing hard, and the ledge ahead pinched down to nothing where a waterfall the width of a house came off the upper wall and detonated against the gorge floor.

"There's a chimney behind the fall," I yelled. "Suri's people cut steps in it generations ago. We go through the water, not around it!"

Her face said everything her voice couldn't carry over the roar. But she clipped into the anchor I'd set, and followed me into the falling white, and for eleven seconds the world was nothing but cold impact and blind grip and the ancient carved footholds under my boots, worn smooth by a hundred years of valley feet, holding mine now, holding hers.

Then we were through, behind the waterfall, in a pocket of thundering half-silence, and Mara grabbed my arm and pointed down.

Torches. Four of them, on the east bank, maybe four hundred meters downstream and closing. Orange points bobbing through the rain-gloom like embers blown from a fire.

They weren't following our track. They were simply walking the gorge, methodical as tide, and sooner or later the tide finds everything.

* * *

The river gave us one gift that afternoon, and I took it with both hands.

Below the falls the gorge narrowed to a slot where the water compressed into a single raging vein—and across it, wedged between two walls, lay a logjam: whole trees the flood had torn from the high forest, packed into a bridge of timber sixty feet long, groaning under the pressure of the current but holding, holding, the way wreckage holds.

Nobody crosses a logjam. Every guidebook ever written says so; every dead rafter's report card agrees. Logs roll. Gaps open. The whole structure shifts with the pulse of the flood like something breathing.

But the hunters couldn't cross it either, and on the far side the old valley trail climbed out of the gorge toward the low country, and behind us the torches were closing, and ahead of us the jam was the only door.

I anchored Mara to a flake of granite and crossed alone first, testing each log with my boot before committing weight, reading the grain, reading the flex, feeling the whole wrecked cathedral of timber shudder and settle beneath me. Halfway across a cedar trunk rolled a quarter-turn under my foot and dropped me to my chest on the wet wood, arms wrapped around it, the river's breath hot and cold on my neck at once, and I lay there for three full seconds feeling the log decide whether to keep me.

It kept me. I crawled the rest.

Then I rigged a tension line back across, and brought Mara over hand-over-hand, talking her through it in a low steady voice I barely recognized as mine—the voice I'd used on clients, once, in another life, before I learned what my judgment cost. *Weight low. Move on my count. When it rolls, ride it, don't fight it.* She rode it. When the second log shifted under her she rode that too, and came off the far end shaking, alive, and laughing—one short wild bark of laughter at the sky, at the river, at the sheer stupid audacity of still existing.

That was when the first torch reached the near bank.

The lead man raised a rifle, and I watched him do the arithmetic we'd already done: the jam, the flood, the rope still swaying between banks. He shouted something to his people. Then he stepped onto the logs anyway.

I cut the rope.

The line whipped away into the gorge, and the logjam groaned, and whether it was the lost tension or simply the river's schedule arriving on time, the entire structure shuddered, settled, and began—slowly, majestically, tree by grinding tree—to come apart. The hunter froze halfway across, spread-eagled on a cedar that was now a raft, going wherever the river decided. His companions fell back from the bank shouting. The rest of the jam folded inward like a closing hand, and the Ashen Fork swallowed its own bridge whole, and the gorge between us and Meridian's men became exactly what it had been trying to become all day: uncrossable.

The river had been their road. Now it was ours.

Mara stood beside me in the rain, clutching the oilcloth bundle to her chest, watching the water take the logs downstream into the dark.

"That was the only way across," she said. It wasn't a question.

"For them," I said. "There's a ford two miles down. Suri's people use it in low water." I looked at the sky, at the wall of rain still coming off the peaks, at the river running high and angry and full. "In this water, nobody uses it. Except maybe someone who grew up reading rivers that wanted to kill him."

"And if the ford is gone too?"

I started down the valley trail, into the roaring dusk, and heard myself answer like a man who had finally stopped lying to himself about which side of the story he stood on.

"Then we swim."
