## 9. The Gorge Decides

The river told us before the sky did.

All morning it had been rising—not the slow green swell of meltwater but a brown, muscular creep, the kind of rise that comes from rain falling on country we couldn't see, high up beyond the shoulders of the gorge where the monsoon had already made landfall. Every hour the water climbed a hand's width up the granite walls, and every hour the sound of it grew less like a river and more like a crowd.

Kestrel read it the way he used to read weather for clients who trusted him with their lives: by the debris line. Sticks and foam and a dead marmot riding the current at chest height where an hour ago they'd have been at his knees.

"We're not crossing that," Mara said. She wasn't arguing. She was confirming, the way you confirm a diagnosis.

"Not here." I crouched at the lip of the cataract, where the whole gorge funneled into a slot barely wider than a truck and dropped twenty feet into churning white. The spray reached us even at this distance, cold as snowmelt, tasting of stone. "There's a shelf above the falls. On the far side there's a ledge system—Suri's people call it the Ladder. It runs down the inside of the gorge to the lower valley. If we can get across above the drop, we're ahead of them. If we can't—"

I didn't finish it. Behind us, somewhere back along the rim, a rock clattered. Not wind. Wind doesn't kick stones downhill in ones and twos with the deliberate spacing of men who are being careful because they know exactly what care costs.

Mara heard it too. Her hand went to the waterproof roll strapped across her chest—Aldous's confession, the corrected survey, the torn map page, all of it sealed in plastic and wrapped in her father's own survey flagging tape, red as a wound. Three days of climbing to put that roll in her arms. Meridian had known about it for two.

"How long do we have?" she asked.

"Minutes. Maybe."

* * *

The crossing point was a place I'd been shown once, eleven years ago, by a woman named Suri Oyate who had looked at my guide's insignia and decided I was worth teaching anyway. A pair of iron rings, driven into the rock by some forgotten prospector, anchored on either side of the narrows above the falls. Between them, in flood years, the Ashen people strung a rope bridge that they took down every autumn and never let outsiders see standing.

The rings were still there. The rope was not.

But I had rope. Fifty meters of 9-millimeter static line coiled across my chest, plus the tag lines, plus the prusiks and carabiners I hadn't let myself sell even in the worst winter, when selling them would have meant eating better than I did. Gear for a rescue. Gear for the kind of work I'd sworn off, because the last time I'd rigged a system like this, a client named Daniel Okafor had gone into the water below me and stayed there, and I had stood on the bank doing arithmetic instead of moving, and the arithmetic had said *wait*, and waiting had been the wrong answer, and everyone who reviewed the incident had agreed.

"You've done this before," Mara said. It wasn't quite a question. She was watching me flake out the rope with hands that remembered the motions even while the rest of me shook.

"I've done this before," I said. "Once. Badly."

"Tell me."

"No time."

"Then tell me while you rig. I need to know who's getting me across this thing."

So I talked while I worked, because she was right—that was the price of trust, and I'd been paying it in installments since the valley—and the talking kept the other voice quiet, the one that said *you froze, you'll freeze again*. Anchor the static line through the upstream ring with a tensionless wrap, because the ring itself was pitted and I didn't trust it to hold a hard point load. Run the line downriver to the far ring, but leave slack—a ferry line, not a tightrope, so the current would push us along it instead of pinning us against it like flies on a window. The water below the falls wasn't a river anymore; it was a machine for drowning things, and anything it caught against a taut rope it would hold until it was done.

"The trick isn't strength," I said, clipping the first carabiner. "Everyone thinks it's strength. It's angle. You stay upstream of your own rope. The moment the water gets downstream of you, you're a sail."

"And if I lose the rope?"

"You won't."

"But if I do."

I looked at her. Rain had started—not the real monsoon yet, just the outriders of it, fat drops that struck the granite and exploded. Her face was gray with exhaustion and fear and something underneath both that I recognized: the specific stubbornness of someone who has decided that turning back is no longer among the available options. Her father had it. Whatever else Aldous Voss had been—a fraud, a coward, a man who'd spent three years letting a lie stand because undoing it was expensive—he'd gone up this river at sixty-one years old to unmake his own work, and he'd sent his daughter the compass that found me.

"If you lose the rope," I said, "you swim for the eddy behind the far pillar. Left side, below the ledge. And I come in after you. That's the whole plan. There is no version of this where I stand on the bank."

Something moved in her face. She didn't say *like you did for Okafor*. She didn't have to. We both knew the shape of the sentence.

"Okay," she said. "Rig it."

* * *

She crossed first, because I said so and because she was smart enough not to argue. Documents go last across any hazard—you don't risk the evidence before the witness. I clipped her harness into the ferry line, showed her twice how to keep her body angled into the current, how to walk backward along the rock shelf with the rope running through her hands.

"The water will try to lift your feet. Let it. Don't fight the lift. Fight only the sideways."

"What happens when I get to the middle?"

"The middle is where you find out what you're made of. Go."

She went.

For six seconds it was textbook. Then the river hit her.

It came as a surge—the kind of pulse that travels down a gorge when something upstream lets go, a slide, a dam of logs, the sky itself emptying—and it took her to the waist and lifted her clean off the shelf, and for one full second she was flying, splayed on the brown water like a starfish, and the sound she made was not a scream but something worse, a short exhaled *huh* of pure animal surprise.

The ferry line caught her. Held. The current slammed her downstream along its arc, and I fed slack the way Suri had taught me, hand over hand, letting the pendulum carry her toward the far wall instead of fighting it, because fighting it would have snapped her grip or snapped the line, and the old arithmetic rose in me—*calculate, wait, be sure*—and I killed it the way you kill a snake, fast and without ceremony.

"Mara! Feet up! Roll onto your back!"

She rolled. The water took her feet downstream, put her body parallel to the current, and suddenly she wasn't fighting a ton of moving river, she was riding it, sliding along the arc of the rope like a bead on wire, past the lip of the cataract, past the thunder, into the slack water behind the far pillar where the eddy turned her gently around twice and set her against the ledge like a leaf against a fence.

She lay there gasping. Then she looked up at me across forty feet of roaring brown death and shouted something I couldn't hear over the falls, and I'm glad I couldn't, because whatever it was, I'd have wanted to answer it, and there was no time.

Because the hunters had arrived.

* * *

Three of them. They came down the rim trail in single file, moving fast and careless with it, the way trained men move when they think the outcome is settled. The lead one carried a rifle slung across his back; the other two carried pistols and the flat confidence of contractors whose contracts always close. I knew the type. I'd guided for men like them once, before I understood what their confidence was built on.

They stopped at the top of the shelf. The lead one looked at the rope, at the falls, at Mara huddled on the far ledge with the document roll strapped to her chest. Then he looked at me, and smiled, and shouted over the water:

"Nakamura. Give us the girl and the papers, and you walk away. That's the deal. Last offer."

The rain was real now, sheeting, and the river answered it, surging again, another foot on the rocks. I looked at the crossing. I looked at the three of them looking at it. And I saw what Suri would have seen, what any river reader would have seen, what I had failed to see eleven years ago on a different bank with a different drowning man: that the surge was coming in waves now, four minutes apart, maybe five, and that the middle of the crossing was a coin flip between manageable and lethal, and that these men had watched Mara cross and drawn the wrong conclusion—that the rope made it safe.

The rope didn't make it safe. The rope plus timing plus reading the water made it safe. They had none of those things. They had rifles and certainty.

"Last chance, Nakamura!"

I waved acknowledgment—hands up, palms out, the universal gesture of surrender—and began pulling the rope in.

They thought I was giving up the crossing. All three of them stepped down onto the shelf to hurry me, which meant all three of them were committed to the near side, which meant the far side—the Ladder, the descent, the whole escape—was mine.

Except I wasn't surrendering the rope. I was re-rigging it.

While they watched, I cast the free end out across the narrows, underhand, a fisherman's throw I'd learned gutting seasons ago in a town that didn't know my name, and Mara caught it on the second attempt and made it fast to the far ring. Now there were two lines spanning the gorge: my original ferry line, sagging, and this new one, taut, low over the water.

A Tyrolean. A traversing line. Rigged wrong on purpose.

"Come on!" I shouted to them, waving them forward, playing the broken man, the guide who'd cracked, the famous failure doing the only thing failures do—which is cooperate. "Before the next surge!"

Greed does what fear cannot: it makes careful men careless. The lead hunter clipped himself to the taut line with a carabiner from his own kit—wrong, all wrong, a hard point on a hard line, everything I'd spent ten minutes avoiding—and stepped off the shelf into the gap above the falls, and his weight came onto the rope, and I waited for the wave.

I felt it before I saw it, a shudder through the granite, a change in the river's voice like an orchestra inhaling.

The surge hit him mid-span.

The taut line did exactly what taut lines do in flood: it pinned him against the current, chest-deep, then shoulder-deep, the water folding him over the rope like laundry over a wire. He got one arm free, clawed, went under, came up. His partners ran to the edge and did the worst possible thing—they grabbed the line to pull him back, adding their weight, lowering the rope further into the torrent, and the river took the whole arrangement, man and line and helpers, and simply began keeping it.

I cut nothing. I didn't have to. The river was doing all the work. But I did take my knife to the ferry line's near anchor—one clean stroke, releasing the slack span—so that the taut line, overloaded, dragged sideways off the far ring and swung the whole tangled system against the near wall, where the two men on the bank suddenly found themselves holding a rope that led to a drowning colleague and could neither pull him out nor let him go without admitting what they'd done.

The lead hunter's rifle went into the falls. Then his hat. Then, after a long minute of thrashing, with the help of his partners hauling hand over hand, the man himself—coughed up onto the shelf like a bone, alive, stripped of everything including his certainty.

They didn't follow us. Men who have almost drowned in a river don't argue with it twice, not today, not with the monsoon still climbing. While they knelt over their man, I crossed on my own sagging ferry line, feeling the pulses come four minutes apart and timing each movement between them, and pulled myself up onto the ledge beside Mara, who was crying and laughing and holding the document roll above the spray like a torch.

"The Ladder," I said. "Now. Before they remember they have radios."

We climbed down into the gorge's throat, into the roar and the rain, and below us, far below, the lights of the lower valley were coming on through the storm—one light, then five, then dozens, the Ashen people lighting their windows against the dark, a constellation at the bottom of the world showing us the way home.

Behind us, faint under the falls, I heard the crackle of a radio transmitting our position to someone who would be waiting at the trailhead.

Good, I thought. Let them wait. I was done arriving unseen.
