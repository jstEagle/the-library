## 1. Miles, Not Years

The *Kestrel's Grace* came up off the bottom of Chittagong harbor at seven in the morning, trailing silt and forty years of other people's mistakes, and Ada Dunmore watched her break the surface with the particular satisfaction of a woman crossing another job off a list that never got shorter.

"She's a beauty," said Tomás Okafor, leaning on the rail beside her, his diving mask pushed up into his curls. "In the way that rusted things are beautiful."

"She's a payday," Ada said. "Beauty is for people who can afford it."

Below them, in the green-brown water of the harbor basin, the salvage crane groaned as it took the weight of the trawler they'd spent three weeks raising. The *Grace* was their own vessel—a converted coastal freighter with a derrick bolted to her deck and a hull that had been patched so many times the welds told a story older than most of the crew. She sat low in the water now, heavy with the day's first lift, and Ada felt the familiar itch start behind her sternum. The itch that meant: *done here*. The itch that meant: *move*.

Three weeks raising a colonial-era steamer full of nothing but ballast stone and one miraculously intact cargo of brass fittings. Enough to pay the crew, enough to pay the harbormaster's fees, enough to fuel them to the next contract. Not enough to get comfortable.

Comfort was a trap. Ada had learned that at nineteen, standing in a courthouse in Khulna while a magistrate read out the findings on her father's last expedition—*pilot error, sudden squall, no foul play suspected*—and she'd understood, with a clarity that never left her, that the world would always have an explanation ready for men who drowned in the Sundarbans. Neat words. Closed files. Rivers that kept what they took.

So she'd built a life out of leaving. Seven years of salvage contracts strung like beads from Chittagong to Cox's Bazar to the Burmese coast and back. A crew that had become something more than a crew, though she'd never say that aloud where any of them could hear it. And one rule, unspoken but iron: *never south of the Haringhata line*. Never the Sundarbans concession. Never the water that had taken Elias Dunmore.

"You've got that look," said Priya Nair, appearing at her elbow with two cups of tea, handing one over. Priya was their engineer, small and sharp-eyed, with grease permanently worked into the creases of her knuckles. "The look where you're already three hundred miles away."

"I'm always three hundred miles away."

"That's my point." Priya blew on her tea. "The harbormaster's clerk asked me this morning if we'd be staying through the season. I told him I'd have to ask you. He looked at me like I'd told him the tide answered to a committee."

"What did you want to tell him?"

Priya shrugged, and didn't answer, and that was its own kind of answer. Ada drank her tea and looked out at the harbor—the forest of masts, the lighters hauling coal, the gulls arguing over fish guts—and made her plans the way she always did, fast and clean, before anyone could make plans for her.

There was a wreck off Sandwip, a coaster gone down in the last cyclone season, reported by fishermen but not yet claimed. Two weeks' work, maybe three. After that, a lead in Cox's Bazar. After that—well, there was always an after that. That was the whole architecture of her life: a chain of *afters*, each one far enough from the last to keep the past from catching up.

"We'll take the Sandwip job," she said. "Provision today, sail tomorrow night's tide. Tell the others."

"Tell them yourself," Priya said. "They're your family too, even if you file that under classified."

But the others found her before she could find them, gathered in the *Grace*'s cramped saloon that evening around the table where they ate, argued, and divided their pay. Tomás, still damp-haired from the last dive of the day. Priya, hands scrubbed pink. Yusuf Barlaskar, their fixer and quartermaster, a broad, patient man who could talk a customs officer out of his own uniform. And young Devi, eighteen, who'd come aboard as a cook's assistant two years ago and stayed because, she said, nowhere else let her drive a boat.

Ada stood at the head of the table with the chart of the Sandwip coast unrolled, and delivered the plan, and waited for the usual chorus of grumbling and agreement.

Instead, silence. The bad kind. The kind with weight in it.

Yusuf cleared his throat. "Before we vote on Sandwip," he said carefully, "there's something you should see. It came aboard this afternoon. While you were at the harbormaster's office."

He set a document wallet on the table. Heavy paper. Official watermark. Ada's stomach did something slow and cold before she even touched it.

"A debt collector brought it," Yusuf went on. "Well dressed. Very polite. Waited until you were gone, which I thought was interesting. Said he represented the holder of certain notes of credit." He paused. "Said we'd know the name."

Ada opened the wallet.

The papers inside were immaculate—typed, stamped, notarized, everything the opposite of how her father had died, which had been messy and wet and explained away in three sentences. She read the first page twice because the words refused to arrange themselves into sense.

Their boat was mortgaged. The *Kestrel's Grace*, every rivet and patch-weld of her, stood as collateral against debts Ada had never signed. Debts the crew had been carrying quietly for years—Tomás's sister's hospital bills in Manila, the engine refit Priya had financed when they couldn't afford to haul out, Devi's passage money owed back home, the small crushing weights that people carry and don't speak of because speaking makes them real. Someone had bought those debts. All of them. Quietly, through brokers, over months. And consolidated them under a single lien against the vessel.

And the name at the bottom of the letter of assignment, in handwriting so elegant it looked engraved:

*M. Hale & Associates, Concessions and Marine Survey.*

Marguerite Hale.

Ada set the papers down very carefully, as if they might go off.

"How long have you known?" she asked.

"Two days," Yusuf admitted. "I've been trying to find a way to unwind it. There isn't one. Whoever did this knew what they were doing. Every note was bought fair, at market rate, through legitimate channels. It's not fraud. It's worse than fraud—it's *clean*."

"The crew's debts," Ada said. "All of them?"

"All of them. Which means—" Tomás started, and stopped, and Ada watched him arrive at the arithmetic she'd already done. If the lien was called, the boat was seized, and the debts came due in full against each of them personally. Tomás's sister would lose her care. Priya would lose everything she'd sunk into the engine. Devi would owe money she couldn't earn in ten years ashore.

Hale hadn't bought a boat. Hale had bought her crew. Had bought the only family Ada had, stitch by stitch, while Ada slept thirty feet above the water, planning her next escape.

"When does he come back?" Ada asked. "Your polite collector."

"He left a card." Yusuf slid it across the table. Cream stock, embossed, an address in Kolkata. "He said the holder requests the pleasure of a meeting. Tomorrow morning. The Inland Revenue Wharf offices." A beat. "He also said—" Yusuf hesitated, clearly deciding whether to soften it, then decided against it; that was why she trusted him. "He said Ms. Hale looks forward to renewing an old acquaintance. She mentioned your father."

The saloon went very quiet. Somewhere above them, Devi stood her watch on the foredeck, and the harbor slapped against the hull, and the tide began to turn without asking anyone's permission.

Ada looked down at the signature again. *M. Hale.* Her father's old rival. The woman who'd argued with Elias Dunmore across a dozen survey conferences, who'd wanted the Sundarbans concession for her firm years before the government ever drew the maps, who'd sent flowers to the funeral with a card Ada's mother had burned unread.

Seven years Ada had run from that river. She'd measured her whole life in miles precisely so that no one—not a creditor, not a magistrate, not a ghost—could ever pin her to a place long enough to take anything from her again.

And now the past had arrived anyway. Not with a squall, not with a drowned man's body. With paperwork. Immaculate, notarized, patient paperwork.

"Tomorrow morning," Ada said. Her voice came out level, which cost her more than shouting would have. "I'll meet her alone."

"The hell you will," said Priya.

"It's my name she wants. My father's name." Ada rolled up the Sandwip chart, the next jump, the next hundred miles, and put it away in the drawer where all her escapes lived. "You all stay aboard. Whatever happens at that wharf tomorrow—we sail on the night tide either way. Nobody signs anything. Nobody owes that woman a thing but me."

But even as she said it, looking around the table at their faces—Tomás's worry, Priya's anger, Yusuf's careful blankness—she saw the debts sitting on their shoulders like ballast, and she understood, with a coldness that spread through her chest like harbor water, that Marguerite Hale hadn't needed to buy the boat at all.

She'd bought the one thing Ada couldn't cut loose.

* * *

That night Ada climbed to the wheelhouse alone. The harbor lay dark except for the riding lights, hundreds of them, each one a vessel at anchor, going nowhere until morning. She stood at the window with the mortgage papers in her hand and watched the lights bob, and thought about her father, who had loved these waters enough to die in them, and about Marguerite Hale, who had loved them enough to own them instead.

On the chart table, half-unrolled, the Sandwip coast waited—shallow, honest, forgettable. Two hundred miles north of it, past the Haringhata line, the Sundarbans sprawled across the charts in green ink, the mangrove maze her father had been surveying when the river closed over him. The concession permits were due to lapse soon; everyone in the trade knew it; the whole delta was holding its breath over who would claim the ground when they did.

Ada smoothed the mortgage flat against the chart table and read the terms once more, all the way to the clause she'd been avoiding. Buried in the fine print, printed neat as scripture:

*Recovery of services rendered shall be negotiated upon presentation of the vessel and crew at a location to be specified by the holder.*

*A location to be specified.*

Not Chittagong. Not Kolkata. Somewhere Hale would choose. Somewhere south.

Ada stood in the dark wheelhouse with the paper trembling very slightly in her hand, and for the first time in seven years, the miles she'd put between herself and that river felt less like distance than like delay.

The collector would come at dawn. And somewhere in the green maze of the Sundarbans, whatever Marguerite Hale wanted recovered was already waiting for her—patient as the tide, and just as impossible to argue with.
