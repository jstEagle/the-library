## 2. The Rival's Terms

The papers came back at noon, and with them the smell of the harbor at low tide—rot and diesel and salt gone sour in the heat. Ada sat on the *Kestrel*'s aft deck with the mortgage documents spread across her knees, reading the same line for the fourth time because the fourth time refused to be different from the first.

*Mortgaged to M. Hale Holdings (Chittagong) Ltd. All rights of salvage, charter, and conveyance transferred to lienholder until satisfaction of debt.*

Marguerite Hale. Of course it was Marguerite Hale. In the salvage trade along this coast there were perhaps a dozen people with the capital to buy a boat's debt out from under a bank, and only one of them had spent thirty years collecting concessions the way other women collected porcelain. Only one of them had known her father well enough to hate him properly.

Ada looked up. The crew was scattered down the dock in the loose geometry of a paid-off contract: Dev crouched over the winch housing with a wrench and a cigarette he wasn't smoking so much as chewing; Priya and old Tomas were arguing about engine mounts with the particular joy of people who had nothing urgent to argue about; young Rafi was asleep in the shade of the fuel drums, his cap over his face, nineteen years old and finally not hungry. Seven years she'd kept them fed and afloat and free. Seven years of routine contracts, honest wrecks, small profits, and one rule stated so often it had become a joke among them, then a proverb, then something quieter than either—the thing they simply did not do.

*We don't work the Sundarbans concession.*

She had never told them all of why. They knew her father had died surveying there when Ada was twenty-two. They knew she'd walked away from a lucrative government contract rather than bid on the follow-up survey. They knew that once, in Mongla, a broker had offered triple rates for a delta job and Ada had thrown him off the boat. That was enough. A family didn't need the autopsy of a rule; it only needed the rule.

Dev climbed aboard without asking, which was his privilege as first mate, and dropped onto the bench across from her. He nodded at the papers.

"So it's true."

"It's true."

"Hale." He said the name like spitting a seed. "How?"

"The bank sold the paper. Banks do that. Debts are just... cargo." Ada heard how flat her own voice had gone and made herself unclench her hands. "It's legal. That's what makes it filthy."

"What does she want? Nobody buys a debt to sit on it."

"I don't know yet." Ada folded the documents along their creases, once, twice, precise as folding a flag. "But I'm going to find out before I let her tell us."

* * *

Marguerite Hale received them in an office above the harbor master's building, a room with ceiling fans turning slow as thought and a view of the anchorage where forty boats rode at their moorings, every one of them, Ada suspected, owing its existence to somebody's paperwork. Hale was smaller than her reputation, silver-haired, dressed in white linen that would have looked absurd on anyone else and looked on her like a uniform. She poured tea herself, which was worse than having a servant do it. Hospitality as a weapon required a personal touch.

"Captain Dunmore." Hale's voice was low and unhurried, the voice of a woman who had never once needed to raise it to end a career. "Seven years. You've done well avoiding me."

"That wasn't difficult. We've been busy staying solvent."

"Solvent." Hale smiled and slid a ledger across the desk, open to a page of figures. "Your boat is mortgaged to me for eleven thousand two hundred taka more than she's worth on a good day. Your crew owes four months' wages against a contract you finished yesterday. Your insurance lapsed in March—you didn't renew because renewal requires a survey, and a survey requires money. Shall I go on? I've been reading your books since Tuesday. You run a beautiful operation, Captain. Beautifully broke."

Ada kept her eyes on the ledger. The numbers were right. That was the humiliation of it—they were exactly right.

"Say what you bought," she said.

"A job." Hale sat back, folding her hands. "There's a survey station upriver, in the Sundarbans concession. Station Nine. It holds a sealed archive—records from the original colonial surveys through the last expedition run under permit. Permits lapse in six weeks. When they lapse, everything inside becomes the property of the successor firm, which is to say, mine, but by then the station will have been 'cleared,' and cleared stations have a way of losing their interesting paper. I want the archive recovered intact and delivered to me before the permits expire. Do that, and I tear up every debt your crew carries. Clean slate. Boat, wages, the lot."

The fans turned. Somewhere below, a ship's horn sounded, long and mournful, like the harbor clearing its throat before saying something regrettable.

"No," Ada said.

"You haven't asked what's in the archive."

"I know what's in the archive. My father helped seal it." She stood. "Find another crew."

"Sit down, Captain." Hale didn't raise her voice; she lowered it, which was somehow worse. "I haven't finished. If you refuse, I foreclose Monday. Your boat goes to auction, your crew scatters to whatever contracts will take them, and I send a different crew upriver anyway. The archive comes out either way. The only question your refusal answers is whether the people who bring it out are the people who trust each other—or strangers working for me who've never heard your father's name."

Ada sat down because her knees advised it, not because she'd agreed to anything.

"Why?" she asked. "Why that station? Why now? There's nothing in those records worth this kind of money unless—" She stopped herself. Unless someone wanted it buried. Unless someone feared it surfacing. Unless the whole offer was shaped like a key looking for its lock.

Hale watched her work it through, and something almost like respect crossed the older woman's face. "You're your father's daughter after all. Ask fewer questions, Captain. Six weeks. My enforcer will make periodic contact. And Captain—if you try to sell the job to another party, or lose the archive, or simply dawdle, I will take the boat and blackball every member of your crew from Chittagong to Kolkata. I've done less with better reason."

* * *

Ada called the vote herself, because if it had to happen—and some cold part of her already knew it would happen—then it would happen honestly, on deck, in the open, with her standing in front of them instead of hiding behind a cabin door.

She laid it out plainly: the debt, the terms, the deadline, the station. She watched their faces as she named the place. Dev's jaw tightened. Priya went still in the way she went still before bad weather. Old Tomas took off his cap, which he did for the dead and for hard decisions, and apparently this counted as both.

"And my answer is no," Ada finished. "So we vote. Anyone who votes to take it, say so now. Anyone who votes with me—we'll lose the boat, and I won't pretend otherwise. But we'll still have each other, and we'll find work. It'll be thin for a while. It's been thin before."

Silence. The harbor slapped against the pilings. Rafi, who had never voted on anything in his life, looked from face to face like a man watching a fire decide which way to jump.

Priya spoke first, gently, the way you speak to someone you're about to hurt. "Ada. Eleven thousand taka. Four months' wages. Rafi sends half his pay home to Khulna—his mother's got debts of her own, the same kind, the kind that eat people. One clean job and we're free. Free, Ada. Not trading one lienholder for the next for the rest of our lives."

"There are other jobs."

"Not ones that erase the past. Only this one." Priya spread her hands. "You taught us the rule. You never taught us why. And I've followed it seven years without asking, because you're you. But I'm asking now, and I'm sorry, and I'm voting yes."

"Tomas?"

The old man put his cap back on, which meant the moment for mourning was over. "I'm sixty-one, girl. I can't start over again. I'm sorry too." His voice cracked on *sorry*, and he looked at the deck planks like they'd betrayed him personally.

"Dev."

Dev met her eyes, and Ada saw him carry the weight of it honestly, which was somehow the cruelest part—that he wouldn't hide behind necessity, that he'd hand her his betrayal face-to-face like a man returning borrowed tools. "You saved my life twice and my family three times," he said. "I'd burn for you. But burning isn't a plan. Yes."

"Rafi?"

The boy swallowed. "Yes, Captain. I'm sorry."

Four yeses. The vote carried without her, around her, the way water carries a piling—no malice in it, just current. Her family had chosen survival over her one sacred rule, and they hadn't even chosen it *against* her; they'd chosen it around her, apologetically, the way you step around a sleeping dog.

Ada looked at the harbor a long time. Then she turned back, and her voice came out level, which cost her more than screaming would have.

"Then we go. Under my command, by my rules. We recover the archive, we deliver it to no one until I've read every page, and if I find one line in it that smells wrong, the job ends and Hale can take the boat and choke on it. Those are my terms. Take them or mutiny now."

Nobody moved. Nobody argued. Dev nodded slowly, and Priya reached out and squeezed Ada's arm, and the touch nearly undid her.

"One more thing," Ada said. "We need a pilot. Pre-monsoon season, upriver, into the delta channels. Nobody sane runs that water in June."

Dev almost smiled, grim as it was. "Actually, Captain. There's a rumor about a pilot down at the fish quay. Rahman. They say he's the only one who'll take the job."

"They say anything for money."

"This one's different. They say he asked who the crew was before he asked the rate." Dev shrugged. "And when he heard your name, Captain—he signed on before we even offered terms."

The wind off the harbor shifted, carrying the tide's sour breath across the deck. Ada felt the river waiting for her somewhere north in the darkening green, patient as debt, patient as grief.

A pilot who knew her name before he knew his price. In her experience, men who sought you out by surname were either running toward you or running from something that shared your blood.

She'd learn which soon enough.
