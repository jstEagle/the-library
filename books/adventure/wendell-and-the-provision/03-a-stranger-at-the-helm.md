## 3. A Stranger at the Helm

The name came through the harbor office like weather off the water—arriving before you saw it, changing everything it touched.

Idris Rahman stood at the counter of the Chittagong pilot registry, third in a line of men seeking work, when the clerk read it aloud: *Dunmore*. The word went through him the way cold water goes through a hull seam, slow and then all at once.

"Salvage crew," the clerk said, not looking up. "The *Kestrel's Sparrow*. They want a delta run. Sundarbans concession."

Nobody in the line moved. Nobody would. It was late April, and the Bay had already begun to breathe wrong—the mornings too still, the horizon stacked with cloud that hadn't committed to anything yet. Every pilot with sense was laying up his boat or taking coastal work until September. Every pilot with sense knew what the Sundarbans did to charts in monsoon season, how channels that held twelve feet of draft in March could hold your keel and nothing else by June.

"I'll take it," Idris said.

The clerk finally looked up. He was a small man with ink-stained fingers and the particular patience of someone who processed desperation all day. "You know what they're asking."

"I know what they're asking."

"The Dunmore woman. They say she's particular. They say she's fired two pilots already this season for—"

"For reading her charts wrong," Idris said. "I don't read charts wrong."

He signed where the clerk indicated. His hand was steady. He made sure of that first, before anything else—made sure the pen didn't shake, because the clerk was watching now, because everyone watched everyone in a harbor town, and a man who trembled at a surname was a man who carried something.

He carried something. He had carried it eleven years, folded into a plastic sleeve at the bottom of a duffel bag he'd owned since he was nineteen: a photocopied case file, forty-one pages, the paper gone soft as cloth from handling. *State v. Rahman, T.* His brother Tariq, twenty-three years old, sentenced to nine years for theft of government survey equipment—equipment recovered intact, in its crates, three days after the arrest, from a barge that had never left port.

The file cited an expedition. *Dunmore Survey Expedition, 1979, Sundarbans Concession, ref. permit SC-114.* The testimony that put crates on a barge and a name on a charge had come from men attached to that expedition. Men paid, his mother had always said, though she'd never been able to prove it. Men whose expedition leader had died in the delta the following spring, drowned, they said, when his survey launch capsized in a channel every pilot in Bengal knew was safe.

Elias Dunmore. Ada Dunmore's father.

Idris had spent eleven years learning everything about that man the way other men learned scripture—piecemeal, obsessively, from bar talk and shipping manifests and a retired harbormaster who drank too much and remembered too much. And in all those years he had never once heard the daughter's name spoken without the father's shadow behind it. *Dunmore's girl. Runs salvage out of Chittagong. Good hands. Won't touch the delta.*

*Won't touch the delta.* That had been the one fact about her that mattered, the one that had kept Idris from walking up to her berth years ago and saying *your father's expedition put my brother in prison, look at this file, help me.* Because a woman who refused the Sundarbans was a woman who had built her whole life on the far side of a locked door, and you didn't get anywhere hammering on a locked door. You waited for someone else to open it.

Now she was hiring a pilot to take her through it. Now the door was open, and Idris intended to walk through it standing next to her.

* * *

He found the *Kestrel's Sparrow* berthed at the south wall, and he stood on the quay for a long moment just looking at her, because a boat tells you more about her owner than any interview will.

She was a converted coastal freighter, sixty feet, steel-hulled, with a salvage crane bolted amidships and the rust streaks of hard use down both flanks. But her lines were fair. Her fenders were hung square. Someone had painted her name recently, in careful white letters, and someone had coiled the lines on her pins in a way that spoke of discipline rather than show. A working boat, kept working, kept honest. Not the vessel of a woman running from anything. The vessel of a woman running *toward*, always toward, and never arriving.

Ada Dunmore came up the gangway carrying a crate of chart tubes, and Idris understood immediately why two pilots hadn't lasted. She was not tall, but she occupied space completely, the way a channel marker occupies water—you adjusted around her or you struck her. Her hair was cut short and practical, sun-bleached at the ends, and her eyes did a thing he recognized from his own mirror: they took inventory. Of him. Of his boots, his hands, the salt wear on his jacket. In four seconds she had priced him, and he watched her arrive at a number she wasn't sure she trusted.

"You're the one who signed this morning." Her voice was flat river English with a Bengali undertow beneath it. "Everyone else is laying up till September. You know that."

"I know that."

"So either you're desperate, stupid, or you want something." She set the crate down on the quay. "Which is it?"

Honesty, he decided, but only the portion she could carry. "Desperate pays the same as stupid. I have a sister-in-law and a nephew in Khulna. My brother's boat was sold out from under the family when he—" He stopped, chose the shape of the lie with care. "When he lost his papers. There's no delta work for a man without papers except the kind that gets you drowned. Your contract is legitimate. I need legitimate."

It was close enough to true that it sat easily in his mouth. It was also, he noted, exactly the kind of story she would check—and he had made sure it checked. The sister-in-law existed. The nephew existed. The sold boat existed. Eleven years of grief gave a man plenty of true material to build a false wall with.

Ada studied him a moment longer. "References?"

"Ask the harbormaster. Ask anyone who runs the Meghna estuary. I've piloted the delta since I was fifteen. My grandfather ran timber barges up the Passur when the British still thought they owned it."

"And the pre-monsoon season? You've run it?"

"Twice." He let that land. "Once coming back. Once not needing to."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite humor, but adjacent to it. "That's the most honest sentence anyone's said to me this month." She picked the crate back up. "Come aboard. We'll talk money, and then I'll tell you the parts of this job nobody puts in a posting."

* * *

The parts, delivered in the wheelhouse while the crew worked cargo below: a sealed archive at an abandoned survey station, six weeks before colonial permits lapsed, a client named Marguerite Hale whom Ada pronounced like a bone she was spitting out.

"She bought our debts," Ada said. She stood at the wheelhouse window with her arms crossed, watching the harbor, not watching him—a woman who found it easier to say difficult things to water than to faces. "Every taka we owed, every lien on the boat. Clean slate if we bring her this archive. Which means we go where I swore I'd never go, for a woman I'd cross the street to avoid, and I don't get a vote because apparently debt cancels votes."

Idris kept his face still and his heart loud. *The station.* His brother's file had a map appendix, and the map appendix had marked the station—SC-114, the survey camp where the crates had supposedly been stolen from, where the testimony had been sworn. He had assumed it was rubble. He had assumed whatever it held had rotted or scattered decades ago.

"And you took the job," he said carefully.

"My crew voted." Her jaw tightened. "I don't get to be a martyr on their backs. So yes. We're going." She turned and looked at him directly for the first time, and her eyes did the inventory thing again, deeper this time. "One question before I sign you. Everyone in this harbor knows my last name. Everyone knows what happened to my father here. Most people who take this job will spend the whole run wondering if I'm going to fall apart when we pass the place he died. Are you going to wonder?"

The question was a trap and a test at once, and Idris felt the whole architecture of his silence tremble—because here it was, offered to him on a plate: *ask me what I know, ask me about 1979, ask me about the file.* One question from her and he could begin. He could put the photocopied pages on her chart table right now.

But he had waited eleven years, and he had learned what impatience cost. If he led with the file, she would do what grieving daughters did: defend the dead, close the door, put him ashore at the first fuel stop. No. He needed her to trust him first. He needed to be the pilot who brought her safely through the water that killed her father, so that when the truth came—and it would come; truth in the delta always surfaced eventually, like the drowned villages did after big floods—it arrived wearing a trusted face.

"No," he said. "I'm not going to wonder. Grief isn't cargo. It rides free or it doesn't ride."

Something shifted in her expression—surprise, quickly banked, but real. "My father used to say almost exactly that."

"I know," Idris said, and then heard himself, and corrected smoothly: "River men all say it. It's an old saying."

She nodded slowly, and signed him on at a rate slightly above his asking, which told him she'd been prepared to pay more and respected him for not asking. Below them, the crew worked on, laughing about something, unaware. The *Kestrel's Sparrow* rocked against her fenders. Somewhere across the harbor a ship's horn sounded, low and long, like something clearing its throat before speaking.

* * *

He went home that night and took the file out of the duffel for the first time in a year. Forty-one soft pages under a bare bulb in his rented room above the chandler's. He read the passages he knew by heart anyway, the way a man touches a scar to confirm it's still there.

*Testimony of R. Okonkwo, survey technician, re: removal of equipment from Station SC-114, witnessed transport aboard lighter 44-B...* Men's names he had memorized like enemy orders of battle. And in the margin of page thirty-one, in the handwriting of some bored court clerk, a note that had kept Idris awake for a decade: *Original survey records referenced but not produced. See concession archive.*

*See concession archive.*

For eleven years he had thought those words pointed at nothing—at paper burned or pulped or lost in some government basement. Tonight, for the first time, they pointed somewhere. At a sealed room in a stripped station at the end of a dying channel, six weeks before the permits expired and whatever remained inside passed into whoever's hands Hale's lawyers arranged.

His brother had eight months left on a sentence built on evidence from that station. Eight months, and Ada Dunmore was sailing straight at the answer with a salvage crane and a crew that loved her, piloted by a man lying to her with every steady word.

Idris slid the file back into the duffel and lay in the dark listening to the harbor work its night shift. He was not a dishonest man. He needed her to understand that about him eventually, needed her to believe it, because the difference between using someone and betraying them was whether you meant to make it right at the end.

He meant to make it right. He told himself that until it sounded true, and slept badly, and woke before dawn to load charts.

At the quay, the first person he saw was Ada, already aboard, already working, and beside her at the rail a woman he didn't know—expensive coat, harbor-inappropriate shoes, a smile like a signed document. Ada's face wore an expression Idris had never seen on it, and needed no file to translate:

The past had arrived with paperwork, and it wanted to come aboard.
