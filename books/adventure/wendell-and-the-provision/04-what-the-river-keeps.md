## 4. What the River Keeps

The delta began the way a fever begins—gradually, then all at once.

For two days out of Khulna the rivers had been wide and businesslike, brown as tea, lined with brick kilns and jute barges and the ordinary commerce of people who had not chosen to be here. Then the channels braided, split, forgot their own names. The banks went soft and green. The horizon dissolved into a haze of water and mangrove that could have been ten miles or a hundred, and Ada stood at the bow of the *Magpie* with her father's old survey charts unrolled on her knees and understood that she was looking at a country that had stopped agreeing to exist.

"Chart's wrong," said Priya behind her, squinting at the same water through the rangefinder. "Or the river is."

"The river," Ada said.

She had known it would be. She'd told herself she was prepared for it—the way you tell yourself you're prepared for a funeral. But knowing a thing and standing inside it were different animals, and the animal currently looking back at her from the water had her father's handwriting all over its face.

He had drawn these channels in 1979. She had been six years old, asleep in the cabin of his survey launch while he worked the echo sounder past midnight, and she had woken more than once to the smell of pencil shavings and diesel and the sound of him humming something tuneless and content. The charts in her hands were copies of copies, but the hand was unmistakable: the small precise letters, the little arrows marking current direction, the annotations in Bengali he'd never bothered to translate for her because, he said, *you'll learn it when you need it.*

She had never learned it. She had left before she needed it.

"Ada." Idris's voice came down from the wheelhouse, flat as the water. "You'll want to see this."

* * *

What she wanted to see was nothing. What she saw was a village.

Or the idea of one—a grid of rooftops just breaking the surface of a wide, slow channel, terracotta tiles furred green with algae, a minaret leaning at an angle that suggested surrender rather than collapse. A banana tree stood entirely submerged, its leaves moving gently in the current like something waving goodbye. Around the drowned houses, wooden stakes rose from the water in rows: the frames of fishing platforms, still maintained, still used, by people who had simply moved up instead of away.

"People live here?" Priya asked.

"People lived *there*," Idris said. He had come down to stand beside them at the rail, arms folded, eyes doing the pilot's slow arithmetic of depth and current. "Char villages. The land builds itself up, the land washes away. Sometimes both in the same year. That one went under in—" he paused, recalculating, "—'84? '85. There are maps that still show it dry."

Ada looked at the minaret. Her father's chart showed a market town there. *Rampur Bazar,* in his tidy capitals, with a note about a ferry crossing and a warning about shoaling. She turned the chart over, uselessly, as if the village might have relocated to the blank side.

"It moved," she said.

"Everything here moves," Idris said. "That's the first lesson. The second lesson is that the map is always a letter written to someone who has already left."

He said it without poetry, the way you'd recite a shipping regulation, and it landed in Ada like poetry anyway. She thought of her father writing those letters. She thought of how many of them she'd refused to read.

* * *

They anchored that night in a side channel off the main stem, tucked into a screen of mangrove that smelled of mud and salt and something faintly sweet, like rotting flowers. Dev cooked rice and dried fish over the deck stove, and the crew ate in the tired, quiet rhythm of people conserving energy they didn't have to spare. Six weeks until the permits lapsed. Four days spent. The math got worse every time Ada did it, so she did it constantly, the way you press a bruise.

Idris ate apart from them, at the stern, the way he always did—not hostile, just separate, a man keeping his own books. He'd been with them nine days now and had said perhaps four hundred words total, most of them navigational. He knew the delta the way she knew the inside of her own eyelids, which was to say intimately and without affection. When she asked him questions about channels, he answered. When she asked him anything else, he looked at her with those flat dark eyes and waited until she gave up.

Tonight she didn't give up.

"My father surveyed these channels," she said, settling onto the rail near him. Not too near. "Nineteen seventy-nine through eighty-one. You'd have been a boy."

"I was."

"Did you know him?"

The question hung in the mosquito-humming dark. Idris chewed, swallowed, looked out at the black water where the drowned village lay somewhere beyond the mangroves.

"Everyone on this river knew Elias Dunmore," he said finally. "He was the only foreigner who ever learned the names of the channels. Not the official names. The real ones."

"He never mentioned that."

"He wouldn't." Idris set down his plate. "A man doesn't mention the things that make him different from other men. It invites correction."

It was the longest speech she'd gotten from him, and every word of it felt load-bearing, structural, hiding something behind the wall. She leaned forward.

"What else do you remember?"

"Currents," Idris said, and stood, and took his plate below.

* * *

The third day upriver, the delta tried to kill her.

It happened fast, the way everything happened here—no drama, no warning horn, just water deciding to be somewhere else. They were running a narrow channel between two char islands, following her father's chart because Idris had gone forward to read the water and had called back that the marks looked true. Ada was at the bow with the lead line, swinging it, calling depths. Eight feet. Seven. Six and a half—

The bank simply opened.

There was no crack, no groan. One moment the mud wall beside her was a bank; the next it was a wave, a whole slab of the island sliding into the channel with a sound like a held breath released, and the *Magpie*'s bow swung toward the hole it left, and Ada—who had been crouched at the very edge with the lead line—felt the deck tilt under her and the world go brown and sideways.

She heard Idris shout. She heard Dev scream her name. She did not hear any of it properly, because she was already in the water.

The Sundarbans in early monsoon was not cold, but it was *heavy*—water with silt in its muscles, water that pulled. It took her under and turned her over and she came up gasping with her mouth full of the river, tasting four decades of everything the river had been given. The collapsing bank had made a whirlpool, a slow brown gyre sucking toward the breach, and she was in it, and the lead line was wrapped around her wrist, and the weight at the end of it was doing what weights do.

*Cut the line.* Her hands, scrabbling at her belt knife, were stupid with cold-shock and panic. *Cut the line, cut the—*

An arm closed around her chest from behind.

Idris had come off the boat without a rope, without a life vest, without anything but himself, and now he hauled her backward against the suction with a strength that seemed to come from somewhere outside his lean frame. Her head broke water. The *Magpie* loomed, Dev hauling on the throttle to swing the stern around, Priya throwing a coil that fell short, then another that didn't.

Hands. Deck. Planks under her cheek, her ribs heaving, the lead line still cinched to her wrist with the twelve-pound weight swinging free above the water like a pendulum counting down to nothing. Someone cut it. The weight dropped away and the sound of it hitting the river was very small and very final.

Idris knelt beside her, streaming river water, breathing hard for the first time since she'd known him.

"You kept the line in your hand," he said. "In salvage. In *salvage*. You know better."

"I know better," Ada agreed, and laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle, and turned into something else entirely.

She cried the way she hadn't cried at the funeral, or after the funeral, or in seven years of competent, well-managed grief—ugly and loud and doubled over on the wet deck of a leaking boat in the middle of the country she'd sworn never to enter again. The crew, wisely, found reasons to be elsewhere. Idris stayed. He didn't touch her again. He just sat on his heels in the sun, dripping, waiting, the way you wait out weather.

When it was over she sat up, wrung out her hair, and discovered she was shaking.

"Why did you jump?" she asked. "You don't even like me. You took this job for your own reasons and you don't tell me what they are and you eat alone at the stern of my boat."

"I don't eat alone because I don't like you," Idris said. He looked out at the channel, at the raw brown wound in the bank where the island was still quietly finishing its collapse. "I eat alone because the last time I trusted a Dunmore, my brother went to prison."

The words dropped between them and sank.

"Your file," Ada said slowly. "Your brother's case. The evidence came from—"

"From the last expedition. Yours truly. Elias Dunmore's final survey, 1987." His voice was level, but she could see the cost of the levelness in his jaw, in the tendons of his neck. "Two crates of survey materials handed to the district magistrate. Photographs, logs, testimony notes. Enough to convict Karim Rahman of theft from a government concession he had never entered." He turned and looked at her, and his eyes were not flat at all; they were the deepest thing on the river. "Your father signed the chain-of-custody form. Three weeks later he was dead. And I have spent eleven years wondering which of those facts explains the other."

The monsoon clouds were building in the west, tall and blue-black, stacked like cargo. Somewhere ahead of them, three days up a channel that no longer matched any chart, the survey station waited with whatever it had waited eleven years to give up.

"I didn't know," Ada said. "About the conviction. About any of it."

"No," Idris said. "I know. I've known since Chittagong. I've been waiting to see if it mattered to you." He rose, wrung out his shirt, and offered her a hand up—dry-handed, steady, the hand of a man making a decision. "It matters to me that you're about to find out what your father was carrying when he died. Because I think, Miss Dunmore, that he wasn't carrying evidence against my brother at all."

The first raindrop hit the deck between them, fat and warm as a coin.

"I think he was carrying proof of what Hale's company did to get that concession," Idris said. "And somebody killed him to keep it underwater."

The rain arrived like a decision.
