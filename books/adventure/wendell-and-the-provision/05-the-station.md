## 5. The Station

The station announced itself the way the dead do—by absence.

Idris saw it first as a gap in the treeline, a pale rectangle where the mangroves refused to grow, and then as a shape: a long low building on stilts, its corrugated roof furred green with moss, a signal mast leaning against the sky like a man too tired to stand straight. Behind it, half-swallowed by banyan, stood the old barracks and a water tower with a bullet hole through its belly that had been there since before either of them was born.

He cut the engine and let the boat drift the last hundred meters, because there was no reason to arrive loudly at a place like this.

"Survey Station Nine," Ada said beside him. She had her father's chart open across her knees, and her finger rested on the inked notation without touching it, the way you'd hover a hand over a grave marker. "He drew this when he was twenty-six."

"You've never been here?"

"Once. I was nine. He carried me up the ladder on his shoulders and told me the whole delta was going to be mapped before I grew up." Her voice was flat in the way he'd learned meant the opposite of calm. "Every channel named. Every island accounted for."

"And then?" Idris asked, though he knew.

"And then they stopped letting him finish." She folded the chart along its old creases, careful as a nurse folding sheets around a body. "Bring us in. The east side. There'll be a landing stage under the stilts if it hasn't rotted through."

It hadn't rotted through. That was the first wrong thing. Idris noticed it as he looped the mooring line—the pilings were black with age but sound, and the boards of the landing stage had been replaced within the last few years, cheap treated pine still sharp-edged, no green in the grain. Someone had maintained a path into an abandoned station.

He said nothing. He was getting good at saying nothing around Ada Dunmore. It was becoming, he suspected, a kind of dishonesty he'd have to answer for eventually.

* * *

Inside, the main hall was a cathedral of dust and empty shelving.

Steel racks ran the length of the room in ranks, floor to ceiling, thousands of slots for map drawers and document boxes, and every slot gaped open and hollow. Dust lay over everything in a gray felt thick enough to hold their footprints—but not uniformly. Through the middle of the room ran a swept corridor, wide as a trolley, and the dust on the lower shelves had been disturbed in long clean drags, as if heavy boxes had been pulled out recently and hauled away.

Ada walked the aisle slowly, trailing one finger along a shelf edge, and came back with the finger gray to the knuckle.

"This isn't looting," she said.

"No," Idris agreed.

"Looters take what's valuable and wreck what isn't. Look at it—" she turned a slow circle, and he watched her mind work the way he'd watched her read water, fast and total. "The racks are intact. The windows aren't broken. There's no trash, no sleeping rolls, none of the mess people leave when they squat somewhere. And the dust tells you exactly what happened. Somebody came with a vehicle or a barge, worked methodically from the door inward, and took *everything* in the catalogued sections. Recently. Within the last year, maybe two. The dust hasn't had time to even out."

"The concession office," Idris said. "When the permits lapsed, someone would have been sent to secure the records."

"Secure." She laughed once, without any humor in it at all. "Yes. That's one word for it." She stopped at the far end of the hall, where a door stood half open on a stairwell going down. A paper seal had been stuck across the jamb—a strip of official cream stock, stamped in blue—and though the stamp had faded, the adhesive hadn't finished failing. The edges still held.

Idris looked at the seal and felt the skin tighten across his shoulders. Paper didn't rot that fast in dry air. That seal was newer than the dust everywhere else. Newer than the swept corridor. Whoever had emptied the shelves had come, gone, and then someone else had come after and sealed the empty room behind them—recently, deliberately, officially.

"Ada," he said quietly.

"I see it." She didn't touch the seal. She stood looking at it for a long moment, and he saw her throat move. "My father's last letter to my mother was posted from Khulna, six days before he died. He wrote that the survey was done, that the final archive was being sealed at Station Nine pending transfer, and that he'd be home by the new moon." Her voice stayed level; only her hands betrayed her, pressing the crease of the folded chart flatter and flatter as if trying to iron history smooth. "The inquiry found the archive had already been removed for storage in Calcutta. Standard procedure, they said. Nothing unusual."

"And now we find a room sealed *after* it was supposedly emptied," Idris said. "By someone who wanted it to look closed."

"Yes." She turned to face him, and in the dim light her eyes were the color of the river in shadow. "Which means somebody has been lying about this room for twenty years, and somebody else thought it was worth re-lying about recently enough to use fresh paste."

* * *

They spent the afternoon on the upper floors, and the upper floors told a different story.

Where the archive hall had been stripped with bureaucratic thoroughness, the working rooms had been abandoned mid-sentence. In the drafting room, a tide table for a year twenty years gone still hung on its roller. A mug sat fossilized to a desk ring. In the radio room, the set had been smashed—not looted, smashed, its vacuum tubes ground into the floorboards with a boot heel—and Idris crouched over the wreckage longer than he needed to, because men who destroyed radios before leaving were men who did not want anyone calling out from here.

Ada moved through it all silently, opening drawers, running her hands along wall seams, and Idris understood what she was doing before she said it, because he'd have done the same. She wasn't searching the rooms. She was reading them. Looking for the places a man who knew this building would hide something from the people who came to strip it.

She found it at dusk, in the surveyor's quarters, in the last place anyone would look because it was the first place anyone would look: the mattress frame of the narrow bunk by the window. But not under it. Inside it. The steel tubing of the frame had been cut, hinged, and re-welded so cleanly you'd only find the seam if you knew to press your thumb against the cold metal and feel the join.

A oilcloth packet, flat and heavy, wrapped in waxed string.

Ada sat down on the bunk with it in both hands and didn't move for almost a minute. The light through the window had gone the color of weak tea. Somewhere out in the mangroves a bird started its evening argument, and the river breathed against the pilings below, and Idris stood in the doorway and waited, because there are moments you don't walk into uninvited.

"He slept here," she said finally. "The summer he surveyed the eastern channels. My mother kept his letters. He complained about the bed—it squeaked. He said it squeaked because he'd broken it and repaired it himself and the weld wouldn't hold." A breath that was almost a laugh. "He was lying to her. He was telling her exactly where it was, in code, in case anything happened to him."

"Open it," Idris said gently.

She untied the string with fingers that weren't quite steady.

Inside were field notebooks—three of them, canvas-bound, swollen with damp but legible—and beneath them a bundle of papers that made her go very still. Survey tracings. Land schedules. And letters on concession letterhead, the same firm name Idris had seen on the permit documents Hale carried, the same firm whose signature block sat at the bottom of every legal fiction that had ever been used to take land in this delta.

Ada read. Idris watched the color leave her face by degrees, like a tide going out.

"It's all here," she said, and her voice came out strange, distant, as if she were reading someone else's eulogy. "The concession maps. The real ones. The permits say the land was surveyed as uncultivated wasteland—unclaimed, unworked, free to grant. These—" she lifted a tracing with a shaking hand— "these show forty-one villages inside the concession boundary. Rice land. Orchard land. Graveyards, Idris. They mapped the graveyards and classified them as wasteland. There are thumbprint petitions here—villagers attesting they'd farmed these fields for generations. Dated *before* the grant. And there are minutes from the firm's own meetings discussing how to handle 'the occupancy question,' which is a word for people, and how to make them not be there anymore."

"Evictions," Idris said.

"Worse. Some of these pages are police correspondence." She held a sheet toward the window light, and he saw the official crest, the typed lines, the initials scrawled in approval. "Coordinating clearance operations with the district authorities. Lists of names. Men marked as agitators, marked for detention." Her hand dropped. "Marked for *removal*."

And then she went silent, because she'd reached the last notebook, and whatever was in it she read three times, her lips moving, and when she looked up at him her eyes were wet and burning at once.

"My father testified," she said. "That's what this is. His own record of his testimony to the commission—he kept a copy. He certified the boundary surveys himself, and then he found out what the boundaries were for, and he went to the land commission and told them the concession was built on stolen ground. He documented everything. He gave them *everything*." She pressed the notebook flat against her sternum. "And six weeks later his boat capsized in a channel he'd run two hundred times, and the commission's report came out citing 'inconclusive evidence,' and the concession proceeded, and everyone who signed those eviction orders got a career instead of a cell."

The bird outside had gone quiet. Even the river seemed to be listening.

"They ruled it accidental drowning," Idris said. It wasn't a question. He knew the shape of that finding better than she did. He'd grown up inside its echo. "An experienced surveyor, alone, in waters he knew better than anyone alive."

"In a season with no storms." She stood, and the packet of her father's life was clutched against her chest, and her voice cracked clean down the middle. "He didn't capsize, Idris. He was silenced. This is proof he was silenced—he *knew*, he hid it where only family would look, he wrote to my mother that he'd be home by the new moon because he expected to *be* home—"

She stopped. Went white.

"What," Idris said.

"The seal downstairs." She was already moving, past him, down the stairs, taking them two at a time, and he followed her into the darkening archive hall where the cream-colored strip still clung to the empty doorway. She stopped in front of it and pointed with a hand that trembled with something beyond grief now.

"That seal is fresh. The shelves were cleared years ago—but someone came back. Recently. After the permits started lapsing, after Hale bought our debts, after this job became inevitable." She turned to him, and the last of the daylight caught her face, and what he saw in it frightened him more than Vasquez ever could. "Someone knows this station matters. Someone knows what might still be hidden here. And they sealed an empty room to make sure no one would wonder why."

"Ada—"

"If they came back for the archive," she said, very quietly, "and it wasn't where the records said it should be—"

Down at the landing stage, out in the blue dusk, an engine started. Not theirs.

Idris was moving before the sound finished registering, hand closing on her arm, pulling her toward the back stairs and the mangrove dark beyond the stilts, and the last thing he saw of the hall was his own footprints and hers in the dust—two fresh trails leading to a room that had been waiting twenty years for someone to ask it the right question, while somewhere out on the black water, someone else arrived asking their own.
