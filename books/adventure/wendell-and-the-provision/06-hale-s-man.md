## 6. Hale's Man

The second boat came upriver at dusk, running without lights.

Ada heard it before she saw it—a diesel note deeper than their own engine's, a big-block throb that had no business in water this shallow. She was on deck with a mug of tea going cold in her hands, watching the last light drain out of the sky over the mangroves, and the sound reached her through the channel mist like something walking on the bottom of the river and rising.

"Idris." She said it quietly, but he was already there, materializing out of the wheelhouse the way he did, as if he'd been standing behind her for some time. He'd heard it too. Of course he had.

"One engine," he said. "Big one. Steel hull, by the sound of the wake against the banks." He tilted his head, listening the way other men read. "Not a fisherman."

"No," Ada said. "Fishermen don't run dark."

They watched the channel together. The mist hung low and gray-green, and somewhere in it a boat the length of theirs or longer was coming up the Sibsa at eight knots with its navigation lights doused, which meant only one of two things: smuggling, or someone who didn't want to be seen arriving. In Ada's experience the two categories overlapped more than either liked to admit.

The crew gathered without being called. Sanjit came up from the engine room wiping his hands on a rag that had been black since before Ada met him; Priya appeared at the rail with her hair still wet from the pump room; old Feruz climbed the ladder from the hold with the unhurried dignity of a man who had decided long ago that panic was for people with worse pensions. They lined the rail like passengers on a ferry, all of them looking at nothing, all of them listening.

The sound grew. Then, forty meters off their port bow, it stopped.

The silence was worse. An engine cutting dead in open channel meant a man who knew exactly where he was and didn't need to listen to find out.

A searchlight snapped on and pinned them like insects.

Ada threw up a hand against it, blinking hard, and when her eyes adjusted she made out the shape beyond the glare: a converted patrol launch, gunmetal gray, high freeboard, a winch on the foredeck that had never winched anything legal in its life. Figures moved along its rail. At the bow, alone, stood a woman in a field jacket, hands loose at her sides, face invisible behind the light.

"Marguerite Hale sends her regards," the woman called, and her voice carried flat across the water, unaccented, unhurried, the voice of someone reading terms aloud. "You'd be Dunmore's crew."

*Her crew.* The possessive landed exactly where it was aimed. Ada felt Priya stiffen beside her.

"And you'd be?" Ada called back.

"Vasquez." A pause, letting the name sit there doing its work. "I'm your escort for the remainder of the contract. Ms. Hale wants the archive recovered cleanly and on schedule. Permits lapse in thirty-one days. I'm here to make sure nothing interferes with that."

"Nothing," Ada said, "like what?"

The light held steady on them. Behind it, Vasquez's voice didn't change register at all. "Like delays. Like complications. Like records that leave the station in any condition other than sealed and delivered. Ms. Hale is very specific about what constitutes the complete archive." Another pause. "She's also specific about witnesses."

The word went through the deck like a spike through a plank. Ada heard Feruz shift his weight. She heard Sanjit stop breathing for half a second.

"That's a strange thing to say about a salvage job," Ada said, keeping her voice level, and hating how much effort it took.

"It's not a salvage job." Vasquez said it simply, the way you'd correct a child's arithmetic. "It's a recovery. There's a difference, and you'll learn it. We move at first light. I'll take point—my pilot knows the channels better than yours does." The light clicked off, abrupt as a door closing. "Get some sleep, Dunmore. You look like a woman who doesn't."

The launch's engine turned over, a low animal growl, and the gray shape slid past them down the channel into the dark, close enough that Ada could see two men at its rail watching them go, and one of them smiling.

Nobody spoke until the sound faded below the frogs and the river.

"Well," said Feruz at last, into the blackness. "That's put the cat among the pigeons."

* * *

They argued below decks with the lamp turned low, voices pitched under the hum of the bilge pump, an argument conducted in the grammar of people who have been a family too long to bother with pleasantries.

"She just told us she's going to kill us," Priya said. "You all heard it. *Witnesses.* That's not a threat, that's a schedule."

"Hale won't—" Sanjit started.

"Hale bought our debts, Sanjit. Hale sent a gunboat up a river to babysit us. Hale's man just used the word *witnesses* like she was reading a grocery list." Priya's voice cracked and she pressed on anyway. "We're not crew anymore. We're loose ends with salaries."

"We're also broke," Sanjit said, miserable, honest. "Thirty-one days and we're done, debts cleared, boat ours again. Or we turn around now and we spend the next ten years paying Hale back through lawyers we can't afford, if she even lets it get to court. You think a woman like that leaves paper trails? She'll bury us in filings until we drown."

"So we finish the job and hope she lets us walk away?" Priya laughed, and it wasn't a laugh at all. "Sanjit. She said *nothing else standing.*"

"Enough." Ada hadn't spoken since they'd come below. Now she did, and the room quieted, because they were used to her voice deciding things, and that was precisely the problem. She looked around the lamplit circle of their faces—the faces she'd spent seven years keeping fed and afloat, the family she'd built out of other people's shipwrecks—and felt the rule she'd lived by bend under her hands like bad planking.

"Idris," she said. "You've been quiet."

He sat apart, near the chart table, and he'd been tracing a line on the survey map with one finger, not really seeing it. When he looked up, his face was the closed, patient face she'd come to know, the pilot's face, giving away current and nothing else.

"I've been thinking about my brother," he said.

The room stilled. He'd mentioned Karim twice in three weeks, both times sideways, both times like a man touching a burn to check whether it still hurt.

"The file that convicted him cites your father's expedition. Survey evidence. Chain of custody through the concession office." His finger stopped on the map, on the small printed square marking the station. "If that archive is down here—if any of the original record survived—it cuts both ways. It could hang him again. Or it could prove every page they used against him was manufactured." He looked at Ada. "Vasquez wants it sealed and delivered. Sealed means unread. Unread means Karim rots."

"You think I'd hand it over unread?"

"I think you'd hand it over to keep these people alive." No accusation in it. Just the pilot's flat honesty, current reported as found. "And I think Vasquez knows that. I think that's why she said *witnesses* where you could hear it. Fear makes people compliant. She's seasoning you."

Ada looked at her crew. Sanjit staring at his hands. Priya's jaw set like poured concrete. Old Feruz, who had survived three owners and two sinkings, watching her with the weary attention of a man waiting to see which way the wind would swing.

"Here's what we do," she said slowly, feeling her way along the words like a diver feeling along a wreck in bad visibility. "We work the job. We do it visibly, by the book, where Vasquez can see us being good little contractors. And whatever comes out of that station gets copied before it gets sealed. Every page. Priya, your camera. If it costs us the contract, it costs us the contract."

"And if Vasquez catches us copying?" Priya asked.

Ada thought of the smile on the man at the rail. "Then we'd better not get caught."

* * *

It rained that night, the first real rain, warm and heavy, drumming on the deck plates like fingers testing for weakness. Ada couldn't sleep. She stood her own watch out of habit rather than necessity, wrapped in an oilskin at the rail, watching the channel breathe.

She heard the dinghy before she saw it—one paddle stroke too many, then silence, the particular silence of a boat holding position. Her hand found the flare gun under the wheelhouse coaming before her mind finished the thought.

"Easy." Idris's whisper, off her shoulder. "It's me. I went out to look at their anchor light."

"And?"

"They anchored wrong." He climbed aboard, water sheeting off him, and crouched beside her at the rail, close enough that she could smell the river on him, mud and rain and diesel. "Deliberately wrong. Their light sits over twelve feet of water. Their draft is nine. Any fisherman passing would read it and steer wide of nothing." He paused. "But a boat that draws four feet could come at them from the bank side, in the dark, and they'd never see it until contact."

Ada stared at him. "You're telling me their anchorage has a hole in it."

"I'm telling you Vasquez's pilot anchored them like a man expecting visitors." His teeth showed briefly in the dark, not quite a smile. "Or like a man planning some."

The rain thickened. Somewhere down the channel, a night heron cried once and gave up.

"Idris." She kept her voice under the rain. "What aren't you telling me? About my father. About why you really took this job. Because I've watched you read this river for three weeks and you read it like you grew up in it, and tonight you read Vasquez's anchorage like you've run guns past worse. So."

For a long moment he said nothing, and the rain filled the silence, and she let it.

"My brother was arrested eleven days after your father's expedition filed its final survey," he said at last. "Eleven days. I was nineteen. I spent the next fifteen years learning everything about that expedition, and about the man who led it, and about the concession that ate our village's land title and called it development." He turned his head, and even in the dark she felt the weight of his regard. "Your father's name was on the permit. Elias Dunmore. Chief surveyor. The man whose instruments drew the lines that erased us."

Ada's chest went tight. "He didn't know—"

"Maybe. Maybe not. That's what the archive will tell me." He rose, water running off him, and stood at the rail looking toward the gray smudge where Vasquez's launch lay at anchor. "But here's what I know tonight, Ada Dunmore. That woman didn't come upriver to guard an archive. She came to end a story. And tomorrow we sail into the last chapter with her."

He swung himself back over the side into the dinghy, and the rain took him, and Ada stood her watch alone with her heart going like a pump in a flooding hold.

* * *

The skirmish, when it came, came three nights later, and it came the way disasters always come: from the direction everyone had checked least.

They'd made the outer approach to the station creek and anchored in the lee of a drowned char, a sliver of silt island that surfaced at low tide like the back of a sleeping animal. Vasquez's launch lay two hundred meters downstream, her riding lamp burning steady and proper. All day the two boats had performed their pantomime of cooperation—Vasquez's men coming alongside to inspect their progress, Vasquez herself standing on Ada's deck with a clipboard, asking questions she already knew the answers to, her eyes moving over the bulkheads, the hold, the crew, cataloguing.

Ada had almost begun to believe the night would pass like the others.

She woke at 0300 because the bilge alarm was screaming.

No—not the bilge alarm. The bilge alarm was screaming *also*, but underneath it, the sound that had torn her from sleep was different: the wet, tearing crunch of hull meeting hull, of steel finding wood below the waterline.

She was out of her bunk and moving before the second crunch. The companionway ladder, the deck, the rain—when had the rain started again?—and the scene resolved itself in pieces, the way wrecks resolve themselves, fragment by fragment.

A black inflatable, engines muffled, backing off their port quarter. Two figures in it. And on their port side, forward of the engine room, a hole in the boat.

Not a hole yet. A wound. The inflatable had rammed them with a sharpened girder lashed to its bow, a boarding spike out of some pirate's nightmare, and it had gone in below the waterline between two frames, and the river was already coming in through it with the calm enthusiasm of water doing what water does.

"Fenders! Plugs! Get me anything!" Ada was already hauling the collision mat from its locker, dropping to her knees on the flooded foredeck, pressing the heavy canvas against the gash while the river pushed back against her palms like a living thing. Cold water surged around her wrists, her elbows, soaking her to the shoulders in seconds.

Lights bloomed downstream—Vasquez's launch, running lights snapping on, searchlight swinging lazily across the water, illuminating nothing, illuminating the empty channel, illuminating a performance. The inflatable was already a shadow among shadows, gone.

"They holed us and their guardian angel slept through it," Priya shouted, hauling the pump handle, water arcing out over the rail. "That's not negligence, Ada. That's choreography!"

Ada pressed the mat harder. Under her knees the deck tilted, slow and sickening, the boat learning a new angle. Below her feet, in the dark, the river poured in through a wound the size of a forearm, and she understood—with the clarity that arrives only in the worst moments—that Vasquez hadn't tried to kill them. Killing them would be messy, investigable. This was neater. A crippled boat in rising water, days from anywhere, a crew too busy surviving to copy documents, dependent on the nearest vessel for rescue.

Dependent on Vasquez.

"Sanjit! Shoring timbers, the long ones—and get the cement box ready!" She was shouting into the rain, into the dark, into the tilt of the world. "Priya, keep pumping! Feruz—"

"Already moving, girl." The old man's voice, steady as ballast, from below.

Idris appeared at her side in the strobing searchlight, soaked, silent, and put his hands over hers on the mat, adding his weight, and together they held the river back from the hole in their lives while the boat groaned around them.

Downstream, Vasquez's searchlight swept the empty water once more, patiently, thoroughly, and went dark.

In the sudden blackness, Ada heard Idris speak close to her ear, very quietly, under the scream of the pump.

"The station's six hours upstream. She thinks we're crippled." His hands tightened over hers. "So we let her think it until dawn. Then we run for it—and pray Sanjit's patch holds longer than her patience."
