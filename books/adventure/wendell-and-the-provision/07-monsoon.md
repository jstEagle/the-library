## 7. Monsoon

The rain came the way Idris had always been told it would, the way his grandmother described it when he was small enough to sit under her loom: not as water falling but as a decision the sky had finally made. One hour the channel ahead of them was a green corridor stitched with light. The next, the world went grey and vertical, and the Sundarbans closed around them like a fist.

He had felt it coming for two days. The air had gone thick and sweet, the way it did before the delta changed its mind. He'd said nothing to Ada, because there was nothing to say that would help—no pilot alive could outrun an early monsoon, and no crew wanted to hear their helmsman admit the season had beaten them. But when the first true squall hit and the *Kestrel's* wheel went heavy in his hands, he heard Ada come up beside him without being called, and he knew she'd felt it too.

"South channel," she shouted over the rain.

"South channel is a grave in this wind."

"North is where Vasquez will be."

That settled it, the way most things got settled between them now—not by argument but by naming the lesser death. He put the bow south.

The mangrove channel took them in like it was doing them a favor, which Idris knew from thirty years on these waters was the cruelest thing about it. The trees leaned close, roots standing on their knuckles in black water, and for twenty minutes the wind dropped as if the forest were holding its breath. Then the hull struck something soft—a submerged log, or a sandbar that hadn't existed when the charts were drawn—and the sound came up through the deck plates into his boots, a deep wrong note, the sound of a boat taking a wound.

Ada was below before he could shout. He heard her voice change pitch, heard the word she used, and then she was back on the ladder with her hand pressed flat against the frame and her face gone the color of the river.

"She's taking water. Below the stringer. It's slow."

"Slow is a word," Idris said. "In monsoon, slow is a rumor."

They wedged the *Kestrel* between two stands of nipa palm where the current couldn't work at her hull, and then there was nothing left to do but the oldest work on the river: bail. Ada found the buckets. Idris rigged a tarp over the companionway so they weren't bailing the sky along with the sea. And the rain kept its decision, hammering the canopy above them until the whole world was one sound.

* * *

They bailed through the afternoon and into the dark, trading off when arms gave out, and somewhere in the third hour of the night watch the rhythm of it became something almost like conversation—a call and response older than either of them. Splash, pause, splash. Water out, water in. A boat leaks; you bail; you live until you don't.

Idris sat on the engine housing with a bucket between his knees, watching Ada work by the light of a single shielded lamp. She had tied her hair back with a rag and her sleeves were soaked to the shoulder, and she moved with the economy of someone who had done hard things alone for a long time. He had watched her for weeks now across the width of a wheelhouse, cataloguing her the way he catalogued channels—looking for the safe passage, marking the hazards. Somewhere in those weeks the cataloguing had stopped being navigation.

"You're staring," she said, without looking up.

"I'm resting."

"You're staring and resting." She dumped her bucket over the side. "Ask what you're going to ask."

He turned the bucket in his hands. The radio crackled behind him—he'd been sweeping the bands every hour, low volume, listening for Vasquez. Nothing tonight but static and a Bengali weather service repeating itself like a prayer. Even hunters needed sleep.

"My brother," Idris said. "You've never asked me about him."

"I figured you'd tell me when you told me."

"That's a strange way to run a salvage operation."

"That's a strange way to run a friendship," she said, and then seemed to hear herself say it, and looked up, and neither of them looked away.

So he told her. Not all at once—the rain made pauses necessary—but in pieces, the way he'd carried it for eleven years. Karim, four years younger, who'd signed on as a deckhand for a survey expedition because the pay was triple fishing wages and their mother needed medicine. The expedition led by Elias Dunmore. The incident at the survey camp—Karim blamed, charged, convicted on evidence Idris had spent a decade trying to disprove: a stolen instrument log, testimony from men who'd been paid to give it, a chain of custody that began and ended inside the concession company's own offices.

"Elias Dunmore testified," Idris said. "Your father stood in that courtroom and gave evidence against my brother."

Ada set down her bucket. The lamp swung slightly, shadows moving across the wet planks.

"I know," she said.

"You know?"

"I found out when I was nineteen. Hale's firm published the expedition report. My father's name was on the survey team. I cross-referenced the court records." She laughed, short and without any humor in it. "I've known since before I ever met you. Why do you think I never worked the Sundarbans? It wasn't only that he died here. It's what he did here."

The rain filled the silence. Idris realized his hands had gone still on the bucket, and that some part of him had been waiting eleven years for this exact sentence and had never once imagined how it would feel to hear it.

"He talked about it," Ada said. "Near the end. He drank more than my mother liked, and some nights he'd sit on the porch and talk about the delta like it was a person he'd wronged. He said he'd signed something. Testified to what the company told him was true, because they paid for the survey and he believed—or he let himself believe—that the paperwork was honest. He said—" Her voice caught, steadied. "He said the worst thing a man can do isn't lie. It's agree."

* * *

They bailed until dawn without saying much else, but something had shifted below the waterline of them, deeper than the hull's wound. When the rain thinned to a steady grey drizzle and the channel emerged dripping around them, Ada climbed up beside him at the rail and handed him tea from the thermos, and their shoulders touched, and neither of them moved away.

"My mother asked me once why I kept running contracts in other people's waters," Ada said. "I told her it was the work. It wasn't the work. Every mile I put between me and this delta was a mile I didn't have to be Elias Dunmore's daughter in it." She looked out at the mangroves, at the half-drowned marker post leaning offshore like a man wading home. "And now Hale has dragged me right back to the center of it, and I'm starting to think that's what she wanted. Not the archive. Me. Because if anyone can find what my father hid, it's the person he taught to find everything."

"And if you find it," Idris said, "you find proof of what happened to Karim too. Whether you want it or not."

"Whether we want it or not." She turned to look at him, and in the grey morning light her eyes were the color of the channel after a storm—muddy, churning, impossible to see the bottom of. "Your duty says take this boat and this crew and me straight to Vasquez and collect whatever Hale promised you. Mine says burn every page we find and let the river keep it. We're both standing here anyway."

"Yes," Idris said. "We are."

It wasn't a declaration. It was barely a confession. But when she leaned her head against his shoulder, salt-wet hair cold against his neck, he understood that something had begun between them that neither duty nor the river was going to be permitted to end—not cleanly, not cheaply. Love, if that's what it was, arriving the way the monsoon arrived: not as a surprise, but as a decision the sky had finally made.

The radio crackled. He almost didn't turn toward it—almost let the moment stand undisturbed. But the voice that came through the static was clipped, accented, unmistakable, and it spoke a single phrase in English before dissolving back into weather:

"—confluence by tomorrow. All parties. No exceptions—"

Ada lifted her head. They looked at each other over the sound of the rain, and Idris reached down and turned the volume knob all the way to zero, as if silence could unmake what they'd both just heard.

Vasquez knew where they were.
