## 9. Permits Expire

The gap in the mangroves was shadow, barely, and the *Kestrel's* hull screamed through it in a way that told me we'd pay for it later—if there was a later. Behind us, Vasquez's boat drew too much water to follow into the channel. Ahead, the confluence waited like a held breath: two rivers meeting, the old survey station's pilings on the far bank, and six hours left on permits that had never been honest to begin with.

I put us out onto open water because there was nowhere else to go, and because Ada Dunmore had that look she got—the one where her jaw set and her eyes went flat and calm, the look of a woman doing arithmetic with things that couldn't be counted.

"He'll cut the corner," I said. "He knows these channels well enough."

"Then he knows what happens at the confluence when the tide turns." She was watching the water, not me. "Four hours of ebb. The east channel goes to mud."

"You want to run him aground."

"I want to stop running." She turned then, and I saw how tired she was—seven years tired, a whole childhood tired—but her voice didn't shake. "Idris. He's not going to let us leave with this. You know that. Priya's carrying enough paper to hang Hale's firm from every court in Calcutta, and Vasquez has orders to make sure it never gets read. We can't outrun him to Khulna. Not with the hull the way it is."

She was right about the hull. I'd been listening to it all morning, that soft irregular knock below the waterline where the patch was holding by stubbornness and prayer, and neither of those had ever kept a boat dry for me.

"So what do you want?" I asked, though I already knew. I'd known since the night we bailed water side by side and she told me about her father's hands shaking over his testimony. Ada Dunmore did not run from fires. She walked into them carrying buckets.

"I want to give him my boat," she said.

* * *

We argued for ten minutes, which was nine more than the tide allowed, and then the crew voted—which is to say Priya said "do it," Osei said "she's right, and I'm tired of being owned," and Marcus, who had signed on with Hale's money around his neck like an anchor chain, looked at the smoke on the horizon and said nothing at all, which from Marcus was a surrender.

Ada's plan was simple and terrible, exactly like her. We'd run for the confluence openly, let Vasquez commit to the chase, and at the moment he closed the last hundred meters, we'd scuttle the *Kestrel* in the shallow water off the mudflat—open her seams, drop her like a stone in four feet of river—and take to the mangroves with the satchel while his deep-drafted boat grounded itself trying to reach us. He'd be stuck until the tide came back. Six hours. More than enough.

"It's your boat," I said quietly, while the crew rigged the seacocks. "Your father's boat."

"That's why it works." Her hand rested on the rail, on wood worn smooth by twenty years of other people's voyages. "He'd have done it. For something that mattered." A pause. "He did do it, actually. That's what killed him."

There was nothing to say to that, so I took the wheel.

* * *

Vasquez came on like weather.

His boat was bigger, faster, flying no flag at all now—he'd stopped pretending hours ago—and as we cleared the confluence point I watched him swing wide to cut the corner exactly as I'd predicted, engines up to full, closing the gap with the patience of a man who had done this before and enjoyed it. Through the binoculars I could see him at the helm, unhurried, one hand raised in something that might have been a wave.

"He's signaling someone," Priya said. "There's a second boat. Smaller. Holding back near the station pilings."

Of course there was. Men like Vasquez always had a second boat; it was the first rule of the delta that Idris Rahman's mother ever taught me: *never count one crocodile.* The small boat sat quiet in the lee of the pilings, and I understood the shape of the trap even if Ada wouldn't say it aloud. Run them aground, and the small boat picked us out of the mangroves at leisure. Outrun them, and the radio called ahead to every landing between here and the sea.

"There's no clean way out of this," I said.

"No," Ada agreed. "But there's a way through."

The *Kestrel* ran shallow and light, and I flew her. I'd piloted this delta since I was twelve years old; I knew where the river deepened and lied about it, knew the sandbars that moved overnight like sleeping animals. I gave Vasquez a chase worth having—threaded the channel markers, swung close enough to the mudflats to make him think I was desperate rather than deliberate—and behind me his engine note climbed, greedy, committed.

Two hundred meters. His bow wave was white and high. I could see the rifle slung across his back now, and the man beside him with a radio handset pressed to his ear.

"One hundred meters," Priya said, flat as a sounding line.

"Now," Ada said.

Osei opened the seacocks. Marcus pulled the floor panel over the patched hull and drove the pickaxe through it once, twice, and the river came in the way rivers do—not rushing but *arriving*, inevitable, a black tide rising through our own deck. I swung the wheel hard and laid the *Kestrel* against the mudflat's edge with the last of her momentum, and we went over the side into waist-deep water, satchel held high, wading for the mangrove roots while our ship drank herself dead beneath us.

Vasquez saw it too late. I heard his engine reverse, hard, the propeller churning brown water into foam—but a boat like his needed fifty meters to stop, and the channel edge gave him thirty. He came off the plane, wallowed, and settled with a grinding shudder as the tide went out from under him and the mud took hold of his keel like a fist.

For a long moment there was only the sound of water finding its level, and Vasquez standing at his rail, looking down at the exposed mud as though he could argue with it.

Then he looked across the water at us—at Ada, chest-deep among the mangroves, holding the satchel above the surface like an offering—and he smiled.

"Tide comes back in five hours," he called. His voice carried flat across the water, unhurried. "And my friends are already on your side of the river."

* * *

The mangroves were a cathedral nobody built. Roots arched overhead, light came down green and broken, and the mud sucked at every step. We moved inland in single file, Priya with the satchel strapped tight, Osei carrying Marcus—who had twisted his ankle going over the rail—and me watching the water behind us, counting the shapes moving along the far bank.

Three men from the small boat. They were good; they moved quiet, spread wide, cutting our line of retreat toward the village landing. Which meant they weren't trying to catch us in the trees. They were herding us back toward the confluence, toward Vasquez, toward the arithmetic of a man who wanted witnesses reduced to zero before the permits expired and the paperwork became worthless—or worse, became evidence.

"We should split," Marcus panted. "Give them the satchel, some of us walk away—"

"No," Ada said, and it wasn't loud, but it ended the conversation the way a keel ends a roll.

We reached the old survey station at midday. It was stripped and hollow, her father's ghost in every room, and Ada stood in the doorway of what must have been his office for three full seconds before Priya touched her arm and pointed: through the back window, across the clearing, the three men were entering the treeline.

"They'll be here in minutes," I said. "The tide won't turn for hours. We can't reach the village."

Ada set the satchel on her father's desk. She opened it, and the letter was on top—Hale's firm's name in ink older than I was, the count of two hundred and six households, the word *silenced* in her father's careful hand—and I watched her look at it the way a woman looks at a child she's deciding whether she can save.

"Fire," she said softly. "Burn it, and we're just salvagers again. He lets us live. Maybe."

"Burn it," Marcus said immediately, "and my brother-in-law keeps his shop, and we keep breathing—"

"And two hundred and six families stay landless forever," Priya said, "and Idris's brother rots in a jail built on a lie, and every year somebody new signs a concession over stolen ground because nobody wrote down what happened here." She looked at Ada. "You didn't come this far to burn it."

"No," Ada said. "I came this far to carry it out."

The men came out of the treeline.

It happened fast after that, the way bad things do. The first man fired wide—a warning, or nerves—and the sound in the stripped station was enormous, off every bare wall. Osei pulled Priya down behind the desk. I grabbed the satchel because Ada had her father's field notes in her free hand and wasn't letting go of either, and we went for the back door together, into the open, into the clearing where there was no cover at all except each other.

The second shot didn't go wide.

I felt it before I understood it—a punch under my shoulder blade, folding me sideways—and the strange thing is that my first thought was purely practical: *the satchel*, keep the satchel dry, keep it out of the mud. My knees found the ground. The sky tilted. And Ada was suddenly there, on her knees in the dirt beside me, one hand pressing hard against my chest, the other still locked around the strap, and her face was the calm-flat-arithmetical face breaking apart all at once.

"Stay," she said. "Idris. *Stay.*"

Behind her, shouting—Osei's voice, and then another voice I didn't know, and then Marcus, of all people, stepping out from the station with his hands up and his voice cracking across the clearing: "Vasquez! VASQUEZ! Hale's crew stands down! All of us—you hear me? We're done carrying her water!" And the third shot never came, because mutiny, it turns out, moves faster than bullets.

Ada was tearing her shirt into bandages with hands that shook only after the work was done. The bullet had gone through, high, ugly, missing everything that mattered by luck or by whatever gods pilot rivermen home. I lay in the mud of my own delta, in sight of the water that had taken her father and nearly taken her brother-in-salvage and had tried, today, to take me, and I laughed until it hurt.

"What," she demanded.

"The permits," I said. "They expire at midnight." I pressed her hand flatter against the wound. "Doesn't matter anymore. None of it does. You've already decided what this is worth."

She looked at me—really looked, the way she'd stopped letting herself look at anything she meant to keep—and above the treeline the afternoon was turning gold, and far off across the flats the tide had begun, at last, to turn.

"Khulna," she said. "There's a journalist in Khulna. And a court that sits Monday." She hauled me upright against her shoulder, satchel and all, and started walking. "We're going to be early."
