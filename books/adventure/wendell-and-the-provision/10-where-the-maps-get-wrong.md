## 10. Where the Maps Get Wrong

The monsoon broke the way it always did in the delta—not all at once, but in stages, like a held breath finally released. By the time Ada stood on the veranda of the pilot station above the river, the rain had softened to a steady whisper against the tin roof, and the water below had turned from brown violence to a slow, patient green.

She had been in Chittagong when the news came. She had been standing at the harbor office, watching the cranes swing their slow arcs over the water, when Idris's call crackled through on the borrowed radio. His voice was rough with something she hadn't heard in it before—something that might have been hope.

"They let him out," he said. "Karim. This morning. The court threw out the evidence—said it was fabricated, said the survey data her father's firm submitted was falsified to justify the seizure." A pause, and then, quieter: "He's coming home, Ada."

She had gripped the railing of the harbor office until her knuckles went white, and she had not been able to speak for a long moment. Seven years of running, seven years of keeping one rule so she'd never have to face what her father's last expedition had cost—and now the ledger was being torn up, page by page, and she didn't know yet whether to weep or laugh or simply stand very still and let the news wash through her like the tide.

"Come home too," Idris said. "Not Chittagong. Here."

Here. The word sat strangely in her chest. She had spent her whole adult life making sure *here* never lasted longer than a contract.

"I'll come," she said, and meant it.

* * *

The archive had gone first to a journalist named Farhana Chowdhury, who worked out of a cramped office in Dhaka above a print shop that smelled of ink and frying oil. Ada had handed over the sealed case herself—every document, every field note, every page of her father's handwriting describing the seizure disguised as concession, the villages drowned on paper before they were ever flooded, the families whose land became Marguerite Hale's empire with a signature and a lie.

Farhana read for three hours without looking up. When she finally raised her head, her eyes were red-rimmed and hard as river stones.

"You understand what this does," she said. "To you. To your crew. Hale's lawyers will come after everyone whose name is in this."

"My name's already in it," Ada said. "My father wrote it there himself, twenty years ago. I'm just delivering his mail."

The story ran on a Tuesday. By Thursday, the land-rights court had convened an emergency session. By the following month, the concessions were collapsing one after another like channels silting shut—Hale's permits revoked, her assets frozen, her name moving from boardrooms into courtrooms. Ada never saw Marguerite Hale's face when the news reached her; she imagined it anyway, sometimes, on long nights, and found she felt no triumph in it. Only a tired kind of symmetry. The river keeps its accounts. Sooner or later, everything comes downstream.

* * *

The crew kept the smaller boat. They called her *Second Chance*, which Yusuf thought was sentimental and Priya thought was funny and Ada thought was exactly right—a salvage boat, forty feet where the old one had been sixty, honest in her lines and honest in her ledgers. No mortgages. No debts. No names on the ownership papers but their own.

They worked the Bay routes again, the safe ones, the ones Ada knew by heart: wreck surveys off Kutubdia, cargo recovery near Sandwip, the slow patient work of pulling value out of what the sea had given up. Business was thinner without Hale's contracts—which was the point, Priya said. "Thin and clean beats fat and owned."

Ada ran the deck less than she used to. She found, to her own surprise, that she didn't mind. There was a new arithmetic working itself out in her, and it took some getting used to.

All her life she had measured time in miles. Nautical miles between contracts, miles of open water between the person she was and the person she'd been at seventeen, standing on a dock in Chittagong with her father's effects in a cardboard box and a vow forming in her chest like scar tissue: *never go back, never stay, never let anything anchor you where grief can find you.* Miles had been safety. Miles had been the only unit of account she trusted.

But miles, she was learning, were a coward's currency.

* * *

The house above the pilot station had two rooms and a wide veranda and had belonged, once, to Idris's grandmother. It needed a new roof and paint and a hundred small repairs, and Idris listed them all cheerfully the first evening he showed her through it, as though the flaws were features, as though a house with problems was a house worth committing to.

"You don't have to decide tonight," he said. "Or this season. Or—"

"I'm deciding now." She said it before the fear could catch up with her, before the old reflex could map the exits. "That's the whole point. I've decided things my whole life by leaving early. I want to try deciding by staying late."

He looked at her for a long moment, rain-light moving across his face, and then he laughed—the low, unguarded laugh she'd first heard across a bailing bucket in a flooding channel, when they'd both been soaked and terrified and honest for the first time.

"The roof leaks," he warned.

"So do I," Ada said. "We'll match."

* * *

Unpacking took her three days, and she made a ceremony of it without meaning to. The oilskin jacket went on its hook by the door. Her father's field notes—the ones that hadn't gone to Farhana, the personal pages, the ones about tides and birds and a daughter he'd loved badly and from far away—went on the shelf above the desk, spine cracked, cover salt-stained. The chart of the Sundarbans, the one with the wrong channels marked, the one that had nearly killed them all, she pinned to the wall not as a warning but as a reminder: *the maps get things wrong. That's how you know to keep looking.*

On the third day she unpacked the last bag, the small one she'd kept packed for seven years under every bunk, in every boat, ready for the jump before anyone could ask her to stay. She opened it on the bed and found almost nothing inside—a change of clothes, a passport, a photograph of a man she'd spent half her life resenting and the other half mourning. She looked at all that empty space, all that room she'd left for running, and she folded the bag flat and put it in the bottom of a trunk, under the winter blankets, where bags go when they're done.

Then she went out onto the veranda, where the rain had stopped and the light was doing that thing it did here in the evenings, turning the whole river to hammered gold.

Idris was on the dock below, talking with Karim—his brother, thin from seven years inside, squinting at the water like a man relearning a language. Two pilots' boats rode at the moorings, and beyond them the *Second Chance* lay at anchor, her new paint still bright, her crew laughing about something Ada couldn't hear and didn't need to.

Karim looked up and saw her, and raised a hand—not a wave exactly, more an acknowledgment, a salute between people who had survived the same storm from different sides of it. Ada raised hers back.

Idris climbed the steps a few minutes later, drying his hands on a rag, and stood beside her at the rail without speaking. Below them the river moved, carrying silt and stories toward the sea, indifferent and enormous and, for the first time in her life, feeling less like an escape route than like a neighbor.

"Priya wants to run a survey contract off Cox's Bazar next month," he said eventually. "Says you'll say no because it's boring."

"Priya's right. It's boring." Ada leaned her shoulder against his. "I'll say yes anyway."

He glanced down at her. "Since when?"

"Since I stopped measuring in miles." She watched the light move on the water. "You can't measure a place in miles. You have to use years."

"And how many are you planning?"

Ada considered the question honestly, the way she'd learned to consider everything since the delta—the archive, the bullet Idris had taken in his side shielding a box of paper, the long slow recovery, the courtroom in Dhaka where her father's testimony had finally been read aloud, twenty years late, by a lawyer who cried while reading it.

Her father had died trying to get the truth downstream. She had finished the delivery. Whatever else the ledger held, that line balanced.

"All of them," she said. "I'm planning all of them."

Idris put his arm around her, and the river kept moving, and the house above the pilot station kept its leaking roof and its new paint and its woman who had finally, at long last, unpacked.

Downriver, where the maps got the channels wrong, the water was already finding its true course home.
