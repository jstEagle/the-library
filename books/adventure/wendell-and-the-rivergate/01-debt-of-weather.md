## 1. Debt of Weather

The storm came up out of the west like something with an opinion.

Emrys Ishikawa had been reading it since dawn—watching the CAPE numbers climb, watching the dryline sharpen on the mesoscale discussion, watching the sky over the Texas panhandle go that bruised yellow-green she'd learned to trust before she'd learned to distrust anything else. By two in the afternoon the supercell had gone tornadic near Amarillo, and by three it was a monster, a rotating cathedral of cloud dragging its skirt across the plains, and Emrys was doing ninety down a farm road with her salvage rig bolted to the truck bed and her heart doing what it always did when the air turned electric: forgetting, briefly, everything she owed.

That was the trick of chasing. That was why she kept doing it even now, even broke, even hunted. Out here under the anvil there were no ledgers. There was only the storm and her read of it, and her read was good. Her read had always been good.

"Come on, sweetheart," she murmured, one hand on the wheel, the other thumbing the radar on her dash-mounted tablet. "Give me a hail core. Give me something worth carrying."

The salvage business wasn't glamorous. When a hailstorm chewed through a county, it stripped roofs, totaled crops, flattened pivot irrigation rigs, and left behind a landscape of insurance claims that could take months to process. Emrys got there first. She documented damage for adjusters who paid cash, recovered equipment for co-ops that paid better, and occasionally—if the storm hit a dealership or a feedlot—she hauled off scrap metal by the ton. It wasn't much. It was never much. But it was hers, earned under her own judgment, at her own risk, and for six years it had kept one step ahead of the hole in her finances.

One step was no longer enough.

She took the corner too fast, felt the trailer sway, corrected. Ahead, the wall cloud hung like a fist about to open. The RFD was carving dust out of the fields in long brown sheets, and somewhere inside all that violence was a tornado trying to decide whether to be born.

Her phone buzzed against the cup holder. She glanced at it—one eye, half a second—and felt the day change temperature.

RIVERGATE HOLDINGS. FINAL NOTICE.

She let it ring out. It rang again immediately. She knew how these calls went; she'd had three of them this month alone. A pleasant voice, a man named Oduya who never raised his tone, walking her through the arithmetic of her failure. Principal. Interest. Penalty. The word *collateral* deployed like a scalpel. Her truck. Her rig. Six years of work, signed away in a moment of desperation two winters ago when the transmission blew and the medical bill came due and the bank doors had all closed except one.

Rivergate's door had been open. Rivergate's door was always open. That was the whole design of it.

The phone stopped. Started again. She pulled off onto a gravel spur beneath a line of dead cottonwoods, killed the engine, and answered, because not answering had never once made them smaller.

"Ishikawa."

"Ms. Ishikawa." Oduya's voice, smooth as poured concrete. "You're a difficult woman to reach."

"I'm working."

"So I hear. Chasing weather." A pause with a smile in it. "It must be exciting. Unfortunately, excitement doesn't service debt. I'm calling because the board has reviewed your account, and I've been authorized to offer you clarity rather than another extension."

Emrys watched the wall cloud through the windshield. The rotation was tightening. Somewhere a siren was starting up in a town she couldn't see, a thin electronic wail swallowed by wind.

"Clarity," she said.

"One season. Through the end of storm season. If your balance isn't current by the first of October, we exercise collateral recovery on the vehicle, and we refer the remainder of your obligation to our recovery division." He said it the way a man reads a menu. "I'd hate to see that happen. You've always struck me as someone who values being... unencumbered."

*Freedom,* translated into collections language. They'd take the truck, and then they'd take her—not to jail, nothing so clean, but into the syndicate's own economy, where debts were worked off in ways that didn't appear on any ledger she wanted to be part of. She'd seen what Rivergate's "recovery division" did to people around Lake Wendell. Everyone in the flood country had seen it. That was the point of seeing it.

"One season," she said.

"And Ms. Ishikawa—the board asked me to mention that they're aware of your particular talents. If an opportunity arose that aligned with your skills, they would look favorably on accelerated terms. We believe in rewarding initiative."

The call ended before she could ask what that meant, which told her he'd wanted it to end there. A hook, dangled. She sat with the engine ticking and the cottonwoods thrashing overhead and felt the old dread settle back into its seat beside her, patient as a passenger.

Then the sky opened, and for forty minutes she forgot him completely.

* * *

The tornado dropped at 4:12 p.m., a white cone against gray rain, and Emrys chased it for eleven miles along a section road while it chewed through fallow ground and then, cruelly, through a mile of center-pivot irrigation systems, folding them like paper clips. She filmed everything, logged coordinates, marked the debris field. When the rope-out came—tornado thinning to a needle and vanishing into its own cloud—she peeled off toward the damage path and started working.

Two hours of it. Hailstones the size of fists still melting in the ditches. A grain elevator with its corrugated skin peeled back like a sardine tin. She filled her rig with aluminum sheeting and copper wire salvaged from a destroyed pump station, photographed a collapsed equipment shed for the co-op's adjuster, and by seven o'clock had a load worth maybe nine hundred dollars and a documentation invoice worth four hundred more.

Thirteen hundred dollars. Against a debt of sixty-one thousand.

She did the math sitting on her tailgate eating cold noodles, and the math did what it always did: it laughed at her. Even a perfect season—a monster every week, salvage maxed out, expenses cut to bone—got her maybe eight thousand. She'd need eight seasons. Rivergate had given her one.

The sun was going down red behind the storm's receding anvil, painting the wet fields the color of an old wound, when the second message arrived.

Not from Rivergate. From a broker she'd done contract work for years ago, back when she'd had a reputation for finding things in bad country—a downed plane in the Wichita Mountains, a missing rancher after the Woodward flood. The message was short, no greeting:

*Contract posting, high rate, needs someone with flood-country experience. Meridian Survey Group, government subcontractor. Team of six went dark eleven days ago near Lake Wendell. Families are screaming, agency is stonewalling, insurance is stonewalling harder. Someone has to find them or find out what happened. Rate is triple standard plus completion bonus. Reply if interested and I'll send the packet.*

Triple standard. Plus bonus. Her thumb hovered over the screen.

Lake Wendell. She knew those waters—not the lake itself, which hadn't existed until the government's levee project drowned the valley two years ago, but the country around it. The towns. The farms standing in shallow water with their attics above the surface like people wading. The displaced families camped in Rivergate's rented trailers, waiting on compensation checks that never came, while the syndicate bought up their foreclosed land at ten cents on the dollar. She'd driven through it twice last year and both times had come away with the feeling of having passed through a mouth that hadn't finished swallowing.

And now a survey team had gone into that country and come apart like sugar in rain. Six people. Generators, satellite uplinks, government credentials—and no word in eleven days. The agency stonewalling. The insurers stonewalling. Everybody stonewalling, which meant everybody knew something, which meant whatever was out there wasn't just dangerous. It was *owned.*

Emrys looked at the message for a long time. The light died out of the western sky. A killdeer called somewhere in the dark, thin and repeated, like a question nobody wanted to answer.

Cornered animals don't get to be choosy. That was the pragmatism, and she reached for it the way you reach for a handrail in the dark. Triple rate. Completion bonus. Maybe two months' worth of the debt cleared in a single job, and Rivergate had practically invited her to show initiative. Find some lost bureaucrats, collect a check, keep her truck, keep her name out of the recovery division's files. Simple. Transactional. Clean.

Except.

Except that nothing about a vanished government team in a drowned valley was clean, and some cold, clear part of her—the part that read storms, the part that had kept her alive through eleven years of weather that killed better-funded people than her—that part was already whispering what the shape of this thing meant. Teams don't vanish. Agencies don't go quiet unless silence is the product. And a broker desperate enough to post triple rates doesn't do it out of concern for families; he does it because somebody upstream is terrified.

She typed: *Send the packet.*

The reply came in under a minute, which frightened her more than anything else had all day. Attached was a briefing document, a set of last-known coordinates twenty miles inside the flood zone, and a single line from the broker:

*Fair warning, Emrys. Two other contractors took this posting. Neither has checked in.*

Emrys stood at her tailgate in the dark with the packet glowing on her tablet and the storm's last thunder rolling away eastward, and she felt the dread she'd been holding at arm's length all afternoon finally put its hands on her shoulders.

One season. One chance. One road, and it ran straight into the water.

She closed the tablet, climbed into the cab, and turned the truck south toward Lake Wendell, headlights cutting a narrow tunnel through the night, telling herself the whole way that she was going for the money.

Knowing, the way you know a storm is turning before the radar shows it, that she was already lying.
