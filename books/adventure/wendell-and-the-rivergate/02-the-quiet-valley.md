## 2. The Quiet Valley

The water came first, the way it always does in flood country—not as a wall but as a suggestion. A sheen over the soybean fields. A fence line standing to its knees in brown glass. Then the road itself gave up pretending and became a river with delusions of being a road.

Emrys Ishikawa drove into it anyway, because the contract said *Lake Wendell* and the contract was the only thing between her and a season of Rivergate collectors learning her truck's value by disassembling it.

She killed the engine at the high-water mark where the county route climbed onto an old rail embankment, and for a long moment she just sat there with her hands on the wheel, listening to the tick of cooling metal and the sound of a valley breathing underwater.

It wasn't supposed to look like this. She'd read the briefing twice on the drive down from the plains: Meridian Survey Team Seven, six hydrologists and geodetic specialists, contracted by the Department of Water Resources to assess the Wendell basin levee after the spring floods. Last contact eleven days ago. Satellite pass showed their camp intact at the foot of Corncrake Ridge. Recover personnel or remains; recover equipment; recover any data products. Standard salvage language, the kind that let agencies pretend grief was logistics.

But the satellite hadn't shown her this: a whole valley drowned to the second-story windows, church steeples and grain elevators standing out of the water like the masts of a fleet that had sunk in its sleep. A power pole leaned at forty degrees, wires trailing in the current like kelp. Somewhere under all that brown water was a town—Wendell proper, population maybe four hundred before the evacuation orders—and nobody had mentioned to her that the levee failure had been this total, this *tidy*, as if the valley had been designed to hold exactly this much river.

She got out. The air smelled of silt and diesel and something sweeter underneath—cut hay rotting somewhere beneath the surface, the whole growing season drowning in place. Her boots found the embankment gravel, then the mud beyond it, and she started walking toward the ridge with the survey coordinates glowing on her handheld.

A mile in, the silence arrived.

Emrys had spent nine years chasing storms. She knew every kind of loud the sky could make—the freight-train roar of a tornado on the ground, the crack-and-boom of dry lightning, hail hammering a truck roof like God throwing gravel. She'd built her whole ruined life around noise. So she noticed silence the way other people noticed gunshots.

There were no birds. That was the first thing. No redwings working the cattails, no killdeer crying off the mudflats, nothing. Just water moving with a slow arterial patience through the drowned fields, and the ridge ahead, black-green with cottonwoods, and her own breathing.

*Storm's coming,* she thought, reading the sky out of habit—a bruised anvil building west over the hills, the kind of cell that would turn this whole valley into a washing machine by nightfall. She had maybe five hours. Fine. Five hours was enough to find six bodies or six empty cots, load whatever the contract paid for, and be gone before Rivergate's people even knew she'd been here.

The camp appeared through the trees all at once, the way camps do: a cluster of white and orange dome tents on a gravel bench above the flood line, two trucks parked nose-out, a trailer generator humming.

Humming.

Emrys stopped dead in the mud.

Generators don't run for eleven days unattended. They run out of fuel, or they seize, or they flood out, or somebody comes back for them. This one was running with the steady, contented drone of a machine that had been fed recently. Its exhaust shimmered faintly in the cool air. A string of work lights hung between two tents, and one of them was on, burning a soft yellow hole in the gray afternoon.

Her hand went to the radio on her shoulder and stopped. Contract protocol said check in. Something older and more animal said *don't announce yourself to a camp that's been waiting for someone.*

She drew the pistol instead—salvage contractors in syndicate country carried, everyone knew it, nobody said it—and moved up the gravel bench with her weight on the outside edges of her feet, the way you walk when you don't want the ground to remember you.

The camp was immaculate. That was the second wrong thing. Tents staked taut, guy lines dressed and coiled, mud flaps over the tent doors folded neatly back. A folding table held a field laptop, closed, a coffee mug beside it, a clipboard weighted down with a rock. On the far side, a mess canopy sheltered a camp stove, a rack of drying dishes, a cooler with its lid latched.

She looked at the coffee mug for a long time.

Then she touched it.

Still warm.

Not lukewarm-not-quite-cool warm. Warm the way a mug is warm ten minutes after somebody sets it down to go do something, meaning to come right back. Emrys stood very still in the middle of the tidy camp and felt the hair rise along her forearms, because eleven days of missing persons did not leave warm coffee, and warm coffee did not leave eleven days of missing persons, and both of those facts were sitting in front of her refusing to reconcile.

*Okay,* she told herself. *Options. One: they left this morning and the timeline in the briefing is garbage. Two: someone else is here and maintaining the site. Three—*

She didn't finish option three. Option three was the kind of thing you finished only if you had to.

She checked the trucks first. Both gassed, both operational, keys in the ignitions of one. Nobody abandons two working trucks. Nobody walks out of a flood valley on foot when they have vehicles, not unless something made walking seem safer than driving, and nothing made driving seem dangerous except—

Except being followed.

She worked the camp methodically after that, because method was the only thing keeping the creeping feeling at arm's length. Tent one: six sleeping bags, six foam pads, personal gear squared away. Tent two: comms shack, a satellite phone in its charging cradle showing a full battery, a logbook open on the desk. She flipped pages. Neat entries, daily position reports, weather notes in a small precise hand—*Dr. Okafor* signed at the bottom of each page, the last entry dated nine days ago:

*Levee readings don't match design specs. Requesting original engineering drawings from DWR. Team advised to remain on site until resolved.*

And below that, in different ink, pressed hard enough to score the paper:

*DO NOT transmit findings. Repeat: DO NOT transmit.*

No signature. No entry after it. Nine days of blank lines.

Emrys set the logbook down with fingers that weren't quite steady. Outside, the generator hummed its idiot contented hum, and the work light burned, and the coffee cooled by degrees toward the truth.

* * *

She found the case in the third tent, the one marked DATA / EQUIPMENT.

It sat centered on the folding table like an exhibit in a courtroom nobody had gotten to yet: a hard-shell transport case, olive drab, about the size of a suitcase, with two combination locks and a laminated tag zip-tied to the handle. The tag read MERIDIAN SURVEY 7 — SEALED DELIVERABLE — DO NOT OPEN EXCEPT AUTHORIZED RECIPIENT. Stamped across the lid in red, in letters that seemed louder than everything else in the silent valley: **CLASSIFIED**.

Contract said recover data products. This was a data product the way a live grenade was party favors. She circled it once, twice. Tried the locks without real hope—they held. Weighed it in her hands: dense, heavier than paper alone, which meant drives, which meant instruments, which meant the actual readings of six people who had measured a levee and discovered something worth stamping red.

Eleven days of hazard pay plus recovery bonus plus data bonus. Enough. Maybe enough. The number did its little arithmetic in the back of her skull, the number Rivergate had taught her to hear everywhere, whispering that this box was freedom, this box was her truck and her name and her skin, all she had to do was carry it out of the valley and hand it to whoever the contract named and never ask what happened to the six hydrologists who had left warm coffee behind.

She set it down. Wiped her palms on her thighs.

That was when she saw the drawing.

It was pinned to the inside wall of the tent with a pushpin, at the height a child would pin things, low, near the floor of the adult world. A sheet of printer paper, softened by handling. Crayon. A green hill, a blue river running obediently between its banks, a farmhouse with smoke curling from a chimney drawn in careful loops. Four stick figures stood in the yard holding hands. Above them, in the huge wobbling capitals of someone learning to write: *OUR HOUSE AND THE RIVER IS OUR FREND.*

At the bottom corner, smaller, in pencil, in an adult hand: *For Dr. Wendell — thank you for watching our water. — Mireille, age 6, Wendell Township.*

Emrys stood in the humming tent with the classified case at her back and the child's drawing in front of her, and the silence of the valley came in around her like water rising, and she understood, finally, why there were no birds.

Birds leave before a storm. Birds leave before bad water. Six scientists had measured a levee, found it lying, asked for the original drawings, been told in hard ink to keep quiet—and then, sometime in the last nine days, every one of them had walked away from running generators, working trucks, warm coffee, and a locked case stamped CLASSIFIED, into a drowned valley, without leaving a single track in the mud around the camp.

She'd walked the perimeter twice. She was sure of it now, sure the way you're sure of a bearing: no footprints but hers. No drag marks. No tracks at all.

The sky over the western hills went the color of a bruise deepening, and the first cold wind of the coming storm moved through the cottonwoods, and Emrys Ishikawa—who had spent her whole life running toward the loudest thing on the horizon—stood in the quietest place she had ever been and felt, for the first time in nine years, genuinely afraid to be alone.

Behind her, in the rafters of the old church half-sunk across the flooded flat, something shifted. A board creaked, deliberate, the way a person creaks when they've decided to stop hiding.

Emrys turned, pistol coming up, and called out to the empty valley:

"I know you're there."
