## 3. Wendell

Wendell Okafor had learned to measure time by the water.

Not by the clock in the church vestry, which had drowned at 4:17 and never ticked again, and not by the sun, which came and went behind the cloud deck like a rumor. By the water. It rose a knuckle's width each morning against the stairwell door of the bell tower, then fell back by dusk as the levee upstream bled its controlled releases. Six inches up, six inches down. The valley breathing around him while he sat in the dark with his knees drawn to his chest and counted the things he should have said to people who were no longer alive to hear them.

On the ninth day, someone came looking for the dead.

He heard her before he saw her — an engine, low and diesel-rough, working its way along the old county road where it still stood above the floodline. He'd been hearing engines for nine days. Helicopters twice, both times black-bodied, both times circling the survey camp with the patient, gridlike discipline of men reading a map they already understood. Those he'd watched through the louvers of the belfry with his heart going like a trapped bird, because helicopters meant Corliss, and Corliss meant the others were still not coming back.

But this was a truck. One truck, moving slow, stopping at the camp.

He eased himself up from the floor where the stiffness of nine days lived in his joints and put his eye to the gap between the louvered boards.

The woman who stepped out was not government. He could tell that from thirty meters and four days of learning what government looked like. She wore no uniform, carried no case, moved with none of that squared-away economy the agents had. She walked the way water walks — finding the low places, taking the path of least resistance, but relentless about it all the same. Tall, weathered, a canvas jacket gone soft with age, hair tied back with what looked like a strip of torn tarp. She stood at the edge of the camp for a long moment and just looked at it, and Wendell felt something twist in his chest, because she looked at it the way you look at a grave. Not searching yet. Paying respects first.

Then she started searching, and he stopped breathing.

She went through the mess tent first. He watched her lift the coffee pot off the burner, watch her frown at it, touch the side of the generator housing with two fingers the way a doctor touches a wrist. His own hands, pressed flat against the cold boards of the belfry floor, remembered the same gestures made by other hands nine days ago. Priya checking the seismographs. Marsh running the last calibration on the piezometers. Adeyemi laughing at something on the radio, saying *say again, base, you're breaking up*, except the radio hadn't been breaking up, it had been saying exactly what it always said, which was nothing, which was silence where the telemetry used to be.

They'd cut the feed on day three. That was when Adeyemi had gone pale over the data and said the word that ended everything: *engineered*.

Below, the woman had found the mud.

He saw her crouch at the treeline where the ground firmed up past the flood's reach, saw her study it the way he studied stream gauges — patiently, professionally, reading what the surface wrote. There were no tracks leading away from the camp. He knew that. He'd checked himself, twice, on the night he'd crawled out of the culvert where he'd hidden when the black SUVs came. No tracks out. Only tire tracks in, deep and parallel, and boot prints everywhere, and the camp left standing like a stage set between acts. Generators humming. Coffee warm. Six bedrolls unslept-in. As if the team had stepped out for a moment and the moment had swallowed them whole.

That was the point, he understood now. The camp was supposed to look abandoned in a hurry. A vanishing. A mystery that would resolve itself into tragedy once the flood finished doing its work — lost surveyors, presumed drowned, case closed, valley cleared, land transferred. Clean. Tidy. The kind of story that gets one paragraph on page twelve.

Except one hydrologist had been in a culvert instead of his cot, and one woman in a canvas jacket was now standing in that same mud, and she was not buying the story either. He could see it in the set of her shoulders. She turned a slow circle, reading the tire tracks, the boot prints, the absence of tracks out, and when she straightened up she looked — not frightened. Angry. The specific anger of someone who has seen this shape of lie before and knows its dimensions by heart.

Wendell's hand was on the trapdoor before he decided to move it.

He stopped himself. Sat back down on the floor. Pressed his palms against his eyes until he saw stars.

*You don't know her.* That was the voice that had kept him alive for nine days, the voice that sounded like his mother, like caution, like every instinct that had told him to stay small and stay quiet while the world above the waterline did whatever it did. *You don't know why she's here. She could be salvage. She could be worse than salvage — she could be the cleanup crew, the one they send when the helicopters aren't enough.*

But he'd watched her check the coffee pot. He'd watched her touch the generator like it was a pulse.

And below the fear, below the grief that sat in him like ballast, there was the other thing. The thing Adeyemi had put in his hands on the last night, pressing the sealed case against his chest with both hands, saying: *You're the youngest, you're the fastest, you know these back channels better than any of us. If they come, you run, and you don't stop running until this is somewhere they can't bury it. Promise me, Wendell.* And he had promised, and then the headlights had swept the camp, and he had run, and he had not stopped running, and the promise weighed more than the case did though he carried them both.

Six people. He said their names to himself the way he'd been saying them for nine days, a litany, a rosary. Adeyemi. Priya. Marsh. Okonkwo. Lindqvist. Reyes. Six names, and five of them taken away in black vehicles *for debriefing*, the agent's word, delivered flatly to the empty air because there had been no one left to say it to — except the agent hadn't known about the culvert, hadn't known about the boy-shaped shadow pressed into the mud beneath the road, listening to his colleagues' voices fade down the highway and understanding, with a clarity that would never leave him, that debriefing was a word that ends sentences, not begins them.

The truck engine changed pitch below. Moving again — toward the church.

His breath went thin. Of course. The church was the only structure on high ground; anyone reading the valley would read it the way he had. He heard the tires crunch onto gravel, heard the engine die, heard a door slam, and then footsteps on the path he'd walked every Sunday of his childhood, back when the valley had a congregation and a name that wasn't a lake.

The church doors opened. He heard her move through the nave below him — slow, careful, boots on stone. Water had reached the second pew. He could smell the river from up here, that green mineral rot, the smell of everything the valley had been dissolving into everything it was becoming.

Then her voice, pitched upward, carrying the way voices carry in empty churches whether you want them to or not:

"I know you're up there."

Wendell went absolutely still.

"Three days of rations missing from the camp," she said. "Someone's been drinking from the rain barrel behind the vestry. And whoever it is climbs the outside ladder, because the inside stairs are flooded." A pause. "I'm not federal. I'm not syndicate. I'm nobody, and I'm leaving in ten minutes either way."

*She's lying,* said the cautious voice. *They all lie. That's the first thing this valley taught you.*

But he thought of Adeyemi's hands on the case. He thought of the coffee pot, warm, waiting for people who weren't coming back. He thought of nine days of silence so complete it had weight, pressure, a depth like water — and he thought: *if I stay in this steeple, I am not surviving. I am just drowning slower.*

He stood. His legs shook. Nine days of rainwater and ration bars had hollowed him, and the ladder rungs were slick, and his palms screamed where the splinters had been working in since day one. But he climbed down, and he pushed open the trapdoor at the top of the vestry stairs, and he stood in the doorway with the light behind him holding the sealed case against his chest like an infant, like a bomb, like the only thing left in the world worth the weight of carrying.

The woman had a hand near her hip — not on a weapon, near one — and she was looking at him with an expression he couldn't fully read. Surprise, yes. But under it, something else. Recognition, almost. The look of someone seeing a thing she'd half expected to find and had dreaded finding anyway.

"Wendell Okafor," he said. His voice came out rusted. "Meridian Survey. Hydrology." He lifted the case a few centimeters, enough for her to see the stamp. CLASSIFIED. The government seal beside it, crisp and official and obscene. "Five of my team are gone. I'm the only one who knows what's in here."

"And what's in here?" she asked. Careful. Neutral. But her eyes had dropped to the case and stayed there, and he watched her do the arithmetic he'd watched everyone do — the weight of it, the worth of it, the danger of it.

He thought about lying. He thought about telling her it was nothing, boring numbers, sediment cores. But he was done with the mathematics of survival. He'd done that math for nine days in a steeple and it had bought him nothing but the privilege of being alone.

"The flood wasn't a flood," Wendell said. "It was a land grab. The levee was built to fail. My team proved it, and that's why they're gone." He tightened his grip on the case. "I'm not leaving this valley without it. And if you're the one driving — then I need to know right now whose side you're on."

Outside, far off across the water, an engine that was not hers turned onto the county road.

Both of them heard it. Both of them went quiet. Her hand moved the rest of the way to her hip.

"That'll be your answer arriving early," she said.
