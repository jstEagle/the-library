## 4. What the Water Knows

**Wendell**

She followed me up the steeple stairs like a woman walking into church for the first time in years—wary, half-expecting the roof to fall. I'd watched her work the camp below for an hour before I made the choice to show myself. She hadn't touched the case. That was the only reason she was still breathing my air.

"Mind the fourth step," I said. "It's gone soft."

She stopped on the third step and looked up at me through the hatch, rain dripping off her jaw. "You've been up here how long?"

"Nine days." I moved aside to let her through. The bell room was small, smelling of pigeon droppings and old cedar, one window blown out where the wind had taken the louvers. My bedroll sat in the corner beside a crate of survey equipment that had never left my hands since the night the others were loaded into the black SUVs. "The church sits high enough that the water hasn't reached the belfry. Yet."

"You're Okafor." She said it flat, not a question. "Wendell Okafor. The contract says find the Meridian Survey team. It doesn't say anything about finding them alive."

"No," I said. "I imagine it doesn't."

Her name was Emrys Ishikawa—I'd read it off the side of her truck through my binoculars, painted over twice, some older name bleeding through beneath it like a scar. A storm chaser working salvage for whoever paid. Which meant someone had sent her, and whoever sent her wanted either me or the case, and probably didn't much care which.

"I'm not going anywhere without it," I told her, nodding at the aluminum case strapped across my back like a second spine. "So before you decide what kind of person you are today, you should know what's inside."

She crouched by the window, watching the drowned valley—the rooftops of Wendell's main street standing out of the brown water like teeth, the grain elevator leaning, the levee running its long gray scar along the eastern shore. "Show me."

I unstrapped the case and set it between us on the floorboards. Inside: the data drives, the printed transects, the bathymetric charts, and the thing that had broken my team apart in a single night—a set of engineering specifications for the East Fork levee, stamped APPROVED, dated fourteen months before the flood.

"Read the freeboard spec," I said.

* * *

**Emrys**

I'd read flood data before. You don't chase supercells across four states without learning what a hydrograph looks like, what a hundred-year event means on paper versus what it does to a town. So I read the number, and then I read it again, because the first time my eyes must have lied to me.

"The design freeboard is forty centimeters," I said. "For a levee holding back a reservoir this size?"

"For a levee holding back a reservoir they knew would hit record pool," Wendell said. He had a voice like gravel settling—low, patient, the voice of a man who had rehearsed this explanation alone in a bell tower for nine days. "Our own modeling put the probable maximum flood at two point three meters above the crest. We flagged it. We filed the discrepancy report. Three weeks later, our funding was restructured, our access to the dam telemetry was revoked, and Dr. Amara Voss—" he caught himself, corrected, "—Dr. Amara Chen, our team lead, was reassigned. Then the water came."

Outside, the rain ticked against the remaining glass. Somewhere across the valley a roof joist groaned and gave, a sound like a bone breaking underwater.

"And the land," I said slowly, because I could see where the road went and I hated the view. "Flooded valley's federal jurisdiction. Emergency transfer authority."

"Section 4(c) of the Watershed Security Act." He pulled a folded printout from his jacket, soft as cloth from handling. "The whole valley—eleven thousand acres, every farm, every home, the town itself—transfers to federal custody the moment the President declares it uninhabitable. There's a land disposition schedule already drawn up. Parcel numbers. Appraised values from *before* the flood, which is generous of them, except the appraisals are worthless now and everyone knows it. The families get relocation stipends calculated off those same pre-flood values. The government gets the valley for pennies on the dollar, and the water table under it happens to sit on the largest untapped aquifer recharge zone in three states."

I looked at the documents and felt the particular cold that has nothing to do with weather. This wasn't negligence. Negligence I could have sold myself a story around—bad math, bad luck, bad men cutting corners. This was arithmetic. Someone had sat in a warm office and calculated exactly how much water it would take to make eleven thousand acres change hands, and then signed their name to it.

"Why are the others gone?" I asked. "If agents took them 'for debriefing,' why take the people and leave the data?"

"Because they thought the data was here." He tapped the case. "We split it. Amara kept the primary drives; I carried duplicates. When the extraction team came—black vehicles, no plates, badges shown fast enough to blur—they took nine of us. I was checking the upstream gauge when they rolled into camp. I saw them load Amara last. She looked straight at me across the water and shook her head once. *Don't come.* So I didn't. I took the case and I climbed."

He said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. Nine days in a belfry eating ration bars, watching his colleagues' camp sit empty below him, coffee going cold in the pot because no one had lived long enough in that moment to turn off the generator.

"And you want to take this to the press," I said.

"I want to take it to the hearings. There's a congressional oversight session convening in six weeks. Public testimony, public record, cameras on everything. A document entered into evidence can't be disappeared the way a hard drive can." His eyes were dry and very steady. "I watched them take nine people who knew too much. Paper survives people. That's the whole point of paper."

* * *

**Emrys**

Here is what nobody tells you about debt: it changes the units of everything. Before Rivergate bought my paper, a gallon of diesel was a gallon of diesel. Now it was a fraction of a payment. An hour of my life was an hourly rate. And a sealed aluminum case full of evidence that the United States government drowned a valley on purpose—

That was a number. I stood in that bell tower doing the math before I could stop myself, the way you flinch before you decide to flinch.

Rivergate ran salvage contracts all through the flood country. They'd posted the Meridian job—find the team, recover government property, discreet delivery. Standard language. But I'd been around collectors long enough to know that *discreet* meant *valuable*, and valuable meant there was a buyer behind the posting, and a buyer who wanted classified federal engineering documents badly enough to fund a search operation did not want them for a museum.

Sable Voss would pay my ledger in full. Not just the principal—the penalties, the compounding interest, the ugly little clause about collateral recovery that meant my truck and possibly my freedom. One handoff. One boat ride to a marina. Clean.

And Wendell Okafor would go into the water alongside his evidence, because dead men make unreliable witnesses, and I'd be driving away from the sound of it with a zero balance and clean hands that weren't clean at all.

"You're doing it right now," Wendell said quietly.

I looked up. He wasn't angry. That was the worst part—he looked like a man watching weather, tracking something he'd already accepted was coming.

"Doing what?"

"Weighing it. What I'm worth to you against what I'm worth to them." He resettled the strap on his shoulder. "I don't blame you. I've read your truck. Two repossession notices under the wiper, both from Rivergate Financial. They own you the way the government owns this valley—on paper, at a price you never agreed to."

"That's not the same thing."

"Isn't it? Somebody did the math on you too, Ms. Ishikawa. Decided what your labor was worth, structured the terms so you could never quite clear it." He gestured at the flooded town below us, at the rooftops and the leaning elevator and the levee holding back its deliberate ocean. "Eleven thousand people down there, and every one of them got the same offer I'm watching you consider right now: your survival in exchange for your silence. Most of them took it. That's why the streets are empty."

The rain thickened against the window. Down in the valley, the water kept rising the way it had been told to, patient and obedient, filling the shape someone had drawn for it.

"If I take that case to Rivergate," I said, "you understand what happens."

"I understand exactly what happens." He met my eyes. "The question is whether you do."

I opened my mouth to answer—and my radio crackled to life on my hip, the encrypted channel I'd been given with the contract, the voice on the other end one I knew better than I wanted to.

*"Ishikawa. Sable Voss. I know you found him. Don't do anything stupid—we should talk about your ledger. Tonight, the old marina. Come alone."*

Wendell heard every word. Neither of us moved.

"Well," he said. "I guess we both have an appointment."
