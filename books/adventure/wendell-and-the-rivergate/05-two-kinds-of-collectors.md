## 5. Two Kinds of Collectors

The rain found us an hour after dark, a thin cold drizzle that turned the levee road into a ribbon of grease. Emrys parked the truck in the lee of a collapsed grain elevator, killed the lights, and sat with her hands on the wheel, listening to the water work at the pilings below. Somewhere out in the drowned fields, a heron cried once and went quiet.

Wendell was asleep in the passenger seat, the classified case locked around his wrist by a bike chain he'd produced from his pack like a magician's trick. He slept the way soldiers sleep—half-submerged, ready to surface. Even now his fingers stayed curled near the combination dial.

She should have been sleeping too. Instead she was doing arithmetic.

The contract had said *recover survivors*, singular or plural, deliverable to federal custody. It had not said what happened after delivery. It had not mentioned that the Meridian Survey's findings were sealed, or that sealed things had a way of making the people who carried them disappear. She'd told herself for two days that she was a salvage operator, not an accessory. The math kept telling her otherwise.

The radio crackled.

Not the emergency band. Not the syndicate frequency she'd swept twice since leaving the church. A third channel, clean and official, the kind of signal that came with a badge attached.

*"Mobile unit to salvage contractor Ishikawa. Come in, Ishikawa."*

Her hand hovered over the set. Wendell's eyes opened in the dark—not startled, just awake, as if he'd been listening to the silence the whole time and had simply noticed it change.

"That'll be Corliss," he said quietly.

"You know him?"

"I know the voice. He did the debriefings." Wendell sat up slowly, the chain clinking. "He has a very reasonable voice. That's how you know."

Emrys keyed the mic. "Ishikawa. Go ahead."

*"Ms. Ishikawa. Glad we finally made contact. I understand you've located Dr. Okafor."* A pause, measured, professional. *"I want to be clear about your situation. Dr. Okafor is not well. He's been under extraordinary stress, and the survey team's disappearance has affected him severely. The others required immediate medical intervention. He is not a reliable narrator of events."*

"Events," Emrys repeated. "Which events?"

*"Whatever he's told you. Whatever's in that case he's carrying."* Another pause, longer this time, and she could hear the smile in it, the patient tone of a man talking someone down off a ledge. *"I'm authorized to make this simple for you. Deliver Okafor and the case to the extraction point at Marker Seven by 0600, and your contract pays out double. No questions, no paperwork complications. You walk away clean."*

"And if I don't?"

*"Then you're obstructing a federal investigation, Ms. Ishikawa. Your license, your truck, your contracts—all of it goes into review. And Dr. Okafor comes in regardless. The only variable is whether you're standing next to him when it happens."*

She let the silence stretch. Out in the black water, something moved—a fish, a log, a body of current shifting its weight. Wendell watched her face in the glow of the dash lights, saying nothing, letting her hear it all.

"Marker Seven," she said. "0600."

*"Good woman. Corliss out."*

The channel died. She set the handset down carefully, the way you set down a snake you didn't want to wake.

"He's lying," Wendell said. "About the others. About me. About all of it."

"I know."

"Do you? Because he offered you exactly what you need. Double pay. Clean exit. No debt collectors." Wendell's voice wasn't angry. It was worse than angry—it was gentle, the way you talk to someone you're afraid will break. "You could be free tomorrow morning, Emrys. All you have to do is hand me over."

She looked at him. Rain ticked on the roof. "That's the thing about offers," she said. "When they're too good, they're not offers. They're prices. Somebody's already decided what you cost."

* * *

They didn't go to Marker Seven. They went north along the levee crest, running dark, Wendell navigating by memory of a valley he'd mapped himself—the contour lines still living behind his eyes even though the landscape they described was underwater now. Emrys drove with the windows cracked so she could hear the water against the roadbed, judging depth by sound the way her father had taught her, back when the only thing they chased was weather.

She was thinking about the offer. That was the problem. Some traitorous part of her brain kept turning it over, polishing it, holding it up to the light. *Double pay. Clean exit.* Her debt to Rivergate stood at forty-one thousand dollars plus interest compounding monthly, and every month she missed a payment the number grew teeth. One season, Voss had told her. This season. Or the truck went, and then the tools, and then—

Headlights bloomed in the mirror.

Two sets, high-mounted, closing fast. Not federal—federal ran unmarked sedans and government plates. These were aftermarket light bars on lifted chassis, the kind of rig that said *we don't answer to anyone's dispatch.*

"Wendell."

"I see them." He was already moving, pulling the case tight against his chest.

The road ahead curved along the levee top with water on both sides, a quarter mile of no options. Emrys weighed it in half a second and took the curve at speed, gravel spitting, the trailer swaying. In the mirror the headlights split—one set holding the road, one set dropping down onto the flooded field road below, taking the parallel line, boxing her.

A spotlight snapped on behind her, white and absolute, turning her mirrors into sheets of fire. Then a voice through a PA, amplified and unhurried, almost friendly:

*"Ishikawa. Pull it over. You're driving my employer's collateral."*

Sable Voss. Of course. The universe had a sense of humor, and its sense of humor was timing.

Emrys braked—not because she wanted to, but because the road narrowed ahead where the flood had eaten the shoulder, and a wreck out here meant the case went into the water, and whatever else was true, she wasn't ready to drown the evidence yet. She pulled up under a dead cottonwood whose branches hung over the levee like scaffolding. The second vehicle rose onto the road from the field side, mud sheeting off its tires, and boxed her rear bumper. Two vehicles, four doors opening, eight shadows in the rain.

Only one person got out.

Sable Voss walked toward the truck the way she did everything—like the world had been arranged in advance for her convenience. Tall, gray-haired, wearing a rain shell worth more than Emrys's monthly payment, hands loose at her sides. She stopped three meters back, out of door-swing range, and smiled.

"Evening, Emrys."

"Sable."

"You've been hard to find. I had to buy a favor off a weather service tech to narrow your last fuel stop. Cost me two hundred dollars." Voss tilted her head toward the passenger window. "And there he is. The famous survivor. Dr. Okafor, is it? You look better than the reports suggested."

"The reports," Wendell said through the glass, "were written by the people who took my team."

"Mm." Voss didn't argue. That was the frightening thing about her; she never argued. "Here's the shape of the evening, Emrys, and I'll keep it short because the rain's coming in heavy and neither of us enjoys wet boots. My employers are aware you're carrying certain materials. Materials with market value. Considerable market value—to several interested parties, including, incidentally, the people who own the paper your debt is written on."

"So this is a collection call."

"This is an *opportunity*." Voss let the word sit in the rain between them. "Hand over the case. Both of you step out of the story entirely. And the ledger—" she made a small gesture, elegant, final, like a conductor cutting off a note— "burns. Forty-one thousand, gone. Interest, gone. The truck stays yours. You drive out of this valley a free woman tonight."

Emrys felt it land, the way they'd designed it to land, right in the soft place where fear lived. Free. Tonight. One object, one exchange, one closed door behind her forever.

"And Wendell?" she asked.

Voss's smile thinned by a degree. "Dr. Okafor is a separate transaction. Not yours to negotiate."

"There it is."

"There what is?"

"The price list." Emrys opened her door and stepped out into the rain, putting herself between Voss and the truck. Behind her she heard Wendell's door stay shut—he understood, without being told, that the case chained to his wrist was the only reason nobody had simply shot them both yet. "Corliss wants him delivered by 0600. You want the case. Neither of you wants him *talking*. So here's my math, Sable: whoever gets their piece first makes the other party's piece worthless. Which means you're not partners. Which means one of you is planning to close the other one out, and I'm standing in the middle of the closing."

For the first time, something moved behind Voss's eyes. Recalculation. Respect, maybe, of the cold kind.

"You always did do good arithmetic," she said. "It's why we lent to you in the first place. People who can count are people who can be trusted to understand consequences." She glanced past Emrys at the truck, at the silhouette of the man inside it. "But consider this, Emrys. The feds have helicopters, warrants, and the patience of geology. We have money, which is faster than all three. When this valley finishes drowning and the land changes hands—and it *will* change hands, that outcome was purchased years ago—the only question left is who was smart enough to be paid on the way down." She stepped back toward her vehicle. "You have until dawn. Same marker Corliss gave you. Bring the case, leave the doctor, and the ledger burns. Bring the doctor instead, and Corliss pays you—but my employers remember. There's no version of tomorrow where you owe nothing, Emrys. There's only versions where you owe *us*, or you owe *them*."

"And if I bring neither?"

Voss paused with her hand on the door. The rain ran silver down her shoulders.

"Then you're not a debtor anymore," she said. "You're a liability. And we have a different process for those."

The vehicles pulled away in sequence, headlights shrinking down the levee until the rain swallowed them, and the night folded back into darkness and the sound of water.

Emrys stood in the road a long moment, soaked through, listening to her own breathing. Then she climbed back into the cab. Wendell had the case in his lap and the combination dial half-turned, watching her with those steady, grief-hardened eyes.

"Well?" he said. "Do the math."

She started the engine. "The math says everybody's bidding on us because we're worth more together than apart. And the math says dawn is four hours away, and both buyers expect delivery at the same marker, which means somebody's planning an ambush, and it isn't going to be us." She dropped the truck into gear and swung north, away from Marker Seven, away from every road either collector expected her to take. "So we stop doing business."

"Then what do we do?"

Emrys watched the drowned valley slide past the windshield, black water to the horizon, and felt—for the first time in years—something that wasn't fear.

"We run the river," she said. "Through the town. Where your people are."
