## 6. Running Water

The airboat smelled of gasoline and mildew and, faintly, of the church itself—incense soaked so deep into the cushions that no flood could drown it. I sat behind the engine cage with the classified case strapped across my chest like a breastplate, and I watched Emrys Ishikawa work the throttle the way I imagined she worked everything: like the machine might betray her if she blinked.

We ran the drowned county road at first light, or what passed for light under a sky the color of wet slate. The road was gone. What remained was a suggestion of it—fence posts breaking the surface in patient rows, the peaked roof of a barn riding just above the waterline, a stop sign leaning out of six feet of brown water as if still trying to do its job. The propeller roared behind us and threw spray in a cold fan across my back, and every few hundred yards Emrys would kill the throttle to half and listen.

"Corliss will have the main channel," she shouted over the engine. "Highway 12 bridge, the boat ramp at Miller's Bend. We go around."

"Around adds two hours."

"Around keeps us breathing." She glanced back at me, rain streaming off her jaw. "You want to argue, argue. But we're doing it my way until we're somewhere they can't reach us."

I didn't answer. I'd spent four days in a steeple watching the water take my valley, and I had learned something about arguing with forces bigger than you: you don't win by shouting at them. You win by knowing where they have to flow.

The case against my ribs was warm from my body heat. Inside it were eleven months of readings, calibration logs, flow models—the whole quiet arithmetic of a crime. People think evidence is dramatic. It isn't. It's a man named Okafor sitting in a government trailer at two in the morning, running the levee specifications one more time because he cannot believe them, and then running them again, and then sitting very still while the coffee goes cold, understanding that the numbers are not an error. Errors don't repeat themselves this neatly. Errors don't come with a signature block.

* * *

We cut south through what had been the Hollis farm. The farmhouse stood to its windows in water, and a piano had floated out through an upstairs wall and lodged in the branches of an oak, keys furred green with algae. I looked at it too long. Emrys saw me looking.

"You knew these people," she said. Not a question.

"I calibrated gauges on their irrigation ditches. Tom Hollis paid me in tomatoes because he didn't trust banks." I heard my own voice come out flat and hard, and I let it. "His family got thirty-one days' notice and a relocation voucher worth less than his tractor."

"And your plan is to stand up in front of cameras with that case and fix all of it."

"My plan is to make sure it can't happen again. To anyone."

"The plan is you get shot before lunch." She banked us around a submerged grain silo, its steel flank streaked with the high-water mark like a tide chart written in rust. "Listen to me, Wendell. I've been chased before. Not by governments—by weather, which is worse, because weather doesn't negotiate and neither does it pretend. Corliss isn't pretending either. When a man like that says someone is unstable and dangerous, that's not a description. That's paperwork for a bullet. You walk into a press conference waving classified documents, you're not exposing anything. You're giving them a clean scene."

"And if I run? If we both run?" I leaned forward so she could hear me without shouting. "Then the survey team died for nothing. Then Meridian, Alvarez, Chen—they're just names on a debriefing report, and the valley gets rezoned, and ten years from now there's a reservoir with a nice plaque and nobody remembers there was ever a town called anything but Lake Wendell." I said the name of the lake and tasted how wrong it was. They hadn't even waited for us to be gone before they renamed us. "I watched that water come up, Ms. Ishikawa. I know exactly what it rose over. I'm not going to let it rise over the truth too."

For a long moment there was only the engine and the water slapping the hull. Then Emrys said, quietly, "Survival first. Exposure second. In that order, or not at all." And she opened the throttle, and the argument was over without being settled, which is how most true arguments end.

* * *

They hit us at the old rail crossing.

I saw it before she did—a glint on the embankment where a glint shouldn't be—and I grabbed her shoulder and pointed, and she read it in half a second and hauled the boat into a turn so hard I went to one knee on the deck plates. The first round skipped off the water twenty feet behind us, a strange flat sound, like a stone thrown by God. Then the real firing started, three positions along the rail bed, disciplined, bracketing, walking their shots toward our line of travel. Professionals. Corliss's people didn't miss; they herded.

"He's not trying to kill us," Emrys snarled, flattening us both below the console. "He's pushing. He wants us going where he wants us going."

"Where?"

She looked at the map in her head—I could see her doing it, eyes flicking, calculating currents—and her face did something complicated. "Rivergate."

"The town's flooded."

"The town's empty. Which means it's cover, and it's maze, and it's the last place a federal team wants to be seen shooting at civilians." She swung the bow toward the drowned streets. "Hold on to that case like it's your heartbeat, professor. Because today it is."

We went into Rivergate between the rooftops.

That is a sentence I will carry the rest of my life. Main Street lay nine feet under, and we ran it like a canal, threading between traffic lights that still cycled green-yellow-red for no one, past a hardware store with its awning underwater and a barbershop pole turning slowly in the current, still spinning, still spinning, as if the town's heart hadn't quite stopped. Water lapped at the second-story windows. A church bell hung loose in its yoke and tolled once when our wake rocked the tower, one low bronze note rolling across the flooded blocks, and I felt it in my sternum like a hand pressing down.

Emrys killed the engine and we drifted, silent now except for dripping, into the lee of a collapsed parking structure. Behind us, faint across the water, came the sound of an outboard—not ours, not one, several—circling, searching, patient as herons.

"They'll sweep street by street," she whispered. "We move roof to roof, stay off open water, and—" 

"Wendell?"

The voice came from above us. From a window.

A woman leaned out of the second floor of what had been the pharmacy, gray-haired, one arm braced on the sill, and behind her in the gloom of the room I could see others—faces, a dozen faces, maybe more, drawn back from the light like fish under a dock. She stared down at me and her mouth trembled.

"It's him," she called back into the room, and her voice cracked on it. "It's Wendell Okafor. The hydrologist. He tried to tell us—he came to the town meeting in March, he tried to tell us about the levee—" She turned back to me, and there was no accusation in her face, which undid me more than accusation would have. There was only a terrible, exhausted hope. "Son, tell me you brought it. Tell me you brought the proof."

I looked up at her. At the faces crowding behind her—an old man holding a cat in a pillowcase, a boy about ten with a life vest three sizes too big, a young couple pressed together at the glass. Displaced. Evacuated on thirty-one days' notice, scattered to vouchers and gymnasium floors, and now come home anyway, because there is nowhere else that knows your name.

Emrys's hand closed on my arm. *Don't*, she didn't say. Her grip said it for her. Survival first. Every instinct she had, every scar, every mile of running—it was all in that hand on my sleeve.

But the woman at the window was Mrs. Adeyemi, who had rung up my batteries and coffee for eleven years and never once let me pay on a Sunday. And behind her were the Hollises' neighbors, and the choir director from my own church, and the boy in the too-big life vest who would grow up in a town that existed only on a federal land transfer document unless somebody stood up right here, right now, in the teeth of it.

I unslung the case and held it up where they could see the CLASSIFIED stamp bleeding through the rain.

"I brought it," I said. "All of it. And I'm not leaving without telling you what's inside."

The murmur that moved through that flooded building was quieter than any storm I've ever chased, and louder than all of them put together. Emrys's grip loosened. She looked at me for a long moment—measuring, recalculating, the way she read a sky—and then she did the last thing I expected.

She pulled the airboat around to the loading dock below the window and held it steady.

"Get them down here," she said. "All of them. Because if Corliss is sweeping this town, we've got maybe twenty minutes before he finds a building full of witnesses—and I need to decide whose side of that story gets told."

Across the water, closing fast, an outboard engine changed pitch.

It wasn't alone.
