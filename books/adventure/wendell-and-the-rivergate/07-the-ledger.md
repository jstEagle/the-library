## 7. The Ledger

The bank of Rivergate had been a handsome building once. Emrys could see it in the bones of the place—the fluted pilasters furred with mold, the marble floor buckled into waves where floodwater had gotten underneath and lifted it like skin. Silt lay over everything in a gray velvet half-inch deep, softening the edges of the world. Her boots left prints that filled slowly with seepage, as if the building were bleeding from below.

Wendell stood watch at the broken front doors while she swept her flashlight across the lobby. The beam caught a teller's counter, its brass rail green with tarnish. A calendar still hung on the wall behind it, turned to April, the month the water came.

"You're sure nobody's been through here?" she asked.

"Nobody's been through anything," Wendell said. "The town emptied in nine days. The ones who stayed past that were carried out." He said it flatly, the way he'd said everything since the levee—grief compressed until it rang like struck metal. "Feds sealed the north road on day three. Called it a safety perimeter."

"And the syndicate?"

"Rivergate's people were gone before the water crested. Voss's crews came through two weeks later with clipboards." His jaw worked. "Buying."

Emrys moved behind the counter. Old habit: when she entered a structure, she read it for exits, load paths, places to put her back. But something else was operating in her now, something colder and more patient. She'd spent the airboat run out here doing arithmetic—Voss's offer on one side of the ledger, Corliss's voice on the radio on the other, and Wendell's case sitting between them like a stone that both of them wanted her to carry. Two kinds of collectors, one debt. The math only worked if she was the variable being solved for.

So when her light found the door marked RECORDS VAULT, she didn't think about exits at all.

* * *

The vault door stood open an inch. That was the first wrong thing. Banks did not leave their vaults open; even drowned banks, even abandoned ones, kept their steel shut unless someone had come with a reason and a crowbar. She put her shoulder to it and the hinges groaned, shedding rust in orange flakes, and the smell that rolled out was paper and mildew and something faintly chemical—toner, maybe, or the ghost of a fire suppressed decades ago.

Inside, the shelves had been built to survive anything except neglect. Steel cabinets in ranks, drawers labeled in a dead man's careful hand: MORTGAGES 1987–1994. DEEDS. LIENS. TRUST ACCOUNTS. And along the back wall, incongruous among all the civic dust, a modern filing cabinet, matte black, no labels, its lock punched out cleanly.

Emrys crouched in front of it and told herself she was looking for cover documents, proof of identity, anything that might help them move unseen. That was the story she gave herself. But her hands already knew what they were reaching for.

The top drawer held loan files. She pulled one at random: HALLORAN, D., RIVERGATE TWP., PARCEL 114-C. A farm mortgage, sixty acres of bottomland, payments current for eleven years. Then a second document clipped behind it, dated four months before the flood—a foreclosure notice, initiated by Rivergate Holdings LLC, citing a *material change in regional risk assessment*. Emrys stared at the phrase. Material change. As if the valley's risk had shifted on its own, like weather. As if nobody had signed anything.

She pulled another file. OKAFOR, J., PARCEL 121-A. The name went through her like a current.

"Wendell," she called, and her voice came out wrong, too loud in the vault. "Come here. I need you to look at something."

His footsteps crossed the lobby slow, reluctant, a man walking toward a grave he already knew the location of. He ducked through the vault door and she held up the file without a word. He took it. She watched him read his father's name, watched his face do nothing at all, which was worse than watching it break.

"Payments current," he said finally. "Eleven years. Dad kept every receipt in a shoebox. He used to joke the bank knew his handwriting better than Mom did." He turned the foreclosure notice over as if there might be more on the back. There wasn't. "Four months before the flood. They foreclosed on him for a risk assessment nobody outside this valley had ever heard of."

Emrys pulled the next file. Then the next. The pattern repeated down the whole drawer like a printed circuit: current loans, healthy farms, then a foreclosure notice citing the same phrase, the same risk assessment, stamped by the same office—RIVERGATE HOLDINGS, ACQUISITIONS DIVISION. And behind each notice, a purchase record. Sale prices that made her breath go shallow. Forty acres of irrigated bottomland for eleven thousand dollars. A century farmhouse with two barns for six. Pennies. Less than pennies. The numbers weren't prices; they were receipts for theft conducted with paperwork instead of guns.

"They bought the valley before the water ever moved," she said. "They didn't wait for the flood. They *scheduled* it."

Wendell set his father's file down on the shelf with exaggerated care, the way you set down something when the alternative is throwing it. "The survey data," he said. "The undersized levee. It wasn't just the government wanting the land. Somebody had to be ready to take it off everyone's hands the moment they were forced out. You don't run a transfer like that without a buyer standing by."

"The buyer was standing by with a filing cabinet." Emrys was already moving down the row, pulling files, fanning them open under her light. "Look at the dates. Foreclosures cluster here—" she tapped a column of them, "—right after the survey team's first readings would've gone internal. Right after somebody knew the levee spec had been approved low. This isn't opportunism. This is sequencing."

She heard her own voice change as she said it. It dropped out of the register she used for haggling with collectors and into one she didn't recognize—level, cold, certain. Somewhere in the last five minutes the fear had drained out of her and left something else standing in its place, and the something else was furious.

Because she knew these documents. Not their contents—their *shape*. She had spent six years signing things shaped exactly like this. Risk assessments. Forfeiture clauses. Material changes in terms. The syndicate hadn't invented its machinery for the valley; it had simply pointed the machine at a new field. Her truck note, her salvage contracts, the season deadline Voss had given her on the plains—all of it ran on the same grammar. *You agreed. You understood the risk. The terms changed; your obligation didn't.* The difference between her and Daniel Halloran was only that her collateral had wheels.

Her debt and this conspiracy shared a spine. She could feel it running up through the floor of the vault, through the drowned town, all the way to whatever office Voss reported to—and beyond him, apparently, to whoever in the government had signed off on a levee built to fail. One animal. Many hands.

"Emrys." Wendell's voice pulled her back. He was holding a folder from the bottom of the cabinet, thinner than the rest, and there was something in his posture she'd learned to read on the airboat: the hydrologist reading data he didn't want to be right about. "There's a schedule here. Acquisitions, by parcel, with target dates." He looked up. "Target dates. They had the flood on a calendar."

She took it from him. The final page listed dispositions—what Rivergate Holdings intended to do with the assembled land. Most of it was legalese, but one line had been highlighted in yellow by some clerk who never imagined an audience: *Consolidated parcel to be conveyed to federal authority upon completion of emergency transfer, per agreement, with development rights retained.*

Development rights retained. The syndicate wasn't just the government's fence—it was the government's partner. They'd split the valley between them like men splitting a coat.

"Photograph everything," Wendell said quietly.

"I'm photographing everything."

She worked fast and methodical, the way she shot storm structure—wide establishing frames, then detail, then the context shot that tied it together. File headers with parcel numbers. Foreclosure dates beside acquisition dates. The highlighted disposition page. She shot the cabinet label-less and anonymous, then shot it again with the vault door in frame so no one could claim the documents had come from anywhere else. Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Under the fear there had been a girl who'd grown up watching her mother's shop get eaten by a lease agreement clause by clause, and that girl had waited a long time to see the machinery written down.

When her phone's storage warning blinked, she swapped to the backup card in her jacket pocket—the one she'd always kept for irreplaceable storm footage, the one she'd told herself she'd fill someday with something worth keeping. Well. Here it was.

* * *

It was Wendell who heard it first. He raised a hand and she killed her light mid-frame, and they stood in the dark listening to the town breathe.

Water, mostly. Water moving through streets, dripping from eaves, finding its old channels. But underneath it, distant and rhythmic, an engine—not the airboat's idle, not any sound a displaced fisherman made. An outboard running at patrol speed, throttling down.

Then voices. More than one. Carrying flat across the water the way sound does when there's nothing left standing to interrupt it.

Emrys eased to the vault doorway. Through the ruined front doors she could see the flooded intersection beyond, gray under a lid of cloud, and a boat sliding into view around the collapsed corner of the hardware store. Dark hull. Three figures. The lead one stood at the bow with the balanced, unhurried stance of someone who had done this a hundred times in a hundred drowned towns, and even at this distance Emrys knew the silhouette the way you know a voice on the phone.

Sable Voss.

Beside her, taller, in a rain shell that cost more than Emrys's truck, a man raised a radio to his mouth. Even muffled by distance and water, one word reached the bank intact because it was spoken with the crispness of a man who expected to be obeyed:

"—Corliss to all units."

Emrys drew back from the doorway. Her heart was going hard now, but her mind had gone strangely quiet, the way it did inside a wall cloud when there was nothing left to decide and only things to do. She looked at Wendell. Wendell looked at the black cabinet full of other families' names.

"They're coming for the records," he whispered.

"No." Emrys slid the backup card into the waterproof case strapped against her ribs, next to the survey findings, and felt the weight of both like a second heartbeat. "They're coming for us. The records are just how they'll know it worked."

Out on the water, the engine cut. In the silence after, she heard Voss call out, clear and carrying, aimed at the bank like a thrown knife:

"Emrys Ishikawa! We know you're in there. Come out with the case, and we'll talk about your ledger!"

Emrys closed her eyes for one breath. *My ledger.* Of course Voss knew about the vault. Of course the syndicate had known what sat in this building all along—and had left it, guarded by nothing but floodwater, because no debtor was ever supposed to be desperate enough, or angry enough, to come looking.

They'd made one mistake. They'd sent her here.

She checked the charge on her flare gun, put her back to the marble, and answered the silence with silence—while outside, hulls bumped against stone, and the water in the street began to carry footsteps toward the doors.
