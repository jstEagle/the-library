## 8. High Water Mark

**Wendell**

The storm came up the valley like something returning to collect.

Wendell felt it before he saw it—the pressure drop in his bad knee, the way the flooded cottonwoods all leaned east at once, the birds going quiet in a way that wasn't quiet at all but a held breath. He stood on the levee above what used to be Main Street, Rivergate's drowned spine, and watched the wall cloud build itself out of the western sky, green-black and rotating slow, the way the big ones did when they'd decided the land belonged to them.

Behind him, Emrys was rigging the airboat's radio to the emergency band repeater they'd stripped from the fire station. Her hands moved fast, sure, stripping wire with her teeth when the pliers slipped. She hadn't slept in thirty hours. Neither had he. Grief made a strange kind of fuel; you burned it and burned it and it never ran low.

"Antenna's good for maybe six miles," she said, not looking up. "The band's monitored in Halstead and Corning. After that, somebody has to rebroadcast."

"Somebody will." He thought of the Okafor farm, forty miles south, where his mother still listened to the emergency band every evening out of habit, out of the years when it was the only voice that told the truth about rising water. "My mother's set will pick it up. And Marta Reyes at the grain elevator keeps hers on all night. Once it's out, it's out."

Emrys finally looked up at him, and in her face he saw the argument they'd already had three times, still unfinished. *Run now, broadcast later. Live first, testify second.* She was a woman who'd survived by never being where the danger was looking. He understood it the way you understood a foreign language—could translate it, could never speak it.

"They'll come before the storm does," she said. "Voss doesn't drive through weather. She *is* weather."

"Then we'd better be done before she arrives."

He knelt beside the case and opened it one more time, though he knew its contents by heart now, had dreamed them every night since the steeple: the levee specifications, the 2019 engineering review stamped *RECOMMENDED HEIGHT: 14.2 METERS* and beside it, in the same federal hand, *APPROVED: 9.6 METERS — BUDGET DIRECTIVE 7-C.* The memo explaining that a controlled overtopping would qualify the valley for emergency land transfer. The signatures. Eleven names, and one of them belonged to a man who was now Undersecretary for Water Resources.

The data didn't shout. That was the terrible thing about it. It sat in its tables and its cross-sections and it whispered, *we decided your town was worth less than the paperwork of taking it,* and it whispered in a voice no one could call a lie.

Wendell keyed the transmitter. The emergency band hissed open, empty, waiting.

"Testing," he said, and his voice went out over the drowned valley, over the rooftops poking from the flood like the backs of sleeping animals. "This is Wendell Okafor, hydrologist, Meridian Survey. If anyone is listening, stay listening. I have the findings."

* * *

**Emrys**

She saw the headlights first—two sets, coming up the levee road from the north, running dark until the last half mile. Voss. It had to be Voss. Rivergate's collectors didn't announce themselves; they arrived like a diagnosis.

"Company," Emrys said, and Wendell kept talking into the microphone, steady as a man reading scripture, listing gauge numbers and dates while the sky behind him turned the color of a bruise.

The trucks stopped a hundred meters out. Sable Voss got out of the first one alone, hands visible, coat snapping in the wind that was arriving ahead of the storm like an advance guard. That was the performance. Emrys knew the second truck held two men who were not performing anything.

"Dr. Okafor," Voss called, and her voice carried clean over the water, pitched for the microphone as much as for them. Clever woman. "I represent parties with a genuine interest in the valley's future. Whatever you've been told about us—"

"I've read your ledgers," Wendell said into the mic, and didn't stop reading gauge numbers.

Voss's smile thinned. She started walking. Emrys stepped off the levee crown and put herself in the path, and the two of them regarded each other in the failing light, two women who understood debt from opposite ends of the barrel.

"You're out of your depth, Ishikawa," Voss said quietly. "Your paper is worth more to me than to anyone alive. Clear ledger, clean title on the truck, cash besides. Walk away and the season's yours again. Stay—" she glanced at Wendell, at the little transmitter, at the storm stacking itself on the horizon— "and you drown with the rest of this town's history."

"You bought the foreclosures," Emrys said. "Pennies on the dollar. I've seen the records. You didn't come to buy the case, Voss. You came to bury it, and me with it, and you're offering me my own life back as the price of my silence."

Voss's eyes flickered—just once, just enough. "The offer stands until the rain starts."

"Then it's about to expire."

The first drops came down fat and warm, and Voss stepped back, and Emrys saw it happen—the moment the enforcer's arithmetic changed. Not mercy. Not conscience. Just a woman recalculating odds as the sky turned against her employer. Emrys filed it away, the way she filed everything, and turned back up the levee.

Wendell was still broadcasting. His voice had gone hoarse but his cadence hadn't changed, and now he'd stopped reading tables and started telling it plain: *they built the levee to fail, they built it to fail so they could take the valley, and here are the names—*

Headlights again, from the south this time. Three vehicles, government plates, moving fast through the rising rain.

* * *

**Wendell**

He knew the lead vehicle. He'd ridden in it eleven days ago, in the front seat, handcuffed and courteous, while Agent Corliss explained about debriefing and national security and how the others were being taken somewhere comfortable. Four of his team had climbed into that vehicle. He'd watched the taillights go and hidden in the church and told himself it was strategy and not cowardice, and he had never once believed himself.

Now the vehicles fanned out at the base of the levee and Corliss stepped out into the rain with a megaphone she didn't need and a pistol she probably did.

"Dr. Okafor!" she called. "You're a witness, not a suspect. Come down. We'll protect you."

Wendell looked at Emrys. Emrys looked at the sky, where the wall cloud had lowered into a ragged, turning skirt, and said, "That's a hook echo. That's a tornado trying to decide."

"Then it can decide," Wendell said, and keyed the mic again. "People of Rivergate, if you can hear me—the men at the base of this levee are not here to help you. Agent Corliss, you took my team on March the fourth. Nadia Ferro. Samuel Ochoa. Priya Raman. Tomás Beck. Tell me where they are, and tell these people why you really came."

The rain hit then, all at once, a white roaring curtain, and through it Wendell saw Corliss's face change. Not guilt. Something worse—*recalculation*. Her hand came up, not with the megaphone but with a radio, and though the storm ate the words, he saw her mouth move and saw the vehicles reposition, spreading along the levee's base like fingers closing.

Not arrest geometry. Containment geometry.

"They're going to silence everyone," Emrys said beside him, shouting now. "Us, the broadcast, the whole story—storm cover, no bodies ever found, another flood tragedy."

"Then we'd better be found," Wendell said.

The transmitter crackled. A voice came through the emergency band, thin through the static, and it was Marta Reyes at the grain elevator, forty miles south, and her voice was shaking: *"Say again, Rivergate. Say again. We hear you. God help us, we hear you."*

And then another voice. And another. Farmers, displaced families in the Halstead gymnasium, a night dispatcher in Corning who keyed in and simply said, "Rebroadcasting now. Everyone rebroadcast."

Wendell Okafor stood on the levee in the teeth of the storm, rain hammering the classified case open at his feet, and felt something he hadn't felt since the water took his town. Not safety. Nothing like safety. But *witnessed*. The data was out of his hands now, out into the dark, multiplying in other people's radios, and no one—not Corliss, not the Undersecretary, not the flood itself—could call it back.

Corliss started up the levee with six agents fanned behind her, and the tornado siren in the drowned town below began to howl, and Emrys grabbed his arm and pointed south along the levee crown, where headlights were coming again—Voss's truck, moving fast, and Voss was not slowing down.

"Dr. Okafor," Emrys shouted over the roar, "whatever you're about to do—do it *now*."
