## 9. Rivergate Rising

The storm came down the valley like something with intent.

Emrys had chased weather for eleven years and she had never seen a cell organize this fast—wall cloud spinning up out of nothing, the whole southern sky gone the color of wet slate, lightning strobing inside it like a pulse. The wind hit the levee in long shoving gusts that made her lean her shoulder into it just to stay upright. Below them, the drowned town of Rivergate flashed its streetlights through brown water, and above them the tornado siren howled its one broken note.

And Voss's truck was still coming.

"Get off the crown," Emrys shouted at Wendell. He wasn't listening. He had the survey's field transmitter braced against his chest, antenna up, feeding the emergency band with everything Meridian had died carrying—the levee schematics, the undersized spillway calculations, the internal memos with the federal letterhead still attached. His voice went out over the same frequency every displaced family in three counties kept tuned, because it was the only thing left that told them anything true.

"—repeat, the flood stage was set at nineteen feet when the design called for twenty-six," he was saying, rain hammering the transmitter's casing. "The documents are real. I'm reading you the file numbers now."

Voss's truck slewed to a stop thirty yards down the levee. She came out alone, coat snapping in the wind, one hand raised—not a weapon, an open palm—and Emrys felt the old animal part of herself catalog it anyway: *open palm means nothing, check the other hand.* Empty. Voss walked toward them through the horizontal rain like the storm was a minor administrative inconvenience.

"Ishikawa!" she shouted. "You want to live? Get him off this levee!"

"He's a little busy!"

"He's *dead* if Corliss gets here first, and so are you, and I am not paid enough—" Voss stopped, and Emrys saw it happen: the enforcer's eyes flicked south, toward the headlights of Corliss's convoy grinding up the access road, then down at the water swallowing the town, then at Wendell broadcasting into the dark. Something behind her face changed gears. Eleven years of reading storms had taught Emrys what a system looked like right before it flipped.

"You've read the ledger," Emrys shouted over the wind. "You know what Rivergate did to this valley. You're standing on their levee in their storm, Voss. How's the math feel from this side?"

Voss didn't answer. She turned and looked at the water again—at the rooftops, at the church steeple where Wendell had hidden for weeks, at the shapes beginning to move along the far bank.

Because that was the thing. They were coming.

* * *

They came out of the treeline first, then the boat ramp, then the ruined main street itself—wading, poling flatboats, riding airboats with their running lights off. Families. Dozens of them, then more than dozens, faces lit by lightning and by the phone screens they held up like candles. Emrys's photos were already on those screens; Wendell's voice was already in their ears. Somebody had printed pages and was passing them hand to hand down a line of boats. A woman Emrys recognized from the gas station on Route 9 stood hip-deep in what used to be her own front yard, holding a flashlight beam steady on the levee path like she was lighting a runway.

"They heard him," Emrys said. Her throat hurt. "Corliss cut the emergency band hours ago. She forgot about the boats."

Wendell kept transmitting. His voice cracked once—when he read the name of the survey team, all six names, slowly, into the storm—and then steadied. Emrys stood between him and the access road with nothing but a tire iron and eleven years of bad decisions, and found, to her genuine surprise, that she wasn't afraid. There was no room left for it. The storm had taken all the space inside her and filled it with something else.

Corliss's convoy reached the base of the levee and stopped. Six agents fanned out onto the crown, and Corliss climbed up last, and Emrys watched the exact moment the arithmetic failed her. The agent raised a megaphone, and whatever she intended to say was swallowed—because the crowd answered first. Not with violence. With light. Every flashlight, every phone, every boat lamp swung toward the levee at once, and the agents stood blinking in it like men caught mid-burgle.

Then somebody's radio crackled, loud, carried on the wind—and not just one radio. The agents' own comms were bleeding across channels in the electrical chaos of the storm, and the nearest boat had a marine band scanner, and whoever held it had turned the volume all the way up.

*"—extraction authorized, treat witnesses as hostile, repeat, treat witnesses as hostile—"*

Corliss's voice. Clear as a bell. Carried to two hundred people standing in the ruins of their homes.

The sound the crowd made wasn't a roar. It was quieter than that and worse—a low collective exhale, the sound of a valley deciding something. The line of boats began to move toward the levee. The woman with the flashlight started walking up the slope. An old man with a cane followed her, then a teenager, then everyone, a slow tide of mud-caked townsfolk flowing up onto the crown until the agents were ringed by people who had lost everything except each other.

Corliss lowered the megaphone. For a long moment nobody moved except the lightning.

"Dr. Okafor," Corliss said, and her voice carried without any megaphone at all, pitched to carry, calm as a briefing. "This is a federal operation. These people are being manipulated by stolen materials."

"No," Wendell said into the transmitter, and also just said it, out loud, to her face. "They're being informed. There's a difference. You should look it up—it used to be your job."

An agent near Corliss shifted his weight, hand drifting toward his holster, and Emrys moved before thinking—stepped into his sightline, arms wide, tire iron hanging loose and visible. Behind her she heard Voss say, clearly, conversationally:

"Stand down, Reyes."

Every head on that stretch of levee turned. Voss stood in the rain with her hands at her sides and her chin up, and she said it again, louder, to Corliss directly: "I said stand down. You heard the traffic. Everyone heard the traffic. This is over, and I am not drowning in a federal hole so Rivergate can keep a valley." She pulled a slim black phone from her coat, held it up, and let it drop into the floodwater. "That was my only copy of tonight's orders. Ask me why."

Nobody asked. Everybody knew.

Corliss looked at her six agents, at the crowd, at the sky tearing itself open above the valley, and Emrys watched her arrive at the same conclusion Voss had, one system flipping after another. The agent with the drifting hand took it off the holster. Corliss keyed her radio and said, flatly, "All units, stand down," and the words went out over the same storm-tangled channels that had betrayed her, and somewhere in the dark a scanner repeated them to the boats, and the tide of people on the levee made a sound this time that was almost a cheer.

It wasn't over. Emrys knew it wasn't over—hearings and lawyers and years of it waited downstream—but here, tonight, on this drowned crown of earth, it was *over*, and her body understood it before her mind did.

* * *

She went back to the truck while the storm spent itself over the far bluffs.

Her debt ledger lived in the glovebox because she'd never been able to decide whether keeping it close was discipline or punishment. A battered composition book, four years of entries in her own handwriting—every advance, every interest charge, every salvage job that evaporated into fees, the running total that had grown teeth. Rivergate's bookkeeping, transcribed by its debtor. She'd read it so many times she could recite the final figure like a prayer she hated.

She climbed onto the tailgate with the book and a road flare, and the crowd below had thinned toward shelter, but not all of them. Wendell stood at the truck's bumper with the transmitter case under his arm, rain streaming off his jaw, watching her. Voss leaned against the fender, arms crossed, saying nothing, which from Voss was a eulogy.

"This number," Emrys said, holding the ledger up. Her voice shook and she let it. "Four years I thought this book owned me. Turns out it was the same book that owned this whole valley. Same ink. Same hands." She struck the flare. Red light washed over the pages, over the columns of figures, over the word RIVERGATE stamped inside the cover. "I'm done paying rent on my own life."

She touched flame to paper.

It caught slow in the wet air, then caught properly, and she held it while it burned—held it past the point where heat bit her fingers, because some things you need to feel finish. Ash lifted off the tailgate and swirled into the storm. The final figure curled, blackened, and came apart into nothing, and Emrys Ishikawa stood in front of witnesses and watched four years of fear go up as smoke.

When there was nothing left but the cover, she dropped it in the standing water and stepped down. Her hands were trembling. That was fine. Storms passed; hands steadied.

Wendell was looking at her with an expression she'd have called pity in anyone else and recognized, in him, as recognition. "Feels different," he said. "Doesn't it."

"Yeah." She laughed, or something adjacent to it. "Lighter. Stupid lighter."

Down the levee, the last of Corliss's vehicles was turning around, headlights sweeping across the crowd, across the boats, across the woman with the flashlight who had started all of it. The siren in the town finally wound down into silence. In the west, behind the retreating storm, there was a seam of clean sky the color of the inside of a shell.

Voss pushed off the fender. "For the record," she said, "I still think you're both idiots. Brave ones. There'll be lawyers by morning, and hearings, and people in Washington very interested in that case." She glanced at Wendell. "Guard it with your life. Both of yours, apparently—you seem to share expenses."

She walked away down the levee into the dark, and Emrys realized she hadn't asked which side Voss would be on tomorrow, and realized she didn't need to. The current had turned. You could tell by which way the debris floated.

Wendell settled the case under his arm and looked east, where the water was already starting—imperceptibly, inch by inch—to fall.

"First hearing's Thursday," he said. "In the high school gym. If it's still standing." A tired grin. "You could drive me. You're the only one whose truck isn't underwater."

Emrys looked at the seam of sky, at the falling water, at the ash circling the drain of the world, and heard herself answer before the old reflexes could vote.

"Thursday," she said. "I'll be here."

And for the first time in eleven years, *here* wasn't a place she was passing through.
