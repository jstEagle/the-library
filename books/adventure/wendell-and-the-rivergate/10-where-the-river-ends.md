## 10. Where the River Ends

The hearings began on a Tuesday, in the gymnasium of a school that had spent six weeks underwater and still smelled faintly of silt and chlorine. Wendell Okafor arrived early, as he always did, to watch the light come through the tall windows and fall across the folding tables where the valley's families would sit. The water had taken their homes. It had not taken their names.

He set up the archive table by the door: the survey case, unlocked now, its contents laid out in acid-free sleeves. The readings. The levee specifications with their deliberate margins shaved thin. The memos with their careful bureaucratic language—*acceptable displacement thresholds*, *strategic land acquisition windows*. He had read them so many times he could recite them like scripture, and like scripture they had not lost their power to wound.

People filed in past him. The Aldridges, who'd farmed the north bend for three generations. Mrs. Okonkwo from the feed store, her hands still cracked from hauling salvage out of the flood line. The Delgado kids, barefoot because their shoes had floated away in April and nobody had found replacements that fit. Each of them paused at his table. Each of them looked at the evidence—not touching it, just looking, the way you look at a grave marker—and then signed the claim register Emrys had photographed into existence back when the bank was still full of river water.

Wendell watched them sign. He had spent his whole professional life believing data was neutral, that numbers belonged to no one. He knew better now. These numbers had owners. They had always had owners; the only question was whether the owners would be the men who engineered the flood or the people it was built to drown.

The committee chair called the session to order at nine. A federal panel, three of them, faces arranged into expressions of solemn neutrality. One of them had been at Lake Wendell the day the survey team scattered. Wendell recognized the jawline, the way he sat slightly apart from his colleagues, as if proximity were contagious. The man's name was Hargrove. Wendell had memorized it from the personnel files Corliss's team had left behind in their hurry to abandon the levee.

"Mr. Okafor," Hargrove said, when Wendell's name was called. "You were the senior hydrologist on the Meridian Survey."

"I was."

"Your colleagues have been... unavailable to testify."

"They've been sequestered," Wendell said. "That's the word your office uses. I've read the correspondence." He let the silence sit. In the old days—the days before the steeple, before the airboat, before Emrys Ishikawa crashed into his hiding place like weather made human—he would have softened it. Would have offered the man a door to walk through. "I'm the one who stayed. So I'll be the one who answers."

Hargrove consulted his papers, though Wendell doubted there was anything on them he didn't know by heart. "You allege the levee failure was foreseeable."

"I allege it was designed." Wendell opened the first sleeve. "Foreseeable is what happens when you're unlucky. Designed is what happens when you write the margin of safety down to eleven percent and then file an internal memo celebrating the cost savings six months before the rain comes." He slid the pages across the table. "Page four has the signature. Page seven has the date. I can wait while you find them."

It took Hargrove forty minutes to find them. Wendell counted every second, and so did the gymnasium, two hundred displaced people breathing quietly behind him, and somewhere near the back, he knew without looking, Emrys Ishikawa leaning against the wall with her arms crossed, watching the exits out of habit she hadn't yet unlearned.

* * *

The survey station had been his idea before the hearings even convened. The building still stood on its rise above the floodplain—one of the few structures the water had spared, as if it understood the difference between a home and a record. The roof leaked in two places. The generator shed needed a new fuel line. But the walls were sound, and the elevation was good, and when Wendell stood in the empty main room in the third week of September, he could see what it wanted to become.

A public archive. Not a vault, not a classified annex where truth went to be preserved by being buried. A room with a door that locked at night and stood open all day. Shelves for the survey data. Tables for the families' claims, the photographs, the deeds, the foreclosure records Emrys had pulled out of Rivergate's own bank. A copy machine that worked. Coffee that anyone could pour.

The town came together around it the way water finds its level. Mr. Aldridge repaired the roof and refused payment. The Delgado kids painted the sign—*WENDELL VALLEY PUBLIC RECORDS*—in letters that leaned but did not waver. Mrs. Okonkwo donated a coffee urn the size of a small engine block. And Emrys showed up with a truck bed full of shelving she'd salvaged from a school library two counties over, and said nothing about it, and unloaded it in the rain, and drove off before Wendell could thank her.

He catalogued for six hours that first night, alone in the lamplight, and found that for the first time since the water rose, his hands were steady. Grief had hardened into resolve, and resolve had done its work, and what remained underneath was something quieter. Purpose, maybe. Or the shape purpose leaves behind once it stops needing to be sharp.

He pinned the child's drawing to the wall above the catalogue desk. It had hung in the survey tent all those months ago—a crayon river, a crayon house, a stick family holding hands beneath a sun with too many rays. One of the team's daughters had drawn it. He had never learned which one. He kept it anyway, as a reminder of what the data was for. Every number in the archive pointed, eventually, at a sun with too many rays.

* * *

He found Emrys at the town limit on a Thursday morning in October, because that was where she had told him she'd be, and Wendell Okafor had learned that when Emrys Ishikawa told you where she'd be, she meant it. That was new. That was hers.

Her truck sat on the gravel shoulder where the county road crossed the old flood line, loaded to the rails: the storm-rigged antenna, the anemometer, the jerry cans, the duffel that had survived three years of running and looked ready for thirty more. She stood beside the driver's door with a paper cup of Mrs. Okonkwo's coffee, looking north up the valley road, and she did not get in.

"You've been sitting here twenty minutes," Wendell said, coming up beside her.

"Fifteen." She didn't look at him. "I'm deciding."

"Deciding what?"

"Where I'm going." She turned the cup in her hands. "I used to know automatically. Storm builds, I chase it. Debt grows, I chase work. Somebody's after me, I drive until the map runs out and then I make a new map. There was never a question. There was just the next thing running away from the last thing." She took a sip. "This is the first time in my adult life the next thing isn't defined by the last thing. Turns out I don't know how to do it."

Wendell looked north too. The valley spread out below the road, green again in patches, the flood scars fading into something the grass was slowly learning to cover. The river ran quiet in its channel, doing the patient work rivers do. Somewhere out there, the season's last supercells were building over the high plains, fat and blue-black and indifferent to everything humans wrote down about them.

"The ledger's burned," he said. "Voss testified yesterday. Corliss is in front of a Senate subcommittee by Christmas, if Hargrove's memo holds. Nobody's chasing you, Emrys. For the first time, the road is actually open."

"That's the problem." She laughed, short and dry. "An open road and no reason to run down it. I keep waiting for the dread to kick in. The math that says staying costs more than leaving. It doesn't come. I just stand here and the coffee gets cold and the truck doesn't move." She shook her head. "Do you know how loud it is? Stillness? Louder than any cell I ever chased."

"It gets quieter," Wendell said. "Not silent. Quieter. You learn to hear what's under it." He nodded back toward town, toward the archive with its crooked hand-painted sign. "Storm season's starting. The plains will be live by May. And this valley's going to need someone who knows how to read the sky—someone the townsfolk trust, which narrows the field considerably."

She finally looked at him. Her eyes were the color of the sky before hail, and for a long moment they searched his face the way she used to search a horizon.

"You're offering me a job."

"I'm offering you a choice," Wendell said. "Which is more than either of us got last spring. The station needs a warning system. The archive needs protecting when the next flood comes—because there will be a next flood; water doesn't hold grudges but it doesn't forget either. And I need—" He stopped, and found the honest word waiting. "I need someone who stays. I've had enough of people who leave. Including myself."

Emrys looked north up the open road a moment longer. Then south, toward the town, toward the smoke rising from Mrs. Okonkwo's chimney, toward the gymnasium where the hearings would resume at noon and the families would file in and sign their names in a register that could not be burned or buried or bought.

She drained the cold coffee. Set the cup on the gravel, deliberate, like closing an account.

"First storm of the season," she said, climbing into the cab—but she didn't start the engine, didn't turn the wheel toward the highway. Instead she reached across and pushed open the passenger door. "Ride along. I want to show you something the anemometer does when the shear lines cross. If I'm going to build your warning system, you should know what you're buying."

Wendell got in. The truck sat there a moment longer at the town limit, engine idling, pointing nowhere yet—and then Emrys turned it south, slow, unhurried, back down the hill toward the valley, and the open road waited behind them, patient as weather, for whenever she decided it was time.
