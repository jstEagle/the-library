## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

The storm had a name — Pakhar, some committee's idea of dignity for a thing that wanted to kill you — and Felix Eriksen chased it anyway, because chasing it paid and not chasing it didn't.

"Cell's tightening," Priya said from the nav station, her voice doing that calm thing it did when her hands were doing the opposite. "Rotation's clean. If we're going in, it's now or it's never."

"Now," Felix said, because *never* was a word his bank account couldn't afford.

The Kestrel leaned into the swell like a dog pulling a leash. Forty-one feet of stubborn steel and fiberglass, twenty years old, held together by Tomas's welding and Felix's refusal to admit what she cost to keep alive. Ahead of them the Gulf of Tonkin had turned the color of wet slate, and inside that slate something was turning over.

He loved this part. That was the honest truth nobody said out loud: he loved the moment before the moment, when the sky was still deciding. Everything after was just work. This — this was the only hour of his life that didn't come with an invoice attached.

"Tomas, give me everything she's got."

"She's got most of it," Tomas called up from below, and then, because he was Tomas: "Most is a generous word, patrón."

Felix grinned into the spray. Behind him, Dario was already rigging the instrument mast, and Marisol had the cameras up, and for about ninety seconds everything on board was exactly what it was supposed to be.

Then the cell did what cells do when you've read them wrong, which is become a different animal entirely.

* * *

It went wrong the way things go wrong at sea — fast, then slow, then fast again. The rotation collapsed instead of tightening, dumping its energy sideways into a squall line that hadn't been on any model Priya trusted. The wind came at them out of the northeast at a speed Felix's anemometer refused to display honestly, and the Kestrel took a wave over the bow that put two inches of green water down the companionway.

"Instrument mast!" Dario shouted, and Felix heard the crack of aluminum even through the roar, saw the whole rig whip back like a snapped fishing rod and hang there, trailing cable, dragging their data — their *product*, the thing they'd sell to the insurance consortium to make this month's numbers — into the sea.

"Cut it loose!"

Dario cut it loose. That was the right call and they both knew it and it still felt like cutting off a finger to save the hand.

They ran before it for six hours. Not chasing anymore — being chased, which was a different profession entirely and one Felix had no talent for. Priya threaded them south along a depth contour she swore existed on no chart but lived somewhere behind her eyes, and Tomas kept the engine turning with the tenderness of a man keeping a dying animal comfortable, and by the time the sky went from black to bruise-gray to a pale, embarrassed morning, the Kestrel was limping toward Haiphong with a cracked spreader, a drowned electronics bay, and nothing sellable.

Felix stood at the wheel with salt crusted white in his eyebrows and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding for three days.

Fuel: borrowed against next month. The camera housing Marisol had saved: cracked. The mast: gone. And the charter payment from the consortium — contingent on delivery of usable data — now contingent on nothing at all.

"Patrón." Tomas appeared beside him with two cups of coffee that tasted like the inside of a thermos that had seen things. "You have the face."

"What face?"

"The one where you are counting money you don't have." Tomas handed him a cup. "Drink it. Counting is worse sober."

Felix drank it. It was worse either way.

"How bad?" Tomas asked.

"You know how bad."

"I know how bad *I* think it is. I want to hear you say it, so I know if we are lying to each other with the same number."

Felix looked out at the water, flat and innocent now, like it hadn't tried to murder anyone all night. "Eleven thousand short," he said. "For the repairs alone. Before fuel. Before the crew."

Tomas nodded slowly, the way men nod at funerals. "And the Meridian people?"

"Are going to be very interested in my schedule."

* * *

Haiphong harbor received them the way it received everyone: without ceremony and with a fee. Tugs, lines, customs, the harbor master's palm-out smile. Felix paid what he could pay and smiled back with the specific brightness of a man whose smile was also a loan.

By noon the Kestrel was tied up at a berth he'd negotiated down to a rate he'd be ashamed to say aloud, and the crew had scattered to do the small mercies — Marisol drying the cameras with rice, Dario hauling the broken mast section to a welder who'd take payment in installments and insults, Priya locked in the cabin with her charts and her silence.

Felix walked the dock toward town because walking was free, and because he needed to see the message before it found him.

It found him anyway. They always did.

There were two of them, seated at the plastic table outside the dockside pho place like they'd been assigned the spot by a regional manager. One was large in the way of men who were paid to be large — shaved head, linen shirt too nice for the neighborhood, hands folded with executive patience. The other was thin and neat and writing in a small notebook, which was somehow worse. The big ones were theater. The notebook was the actual job.

"Eriksen." The big one smiled. His teeth were excellent. Everything about him was excellent, in the way of things maintained on other people's money. "Nguyen Van Duc. We spoke on the phone. This is my colleague, Mr. Bao."

"We spoke on the phone twice," Felix said. "Both times you told me Meridian was flexible."

"And we meant it. We're still meaning it." Duc gestured at the opposite chair. "Sit. Have some soup. You look like a man who's been swimming."

Felix sat, because sitting was free too, and because refusing would cost more. A bowl arrived he hadn't ordered. He wondered what account that would post to.

"Here's where we are," Duc said, pleasant as weather. "Principal, interest, penalties, and the servicing fees on the vessel loan — you're at forty-three thousand, seven hundred US equivalent. As of Friday. After Friday, the penalty structure changes."

"It changes every month."

"It improves every month. For us." Bao wrote something. Felix hated the sound of the pen. "We understand the charter fell through," Duc continued. "Storms are unpredictable. Meridian is sympathetic. Sympathetic, but not infinite. So we've prepared options."

He laid a folder on the table, square and heavy, the paper stock of institutions. Felix didn't touch it.

"Option one, you sign the vessel over. The Kestrel retires your debt and leaves you walking-around money. Option two, we extend terms another quarter, at the improved rate, secured against your personal guarantees — which, Mr. Eriksen, given your late father's estate, we understand to include certain intellectual property holdings."

Something cold moved down Felix's spine, slow as ballast.

"My father's patents aren't collateral. They're not anything. They're locked in probate, half of them voided, the rest worthless since the survey company folded."

"Worthless is such a strong word." Duc's smile widened by a professional millimeter. "Meridian employs appraisers. Some of those old hydrographic surveys — the Sunda work especially — have appreciated. There are parties who find that era of documentation interesting."

"The Sunda work is burned. My father burned it."

"So the story goes." Duc closed the folder and tapped it once, gently, like knocking on a door he already owned. "Stories burn. Records don't. Think about the options. Mr. Bao will be in port through the week."

They stood. Duc shook Felix's hand with a grip calibrated to remind rather than injure. Then they walked away down the dock, unhurried, men with no weather to chase, and Felix sat alone with a bowl of soup he hadn't ordered and couldn't taste.

* * *

That night he climbed to the Kestrel's deck and sat against the wheelhouse with a beer he'd charged to a card with four hundred dollars of room left on it, and he let himself do the thing he never did in daylight: the full accounting.

Not money. He could always see money. Money was just weather — it came, it went, you read the sky and adjusted sails. What he counted now was smaller and heavier. The look on Marisol's face when he told her the camera housing wasn't covered. The way Dario had asked, casually, too casually, whether there'd be a charter next month. The way Priya hadn't asked anything at all, which from Priya was the loudest question there was.

Five people had signed onto this boat because he'd promised them a certain kind of life. Free, was the word he'd used. *Nobody owns our mornings.* He'd believed it when he said it. He was starting to suspect he'd been describing a product he didn't actually have in stock.

Forty-three seven. By Friday the number would grow legs. And underneath it, coiled and patient, the thing Duc had dangled without naming: the Sunda surveys. His father's work. The reason his mother was at the bottom of a sea eleven time zones away, and the reason his father had spent the last ten years of his life drinking coffee in silence on a porch in Da Nang, staring at horizons like they owed him an apology.

*There are parties who find that era of documentation interesting.*

His father had sworn — on the beach, with smoke still coming off the burning charts — that no Eriksen would ever go back for it. *Some inheritances are curses wearing a suit,* he'd said, the last coherent sentence of his last lucid week. *Promise me you'll stay poor, boy. Poor and free beats rich and owned.*

Felix had promised. He'd been nineteen and grieving and it had been the easiest promise of his life, because at nineteen the debts were all emotional and those seemed payable.

Now the interest had compounded on that too.

He drained the beer and looked down the dark dock toward the lights of town, where a man named Duc was sleeping soundly in a hotel room Meridian was paying for, holding a folder full of Felix's future in three tidy options.

There had to be a fourth option. There was always a fourth option; that was the whole religion of people like him. You just hadn't met it yet.

As if the harbor itself had heard him thinking, a long black yacht slid past the breakwater — too sleek for these waters, running lights low and elegant, moving like it had never once been surprised by the sea. Felix watched it turn toward the marina's private berths, and on its stern, in gold leaf catching the dock lamps, a name he knew from racing circuits and rumor and one very bad summer in Subic Bay:

*Venganza.*

Ines Varga was in Haiphong.

Felix sat very still in the dark, and felt the first cold edge of something that wasn't quite dread and wasn't quite hope, and understood that whatever the fourth option was, it had just docked.
