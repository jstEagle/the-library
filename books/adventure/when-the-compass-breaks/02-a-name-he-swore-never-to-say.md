## 2. A Name He Swore Never to Say

The Salt Line Bar sat close enough to the water that at high tide you could lean over the rail and spit into the harbor, and most of the regulars did, as a kind of punctuation. Felix Eriksen had been doing it for twenty years. Spit, wipe his mouth, order another. The ritual of a man who had nothing better to measure time with.

He took the corner table tonight because it was the only one with a working lamp, and because from there he could see the door without seeming to watch it. Old habit. Storm chasers learned to read rooms the way they read skies — pressure dropping in the set of a stranger's shoulders, the green light before something ugly came ashore.

The collectors were still out there somewhere. That was the thing about the Meridian Syndicate: they didn't loom. They waited. A man could almost convince himself the day had gone fine, that the phone call about "restructured terms" was just paperwork, right up until the moment the paperwork grew hands.

Felix ordered rum he couldn't afford and told himself it was research. Weather research. The bar's television was muted, showing a satellite loop of the South China Sea, and there was a system spinning up east of Luzon that made his teeth ache in sympathy. Somewhere in his chest, under the debt and the dread, the old hunger stirred — the one that wanted to drive straight at the ugliest thing on the horizon just to feel the wheel fight him. He killed it with a swallow. Hunger cost fuel. Fuel cost money. Money was the one forecast he couldn't outrun.

The door opened at nine-forty, and the room changed temperature.

Not dramatically. Nothing so honest as a cold front. But conversations thinned, the bartender found sudden urgent work polishing glasses that were already clean, and Felix felt the specific prickle at the back of his neck that meant someone expensive had entered a room full of people who weren't.

Ines Varga crossed the Salt Line like she owned the tide schedule.

She hadn't changed much in six years, which annoyed him. Same dark hair cut sharp at the jaw, same way of walking as if the floor had been poured for her benefit. The only difference was the clothes — six years ago she'd dressed like money she hoped to have; now she dressed like money that had stopped needing to prove anything. Linen the color of bone. A watch that probably cost more than the Kestrel, hull and engine and pride included.

She didn't scan the bar for him. She already knew exactly where he was sitting, which meant she'd known before she ever pushed the door open, which meant this whole evening had been charted back at her office with a ruler and a pencil. Felix hated how much that steadied him. At least with Ines you always knew you were being played. It was almost restful.

"Felix Eriksen." She slid into the chair across from him uninvited, set a slim leather folder on the scarred wood between them, and did not touch it again. "You look terrible."

"You look expensive."

"One of us is doing well." She flagged the bartender with two fingers and ordered, of all things, a beer — cheap, local, the kind of gesture calculated to say *I'm not above you* while the watch on her wrist said otherwise. "I'll get to the point, because I know you'll assume there's a knife in whatever I say, and I'd rather you find it quickly and be disappointed."

"Generous of you."

"I'm offering you a job."

"I have a job."

"You have a boat and a habit of pointing it at weather," Ines said. "That's not a job, that's a symptom. I'm offering you work. One salvage run. Triple your best season's take, up front, half before you even cast off. And when it's done—" she turned the folder ninety degrees with one finger, so the clasp faced him, an invitation shaped like a trap, "—your ledger with Meridian goes to zero. Not reduced. Zero. Every cent, every penalty, every interest accrual they've been using to bleed you since the typhoon year. Gone. Signed, witnessed, notarized. I've already spoken to them."

Felix looked at the folder the way he'd look at an unexploded ordnance washed up on a beach.

"You've spoken to them."

"I bought your paper, Felix. All of it. This afternoon, while you were arguing with a fuel dock about a payment plan." She smiled, and it wasn't cruel, exactly. It was worse than cruel. It was patient. "You work for me now, or you work for them. Those are the only two employers left in your life, and only one of them is offering to let you walk away whole."

There it was. The knife, presented handle-first, which somehow made it sharper.

He should have said no immediately. Any sensible man would have. Instead he heard himself ask the question that mattered, the one his gut raised before his brain could stop it:

"What's the wreck?"

Ines leaned back, and for a moment — just a moment — the broker's polish slipped and something older showed through, something that looked almost like grief wearing ambition as a coat. "A survey vessel. Lost in the Sunda Archipelago eleven years ago. The *Halcyon Star*."

The name went through him like a spar through canvas.

He kept his face still. He'd had practice; his father had taught him that much, in the years after, teaching him to hold expression the way other fathers taught sons to hold a wrench. But inside, everything tilted. The *Halcyon Star*. Eleven years. The archipelago. The word *Sunda* alone was enough to taste salt blood.

"That's a long way to sail for a rumor," he said, voice flat as a becalmed sea.

"It's not a rumor. I have partial coordinates, insurance records, a survivor's deposition. There's equipment on that ship worth recovering, and I'm not asking you to love the job. I'm asking you to do what you're good at — go where sane men won't, bring back what everyone else wrote off." She drank her beer like it was water. "Triple pay, Felix. Debt erased. One run. You'd be free."

*Free.* She used the word like bait on a hook, and he resented how well it fit his mouth. Free of Meridian. Free of the collectors who'd stood at the end of his dock that morning with their laminated smiles and their folders full of his failures. Free of waking at 3 a.m. doing arithmetic against the ceiling.

And free to sail into the one stretch of ocean on this earth he had sworn — *sworn*, hand in his father's grip, breath rasping in a hospital room that smelled of iodine and endings — never to enter again.

"Ines." He set down the glass carefully, because if he set it down carelessly he'd throw it. "Why me? You've got money now. Real money. You could hire any salvage captain in Southeast Asia. Men with crews that don't flinch, boats that don't leak, no history. Why come to a dockside bar in a port nobody chooses, to buy the debts of a man who's been failing publicly for three years?"

For a second she didn't answer, and in that second Felix saw her decide whether to lie. He watched her choose the half-truth instead, the way sailors choose the channel marker over the direct line through shoals.

"Because you're the best storm handler I've ever seen," she said. "Because the monsoon window is narrow and closing, and I need someone who can thread it. Because your crew is loyal in ways money can't buy, which matters when a job turns ugly. And because—" the pause again, brief as a squall gust, "—there are things on that wreck that only an Eriksen would recognize."

*Only an Eriksen.*

His father's name surfaced then, unbidden, the way drowned things surface when the water goes still. Magnus Eriksen. Twenty-two years a survey captain, a man who read currents like scripture and trusted no chart he hadn't corrected in his own hand. A man who had come home from the Sunda Archipelago one time, one final time, carrying nothing but a duffel bag, a broken compass, and a silence that lasted the rest of his life.

Felix had been nineteen. He'd sat in that hospital corridor for six hours while the machines argued with each other, and near the end they'd let him in, and his father had grabbed his wrist with a strength that shouldn't have been possible in a man already three-quarters gone, and pulled him close enough to smell the salt that never left his skin.

"You don't go back," Magnus Eriksen had said. Each word costing him something. "Whatever they offer. Whatever they promise. You don't go back to those waters. Promise me, boy. Promise me on your mother."

And Felix, nineteen years old and drowning in a grief too large to think inside, had promised. On his mother. On her name, on her grave, on the empty chair at every table since. *I promise. We don't go back.*

His father had died four hours later holding that promise like a rope, and Felix had spent eleven years keeping it, and it had been easy, at first, because keeping it required nothing but staying poor in familiar waters. It had gotten harder as the debts grew, but it had held. Through the typhoon that ate half his savings. Through the lawsuit. Through the morning the Kestrel's engine seized outside Singapore and he'd sold his mother's rings to pay the tow. The promise had held because promises were the only inheritance his father had left him that couldn't be repossessed.

Until tonight. Until Ines Varga walked into the Salt Line with a folder full of his sins and a wreck name that fit the shape of his father's silence exactly.

"The *Halcyon Star*," Felix said slowly. "Where did you hear that name?"

"Insurance archives. Why?"

"Because my father never said it. Not once, in eleven years. Not drunk, not dying, not dreaming. A man spends his whole career on survey ships and never once mentions the name of the last one?" He leaned forward. "He burned everything, Ines. Charts, logs, photographs. On a beach, in a steel drum, the week after the funeral. I watched him do it. So either your archives are wrong, or you know something about my family that you haven't said."

Something moved behind her eyes — quick, controlled, gone. But Felix had spent his life reading weather, and weather and women like Ines Varga operated on the same principle: what hides is what matters.

"My sources are good," she said finally. "Better than you'd believe. The question isn't where I learned the name, Felix. The question is whether you want your life back badly enough to stop asking questions you're afraid of the answers to."

"And if I say no?"

"Then tomorrow morning I make one phone call, and Meridian buys your paper back from me at a markup, and we all return to our regularly scheduled programming. They take the Kestrel. You chase storms in a rented skiff until your luck runs out, which at current velocity gives you maybe eighteen months." She said it without heat, the way you'd read a forecast. "I'm not threatening you. I'm describing the weather."

Felix laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "God. You always did know how to talk to me."

"I've always paid attention." For a heartbeat her voice softened into something that might have been the past — the two of them at twenty-three, racing charter boats out of the same marina, trading wins and insults and once, memorably, a kiss behind the fuel shed that neither of them had ever mentioned again. Nostalgia rose in him like floodwater, bitter and warm at once, and he hated it, hated her for summoning it, hated himself for feeling it. "You were going to be great, Felix. Before. Everyone said so."

"*Before.*"

"Before the debts. Before the typhoon. Before—" she gestured at him, at all of him, the worn jacket and the tired eyes, "—this."

"Before my father died, you mean. Before he burned our name off the face of the sea and left me a promise instead of a patrimony." Felix stood. His chair scraped, and three heads turned, and he didn't care. "You want to know why I said the name *Halcyon Star* makes me sick? Ask yourself what kind of disaster makes a man burn thirty years of his life's work in a oil drum on a beach. Ask yourself what's buried in those waters that was worth my mother's life and my father's soul. Then ask why you want *me* — specifically me, an Eriksen — to go dig it up."

Ines looked up at him, and for the first time all night she seemed to be measuring him rather than managing him. "Those are excellent questions," she said quietly. "I look forward to the day you trust me enough to hear the answers."

"Don't hold your breath."

"So that's a no."

"That's a no." He picked up the folder — she let him take it, let him feel its weight, let him see the neat typed columns of his own ruin itemized in another woman's handwriting — and dropped it into the empty glass of rum, where the ink would bloom and blur and die. "Tell Meridian whatever you like. Tell them Felix Eriksen said no. Tell them some things aren't for sale."

"They're all for sale," Ines said, standing, smoothing her linen. "Some just haven't met the right price yet. Sleep on it, Felix. The offer expires when the tide does."

She left money on the table — too much, deliberately, another small humiliation — and walked out the way she'd walked in, like the tide schedule obeyed her personally. The door swung shut. The bar exhaled. The satellite loop spun on mute, the storm east of Luzon tightening its spiral, patient as everything else in his life.

Felix sat back down in the lamplight and put his head in his hands, and the promise rose in him again, his father's grip on his wrist, the iodine smell, the words *on your mother* — and underneath it, faint but unmistakable, the other sound. The one he'd been hearing for eleven years and pretending was the wind.

The *Halcyon Star*, calling his family's name across eleven fathoms of green water.

He finished the ruined rum. He did not sleep on it.

At dawn, barefoot and hollow-eyed, Felix walked down to the Kestrel intending to check the bilge and forget the whole night — and found Tomas waiting at the gangway, arms crossed, crew gathered behind him in a silent half-circle, and an expression on the big engineer's face that Felix had seen only twice before, both times right before mutiny.

"We need to talk, Captain," Tomas said. "About money. About food. About a job."

* * *

Behind Felix, down the dock, a fishing skiff idled in the gray light, a woman at the tiller watching the Kestrel's deck with the stillness of someone comparing faces to a photograph.
