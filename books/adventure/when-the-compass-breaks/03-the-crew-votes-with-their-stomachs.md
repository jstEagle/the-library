## 3. The Crew Votes With Their Stomachs

Tomas Reyes had a rule about engines and a rule about people, and both rules were the same: you could tell what was broken by listening to what it wasn't doing.

The Kestrel's main engine wasn't doing a lot of things that morning. It wasn't idling clean. It wasn't holding compression on the third cylinder. It wasn't going to make it through another charter season without parts Tomas could not buy, because there was no money, because there had been no money for eleven weeks, and because Felix Eriksen had spent the last three of those weeks chasing storms in the Gulf of Tonkin for a weather research grant that had, apparently, evaporated along with the storm.

Tomas stood in the engine room with a stethoscope pressed against the block and listened to the sound of a boat dying slowly, and he thought: this is what we sound like too.

He climbed the ladder into the gray dawn light and found the crew already on deck. That was the thing about hunger. It got everyone up early. Nobody slept well on an empty stomach, and nobody slept at all on an empty stomach in a hot bunkroom that smelled of diesel and salt and the ghost of the last real meal, which had been Priya's birthday, six weeks ago, when they'd pooled their last cash and bought a crate of mangoes and a chicken that Priya had named before they killed it, which had been a mistake nobody repeated.

Priya was at the chart table, not looking at charts. She was looking at a ledger — the crew's shared ledger, the one they kept in a plastic bag nailed inside the galley cabinet, because paper records of debt made Felix twitchy. Amara wasn't aboard yet; the woman from the skiff wasn't crew, not yet, though Tomas had noticed her at the dock three mornings running and hadn't mentioned it to anyone, the way you don't mention a sound your engine is making until you know what it means.

And Ravi, the deckhand, was sitting on the winch housing with his elbows on his knees, doing the thing he did when he was about to say something everyone had already thought.

"So," Ravi said.

"Don't," said Priya.

"Somebody's got to."

"He'll come around."

"He'll come around when the bank comes around," Ravi said. "You seen the letters. They come on blue paper now. Blue paper means lawyers."

Tomas wiped his hands on a rag that was more rag than hands at this point and leaned against the rail. He was the biggest man aboard and the slowest to speak, which the crew had learned early was not the same as slowest to decide.

"The engine needs a fuel pump," Tomas said.

Everyone looked at him.

"And the third cylinder's scoring. And the raw water impeller's half gone. And the hydraulic line on the A-frame weeps if you look at it wrong." He ticked them off on thick fingers. "I can keep her running on prayer for maybe two more months. After that she's a barge with opinions."

"How much?" Priya asked.

"For everything? Parts, real parts, not salvage-yard parts?" He named a number. It was not a large number, in the scheme of oceans. In the scheme of the Kestrel's bank account, it was the moon.

Priya looked back down at the ledger. Nobody said anything for a while. The harbor did its morning noises around them — gulls, a ferry horn, the slap of water against pilings, somewhere down the dock the fish market waking up with its smell of ice and blood and money changing hands, which was more money than the Kestrel had seen in a season.

"The research grant," Ravi started.

"Is gone," Priya said. "The institute lost its funding, the funding lost its donor, the donor lost interest. I emailed them twice. Second email bounced."

"Charters, then. We could—"

"Nobody charters a boat with a rust streak down the hull and a captain who argues with the weather bureau for sport." Priya closed the ledger. "I ran the numbers last night. If we take no work, we are done in five weeks. If we take bad work, we are done in seven. Either way we are done before the monsoon."

"Then we take the other work," Ravi said.

And there it was. The thing they'd all been circling for three days like gulls over a fish head.

Tomas looked down the dock, toward the marina at the far end where the yachts lived, white and fat and smug behind their security gates. Somewhere down there was a yacht called the *Corvina*, and aboard the *Corvina* was a woman named Ines Varga, and aboard Ines Varga was an offer that had been sitting in Felix's pocket for two days now like a fishhook nobody had managed to spit out.

Triple pay. Debt erased. One salvage run.

Tomas didn't know all of it. He knew the shape of it. He knew that when Felix had come back from the bar two nights ago he'd had the look of a man who'd been offered exactly what he wanted by exactly the wrong person, and that he'd spent the next day being twice as loud and three times as busy, which was how Felix said *no* when part of him meant *maybe*.

"He said no," Priya said, as if reading Tomas's mind. "He told me himself. He looked me in the eye and said we're not taking Varga money."

"He told *you*," Ravi said. "He didn't tell us. There's six of us on this boat. Six. And one man decides we starve because of something that happened before half of us even signed on?"

"You don't know what it is he's—"

"I know what hunger is," Ravi said, and his voice cracked, which shut everyone up, because Ravi was twenty-three and never cracked. "My sister's got a kid in Cebu. I send money home every month. You know what I sent last month? A letter saying sorry. You know what I sent this month? Nothing, because there's nothing. I didn't sign on to this boat to write apology letters."

The gulls argued over something in the water. The ferry horn sounded again, farther away.

Tomas let the silence sit, because silences were like engines — you learned more from them than from noise. Then he said, "Vote."

Priya's head came up. "Tomas."

"It's how we decide things. Always has been. Captain gets the wheel, crew gets the vote. That was the deal when we founded this outfit, drunk, in this exact spot, four years ago." He held up his hand. "I say we take the meeting. Not the job. The *meeting*. We hear the terms. All of us. Then we decide."

"I'm with Tomas," Ravi said immediately, hand up.

Priya looked at the ledger. At the engine room hatch, where the smell of hot metal drifted up like a confession. At the dock, where the blue-paper letters sat in a drawer in the cabin Felix hadn't opened in a week.

"You understand what you're doing," she said quietly. "If we go around him on this, even once, even for a meeting — something breaks. And you don't patch a thing like that with epoxy."

"I patch everything with epoxy," Tomas said. "Watch me."

* * *

They told Felix at dawn because telling him at night meant he'd have all dark to brood and all morning to be dramatic about it.

Tomas delivered it, because he was the biggest and because Felix had never once managed to stay angry at him for longer than an hour, a fact Tomas was quietly proud of and had never once abused. Until now, maybe.

He stood at the gangway with his arms crossed and the crew behind him in a half-circle, and he watched Felix come down the dock barefoot and hollow-eyed, and he thought: *here is a man carrying a weight he won't set down even to swim.*

"We need to talk, Captain," Tomas said. "About money. About food. About a job."

Felix stopped three meters out. Tomas watched him read the formation — crew assembled, faces closed, Ravi sitting with his arms crossed like a smaller, anglier copy of Tomas — and watched him arrive at the correct conclusion, and watched him hate it.

"This is a mutiny," Felix said. "This is an actual mutiny. On my own boat. While I'm standing on the dock."

"It's a vote," Tomas said. "Mutiny's when we don't tell you. We're telling you."

"The job's off. I told Priya—"

"You told Priya. Priya's one vote. You're one vote." Tomas unfolded his arms. "That was the deal, Felix. Four years ago. Your wheel, our vote. You don't get to be a king just because the kingdom's broke."

Felix's jaw worked. Tomas knew that jaw. It was the jaw of a man building a speech — about honor, about his father, about the vow, about all the architecture of *no* that Felix had been constructing his whole life out of other people's disasters. Tomas had heard the speeches. They were good speeches. That was the problem with Felix Eriksen: he could make starving sound like a philosophy.

So Tomas did the one thing that had never once failed him in thirty years of engines and crews.

He made it concrete.

"Ravi's sent nothing home in two months," Tomas said. "Priya's down to her last four hundred dollars and a satellite subscription she's paying out of pocket so we don't go blind. I've been eating rice and the fish Ravi poaches off the stern, which is illegal, by the way, so add that to the list of crimes this crew is committing out of loyalty to you. And this morning the third cylinder started scoring, which means in two months the Kestrel is a reef, and reefs don't chase storms." He let it land. "You want to give a speech, give it after. But first you stand here and you look at these people and you tell them their hunger is a philosophy."

The dock was very quiet. Down at the far end, the fish market was waking up. Somewhere behind Tomas, Ravi shifted his weight.

Felix looked at them. Really looked — Tomas watched him do it, watched the bravado drain out of his face the way water drains out of a bilge when you finally find the right valve, and what was left underneath was younger and more scared and a lot more honest.

"I made a promise," Felix said. His voice came out lower than he probably wanted. "To my father. On his deathbed. There are things — there's a reason, Tomas. There's a reason I said no."

"I believe you," Tomas said. "I've known you nine years. You don't lie about your dead. But here's what I know about promises." He held up one thick hand and counted. "A promise made to a dying man is made in a room full of grief, and grief is a liar. A promise that starves six people is a promise with a bill attached, and the bill always comes due. And a promise you won't even explain—" he folded the fingers down, one by one, until the fist was closed, "—is a promise that's already broken something, and you're just trying to keep the break from spreading."

Felix flinched. Actually flinched, like a man touched with a live wire.

"You don't know what it cost him," Felix said. "What it cost *us*. You weren't there."

"No," Tomas agreed. "I wasn't. And you won't tell me. So I get to choose between your secret and my crew's dinner." He shrugged, and the shrug was the whole argument, and they both knew it. "That's not a hard choice, Captain. It's only a sad one."

Felix stood there a long moment. The gulls wheeled. The water slapped the pilings, patient as a creditor.

Then Priya stepped forward, and Tomas could have kissed her, because Priya always knew when to stop pushing and start building.

"We're not asking you to say yes," she said. "We're asking you to walk down to that yacht and hear the terms. All of them, out loud, in front of all of us. You come back, you tell us everything, and then this crew votes — you included. One vote each. If the vote says no, we all starve together with our honor intact, and I will never bring it up again. If it says yes—" she spread her hands, "—then at least we starve *informed*."

Felix laughed. It was a terrible sound, dry and cracked, the laugh of a man who has just realized he's been outmaneuvered by people he loves.

"You've been planning this," he said.

"Since Tuesday," Priya said.

"Since the mangoes," Ravi said. "You went quiet at the mangoes."

Felix looked at Tomas. "And you. The engine — is it really that bad?"

Tomas thought about lying, decided Felix had earned the truth on a day like this, and gave it to him straight: "Two months. Maybe ten weeks if the weather's kind, and the weather is never kind. You taught me that."

Felix looked out at the harbor. At the water, gray and moving. At the far dock, where the white yachts sat behind their gates like a different species. Somewhere down there, a woman was waiting, and Tomas watched Felix understand that she had probably planned this too — that everyone, apparently, had planned everything except the man holding the wheel.

"Fine," Felix said. "A meeting. Terms, out loud, all of us. Then a vote." He held up a finger. "But I'm telling you now, so nobody's surprised: I know what she is. I know what that woman does to people. I've watched her do it for fifteen years. Whatever she's selling, the price tag is always bigger than the sticker."

"Then you'll be our expert," Tomas said. "That's what a captain's for."

Felix looked at him for a long moment, and something passed between them — not forgiveness, not yet, but the thing that comes before it, the acknowledgment that both of them had done what they had to do and would do it again.

"Get the engine running," Felix said finally, turning back up the dock. "If I'm going to sell my soul, I want the boat ready to sail it somewhere."

He walked away up the pier, barefoot on the wet planks, and the crew watched him go in silence.

Ravi exhaled. "Is he going to say yes?"

"He's going to say no," Priya said. "Ines Varga is going to make him say yes anyway. That's what she does. That's what she's *for*."

Tomas said nothing. He was watching the far end of the dock, where a fishing skiff that had been idling in the gray light all morning finally turned and puttered away — but not before the woman at the tiller looked back once at the Kestrel, and at Felix walking up the pier toward the yachts, with an expression Tomas had seen before, on faces in engine rooms, right before something either got fixed or got a lot worse.

He went below to listen to his engine.

It sounded, he thought, exactly like the rest of them: running, barely, on borrowed time and prayer.
