## 4. Terms Written in Salt Water

The yacht was called *Meridian's Daughter*, which should have been my first warning. Nobody names a boat that honestly unless they want you to feel the weight of it before you even step aboard.

She sat at the far end of the harbor where the charter slips turned into money slips, white as a pulled tooth, all clean lines and radar domes and a tender worth more than the Kestrel's hull, engine, and my remaining dignity combined. A crewman in a polo shirt — a polo shirt, on a boat, in this heat — met me at the gangway and looked at my salt-stained jacket the way you'd look at a seagull that had wandered into a wedding.

"Felix Eriksen," I said. "Your boss invited me."

"She's expecting you. Aft deck."

I'd dressed for the meeting, which for me meant I'd rinsed the worst of the Gulf out of my clothes and left the jacket that still smelled faintly of burnt wiring. The Kestrel was moored two hundred meters away, and I could see Tomas on her deck pretending not to watch me walk here. Pretending badly. The whole crew knew where I was going. The whole crew had opinions. I'd told them I was going to tell Ines Varga exactly where she could put her salvage job, and they'd all nodded with the sincerity of people who had already made up their minds otherwise.

The aft deck was teak and shade and a low table with two glasses and a bottle of something the color of old gold. Ines Varga stood at the rail with her back to the harbor, which was a power move, because it meant she'd rather look at me than at the view she owned.

She hadn't changed much. Ten years will put lines on anyone, but hers were the kind that come from squinting into weather, not from crying. Same black hair cut short and practical. Same way of standing like the deck was a courtroom and she already had the verdict in her pocket.

"Eriksen." She said it like she was tasting the name to check if it had gone off.

"Varga."

"You look terrible."

"You look expensive. I guess we've both been busy."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more the memory of one. She gestured at the chair across from her. I stayed standing. She raised an eyebrow, poured two glasses anyway, and sat down herself with the unhurried ease of a woman who knew exactly how long I could afford to keep up this posture.

"Sit down, Felix. You're not here to fight. If you wanted to fight you'd have brought Tomas, and Tomas would already be over the rail."

I sat. The chair was the kind of comfortable that makes you angry.

"Let's skip the part where we exchange insults for twenty minutes," she said. "I know you're broke. You know I know. I know you hate me. You know I don't care. Can we talk about the job?"

"What job? You haven't told me a thing. You sent a message through a bartender like we're arranging an affair."

"I sent it through a bartender because I knew you'd tear up a letter and I wanted to watch your face when you heard the name."

"What name? You haven't said anything yet."

"Halcyon Star," Ines said, and watched me.

I kept my face still. I'd had practice. There's a specific kind of stillness you learn when someone says a word that has a body attached to it, a body at the bottom of the sea, and you decide — right there, in the half-second before you answer — whether you're going to let them see it hit.

"Survey vessel," I said. "Lost in the Sunda Archipelago. Ninety-eight. That's public record, Ines. There have been three salvage attempts. All failed. The reef there eats equipment for breakfast."

"Four attempts. The fourth wasn't public." She turned her glass on the table without drinking. "And the reef didn't eat them. The reef just did what reefs do. What killed those attempts was bad charts, bad timing, and bad luck. I'm offering you good charts, good timing, and you."

"You're offering me debt forgiveness and triple rate to dive a wreck in monsoon country. Either you're desperate, or this is a trap, and Ines, I have known you since we were nineteen and you have never once been desperate."

"No," she agreed. "I haven't. But I've been patient, and patience and desperation look the same from the outside."

She slid a folder across the table. Real paper, which in her world was practically a love letter. I opened it. Inside: a vessel spec sheet for a ship I didn't recognize, a survey contract redacted so heavily it looked like a redacted document, and a single page of coordinates — except where coordinates should have been, there was a black bar and a typed line: *Provided upon departure.*

"You want me to sign on to a job where I don't even get the destination until we're underway."

"Yes."

"Then no. That's not a job, that's a kidnapping with better catering."

"Felix." She leaned forward, and for a moment the yacht, the money, the polo-shirted crew all dropped away and it was just her, the way she'd been at the maritime academy, the girl who could read a chart faster than the instructors and never let anyone forget it. "The monsoon is coming early. Everyone in the region knows it. That gives us a window — a narrow one. I can't brief you on the exact site until the window opens because I can't risk the information leaking. Not to your crew, not to the harbor, and not—" she paused, and chose the next words with visible care, "—not to anyone who might be listening to your radio traffic."

That landed somewhere cold in my stomach. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning the Meridian Syndicate knows your name, Eriksen, and I'd bet my hull they know your route history. If I put coordinates on paper and that paper exists in the same hemisphere as you, they'll have divers on that wreck before you've finished your safety briefing."

"And what's on this wreck that the Syndicate wants so badly?"

Ines sat back. She picked up her glass, finally, and drank, and I realized the whole conversation so far had been her deciding how much to tell me, one measured sip at a time.

"Survey data. Hydrographic charts. Patent documentation for a seabed mapping system that was worth a fortune when the ship went down and is worth considerably more now." She set the glass down. "And personal effects. The Halcyon Star wasn't just a survey ship. It was carrying the assets of a partnership — three families who pooled everything into one venture, one vessel, one season. The ship sank. The assets sank with it. The families were ruined. Two of the three are gone now."

"And the third?"

"Is sitting in front of you."

I looked at her. She looked back, and there it was — the thing under the polish, the thing she'd sailed all the way to this harbor to show me. Not greed. Something rawer.

"You had family on the Halcyon Star," I said slowly. "That's not in any file I've ever seen."

"My brother was her hydrographer." Her voice didn't change at all, which was how I knew how much it cost her. "He was twenty-six. They never recovered him. The wreck sits in forty meters of water on a reef that has already taken four salvage crews' money and one crew's lives, and every year I get older and the monsoon gets less predictable, and I have decided that this year I am done waiting for the sea to give him back on its own schedule."

The harbor noise seemed to recede. Somewhere behind me a gull was screaming at another gull about something, and it sounded very far away.

"You want a recovery," I said. "Not a salvage."

"I want both. I want the assets, because the assets are mine by blood and I have spent fifteen years watching other people profit from my family's ruin. And I want my brother, because nobody else is coming for him." She spread her hands. "You're the best wreck diver on this coast and the only one who owes me nothing and hates me enough to tell me the truth instead of what I want to hear. That's why it's you. That's the whole pitch. I could have hired cheaper. I hired honest."

"I don't hate you."

"You've hated me since Singapore."

"I've *disagreed* with you since Singapore. There's a difference."

"You cut my mooring line."

"Your anchor was fouled on my net. There was a *procedure*."

"And you followed it with such pleasure that the harbor master still talks about it." She almost smiled again, and this time I almost let myself. Ten years fell away for about a second and a half, the two of us trading cuts across a chart table while the instructors pretended not to be impressed, and then the second and a half ended and I remembered my father's hands, the way they'd shaken burning the charts on the beach, and the vow, the vow, the vow.

"I can't do it," I said. "You know I can't. You know why I can't."

"I know your father had a history with those waters. Everyone in the trade knows that. The great Anders Eriksen, who surveyed half of Southeast Asia and then, overnight, without explanation, refused to ever sail east of Vietnam again. Burned his own charts, they say. Threw away a career." She tilted her head. "Nobody ever knew why. I've wondered for fifteen years, Felix. And I've noticed something you may not have noticed, which is that the Halcyon Star went down in the exact waters your father swore off, in the exact season your mother died, and your father — who could have named his price consulting for any of the four salvage attempts — never once came near it. Not to advise. Not to observe. Not even to sell his silence."

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying I don't know. I'm saying there's a hole in the story of Anders Eriksen shaped exactly like the Halcyon Star, and I'm saying you're going to help me fill it whether you like it or not, because—" and here she did the thing I should have seen coming, the thing she'd probably been building toward since the bartender, "—I've already bought your debt."

The gull stopped screaming. Or maybe I just stopped hearing it.

"You what?"

"Meridian holds your paper. All of it. The chase boat, the equipment lease, the personal loans you were stupid enough to sign at sea, which, by the way, never do that. They bundled it. I bought the bundle through a holding company three weeks ago." She said it the way she'd say the wind was from the northeast. A fact about the world. "Which means, as of three weeks ago, Felix, you don't owe the Syndicate. You owe me. And I'm prepared to forgive all of it — every cent — on completion of one job."

The deck seemed to tilt, very slightly, though the water in the harbor was flat as bathwater. I thought about the collectors at the dock, the way the big one had smiled, the way they'd known — they'd *known* — exactly how much rope I had left. They'd known because the rope had changed hands. They'd been leaning on a man whose debt they didn't even own anymore, squeezing him on someone else's behalf, and I had walked into this meeting already owned and hadn't known it.

"You let them threaten me," I said. "Last night. At the dock. You let them lean on me so I'd walk in here desperate."

"I let them be themselves. I didn't have to arrange anything." Ines's eyes were steady and unapologetic and, underneath, something almost gentle, which was worse. "Felix, I have watched you drown slowly for three years. You were going to die out there — not dramatically, not in a storm, just quietly, chasing weather for people who own you, until the boat or the body gave out. I could have offered you this job like a gentleman and you'd have refused, out of pride, out of that vow, out of whatever ghost your father left you, and you'd have gone back to the Gulf and died on schedule. So I didn't offer. I arranged. I'm not sorry."

"You should be."

"I'm not."

I stood up. The chair was so comfortable it felt like a trap releasing me. I walked to the rail and looked at the harbor, at the Kestrel sitting low and tired in the water, at the life I'd built out of other people's money, and I tried to find the shape of the vow in all of it — *never again, Felix, you swear it on her memory* — and the vow was there, it was always there, but it had gone strangely quiet, the way a radio goes quiet when the battery finally dies.

"You could have just asked," I said.

"I did ask. Three years ago, at Singapore, at the regatta. I asked if you'd ever consider eastern waters again and you looked at me like I'd spat on your mother's grave." She came and stood at the rail beside me, an arm's length away, not touching. "So no. This time I didn't ask. This time I built a door and put you in a room with only one exit, and the exit is the Sunda Archipelago, and I won't pretend that's kind, but it's honest, and I think you're the one person left who prefers honest to kind."

We stood there while the harbor worked around us. A fuel barge. A fishing boat coming in with the day's catch, its crew waving at someone on the quay. Ordinary life, going on, for people who weren't owned.

"Terms," I said finally, because there was no other direction left to walk. "If I do this, it's on my terms. One: my crew comes with me, my crew stays mine, and you don't countermand an order on my deck, not for triple rate, not for anything. Two: the coordinates come to my navigator the moment we clear the harbor, not at the site, because I don't sail blind and I won't have my people think they've been kidnapped. Three: any recovery of remains — your brother, anyone — they come home before a single asset comes up. Before. You can take it or find yourself another honest man."

Ines was quiet for a long moment. I watched her weigh it, and I watched something in her face shift — surprise, maybe, that I'd led with the dead instead of the money, or maybe recognition, like she'd been hoping I'd say exactly that and hadn't let herself count on it.

"Accepted," she said. "All three. And my terms, in return: you sign on for the full window. If the weather closes in mid-operation, we don't run for port at the first squall — we hold and finish, or we hold and die trying. I've watched four crews turn back with their equipment still working and their nerve gone, and I will not be the fifth. You sail this job to its end, Felix. Whatever its end is."

"That's not a term, that's a suicide pact."

"That's the job." She turned to face me fully. "The monsoon is early this year. Every forecast model says so. We have maybe three weeks of workable sea state, and then those waters close for months. If we turn back early, we lose the window, and I don't get another window, and neither do you, because by next season the Syndicate will have rebuilt whatever leverage you think you've escaped. This is the season, Felix. This boat, this crew, this month. Or never."

I looked at her hand, extended across the space between us, and I thought about my father's hands on the beach, feeding charts to the fire, and how the last thing he'd ever said to me about the sea east of Vietnam was *some water keeps what it takes, and some water keeps what you give it, and you'll never know which is which until you're in it.*

"Salt water," I said. "That's the only contract out here that ever held anything. You know that, right? Paper burns. Ink runs. But you shake on it over open water, and the sea remembers."

"The sea remembers," Ines agreed. "That's rather the point."

I took her hand. Her grip was dry and strong and it did not let go a moment before mine did, and somewhere under the yacht's keel, in forty meters of green dark on a reef four hundred miles east, I had the sudden, certain, unwelcome feeling that the sea had just written us both down in its book.

"One more thing," I said, pulling my hand back. "The fourth salvage attempt. The one that wasn't public. What happened to it?"

Ines looked at me for a long time, and I watched her decide — again, one measured sip at a time — how much truth this contract could hold.

"Two of the four divers came back," she said. "The other two are still down there. And the two who came back won't say what they saw, except one of them told me, drunk, one night, that the wreck isn't sitting the way a sinking ship sits." She turned back to the rail, to the east, to the water beyond the harbor mouth where the horizon was already building the first pale towers of a season that wasn't due for six weeks. "He said someone moved things around down there. Deliberately. Before the ship ever settled."

I felt the deck tilt again, or I did.

"Anders Eriksen surveyed those reefs," Ines said quietly. "Better than anyone alive. And then he burned his charts. And I want to know, Felix — before we're both committed — whether your father knew exactly what he was doing when he swore you all away from that water."

Behind us, on the aft deck, the bottle of old gold sat open between two glasses, one full, one untouched, and out past the harbor the first edge of the early monsoon was coming up the horizon like something that had been waiting a long time for its appointment.

* * *

I walked back to the Kestrel through the gold light of late afternoon, and the crew was waiting on deck, all five of them, arranged with the studied casualness of people who had been watching the yacht through binoculars for two hours.

"Well?" Tomas said.

I stood at the gangway with Ines's folder under my arm and the salt of her handshake still on my palm, and I looked at my crew — hungry, unpaid, loyal in the way that hurts — and I heard myself say the thing I'd sworn on a burning beach I would never say.

"Pack for a long trip. We sail east."

Priya was the first to move, already heading for the chart table, and it was only later — much later — that I'd remember the look on her face as she went. Not relief. Not excitement.

Recognition.
