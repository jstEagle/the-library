## 5. What Papa Buried at Sea

The smoke came first, before Felix understood what it was.

He was nine years old, and he had followed the smell of burning paper down the dune path the way he followed everything his father did — eagerly, unquestioningly, with the whole of his small body. The evening was the color of a bruise, purple at the edges, gold in the middle, and the sea below the beach made that long hushing sound it always made, like someone breathing slowly in another room.

His father stood at the tideline with an armful of charts.

Not just charts. Felix knew them by sight, had grown up with them spread across the kitchen table while he ate his cereal, weighted at the corners with coffee mugs and the salt-shaker and once, memorably, a live crab Tomas's brother had brought as a joke. These were the Sunda sheets, the ones with the hand-annotated soundings in his mother's looping script, depth figures written in pencil so fine they looked like spider silk. His mother had drawn those numbers. His mother, who was eleven days dead.

"Papa?"

His father didn't turn. He fed another chart to the small fire he'd built between two pieces of driftwood, and the flames took it greedily, and for a moment Felix could see through the burning paper — could see the reef outlined in blue ink, the channel markers, the little anchor symbol his mother always used for safe harbors — and then it curled and went black and was gone.

"You shouldn't be here, Lille." His father's voice was rough, like a rope dragged over coral. He hadn't called him Lille since the funeral.

Felix came down the sand anyway. That was the thing about being nine: you didn't know yet that some doors were meant to stay shut, so you walked through all of them.

"What are you doing?"

"Burning lies." His father crouched and pulled another sheet from the oilskin satchel at his feet. This one Felix recognized even upside down — the big survey chart of the archipelago, the one that hung above his parents' bed, the one his mother had kissed her finger and touched every morning before they sailed, some old sailor's superstition she'd learned from her own father. "That's what they are. Pretty lines on paper. Safe passages. And people believe them, and they sail their families into—" His voice caught, snapped like a halyard parting under load. He didn't finish.

He threw the chart on the fire.

Felix watched it burn. He watched the reef where his mother died curl up in flame, watched the blue ink bubble and go brown, watched the safe-harbor anchors disappear one by one. It should have felt good, maybe. Justice, or something. But instead it felt like watching someone burn a photograph of her face, and his chest did something complicated and terrible, and he heard himself say:

"Those are Mama's."

His father went very still.

For a long moment the only sounds were the fire eating paper and the sea doing its slow breathing. Then his father turned, and Felix saw his face properly for the first time since the funeral — really saw it — and what he saw frightened him more than any storm ever had. Not sadness. Sadness he knew; sadness had been sitting in the house for eleven days like an uninvited guest who wouldn't leave. This was something else. This was a man standing on the far edge of a decision.

"They're lines on paper," his father said quietly. "She was flesh. She was laughter. She was the only person on that boat who knew exactly where we were, and the chart lied anyway, and the sea took her, and I am never going back."

"Back where?"

"The archipelago. Any of it. None of it." He said each word like he was driving a nail. "You hear me, Felix? Never. Not for money. Not for memory. Not if they put the patents in your hand and begged. There is nothing out there but water over her, and water doesn't give anything back."

* * *

They had come to this coast to wait, after. That was the story the adults told each other. In truth his father had pointed the Kestrel's old diesel engine south and simply gone until the fuel gauge made him stop, and they'd rented a house on stilts above a gray beach, and there they sat, the two of them, like debris the tide had left behind.

The house was full of his mother and empty of her at the same time. Her rain jacket still hung by the door. Her reading glasses were still folded on the sill above the sink, waiting for her to come back and squint at something small. Every night Felix slept in her side of the bed because his father had stopped sleeping entirely, and every morning he woke to the sound of his father in the kitchen, not making breakfast, just standing there, holding a coffee cup that never got drunk.

The lawyers had come twice. Felix remembered them as men with soft hands and hard voices, men from the insurance company and men from the Syndicate — though at nine he'd only understood the word *Syndicate* as the tone adults used when they said it, which was the tone you used for things in the walls, termites, rot. They wanted to talk about the Halcyon Star. About the survey contracts. About the patents pending in his mother's name, the navigation instruments she'd designed, the charts she'd been compiling — assets, they called her life's work. Assets with a value. Assets that were now, technically, in dispute, because the vessel was lost and the documentation was lost and the woman was lost, and lost things belonged to whoever was willing to dig hardest.

His father had shown them off the boat with a shotgun across his knees and no shells in it, and both facts had been perfectly clear to everyone present.

Now, on the beach, the fire was getting low, and the satchel was still half full.

Felix knelt beside it without asking. He picked up a chart — a small one, a detail sheet of a passage his mother had named the Devil's Throat, because she'd surveyed it three times and laughed about the name every time — and he held it over the flames, and let it go.

His father watched him do it. Something moved through the man's face, too fast to name.

"She'd have hated this," Felix said. It wasn't a question. He'd been thinking it for days, the way children think things they can't say: that his mother loved these waters, that she'd spent ten years drawing them, that burning them was like burning her handwriting, which was the last of her that existed anywhere except inside his own head, already starting to fade.

"Yes," his father said. "She would have hated it." He fed the fire another sheet. "I'm doing it anyway."

"Why?"

"Because as long as these exist, someone will come for them. The Syndicate. The insurers. Men who think a patent is a treasure map." He looked at Felix then, and his eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely level. "And because as long as these exist, *I'll* want to come back. And I can't want that, Lille. Wanting it would kill me faster than the sea did. So we burn the wanting out of ourselves. Both of us. You understand? We start now, while you're young enough for it to take."

"I don't want to forget her."

"You won't. That's not what this is." His father reached over and gripped the back of Felix's neck, firm, the way he did when a swell knocked the boat sideways and he needed his son to know he was there. "This is the opposite. We keep everything about her. Everything except the map. Because the map is the thing that killed her, and I will not leave my son a map to his own drowning."

* * *

The last chart was the master sheet.

His father held it a long time. The light had gone fully now; the fire was the only sun on the beach, throwing their shadows huge and wavering up the dune face. Out past the breakers, the first stars were coming out over water that had taken his mother and given nothing back, and Felix thought — with the strange, cold clarity children sometimes get — that the sea didn't even know. That was the worst part. Eleven days, and the sea hadn't noticed.

"This one," his father said, "she finished the night before we sailed." His thumb traced the margin, where his mother had written in her looping hand: *For Felix, when he's old enough to read water.* "She was going to teach you navigation yourself. Said school charts were garbage. Said she'd show you how the real ones lie politely and how to catch them at it."

He almost smiled. Then the smile broke apart, and he fed the master sheet to the fire all at once, and the flames roared up, and for a few seconds the whole beach was lit orange and the reef and the channels and the Devil's Throat and every safe harbor she'd marked burned together in one bright bloom, and Felix felt something tear loose in his chest that he would spend the next twenty-five years pretending hadn't.

Then his father kicked sand over the coals, one shovel-kick at a time, methodical, until the fire was dead and buried, and stood there in the dark with his fists at his sides like a man who has just closed a door on a room where someone is still sleeping.

"Say it back to me," he said.

Felix said it. "We never go back."

"Not for money."

"We never go back. Not for money."

"Not for memory."

"Not for memory."

"Not if they beg. Not if they pay. Not if they put it in your hands." His father crouched to his level, and in the dark his eyes were just glints, and his hands on Felix's shoulders weighed more than hands should weigh. "One day someone will offer you everything we lost, Lille. A box with our name on it. And you'll be tired, or scared, or hungry, and it will look like a door out. When that day comes, I need you to remember this beach. Remember the smoke. Remember that whatever's in that box, it cost your mother everything, and it will try to cost you the same."

Felix nodded, because nodding was easier than saying that he was nine years old and hungry and already tired, and that the smoke smelled like his mother's handwriting, and that some traitorous part of him had memorized the shape of the reef as it burned, the way you memorize a face you know you're losing.

His father took the compass from his pocket then — the brass one, his mother's gift to him, the one with the hairline crack across the crystal that she'd always said gave it character — and he held it over the buried fire for a moment, arm cocked, and Felix thought he was going to throw it into the sea.

He didn't. He couldn't. Felix watched the man's arm tremble with the effort of not throwing it, and then lower, and then his father pressed the compass into Felix's hands and closed the boy's fingers around it.

"Then keep this," he said. "It's broken anyway. Look — needle wanders. Your mother thought it was funny. Said a compass that can't decide was honest, at least." His voice cracked clean through on the word *honest*, and he turned away fast, and walked up the beach toward the house on stilts, and did not look back.

Felix stayed on the sand a long time, holding the broken compass in both hands, feeling its needle swing and settle and swing again, hunting for a north that kept moving.

Behind him, under the sand, the ashes of his mother's charts cooled slowly, going down into the beach with the tide's slow breath, becoming part of the coastline itself. Somewhere out past the breakers, the Sunda Archipelago lay dark under the beginning of the monsoon, keeping what it kept.

He was nine years old, and he made the vow with his whole body, the way children do — completely, sincerely, with no idea at all that vows made in grief are the easiest kind to break, because grief doesn't die, it just waits.

Twenty-five years later, a woman with a yacht and an old grudge would slide a photograph across a bar table, and the photograph would be of a wreck called the Halcyon Star, and Felix Eriksen would feel the smoke in his throat again before he even knew why.

But that night, there was only the beach, and the buried fire, and the boy with the broken compass, learning the first lesson his father ever taught him about inheritance:

that some things are passed down whether you agree to carry them or not.
