## 6. Departure at Slack Tide

The tide turned at 5:47 in the morning, and Amara had been awake for all of it.

She'd learned to read slack water the way other people learned to read faces — the sea going briefly still between its moods, holding its breath, deciding which way to pull. Her grandmother used to say nothing true could be done at slack tide; you could only wait, and watch, and let the water tell you what it wanted. Amara had spent eleven years doing exactly that. Waiting. Watching. Letting the water keep its secrets a little longer.

Now she sat in a fishing skiff she'd borrowed — *borrowed* was generous; the old man who owned it had been paid enough not to ask questions for three days — and watched the harbor of Tanjung Selat wake up around her. Diesel smoke. Gulls arguing over fish guts. A woman on the pier hosing down crates of ice, her rubber boots black with meltwater. The kind of ordinary morning that happened everywhere in the world, indifferent to the fact that today, finally, after eleven years and four false starts and one arrest she did not like to think about, the boat was leaving.

The *Kestrel* sat at berth nine like something that had been dragged out of retirement against its will. Amara had been studying her for two days, and everything about the vessel said *honest poverty*. Rust bleeding down from the anchor hawse in long orange tears. A radar dome bolted on with brackets that hadn't come from any factory. Paint scraped back to gray primer along the starboard rail where something — a fender, a dock piling, a bad night — had chewed the hull. She was a working boat kept alive by stubbornness and baling wire, the kind of ship that either sank quietly or refused to sink at all, and there was no way to know which until you were aboard.

Amara checked her watch. Then, out of habit older than the watch, she checked the sky.

Monsoon clouds were stacking up over the strait to the southwest, fat and blue-gray, building earlier than they should. She'd noticed it three days ago and told herself she was imagining it. She'd stopped telling herself that yesterday. The season was coming early this year, and everyone in this harbor knew it except, apparently, the people about to sail into it.

*They'll figure it out,* she thought. *Or they won't, and I'll be there when they don't.*

She reached into the oilskin bag beside her feet and took out the photograph.

It was soft at the corners now, worn the way only things touched every day get worn. Eleven years of thumbs and salt air and being carried through checkpoints sewn into jacket linings. In the picture, six people stood on the deck of a white survey vessel with *HALCYON STAR* painted across her bow in letters you could read even through the sun flare. Her father stood second from the left, one hand on the rail, squinting into a camera he didn't trust. He never trusted cameras. He said they took your face and gave you back someone else's. Beside him, his arm around a laughing man with a beard gone half gray, stood —

Amara's thumb found the edge of the frame, the place where the paper had gone smoothest.

Elias Eriksen.

She had looked at that face so many times she sometimes dreamed it. Not the photograph — the dream was always moving, always turning toward her, always about to say something. She'd been nine years old the last time she'd seen Elias Eriksen in person, standing on a dock very much like this one, and he'd crouched down to her eye level and given her a shell with a hole drilled through it and said, *For luck, little fish. Your father and I are going to make us all rich.* And then he'd smiled, and then the Halcyon Star had sailed, and then the sea had taken everything, and the shell was still in a box under her bed in a city she no longer lived in, because some promises you keep even when the person who made them turns out to be a liar.

Because that was the thing. That was the thing she had spent eleven years and most of her money and one arrest learning how to say without flinching:

Elias Eriksen had lied to them. All of them. Every family on that manifest.

And his son was about to sail out of berth nine looking for what Elias left behind.

* * *

The first face appeared on deck at ten past six.

Amara lowered the photograph and raised a pair of battered binoculars she'd bought in Surabaya from a man who swore they'd come off a naval patrol boat. They were probably lying. Most things in Surabaya were. But the glass was clear enough, and the light was coming up gold behind the breakwater, and the man who stepped out of the Kestrel's wheelhouse door was big and broad-shouldered and moved like someone who'd spent his whole life compensating for a rolling deck.

Tomas Reyes. Chief engineer. Second name on her list.

He went straight to the engine hatch, threw it open, and disappeared below, and Amara felt something loosen slightly in her chest. Of everyone on the crew, Tomas was the one she'd worried about least and prepared for most. He was the one who'd answer questions honestly if she asked them right. He was also, according to the harbor master's ledger she'd memorized, the one who hadn't been paid in five weeks, which made him either the easiest recruit or the most dangerous variable, depending on how desperate he'd let himself become.

Desperate men signed anything. Desperate men also talked too much. You just had to pick your moment.

The next one up was a woman, small and quick, carrying a rolled chart tube under one arm and a mug in the other hand. Priya Nair, navigator. Amara watched her walk to the bow, lean on the rail, and look out at the harbor entrance for a long time — not sightseeing. Measuring. Amara recognized the posture. It was the way her father used to stand at the rail of their own little boat, reading the water before anyone asked him to. Navigators were the dangerous ones. Navigators noticed when charts lied, and charts were going to lie on this voyage, because Amara had made sure of it — or rather, Ines Varga had made sure of it, and Amara had simply chosen not to correct the record.

That was the arrangement. That was the deal she'd cut in a hotel room in Jakarta with a woman whose handshake felt like signing a contract with a knife.

*You want the wreck,* Varga had said. *I want what's inside it. Neither of us can find it alone. So we do this my way, and we don't lie to each other more than strictly necessary.*

*Strictly necessary,* Amara had repeated, and Varga had almost smiled.

There was a third party on that call, though. There was always a third party. And the third party's interests and Amara's interests overlapped like two circles in a diagram — a lot of shared space, and a hard bright edge where they didn't.

The third party wanted the strongbox opened and emptied. Amara wanted it opened and *read*. There was a difference, and the difference was worth more to her than money, and she was fairly sure that by the end of this she would have to choose which side of that hard bright edge she was standing on.

But that was later. Right now the tide was still slack, and the gulls were still screaming, and the third face came up the Kestrel's gangway carrying two bags and an expression Amara recognized from mirrors.

Felix Eriksen walked aboard his own ship like a man reporting to a prison he'd paid for himself.

* * *

She'd seen him once before, eleven years ago, across a crowded memorial hall in Surabaya, and she'd hated him instantly and completely, and she'd spent years being embarrassed about it, and then she'd stopped being embarrassed, because the hate had turned out to be load-bearing.

He'd been nineteen. Skinny in a cheap suit, standing next to an aunt or a family friend, receiving condolences with a face like a locked door. Everyone in that hall had known the story — the survey vessel lost with all hands in the Sunda waters, the storm that came out of a clear sky, the miracle of the two survivors washed onto Anak Krakatau's flank. Felix Eriksen was the son of the captain, the son of the man whose name was on the charter, and Amara, fourteen years old and furious at God and the ocean and everyone who got to cry in public, had looked across that hall at the boy who inherited the sympathy and thought: *you don't even know what he did.*

She knew better now. Knowing better had cost her everything she had, twice.

Through the binoculars, Felix Eriksen crossed his own deck and stopped at the rail not four meters from where Priya stood, and Amara watched the two of them talk — short exchanges, his hands moving, hers pointing at the harbor mouth. Even at this distance she could see the argument in it. He didn't want to leave. Or he didn't want to leave *today*, or he didn't want to leave on this heading, and the crew had decided otherwise. She'd heard the story by now, secondhand, from a dockhand who liked to talk: the crew had voted with their stomachs, the owner had lost the vote, and here he was anyway, sailing a course he'd sworn on his father's grave never to sail.

Which meant he was already breaking one vow. Which meant vows, for Felix Eriksen, were negotiable under pressure.

Good. So were hers.

He turned, suddenly, and looked out across the harbor — not at anything, the way people look when they're checking whether the world is watching them — and for one unguarded second his face did something that undid a decade of Amara's careful architecture. He looked afraid. Not the fear of a man sailing into weather. The fear of a man sailing into a memory, hauling it up on hooks, planning to open it on a table under good light.

Her thumb pressed the photograph flat against her knee.

*You don't know what he did,* she thought at him, across the water, across eleven years. *But you're about to. And I need to be there when you do, because if you learn it alone, you'll bury it again, and I cannot survive another eleven years of buried.*

* * *

The fourth face arrived at 6:40, and it arrived wrong.

A launch came chugging across the harbor from the direction of the yacht basin, white and gleaming and about forty times too expensive for this part of town, and the dockhands actually stopped working to watch it tie up. A man in a crisp shirt descended, handed a document case to Felix Eriksen, exchanged perhaps thirty seconds of conversation, and departed. And Amara, watching through the glass, felt the morning tilt.

Not Varga. Varga wouldn't send a courier; Varga would come herself, in heels, to be seen. This was someone else. Someone with a document case and a corporate walk and the particular confidence of a man who has never once been punched in the mouth.

Amara memorized his face anyway. Face, build, the way he held his left hand slightly away from his body — holstered, probably, or at least armed. She wrote nothing down. Writing things down was how people got caught, and Amara Ravensworth — no, that wasn't the name on her papers anymore, but it was the name in her head, the name her mother had shouted across beaches, and she kept it the way you keep a language you refuse to stop thinking in — Amara had gotten caught exactly once and had designed the rest of her life around never repeating the experience.

The Syndicate. It had to be. The Meridian Syndicate's fingers were in every port from Da Nang to Darwin, and if they weren't already watching the Kestrel, they were incompetent, and Amara had learned the hard way that they were never incompetent. Which meant somewhere out on this water, probably beyond the breakwater where the fishing boats thinned out, there was already a boat with better engines than hers and worse intentions, and the clock on this whole operation had started ticking before the Kestrel's lines were even cast off.

Fine. Let them watch. Let them follow. Amara had spent eleven years learning to move inside other people's surveillance like a fish through a net — the trick was never to fight the mesh, only to know exactly where the holes were.

The problem, the thing that made her jaw tighten, was that the Syndicate's presence changed the arithmetic for everyone. If the collectors caught the Kestrel at sea, there would be no negotiation, no salvage, no reckoning — just a hold full of frightened sailors and a strongbox that vanished into a vault forever. Everything Amara had traded to get here would be wasted. Everything her father had died not knowing would stay unknown.

So the plan had a second layer. It had always had a second layer. Varga knew about it. Varga had approved it, in the way Varga approved things — with a shrug that transferred all risk to someone else.

*If the job goes clean, you never board,* Varga had said. *If it goes dirty, you're already aboard. Either way, you're positioned.*

*I'm not your insurance policy.*

*No,* Varga had agreed pleasantly. *You're your own. I'm just paying for the privilege of watching you spend yourself.*

Amara had hated her a little, then. She suspected she'd hate her considerably more before this was over. She also suspected — and this was the part she kept in the sealed room at the back of her mind, the room she didn't open — that Varga was not telling her everything either. Nobody in this story was telling anybody everything. That was practically the theme.

* * *

At seven o'clock, the tide began to make, and the Kestrel began to live.

Tomas came up from the engine room wiping his hands on a rag that had started life a different color, and nodded at something Felix said, and the nod had the quality of a truce. Priya unrolled a chart on the fish-cleaning table on the aft deck, weighted the corners with mugs, and the three of them bent over it together, and Amara watched Felix's finger trace the route and stop — she could see it, even at this distance, the finger stopping — somewhere south of where any honest chart would have him stop.

The sealed coordinates. Varga's little envelope of secrets. Amara knew what was written in it, because she'd written half of it herself, and she knew what was *not* written in it, which was the reef, the real one, the one that didn't appear on any chart issued after 1998 because certain parties had paid certain hydrographic offices to make sure it didn't. The reef where the Halcyon Star had actually died. The reef with two names — the printed one, and the one the fishermen's grandmothers used, which translated roughly to *the place that eats*.

Felix Eriksen was going to steer his dying boat directly over his mother's grave, and he didn't know it yet, and Amara was going to be aboard when he found out, and she had rehearsed that moment so many times that it had worn smooth as sea glass, and she still did not know what she would say.

That was the honest truth of it, the thing beneath the plans and the layers and the hard bright edges: she didn't know. She'd imagined confronting him a hundred ways — cold, furious, righteous, merciful — and every version collapsed the moment she pictured his actual face doing its actual afraid thing at the rail. Because grief, she had learned, does not care whose fault it was. Grief just wants company.

Maybe that was why she was really here. Not the letter. Not the claim. Maybe she'd spent eleven years chasing a wreck and what she'd actually been chasing was the one other person alive who missed the same people she missed, even if he didn't know he missed them yet.

*Sentimental,* her mother would have said, and laughed, and meant it kindly.

*Strategic,* Varga would have said, and meant nothing at all.

Both of them could be right. That was the trick of it.

* * *

Lines came off at 7:15.

The Kestrel backed out of berth nine with the grace of a man getting out of a chair with bad knees, swung, and pointed her rusted bow at the harbor mouth. On the aft deck, Priya rolled up her chart. Below, Tomas's engine settled into a rhythm that sounded, to Amara's trained ear, like a complaint being managed rather than cured. And at the helm, visible through the wheelhouse glass, Felix Eriksen stood the way men stand when they've lost every vote available to them and are steering anyway.

Amara started her borrowed engine, low, and fell in behind a line of fishing boats heading for the same gap in the breakwater. One skiff among forty. A woman in a wide hat mending a net, which was even true, because she'd found that her hands needed something to do while her eyes worked, and nets were as good as anything.

As the Kestrel passed abeam, close enough now that Amara could hear the slap of her hull water, could see the rust tears and the patched radar brackets and the scrape of gray primer, Felix Eriksen turned at his wheel and looked out across the fleet — that same unfocused check, the world-is-watching check — and his gaze slid across the fishing boats and past her skiff and moved on.

Amara did not duck. Ducking was for amateurs. She simply became, with the total commitment of eleven years' practice, a fisherman's wife mending a net, and let him look straight through her.

Then she lifted the photograph one last time, held it up beside the receding boat, and matched the faces. Six in the picture, four on the deck, and the arithmetic of the missing two — the dead, and the stowaway — sat in her chest like ballast.

*Soon,* she told the photograph, and put it away, deep in the oilskin bag, against her ribs where it had lived through everything.

Ahead, the Kestrel cleared the breakwater and met the first long swell of the open strait, and rose to it, and fell, and rose. Behind her, far out past the shipping lane where the water went the color of gunmetal, a white shape with too-clean lines was holding position — a boat pretending to fish, with antennas no fishing boat had ever needed.

And above all of it, stacked over the southwestern horizon where no monsoon had any business being yet, the clouds kept building, blue-black and patient, like something that had been waiting eleven years too.

Amara trimmed her little engine, adjusted her heading to fall in a hundred meters astern and one point off the Kestrel's quarter — close enough to watch, far enough to be nobody — and followed the boat out to sea.

By tomorrow night, she'd be aboard. By the day after, the charts would start lying, and the navigator would start noticing, and the whole careful machine would begin to grind toward the reef with two names.

She mended her net, and watched, and waited for the water to tell her what it wanted.
