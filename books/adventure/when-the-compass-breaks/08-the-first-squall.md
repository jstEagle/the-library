## 8. The First Squall

The sky told them three hours before it arrived, and only Priya was listening.

Felix was in the galley at midmorning, doing the arithmetic of a man losing a war slowly — fuel burn against distance, distance against the days the Kestrel could stay afloat on the fuel they had, the whole ledger of the trip balanced on a knife edge of diesel. The numbers didn't work. They never worked. He'd learned a long time ago that the trick was to keep doing the arithmetic anyway, because the moment you stopped was the moment the sea noticed you'd stopped paying attention.

Priya came down the companionway without knocking, which she never did.

"Barometer's falling like it owes someone money," she said. "Two millibars in ninety minutes. That's not a normal cell. There's a squall line on the western horizon running north to south, and it's got a shelf cloud on it I don't like."

Felix looked up from his ledger. "How far?"

"Forty miles. Maybe less. It's moving faster than the forecast said anything should move." She dropped the satellite printout on the table between them. The image showed a long white scar of cloud dragging across the Java Sea like a scratch on glass. "We've got a decision to make. Run north of it, or shorten down and take it."

Felix studied the image. Running north meant burning fuel they didn't have and adding a day they didn't have either, because whatever window Ines had bought them with her sealed coordinates was already narrowing. Taking it meant putting the Kestrel — twenty-two years old, under-insured, and carrying a hull he'd personally patched twice in the last decade — into weather she'd been built to survive and hadn't been maintained to.

He thought about the Syndicate. He thought about the gunboat that might or might not already be shadowing them, and how a day lost was a day closer to default.

"We take it," he said. "Shorten to the storm jib and double-reefed main. Secure everything below. And Tomas gets a look at the bilges before it hits — I want to know how much water she's making in a flat sea, because I want a baseline."

Priya's eyebrows went up a fraction. "Baseline."

"When the water starts coming in faster, I want to know it's the weather and not the boat." He was already moving. "Go."

She went. And Felix climbed the companionway into a light that had already gone strange — greenish, bruised, the sun a white coin behind gauze. The sea was still flat, which was the worst part. Flat seas before a squall are a held breath. Everything about the morning was a held breath.

* * *

The Kestrel's crew was five people and a boat that had outlived three owners, and by the time the shelf cloud was twenty miles out and the wind had backed into the east and begun to moan in the rigging, Felix had learned something about his crew that two days of suspicion had not taught him: they moved well.

He'd expected grumbling. He'd expected the sullen, slow compliance of a crew that had voted behind his back and knew he knew it. What he got instead was Tomas in the engine room with the inspection hatch off, calling up readings in his flat, unhurried voice — "half a liter an hour in the forward bilge, skipper, call it a baseline" — and Priya at the nav station with the radar tuned like a surgeon's instrument, and Dev, the youngest of them, lashing the deck cargo with a speed that suggested he'd done it in worse weather than this and been afraid then too.

They were good. That was the thing that sat in Felix's chest like a stone as he worked the winch handles: they were good, and he had spent two years being too proud and too broke to deserve them, and they had voted to take a stranger's job behind his back because he'd let the money get so bad that loyalty had to be weighed against hunger.

The wind arrived at 1310, and it arrived all at once.

There was no gentle ramp. The shelf cloud came overhead like a dropped ceiling and the world went the color of gunmetal, and the first gust hit the Kestrel's sails with a sound like a door slamming in a cathedral. Felix had the helm. He'd taken it himself, which was not humility — it was the opposite, it was the old reflex, the conviction that no one else could hold his boat through the first thirty seconds of anything — and the boat rounded up hard before he caught her, the wheel going heavy in his hands, the rail dipping until the deck was a wall.

"Ease the main! EASE IT!"

Dev was on the halyard before Felix finished the word, and Tomas came up the companionway ladder two rungs at a time and threw his weight on the mainsheet, and the boat came back under the wheel like a horse deciding, reluctantly, to be ridden.

Then the rain came, and the world disappeared.

It wasn't rain like rain. It was rain like a solid thing, a white roaring curtain, and inside it the wind gusted in fists — thirty knots, forty, a fifty-knot punch that laid the Kestrel over until Felix's boots were braced against the leeward coaming and the compass card was spinning like a roulette wheel. He steered by feel and by the sound of the water against the hull, the old trick his father had taught him in another life: *the hull talks, Felix. A boat going through water happy makes one sound. A boat fighting it makes another. Listen for the difference.*

He listened. The Kestrel was fighting it. He bore off ten degrees, took the seas on the quarter instead of the beam, and felt the hull's voice change — still complaining, but complaining in the register of a working boat instead of a dying one.

"Radar!" he shouted aft. "What's behind the leading edge?"

Priya's voice came back through the rain, muffled and steady. "Line's forty miles deep but it's broken — there's a gap forming at our latitude, maybe six miles wide. If we hold this heading for twenty minutes we punch through into the back side."

"Twenty minutes," Felix said, to no one, to the wheel, to the sea. "We can do twenty minutes."

The sea, characteristically, did not answer.

* * *

It was in minute fourteen that the hull sang.

Not sang — *knocked*. A sound from below the waterline, forward of the engine room, a deep wooden knock like a fist on a door from the inside, and Felix felt it come up through the wheel and through his arms and into his teeth, and every hair on his body stood up at once.

"Tomas! Below! Forward bilge — NOW!"

Tomas went, and Felix held the boat alone through the worst of it, the gusts coming now in long screaming sets, and he heard the bilge pump start below and heard it keep running, which was the sound of water arriving faster than a hull should make it in an hour-old squall.

Two minutes. Three. The rain began to thin at the edges, the gunmetal light lifting toward something merely gray, and then Tomas's voice came up the ladder and it was the voice of a man trying very hard to sound calm and not quite getting there.

"Skipper. You need to see this."

"I'm kind of busy holding the boat together!"

"It'll hold for five minutes without you. The bilge won't."

* * *

He put Dev on the wheel with orders that were simple and absolute — *quartering seas, don't let her broach, scream if the wind backs* — and went below into the smell of diesel and bilgewater and the particular green darkness of a boat's guts in bad weather.

Tomas crouched at the forward inspection hatch with a flashlight in his teeth. The bilge pump was running. The water in the bilge was shin-deep and rising, slowly, the way a debt rises — not fast enough to panic, fast enough to notice, fast enough to kill you in a day if you ignored it.

Tomas took the flashlight out of his mouth and played it along the hull planking where it met the stringer, and Felix saw it.

A crack. Hairline — literally hairline, a black thread no wider than a fingernail's edge, running maybe two feet along the seam where the garboard strake met the keelson. Water wept through it in a fine silver beading, a seam of tiny jewels in the flashlight beam, each one swelling and letting go and swelling again.

"That's not storm damage," Felix said.

"No," Tomas agreed.

"A squall doesn't open a hull like that. Stress does. Fatigue does. Years of it." Felix crouched, put his finger against the crack, felt the water push against his skin like a small cold pulse. "This has been opening for months."

"Years, maybe." Tomas's voice was flat as always, but his eyes weren't. "I've been reading her hull numbers since we took this job. She's been working harder than her paperwork says. Somebody's been running her heavy and logging her light."

Felix heard what Tomas wasn't saying. *Somebody* was him. Two years of debt-chasing, of running charter jobs that paid too little to maintain the boat properly, of patching instead of repairing, of deferring the haul-out one season and then another because the haul-out cost money he didn't have. The Kestrel hadn't failed him. He had failed her, season by season, in increments too small to see, the way all the worst failures work.

The crack was his ledger, written in the hull.

" epoxy and sailcloth?" he said. "In this sea state?"

"Below the waterline we can't do anything until she's beached or hauled. What we can do is slow it." Tomas was already moving aft, pulling the spares locker. "I've got two tubes of marine epoxy and a sheet of fiberglass cloth. We glass the seam from inside, get a pump on it running continuous, and she'll make harbor. She'll make the wreck site. She won't make it pretty, and she won't make it twice."

Felix looked at the water in the bilge. Silver beading. Small cold pulse.

"Then we glass it," he said.

* * *

They worked the seam by flashlight while the squall's back half rolled over them, and Felix worked it with them — on his knees in the bilgewater, holding the cloth against the curve of the hull while Tomas mixed epoxy with hands that did not shake because Tomas's hands never shook, while Dev fed them supplies and Priya called down headings and adjustments every ten minutes in her calm arithmetic voice.

It was filthy work. The epoxy got into the creases of Felix's knuckles and wouldn't come out. The bilgewater soaked through his trousers and turned cold. At one point the Kestrel took a gust and a sea together and laid over far enough that the three of them slid into the forward bulkhead in a pile of limbs and epoxy tubes, and Dev laughed — actually laughed, a young unguarded bark of a laugh — and then caught himself and looked at Felix to see if laughing was allowed.

Felix was on his back in two inches of bilgewater with fiberglass cloth stuck to his forearm and epoxy in his hair.

"Again," he said, "and I'm docking everyone's pay," and Dev laughed harder, and Tomas made a sound that was almost a smile, and something shifted in the boat that had nothing to do with the hull.

They glassed the seam in two overlapping layers. Tomas ran a bead of epoxy along the crack's whole length and smoothed it with a spatula he'd clearly stolen from the galley, and when they were done they sat back on their heels in the dripping dark and looked at their work — ugly, functional, a bandage on a wound that would need surgery in a real yard with real money, neither of which was coming.

"Baseline was half a liter an hour," Tomas said. "I'll check it again in an hour. If the glass holds, we're maybe at a liter. Manageable."

"Manageable," Felix repeated.

"It's not a fix, skipper."

"I know what it is." Felix stood, braced against the deck's motion, and found that his legs were steady and his hands were steady and something in his chest was steady too, for the first time in two years. "It's the truth about the boat. I've been avoiding worse truths for longer."

Tomas looked at him for a moment. Tomas was a man who rationed his looking, the way he rationed his words, and when he spent one it meant something.

"You handled the squall," Tomas said. "First thirty seconds. That's the part that kills boats. You didn't lose her."

It was not praise, exactly. It was a measurement, delivered in the tone of a man reading a gauge. But Felix had been running on empty measurements for two years — the Syndicate's ledgers, Ines's sealed coordinates, Priya's charts that lied politely — and this one, this small flat gauge-reading from his engineer, went into him like fuel into a dry tank.

"Get topside," Felix said, gruff, before the moment could become a moment. "Dev can't hold that wheel forever."

* * *

The back side of the squall, when they punched through it near sunset, was a world washed clean. The sky to the east went the color of hammered copper, and the sea ran long and glassy under it, and the wind settled into a decent honest fifteen knots on the quarter. The Kestrel, storm jib and double-reefed main, ran down the copper light like she was glad of it.

Felix stood at the wheel and let her run.

His arms ached. His back ached. There was epoxy in his hair that would take a week to get out, and below his feet, under the clean copper water, a hairline crack was being held shut by two layers of fiberglass and a promise.

He thought about his father. He thought about a man who had looked at a broken thing — a family, a fortune, a sea with his wife's grave in it — and chosen to seal it over rather than open it up, to glass the seam from the inside and keep sailing. He'd hated his father for it his whole adult life. Standing at the wheel with his own hands still tacky with resin, Felix understood the choice for the first time, and understanding it frightened him more than the crack did.

"Position," he called aft.

Priya appeared beside him with the chart, and the sunset caught it, and for a moment the paper glowed like it was lit from within. "Forty-one miles to the supply anchorage. We make it by midnight if the wind holds."

"Good. We anchor, we take on water and provisions, we check the bilge every hour." He paused. "And Priya — when we anchor, I want eyes on every skiff that comes near us. Every one. We're a slow boat with a cracked hull and a sealed envelope of coordinates, and I'm done pretending nobody knows where we're going."

Priya folded the chart. "You think someone's coming."

"I think someone always comes." Felix watched the copper light fade off the water. "I just don't know yet whether they're selling something or taking it."

They ran through the dusk. At 2340, Priya raised the anchorage — a hook of dark land, three riding lights, and, on the beach, the small scattered fires of a fishing village gone to bed.

And one skiff, dark, no lights, sitting a quarter mile off their approach line, engine silent, as if it were waiting for them to arrive.

As if it had been waiting for some time.
