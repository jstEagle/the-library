## 9. Ines at the Rail

The yacht was called *Meridian's Daughter*, which Ines had always considered a small act of blasphemy — naming a boat after the syndicate that owned half her life and all of her patience. She'd bought it with the first money she'd made that the Syndicate hadn't touched directly, and even then the ledger men had smiled at the purchase in a way that meant they'd simply added another column somewhere.

Tonight she stood at the rail aft of the bridge deck, a glass of something expensive untouched in her hand, watching the lights of Tanjung Priok smear across black water. The *Kestrel* was three berths down, riding low and shabby, her running lights flickering like a man trying to stay awake. Felix Eriksen's boat. Felix Eriksen's crew. Felix Eriksen's debt, which she now owned as surely as if she'd signed for it herself.

She hadn't told him that part yet.

Below her feet, the tender garage hummed with refrigeration units and the soft tick of electronics cooling. Somewhere in there, sealed in a waterproof case bolted under the floor plates, sat the thing that had brought her across two oceans: a single sheet of sonar imagery, twenty-two years old, annotated in a dead man's handwriting.

She didn't need to look at it again. She could see it with her eyes closed.

*Halycon Star*, hull-down survey vessel, lost with eleven souls in the Sunda approaches during an early monsoon. Official cause: navigational error compounded by weather. Unofficial cause, according to every rumor that had ever drifted through the salvage bars of Southeast Asia: bad luck, bad steel, bad sea. The Syndicate had run three recovery operations over the years and come back with rust, silt, and a bill. The wreck sat in water too deep for divers without saturation gear, in a current too mean for ROVs to hold station, on a reef charted wrong by everyone who'd ever tried to chart it.

And none of that — not the depth, not the current, not the reef — was why the Syndicate had failed.

Ines turned the glass in her fingers. Ice clicked. Somewhere behind her, her first officer, Dario, pretended very hard to be checking a mooring line he'd checked twice already.

She needed Felix because of his father.

Not his money — Henrik Eriksen had died with less than his son owed now. Not his reputation, which had been worth more before the disaster than after. She needed him because Henrik Eriksen had been the one man aboard the *Halcyon Star* who knew what was actually in her hold, and he had spent the last years of his life making sure no machine would ever find it.

The sonar sheet proved it. Ines had paid a corrupt archivist in Rotterdam more than most people earned in a decade for that single page — the final survey pass run by the *Halcyon Star* herself, hours before she went down. On it, in Henrik's cramped engineer's hand, were markings that made no sense as geology. A cluster of returns off the wreck's aft cargo bay, dense as solid metal, sitting where the manifests said there should be nothing but ballast and seawater. And beside them, boxed twice, a note in Danish that Ines had translated four times just to be sure:

*Ikke på kortene. Ikke i maskinen. Kun i hovedet.*

Not on the charts. Not in the machines. Only in the head.

Henrik Eriksen had hidden his family's inheritance aboard that ship — patents, deeds, survey data worth more than the vessel ten times over — and when the Syndicate came for it, he had done something no one had ever proven. He had moved it. Buried it inside the wreck itself, or beneath it, or in some cavity only he knew, and then he had let the ship die around it like an oyster sealing a pearl. Every instrument the Syndicate pointed at that reef since had read exactly what Henrik wanted them to read: nothing.

Only in the head. But heads died. Henrik's had, slowly, in a rented room above a chandlery in Da Nang, coughing salt out of lungs the sea never quite gave back. And whatever map he'd kept there, he'd passed to someone. Men like Henrik didn't take secrets into the dark willingly. They left them somewhere. A letter. A lesson. A bedtime story dressed up as a sailing tale.

Ines had bet everything she had left that he'd passed it to his son.

"Ma'am." Dario's voice, careful. "You wanted to know if the Kestrel's generator cycled."

"It did?"

"At 21:40. Ran eleven minutes. Shut down clean."

So Tomas Vukovic was nursing their power like a miser counting coins, which meant the hull was worse than Felix was admitting, which meant the clock was already running whether anyone had said so aloud. Good. Ines had built her career on other people's clocks.

"Thank you, Dario. Go sleep."

He went. He always went. That was the trouble with hiring competent people; they assumed competence in return, and neither of you ever said what you were actually doing out here at midnight, staring at a rust-bucket full of ghosts.

* *

She had met Henrik Eriksen exactly once.

She was nineteen, working the refit docks at Sunda Kelapa, hauling grit paper along hulls for wages that evaporated before they reached her pocket. Her brother Mateo worked the same dock, two slips down, and between shifts they'd sit on the bollards splitting a bag of fried bananas and inventing futures. Mateo's future always involved a boat of his own. Hers involved getting out — off the docks, out from under the Syndicate's tally-men, out of the country where her mother's medical debt had been sold three times like a used engine.

Henrik came through that spring, chartering survey work, and the Syndicate had contracted him for a run to the eastern archipelago. Ines remembered him because he was the only captain she'd ever seen argue with a Syndicate factor and win. He'd stood in the harbormaster's office with his cap in his hands and refused, flatly, to carry a second set of "insurance" crates — Syndicate freight, unmanifested, riding below decks on every licensed vessel in the fleet. Everyone knew what those runs meant: you hauled their boxes, you kept your mouth shut, and if the boxes went missing or the ship got impounded, the debt landed on your name.

Eriksen refused. Loudly. In front of witnesses.

The factor had smiled the way they all smiled and said something about how a man with a family should think carefully. And Eriksen had said — Ines could still hear it, could still feel the dockside heat of that afternoon — *"My family is exactly why I'm saying no."*

Mateo had heard it too. She knew because that night, on the bollards, Mateo said: *That's the kind of captain I want to be.* And six months later, when Eriksen put out word that he was taking on deck crew for a long survey contract — good pay, real papers, no Syndicate freight — Mateo signed before Ines could talk him out of it.

She hadn't tried very hard to talk him out of it. That was the wound she carried, the one she never showed anyone: not that she'd failed to stop him, but that some ambitious, starving part of her had thought, *good, one of us gets out.*

The *Halcyon Star* sailed in October with a crew of eleven and Henrik Eriksen's family secret already in her belly, though nobody alive knew that then. The monsoon came three weeks early. The ship stopped answering on a Tuesday. Eleven names went onto the lost list, and Mateo Varga was the fourth one down, seventeen years old, and the Syndicate sent a condolence letter with a bill attached for his unpaid training bond.

They billed a dead boy's sister for the privilege of losing him.

Ines had spent twenty-two years learning how systems worked — ledgers, liens, salvage law, the particular grammar of maritime insurance fraud — and every skill she'd sharpened had one purpose underneath it, though she'd buried that purpose so deep she sometimes forgot it herself. She told people she was in it for the money. She told herself that too, most days. It was easier than the truth, which was that she intended to burn the Meridian Syndicate's claim on that wreck to the ground, and she intended to do it with their own paperwork, and she needed the contents of Henrik Eriksen's strongbox to do it — because buried in those patents and deeds, if her researchers were right, was proof of what the *Halcyon Star* had really been carrying when she died.

Syndicate freight. Unmanifested. The boxes Eriksen had refused to haul — except the factors had loaded them anyway, on a different ship, the same route, the same storm. And the loss of that freight, written off against the wreck, was the founding lie of the Syndicate's eastern holdings. The inheritance Henrik hid wasn't just his family's. It was the evidence.

Ambition, she'd learned, was just grief that had learned to walk.

* *

The problem — the problem she turned over now like a stone with something living under it — was that she might be wrong about Felix.

She'd watched him for three years. Storm-chasing footage out of the Gulf of Tonkin, port gossip from Haiphong to Surabaya, a credit file the Syndicate itself had compiled before she acquired it. Felix Eriksen was talented and broke and proud in the specific way of men carrying a father's vow they didn't fully understand. He'd sworn off the archipelago. His father had burned the charts on a beach and made him promise, and Felix — to his credit, or his damage — had kept that promise for fifteen years.

Which raised the question she couldn't answer from a distance: had Henrik told him anything at all? Or had the old man died with the secret intact, leaving his son nothing but a broken compass and a terror of these waters?

If Felix knew nothing, the whole plan was a corpse. No sonar would find the strongbox. Her own divers couldn't work that depth without a month of setup she couldn't afford and couldn't hide. She needed the map that lived in a dead man's head, and the only possible heir to that map was standing three berths away on a leaking boat, drinking cheap rum and pretending he wasn't afraid of her.

So she'd built the trap out of the truest materials she had. Triple pay — true, she could afford it. Debt erased — true, she'd bought the paper. The *Halcyon Star* — true, and the look on his face when she'd named it had told her everything: not greed. Recognition. Somewhere under all that bravado, Felix Eriksen had been waiting his whole adult life for someone to say that name out loud.

But here was the part she didn't say to herself in daylight: she could have hired any desperate captain in the Java Sea. She chose Felix because she needed him. And she needed him — this was the thought that arrived now, punctual as tide — because she also wanted him to suffer a little. Wanted him to stand over that reef where his mother drowned and feel what she'd felt for twenty-two years reading a name off a lost list. She had dressed it up as strategy. Strategy required Felix specifically. Fine. But strategy did not require the small hot pleasure she'd taken when he signed, jaw tight, hating her, exactly the way she'd hated his father's name for two decades.

Guilt was a bilge pump that ran backwards. You could feel it working and still go down by the bow.

She set the untouched glass on the rail cap and looked at the *Kestrel*'s dark shape again. Eleven minutes of generator. A hull that sang when it shouldn't. A monsoon the forecasters were already whispering about, three weeks ahead of schedule, as if the sea itself had decided to keep the appointment Henrik Eriksen missed.

And one more thing. The thing she'd been circling all night the way you circle a smell you can't place.

Her researcher in Jakarta had flagged it that morning, buried in harbor manifests: a fishing skiff, registered to nobody, that had appeared in three ports along the *Kestrel*'s likely route in the past two weeks. Always a day ahead. Always gone by morning. The skiff's registration traced back, through two shell companies she recognized, to a family name she knew from the *Halcyon Star*'s lost-crew list.

Vasquez. Amara Vasquez, marine archaeologist, daughter of Elena Vasquez — hydrographic surveyor, *Halcyon Star*, lost with all hands.

Someone else was hunting Henrik Eriksen's ghost. Someone with a better right to it than Ines had, maybe, and certainly a better right than Felix, whose father had — if Ines's documents were honest, and she'd paid enough to make them honest — helped sink that ship to keep what was inside it from everyone, including the families of the men who died aboard.

Ines looked at the skiff's last reported position and felt something move in her chest that wasn't quite fear and wasn't quite hope. If the woman reached Felix's crew before the reef, the balance of everything changed. Felix would learn what his father had done. The strongbox would open with two claims on it instead of one, and grief would sit across the chart table from grief, and Ines's clean, cruel, beautiful plan would get complicated in ways she couldn't model.

She should warn Felix. A message, anonymous, one line: *you're being followed.*

She stood at the rail a long time, watching the *Kestrel*'s lights gutter and steady, gutter and steady, and did not send it.

Let the currents meet where they would. She'd spent twenty-two years believing the sea kept better accounts than any syndicate ever had.

Behind her, the satellite phone buzzed once against the chart table — a priority flag from her Jakarta contact, the subject line already visible on the cracked screen: **SKIFF SIGHTED — TANJUNG PRIOK — TONIGHT.**
