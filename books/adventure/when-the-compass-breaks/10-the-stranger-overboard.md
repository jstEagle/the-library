## 10. The Stranger Overboard

Tomas Reyes had spent twenty-two years listening to boats, and he'd learned early that a vessel tells you everything if you pay attention. The Kestrel talked in her sleep — a tick in the port engine that was really a worn bearing, a whisper of water where the hull had been patched too many times, a rattle in the galley pipe that meant nothing at all but liked to complain anyway. He knew her voice the way he knew his mother's.

Which is why the extra weight bothered him before anyone else noticed it.

They'd tied up at Pulau Sembilan on the third morning out, a fuel-and-supply stop Ines's itinerary insisted on. Tomas didn't like it. The island was the wrong kind of quiet, the kind of place where a man could watch you without being seen, and after Priya had shown him those doctored charts, he'd started counting wrongnesses like coins he couldn't spend.

But the weight came aboard sometime during the two hours they were loading diesel and fresh water, and Tomas felt it in the way the Kestrel sat at her lines. Half a degree off trim. Maybe less. A hundred and thirty pounds of something, give or take, distributed low and aft.

He told himself it was the extra water jugs. He told himself it was the humidity swelling the woodwork. He told himself a lot of things while he checked the bilge twice and found nothing, because a man who reports ghosts gets locked out of engine rooms.

By evening they were back underway, running south-southeast through water the color of hammered tin, and the weight was still there. Tomas lay in his bunk that night with one hand flat against the hull, feeling for changes in the vibration, and around two in the morning he heard it: not a sound from the boat, but a sound *in* the boat. Faint. Rhythmic. From the lazarette aft, where the spare line and the emergency water were stowed.

Somebody breathing.

* * *

He didn't wake Felix right away. That was a decision, and he made it deliberately, standing in the companionway in his underwear with a wrench in his fist, weighing what would happen next. If it was a Syndicate man, waking the captain first made sense. If it was a kid, or a castaway, or some poor devil who'd swum to the wrong boat, waking Felix first meant the poor devil got put over the side before anyone asked a single question.

Felix Eriksen was a good captain having a bad year, and bad years made men hard in the wrong places.

So Tomas went aft alone, wrench in hand, and opened the lazarette hatch the way you open anything that might be a trap — fast, from the side, ready to swing.

A woman looked up at him.

She was maybe thirty, brown-skinned, black hair cut short and practical, wedged into the space between a coiled mooring line and the emergency water cans like she'd been built to fit there. She didn't scream. She didn't beg. She looked at the wrench, then at Tomas's face, and said, quietly, "Is there coffee?"

Tomas stood there for a long moment, wrench half-raised, feeling the Kestrel roll beneath them both.

"You've been aboard since Sembilan," he said.

"Since before you left the dock." She unfolded herself from the locker with the economy of someone who'd done this before, which raised about nine new questions. "I moved aboard during the fueling. Your deckhand walked past me four times."

"Rafi walks past everything. He once stepped over a sleeping monitor lizard." Tomas kept the wrench up anyway. "You're coming with me. Upstairs. Slow."

"Fine." She paused at the companionway ladder, glancing back at him. "For what it's worth, I wasn't going to hurt anyone. I just needed to get where you were going."

"That's what everybody says right before they hurt somebody."

She almost smiled. "You're the engineer. Tomas. You talk to the boat when you think no one's listening."

"I don't talk to—"

"You say 'not tonight, sweetheart' when the port bearing runs hot." She climbed the ladder ahead of him, unhurried. "I listen too."

* * *

The salon lights came on and the crew assembled the way crews do — drawn by noise, then by the smell of a story. Rafi arrived first, blinking, still holding half a sandwich. Priya came down from the flybridge with her tablet clutched to her chest like a shield. And Felix came last, barefoot, moving with the coiled stillness he got when the world handed him something he hadn't budgeted for.

Felix looked at the stranger for a long five seconds. Tomas watched his captain's face go through recognition, denial, and arrival in quick succession.

"You," Felix said. "The skiff. Outside the harbor the day we left. You were watching us."

"I was checking faces against a photograph." The woman said it plainly, no apology in it. "Amara Sandoval. Marine archaeologist. I have a claim on the wreck you're heading for, and I needed to be on this boat, and asking politely was going to get me a no."

"It got you a no anyway," Felix said. "Over the side. There's a fishing village six miles back. They'll run you in."

"Felix." Priya's voice, careful. "Before we do anything — how did she know our destination? We haven't broadcast it. The charts are sealed."

"I know the Halcyon Star," Amara said.

The name landed in the room like a dropped tool. Tomas saw Felix's jaw tighten, saw Priya's fingers whiten on the tablet, and understood that whatever the Halcyon Star was, it wasn't just a job.

"My family lost people on that ship," Amara went on, and now something moved under the plainness of her voice, a current under ice. "Eleven years ago. I've spent eleven years trying to find out why. When I heard a salvage crew was running for those coordinates, I stopped caring about polite."

"The coordinates are sealed," Priya said again, sharper.

"They're sealed in your safe, behind your navigation station, combination set the day you left port." Amara looked at Priya without malice. "I watched you open it twice. I'm sorry. It seemed kinder than following you out here and hoping."

Silence. Rafi took a bite of his sandwich, thought better of it, and lowered it.

Felix crossed to the chart table and stood over it with both hands flat, the way he did when he was deciding whether to be a captain or an arsonist. "Here's what happens now," he said. "We alter course, we put you ashore at the first anchorage, and you never see these waters again. That's not negotiable, and it's not cruelty — it's that I don't know you, I can't vet you, and my boat is already carrying more trouble than she can float."

"She's carrying more trouble than she can float regardless," Amara said. "Your hull has hairline fractures forward of the engine room. Two of them. Port side, below the waterline seam."

Every head turned toward Tomas. He felt the heat climb his neck, because he'd found the first fracture himself three days ago and told only Felix, and the second one he'd found last night and told nobody, because telling people your boat is dying is a slow way of admitting you can't save her.

"How," he said.

"I grew up on wooden hulls. My father repaired them. You learn to read stress the way you learn to read weather." She shrugged. "Also, I was lying against that section of the hull for thirty hours. It talks."

"The boat talks," Tomas repeated faintly. "Yeah. It does."

"Tomas." Felix's voice was flat. "Opinion."

Tomas looked at the woman who'd listened to his engines, at his captain who was one bad week from drowning, at Priya who hadn't slept properly since the charts, at Rafi who was unpaid and hungry and had already, visibly, decided. He thought about the vote they'd taken behind Felix's back in a dockside bar, the one that had gotten them all onto this doomed errand, and he thought about how the Kestrel had sounded since Sembilan — wrong, trimmed heavy, like a boat carrying more than cargo.

"She stays till morning," Tomas said. "We decide at dawn. All of us. Not just you."

"We already voted once without me," Felix said bitterly.

"Yeah," Tomas said. "And look how well that turned out. Maybe voting works."

* * *

Dawn came up gray-green over a sea like poured glass, and the crew gathered in the cockpit with coffee nobody tasted. Felix laid it out hard: stowaway, unknown quantity, possible Syndicate plant, possible worse. Throw her at the first anchorage, keep the crew small and the secrets smaller.

Then Priya spoke. "She broke into a locked safe without leaving a mark. If she wanted to hurt us, we'd already be hurt. If she wanted to rob us, there's nothing worth robbing. What she wants is what we want — the wreck. That makes her either our best ally or our most dangerous problem, and I genuinely can't tell which, and neither can you."

"I can tell which," Felix said.

"No," Priya said gently, "you can't. You're deciding with your father's ghost, not your head."

Rafi, who rarely spoke at meetings, spoke. "She fixed the galley pump while we were arguing. Did anyone notice? It was dripping for a week. It's not dripping now." He paused. "Also I'm hungry, and hungry people make bad decisions, and throwing away someone who knows the wreck site seems like a bad decision."

Tomas laughed despite himself. Then it was his turn, and everyone looked at him, because Tomas's opinion was the boat's opinion, and everyone knew it.

"She heard my bearings," he said. "She heard the hull talk. Captain, I've been alone with this boat's voice for twenty-two years, and this stranger picked it up in thirty hours. Either she's exactly what she says, or she's the best liar I've ever met, and honestly?" He spread his hands. "Either way, I want to watch her longer. Liars slip. Archaeologists don't."

Felix looked around the cockpit — at Priya's careful neutrality, at Rafi's stubborn hunger, at Tomas's raised hands — and Tomas watched him do the arithmetic he hated: three votes against one, again, the second time in a month, his authority eroding like a coastline.

"Fine," Felix said at last, and the word came out ground fine as sand. "She rides. She eats. She works. But she stays clear of the nav station, she reports to Tomas, and the moment I think she's playing us—" He didn't finish it. He didn't have to.

Amara nodded once, accepting terms like a diver accepting depth. "Fair enough."

"And one more thing." Felix leaned close, and though his voice stayed level, Tomas heard the thing underneath it, the old wound talking. "The Halcyon Star. Whatever you think you know about that ship — whatever your family thinks they lost — you don't know it better than I do. My mother died on that wreck. So whatever claim you're carrying, mine goes deeper. Don't forget it."

Something flickered across Amara's face then — not fear, not guilt, something older and sadder, like a door opening onto a lit room and closing again before anyone could see inside.

"I won't forget it," she said quietly. "I promise you that."

Later, Tomas found her at the rail, watching the water slide past, and offered her the coffee she'd asked for in the dark. She took it and held it without drinking.

"What did you mean by that?" he asked. "Back there. About promises."

Amara was quiet long enough that Tomas thought she wouldn't answer. Then she said, "My father used to say grief is a debt. Somebody always ends up paying it." She looked at him sideways. "I just need your captain to understand whose debt it actually is. Before we get there."

Tomas felt the Kestrel roll beneath them, steady and tired, carrying them all south toward a reef none of them should have been able to name — and for the first time since Sembilan, he wondered whether the extra weight he'd felt aboard wasn't the woman at all.

It was everything she knew.
