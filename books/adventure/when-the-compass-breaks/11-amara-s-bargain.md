## 11. Amara's Bargain

They kept her in the galley for the first hour, which Amara understood. A stowaway caught in a supply locker forty miles off Java didn't get the wheelhouse; she got a bolted bench and a locked door and Tomas standing in front of it with his arms crossed like a man guarding a door he'd rather not be guarding.

She used the hour to study them through the porthole glass — no, that was wrong, they'd taken her to the galley *after* the deck, after Felix Eriksen had hauled her up by the collar of her jacket and demanded to know who sent her. She'd used the hour since to reconstruct what she knew.

The Kestrel was smaller than the photographs had suggested. Older. Her paint was sun-scalded and her rail had been re-welded at least twice, and she sat low in the water the way boats do when their owners are behind on maintenance and pretending otherwise. But her wake was clean, and the engine note through the deck plates was steady, and someone aboard loved her enough to keep her honest even when they couldn't keep her pretty.

That told Amara something about the crew. The boat was the ledger you couldn't hide from.

Felix Eriksen himself was harder to read than his father had been. She'd studied the old man's face for years — in the one surviving photograph, in the memory of a story her grandmother told so many times it had worn smooth as sea glass. The son had the same jaw, the same way of holding his shoulders like a man braced against weather that hadn't arrived yet. But where the father's eyes had been, according to everyone who ever wrote about him, steady and certain, the son's were restless. A man looking over his shoulder for something. Debt, probably. It was always debt, out here. The Syndicate fed on men like him the way pilot fish fed on sharks — riding along, taking their cut, waiting for the big animal to bleed.

Good, she thought, and then hated herself a little for thinking it. His desperation made him predictable. Predictable meant negotiable. That was all she could afford to feel about Felix Eriksen right now: that he was a door, and she needed it open.

Tomas came back with coffee she hadn't asked for and set it down without a word. Big man, engineer's hands, knuckles scarred from machinery rather than fighting. He'd been the one who argued against throwing her overboard — she'd heard him through the bulkhead, low and reasonable, while Eriksen's voice climbed. *We're forty miles from anywhere and you want to put a woman in a rubber raft? Read me your insurance policy, captain.*

She wrapped her hands around the mug. It was hot and terrible and exactly what she needed.

The door opened, and Felix Eriksen came in with the expression of a man arriving at a meeting he'd already lost.

* * *

He sat across from her at the galley table, which was scarred with knife marks and a burn shaped vaguely like Australia, and he did not touch the coffee Tomas poured him. Priya came too — the navigator, sharp-eyed, arms folded — and stood by the door where she could watch both Amara and the captain at once. Smart woman. Amara filed that away.

"Marine archaeologist," Felix said. It wasn't a question so much as an accusation wearing a question's clothes.

"That's what my papers say."

"I read your papers. They're good. Too good. The university seal is right, the laminating is right, everything is right." He leaned forward. "People who are really who their papers say don't laminate things perfectly. They lose the originals and carry photocopies in plastic sleeves. You built this identity. Which means somewhere there's a real one underneath, and I'd like to know what it says."

Amara let the silence sit for three seconds. Long enough to show she wasn't afraid. Not long enough to insult him.

"It says Amara," she said. "The rest is true enough. I've worked wrecks. I've dived survey sites off Sulawesi and the Sunda shelf. And I know what you're looking for, Captain Eriksen, because I've been looking for it longer than Ines Varga has been paying you to."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not surprise — she'd expected surprise and gotten something better. Recognition. The look of a man hearing a sound he'd been hearing in dreams.

"The Halcyon Star," he said quietly.

"The Halcyon Star," she agreed. "Sank in the outer Sunda approaches during the monsoon surge, eleven years ago this October. Official record says hull failure in heavy seas. Unofficial record—" she spread her hands— "depends on who you ask."

"And who did you ask?"

"My grandmother." She said it flatly, no decoration, because decoration would have been a lie and lies were currency she couldn't waste here. "My grandfather was second engineer aboard her. My mother grew up on stories about that ship. When she went down, we got nothing. No pension. No settlement. The owners' consortium dissolved itself six months later and every claim filed against it drowned in paperwork. Fourteen families, Captain. Fourteen families lost people and got lawyers' letters instead of money." She turned the mug a slow quarter-turn on the table. "I started diving wrecks because I wanted to find ships. I kept diving them because I wanted to find out why ours went down."

Priya shifted by the door. "You think it wasn't the weather."

"I think," Amara said, "that the Halcyon Star was carrying survey patents and charts worth more than any cargo she ever listed, and that the men who wanted those charts were willing to let the sea do their accounting. And I think your father knew it, because he was the one who hid them."

The change in Felix was immediate and total, like a squall line crossing water. One moment he was a tired debtor playing captain; the next he was something older and colder, a boy standing on a beach watching his father burn paper.

"You don't know anything about my father."

"I know he was aboard. I know he survived when fourteen other families' people didn't. I know he never testified at the inquiry. And I know—" she held his stare, refusing to flinch from what she was about to say, because softening it would make it worthless— "I know that whatever he pulled off that ship, he kept it. Twenty years of patents and charts, sitting in some vault or some strongbox or some grave, while the families of the dead got nothing. Your inheritance, Captain. Bought with our dead."

The galley was very quiet. Through the hull she could hear the engine, patient as a heartbeat, and above it the small sounds of a boat under way — water against plating, rigging ticking, the wind finding its voice in the wires.

Felix stood up. For a moment she thought he was going to walk out, and she'd have deserved it, maybe, though she'd have followed him anyway. Instead he walked two steps to the porthole and stood looking at nothing, his back to her, his hands flat on the bulkhead like a man steadying himself against a list only he could feel.

"My mother died in that disaster," he said, to the porthole.

Amara went still.

"He never talked about it. Not once, not ever. He burned everything — charts, logs, letters — on the beach at Tanjung Pinang, and he made me watch, and he swore us both off these waters until the sea gave back what it took." A short, airless laugh. "I thought he was grieving. I spent twenty years thinking my father was just a broken man who couldn't look at an archipelago without falling apart." He turned around. His face had gone gray around the mouth. "And now you're telling me he might have been hiding."

"I'm telling you he was *there*," Amara said, gentler now, because the anger had gone out of the room and left something rawer behind. "What he was hiding, and from whom, and whether it was shame or survival — I don't know. That's what the wreck will tell us. If we can reach it."

* * *

Priya spoke first, because navigators always do when the map is on the table. She unrolled Ines Varga's supplied charts across the galley table, weighting the corners with the salt shaker, a tin cup, and Amara's confiscated dive knife, which she returned with a pointed look.

"Here's the search box Varga gave us." Her finger traced a rectangle south of Panaitan Island. "It's plausible. Right depth band, right seabed type, close to the last known heading. Any salvage outfit would buy it."

"But?" Amara asked.

"But I've been over the current data and the sediment reports, and if the Halcyon Star went down where the official record says, she should have drifted north-northeast before settling. This box is south-southwest of the projected drift line." Priya looked up. "It's not wrong enough to be obvious. It's wrong enough to waste nine days of diving."

"Or," Amara said, "it's wrong on purpose, because Ines Varga doesn't actually know where the wreck is, and she hired you to be her expensive metal detector."

Felix's jaw tightened. "You know where it is."

It wasn't a question either. Amara met it straight on.

"I know where my grandfather's ship settled. My family spent eleven years and most of what we had buying hydrographic surveys, bribing fishing crews, running side-scan off rented boats. We found debris fields twice. Anchor chain scarring. A boiler plate with the Halcyon's registry stamp." She drew a breath. "I can give you coordinates accurate to within two hundred meters. On a good day, fifty."

"And what does it cost me?"

"Passage. A share — equal, not token. And when we open whatever your father hid down there, my families get what's owed to them. The patents, the charts, whatever compensation those documents represent. That was stolen from us twice, Captain. Once by whoever sank the ship, and once by the man who swam away with the proof."

Felix stared at the chart. Amara watched him do the arithmetic she knew he was doing — the Syndicate debt, the triple pay Ines had promised, the share shrinking, the whole rotten structure of the deal tilting under her weight. A man whose inheritance was already spoken for three times over, being asked to promise it a fourth time.

"If the documents name your families," he said slowly, "then they were owed from the start. Legally. Morally." He rubbed his face. "I'm not going to pretend I'm in a position to be generous. I owe people who don't accept apologies. But I'll tell you what I can give you, and I'll give it straight, because apparently everyone else on this boat has decided to lie to me and somebody has to hold the line."

He laid it out: passage, protection, a full share of whatever the salvage yielded. And a written agreement — witnessed by Priya, signed before witnesses — that any patents, deeds, or claims documents recovered from the wreck would first satisfy the fourteen families, before a single credit touched his own ledgers.

Amara studied him across the chart table. Looking, deliberately, for the flaw. The angle. The tell of a man saying what she needed to hear to get what he needed from her.

What she found instead was exhaustion, and under it something that looked almost like relief — the relief of a man who has been carrying a debt so long that being handed a reason to spend it comes as a kind of mercy.

"All right," she said. "One more condition."

His eyes narrowed. "Of course there is."

"When we get there — wherever there is — I dive first. Before you, before anyone. My grandfather's ship. My family's dead." She held out her hand across the chart, across the burn mark shaped like Australia, across eleven years. "Those are my terms, Captain Eriksen. Take them or throw me back."

He looked at her hand for a long moment. Then he took it. His grip was calloused and cold from the wheelhouse and entirely steady.

"Welcome aboard the Kestrel," he said. "God help us both."

* * *

That night, unable to sleep in the narrow bunk Tomas had cleared for her, Amara climbed to the aft deck and found the sea running silver under a half moon, and Felix Eriksen already there, leaning on the rail like a man keeping vigil.

She almost went back below. Instead she stood beside him, an arm's length away, and for a while neither of them said anything, because there is a kind of company that only works in silence.

"She'd have been thirty-four this year," Felix said finally. "My mother. I don't even know what she sounded like anymore. I know I used to know."

"My grandfather whistled when he worked," Amara said. "Engine rooms and whistling. My mother says the whole ship could tell his mood by the tune." She watched the wake unspool behind them, pale and brief and gone. "When we find her, I'm going to whistle down the hatch. Stupid, probably. But I want the ship to know someone came back."

Felix turned to look at her then, and in the moonlight his face was younger and more frightened than it had been in the galley, and she understood suddenly that this man was not her enemy, and never had been. He was just another child of the same wreck, standing on the other shore of the same grief.

"Two hundred meters," he said. "You said within two hundred meters."

"Fifty, on a good day."

"Then tomorrow," Felix Eriksen said, "you'd better teach Priya how to find a good day. Because something's been bothering me since the galley, and I haven't said it out loud yet." He nodded astern, toward the dark horizon behind them, where — if you watched long enough, and she had been watching — a light blinked. Steady. Patient. Keeping pace.

"We're not alone out here, Amara. Somebody's been following us since the supply stop. And I have a bad feeling Ines Varga sold our route the moment we cleared the harbor."

Out in the dark, the light blinked again — closer now, or perhaps that was only the sea playing tricks.

Amara put her hand on the rail and held on.
