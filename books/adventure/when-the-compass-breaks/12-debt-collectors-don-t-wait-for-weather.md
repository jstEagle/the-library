## 12. Debt Collectors Don't Wait for Weather

The first sign was the wake.

Felix saw it at 0400, during the dog watch he'd taken because sleep had become a country he couldn't get a visa for. The Kestrel ran south-southwest under a half-moon, the sea flat as poured pewter, and off their port quarter — two miles back, maybe less — there was a line of white that no wave should have made.

Waves broke in patterns. This line held its shape. It moved when they moved. When Priya eased them up half a knot on the log, it kept pace like something on a leash.

He stood at the wheel and watched it for ten minutes before he woke her.

"Priya." He kept his voice low. "Radar."

She came up the companionway with her hair still braided from yesterday and her eyes already doing the math before she reached the console. She looked at the screen. Looked aft. Looked back at the screen.

"Contact bearing two-six-five relative," she said quietly. "Range closing by four hundred meters since you started staring at it."

"Running lights?"

"None."

"No fishing boat runs dark," Felix said.

"No fishing boat holds formation with us for an hour, either." Priya's fingers moved across the plotter, pulling up the track history, and Felix watched her face do what it always did when the numbers turned ugly — go very still, very clean, like a surgeon scrubbing in. "It matched our course change at midnight. We altered to clear the shoal water east of Pulau Senang and it altered ninety seconds later. Same heading change. Same rate of turn."

Ninety seconds. A big boat, then. Heavy. Something that didn't dance on waves but bullied through them.

Felix felt the old sensation crawl up his spine, the one he'd spent three years trying to drown in cheap rum and expensive adrenaline: the feeling of being *measured*. He'd grown up with it. His father used to say the Syndicate never chased you — they just followed, patient as tide, until following became owning.

"Wake Tomas," Felix said. "Don't touch anything else. Don't answer if they hail us."

"They haven't hailed us yet."

"I know." That was the part that sat worst in his stomach. "They're letting us know they're there first. The talking comes after."

* * *

By dawn the gunboat had closed to a mile and a half and stopped pretending to be weather.

It sat off their port beam like a statement: sixty feet of gray steel, angular superstructure, a bow built to shove seas aside rather than climb them. No flag. No name visible at this range. Just the blunt authority of its silhouette against the rising sun, and the long slow roll of its wake spreading out behind it like a signature on a contract nobody had read.

Tomas stood at the rail with a mug of coffee going cold in his fist. He hadn't shaved. He looked older than thirty-eight this morning, which Felix suspected was how the whole crew looked now, and would look more so every day between here and the Halcyon Star.

"That's a Meridian hull," Tomas said. Not a question. He'd worked Syndicate contracts once, back before either of them knew better. "Class Seven patrol boat. Twin diesels, twenty-two knots sustained. We make eleven on a good day with the wind apologizing to us."

"So we can't run."

"We can't run, we can't fight, and we can't outrun their radio." Tomas took a sip of the cold coffee anyway, out of stubbornness or ritual. "Question is why they haven't called yet. They've been shadowing us six hours. That's not intimidation. That's scheduling."

"Scheduling."

"Somebody over there is waiting for permission." Tomas set the mug down on the rail mount with a click that sounded final. "Collectors don't freelance, Felix. Somebody gave them our position, our heading, and our timetable. And there are exactly three people who know all three."

He didn't name them. He didn't have to. The list lived in Felix's head already, written in ink he'd been trying not to read since Singapore: Ines Varga. Amara. And himself.

* * *

The hail came at 0830, right on schedule, as if someone ashore had penciled it into a calendar between lunch meetings.

*"Motor vessel Kestrel, motor vessel Kestrel, this is Meridian recovery vessel Audacious. You are operating in a restricted commercial corridor under lapsed clearance. Acknowledge."*

Priya looked at Felix across the chart table. Amara stood near the companionway, arms folded, saying nothing, watching him with those level dark eyes that gave away exactly nothing she didn't want to give away. Ines wasn't aboard — she ran her own yacht a cautious five miles ahead, close enough to coordinate, far enough to claim distance. Which, Felix was beginning to understand, might be the entire point of the arrangement.

"Acknowledge what?" he said into the mic. "We're in international water with a salvage permit filed in Jakarta."

A pause. Long enough to be contemptuous.

*"Kestrel, your permit is noted. Your debt is also noted. Account holder Eriksen, F., outstanding principal and accrued interest, one hundred forty-two thousand, six hundred and ten US dollars as of this morning's ledger. You will transmit your current position, destination, and cargo manifest within the hour. Failure to comply constitutes flight from obligation. Flight converts your account to enforcement status."*

Enforcement status. Three words that meant different things depending on where you were standing. From a desk in Singapore it meant asset seizure. From the deck of a boat in the Sunda Sea it meant whatever the men on the gray hull decided it meant, and the sea was very good at making things disappear.

Felix keyed the mic. "Tell your ledger I'll settle when the job pays out. That was the arrangement."

*"Your arrangement is with a private party. Your obligation is with Meridian. These are not the same thing, Mr. Eriksen. You have fifty-seven minutes."*

The channel clicked dead. Static filled the wheelhouse like dust.

Amara spoke first. "They know the exact figure to the dollar. Down to today's interest accrual."

"So?"

"So that means somebody updated them recently. Interest doesn't post itself to a gunboat at sea." She unfolded her arms. "Someone sent them a live feed. Position, heading, balance, timetable. Someone who wanted them to find us *here*, at this point in the run, rather than back at harbor where they could have boarded us legally."

Felix stared at her. "Why would anyone want that?"

"Because a boarding at harbor stops a voyage." Her voice stayed even, almost gentle, which somehow made it worse. "A shadow in open water *steers* one. Every course you choose now, you'll choose knowing they're behind you. That's not collection, Felix. That's herding."

* * *

He called Ines on the encrypted set at noon, and the conversation lasted four minutes and told him everything and nothing.

"You're being shadowed," she said, before he'd finished the first sentence. Not surprised. Not alarmed. *Informed.*

"You knew."

"I knew Meridian had enforcement assets in the archipelago. Everyone running salvage here knows that. It's the cost of doing business." A pause, and he could hear her moving around her cabin, the clink of glassware, the composed domestic sounds of a woman entirely unbothered. "Did they cite your account?"

"To the dollar. Today's interest."

Another pause, slightly longer. "That's precise."

"It's *live*, Ines. Somebody's feeding them. My engineer did the arithmetic — there are three people who know our route and timetable, and one of them is you."

"And the other two are a woman you fished out of a supply dock nine days ago and yourself." Her voice cooled by degrees, frost forming on the line. "I don't sell routes, Felix. I buy them. If I wanted Meridian to take your box, I wouldn't need a gunboat — I'd simply never have hired you. Think about the economics before you insult me again."

He wanted to believe her. That was the trap of Ines Varga — she made disbelief feel like bad manners. Twenty years ago she'd been the only person who could keep up with him on a storm line, and some traitor part of him still flinched at the idea of them being enemies, even now, even after everything.

But his mother was dead and his father had burned their life on a beach, and both of those facts had Ines-shaped shadows attached to them somewhere in the margins.

"The box hasn't been raised," he said finally. "There's nothing to deliver. What do they think they're collecting?"

Silence. Then, quieter: "Maybe they're not collecting yet. Maybe they're positioning." And before he could ask what that meant: "Stay on schedule. The window closes whether they like it or not. Monsoon doesn't negotiate, Felix. Neither should you."

She hung up. He sat with the handset in his hand and realized she'd answered none of his questions and confirmed all of his fears, and done it in a way that left him grateful for the confirmation. That was Ines. She could rob you and make you thank her for the receipt.

* * *

The deadline passed at 0930 the next morning. Felix didn't transmit. Nobody slept well the night before, and at 0931 the gunboat answered his silence.

It came about.

Not toward them — across them. The Audacious swung wide, ran out two miles to starboard, and settled onto a parallel course ahead of them, sitting squarely on the line between the Kestrel and the coordinates Amara had traded for her passage. It didn't threaten. It didn't signal. It just *sat there*, a wall of gray steel drawn across their future, and let the geometry do the talking.

Deliver or default. Or try to go around.

Priya plotted it without being asked. "If we hold this heading, we pass within eight hundred meters of them in four hours. If we divert north, we add nineteen hours and burn fuel we don't have. If we divert south—" she tapped the chart, where a lacework of reefs crawled across the paper like veins, "—we die."

"So we sail straight at them."

"So we sail straight at them," she agreed, "and we decide in the last mile what kind of people we are."

Felix looked at the crew gathered in the wheelhouse. Tomas leaning against the doorframe with his arms crossed, jaw set. Priya at the chart table, pencil steady. Amara by the windows, watching the gray shape on the horizon with an expression he was starting to learn how to read — not fear, not calculation. Recognition. Like she'd seen this exact scene before, in another life, from the other side of the water.

And underneath all of it, the winch turning. Click by click by click. Every hour the gunboat held station, the cable shortened. Every radio call, every silent hour, every precise-to-the-dollar figure read aloud over open water — ratchet, ratchet, ratchet. His father had described it once, drunk, the night before he died: *They don't chase you, boy. They just keep taking up the slack until you can't tell anymore whether you're sailing or being reeled in.*

Felix had sworn he'd never feel that. He'd built a whole loud, fast, debt-drunk life on never feeling that.

Here it was. Here he was.

"Tomas," he said. "Check the bilge pumps. Both of them. And then check them again."

Tomas frowned. "We're dry. I checked last night."

"Check them again anyway. Twice." Felix turned back to the wheelhouse windows, to the gray wall waiting on their heading. "Because if someone sold our route, they didn't stop selling. And I want to know what else on this boat belongs to them before they come to collect it."

Tomas held his gaze a moment, then nodded slowly and went below. His footsteps faded down the ladder, into the engine room, into the dark.

Ten minutes later he came back up the ladder faster than he'd gone down.

"Felix," he said, and his voice had changed. "You need to see this. Now."
