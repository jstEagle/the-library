## 13. The Hull Sings

Tomas Lindqvist had a theory about boats: they never lied to you, but they sang. A happy hull was quiet — a low hum of water against plating, nothing more. An unhappy hull talked. It ticked and groaned and muttered in its sleep. And a hull that was dying?

A dying hull sang.

The Kestrel had been singing for two days now, and Tomas had stopped sleeping.

He lay in his bunk in the dark with one ear pressed to the bulkhead like a doctor listening to a chest, cataloguing the sounds the way other men counted sheep. The normal stuff first, to calm himself down: the diesel's idle thrum through the engine beds, two decks up the slap of spray on the foredeck, Priya's footsteps crossing above as she came off her watch. All fine. All familiar.

Then, underneath it all, fainter than any of that — a sound he had no name for yet. Not a groan. Groans were structural, big-boned, honest. This was smaller. A thin metallic whisper, like someone dragging a fingernail along the inside of a tin can, three seconds long, then silence, then again four minutes later.

Regular intervals. That was the part that kept him awake. The sea didn't keep schedules.

* * *

At 0300 he gave up on sleep, pulled on his boots, and went below.

The Kestrel's bilge was not a place designed for human beings. It was a crawl space between the fuel tanks and the hull skin, lit by a single caged bulb Tomas had wired himself eight years ago, smelling of diesel, salt rust, and the particular sourness of seawater that had been sitting somewhere it shouldn't. He wedged himself in with a flashlight clamped between his teeth and a stethoscope — an actual medical stethoscope, bought off a retired nurse in Cebu for two bottles of beer — pressed against the stringer.

He'd been doing this every night since the squall. After they'd patched the hairline fractures Felix had found topside, after the epoxy had cured and everyone had congratulated themselves and moved on, Tomas had come down here because that was his job: to be the man who checked twice. And he'd found what he'd expected to find, which was nothing much. Hairline cracks sealed. Plating sound. A boat with a bruise but no broken ribs.

That had been six days ago.

Now he moved the stethoscope along the port stringer, inch by inch, and listened to the sea talking through steel that should have been silent.

There. Forward of the tank saddle, just past where the light reached properly. He shifted, wedged himself deeper into the crawlspace, and put the flashlight beam on the spot.

The crack was back.

Not the old one — he'd photographed the old ones, printed the photos, dated them, taped them inside his tool locker because he was that kind of man and everyone on the crew teased him for it. This was new. A fresh seam running maybe thirty centimeters along a weld line that had shown zero stress in six days of moderate seas. And at its end, where cracks tapered off into healthy metal, this one didn't taper. It ended clean. Sharp. Like it had been helped along.

Tomas lay there in the diesel smell with his heart going hard and slow, the way it did when he was more frightened than he wanted to admit even to himself.

Metal fatigues. He knew that better than anyone alive; he'd spent twenty years listening to boats complain. Metal fatigued from the outside in, from stress concentrations, from bad welds and worse luck, and it did it over months and years, not days. A crack like this appearing in under a week, in seas the Kestrel had shrugged off a hundred times before, in a spot with no history, no corrosion, no reason —

It didn't happen. Unless somebody made it happen.

He thought about the bilge pumps. He'd serviced them in Singapore, both of them, rebuilt the impellers himself. When was the last time he'd actually looked at them? Four days ago? Five? He'd checked the float switches, run them dry for ten seconds each, wiped them down. Routine. He hadn't opened the housings. Why would he open the housings? They were his pumps. Nobody else touched his pumps. That was the law aboard the Kestrel, unwritten but absolute, and every soul aboard respected it —

Every soul aboard.

He lay very still in the dark and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the squall. Seven people on a forty-meter boat. Seven people who ate together, sweated together, bailed together. Himself. Felix. Priya. Old Mateo on deck hands. Young Dev, who'd signed on in Manila and still flinched at thunder. Ines Varga's two contracted hands, Renn and Okafor, quiet men who kept to themselves and whose references nobody had ever actually called.

And Amara. The stowaway turned shipmate, the woman with her own claim on the wreck, who'd traded coordinates for passage and whose grief matched theirs like a key matches a lock — which was exactly what made it impossible to know whether the lock was being opened or picked.

Tomas closed his eyes. He hated this. He hated it the way you hate a splinter working its way toward your heart — not the pain of it so much as the knowledge of where it was going. Because suspicion aboard a small boat is a fire in a paint locker. You don't get to have a little suspicion. Either you smother it or it takes the whole vessel.

He thought about Felix, seventeen years old, standing on a dock in Da Nang while the collectors took the Kestrel's sister boat apart bolt by bolt, and saying to Tomas, *As long as I've got a hull and a crew, nobody owns me.* He thought about Priya redrawing charts by lamplight because she didn't trust the ones Ines supplied. He thought about the gunboat on the horizon, radioing deadlines.

Maybe the Syndicate didn't need to chase them. Maybe the Syndicate had already caught them, quietly, from inside.

* * *

He didn't sleep the rest of the night. At dawn he climbed out of the bilge stiff as a folded ruler, washed the grease off his hands in the galley sink, and ate nothing.

Priya found him there at 0600, staring into a mug of coffee he'd forgotten to drink.

"You look like the engine room spat you out," she said.

"Close." He turned the mug a quarter turn on the table. "Priya. Question. Hypothetical."

"I love hypotheticals before sunrise."

"If somebody wanted this boat dead — quietly, deniably, no explosions, nothing obvious — how would they do it?"

She sat down across from him. She was a compact woman who treated everything, including this, as a navigation problem. "Slow leak in a place the auto-bilge can't reach fast enough. Or —" she paused, and he watched her arrive at the same place he'd arrived, watch it land behind her eyes — "or you let the existing damage do the work. Worsen it. Accelerate it."

"The crack forward of the port tank saddle," Tomas said. "New one. Six days since we sealed the others. It's thirty centimeters and it ends clean."

Priya was quiet for a moment. Above them, feet crossed the deck — Dev going forward on morning rounds, the familiar rhythm of a working boat. Tomas listened to it and felt, for the first time in his life aboard the Kestrel, that the rhythm itself might be a disguise.

"Show me," Priya said.

* * *

They went down together, which was its own problem, because two people missing from the deck at once got noticed, and Tomas caught himself cataloguing who noticed, and hating himself for it. Mateo glanced up from coiling line. Renn stood at the rail doing nothing in particular, which Renn did a lot, and Tomas filed that away too, and hated that he'd filed it.

In the bilge, Priya took the stethoscope without comment and listened the way she read charts — completely, patiently, twice. Then she put the flashlight on the crack and studied the end of it for a long time.

"This isn't fatigue," she said finally. Her voice was flat, careful, the voice she used when a channel marked on a chart wasn't where the chart said it was. "Fatigue propagates in beach marks. You can count them like tree rings. This is —" She touched it with one fingertip. "This is a wedge. Something's been driven into the weld line and worked back and forth. Repeatedly."

"You can't know that for certain."

"No," she agreed. "But I can know that the timing is wrong, the location is wrong, and the morphology is wrong, and when three independent things are all wrong in the same direction, a navigator calls it what it is." She clicked off the flashlight. In the sudden dark her voice came quieter. "Somebody's been down here, Tomas."

"When? The bilge's mine. Nobody comes down here."

"Somebody comes down here at night while you're asleep, or during watch changes when the compartment's empty, or —" She stopped. He heard her breathing change. "Or they have a reason to be down here that doesn't look like a reason."

"What reason?"

Priya didn't answer right away. When she did, her voice had gone very controlled, the way it did in weather. "Who did the last full pump inspection?"

"Me. Four, five days ago. Housing checks only."

"And before that?"

"Singapore. Full rebuild. Both pumps."

"So between Singapore and now, the only person with a legitimate excuse to be in this compartment, repeatedly, unremarked, is you." A pause. "Which means whoever it is has been using your routine as cover. Your rounds are the schedule they hide inside."

Tomas sat back against the fuel tank and felt the cold of the hull through his shirt. His own home. His own engine room, the one place on any ocean where he'd always felt competent, sovereign, safe. And now it was a room where some invisible hand had been at work in the dark, patient as rust, turning their refuge into a coffin one millimeter at a time.

"We tell Felix," Priya said.

"Not yet."

She turned toward him. Even in the dark he could feel her looking. "Explain that."

"Because telling him means naming someone, and we can't name anyone. Not yet. We've got seven people on this boat and if I point at the wrong one, we're done — not sunk done, worse. Crew done. You don't come back from a captain who thinks his people are traitors, and you don't come back from crew who think the captain suspects them. Felix is barely holding this together as it is. The debt, the gunboat, his father's ghost sitting on the stern like ballast." Tomas rubbed his face with both hands. "You give him this without proof and he'll either freeze at the wheel or start watching everyone, and either way we lose the boat to paranoia instead of water."

"And if we say nothing and the crack runs?"

"Then I fix it. Again. Better this time — I'll plate it, through-bolt it, not just epoxy. And I set a trap."

"A trap."

"Flour," Tomas said. "Bilge dusting. Fine white flour on every surface around the pumps and the stringer, brushed on dry, invisible unless you disturb it. Whoever comes down here leaves handprints, knee prints, tool marks. Flour doesn't lie and it doesn't take sides." He managed something that was almost a smile. "I'm an engineer, Priya. I don't do accusations. I do evidence."

Priya was silent a long moment. Then: "How long to plate the crack?"

"Two nights' work. I'll do it in pieces so it looks like routine maintenance."

"And the flour goes down tonight."

"Tonight."

She squeezed his shoulder in the dark — Priya, who almost never touched anyone — and started the crawl back toward the hatch. Halfway there she stopped.

"Tomas. One more thing."

"Yeah."

"Whatever we find in that flour tomorrow morning." Her voice was steady, but underneath the steadiness something was shaking, faintly, like the whisper of metal he'd heard through the stethoscope. "It's going to be someone we eat breakfast with."

* * *

He spent the day performing normalcy, and it was the hardest work he'd ever done.

He checked the pumps in plain view of the deck, whistling — actually whistling, an old Swedish shanty his grandfather had taught him, off-key on purpose because the crew expected him off-key. He greased the winch. He argued with Dev about the proper way to coil a line, the same argument they'd had eleven times, and let Dev win for the first time, because today he couldn't spare the attention to be right. Every hour he caught himself watching: Renn at the rail, motionless. Okafor reading in the shadow of the wheelhouse, page-turns regular as a metronome. Amara at the bow, hair whipping, staring at the water ahead with an expression Tomas couldn't read and had stopped trying to, because every time he tried, the expression changed into something else.

Felix came down at midday, leaned in the engine room doorway, and said, "She sounding okay?"

"She's singing," Tomas said, and watched Felix's face, and Felix smiled — tired, real, trusting — and said, "Good. Keep her humming," and went back up.

Tomas stood alone among his engines and felt the weight of it settle onto him fully for the first time: that tonight, whichever way the flour went, he would be the man who broke the Kestrel's family open. If it was Renn or Okafor, they were spies and had probably been spies since Ines's dock, and everything Ines Varga had told them was poison. If it was Amara — he couldn't finish the thought. If it was Dev, young frightened Dev who flinched at thunder — then the Syndicate had gotten to one of theirs, and God help them all. And if the flour came up clean, if no print marred the white dust by morning, then the enemy wasn't aboard at all, and the crack was just the sea being the sea, and Tomas would have spent a night poisoning his own home for nothing.

He honestly didn't know which outcome he dreaded most.

* * *

At 2300, after the dinner things were cleared and the watch rotation settled and the boat had gone soft and dim the way she did every night, Tomas went below with a flour sack under his arm and a soft brush in his pocket.

He worked slowly, methodically, an artist priming a canvas. Flour dusted across the pump housings, the deck plates, the stringer, the access ladder rungs, the lip of the hatch sill. A fine, even, innocent snowfall, half a millimeter thick, disturbed by nothing. He finished at midnight, climbed out, closed the hatch, and sat down on the ladder top with his back against the bulkhead to keep the vigil.

The Kestrel ran on through the dark. Water murmured against her sides. Somewhere below him, in the space he'd just made into a witness, the hull whispered its thin three-second song — once, then silence, then again four minutes later.

Tomas sat in the dark and listened, and waited for morning, and understood with perfect clarity that whatever the flour showed him, the Kestrel would never feel like home in quite the same way again.

At 0340, four hours into the vigil, he heard the hatch below him ease open — silently, carefully, by someone who clearly knew exactly how to lift it without a sound.

Someone was coming down.
