## 14. Monsoon Warning

Priya Anand had learned to read weather the way other people read faces — quickly, suspiciously, and with a running commentary of things she'd never say out loud. For eleven years she'd done it professionally, first for a shipping concern that treated typhoons as scheduling inconveniences, then for herself, freelancing her forecasts to anyone with a boat and the sense to pay for one. She knew what the models looked like when they were honest, and she knew what they looked like when they were lying.

The model on her screen at 0400 was lying.

She sat in the Kestrel's cramped nav station with a mug of tea gone cold beside her elbow, the cabin rocking in a long, lazy swell, and stared at the composite satellite feed she'd pulled during the last window of clear sky over the uplink buoy. The infrared layer showed cloud tops blooming over the southern basin like ink dropped in water. The water vapor loop showed the circulation tightening. And the pressure readings from three separate buoys — three, which was two more than she needed — had all begun falling at once, in concert, like singers who'd rehearsed.

She ran it again anyway. That was the ritual. Run it again, because maybe you made an error, because maybe the buoy drifted, because maybe the atmosphere would have the decency to behave like the textbooks said it should.

It didn't.

"Come on," she murmured to the screen. "You're six weeks early. Nobody invited you."

The monsoon trough had been sitting fat and lazy over the archipelago for days, disorganized, shedding rain in fitful bursts that Tomas complained about in the engine room and Felix ignored from the wheelhouse. Every forecast Priya trusted said it wouldn't consolidate for another month at least. The seasonal outlooks, the ensemble runs, the climatology going back forty years — all of it agreed: they had time. Time to reach the wreck site, time to work it, time to run for shelter before the real weather arrived.

All of it agreed right up until it didn't.

She watched the loop play through again. A cluster of thunderstorms west of the Sunda Strait had spent the evening doing nothing threatening, just pulsing in place the way tropical convection does when it's only showing off. Sometime around midnight, by the timestamps, it had stopped showing off. The cells had merged. A low-level center had spun up beneath them almost casually, the way a bar fight spins up out of a crowd, and now the whole mass was organizing with a speed that made her sit back in her chair.

Twenty-four hours ago this system didn't exist. Now it had a closed circulation, deepening convection, and a forward track that pointed like an accusation directly across their working area.

Priya did the arithmetic she didn't want to do. Distance to the target coordinates, adjusted for the Kestrel's honest speed rather than the optimistic one Felix liked to quote. Hours of daylight left for diving. Dive time itself, because Amara and Felix couldn't work the wreck in a seaway, and the seaway was about to stop being negotiable. Then the return leg, or the run to shelter, whichever the sea allowed.

The answer came out ugly no matter how she arranged the numbers.

She pulled up Ines Varga's supplied charts next — the ones with the polite lies near the target zone that she'd already flagged for Felix, the ones that still hadn't been explained — and overlaid the storm track on them. The projected path of the system crossed the wreck site in roughly seventy-two hours. Maybe eighty if the system wobbled east. It wouldn't wobble east. She could feel it not wobbling east in her bones, the same bones that had told her to reef early off Palawan three years ago when every other skipper in the anchorage laughed, and whose boats had needed new rigging afterward while hers needed a beer.

Seventy-two hours. To find a wreck nobody had found in twenty years, dive it, raise whatever they were raising, and get clear of open water before a full monsoon system parked itself on top of them.

"We don't have seventy-two hours," she told the empty cabin. "We have maybe fifty, and that's if everything goes right, and nothing has gone right since we left harbor."

The tea was cold. She drank it anyway.

* * *

She found Felix in the wheelhouse at dawn, or what passed for dawn under the leading edge of the high cloud — a bruised, sourceless light that made the sea look like hammered pewter. He had the watch, one hand on the wheel, eyes doing their slow sweep of the horizon out of habit even though the autopilot could hold the heading better than any human. There were shadows under his eyes that hadn't been there a week ago. The debt collectors' gunboat had stopped shadowing them two days back, which should have been a relief, but Priya had noticed Felix checking the radar twice as often since it disappeared. Absence, she'd learned, scared people worse than presence.

"You're up early," he said.

"I didn't go down." She set her laptop on the chart table and turned the screen toward him. "Look at this."

He looked. She watched his face do the thing it always did with bad news — a fractional tightening around the jaw, a stillness, the quick flicker of a man running numbers behind his eyes and hating every digit. He was better at hiding it than he used to be. Not good enough.

"That's—" He leaned closer. "That's organized."

"It wasn't yesterday. It wasn't twelve hours ago." She tapped the loop. "This is the monsoon consolidating weeks ahead of anything in the record. I've got three buoys falling, cloud tops at minus eighty, and a center that spun up overnight like it had somewhere to be. Best estimate, it crosses our site in seventy-two hours. Worst estimate, sixty."

"And the best case for us getting the job done?"

"Fifty. If we push hard, if the site is where Amara says it is, if the diving goes clean, if the weather holds its edge until we're clear." She let him sit with the list of ifs. "How many of those do you believe?"

He was quiet for a moment. Through the wheelhouse glass, the Kestrel rolled on the pewter swell, and somewhere below, Tomas's engine kept up its steady, asthmatic heartbeat — the heartbeat of a boat that had already given more than it had to give.

"None of them," Felix said finally. "But I've worked jobs on worse odds."

"Have you? Or have you just survived jobs that started with better ones?" She softened it with a shrug. "I'm not telling you to turn back. I'm telling you the clock changed while we slept. Everything we planned assumed we had weeks. We have days. Maybe less, if that system eats something warm and gets ideas."

Felix turned back to the horizon. When he spoke again, his voice had the flatness he got when he was making decisions instead of feeling them. "Then we change the plan. No more easy legs. We run straight for the site, day and night, and we work the wreck the moment we're on top of it. Amara's coordinates—"

"Are another problem." Priya pulled up the second overlay. "Because here's the thing that kept me up. Look at the track versus these charts Ines gave us." She traced the line with one finger. "The safe-water corridor she marked runs us along the northern edge of the shelf. Fine. Sensible. Except the corridor passes within thirty miles of where this system is going to make landfall, and the alternative route — the one her charts conveniently mark as 'unsurveyed, hazardous' — cuts south, closer to the site, and stays clear of the storm track entirely."

"You think she routed us into the weather on purpose?"

"I think," Priya said carefully, "that either Ines Varga doesn't know her own charts are wrong, or she knows exactly what they are, and both options mean we stop trusting anything she gave us. I've been replotting our approach from scratch. My sources, my bathymetry, what Amara's told us. If we take my route, we buy ourselves eight extra hours of margin. Eight hours might be the whole job."

Felix studied the screen for a long time. Outside, the light thickened, and the first spit of rain ticked against the wheelhouse windows — soft, almost apologetic, the way the sea knocks before it kicks the door in.

"Do it," he said. "Your route. And Priya — keep this between us for now. I'll tell the crew at breakfast, all of it, the timeline included. They deserve the truth before the weather gives it to them."

"They deserve the truth about the charts too."

"I know." Something moved behind his eyes — old anger, older loyalty, the war between them that Priya had watched from the nav station for years without ever being asked to referee. "One war at a time."

* * *

Breakfast was rice and tinned sausage, eaten standing up in the galley while the Kestrel worked the swell, and Priya delivered the news the way she delivered all news: plainly, with numbers, so nobody could mistake the shape of it.

She laid it out on the galley table — the satellite loop, the falling pressures, the track line drawn in red across the chart. She watched each face as she talked. Tomas took it the way he took everything, arms crossed, jaw working, his eyes going instinctively toward the deck above as if he could assess the hull's opinion of coming weather through sheer professional suspicion. Amara went very still, the way she did whenever the wreck came up, her fingers resting flat on the table like a woman steadying herself against a rail. And Ines—

Ines stood at the edge of the group, arms folded, expression composed, and Priya watched her look at the red line and show absolutely nothing. Not surprise. Not calculation. Nothing. Which was, in Priya's experience, the most alarming possible response to being shown a storm that had materialized overnight directly across your salvage plan.

Either the woman had known. Or she was very, very good at looking like she had. Priya genuinely could not tell which, and the not-knowing sat in her stomach like ballast.

"So that's the situation," Felix finished. "We run hard for the site on Priya's routing. We dive as soon as we're anchored. And when the box is aboard, we run for shelter and we don't look back. Questions?"

"The hull," Tomas said immediately. "You want to push her hard for two days straight. I've got hairline fractures from the squall that I patched, not fixed. She'll hold at cruising speed. At flank speed in building seas, I can't promise anything."

"How much can you promise?"

Tomas grimaced. "I can promise she'll complain the entire way. Whether she holds is between her and God."

"Then keep her complaining quietly," Felix said. "Amara — your coordinates. How confident are you?"

Amara lifted her gaze from the chart, and Priya saw something settle in her face, some decision arriving fully formed. "Confident enough to bet my life on them. I already have, several times." She touched the red line, the point where it crossed the reef. "My father fished these waters for thirty years. He knew the bottom there like you know your own kitchen. If his notes say the Halcyon Star sits in the channel mouth below the twin heads, then she's there."

"And the weather window?"

"Forty-eight hours of workable sea state after we arrive, if your forecast holds." Amara glanced at Priya. "Does it?"

"It holds," Priya said. "It's everything else I'm worried about."

There was a silence then, brief and loaded, and Priya saw Ines uncross her arms and step forward with the smooth economy of a woman who had decided, precisely, how much truth to spend.

"There's something you should know," Ines said. "About the charts."

Every head turned. Priya kept her expression neutral and felt her pulse pick up anyway.

"I commissioned them from a survey firm in Singapore four years ago," Ines went on. "For a different job. The errors near the target zone aren't deliberate — they're inherited. The firm reused old hydrographic data to cut costs, data that predates the channel dredging. Priya's reroute is correct. I should have had them verified before handing them over, and I didn't, because I was in a hurry." She paused, and something flickered behind the composure — too fast to name. "That's the whole of it. I don't lie to people I'm paying."

"You lied to me for ten years," Felix said quietly. "Free of charge."

The words landed in the galley and stayed there. Ines held Felix's stare without flinching, and Priya, watching, thought: *there it is.* Not the whole story. A payment made on account. Enough truth to buy silence, not enough to buy trust.

"Then consider this a down payment on the other nine years," Ines said. "Captain. Your call. Do we run your route?"

Felix looked at Priya. She nodded once.

"My route," he said. "Full run. All hands to stations in one hour."

* * *

The hour became the longest of the voyage so far, though nothing happened in it. That was the thing about clocks like this one, Priya thought, as she stood her watch in the nav station with the rain ticking harder against the glass — nothing happened, and everything changed. The Kestrel turned onto the new heading and began to eat distance. The engine note rose half a tone. The swell, out of the northeast now, took on a longer, darker rhythm, waves arriving from somewhere deeper and colder than the local wind could explain, messengers sent ahead of the main body.

By midday the barometer had fallen four millibars. By midafternoon, seven. Priya logged each reading in her neat, small handwriting and felt the numbers accumulate like sand in an hourglass turning over faster than it should.

At 1600, the uplink caught a fresh burst of data before the cloud deck swallowed the sky, and she saw the thing she'd been dreading: the system had strengthened again. The center had tightened. The forward speed had increased. Her seventy-two-hour estimate shed six hours in a single update, and the red line on her chart crept westward toward them like something patient and certain.

She did the arithmetic one final time, knowing it wouldn't improve, and it didn't. Fifty hours to the site. Thirty-six hours of workable conditions on arrival. A margin so thin you could read a chart through it.

She carried the update to the wheelhouse, where Felix stood his watch with the rain streaming down the windows and the world beyond reduced to gray-on-gray. She put the new numbers in front of him without ceremony. He read them in silence.

"Sixty-six hours," he said at last.

"Sixty-six," she agreed. "And shrinking."

He looked out at the darkening water, and Priya watched him do the thing she'd seen him do a hundred times in a hundred weathers — square himself against the sea as if it were an opponent with a face. But this time, for just a moment, the mask slipped, and underneath it she saw something she'd never seen in Felix Eriksen before.

Fear. Not of the storm. Of the clock.

"Then we'd better not waste a minute of them," he said, and rang down for full ahead.

Below, the engine answered, and the Kestrel leaned into the building sea, running for the wreck and the window both, while behind them the monsoon came on like a debt collector of its own — patient, implacable, and closing.
