## 15. Confessions at the Wheel

The watch change came at midnight, and Felix found Ines already at the helm when he climbed the companionway.

She'd taken his jacket. Not stolen it — taken it, from the hook by the galley door, because the wind had turned and come off the water cold, and she was wearing it like she'd always owned it. She stood with one hand on the wheel and the other holding a mug of something that steamed, and the Kestrel ran easy under a triple-reefed main, and for a moment — just a moment — Felix forgot to be angry at her.

"You're early," she said, without turning around.

"You're in my spot."

"Your spot." She glanced back at him. The moon was three-quarters full and laid a silver road across the swells behind them, and her face was half light, half shadow. "You've been standing there for eleven days. I've been sailing these waters since before you could spell *archipelago*."

"That's not true."

"No," she admitted. "But you checked."

He took the mug from her hand without asking. Coffee, cut with condensed milk, sweet enough to be dessert. She'd made two. He noticed that and decided not to notice it.

"Tomas says the patch is holding," he said.

"Tomas says the patch is holding *for now*, which is a different sentence entirely."

"It's always a different sentence on this boat."

Ines laughed — short, surprised out of her, as if she hadn't meant to let it escape. She surrendered the wheel and moved aside, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, and then deliberately didn't, stepping back to lean against the rail instead. Felix put his hands where hers had been. The wood was still warm from her grip.

The sea ran long and black beneath them. Somewhere off to the east, lightning flickered inside a cloud bank with no sound attached, silent heat lightning stitching the horizon like a seam about to split.

"Priya's storm," Ines said, following his eyes. "How many hours?"

"She won't say. That's how I know it's bad. When Priya stops giving numbers, the number is bad."

"Mm." Ines sipped from a second mug she'd apparently brought up for herself, because of course she had. "Your father used to say the same thing about his navigators. If they went quiet, batten down."

Felix's hands tightened on the wheel. "Don't."

"Don't what?"

"You know what."

"I know a great many things, Felix. It's my defining flaw." She turned to face the bow, hair whipping loose from its tie. "I'm not going to apologize for saying his name. He's the reason we're both on this boat, whether you admit it or not."

"The reason I'm on this boat is debt."

"The reason you're on this boat," Ines said, "is that when I said the words *Halcyon Star* in that bar in Tonkin, your face did something it hasn't done in fifteen years. You looked alive. Angry-alive, grief-alive, but alive. I could have offered you double and it wouldn't have mattered. I could have offered you nothing and you'd have come."

He wanted to tell her she was wrong. He opened his mouth to do it and found nothing there but the truth, which was inconvenient.

"Why me?" he said instead. "That's the part I keep coming back to. You've got money, Ines. You've got salvage crews who'd swim down barehanded for a fraction of what you're paying me. There are thirty men in that harbor who don't flinch when someone says my father's name. So why hire the one man whose hands shake every time we get near those coordinates?"

The question hung between them. Below decks, faint through the hull, Tomas's engine turned over in its steady rhythm, the heartbeat of the ship. A flying fish slapped the water somewhere off the port beam.

Ines set her mug on the rail. Carefully, precisely, the way she did everything.

"Because I knew your mother," she said.

The wheel felt suddenly very far away from Felix's hands, or his hands very far from it. The boat ran on without him for a second before he hauled his attention back.

"What?"

"Elena Eriksen." Ines watched him say it, watched the name land, and didn't look away. "I knew her. Before you were born. Before the Halcyon Star. Before any of it."

"That's not possible. My father never—"

"Your father never told you anything, that's true. That was his whole religion, wasn't it? Silence. Burn the charts, bury the names, raise the boy on nothing." Her voice stayed level, but something underneath it had begun to move, slow and tectonic. "My family ran supply boats out of Makassar. My father traded with survey vessels all through the archipelago. The Halcyon Star provisioned with us twice. Elena used to sit on our dock and drink ginger tea and complain that your father loved his instruments more than her."

Felix's mouth was dry. "She died when I was nine."

"I know."

"I don't remember her voice anymore. Isn't that—" He stopped. Started again. "Isn't that something. Fifteen years and I can't—"

"Ginger tea," Ines said quietly. "She took it too sweet. She laughed before she finished jokes, so the punchlines got lost. And she was afraid of deep water, which everyone thought was funny, because she married a man who lived on it."

Felix stared at her. The Kestrel rose on a swell and slid down the far side, and the moonlight shifted across Ines's face, and he saw — for the first time since the bar in Tonkin, maybe for the first time ever — that she wasn't performing. There was no angle visible. Just a woman on a deck at night telling him things he had no right to hear and no way to unhear.

"Why are you telling me this now?" His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Eleven days. We've been out here eleven days and you've been on my boat—"

"On Ines's boat. The Kestrel is collateral, technically."

"*Why now?*"

"Because tomorrow night we make the final approach." She nodded east, toward the silent lightning. "Because after tomorrow, everything changes, and I wanted — no." She corrected herself with visible effort. "Because I owed her a truth, and I've been paying it out in installments like a coward."

"What truth?"

Ines was quiet for a long time. The wind filled the silence, and the rigging sang its thin high note, and Felix let her take the time because he understood — he'd spent fifteen years not saying things too.

"The Halcyon Star didn't sink the way the reports say," she said finally.

"Reports say she went down in a squall. Structural failure, wrong place, wrong time."

"Yes. That's what reports say." She turned to look at him fully. "Elena was the one who flagged the structural concerns. Did you know that? Your mother read hull surveys the way your father read charts. She found the fatigue cracking in the frames six months before the ship sailed her last run. She filed reports. She pulled strings. She *screamed* at people, from what my father said, and the people she screamed at were —" Ines stopped.

"Were who?"

"Were the same people whose patents and charts are sitting in a strongbox at the bottom of the Sunda Strait right now. Or so we hope." Ines picked up her mug, found it empty, and held it anyway, like an anchor. "Felix. Your mother didn't die in an accident. I'm not saying I know what happened. I'm saying the word *accident* has never sat right with anyone who actually knew that ship, and I've carried that for fifteen years, and I am so tired of carrying it alone."

The sea said nothing. The engine said nothing. Felix heard his own pulse.

"My father burned the charts," he said at last. "On the beach. I watched him do it. He swore we'd never go back, and I thought — I thought it was grief. I built my whole life on the idea that it was grief."

"And now?"

"Now I think my father knew exactly what killed her." The words came out of him like something being extracted. "And he couldn't live next to the knowledge, so he buried it at sea and called it mourning."

Ines didn't confirm it. He watched her not confirm it — watched her hold whatever she knew behind her teeth, and understood that she was doing it on purpose, and hated her, and was grateful, all at once.

"There's more," he said. "There's more you're not telling me."

"There's more I'm not telling anyone. Including myself, possibly." She gave him a thin smile. "When we open that box, if we open that box, you'll understand why some truths need to arrive all at once or not at all. Piece by piece, they just wound people."

"That's a coward's philosophy."

"Yes," she agreed pleasantly. "I told you. Defining flaw."

A cloud crossed the moon and the world went dark except for the compass light, the small amber glow of the card swinging gently under glass. Felix looked at it — his own compass, bolted at the helm, the cheap modern kind — and thought of the other one, the broken one, locked in the drawer beneath his bunk where it had traveled for fifteen years without once being opened.

"Ines." He kept his eyes on the compass. "Why do you really want what's in that box? Not the money version. The real version."

For a moment he thought she wouldn't answer. Then she said, softly, "Someone I loved was on that ship too. And the ledgers of the world say he was cargo. Ballast. A name in a passenger manifest nobody's read since. I want the box because I want the manifest. I want his name written somewhere that isn't a filing cabinet in Jakarta." She exhaled. "That's the real version. Are you satisfied?"

It was the most honest thing she'd said in eleven days, and it undid something in Felix's chest that he'd been bracing shut since Tonkin.

"No," he said. "But I believe you. Which frightens me more."

They stood together at the wheel after that, not speaking, shoulder to shoulder now because the wind had risen and the space between them had gotten cold. Around two, Priya came up to report a pressure drop — four millibars in an hour, faster than the models — and Felix heard her pause when she saw Ines beside him, and say nothing about it, and go back below.

At three, the first edge of the storm system reached them: a rising moan in the rigging, a new texture to the swell, longer and steeper, waves arriving from two directions at once like a crowd beginning to argue. Tomas would hate it. Tomas would be down there right now listening to his engine and his hull and cataloguing every new sound.

"Felix." Ines had to raise her voice against the wind now. "One more thing. About tomorrow."

"If it's another half-truth—"

"It's a warning." She gripped the rail, looking east into the dark where the lightning had gotten closer, louder, each flash now followed by a delayed grumble of thunder rolling across the water. "Whatever you find down there — in the box, in the wreck, in your father's handwriting — remember that he loved you. Whatever else is true, start from that. Because if you don't, the bottom of the Sunda Strait will take the rest of your life, and I've seen what that looks like from the outside."

Then she pushed off the rail, retrieved her mugs, and went below, leaving him the watch and the jacket and the storm coming on.

Felix steered into the dark. At 3:40, Amara appeared silently at his elbow with fresh coffee, and pointed without a word at the eastern horizon, where a wall of cloud had begun to organize itself with terrible patience.

And at 4:15, Priya's voice came up the hatch, tight and flat:

"Felix. You need to see this. The Syndicate gunboat just lit up on radar — twelve miles out, dead astern, and she's gaining."
