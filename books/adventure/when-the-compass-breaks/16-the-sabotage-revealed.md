## 16. The Sabotage Revealed

Amara had learned to sleep in pieces. On the skiff, crossing from island to island for years, she'd trained herself to wake at any change — a shift in engine note, a change in the slap of water against hull. Her body kept its own watch even when her mind was elsewhere, and so it was no surprise that at three in the morning, somewhere in the gray teeth of the Sunda Sea, she came awake all at once because the Kestrel's rhythm had changed.

Not the sea. The sea was doing what it always did — long confused swell out of the southwest, the monsoon's scouts already testing the coastline ahead of the main army. No, it was something underneath her bunk, a vibration that hadn't been there when she closed her eyes. A shudder. Faint, arrhythmic, like a heartbeat with a murmur.

She lay still and listened.

The crew slept around her in the narrow berths: Tomas snoring like a landslide in the next compartment, Priya's breathing thin and quick behind the chart table curtain, Felix up top on watch alone. She'd catalogued them all in the weeks since they'd pulled her half-drowned off the supply dock — their sounds, their habits, their tells. Felix ground his teeth in his sleep. Priya talked to herself in three languages. And Tomas, who trusted everyone until given one reason not to, snored with the total abandon of a man who believed the world was basically kind.

One of them, Amara thought, is lying about who they are.

She'd known since the charts. Since the night she'd stood behind Priya and watched the navigator's finger trace the deliberate wrongness near the target zone — errors too clean to be accident, errors that steered them wide of the Halcyon Star by eleven miles. Someone aboard had access to Ines's sealed documents. Someone aboard had been reporting position. And someone aboard, she suspected now, had been working on the boat itself, because the Kestrel's hull had been failing faster than any honest sea could explain.

The vibration came again. Underneath. Forward of the engine room, down where the bilge pumps lived in their cramped steel cathedral of pipes and standing water.

Amara slid out of her bunk without a sound. Years of practice: weight on the outside edges of her feet, hand on the bulkhead, breath shallow. She wore shorts and a salt-stiffened shirt and nothing else, and she took the dive knife from her kit — not because she wanted to use it, but because she'd survived this long by never being the unarmed person in the room.

The passage aft was black except for the red glow of the compass binnacle light bleeding down the companionway stairs. Above her, through the deck, she could hear Felix's footsteps on watch, slow and regular, a man pacing his own doubts. Below, the vibration pulsed again, and now she could hear something else beneath it — a wet mechanical sound, metal worrying against metal, the sound of a machine being persuaded to fail.

The bilge compartment was accessed through a hatch just forward of the engine room, a space barely tall enough to kneel in, lit by a single caged bulb that someone had left burning low. Amara put her eye to the gap in the hatch first. Old habit. You looked before you entered anything in this life.

A man knelt in the bilge water, back to her, sleeves rolled, working on the secondary pump's intake coupling with a wrench and something else — a small file, maybe, or a hacksaw blade. He worked with the calm efficiency of someone doing a job he'd done many times, in many places, for people whose names he probably didn't know. His shirt was dark. His hair was cropped close. He moved without hurry.

Ravi.

The crew's newest man, signed on at the last port, quiet, competent, forgettable on purpose. Amara had watched him for days without knowing why, some animal part of her brain flagging what her conscious mind missed: Ravi never ate with his back to a door. Ravi checked the horizon every time he came topside, not like a sailor but like a scout. Ravi's hands were soft in the way of a man who'd only recently started pretending to work for a living.

And now Ravi was kneeling in the bilge at three in the morning, sawing through the intake coupling of the only pump that would matter if the patched hull let go.

Amara felt the cold move through her, the old cold, the one that lived in her since the day the official letter arrived with her brother's name on it and the word *lost* doing all the lying. This was how it happened. Not in storms. Not in dramatic moments on the open sea. It happened in the dark, in the small hours, by the hands of patient men with files and wrenches, while everyone who trusted them slept.

Her family had died that way. A survey vessel, an "accident," a company that needed the insurance more than the people. She knew exactly what patient men looked like.

She should go get Felix. That was the smart play. Wake him quietly, bring him down here, catch Ravi red-handed with witnesses. But Felix was alone on watch, and between her and the deck were twelve feet of companionway, and Ravi was maybe ninety seconds from finishing whatever he was doing to that coupling. If the pump died tonight — if it died during the monsoon that Priya swore was coming early — the Kestrel would fill and founder with all of them aboard, and the Syndicate would collect its debts from the insurance, and the sea would keep its secrets one more time.

No. Some things you didn't delegate.

Amara pushed the hatch open and stepped down into the bilge.

* * *

The water was knee-deep and warm as blood, sloshing against her thighs as she crossed the compartment. Ravi heard her — of course he did; nobody moved silently through bilge water — but he didn't startle. That was the thing she noticed even then, even with her heart slamming: he didn't startle. He simply set down the wrench, turned, and looked at her with the mild expression of a man interrupted at his devotions.

"You should be asleep," he said.

"So should the pump."

He smiled. It was almost a good smile, warm at the edges, and she understood suddenly how he'd gotten aboard, how he'd gotten close, how he'd sat at their table eating Tomas's cooking and laughing at Priya's jokes. Men like this were made of borrowed warmth.

"It's maintenance," he said. "Coupling was rattling. I couldn't sleep either."

"Then you won't mind showing me your hands."

Something shifted behind his eyes — not guilt, nothing so human as guilt. Recalculation. She watched him weigh her, the knife, the distance, the depth of the water, and she watched him arrive at a number he liked.

"Amara." He said her name gently, the way you'd speak to someone standing too close to a ledge. "You've got a claim on that wreck. I know. I've read your file. You lost family — I'm sorry, truly. But you're a guest on this boat, and these people are professionals, and whatever you think you're seeing—"

"I'm seeing a man cut a bilge pump's throat."

The file was in his right hand, tucked low against his thigh, and she'd tracked it the whole time without looking at it directly, the way you track a snake in grass. When he moved, he moved the way she'd known he would — fast, low, going for the knife arm first, because he'd read her file too and knew she was a diver and divers led with their hands.

But she wasn't standing in grass. She was standing in water, and water was hers.

She dropped her weight as he lunged, let his momentum carry him past her into the bulkhead, and drove her shoulder into his hip as he staggered. They went down together into the bilge, and the world became noise and iron and brackish spray. The knife spun away somewhere. His elbow found her cheekbone and white light cracked across her vision. She grabbed his wrist with both hands and held on, because the file was in that hand, and holding on was the oldest skill she had.

"Help!" she screamed, and the word bounced off steel. "FELIX! BELOW!"

Ravi was stronger than her — heavier, trained, fed better than she'd ever been. He got a forearm across her throat and pressed, and the bilge water surged around them as they thrashed, and above the roar of her own pulse she heard boots on the companionway stairs, Felix's voice shouting something, Tomas's deeper bellow answering.

Ravi heard it too. And he made his choice.

He stopped fighting her and started fighting the boat. His free hand shot out — not for the file, not for her — for the primary pump's discharge valve, wrenched it open, and jammed the coupling he'd been cutting sideways into the housing. Metal shrieked. Water hammered. Somewhere below them the intake line let go with a sound like a bone breaking, and cold pressure flooded the compartment as the sea reached in through the wound he'd made.

"You're all dead anyway," he said into her face, conversational, almost kind. "Monsoon's early. Hull's compromised. One way or another, this boat goes down, and the box stays where it is. Nobody steals from Meridian twice."

Then the hatch burst open and Felix came down the ladder like weather, and behind him Tomas filled the entire frame, and Ravi did the last calculation of his employment.

He headbutted her — short, brutal, professional — and in the white blindness that followed she felt his body tear away from hers, heard him scramble up the ladder past Felix, heard Felix shout and grab and miss. By the time her vision cleared, Amara was kneeling in rising water with blood sheeting into her left eye, and the afterdeck hatch was banging in the wind, and Ravi was gone over the side into the black Sunda Sea, leaving nothing behind but a duffel of cash under his bunk, a radio transmitter wired into the galley wall, and a boat that was dying faster than it had been an hour ago.

* * *

They fought the flooding for two hours.

Tomas commanded the engine room like a man defending his own house, screaming for wedges and epoxy and the sailcloth patch they'd sworn they'd never need this soon. Priya rerouted power with one hand and held a flashlight with her teeth. Felix worked beside them all, soaked, silent, hauling on the pump rebuild with hands that didn't shake until afterward — Amara noticed that, filed it away. He shook afterward, alone, where no one could see. So did she.

Her cheekbone was split to the bone. Tomas stitched it at the chart table by lamplight, his big fingers surprisingly gentle, refusing anesthesia on the grounds that the good stuff was for emergencies. "This is an emergency," Amara said, and he said, "This is Tuesday," and threaded the needle again.

When it was done, when the pumps were running rough but running, when the Kestrel limped southeast under a bruised sky toward coordinates that Amara had finally, fully committed to memory, Felix sat down across from her. He looked ten years older than he had at dinner. There was bilge grease in his hair and exhaustion in the lines of his face, and he set two cups of coffee down and pushed one across the table to her like a treaty offering.

"You saved the boat," he said.

"I saved the pump. The boat's still deciding."

His mouth twitched — almost a laugh, strangled at birth. He looked at the transmitter they'd pried from the galley wall, sitting on the chart table between them like a dead insect. "He was reporting our position. The whole time. The gunboat, the deadlines — they always knew where we were because he told them." Felix turned the device over once, then set it down as if it burned. "Ines gave us this crew list. Which means—"

"Don't finish that sentence yet." Amara sipped the coffee. It was terrible and perfect. "Ines might have sold you out. Or Ines might have been sold out herself. Men like Ravi don't stay bought by one buyer. Right now you can't afford to be wrong about which enemy is pointing at you."

"And you?" Felix looked at her then, really looked, and she saw the question underneath the question. "Why'd you go down there alone? You could've woken me. Could've died down there and we'd have found you in the morning."

Amara touched her stitches, feeling the tender ridge of them, the new geography of her own face. She thought about the letter in her kit bag, the one naming her family as partners betrayed, the one she hadn't shown anyone. She thought about trust, and how it was a currency she'd stopped carrying years ago, and how tonight she'd spent some anyway, alone in the dark, on a boat full of strangers.

"Because if I'd waited," she said, "we'd have drowned waiting. And because—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Because I've spent nine years watching a boat sink from the outside. Tonight I got to be on the inside, holding it together. I don't know what that makes me. Crew, maybe."

Felix was quiet a long moment. Outside, the wind had begun to rise, a long low moan in the rigging that none of them wanted to name yet. Priya appeared in the companionway, laptop under her arm, her face gray in the lamplight.

"Felix," she said. "You need to see the satellite feed. The monsoon isn't coming early anymore." She swallowed. "It's here."

Behind her, through the porthole, the first real rain began to fall on the Sunda Sea — fat, warm, relentless drops, the opening volley of everything that was coming.
