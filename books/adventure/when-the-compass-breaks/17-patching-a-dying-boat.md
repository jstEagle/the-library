## 17. Patching a Dying Boat

The sea had a voice for every mood, and Tomas Okonkwo had learned them all across thirty years of listening. There was the lullaby of fair weather, the mutter of a rising swell, the flat gray silence that came before the worst of it. And there was this — the sound the Kestrel was making now, a low groaning along her seams like an old man climbing stairs he knew would kill him.

He lay on his back in the bilge with a headlamp strapped to his forehead, one hand flat against the hull plating, and listened the way a doctor listens to a failing heart.

*There.* Starboard side, just aft of the engine mounts, where the sabotage had torn things loose and the first squall had done the rest. A hairline crack that had been the length of his forearm two days ago now ran nearly the width of his chest. And it was widening faster than it should. Metal didn't fail like that on its own. Metal failed like that when the boat was working too hard, when the sea was working against her, when every wave was a hammer blow and every hammer blow found the same weak place.

He pressed his thumb against the crack and felt water weeping through it, cold and insistent, like a finger testing a lock.

"Tomas." Priya's voice came down the hatch. "How bad?"

"Come see. Bring the light."

She came down the ladder with the economy of movement she brought to everything, crouched beside him, and swept her lamp along the seam. Her face did the thing it always did when she was calculating — went completely still, eyes fixed on nothing, the arithmetic running behind them.

"That's triple the growth rate from yesterday," she said.

"I know."

"At this rate—"

"I know, Priya."

She sat back on her heels. The bilge water sloshed around their knees, an inch of it, maybe two, and it had that particular smell — diesel and salt and something faintly organic — that Tomas had spent his whole career trying to keep out of boats and was now sitting in up to his shins.

"We need to patch it," she said. "Properly. Not the epoxy-and-prayer job we did at the supply stop."

"We don't have a proper patch. We don't have a yard, a welder, or a dry dock. We have sailcloth, epoxy, and whatever's in the scrap bin." He wiped his hands on his trousers, which accomplished nothing. "So we build a proper patch out of improper things. That's the whole job now. That's the entire rest of my life, probably."

Priya almost smiled. Almost. "Felix is on deck. He wants a report."

"Felix can come down here and put his hands on it," Tomas said, and then heard how it sounded — like a complaint, like a mutiny in miniature — and didn't take it back. "I mean it. He should see it. He should feel it. This is his boat."

"He knows it's his boat."

"He knows it's his *responsibility*. Different thing." Tomas hauled himself up using the engine mount, his knees cracking in protest. "A man can own a boat his whole life and never once put his shoulder against her hull and ask her to hold. Time he did."

* * *

Felix came down the ladder ten minutes later, and Tomas watched him do it — watched the way he braced, the way his eyes went immediately to the waterline mark stenciled inside the hull, the way his jaw tightened when he saw how high the water had crept.

"Show me," Felix said.

Tomas showed him. Took his hand — the man flinched, just slightly, at being handled, and Tomas pretended not to notice — and pressed Felix's palm flat against the crack, right where the weeping was worst.

"Feel that?"

"The water."

"The water, and the flex. Every wave, the hull works. Opens and closes like a mouth. Epoxy alone can't survive that — it's rigid, it'll pop free in an hour. So we don't fight the flex. We let the patch flex with it."

Felix kept his hand on the hull. Tomas watched him listen — really listen, the way Tomas had taught apprentices to listen, back in the yards at Cebu, back before everything. The boat groaned. Felix's face changed.

"She's talking," he said quietly.

"She's been talking for days. Nobody was listening."

"I was listening to the wrong things." Felix pulled his hand back and looked at it, at the bilge water glistening on his palm. "The debt. Ines. The Syndicate. All of it up there." He nodded toward the deck, toward the sky beyond it, where the barometer had been falling like a stone all morning. "Meanwhile the actual problem is down here, and it's the size of my chest."

"That's usually how it goes," Tomas said. "Boats are honest. Everything else lies."

Felix crouched down, getting his knees in the bilge water without hesitation, and Tomas felt something shift in his chest that he didn't have a name for. Thirty years an engineer, and he'd worked for owners of every species — owners who never came below, owners who came below to blame, owners who came below to be seen coming below. Felix Eriksen, son of the man who'd built the Kestrel's keel, was crouching in his own bilge with his sleeves pushed up, and he was asking:

"Tell me exactly what you need."

* * *

What Tomas needed, it turned out, was everything they had, arranged in an order nobody had ever tried.

The plan came together on the deck plates over the next hour, drawn in grease pencil on a sheet of torn chart by lamplight, while the Kestrel rolled and the wind outside climbed from a complaint to an argument. The crack ran through a stressed seam, so the patch couldn't be a plate — it had to be a membrane. Something that could move. Sailcloth, laminated in epoxy, laid in overlapping courses like shingles, backed by a flexible bedding compound, and held in place not by bolts through the hull — *never* bolts through the hull, that was how you turned a crack into a hole — but by pressure. An internal frame, braced against the stringers, pressing the patch outward from the inside.

"Like a bandage," Amara said.

They all turned. She was standing at the foot of the ladder with a roll of sailcloth under one arm and her injured arm still strapped tight against her ribs — the arm the Syndicate's agent had nearly taken from her, two nights ago, in the struggle that ended with a man going over the side. She was supposed to be resting. She had the particular expression of a woman who had been told to rest and had decided, privately, that rest was for other people.

"Like a bandage," Tomas agreed. "You've done hull work?"

"I've done *everything* work. Small boats, small budgets." She came forward, moving carefully, and knelt by the crack with the unselfconscious authority of someone who had spent her life with her hands in things. "My father's fishing boat took a coral strike when I was fourteen. We patched it with canvas and tree resin off the coast of Panay and it held for six years." She looked up. "It can be done. It can hold. But the surface prep has to be perfect, and the surface prep has to happen *now*, while we still have light and calm enough to work."

"Calm," Tomas repeated. "You've seen the sky."

"I've seen worse skies." She said it without bravado, the way you'd say *I've had worse colds*, and Tomas believed her completely. Whatever this woman had lived through — and the letter in the strongbox, the disaster, the family she'd lost to the same water that had taken Felix's mother — it had made her unimpressable. "The question isn't the sky. The question is whether this crew can work together for six hours without killing each other."

Felix stood up. Bilge water ran off his knees.

"Then we'd better start," he said.

* * *

They started with the grinding.

It was the worst job and Tomas gave it to himself, as was his right and his custom — a right-angle grinder with a cutting disk, working the seam clean down to bright metal while sparks sprayed and the noise filled the engine room like a scream. Priya ran the boat from the helm above, holding her as steady as she could into the quartering swell, calling down adjustments every time the motion changed. Amara cut sailcloth into overlapping panels with a razor knife, her strapped arm held close, her good hand doing the work of two with a patience that looked like it had been carved out of her by hand. And Felix — Felix took the mixing.

The epoxy was a two-part marine compound, old stock, and it wanted to be mixed in precise ratios, in small batches, in the narrow window before it began its cure. It was finicky, exacting, unforgiving work, and Tomas fully expected the owner's son to hand it off to someone else after ten minutes.

He didn't. Felix mixed batch after batch for six hours, measuring by weight on the little galley scale, stirring with a paint stick in a cut-down jug, and when his first batch kicked off too fast in the heat and hardened into a useless lump in his hands, he didn't swear, didn't blame the compound, didn't blame Tomas. He looked at the lump, looked at his hands, and said, "Smaller batches. Faster application. My fault — I was stretching it."

"Most men would've said the epoxy was bad," Tomas said.

"The epoxy's fine. I was slow." Felix was already weighing out the next batch. "I've been slow my whole life, apparently. Let's not make it a habit."

The work found its rhythm, and the rhythm was the thing Tomas had been chasing his whole career and finding only in fragments — the moment when a crew stops being a collection of people and becomes a single organism with many hands. Priya's voice from above, calm, metronomic: *swell's shifting, ten seconds, hold your work.* Amara's hands laying the first course of cloth, smoothing it against the bedding compound with a roller improvised from a broom handle and a paint tin. Tomas driving the fasteners of the brace frame, checking torque by feel, the way his father had taught him in a Lagos shipyard before he was tall enough to see over the workbench. Felix passing the epoxy forward, batch after batch, always on time, always the right consistency, learning the cure curve by feel within two hours the way some men never learn it in twenty years.

Somewhere in the fourth hour, Tomas realized he was singing.

He did that — an old habit, work songs from the yards, low and wordless, half hum and half chant, something to pace the hands by. Usually people told him to stop. Nobody told him to stop now. And then, unbelievably, Amara picked up the line — a different song, something in a language he didn't know, a fishing song maybe, a hauling song, her voice low and rough and perfectly in time with his — and the two songs braided together in the engine room like the cloth courses on the hull, and Felix looked up from his mixing jug with an expression Tomas had never seen on him before.

"What?" Felix said, catching him looking.

"Nothing." Tomas went back to the brace frame. "Just trying to remember the last time this boat sounded like this."

"When?"

"Never," Tomas said. "That's the point."

* * *

The sea grew uglier on schedule.

By the fifth hour the swell had organized itself into long, steep, gray-green walls, and the Kestrel was climbing and falling down them with a corkscrew motion that made the engine room swing like a bell. Priya's calls from above came faster. The barometer had dropped thirty millibars since dawn. Somewhere to the southwest, a wall of cloud was assembling itself with the patience of something that had done this ten thousand times and had never once been in a hurry.

"Monsoon's early," Priya called down. "I told Felix. We have maybe a day, day and a half, before it's on top of us."

"Then we'd better be watertight by tomorrow night," Tomas called back, and drove another fastener home.

The patch went on course by course. First the bedding compound, troweled thick over the ground-out seam. Then the cloth, wet out with epoxy until it went translucent and clung like skin, each panel overlapping the last by half its width. Then another layer of compound, another course of cloth, until there were five layers built up over the crack and the whole assembly was a flexible armor plate the size of a car door. Last, the brace frame — three lengths of salvaged channel iron, cut and drilled that afternoon, pressed against the patch by threaded rods run back to the stringers, torqued down not to crush the patch but to kiss it, to give it just enough backing to survive the hammer of the sea.

Tomas torqued the final rod, checked it twice, and then did the thing he always did at the end of a repair that mattered. He lay down in the bilge, put his ear against the new patch, and listened.

The sea hammered. The hull flexed. The patch moved with it — a small, living give, like a heartbeat — and no water came through.

"Well?" Felix's voice, from directly above him, upside down, hanging over the engine room floor grating.

Tomas sat up. Bilge water was down to a few millimeters now, the pumps winning for the first time in three days. "She'll hold."

"You're sure?"

"I'm never sure. I'm an engineer. Certainty is for priests." He climbed to his feet, knees screaming, back a single line of fire, and looked at the patch — at *their* patch — glowing faintly under the work lamp, five layers of cloth and compound and stubbornness. "But she'll hold. Long enough."

"Long enough for what?"

Tomas looked at him. Felix was soaked in epoxy to the elbows, gray to the shoulders with ground-metal dust, a smear of bedding compound across one cheekbone like a war paint stripe gone wrong. He looked terrible. He looked, Tomas thought, like a man who had finally arrived somewhere.

"Long enough for whatever you're planning," Tomas said. "Because I've been on this boat eleven years, and I know the difference between *we're running for port* and *we're running from something*. You've got the second look."

Felix didn't answer right away. Above them, through the deck plates, they heard Priya's feet moving to the helm, and the creak of the wheel, and the wind outside rising another notch, finding its voice.

"Get some rest," Felix said finally. "All of you. Eat something. Tomorrow we're going to need everything this boat has left."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got right now." Felix put his hand flat on the hull, over the patch, the way Tomas had shown him — listening again. "But I'll tell you this. Whatever's coming, she holds. *We* hold. That part's done." He looked up, and for a second, in the work lamp's yellow light, he almost smiled. "You built us a boat that'll hold, Tomas."

"I built you a *patch*," Tomas said. "The boat's been holding this crew together for years. She was just waiting for you to notice."

Felix climbed the ladder and was gone, and Tomas stood alone in the engine room for a moment, listening to the new patch flex and hold, flex and hold, the boat breathing under his palm.

Then the lights flickered.

Once. Twice. A brownout stutter, the work lamps dimming to embers and flaring back — and Tomas went cold in a way the bilge water never could, because he knew every sound and shudder this boat could make, and that wasn't one of them. That was the electrical system. That was something drawing power it shouldn't, or failing to deliver power it should.

He was already reaching for his tools when the lights flickered again — longer this time — and somewhere aft, deep in the wiring looms where no one had any business being, he heard a sound he liked even less.

A click. Rhythmic. Patient.

Like something switching itself on and off.
