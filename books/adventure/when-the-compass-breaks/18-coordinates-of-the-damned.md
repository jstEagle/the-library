## 18. Coordinates of the Damned

The numbers came up on Priya's screen at 0417, and I felt them in my chest before I understood them in my head.

She'd been quiet all night, working the plot alone at the chart table, cross-checking Amara's coordinates against the satellite fixes, against the bottom soundings, against the one thing Ines had given us that wasn't a lie — the wreck's last known position from a Lloyd's registry entry forty years old. Three sources. Three lines converging. When they finally crossed, Priya didn't call out like she usually did, didn't bang her mug on the table or holler for Tomas to log the fix. She just turned and looked at me, and the look said everything before she said a word.

"Felix." Her voice was careful, the way you talk when you're carrying something breakable across a rolling deck. "You need to see this."

I came down off the helm watch early, which I never do, and stood behind her chair while she laid it out. The chart was spread flat under its weights, our track a red pencil line running southeast out of the shipping lanes, doglegging around two shoals Ines's charts had politely omitted, and ending at a small, precise X drawn in fresh graphite.

I read the coordinates. Then I read them again.

Then I sat down on the bench beside the chart table because my legs decided for me that standing was over.

"That's not possible," I said.

"It's possible," Priya said. "It's just not kind."

Because I knew those numbers. Not the way a navigator knows coordinates — not as abstractions, degrees and minutes, a position to be plotted and corrected for current. I knew them the way you know an address where something happened to you. My father had kept a copy of the incident report folded inside his copy of *Dutton's*, wedged between the chapters on heavy weather and man overboard, and I'd found it when I was nineteen, after the funeral, going through his things the way grieving sons do, looking for a map of the man himself. The report was four pages of bureaucratic language about a survey vessel called the *Halcyon Star* striking an uncharted reef head in the Sunda approaches during a squall, and the coordinates were printed right there in the header, and I had read them so many times that night, sitting on the floor of his study with a bottle of his terrible rye, that they'd burned themselves into me like a brand.

Eleven years later, they were sitting on my chart table with an X next to them.

"We're here," Priya said quietly, tapping the pencil point against the paper, a mile and a half short of the mark. "At this speed, we're over it in forty minutes."

* * *

Forty minutes is nothing at sea. Forty minutes is a cup of coffee, a radio check, a glance at the barometer. I have run forty-minute passages in my sleep. But that morning, forty minutes was a corridor I had to walk down, and every step of it got narrower.

I went up to the wheelhouse and took the helm myself, which surprised nobody, because everyone on the *Kestrel* knew by now that when the water got serious, Felix Eriksen drove. That had been true since the squall, since the hairline fractures, since the night I'd earned back whatever measure of respect I'd spent in the first week being a captain who led from his debt instead of his deck. Tomas had the engine room. Amara was forward on the bow rail, scanning the water like she always did in the hour before dawn, when the light comes up gray-green and the sea gives up its secrets grudgingly. Ines was below, in the cabin she'd claimed, doing whatever Ines did in there — I'd stopped asking.

The sky was doing that thing the Sunda Sea does before weather: bruising along the eastern horizon, low cloud stacked in shelves, the air gone thick and warm and smelling faintly of rot and rain. The monsoon was coming. We all knew it. Priya's satellite feed had told us we had a window measured in days, maybe hours, and we were spending that window's best morning doing the thing we'd come to do.

The reef showed itself before the depth sounder did. A long smear of paler water ahead, turquoise bleeding into sapphire, the classic signature of coral rising toward the surface. I'd seen a thousand such smears from the air, from other boats, in other lives. This one made my hands go cold on the wheel.

Because I remembered it. Not the reef itself — I'd never been here; my father had sworn us away from these waters before I was old enough to remember anything but fragments. What I remembered was the story, told once, drunk, on a beach in Da Nang when I was eleven, the only time he ever spoke of it without the report's cold language between him and the telling. He'd described the color of the water. He'd said the reef came up out of nowhere, that the *Halcyon Star* had been doing nine knots through a squall line and the coral head rose faster than the sonar could paint it, and that the ship didn't sink so much as get opened, peeled along her port side like fruit. He'd said my mother had been in the survey lab, down below, and that the lab flooded in ninety seconds. Ninety seconds. He'd timed it, afterward, with a stopwatch, over and over, trying to find some version of the arithmetic where ninety seconds was enough. It never was.

And now I was driving a boat at eight knots directly toward the place where ninety seconds hadn't been enough.

* * *

Amara appeared at the wheelhouse door at 0455. I don't know how long she'd been standing there before I noticed her, and that itself should have told me something — Amara had a way of being present in a room without announcing herself, a stillness that most people mistake for shyness until they realize it's patience.

"You're shaking," she said.

"I'm fine."

"You're shaking," she said again, gentler, and she was right. My hands were trembling on the spokes, a fine vibration I couldn't stop no matter how hard I gripped. The boat was steady. The sea was moderate. There was no reason for my hands to shake except the reason they were shaking.

"Slow to bare steerageway," she said. It wasn't a suggestion. She said it the way Priya would have said it, the way a navigator says a thing that is correct regardless of who wants to hear it. "You're coming up on a reef. You want to drift onto this mark, not drive onto it."

I throttled back. The *Kestrel*'s wake flattened and died, and the boat settled into that uneasy lurch she gets at bare steerageway, wallowing slightly, alive under my hands in a way that usually comforted me and now did not.

The pale water was half a mile off the bow.

"Tell me what you're seeing," Amara said. She leaned against the wheelhouse coaming, arms folded, watching me rather than the water. "Right now. Out loud."

"I'm seeing a reef."

"What else?"

I wanted to tell her to go to hell. Some part of me, the part that had grown up learning to meet fear with volume, wanted very badly to tell this stowaway-turned-crewmate that I didn't need a therapy session at my own helm. But another part of me — the part that had watched her pull the Syndicate's saboteur's work apart in the bilge, the part that had seen her take a wrench across the shoulder for this boat and this crew — knew exactly what she was doing, and why it was working.

"I'm seeing the water my father described," I said. "He talked about the color. Green over white, he called it. Coral shallow enough to burn through the blue."

"And?"

"And I'm seeing a lab flooding in ninety seconds." My voice cracked on the number. I heard it crack and hated hearing it. "Ninety seconds. She was down there. My mother was in the lab, and the hull opened, and the water came in, and there were ninety seconds, and he counted them afterward with a stopwatch because he couldn't stop counting them, and I grew up in a house with a stopwatch in a drawer and a man who wound it every Sunday night like it was a living thing."

Amara nodded slowly. She didn't say *I know*. She didn't say *me too*, though she'd told me already, at the chart table weeks ago, that the same disaster had taken people from her — that she had her own dead in this water, her own claim on this wreck, her own arithmetic of seconds. She just nodded, and let me keep talking, which is a rarer skill than navigation and harder to learn.

"My father swore we'd never come back here," I went on. The words were coming easier now, which frightened me more than the shaking had. "He burned the charts on a beach. I watched him do it. I was six. He fed them into a fire page by page and swore on my mother's grave that no Eriksen would ever sail these waters again, and I made a vow of my own at his deathbed, and then I broke it, and I told myself I broke it for the crew, for the debts, for Tomas's unpaid wages, and maybe that was even true, but right now, at this helm, looking at this water, I can't remember a single number in any ledger that weighs what I'm feeling."

The pale water was a quarter mile off the bow. The sun was coming up behind the cloud shelf, turning the reef's smear from gray to gold to that impossible green-white, and it was exactly the color my father had described, drunk, on a beach, twenty-two years ago, and my hands were shaking worse, and the *Kestrel* was drifting toward it at two knots with the momentum of everything I'd ever refused to feel.

"Felix," Amara said, very quietly. "Take your hands off the wheel."

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened in the next ten seconds, because I've thought about it every day since, and honesty is the only currency I have left that isn't owed to someone.

My first instinct was to refuse. To grip harder. Every instinct I had, every lesson the sea and the Syndicate and my father's silence had ever taught me, said that a captain grips the wheel. That the wheel is the whole job. That if you let go, you're admitting the water has beaten you, and the water doesn't get to win, not while anyone's watching, not while the crew is on deck and the Syndicate's gunboat might be over the horizon and the monsoon is stacking up black to the east.

But Amara didn't ask me to admit defeat. She asked me to take my hands off the wheel, and there was a difference, and I could feel the difference the way you feel a door that opens inward versus outward.

"Let me hold her," she said. "Just for the drift. You've brought us here. That was the hard part — the part where a man steers straight at the thing he's spent his whole life steering away from. Nobody else could have done that. But the last hundred meters aren't seamanship anymore. They're memory. And memory drives badly."

Memory drives badly. I've carried that sentence with me since. Because it's true, and because she said it without pity, and pity would have broken me right there. Pity would have made it about my grief, and my grief was mine, and I'd have defended it like a fort. What she offered instead was seamanship. Plain, unsentimental, competent seamanship: a second pair of hands on the wheel for a drift over a reef, because the man currently holding the wheel was compromised, and a good crew doesn't let a compromised hand steer over coral.

That's what let me do it. Not courage. Not breakthrough. Seamanship.

I took my hands off the wheel.

It lasted three seconds before I snatched them back, and I'll admit that too. Three seconds of the strangest sensation I've ever known at a helm — the boat floating free, unheld, the spokes turning lazily under their own weight, the sea suddenly enormous and indifferent and close — and then panic reached up out of wherever panic lives and I grabbed the wheel again like a drowning man grabbing a ladder.

Amara didn't flinch. She put her hand flat on the wheel, next to mine, not taking it, just resting there, the way you rest a hand on a spooked animal's neck.

"Again," she said.

I let go. Longer this time. Five seconds. Eight. The *Kestrel* drifted, wallowed, held her heading anyway, because the sea was calm enough and the wind was light and boats are more forgiving than captains. And somewhere in those eight seconds, something in my chest unlocked — not healed, not resolved, nothing so clean — but unlocked, the way a rusted gate unlocks, grinding, unwilling, half-broken, but open a crack.

"She's yours," I said, and stepped back from the wheel, and my hands hung at my sides feeling weightless and useless and strange, like limbs returned from a long captivity.

Amara took the helm. She handled her gently, feathering the wheel, letting the tide do the work, bringing us over the mark with the kind of touch I couldn't have managed that morning if my life depended on it. Maybe especially if my life depended on it.

Priya came up and stood beside me at the rail. She didn't say anything. She just checked the GPS against the chart, confirmed the fix, and said, "We're here," in the flattest voice she owned, which is how navigators say the most important things.

Below us, the sounder started reading the bottom coming up. Forty fathoms. Thirty-five. Thirty. The reef shelf rose in slow terraces on the display, and then, off the starboard side, the bottom dropped away into sudden black — a scour channel along the reef's edge, deep water cut by decades of tide — and there, painted in the return, unmistakable even to my untrained eye, was a shape.

A long shape. A hull-shaped shape. Broken in the middle.

Tomas came up from the engine room with the news we already knew, shouting it anyway because Tomas shouts good news: strong return, hard target, consistent with a steel vessel of the *Halcyon Star*'s tonnage, lying in the scour channel at thirty-one fathoms, intact enough to dive, broken enough to enter.

Thirty-one fathoms. One hundred eighty-six feet. My mother's lab had flooded in ninety seconds at the surface. She'd spent the rest of her time at the bottom of this water, and I was floating on top of it, and my father had spent thirty years pretending this water didn't exist, and none of us — not him, not me, not the stopwatch in the drawer — had ever once come here to be with her.

Until now.

* * *

We anchored outside the scour channel, in sand, where anchoring wouldn't chew the coral. Amara held the boat on station while Priya ran the anchor evolution, and I stood at the rail and looked down into water so clear it felt like an accusation.

You could see the reef. Not clearly, not at thirty-one fathoms — but you could see the light change, see the shadow of the drop-off, see the faint dark suggestion of the wreck lying in the channel like a rib cage picked clean. The sun was high enough now to push shafts of light down into the blue, and where those shafts touched the wreck's outline, the water shimmered, and I stood there and thought about my mother in her lab, and about ninety seconds, and about a stopwatch wound every Sunday, and about a beach fire and burning charts and a deathbed vow, and about Ines Varga below decks right now, in her cabin, knowing — she had to know — that these were the exact coordinates, that she'd chosen them, that this whole voyage had been built to bring me here whether I consented to it or not.

I thought about going below and confronting her. I actually got as far as the companionway. Then I turned around, because I understood, in the way you understand things at sea that you'd never understand ashore, that confronting her now, before the dive, before I'd seen it with my own eyes, would be a fight about motives instead of a reckoning with facts. Ines had her reasons. They'd keep. The wreck wouldn't. The monsoon wouldn't. Whatever was waiting for me down there — my father's strongbox, the truth about the sabotage, the evidence that would either save us or damn us — it had waited forty years. But it couldn't wait much longer than today.

So I went forward instead, to the bow, where Amara was checking the dive gear, and I knelt down on the deck next to her, and I said the hardest sentence I said all day, harder than taking my hands off the wheel:

"I need you to dive with me. Not because I don't trust Tomas or Priya. Because I don't trust myself down there alone."

She looked up at me, and there was salt drying on her face, and I realized she'd been crying — quietly, at the bow rail, sometime in the last hour, facing the water, facing her own dead, the family the letter in the strongbox would later name as partners betrayed. Two griefs, one reef. We'd recognized each other weeks ago at the chart table. Now we were going to go down together and visit them.

"We go down slow," she said. "We go down together. We touch nothing we don't both agree to touch. And Felix — if you freeze at thirty meters, you squeeze my arm twice, and I carry you. That's the deal."

"That's the deal," I said.

Tomas rigged the compressor and checked my regulator twice, because Tomas checks everything twice, and then a third time when he saw my hands. Priya logged the site, marked the wreck on the chart with a new symbol — not an X, she drew a small circle, and when I asked her why, she said, "X marks treasure. Circles mark graves" — and Ines came up on deck at last, drawn by the anchor chain's noise or by some internal clock of her own, and stood at the rail, and looked down at the water, and said nothing at all.

That silence lasted a full minute, and it was the loudest minute of the voyage. Ines Varga, who negotiated like a knife-fight and lied like breathing, stood at the rail of my boat above the wreck of the *Halcyon Star* and said nothing, and her face did something I'd never seen it do. It went young. For a moment — just a moment, before she caught it and smoothed it away — she looked like a girl standing at a different rail, in a different year, watching the same water.

Then she caught my eye, and the mask came back, and she said, in her dryest voice, "Visibility's better in the morning. You should dive before the tide turns."

As if she hadn't just shown me her whole self for sixty seconds. As if I hadn't seen it.

* * *

We went down at 0930.

The last thing I saw before the water closed over my head was the *Kestrel*'s hull above me, patched and scarred and leaking a little, sailcloth and epoxy holding her together the way this crew held each other together — imperfectly, temporarily, stubbornly. And the last thing I heard was Amara's voice through the regulator, muffled, calm: "Slow. Together. Touch nothing we don't both agree to."

The green dark took us.

Descent is a strange mercy. At thirty feet the reef's colors start dying — reds first, then oranges, then yellows, the spectrum collapsing band by band as the water filters the sun — and by sixty feet the world is blue and shadow and the geometry of things. By a hundred feet, it's not a place anymore. It's a pressure. It presses on your ears, your sinuses, your thoughts. Equalize or suffer. Breathe slow or panic. The ocean does not care which you choose.

At a hundred and fifty feet, we saw her.

The *Halcyon Star* lay on her port side in the scour channel, exactly as the sonar had promised, exactly as my father's report had described: peeled open along the port side, torn from the bow almost to the stern, the hull plates curled back like petals. Forty years of coral had claimed her — soft corals waving from the rail stanchions, hard coral crusting the deck, a forest of black coral trees growing out of the breach itself, as if the wound had become a garden. Fish moved through her in silver schools. A grouper the size of a door watched us from the shadow of the collapsed superstructure with the flat, ancient indifference of everything that lives down here.

And there, amidships, where the superstructure had been, where the survey lab had been, where ninety seconds had ended —

I froze.

Not metaphorically. Not dramatically. I stopped swimming. I hung in the water column, neutral, motionless, my fins barely moving, and I looked at the breach, and I could not make myself go closer. Something in me simply refused, the way a horse refuses a jump. Every stroke toward that opening felt like a stroke toward the inside of my mother's last room, and some animal wisdom in my body understood that before my mind did, and my body said no.

Amara felt it happen. She was three meters behind me, on the tether line, and she felt the line go slack, and she knew what slack meant. She swam up alongside me, took my arm — not squeezing it, not yet, just holding it, the way she'd rested her hand on the wheel — and we hung there together in the blue, two divers, two griefs, forty years of silence, one hundred and eighty-six feet above the place where it had all ended.

"There she is," Amara said, and I couldn't hear her well through the regulators, but I could read her lips, and the words were simple, and they were enough.

I squeezed her arm twice.

She carried me the rest of the way in.

* * *

What follows, I'll tell plainly, because it deserves plainness.

We went in through the breach. The interior of the *Halcyon Star* is a cathedral now — coral-grown ribs arching overhead, silt on the deck like snowfall, light coming down through the torn-open side in visible shafts, dust motes of the sea hanging in the beams. It should have been beautiful, and it was beautiful, and it was also the worst place I have ever been, because every meter of it was furnished with my mother's last minutes. The corridor to the lab. The lab door, half-open, jammed by the hull's twisting. The instruments, fused into the bulkheads, crusted white. A coffee mug, fused to a desk by four decades of growth, still upright, still waiting.

Amara kept her hand on my arm the whole way. Twice more I froze, and twice more she carried me, and I don't mean dragged — I mean carried, the way you carry someone by believing for them when belief runs out. She moved through the wreck like she'd memorized it, and maybe she had; she'd spent years, I'd learn later, reconstructing this ship from drawings and survivor accounts and dreams, hunting this site the way my father had fled it. We were mirror images, she and I. One of us running from the wreck, one of us swimming toward it, and the sea had finally arranged for us to arrive at the same moment, from opposite directions, holding each other's arms.

The lab was small. Smaller than I'd imagined from the report's diagrams. A room maybe four meters by five, one wall open to the sea where the hull had peeled, the other walls crusted and silent. Survey benches. A plotting table, collapsed. And against the far bulkhead, in the corner farthest from the breach — the corner farthest from the water, the corner a man would choose if he were hiding something from the sea itself —

a steel box.

Bolted to the deck. Corroded, but whole. About the size of a sea chest, with a lock that had been designed, I realized as I swam closer, to look like part of the ship's original fittings. Concealed. Deliberately concealed. My father had hidden it here, in the one place he knew the Syndicate would never dare to look because the Syndicate believed this ship was empty — believed it the way everyone believed it, because my father had made sure of it, because my father had —

because my father had scuttled her.

I didn't know that yet. Not fully. I knew it the way you know a shape in fog, the way I'd known the coordinates before Priya showed me the chart. The box was bolted down with marine-grade fastenings, newer than the ship, installed with care, positioned with intent. Someone had put this here on purpose, in advance, knowing the ship would end up on the bottom. Ships don't end up on the bottom by accident twice.

Amara saw it too. I felt her go still beside me, felt her breath change rhythm through the regulator, and when I turned to look at her, her eyes were wide behind her mask, fixed on the box, and her expression was one I'd never seen on her face: recognition, and horror, and underneath both, a hope so fierce it looked like pain.

She pointed. I nodded. We couldn't speak down here, not really, not with words — but we didn't need to. The box was real. After forty years, after a burned chart, after a deathbed vow, after a voyage built on lies and sabotage and storms, after everything — the box was real, and it was here, and it was bolted to the deck of the room where my mother had spent her last ninety seconds, and my father had put it there, and I was looking at it, and I could not breathe, and the gauge said I had maybe twelve minutes of bottom time left, and the monsoon was building to the east, and the Syndicate's gunboat was somewhere behind us, and none of it mattered, none of it, because there was a steel box bolted to the deck of my mother's last room, and it had my family's name on it in corrosion and coral, and I had come forty years and eleven thousand miles and one broken vow to stand in front of it.

Amara put her hands on the box's lid. I put mine over hers.

We worked the corroded fastenings in silence, sharing the pry bar, trading places when one set of arms failed, and it took eleven minutes of the twelve we had, and on the last turn, when the lid groaned and lifted a centimeter and stopped — stuck, jammed, sealed by four decades of mineral deposit — I looked at the gap, and the gap was black, and the black was full of something, and I couldn't see what.

Bottom time expired. Amara tapped my gauge, pointed up.

We ascended. Slowly, with stops, the way you're supposed to, the way my father never got to, the way my mother never got to. Hanging at fifteen feet on the deco line, watching the *Kestrel*'s patched hull overhead grow from a shadow into a boat, I thought about the lid. One centimeter open. Black gap. Something inside.

Something inside.

I surfaced into daylight, into the smell of diesel and salt and Tomas's coffee, into a world that had not changed while I was underwater and had changed completely. Priya took our report. Tomas started rigging the lift bags we'd need for tomorrow's full recovery. Ines watched from the rail with that unreadable face of hers, and when I climbed aboard, soaked and shaken and hollowed out, she handed me a towel and said one word:

"Well."

One word. Ines Varga, who dealt in paragraphs, who negotiated in clauses, who had engineered this entire voyage with the precision of a heist — said *well*, and meant, I think, a hundred things she wasn't ready to say.

I wrapped the towel around my shoulders and stood at the rail, and looked down at the water, and the water looked back, and somewhere beneath me, in the green dark, in a steel box with its lid one centimeter open, something was waiting.

Tomorrow, we'd come back with lift bags and cutting gear and open it all the way.

Tonight, I'd dream about the gap. The black gap. The something inside.

And tomorrow, when we opened it, I would learn what my father had done — what he had really done, out here, in this water, in the year I was six years old — and the knowing would break something in me that I could feel breaking already, like a hairline crack spreading through a hull, silent, structural, inevitable.

The barometer was falling. The clouds to the east had organized into bands. Priya stood beside me at the rail, chart tube under her arm, and said the thing navigators say when the clock starts winning:

"We have tomorrow. Probably not the day after."

Tomorrow, then. The box. The letter. The truth about what Papa buried at sea — and what he'd done to bury it.
