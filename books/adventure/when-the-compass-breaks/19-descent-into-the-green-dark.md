## 19. Descent Into the Green Dark

I didn't sleep. Nobody expected me to, and nobody pretended otherwise.

The Kestrel rode at anchor over the reef in a long, ugly swell, and all night the hull talked to us — that low, structural groaning Tomas had been listening to for a week now, the sound of a boat holding opinions about her own survival. I lay in my bunk with my father's compass on my chest, its needle swinging in slow, drunken arcs, and I listened to the sea pressing up against the deck plates like something patient.

At 0400 I gave up and went topside.

The reef showed itself at low water — a black spine of coral breaking the swell a hundred meters off our port beam, white water stitching along it in the pre-dawn gray. My mother died on that spine. Or near it. Close enough that the distinction didn't matter anymore, because we were anchored directly above it, and Priya's corrected coordinates put the Halcyon Star forty meters down on the seaward slope, exactly where Amara said she'd be.

Amara was already on deck when I came up. She sat cross-legged on the forehatch with a mug of tea going cold in her hands, watching the reef break the way you'd watch an old dog sleep — with something tender and something wary in the same look.

"You're up early," I said.

"I slept on the boat that killed my family for twenty years of nights." She didn't look at me. "This is the first morning I've been able to see it."

* * *

We rigged the dive at first light, and the crew worked around me with the careful silence of people handling something explosive. Tomas checked my regulator twice without being asked. Priya laid out the descent line and shot bearings to the reef markers, calling numbers in her flat navigator's voice because numbers were how she kept her hands from shaking. Even Ines stayed below, which told me everything — Ines Varga did not stay out of anything unless staying out was itself a move.

Only Amara spoke freely. She'd been diving wrecks since she was nineteen, she said, pulling on her wetsuit with the economy of someone who'd done it ten thousand times, and the Halcyon Star was hers as much as mine. More, maybe. Her grandfather had been her family's claim on it, and the letter in the strongbox — if there was a letter — would say so.

"Last chance," I told her at the rail. "Storm's coming. We could run now, come back never."

She looked at me with those level dark eyes and said, "You didn't come four thousand miles to run. Neither did I." Then she spat into the sea — a diver's superstition, luck given back to the water — and stepped off the rail into the green.

I followed.

* * *

The first five meters were bright. Warm. The kind of water they put on travel posters, sun-shafted and gold-flecked, and for those few seconds I could almost believe this was any dive, any wreck, any ocean.

Then the light began to fail, and the color drained out of the world in the order it always does — reds first, then oranges, then the yellows — until we were descending through a spectrum of greens and blues that deepened by degrees into something that wasn't a color at all. Just pressure. Just dark with a faint blue memory of light somewhere far above.

Amara swam ahead of me, fins kicking in slow, even strokes, her torch beam cutting a cone through the murk. I watched the needle of my depth gauge climb: fifteen meters. Twenty-five. Thirty-eight.

And then the Halcyon Star rose up out of the dark like a cathedral.

She lay on her starboard side on the reef slope, half-buried in sand and coral, and twenty years underwater had turned her into something no longer entirely a ship. Hard and soft corals had colonized her rails and davits; a forest of black coral grew from her funnel; a school of fusiliers wheeled around her superstructure in a silver, silent cyclone. Her name was still legible on the stern, picked out in flaking gold leaf beneath a crust of barnacle: HALCYON STAR. Port of registry, Singapore. And beneath that, smaller, almost worn away:

ERIKSEN MARINE SURVEY — CHARTERED.

My father's company. My father's ship. My mother's coffin.

I hung in the water beside that name for a long time, breathing hard through my regulator, watching my own exhaust bubble up toward a surface I couldn't see. Amara drifted back and put her hand on my arm — not pulling, just anchoring. A touch that said: whenever you're ready. Or maybe: I know.

I wasn't ready. I went anyway.

* * *

We entered through the broken skylight of the main salon, because Amara knew the wreck — of course she knew the wreck, she'd spent two decades and a fortune learning every inch of her — and because the salon was where the survey offices had been, and the survey offices were where my father would have kept what mattered.

Inside, the Halcyon Star was a drowned house. Silt lay over everything like gray snow. A filing cabinet stood open, its drawers fanned out, papers long since dissolved into pulp. A desk chair floated tethered by its own decayed upholstery. Fish scattered at our lights and regrouped behind us, unbothered, permanent residents now.

Amara led me down the corridor toward the chart room, and I remembered it — not from the disaster, I'd been six, I'd never been aboard — but from my father's stories, the ones he told before he stopped telling them. The chart room was amidships, behind the wheelhouse, steel-walled, steel-doored. Built to protect paper.

The door was closed. Twenty years of marine growth had welded it shut, but the frame had warped just enough, and Amara worked a pry bar into the gap while I held the light, and the door gave with a groan of rusted metal that sounded, in the dead water, like a voice.

Inside, the chart room was intact.

The tables were still bolted down. The chart tubes were still racked along the bulkhead, their brass caps green with age. And on the deck, wedged beneath the forward chart table where no salvage sonar would ever distinguish it from table leg and sand —

A strongbox.

Steel. Green-black with corrosion. About the size of a seabag, with a combination lock fused solid and a stenciled marking on the lid that the coral hadn't quite eaten:

E.M.S. — F.E. — PERSONAL

F.E. Felix Eriksen. My father. He'd stenciled his own initials on it, and mine inside them, like a man leaving a note.

Amara saw it at the same moment I did, and I felt her go still beside me in the water — felt the whole ocean go still, the way it does when something enormous settles into place.

We lifted it together. It weighed nothing underwater and everything else. We got it through the door, down the corridor, out through the skylight, into the open blue, and began the long ascent, the box slung between us on a lift bag, rising through the green dark toward the light.

And it was during that ascent — hanging in the empty water column, the wreck shrinking below us, my lungs burning, my mind strangely clear — that I saw it.

The hull breach.

Not on the reef side. Not where a ship runs aground, where the coral tears her open along her turn. The breach was on the outboard side, facing open sea, a ragged black wound blown outward — metal peeled away from the inside.

Ships that hit reefs are torn from the outside in.

The Halcyon Star had been torn from the inside out.

* * *

We broke the surface into gray morning light and the crew hauled us and the box aboard, and I sat on the deck dripping seawater with the strongbox between my knees and said nothing, because I was doing arithmetic I didn't want to finish.

Outward. Blown outward. From the inside.

Tomas crouched by the box with a pry bar and a angle grinder, and Priya stood over him with the flare wrench, and Amara knelt across from me with her hand flat on the corroded steel like she could read it by touch. The swell had built while we were down; the Kestrel rolled in long, sickening arcs, and somewhere east the clouds had organized themselves into a wall.

"Felix," Amara said quietly. "You've gone somewhere. Where?"

"The hull," I said. My voice came out strange. Flat. "On the wreck. The breach faces open water. It's blown outward."

Silence on the deck. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"Grounding doesn't do that," Tomas said slowly. He was looking at me the way engineers look at things they wish they weren't understanding. "Explosion does that. From inside."

"My father's ship," I said. "My father's charter. My father's strongbox, hidden in a steel room, sealed, weighted, sunk with the wreck instead of salvaged." I looked down at the box, at my own initials stenciled under his. "He didn't lose the Halcyon Star. He sank it."

Nobody spoke. The wind filled the silence for them.

"He sank it with my mother aboard," I said, and the words came out of me like splinters. "He burned the charts on the beach. He swore us off these waters. He made me swear. And the whole time — the whole time he was the storm. He was the disaster. He killed her himself to keep this—" I kicked the box, and it rang dully against the deck, "—out of Syndicate hands."

"That's not certain," Priya said carefully. "An explosion could be—"

"It could be sabotage by the Syndicate, yes. That's the other story. That's the story I've been telling myself since I was six years old." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "But the Syndicate would have taken the patents and sold them. They wouldn't have left a strongbox sitting in a chart room for twenty years with the family name stenciled on it. Only one person had a reason to sink the evidence along with the ship."

Amara hadn't moved. Her hand was still flat on the box, and when she finally looked up at me, her face was the face of someone standing at the edge of a drop.

"Open it," she said.

Tomas set the grinder spinning.

* * *

The lock surrendered in a shriek of sparks. The lid came up stiff, exhaling twenty years of trapped air and the smell of rust and old oil, and inside, wrapped in waxcloth and tarred canvas, dry as the day they were packed —

Patents. Dozens of them. Deeds. Survey charts of waters no government had mapped, worth more than the Kestrel, more than Ines's yacht, more than every debt I owed stacked on top of each other. Enough to erase Meridian forever. Enough to buy a fleet.

And on top of it all, a single envelope, wax-sealed, addressed in my father's handwriting:

TO MY SON. READ THIS ALONE, OR DON'T READ IT AT ALL.

The crew looked at the envelope. They looked at me. The wall of cloud in the east had begun to eat the horizon, and the barometer needle was falling the way stones fall.

I took the envelope, and my hands were steady, which surprised me, because nothing else was.

Behind us, out on the gray water, a shape that was not weather slid out from under the storm's leading edge — a gunboat, running straight for us, flags flying, coming to collect.

And in my hands, my father's last words waited to tell me whether the man I'd mourned for twenty years had been a victim, or the monster, or both.
