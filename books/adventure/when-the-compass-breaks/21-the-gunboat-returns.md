## 21. The Gunboat Returns

The storm's first edge arrived as a smell.

Felix had been on the aft deck for an hour, watching the western horizon go the color of a bruise, when Priya called it from the wheelhouse: that green-black wall of cloud building over the Sunda Strait like something being poured. The air changed before the wind did — heavier, metallic, tasting faintly of ozone and rotting kelp. Every sailor he'd ever trusted said you could feel a monsoon in your teeth before your instruments confirmed it. His father used to say it too, back when his father still said anything at all.

Behind him, forty fathoms down, the Halcyon Star sat on the reef with its secrets cracked open. The strongbox was lashed to the deck beneath a tarp, and even under canvas it seemed to radiate weight, the way a body does.

Felix hadn't slept since the dive. Neither had Amara. They'd surfaced with the box between them and a truth wedged between their ribs, and neither of them had found words for it yet. *Your father betrayed my family.* She'd said it once, quietly, at the rail, and then she'd gone below and hadn't come back up except to eat, and Felix had stood watch after watch staring at the tarp and thinking about a man burning charts on a beach while a boy watched from the dunes.

Now the radio crackled.

"Kestrel, Kestrel, this is Meridian vessel *Ledger's Due*." The voice was smooth as poured concrete. "We have you on radar, bearing zero-four-seven, range six nautical miles. We request you hold position and prepare to receive visitors."

Felix's stomach dropped through the hull, through the water column, all the way down to the wreck.

He keyed the mic because not answering would be worse. "This is Kestrel. We're a private salvage vessel in open water. We don't receive visitors."

"Mr. Eriksen." A pause, and Felix could hear the smile in it. He'd met the mouth it belonged to three days ago in Jakarta — a collector named Ostrander, silver-haired, soft-spoken, with hands like a man who'd never lifted anything heavier than a fountain pen. "Let's not waste each other's time. You're holding cargo that belongs to our client portfolio. Deliver it, and we'll discuss terms. Refuse, and we'll discuss consequences. Either way, we're coming alongside within the hour."

The channel clicked dead.

Felix stood there with the mic in his fist and the bruised sky in front of him and did the arithmetic every captain does and hates: one converted trawler with a patched hull against a Syndicate gunboat with a bow gun and a crew of twenty. Six nautical miles. Maybe twenty minutes at their cruising speed.

Tomas came up the companionway wiping grease off his hands, took one look at Felix's face, and turned to look west. "That them?"

"That's them."

"The hull won't take a run. Not yet. Another day on the epoxy, maybe—"

"We don't have another day."

Priya appeared behind Tomas, chart tube under her arm, hair tied back like she was already bracing. Amara came last, silent, and stopped beside Felix at the rail. Nobody said anything for a moment. The wind had begun to arrive in long, low gusts that made the rigging hum a single flat note.

"Options," Priya said finally. It wasn't a question; it was an order dressed as one.

"Run," Felix said. "And lose the hull somewhere in the strait with a gunboat behind us."

"Fight." Tomas said it flatly, testing it, and shook his own head before anyone else could. "Four of us. No weapons worth the name."

"Give it up." Amara's voice was quiet. Everyone turned. She was looking at the tarp, at the shape beneath it, and her jaw was set in a way Felix was learning to read as the opposite of what her words meant. "It's just paper. Patents. Deeds. Paper doesn't drown anyone."

"It does if people kill for it," Felix said. "Which they do."

"Then we're agreed it's worthless," she said, "and we should keep it anyway."

Despite everything — the gunboat, the storm, the letter in the strongbox that named her family as the wronged party — Felix almost laughed. Almost.

Ines Varga chose that moment to come aboard.

She'd been keeping to her own yacht since the dive, anchored two hundred meters off the Kestrel's beam, and Felix had been grateful for the distance. But now here she came across the gap in her tender, alone at the tiller, moving with the unhurried economy of a woman who had read the same radio traffic they had and drawn different conclusions. She stepped onto the deck, looked at the four faces arranged around the strongbox like a bad poker hand, and said:

"You're going to want to hear how I handle men like Ostrander."

"And why would we trust you to handle anything?" Felix asked.

"Because I've been handling him for eleven years," Ines said. "He thinks he works for me."

* * *

They gathered in the wheelhouse, which suddenly felt very small. Ines stood at the chart table with her palms flat on the glass, and Felix noticed — for the first time in thirty years of knowing her, first as rival, then as employer, now as whatever this was — that her hands were shaking. Just slightly. She saw him notice and stilled them deliberately, pressing harder, and something about that small dishonesty told Felix more than anything she'd ever said out loud.

"Ostrander is not Syndicate muscle," she began. "He's Syndicate accounting. He doesn't want the box because of what's in it. He wants it because his ledger has a hole in it the size of the Halcyon Star, and until that hole is filled with recovered assets, he's the man who lost a fortune. Understand? He's not coming for us. He's coming for his own neck."

"So we give him the box and his neck is saved," Tomas said. "Problem solved."

"No. Because the box isn't enough." Ines tapped the chart, on the reef where the wreck lay. "Eleven years ago, the Syndicate financed an operation to recover the Halcyon Star. My operation. I reported the site lost, the vessel unsalvageable, the assets dispersed by currents. That report was a lie, and Ostrander signed off on it, and for eleven years we've both been sitting on a fiction. If that box comes up now, it proves the lie. It proves I've known where the wreck was the whole time. It proves he certified a recovery that never happened. The patents are worth millions — but the scandal is worth our careers, and for men like Ostrander, careers are the only currency that matters."

Felix stared at her. "You're telling me you've known where my father's ship was for eleven years."

"I'm telling you I've known where *a* ship was," Ines said, and her eyes flicked to Amara, and something passed between the two women that Felix couldn't parse and didn't like. "The rest — whose ship, what it holds, who put it there — I learned recently. From research. From..." She stopped. Recalibrated. "It doesn't matter. What matters is the next hour."

"The next hour," Priya repeated dryly, "when a gunboat arrives."

"When a gunboat arrives," Ines agreed, "and finds nothing it can use. Here's what happens. I go across to the *Ledger's Due* alone. I tell Ostrander that the salvage failed — that the wreck shifted in last week's swell, that the strongbox location was buried under a collapse, that we spent our dive window on survey and got nothing but video. I offer him the survey footage as proof of effort. He'll hate it, but he'll take it, because the alternative is reporting to his board that the asset is unrecoverable, and unrecoverable is a word he can survive. Stolen is not."

"And he'll just believe you?" Felix asked.

"He'll believe me because believing me is cheaper than the truth." Ines straightened. "But belief takes time. He'll want to verify. He'll send divers down himself, maybe. He'll argue. And while he argues—" she nodded west, at the wall of cloud, which had begun to eat the horizon whole, "—the monsoon arrives. And no Syndicate gunboat stays on station in a monsoon. They're too heavy. Too proud. They'll run for shelter, and we'll be gone before they surface again."

Amara spoke from the doorway, arms crossed. "You'd walk onto that boat alone. With everything they could do to you."

"They need me," Ines said simply. "I'm the only person alive who can make their problem disappear. That makes me the safest person on this water." She smiled, and it was her old smile, the one from regattas and dockside bars, sharp and lacquered — but underneath it Felix saw something else, something tired and true, and it unsettled him more than the gunboat did.

"Why?" he asked. "Why are you doing this? Really. Not the ledger speech. The real reason."

For a moment — half a breath, maybe less — Ines Varga looked like a woman standing at the edge of a confession. Her mouth opened. The wind gusted, hard, and the wheelhouse windows rattled in their frames, and whatever had been rising in her went back down like a diver pulling up short of the light.

"Because I owe a debt," she said, "that isn't written in any ledger. Get the box secured. Keep your crew off the decks when I'm across. And Felix—" she paused at the companionway, not turning around, "—whatever you think you know about your father, you know half of it. When this is over, I'll give you the other half. Assuming any of us live to collect."

Then she was gone, down the ladder, into her tender, out across the darkening water toward the shape that had just resolved on the horizon: gray hull, white superstructure, the Meridian Syndicate's stylized M painted on the bow like a brand on livestock.

* * *

They watched the approach from the wheelhouse, and it was worse than watching, it was like watching a slow-motion collision you were powerless to prevent. The *Ledger's Due* came on through the first real rain — fat, warm drops that hit the windows and ran sideways in the strengthening wind — and hove to a hundred meters off Ines's yacht with the casual arrogance of a vessel that had never been refused right of way. A launch dropped from her side. Figures moved on her deck. Through the binoculars, Felix picked out Ostrander at the rail: silver hair plastered by rain, hands clasped behind his back, posture of a man receiving rather than visiting.

Ines's tender crossed the gap. A ladder went down. She climbed it in the rain without once reaching for the ropes offered to her, and disappeared over the gunboat's rail, and Felix let out a breath he felt he'd been holding since Jakarta.

"She'll stall them," Priya said. It wasn't quite a question either.

"She'll try." Felix lowered the binoculars. "Get the box into the engine room bilge space. Under the floor plates. Tomas, you know the spot. If this goes wrong, I want it findable by us and nobody else."

Tomas grunted and went. Amara helped him lift the strongbox — it took both of them, and Felix pretended not to see the way Amara's fingers lingered on the lid, the way she looked at it like it was a coffin or a cradle. Inside it, along with the patents and deeds, lay the letter. *To the families of the Halcyon Star,* his father's handwriting had begun, and then become an inventory of betrayals, and Amara's family name was in it, in ink, in his father's hand.

Felix still didn't know which side of that letter he was on. He suspected, increasingly, that there were no sides. Only wreckage, distributed unevenly.

An hour passed. Then two. The rain thickened from drops to sheets. The wind backed around to the northwest and began to build in earnest, and the Kestrel rode it at anchor with her engines warm, because Priya had insisted, and Felix had agreed without argument, because Priya's insistence had kept them alive twice already this voyage.

Through the rain-smeared glass, the gunboat was a smear of lights. Once, briefly, Felix caught movement on her deck — a cluster of figures at the rail, gesturing, one of them unmistakably Ines, arms moving in the sharp explanatory geometry of a woman selling a story. Selling it well, from the body language. Ostrander stood apart, listening, and even at this distance Felix could read the tension in his shoulders: a man being told something he didn't want to hear and couldn't afford to disbelieve.

"He's buying it," Amara said softly. She'd come up without him noticing. She did that. "Or he wants to buy it."

"Everyone buys what saves them money," Felix said. "Question is whether he sends divers first."

As if summoned, the launch went down the gunboat's side. Four figures, dive gear. It crossed the rain-lashed water toward the wreck site buoy and stopped above it, and Felix watched through the binoculars as fins went over the side, one, two, three, four, into water that was already beginning to move wrong — long swells rolling in ahead of the storm, the sea starting to breathe heavily, the way it does before it screams.

"They'll get down maybe fifteen meters before the surge makes it suicide," Priya said, appearing with coffee that tasted like burnt rope but was hot, which counted. "The wreck's at forty. They'll see the collapse — and there *is* a collapse, Tomas and I dumped the loose rubble slab back over the breach yesterday, call it insurance — and they'll come up with exactly the story Ines sold them."

"You dumped rubble on the breach," Felix said slowly. "When were you going to mention that?"

"After it worked." Priya sipped her coffee. "Captain's privilege to be surprised. Navigator's privilege to arrange surprises."

He wanted to be angry. He found, examining the feeling honestly, that he wasn't — that what he actually felt was something adjacent to gratitude, and stranger than that, something adjacent to pride. This crew lied to him, voted behind his back, stowed strangers, sabotaged his authority at every turn. And they were the best people he had ever sailed with, and some traitorous part of him had known it since the first squall.

Below, the divers went down. Above, the sky went fully black at four in the afternoon, and the first thunder rolled across the strait like furniture being dragged across heaven's floor.

* * *

At dusk, the launch came back.

Felix watched it cross to the gunboat through rain that had graduated from sheets to ropes, and he watched the figures climb aboard, and he watched the lights on the *Ledger's Due*'s bridge change pattern — running lights shifting, anchor lights coming on full, the particular constellation of a vessel settling in for weather it intended to sit out at anchor rather than fight.

"They're staying," Amara said.

"They're *stalling*," Felix corrected. "Look at the anchor watch. Two men on the bridge, not five. They think they've got days. Ostrander believes the box is buried, so he'll wait out the monsoon and bring heavy salvage equipment after. Which means—" he did the math, and the math was beautiful, "—we have a window. However long the storm lasts, plus however long it takes him to stop sulking and re-rig. Call it three days. Maybe four."

"Three days to do what?" Amara asked. "Run? On this hull?"

"Not run." Felix turned from the window. Rain hammered the glass. Somewhere beyond it, Ines Varga was sitting in the belly of the enemy, smiling her lacquered smile, buying hours with lies, and Felix found — to his considerable surprise — that he trusted her to do it. Not because she was trustworthy. Because she was Ines, and Ines had never once in her life done anything halfway, including betrayal, including loyalty, including whatever unnamed thing was driving her now.

"Priya," he called down the companionway. "That channel you've been muttering about. The one that isn't on any chart. The one through the reef line north of here."

Priya appeared, coffee cup in hand, eyebrows raised.

"How sure are you?"

"Sure enough to bet the boat," she said. "Not sure enough to bet the crew. There's a difference, and it's about sixty meters of coral."

Felix looked at the tarp-shaped absence on the deck where the strongbox had been. He thought about his father, who had chosen a reef over a fortune, sabotage over surrender, silence over truth. He thought about the letter, and Amara's face when she'd read her family's name in it. He thought about the compass in his pocket, his father's broken compass, its needle swinging free and pointing at nothing, at everything, at whatever it pleased.

"Ines bought us hours," he said. "Let's not waste them deciding. Tomorrow, when the eye of this thing passes — if it passes — we move. North. Through your channel."

"And if the channel doesn't exist?" Tomas asked from the engine room hatch, oil-streaked and skeptical.

"Then we'll have a very short conversation with a coral reef," Felix said, "and a much longer one with the Syndicate. Anybody got a better plan?"

Silence, except the wind, which had risen to a register where it sounded less like weather and more like voices — many voices, far away, calling across water.

Amara touched his arm. "Felix. Look."

Westward, where the cloud wall had swallowed the world, lightning lit the interior of the storm from within — a vast, silent, green-white flicker, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. And silhouetted against it, impossibly, riding the outer edge of the maelstrom with her running lights burning defiant, the *Ledger's Due* sat at anchor like a man refusing to leave a party that had clearly ended.

And at her rail, tiny with distance, a single figure raised one arm — not waving, Felix realized. Pointing. North.

Pointing at the channel.

"She's telling us to run," Amara breathed.

"No," Felix said slowly, watching the silhouette, feeling the storm's breath on the back of his neck. "She's telling us *where*. Running's our idea."

He turned the wheel over to Priya and went below to check the bilge, and behind him the rain came down like the sea trying to erase the whole affair — the wreck, the box, the debts, the names — and failing, because some things, once surfaced, stay surfaced.
