## 22. Running Before the Wind

Priya had never trusted a chart she hadn't drawn herself, and the ones she had drawn were all wrong.

That was the thought she kept circling back to as she stood at the chart table in the Kestrel's cramped nav station, a pencil hovering over a blank sheet of tracing paper, the boat rolling under her in long, sickening swells. Everything she knew about these waters came from satellite passes, dredged surveys, and the honest lies of hydrographic offices that hadn't sounded this stretch of the Sunda Archipelago in forty years. And now she was about to steer a patched, leaking, half-drowned sloop through the middle of it with a gunboat behind her and a monsoon in front of her, guided by nothing but a channel that existed in her head.

Not on paper. Not on any screen. In her head.

"You're sure about this." Felix stood in the doorway, braced against the frame, salt water dripping from his hair. His knuckles were white around the compass case in his pocket — she could see the outline of it through the fabric, the way he'd been touching it like a talisman since the island.

"No," Priya said. "I'm sure about the opposite of this. I'm sure that every other option ends with us at the bottom or in chains. This option only probably ends with us at the bottom."

"That's the best offer we've had all week."

"It's the only offer we've had all week." She turned back to the tracing paper and began to draw. Freehand, from memory, the way her grandmother had taught her to sketch the river deltas of Bengal before Priya could write her own name: don't draw what you remember, draw what the water remembers. The water always remembers.

The channel was called — by no one officially, because officially it did not exist — the Devil's Throat. Her father had spoken of it once, late, after too much rice wine, when she was nineteen and already drawing charts for a living. A fissure in the reef shelf south of the unnamed island, a slot of deep water no wider than a harbor entrance, running three miles between walls of coral that would open a hull like a tin can. The old pilots had used it to run spice and worse past the Dutch gunboats. The surveys missed it because the entrance hid behind a false shoal, a sandbar that looked like a reef death-trap on sonar and wasn't. Every modern navigator would route around it. Every modern navigator would be wrong.

"You've never run it," Felix said. It wasn't a question.

"I've never run anything like it. Nobody has, not in a hundred years." She finished the entrance and started down the throat, marking the bends from her father's drunken recitation, cross-checking against the current lines she'd watched in the satellite imagery for three sleepless days. "But I know where it is. I know how it bends. And I know that the Syndicate's gunboat draws four meters and carries a captain who trusts his charts."

Felix was quiet for a moment. Through the hull she could hear Tomas down in the engine space, talking to the Kestrel in his low, coaxing voice, the way a man talks to a horse with a bad leg. The temporary patch — sailcloth and epoxy and Amara's diving skills and two days of terror on a nameless beach — was holding. Was. The word doing a lot of work.

"And if your father was wrong?" Felix asked. "Or drunk? Or both?"

"Then we die quickly, and the Syndicate doesn't get the box, and Amara's family gets its justice in the only currency left to us." Priya looked up at him. "I've been navigating other people's money across other people's oceans for eleven years, Felix. This is the first time I've ever been asked to navigate for something that matters. Let me have it."

He held her gaze for a long second, and she watched him decide — watched the same calculation she'd seen him make in the squall, in the storm, at the wreck site, the calculation that had slowly, over these weeks, stopped being about the money. Then he nodded once and went forward to the wheel.

Priya folded her chart — her chart, the only copy in the world — and followed him into the rain.

* * *

The monsoon did not arrive like weather. It arrived like a decision.

One moment the sky was the color of wet slate, the wind a steady thirty knots from the southwest, ugly but navigable. The next, the horizon simply ceased to exist, replaced by a wall of gray that came down on them like a dropped curtain, and the wind jumped fifteen knots in the space of a single breath, and the rain came so hard and so horizontal that it hit like flung gravel. Priya, clipped to the rail beside Felix at the wheel, could see perhaps thirty meters. The bow wave was a pale ghost ahead of them and nothing else existed.

"Course?" Felix shouted. He didn't look at her. He couldn't afford to; his whole body was in the wheel, fighting the Kestrel's tendency to broach as the following seas lifted her stern and tried to swing her beam-on to the waves. Running before a monsoon in a damaged boat was a controlled fall, and everyone aboard knew it.

"One-nine-five!" she shouted back. "Hold it until the shoal! When I say hard to port, you turn hard to port, no matter what your eyes tell you!"

"What will my eyes tell me?"

"That we're about to die on a reef!"

"Good to know!" And he almost smiled. That was the thing about Felix Eriksen that Priya had come to understand somewhere in the last hundred miles: the man was built out of exactly this. Terror and grin. She'd spent years thinking he was reckless. He wasn't. He was a person who had decided, long ago, that fear was a fuel and not a wall, and he'd been burning it ever since, and it was slowly consuming him, and none of them had figured out how to make him stop. Maybe, she thought, gripping the rail as the Kestrel surfed down a wave face that seemed to go on forever, maybe that was what Ines had actually bought with her yacht and her fortune. Not time. A chance to make him stop.

The gunboat was behind them. They'd all seen it at dawn, when the Syndicate's boats had ringed the lagoon — a gray shape sitting out past the reef like a verdict, its radar mast turning, turning. It had followed them out. It was bigger, faster in flat water, and utterly dependent on its electronics and its charts, and its charts said the coast ahead was a continuous reef wall with no passage for eleven miles. Its captain would follow them to the edge of that wall and then slow down, confident, herding them, waiting for them to turn and be taken.

He didn't know about the Throat. Nobody knew about the Throat.

"Depth!" Priya called aft, and Amara's voice came back through the rain from the stern where she crouched over the sounder — the old lead-line kind, because the electronic one had drowned in the flooding two days ago and nobody had gotten it working. Amara had volunteered for the line without being asked. Her hands were still bandaged from the fight with the agent, the rope burns and the gash, and every cast of the lead had to hurt her, and she never said a word about it.

"Eight fathoms!" Amara called. "Bottom coming up!"

Priya checked her watch, checked the swell pattern, checked the shape of the rain. Somewhere ahead, hidden under all that violence, was the false shoal. On the Syndicate's charts it was a reef. On the honest charts it was a reef. In her father's wine-soaked memory, and in the current lines she'd spent three nights reading like scripture, it was a door.

"Stand by," she said.

The Kestrel rose on a sea, hung at the top for a moment — that weightless, stomach-lifting pause that Priya had always both loved and hated — and then fell. And in the falling, through the rain, she saw it. Not the shoal. The shoal you couldn't see. But the water over it changed color, a subtle wrongness in the gray, a paleness, a texture, the way water looks when there's something beneath it holding the swell up. Eleven years of reading water. Her grandmother's voice in her head: *the water always remembers.*

"Hard to port!" she screamed. "Now! Hard over!"

Felix threw the wheel. The Kestrel, running at eight knots under a scrap of storm jib and a reefed main, came around with a groan that Priya felt in her teeth — the hull patch complaining, the whole boat complaining, a long creaking moan from below that sounded like a living thing in pain. The boom swung. Rain lashed her face. For three full seconds they were beam-on to the following seas and she felt the whole ocean lean on them, trying to roll them under, and she heard Tomas yell something wordless from below, and she heard the hull take a green sea over the rail and dump it down into the cockpit, and then —

— and then the wave passed under them, and the boat came up, and they were through.

"Steady," Priya gasped. "Steady on one-six-five. Felix. Felix. Look at your depth."

Amara's voice, ragged but almost singing: "Twelve fathoms. Fifteen. Fifteen and holding."

The false shoal slid past their port side, close enough that Priya could see the swell breaking on its seaward edge, a line of white standing in the rain like a wall of teeth — the reef that wasn't, the death-trap that was a door. Behind it, the water deepened. The Throat opened in front of them like a held breath.

"Nobody's ever going to believe this," Felix said.

"Nobody's ever going to know," Priya said. "That's the point."

* * *

Inside the Throat, the world changed.

The reef walls rose on either side — not visible, not in this rain, but *there*, the way a wall is there in a dark room. The swell that had been chasing them from astern couldn't follow into the slot; it broke and scattered on the outer reef, and inside, the water went suddenly, eerily smooth. Not calm — the wind still howled, the rain still came in slabs — but the long rolling seas were gone, and in their place was a current. Priya felt it take the Kestrel by the keel and pull, a firm grip, like a hand on the rudder.

"Two knots of current with us," she said, half to herself, half to the boat. "It runs north on the flood. We're riding the flood."

"Of course we are," Felix muttered. "Everything else has gone wrong. Something had to go right just to keep the universe honest."

The channel bent. Priya had it drawn on her folded chart, her father's bends, and she called them one by one — "ease to starboard, starboard ten, come back to one-seven-five" — and each time the Kestrel obeyed and each time the depth sounder sang back fifteen, sixteen, fourteen fathoms, deep water where every chart in the world said coral. The walls closed in. She could hear them now, actually hear them: the sound of swell detonating on reef just beyond the rain on either side, a continuous deep percussion, like the world's largest drum, and they were running down the middle of the drumbeat.

Tomas came up from below, dripping, gray-faced, and stood at the companionway staring out at the walls of rain.

"I patched that hull for open water," he said. "Not for this. If we touch, Priya — if we touch *anything* —"

"We won't touch."

"The patch won't hold an impact. It won't even hold a scrape. It's sailcloth and prayer."

"Then we don't scrape." She kept her eyes on the water ahead, reading the surface the way her grandmother had taught her, reading the current seams, the little dimples where water flowed over deeper holes, the faint glassy lines where it poured over shallow ones. "Tomas. Go back down. Keep her talking."

He looked at her for a moment. "Her?"

"The boat. Keep her talking. She's scared. I can hear it."

Something moved through Tomas's exhausted face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go, on a better day. "Yeah," he said. "Me too." And he went below, and through the engine room hatch Priya heard him start up again, that low coaxing voice, telling the Kestrel she was a good girl, telling her she was almost home.

The channel narrowed. The bends came faster. Priya called them without pause now, one flowing into the next, her pencil-memory and her father's voice and the water's own memory all running together into a single thread, and Felix steered them down that thread with a precision she hadn't known he had — the showman's hands gone, the bravado gone, just a sailor at a wheel in a storm, doing the work. She caught herself watching him and made herself stop. Watch the water. The water is the chart. The water is the only chart.

Then the current did something her father had never mentioned.

It turned against them.

Priya felt it before she understood it — the Kestrel's speed over the ground sagging, the helm going heavy, the water around the hull changing sound. She checked the bends behind them, counted them off. Third bend. Fourth bend coming. And at the fourth bend, her father had said, the channel kinked hard to starboard and the tide — no. The tide. The flood tide pouring in from the south met the flood tide pouring in through some other entrance she'd never known about, and at the meeting point, the current went slack, then confused, then *backward*, a standing eddy, a whirlpool of bad water right at the narrowest point of the whole passage.

"How far?" Felix shouted. He'd felt it too. Every helmsman feels it.

"Four hundred meters!" She was calculating, fast, the numbers sliding through her head. "The eddy is at the fourth bend! Maybe a hundred meters of bad water! If we can carry way through it —"

"And if we can't?"

"Then we go backward into a reef wall in a monsoon, and I'd rather not describe the rest."

"Then we carry way through it." Felix turned his head and shouted aft: "Tomas! Everything she has! Now!"

The engine — the old, flooded, half-drowned engine that Tomas had nursed back to life with sailcloth and prayer and, apparently, genuine love — coughed. Coughed again. Caught. Priya felt the vibration come up through the deck, rough and uneven and the most beautiful sound she'd ever heard. The Kestrel surged. Eight knots became nine. The rain thickened until the world was nothing but gray noise and the drumbeat of the reef and the groaning hull, and Priya stood at the rail with her hand on the shroud and called the water like a prayer:

"Starboard ten. Steady. Steady — ease — hard to starboard at the white water, *hard*, Felix, don't look at it, just turn —"

The fourth bend came out of the rain like a cliff, because it was one — the reef wall shearing away to starboard, white water standing on it in a frozen roar — and Felix turned the Kestrel hard over into the narrowest, ugliest, most impossible hundred meters of water Priya had ever asked a boat to survive, and the current grabbed them.

The boat stopped.

Not slowed. *Stopped.* The way a car stops. The eddy took them by the bow and held them, and the Kestrel sat in the water at the fourth bend of the Devil's Throat with the monsoon screaming and the engine at full throttle and the hull patch groaning like a dying animal, and for two seconds — two seconds that lasted a geological age — they went nowhere at all.

Priya's whole life narrowed to the water alongside. She could see it now, actually see it through the rain: a line, a seam, a place where the current split, a tongue of smoother water no wider than the boat itself, running down the starboard side of the eddy where the two floods met and cancelled and slid past each other. Her grandmother's voice: *when the river fights itself, it always leaves a seam. Find the seam.*

"Starboard!" she screamed. "Two points! There's a seam! *Trust me!*"

Felix didn't hesitate. That was the thing she would remember, later, when she told this story in ports where nobody believed it: he didn't hesitate. He turned the boat two points to starboard, into what looked like the reef itself, into the white water, and the Kestrel slid into the seam, and the seam took them.

The grip released. The current caught them from behind again, two knots, three, and the Kestrel *ran* — down the last mile of the Throat, walls detonating on either side, hull howling, engine howling, Tomas howling something joyful and profane from below — and then the walls fell away and the channel spilled them out into open water, deep blue-gray open water, rolling monsoon seas, and the reef fell astern like a bad memory.

Priya realized she was laughing. She couldn't remember starting.

* * *

They ran north for an hour before anyone thought to look back.

It was Amara who saw it — she was at the stern, coiling the lead line, and she went still in that way she had, that total stillness, and said quietly, "Priya. Come look."

Priya clipped along the rail and looked aft through a tear in the rain.

The gunboat was at the outer reef, a mile and a half back, stopped dead. Sitting there in the boiling white water at the edge of the false shoal, its bow pointed at the reef wall that its charts promised was continuous, its searchlight sweeping the rain in slow, bewildered arcs. It had followed them to the edge of the known world and found the door locked. It was bigger than them, faster than them, armed, and utterly, magnificently lost.

"They're sounding," Priya said. "Look — they're drifting a line off the bow. He's trying to find the channel."

"Will he?"

She watched the gunboat hang there in the rain, and she thought about its captain, a man who trusted his charts, a man who had never in his life been asked to read water. "Not before dark," she said. "And after dark, in this, with that reef? He'll stand off and wait for the weather. He'll tell himself we wrecked inside. He'll be wrong, but he'll sleep fine."

Felix came aft, and the three of them stood at the stern rail together and watched the Syndicate's power shrink to a gray smudge in the gray rain. Nobody said anything for a while. The Kestrel ran north under her storm canvas, patched hull groaning, taking water that Tomas swore he could keep up with, the monsoon roaring around them like something enormous and indifferent that had, for one afternoon, decided to look the other way.

"You knew," Felix said finally. Not a question, not an accusation. Wonder, maybe. "You stood on that beach and told us you knew a channel that isn't on any chart, and you knew."

"I knew a story my father told me when he was drunk," Priya said. "And I knew how to read water. One true thing and one honest skill. That's all navigation ever is."

Felix looked at her for a long moment, and then at Amara, and then back at the smudge of the gunboat, and Priya saw him arrive at the thought that was already forming in her own chest, cold and heavy under all the exhilaration:

The Syndicate would not stop. Men like that didn't stop. They'd report the channel, or they'd find another way around, or they'd simply wait — at every port, in every harbor, with every debt and every gun. The Throat had bought them today. It hadn't bought them tomorrow.

And then the hull spoke.

Not groaned — *spoke*, a long, deep, structural sound from below the waterline, a sound Priya had never heard a boat make and hoped never to hear again, a sound like a giant flexing its fingers. The Kestrel shuddered. The engine note changed. And Tomas's voice came up through the deck, no longer coaxing, no longer joyful:

"Felix. Get down here. Now. The patch — the patch is going."
