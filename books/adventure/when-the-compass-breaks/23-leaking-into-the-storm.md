## 23. Leaking Into the Storm

Tomas had a theory about boats, one he'd never said out loud because it sounded like superstition and he was, professionally speaking, a man of gaskets and torque specs and things that could be measured. The theory was this: a boat tells you when it's dying. Not in words. In rhythm. In the way the pumps cycle, the way the engine loads, the way the hull flexes against a sea. A good engineer listens the way a doctor listens to a chest, and the Kestrel had been telling him for two days that something was wrong.

He'd been ignoring it the way you ignore a cough in flu season.

Now, wedged in the bilge with a flashlight clenched in his teeth and forty centimeters of green water sloshing around his ribs, he was done ignoring anything.

The patch had held through the Devil's Throat. That was the miracle — sailcloth and epoxy and two of Amara's steel plates, bolted over a crack that should have ended them, holding through water that had tried to fold the boat in half. Tomas had checked it twice in the channel's calm eye and twice it had looked good. Solid. Ugly, but solid.

The third check had been ten minutes ago, and the patch was gone.

Not failed. *Gone.* Torn away clean, like something had reached up from below and peeled it off, and in its place the crack had opened from sixty centimeters to nearly a meter and a half, a black zipper in the hull weeping seawater in a steady, muscular pulse that matched the swell outside. Every time the bow rose and fell, the hull flexed, and the crack breathed, and with every breath the bilge gained what looked like a liter. Maybe two.

The pumps were handling it. For now. Both of them, running flat out, the sound of them filling the engine room like panicked hearts.

Tomas spat the flashlight into his hand and keyed the intercom. "Felix. All of you. Listen to me." He made himself say it plainly, because panic in the engine room traveled upward faster than water. "The patch is off. Crack's open wide. We are taking water faster than I can think. I need everyone who isn't steering to get down here with everything that holds a sealant."

He clicked off before anyone could answer. There was nothing to answer. There was only work.

* * *

They came down the companionway in order: Amara first with the epoxy kit, then Priya soaked to the shoulders from a wave over the deck, then Ines — Ines Varga, who owned a yacht worth more than this entire crew's combined futures, on her knees in the bilge water without a word of complaint, sleeves rolled, hair plastered flat. Felix stayed at the helm; nobody had to tell him. The sea outside had stopped being weather and started being a decision the ocean had already made.

The monsoon had arrived the way a wall arrives.

Tomas had chased storms his whole life. He knew their grammar — the build, the peak, the long grumbling decay. This was different. This was a monsoon's opening statement, a system two hundred miles across with no interest in them at all, no malice, no mercy, nothing personal. That was the thing he could never explain to people who hadn't been out in one: the sea wasn't angry. The sea didn't know they existed. They were a fleck of paint on a door that a hurricane was walking through, and the door didn't care.

"Talk to me, Tomas," Felix's voice came down the intercom, tight and level.

"Crack's a meter and a half, maybe more, running along a weld seam. I can't get to the back side of it. I can slow it, maybe. I can't stop it." He was already mixing epoxy, hands moving on their own, muscle memory doing the thinking while the rest of him did the arithmetic he hated. Pump capacity versus inflow. Battery versus pump load. Hours. He didn't like the number. "How long to the nearest shelter?"

Priya's voice, from the chart table above, shouting down the companionway: "Nothing close. Nothing real. There's an atoll — unnamed, not charted for depth — forty miles north-northeast. If it exists. If the pass is open."

"If," Tomas muttered, and pressed the first sailcloth patch into place with his forearm while Amara held the light.

The epoxy grabbed. For a moment — one whole, sweet moment — the flow slowed to a trickle, and Tomas let himself believe. That was the trap of this work, the thing that kept him coming back: the belief always came first, and the ocean always collected on it afterward.

* * *

For an hour, they held the line.

It was almost beautiful, in the way a losing battle is almost beautiful. Amara mixing, Tomas placing, Ines holding plates against the hull with her whole body weight while the boat rolled and the water in the bilge climbed her ribs and she said nothing, absolutely nothing, just braced and held. Felix's voice on the intercom calling the swells so they could time their work between them. Priya's course corrections from above, threading the boat at angles that kept the crack out of the worst of the wave action.

Tomas found himself grinning at one point, teeth gritted, epoxy to his elbows, and he thought: *this is the job. Not the salvage, not the money, not Ines's promises. This. Five people in a flooding engine room, holding a line against something that doesn't know they're there.*

Then the second patch let go.

It didn't tear this time. It simply slid, the hull flexing under it like a living thing shrugging off a hand, and the crack exhaled a gout of water that hit Tomas in the chest and knocked him against the engine mounts. The bilge rose ten centimeters in under a minute. He heard it — heard the *level* of it, the way the pump intakes gurgled, the way the rhythm changed.

"Pumps are losing," he shouted up the companionway. "Both of them. I've got maybe — " he did the math and hated it — "two hours. Less if the sea builds. Felix, we don't make forty miles. Not in this. We make twenty, maybe, and then we're a reef hazard with our names on it."

Silence from above. Just the wind, and the pumps, and the water.

Then Felix's voice, and Tomas would remember it later, would turn it over in his hands like a stone: "Then we find twenty miles of miracle. Priya — that atoll. Take us there. Tomas, keep us floating until we arrive."

"Aye, Captain," Tomas said, and it was the first time he'd said the word without irony in eleven years.

* * *

The next hour and a half was the longest of Tomas's life, and he'd once spent three days adrift in a liferaft off Palawan.

He fought the hull the only way left: by sacrificing everything else. He pulled the floor panels to lighten load. He shut down the watermaker, the freezer, every system that drew power the pumps needed. He rigged the manual backup pump — a hand-cranked relic he'd kept because he was sentimental about old machines — and put Ines on it, and the woman who'd bought and sold half the harbors in Southeast Asia cranked that handle like she was grinding her own bones into flour.

The water still rose. Slower now. But rising.

Tomas knelt in it, up to his chest, flashlight beam dancing across the black zipper of the crack, and felt the thing he'd been holding off all day finally arrive: helplessness. Not panic — he was past panic, panic was a luxury for people with dry socks. This was the older, quieter thing underneath. The understanding that the sea was not negotiating. That all his skill, all his gaskets and torque specs and listening, had bought them hours, not salvation. That the ocean would take this boat whenever it decided to, and everything he did from here was just choosing the hour of the meeting.

He thought about the crew above him. Priya, who navigated by faith. Amara, who'd bled for this boat. Felix, who'd finally become a captain instead of a man fleeing a debt. And Ines, cranking that pump like a penance.

*Not tonight,* Tomas told the crack, and pressed his palm flat against the weeping seam as if he could hold it shut by stubbornness alone. *Not these people. Take the boat if you have to. But not them.*

The Kestrel groaned and drove north into the dark, toward an atoll that might not exist, carrying five people and a rising sea.

And then, through the hull, Tomas felt it — faint, rhythmic, unmistakable.

Breakers. Ahead and to starboard.

Either salvation, or the reef that would end them.
