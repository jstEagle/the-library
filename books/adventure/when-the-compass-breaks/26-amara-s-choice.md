## 26. Amara's Choice

The letter weighed almost nothing.

That was the thing Amara couldn't stop thinking about as she sat in the lee of the overturned skiff, the driftwood fire burned down to orange coals, the island breathing around her in wind and palm-rattle and the long slow percussion of surf. Four pages. Cheap paper gone soft with forty years of salt-damp, sealed inside a waterproof tube that had crossed an ocean and a war and a family's worth of silence. Four pages that could strip Felix Eriksen of everything he'd crossed the world to reclaim.

Her name was in the third paragraph.

Not Amara — her mother's. *Siti Rahayu, partner and co-author of record.* Her grandmother had signed it too, before Amara was born, before the Halcyon Star went down with its cargo of charts and patents and promises. The letter named the Rahayus as equals. Named the profits as shared. Named Felix's father as the man who had signed the papers and then sailed away with the only copies, leaving a fishing family in a fishing village to watch a survey vessel they'd helped fund disappear over the horizon and never come back.

*Stolen twice*, Amara thought. Once by her father's signature, once by the sea.

She had read it eleven times since they'd surfaced from the green dark. She knew the creases now, the way the ink had feathered where a drop of seawater had found it, the place where her mother's name sat on the page like a door someone had nailed shut. She could have recited it. She had, once, quietly, to herself, in the voice she imagined her mother would have used — the flat, careful voice of a woman reading a document she already knew she'd lose.

The fire popped. Down the beach, the crew slept in a scatter of shapes: Priya curled against the hull of the beached Kestrel with a chart case for a pillow, Tomas flat on his back with his boots off for the first time in days, his hands still smelling of epoxy even in sleep, if hands could do such a thing. Ines was a silhouette at the tideline, sitting upright, watching the black water like a woman waiting for a specific wave. And Felix — Felix was on his side near the fire, one arm under his head, the broken compass in its case beside his hand the way other men slept beside a weapon.

He'd carried that compass through a sinking ship. She'd watched him find it in the dark of the Halcyon Star's chart room, in the same drawer where the strongbox had been hidden, and she'd watched his face when he understood what else the drawer held — the sabotage logs, his father's own handwriting, the confession in technical language that no one had ever meant him to read. *Opened the sea cocks myself. Let the Syndicate chase their patents to the bottom. Forgive me, Siti. Forgive me, both of you.*

His father had scuttled his own ship rather than let the Syndicate have the inheritance — and had drowned Amara's mother's share along with it. Not malice. Worse than malice: fear, dressed up as principle. A man so afraid of thieves that he became one.

Amara turned the letter over in her hands. The legal language was old-fashioned but clean. She'd shown it to Priya, who read fast and understood contracts the way sharks understood blood. *Partnership of record. Equal shares. Binding upon recovery of the vessel or its contents.* Priya had said what they were both thinking: with this paper, and the patents recovered, Amara didn't need Felix. Didn't need any of them. A lawyer in Jakarta, a week of filings, and the Rahayu name would stand alone on documents worth more than the whole village had earned in three generations.

She could take it alone.

She had earned it alone. That was the part that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Her mother had drowned — not on the Halcyon Star, but two years later, diving the wreck illegally, trying to bring up proof, trying to finish what the signature had started. Amara had grown up on that absence the way Felix had grown up on his. She had learned these reefs by diving them blind, had memorized the archipelago current by current, had stowed away on a stranger's boat because the sea had finally, after twenty years, arranged itself into a shape that pointed at the truth.

And the truth was a letter. And the letter was hers. And the choice was hers.

The coals shifted. A log collapsed in a small eruption of sparks, and Felix stirred in his sleep — not waking, just turning, his hand finding the compass case and drawing it an inch closer, the way a child draws in a blanket. Amara watched him do it and felt the stone in her chest turn over.

He didn't know yet. About the letter's full weight. He knew the Rahayus were named — she'd told them that much on the beach, her voice shaking, watching his face go through shock and guilt and something rawer — but he didn't know the clause. The one Priya had found at two in the morning, reading by flashlight: *the surviving partner or their heirs may elect sole claim upon recovery, the other party's interest extinguished.* Sole claim. Extinguished. Her mother's lawyer had been thorough. Her mother had been thorough. A woman who had spent her last years preparing her daughter for a fight, even from beyond the edge of a regulator's hiss.

Amara could wake him. Could lay the pages on the sand between them and say *read paragraph seven* and watch the inheritance he'd sold his soul's quiet for dissolve into her hands, legally, cleanly, with the Syndicate's gunboats still ringing the lagoon to make it worse. She could be justified. She could be right. Every ledger she'd kept in her head for twenty years balanced on exactly that page.

She looked at the crew instead.

Tomas, who had argued to keep her aboard when Felix wanted her thrown into the harbor, on the simple grounds that she was hungry and so were they and hunger made better company than pride. Priya, who had caught the forged charts and said nothing to Ines, only to Felix, because loyalty was a thing she did quietly and completely. Ines at the tideline, who had engineered all of this — the job, the wreck, the reckoning — because her own brother had died on the Halcyon Star, and who had confessed it last night with her voice finally, finally unarmored, and then sat alone afterward like a woman who had set down something heavy and didn't yet know how to stand without it.

And Felix. Who had learned, in the space of one dive, that his father was not the man in his stories. Who had come up from the green dark holding a strongbox and a shattered image, and who had stood on this beach an hour ago and said, to all of them, in a voice scraped down to nothing: *It was never ours. None of it. I'm sorry. I'm sorry it took me this long to say it.*

He'd said it to her. Not to the letter. To her.

The wind came off the water and moved the fire's smoke sideways, and Amara did the thing she had been avoiding since the sun went down: she took out the photograph. The one she'd checked against the faces on the Kestrel's deck from a fishing skiff a lifetime ago, a hundred miles and a monsoon ago. Her mother, young, standing on the Halcyon Star's rail with a survey team laughing around her — and at the edge of the frame, half-turned, a man with Felix's jaw and an older man's eyes. Her mother had kept that photograph her whole life. Had kept the man in it her whole life too, Amara suspected, in the way you keep a wound: not for the pain, but to remember it was real.

*She loved the work,* Amara's grandmother had said, once, the only time she'd ever spoken of it. *She loved the sea the way he did. That was the problem. Two people who love the same sea will always be fighting over which of them it belongs to.*

Amara folded the photograph away. The stone in her chest hadn't gone. It had just changed shape, the way stones do when the sea works them long enough — from something sharp into something you could carry.

Because here was the thing she had understood at the tideline an hour ago, watching Ines sit vigil, watching Felix sleep with his hand on a broken compass: vengeance was a solo voyage. That was its whole design. It put you alone on the water with your grievance and called the loneliness justice. Her mother had sailed it. Her mother had died of it — not the drowning, the *sailing*, the twenty years of a life narrowed to a single heading. Amara had watched the letter all evening expecting it to feel like a weapon. It felt like a rudder with no boat attached.

She could take the fortune alone. And then she would have the fortune, and the lawyers, and the vindication, and she would have them in a village house with her grandmother, alone, the way her mother had been alone, the way Felix's father had been alone, the way every person in this whole rotten beautiful story had been alone right up until the moment the sea or the Syndicate or their own silence finished the job.

Or.

Or she could tear out paragraph seven.

Not the letter — the *clause*. Keep the letter. Keep her mother's name on it. Keep the truth, all of it, the scuttled ship and the betrayed partnership and the fear that had made a good man do a bad thing. But burn the part that let her win by making him lose. Stand in front of the crew tomorrow — because there would be a tomorrow, Syndicate or no Syndicate, storm or no storm; she had stopped believing otherwise somewhere in the Devil's Throat, though she couldn't say when — and say: *partners. Equal shares. The Rahayu name restored, the Eriksen name restored, and the money split the way it should have been split before either of us was born.*

And the fund. She'd been turning that over too, in the dark, the way you turn a new idea over to find its edges. The patents weren't just worth money. They were worth *ongoing* money — survey rights, chart licenses, the whole architecture of ownership her mother had helped design and Felix's father had weaponized. Every coastal village in the archipelago had a story like hers. Every one. She'd grown up hearing them the way other children grew up hearing folktales. The inheritance could stay an inheritance, or it could become something else: a ledger with everyone's name in it.

*Forgiveness as an act of will.* Her grandmother had a saying — everything was a saying with that woman — *Anger is a current. It will carry you somewhere. The only question is whether you chose the destination.* Amara had always thought that meant resisting the current. Sitting here in the dark with a letter that could make her rich and righteous and utterly alone, she finally understood it meant steering.

You didn't kill the current. You chose where it took you.

She looked at the letter a long time. The fire had gone down to a red eye. Somewhere out past the reef, thunder muttered — the monsoon circling, patient, the Syndicate somewhere inside it, and forty hours of rain between this island and whatever came next. There would be no better moment than this one. There was never a better moment. That was the lie people told themselves to avoid the moments they had.

Amara stood, folded the letter along its old creases, and walked down the beach to where Felix slept.

She crouched. He woke the way sailors wake — all at once, silent, his eyes finding her face before his body finished moving. She saw him register the letter in her hands and she watched something close over in him, a flinch he was too tired to hide, the flinch of a man who believed he deserved whatever was coming.

"Paragraph seven," she said quietly.

He closed his eyes. "I know. Priya told me. Sole claim." He opened them again, and there it was — not fear, exactly. Readiness. A man braced at the wheel for a wave he'd decided not to dodge. "It's yours, Amara. It was always yours. I'll sign whatever—"

"Listen to me." She sat down in the sand beside him, close enough that the letter was between them, and she did the hardest thing she had done since her mother's funeral, harder than the dive, harder than the stowaway night, harder than holding his hands steady on the wheel while the past came up at them like a corpse. She gave the current a destination.

"I'm not taking it alone," she said. "And you're not giving it away. Neither of those. We're going to do what the letter says in every paragraph *except* the one my mother wrote when she was angriest. Partners. Equal. And the patents go into a fund — for the villages, for the families, for everyone the Halcyon Star took something from. Yours and mine both."

Felix stared at her. The firelight moved on his face and she watched him try to find the trap in it, because he was a man who had been taught by debt that every gift had a hook. "You could have everything," he said finally. "You know that. Legally. No one would—"

"I know what I could have." Her voice didn't shake. She'd made sure of that, practicing it against the sound of the surf for the last hour, and now it came out level and true. "I watched my mother have everything she could have, alone, and I watched it eat her alive. I'm not making her mistake. I'm making her *point*." She held the letter out to him. "Tear it with me."

He looked at the paper. Then at her. Then he took it — carefully, the way he'd handled the strongbox, the way he handled everything that mattered, like it might break — and he found paragraph seven, and he read it once, and she saw his throat work.

"You're sure," he said. Not a question. A last chance for her to take it back, and they both knew it.

"The Rahayus don't take things back," Amara said. "We steer."

They tore it together. Two hands on the page, the sound small in all that darkness, the sound of twenty years of a single heading ending. Felix held the two halves a moment, then stood and walked to the fire and fed them to the coals, and Amara watched her mother's anger go up in a breath of flame and sparks and rise over the black water, and she felt — not light, exactly. Lighter. The stone still there. But shaped now for carrying.

Felix came back and sat down beside her, and for a while neither of them said anything, because some things don't need saying, they just need witnessing. Down the beach, Ines had turned from the tideline. Amara saw the silhouette watching, saw the stillness of someone who has just seen a thing they'll think about for years, and Amara met her gaze across the dark and held it, and Ines — after a long moment — nodded once, slow, like a debt being entered in a ledger.

It was Priya who broke the silence, sitting up against the Kestrel's hull with her flashlight already in her hand, because Priya slept the way she navigated: lightly, and always half-awake to what mattered.

"Amara." Her voice was careful. "The tide gauge. I've been watching it since midnight." She turned the flashlight toward the waterline, and Amara followed the beam to the wooden marker Priya had driven into the sand at dusk — and saw, with a coldness that started in her stomach and moved outward, how much higher the water had climbed. How much faster than the tide tables allowed.

"That's not the tide," Priya said. "That's the storm surge. The monsoon's turned. It's coming around the island's north point." She was already unrolling a chart across her knees, her hands steady, her voice not. "And if the surge is here, the Syndicate won't wait for daylight. They'll come with it."

* * *

The rain arrived twenty minutes later, all at once, the way the monsoon did everything — and out on the black water, at the mouth of the lagoon, Amara heard the engines.
