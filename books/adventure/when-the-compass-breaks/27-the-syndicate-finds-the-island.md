## 27. The Syndicate Finds the Island

Dawn came to the lagoon the color of a bruise going yellow at the edges, and Felix Eriksen woke because the birds stopped.

That was the thing about this island — he'd learned its rhythms in three days without meaning to. The terns started before first light, a ragged argument in the palms. The surf kept its own time against the reef, patient as a metronome. And every morning Amara was up first, walking the tideline with her coffee in a cracked enamel cup, checking the Kestrel where she lay beached and broken-backed on the sand like a whale that had given up.

But today the birds were silent.

Felix came out of the lean-to they'd built against the hull and stood in the gray light, and he understood why.

Boats. Four of them, maybe five — hard to count through the morning haze — sitting in a loose arc across the mouth of the lagoon. Black hulls. No lights. No wake. They had come in dark and quiet and arranged themselves like fingers closing around something small.

"Amara," he said, and his voice came out flat and calm, which surprised him. Calm was new. Calm was what happened when you'd been afraid so long you'd worn a groove in it.

She was already standing at the treeline, the photograph she carried everywhere folded into her fist. She didn't look at him. "I counted six," she said. "There's one holding off past the reef. Bigger. That'll be the command boat."

"You slept on the beach."

"Somebody had to watch the water." She finally turned, and her face in the half-light was unreadable in the way it always was when she was deciding how much truth to spend. "They found us faster than I thought they would."

"The storm drove us here. Storms drive everyone somewhere." He rubbed his eyes. "Ines?"

"Gone since before midnight. Her skiff's missing from the cove." A pause. "So either she ran, or—"

"Or she walked into their camp with her hands up and a smile on." Felix felt something twist in his chest that he refused to name. Hope, maybe. Or its ugly cousin. "Get Tomas. Wake Priya. Nobody goes down to the water alone."

* * *

By full sunrise the whole crew stood at the treeline, and the Syndicate had sent their first boat ashore.

It grounded on the white sand fifty meters out with a hiss, and two men stepped into the shin-deep water and walked up the beach like men arriving at a resort. The one in front was tall and silver-templed and wore a linen shirt that probably cost more than the Kestrel had, back when she'd been worth anything. Felix knew the face from debt negotiations and worse — Voss. Head collector for the Meridian Syndicate's eastern reach. A man who'd once told Felix, over a table in a smoke-filled office in Surabaya, that debts were just promises that hadn't finished dying yet.

Voss stopped at the edge of the wet sand and spread his hands, palms out, a gesture of peace so rehearsed it looked printed.

"Felix Eriksen." His voice carried easily over the water. "You look terrible. Monsoon agrees with no one."

"You found my island," Felix called back. "Congratulations. It doesn't have a name. Neither do most graves."

"I prefer to think of it as an opportunity." Voss took another step forward, unhurried, and Felix noticed the detail that mattered: the two men behind him weren't reaching for weapons. They were carrying a folding table. And a case. "May we speak? You have my word no one fires a shot this morning."

"Your word is a currency I've seen devalued."

"Then consider it an opening offer." Voss smiled, and it was almost warm. Almost human. That was the worst part of him — he could do almost-human better than anyone Felix had ever met. "Bring your people if you like. Transparency builds trust."

Felix looked back at his crew. Tomas, arms crossed, jaw set like poured concrete. Priya with her tablet hugged to her chest, already running numbers nobody wanted. Amara beside him, still as driftwood. And further back, half-hidden by the palms, the others — hungry, exhausted, salt-cracked, watching him the way you watch a man defuse something.

"We talk here," Felix said. "Table stays on your side of the sand."

"As you wish." Voss gestured, and the table was set up anyway, right at the waterline, absurd and formal, and the case went on top of it. "Then let me be brief, because the weather won't be. The barometer is falling, Mr. Eriksen. You've seen the satellite feeds — or your navigator has. Another system is coming, and it's larger than the last one. This island will be underwater from the north beach inward within forty hours. Your vessel is dead. Your supplies are measured in days. And you are, if we're being honest, stranded on a sandbar with a fortune in a box."

"And you're offering to rescue us."

"I'm offering to *settle* with you." Voss opened the case. Inside, on green felt, sat papers. Clean, crisp, stamped. "Every ledger bearing your name — erased. Every marker held against your crew — dissolved. Passage to any port in the archipelago. Medical attention for your injured—" his eyes flicked toward where Amara's bandaged arm showed under her sleeve, "—and a new boat, if you want one. All of it, freely given."

The silence stretched. Somewhere behind Felix, Tomas shifted his weight.

"What's the price?" Felix asked, though he already knew. He'd known since he saw the boats.

"The strongbox. Its contents. Everything recovered from the Halcyon Star, surrendered intact and unopened by anyone but my own people." Voss closed the case gently, like tucking in a child. "And Ines Varga, if she's on this island. Though I'm told she may have already settled her own account differently."

"She's not here."

"No," Voss agreed pleasantly. "She isn't. Which is interesting, isn't it?"

Felix kept his face still. Behind him, he heard Priya exhale slowly through her nose — the sound of a woman doing arithmetic she hated.

"You should know," Voss went on, conversational now, a man discussing tides, "that I've read the recovery manifests. Patents. Deeds. Survey charts of three separate fields. The Syndicate has spent nineteen years and a great deal of money trying to find the Halcyon Star. You found her in eleven days. That's not luck, Mr. Eriksen. That's inheritance. And inheritance," he said, spreading his hands again, "is exactly the kind of asset we respect. We don't want to take it from you. We want to *buy* it. Generously. With your freedom attached."

"There it is," Felix said quietly. "Mercy with a receipt."

"Call it what you like. But look around you." Voss turned, slow, taking in the wrecked Kestrel, the lean-to, the fire pit, the crew standing ragged in the trees. "Look at them. They followed you into a monsoon on a dying hull for a payday that never came. How many more storms do they owe you? How much more of their lives does your father's ghost get to spend?" He turned back, and his voice softened into something almost kind. "This is the deal that ends it, Felix. Not next season. Not after one more run. Today. Everyone walks away clean. You keep your people alive. Someone else carries the weight of the Halcyon Star."

And there it was — the hook inside the bait, laid bare. Because Voss wasn't wrong about any of it. The boat was dead. The food was short. Amara's arm was infected at the edges despite everything Tomas knew about wound care. Forty hours of rain coming, and after it, nothing.

All they had to do was hand over the box. The box that held Amara's family's name alongside his father's crime. The box that was the only proof left of what had actually happened aboard the Halcyon Star — proof that, if it ever reached daylight, would burn the Syndicate's claims to ash in a courtroom. Which was, Felix understood suddenly and completely, exactly why Voss needed it unopened. Why the collectors had chased them across two hundred miles of storm. Not for patents.

For the letter.

"You don't want the value," Felix said. "You want the evidence."

Something flickered across Voss's face — fast, quickly smoothed — but it was enough. Beside Felix, he heard Amara breathe in, sharp.

"I want a clean resolution," Voss said.

"You want the only document that says your Syndicate helped sink that ship." Felix took a step forward onto the open sand, alone, and the Syndicate men behind the table shifted, hands drifting casually toward jackets. "My father stole from you? Maybe. Or maybe he stole back what you'd stolen first, and the letter in that box names names. Nineteen years you've been hunting paper, Voss. You don't send six boats for patents. Patents can be litigated. Letters can't."

The wind moved through the palms. Out past the reef, the sea had begun to change color, that deep bruised green it got before it turned ugly.

Voss studied him for a long moment, and then — infuriatingly — he smiled again, wider this time, genuinely pleased, like a teacher whose student had finally shown his work.

"There he is," Voss said softly. "Erik Eriksen's son. Your father had the same flaw, you know. He'd rather drown than sign." He glanced at his watch, theatrical, unnecessary. "I'll tell you what generosity looks like. You have until the rain starts. When the first drops fall, the offer closes, and my boats stop being negotiators." He picked up his case, nodded courteously to each of them in turn — Tomas, Priya, and last, longest, Amara, which Felix did not miss — and walked back down the beach into the water, climbing aboard without a splash out of place.

The boats withdrew to the middle distance and sat there, patient as the tide.

Nobody spoke until the sound of their engines faded entirely.

"He looked at me," Amara said finally. Her voice was strange. Flat. "At the end. Like he knew me."

"He read the same letter we did," Felix said. "He knows your family's name is in it. He knows what it means." He turned to face them all, and the words came out before he could dress them up. "Here's the truth, then. If we give them the box, we live. Probably. We walk away debt-free with money in our pockets and nothing in our hands. If we don't, we've got forty hours, a dead boat, and the biggest storm of the season coming down our throats with guns behind it."

"So we're voting," Tomas said.

"We're voting."

Priya spoke without looking up from her tablet. "There's a channel." Everyone turned. She was pale, and her thumb was moving across the screen like it was trying to outrun her doubt. "Northwest of here. Through the reef shelf. It's not on any chart I have — not Ines's, not the survey data, none of them. But the bathymetry anomalies are consistent with a passage. Deep enough, maybe, for a shallow-draft hull. Maybe."

"The Kestrel can't float," Tomas said.

"The Kestrel can't," Priya agreed. She looked up, and her eyes went to the tree line behind them — to the cove on the island's far side, where a sleek shape sat half-hidden under palm fronds and netting. Ines's yacht's tender. The sister-sloop. Fast, shallow, patched together with three days of Tomas's stubbornness and sailcloth. "But she might."

Silence. The surf ticked. Somewhere inland, a single tern finally remembered the morning and cried out.

"That channel is suicide," Tomas said. "Uncharted, in a building sea, with a gunboat behind us."

"Yes," Priya said simply. "Probably."

"Then it's the best offer on the table," Amara said.

Felix looked at his crew — his broke, betrayed, half-drowned, unpaid crew — waiting to see who would flinch. Nobody flinched. Tomas spat into the sand, which was, Felix had learned, the closest thing the engineer had to a signature on a contract.

"Forty hours," Felix said. "Tomas, the sloop floats by tomorrow night or we're all swimming. Priya, I want that channel on paper even if you have to draw it yourself. Amara—" he hesitated, because the next part cost him, "—the box stays with you. Whatever happens, whatever we decide, it doesn't go in my hands. It doesn't go in anyone's hands but yours."

Amara looked at him for a long moment. Something passed between them that had nothing to do with charts or debts.

"And Ines?" Priya asked quietly. "If she's in their camp — if she traded herself—"

"Then she bought us time," Felix said, "and we'd damn well better not waste it."

Out past the reef, the first cloud bank of the new system was rising off the horizon, black and enormous and slow as a continent. Felix watched it come and felt, beneath the fear, something he hadn't felt in years: not dread.

A heading.

"Tomas," he said. "Get me forty hours' work done in thirty-six. We're leaving before the rain does."
