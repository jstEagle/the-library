## 28. Forty Hours of Rain

The rain came in sideways, like it had somewhere better to be and we were just in the way.

Tomas Reyes stood in the engine room of a boat that wasn't his — not really, not yet — with a wrench in one hand and a flashlight clenched between his teeth, listening to the sound every engineer learns to dread: water where water shouldn't be. Not rushing. Not gushing. Just *there*, a soft slosh beneath the deck plates that hadn't been there an hour ago.

He spat the flashlight into his palm and crouched over the bilge.

"Come on," he told her quietly. "Not now. I know you're tired. I'm tired too."

The Kestrel lay on her side in the shallows of the nameless island's leeward beach, half-beached, half-drowned, her starboard hull opened like a sardine tin against the reef on the way in. She was done. Tomas had known it the moment they'd driven her ashore — known it in his hands, in the way she'd shuddered under him like a dying animal. Twelve years he'd kept her running on duct tape and stubbornness, and twelve years of stubbornness only buys you so much.

But the Kestrel had a sister.

*Had.* Past tense, mostly. The *Meridian Dawn* — no relation to the Syndicate, though God knew the irony wasn't lost on anyone — sat rotting in the island's shallow lagoon, half-sunk in sand, her mast snapped, her decks green with a decade of algae. Ines had spotted her from the ridge yesterday and gone very quiet, the way people go quiet when they recognize a face at a funeral.

"She was a charter boat," Ines had said. "Same yard as the Kestrel. Same year. Her owner defaulted to the Syndicate and they ran her aground here rather than pay to tow her out." A pause. "They leave their wrecks everywhere. Like scars."

Tomas had spent two days in her bilge already, and what he'd found there was ugly but honest: rotten planking, seized seacocks, an engine that looked like something dredged from a swamp. But the hull lines were good. The keel was true. And unlike the Kestrel, whose bones were broken, the Dawn's bones were merely neglected.

Neglected, Tomas could fix. He'd built a career on neglected.

The problem was everything else.

* * *

They gathered in the Kestrel's cabin that evening, forty hours into the rain, because the Syndicate camp on the far point had floodlights now, and floodlights meant generators, and generators meant they weren't planning to leave.

Felix stood at the chart table with his father's broken compass in front of him, its glass cracked, its needle swinging loose and useless. Amara sat across from him, her arm still bound from the fight with the agent — the man who'd turned out to be the Syndicate's eyes aboard them since day one, the quiet deckhand named Rusk who'd smiled at everyone and sabotaged the pumps by lamplight. Priya had her satellite terminal open, its screen dimmed to almost nothing, the blue glow carving hollows under her eyes.

Ines was absent. That was the thing none of them said aloud. Ines had walked into the Syndicate camp at dawn with her hands raised and her yacht's papers in her jacket, offering herself and everything she owned as collateral, and she hadn't come back. Through binoculars they'd watched her standing in the rain beside the collectors' lead man, talking, gesturing once toward the lagoon. Buying time, Felix insisted. Stalling. Nobody disagreed out loud. Everybody thought it anyway: or selling us.

"The window is Thursday night into Friday morning," Priya said. Her voice was hoarse; she'd been coughing for two days and refusing to admit it. "After that, the monsoon trough settles in for real. Ten days minimum of sustained thirty-knot winds and worse. We don't move before Thursday, we're not moving at all."

"And the Syndicate knows that too," Felix said. "That's why they're sitting on the point. They don't have to chase us. They just have to wait for the weather to do their work."

"So we wait for the weather to do ours instead." Amara leaned forward. "There's a channel."

Tomas watched Felix's face do something complicated. "The Devil's Throat. You've mentioned it. Priya says—"

"Priya says no chart shows it, which is true." Amara pulled a folded sheet of waterproof paper from inside her shirt — she'd been carrying it like a relic since the wreck dive, and maybe it was one. Hand-drawn soundings, faded ink, annotations in two different hands. One of the hands, Tomas knew, was Felix's mother's. "It runs behind the northern reef line, through the old lava shelf. Two hundred meters wide at the entrance, narrowing to sixty at the bend. Depth's marginal at low tide, comfortable at high. It comes out on the ocean side north of the Syndicate's position — puts us downwind and downcurrent of them before they can even get a boat around the point."

"If it exists," Priya said. Not hostile. Careful. She'd earned the right to be careful; her charts had lied to her once already this voyage, politely, deliberately, and she'd nearly put them on a reef for it.

"It exists. My father ran it twice. My grandfather drew it." Amara tapped the paper. "But it exists only in conditions nobody sane would choose. The entrance is invisible except in heavy weather — the break in the reef shows itself when the swell wraps around the point and flattens a lane through the surf. You need big seas to see the door. Which means you need a storm to find it, and you need to be good enough to survive what you find."

Silence. Rain hammered the cabin top like applause from a hostile crowd.

"You're describing threading a needle," Tomas said, "in a hurricane, in a boat held together with epoxy and prayer."

"I'm describing the only door out of this room," Amara said.

* * *

Tomas didn't sleep that night. He went back to the Dawn.

She lay canted in the shallows, and in the dark, with the rain coming down in ropes, working on her felt like grave-robbing. His headlamp made a small yellow world around his hands and nothing else existed outside it — the bilge, the planking, the slow patient work of making a dead thing float again.

The sister-sloop. That was the phrase Felix had used, and Tomas had hated it immediately, because it made the job sound sentimental, and sentiment was how boats killed people. But standing knee-deep in the Dawn's flooded bilge, he understood it. She *was* the Kestrel's sister. Same hull mold, same beam, same quirky hard chine aft that made both boats pound in a head sea and surf like demons in a following one. He knew this hull the way you know your own handwriting. Every repair he'd ever improvised on the Kestrel, he could repeat here from memory.

The problem was material. The Kestrel had given everything she had getting them onto this beach, and what remained of her stores was a sad inventory: two sheets of marine plywood gone soft at the edges, a drum of epoxy maybe a third full, sailcloth patches, a box of fasteners, and the Kestrel's own dismantled parts, which felt like cannibalism even though it was exactly what the Kestrel would have wanted.

Her engine was the worst of it. The Dawn's original diesel had seized years ago — he'd freed the crankshaft enough to confirm it was a paperweight — which meant transplanting the Kestrel's engine, and the Kestrel's engine was the only reason any of them were alive, and pulling it out of a beached boat in a storm was a two-day job he had maybe sixteen hours for.

So he did what engineers do. He triaged.

Engine first. Everything else was decoration.

By three in the morning he'd rigged a tripod from the Dawn's broken spars and a block from the Kestrel's mast step, and he was underneath the Kestrel's engine with the mounting bolts soaking in penetrating oil, when boots splashed up the beach behind him.

"For the record," Priya's voice said, "I think this plan is insane."

"For the record," Tomas said, not looking up, "I agree completely."

"But?"

He sat back on his heels. She stood in the rain without a jacket, holding a thermos, looking like a woman who'd done the math on her own exhaustion and decided to ignore the result. "But the numbers work," she said. "Barely. If the channel's real, if the boat floats, if the engine starts, if the weather holds its pattern for six more hours than forecast — then we clear the reef line before dawn Friday and the gunboat never sees us. Four ifs. Maybe five."

"That's better odds than most of my marriages," Tomas said.

Priya almost smiled. Then she crouched next to him, sheltering the thermos cup from the rain, and poured him coffee that tasted like it had been brewed from driftwood and regret. For a while they just listened to the storm.

"Tomas." Quiet now. "If the channel isn't real — if Amara's wrong, or her family's chart is wrong, or the shoals have shifted in twenty years — we die in there. All of us. And Ines is back there with those people, and whatever she traded for our time, it won't cover a wreck in the Throat."

"I know."

"Do you?" She looked at him, and in the headlamp's spill her face was stripped of everything professional. "Because I keep charts for a living, and everything in me screams that we're about to trust a piece of paper older than I am, drawn by dead people, in conditions designed to hide the truth from us. And I'll do it. I'll call the depths and call the turns. But someone has to say out loud that this might be how all of us end."

Tomas drank the terrible coffee. Thought about his father, who'd been a fisherman, who'd taught him that the sea doesn't take sides, it just takes. Thought about the crew sleeping in the Kestrel's cabin right now — Felix with the compass under his pillow like a child, Amara with her ruined arm, Priya's own bunk empty because she was out here arguing with him in the rain.

"Somebody already said it," he said finally. "You just did. Loud and clear. So now it's said, and we can get to work." He set down the cup. "Hand me that breaker bar. This engine's coming out tonight whether it likes it or not."

* * *

They worked through the fourth day and into the fifth, and the rain never stopped, and neither did they.

It became, in the way that misery sometimes becomes communion, almost joyful. Tomas ran the operation like a field hospital: Felix and Amara on the transom planking, replacing the rotten sections with plywood laminated in epoxy and backed with the Kestrel's salvaged frames; Priya on rigging, splicing the Dawn's surviving halyards to the Kestrel's spare jib and a mainsail sewn together from two ruined ones — a patchwork sail for a patchwork boat, which Tomas privately considered the most honest thing he'd ever stepped. Even the rescued Syndicate sailors helped, after Felix made the call — the collector himself hauling epoxy buckets with the sullen dignity of a man doing penance, which, Tomas reflected, was roughly what it was.

The engine came out at dusk on the fifth day, swung on the spar tripod through driving rain while six people hauled on lines and Tomas guided it with his shoulder and a vocabulary that would have gotten him excommunicated in three religions. They lowered it into the Dawn's bilge on rollers cut from spars, aligned the mounts by feel and flashlight, and bolted it home at midnight.

At 12:40 a.m., soaked to the soul, Tomas bled the fuel line, said a word to no god in particular, and hit the starter.

The engine caught on the third crank. Caught, stumbled, caught again — and settled into a rough, smoky, magnificent idle that echoed off the lagoon like a heartbeat finding its rhythm again.

Nobody cheered. They were past cheering. But Amara put her good hand flat on the deck planking, and Felix closed his eyes, and Priya laughed once — a short, startled sound, like a sneeze of relief — and Tomas stood in the bilge with spray from the cooling system misting his face and felt something he hadn't felt since the Kestrel broke on the reef:

Pride. Ugly, improvised, epoxy-and-prayer pride.

"She'll do," he said. "She's not pretty. She's not fast. But she'll do."

"She sounds like a tractor with asthma," Felix said.

"She sounds like *us*," Tomas said, and that shut everybody up in the best way.

* * *

Thursday evening, the wind shifted.

Tomas felt it before anyone announced it — the quality of the rain changed, from assault to something steadier, and the barometer, which he'd taped inside the Dawn's companionway, began its long slide. Priya confirmed it at the evening council, her voice flat with the particular calm navigators wear when the forecast matches their fears: the monsoon proper was arriving early, exactly as feared. Their window was real and it was closing.

The Syndicate knew it too. Through the rain, their camp lights burned brighter, and twice that day a rigid inflatable had run the shoreline, counting, photographing, reminding everyone on the beach that the trap had teeth.

At the council, Felix laid it out with a clarity Tomas had learned to respect: they'd launch at slack water before dawn, run the coast north under the storm's noise cover, find the entrance to the Throat in the pre-light gray, and be through the reef line and out to sea before the Syndicate could react. The gunboats were heavy, deep-keeled, proud. They couldn't follow into sixty meters of uncharted channel even if they tried.

"And if we miss the entrance," the collector asked — he'd invited himself, and nobody had the energy to throw him out — "in those seas?"

"Then we're wreckage," Felix said simply. "Like everyone the Syndicate has ever ruined. Seems fitting."

Tomas watched Amara during that exchange. She sat with her father's chart flattened on the table, her finger resting on the bend in the channel — the place marked with a small annotation in her grandfather's hand. Tomas had seen the note earlier, in fading ink: *the sea decides here — pray it remembers us.*

Now, as the council broke up and the crew dispersed to make ready, Amara stayed behind, and Tomas stayed with her, coiling line that didn't need coiling.

"You're scared," he said. Not a question.

"My grandfather drew that channel from memory after my grandmother drowned near it," Amara said. "He drew it so no one else would die there. And now I'm going to use it to save the son of the man who—" She stopped. Started again. "I don't know if the sea remembers us, Tomas. But I know it doesn't care which of us it takes. So we go anyway. That's the whole trick of it. Everyone who ever survived anything went anyway."

Tomas finished coiling the line. Outside, the rain thickened, and somewhere beyond the reef, the monsoon assembled itself out of the dark like a fleet.

"One more thing," he said. "The engine. She'll start cold, but she hates salt water in the intake, and the Throat will be nothing *but* salt water. Someone's going to have to ride the intake well and bail it by hand through the worst of it. It's the worst job on the boat."

"I'll do it."

"No," Tomas said. "You'll be navigating. It's my job. It's always been my job — I keep the machinery alive so other people can be brave." He managed a grin. "Besides, I want to hear the hull sing up close. If she's going to come apart, I want to be holding the wrench when it happens."

Amara looked at him for a long moment, and then she did something he didn't expect: she squeezed his shoulder, once, hard, and left without a word.

Tomas stood alone in the patched-up ghost of a boat that should have rotted another decade in the sand, listening to the engine tick as it cooled, listening to the rain, listening to the distant thunder of surf breaking over a reef he'd have to pass through in six hours.

Forty hours of rain. Forty hours of work. And now the only thing left to build was the one thing no wrench could reach.

He checked the fuel one last time, wiped his hands on a rag that was filthier than his hands, and went to wake the crew.

Dawn was coming. So was the storm. Only one of them was on schedule — and Tomas intended to beat them both to the water.
