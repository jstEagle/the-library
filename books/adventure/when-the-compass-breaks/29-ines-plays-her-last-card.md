## 29. Ines Plays Her Last Card

Ines Varga had spent her whole life learning the difference between a gamble and a sacrifice, and the difference was this: a gamble you could walk away from.

She stood at the rail of the *Meridian Dawn* — her yacht, her pride, the only thing she had ever built with her own hands and money that was actually hers — and looked across the grey lagoon at the Syndicate camp on the island's eastern spit. Three boats at anchor. A canvas command tent staked into the sand like a flag of occupation. Men moving with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once been told no by the sea.

Forty hours of rain, Tomas had said, before the window closed. Maybe less. The storm out past the reef was already stacking itself up against the horizon like a wall being built by an angry god, and every hour they waited was an hour the Kestrel's patched hull had to keep holding water instead of taking it.

Felix would never agree to what she was about to do. That was the first thing she'd known. He'd argue, he'd bluster, he'd put that stubborn Eriksen jaw out and tell her it was his debt, his mess, his father's sin to answer for — as if guilt were a currency only men like him were allowed to spend. And while he argued, the collectors would grow bored, or suspicious, or simply greedy, and someone would die over a strongbox full of paper.

So she hadn't told him. She'd told Priya, because Priya could keep a secret the way a hull keeps out water — absolutely, until it couldn't, and then not at all. And she'd told Amara, because Amara had looked at her with those flat, patient eyes and said, "You're going to do something stupid and noble, aren't you," and Ines had said, "Noble is such an ugly word," and that had been the whole conversation.

Now she checked the tide, checked the wind, checked the little waterproof watch on her wrist that had belonged to her brother, and went below to change clothes.

Not the sailing gear. Not the crisp whites she wore when she wanted people to remember she was rich. She put on the oldest thing in her locker: a salt-stained linen shirt, trousers gone soft at the knees, deck shoes without laces. Clothes that said *I have nothing left to protect*. Costume was half of any negotiation, and today she was playing the one role she'd sworn she'd never play.

The ruined woman. The collateral.

Her hands shook as she buttoned the shirt, and she let them. Fear was useful. Fear made the performance honest.

* * *

The tender ride across the lagoon took eleven minutes. She used all of them.

She thought about Mateo. She thought about him the way you take a bitter medicine — all at once, no sipping. Nineteen years old, crewing on the Halcyon Star because their father owed the Syndicate too and a berth on a survey vessel paid better than anything ashore. Ines had been twenty-three, already running charters, already too proud to write home more than twice a year. The last letter he sent her was four lines long and mostly about the food.

Then the Halcyon Star went down in a storm that official reports called anomalous, and the Syndicate called an act of God, and her family called a debt settled — because the insurance payout had gone straight to Meridian, every centime, and her parents had aged ten years in a single afternoon and never really spoken above a whisper again.

She'd spent fifteen years finding out what actually happened. Fifteen years of salvage brokers and retired hydrographers and one very expensive lawyer who finally dug loose the truth: the ship's master, Anton Eriksen, had scuttled his own vessel rather than let the Syndicate take the patents aboard. Had drowned his crew — including a nineteen-year-old boy who wrote home about the food — to keep a fortune out of the wrong hands.

She had found Felix Eriksen three years ago and hated him on sight, and then, infuriatingly, kept finding reasons to be near him. Rivalry was a convenient shape for it. You could compete with a man for a decade and call it anything you wanted.

And when she'd finally traced the whole architecture of the disaster — the debts, the sabotage, the letter that must exist somewhere in that strongbox naming the families the great Anton Eriksen had betrayed — she had understood two things at once, the way you understand a wave is going to break on you a full second before it does.

First: Felix didn't know. He was carrying his father's vow like a sacred trust, grieving a mother the sea took, and he had no idea the man he mourned had murdered her along with everyone else. Or maybe he suspected. Maybe that was why he'd refused the archipelago for so long. Either way, he could not be told. He had to find it himself, in the green dark, with his own hands, or he would spend the rest of his life finding ways not to believe it.

Second: the job had to look like his idea. Which meant it had to look like anyone's idea but hers.

So she'd bought the charts. Planted the errors. Let the Syndicate believe they were tracking a routine salvage run. Let the gunboat shadow them. Every cruelty of the last month had been hers, engineered with a precision she was not proud of, aimed at a single outcome: Felix Eriksen standing on the deck of his father's wreck, opening a box that would break him open too.

It had worked. God help her, it had worked exactly as designed, and now they were all trapped on a nameless island with the monsoon coming down like the sky had a grudge, and the only variable left was time.

Time was what she was going to buy.

* * *

The Syndicate camp smelled of diesel and wet canvas and the particular arrogance of men who expected to win. A collector named Osei met her at the rope line — not the head man, Voss, but his lieutenant, a long-faced Ghanaian with careful eyes and a sidearm worn openly, which on an island full of fishermen was its own kind of message.

"Ms. Varga." No surprise in his voice. Of course not. They'd been watching her yacht since dawn; they'd probably watched her lower the tender. "Mr. Voss will want to know why you've come alone."

"Because what I have to offer doesn't require an audience." She smiled the smile she used on marina managers and customs officials — warm, faintly bored, entirely false. "Is there coffee? I've been up since four."

There was coffee. It was terrible. She drank it sitting in the command tent across from Voss himself, a compact Dutchman with reading glasses pushed up into thinning hair, looking less like a debt collector than a schoolmaster disappointed by his class. That was the trick of the Meridian Syndicate, she'd learned long ago: they dressed the violence in paperwork until you signed yourself into the noose thinking it was a scarf.

"You've come about the Eriksen matter," Voss said.

"I've come about the weather." She set down the cup. "You have three boats and forty armed men and a target that can't sail anywhere. I understand why you're comfortable. Here's why you shouldn't be." She nodded toward the tent wall, beyond which the horizon had gone the color of a bruise. "That system makes landfall inside two days. Your anchorage is exposed from the northeast. When it comes through, those boats of yours will either run for deep water — losing your prize — or stay and get driven onto that reef, which I'm told has eaten better vessels than yours."

Voss's expression didn't change, but Osei shifted his weight, and Ines knew she'd landed it. Sailors always did the math on weather before they did the math on money. It was the one instinct you couldn't train out of them.

"So," she continued, "I'm here to simplify your position."

"Go on."

"The Kestrel can't run. Her hull's compromised — I know because my charts put them on that route in the first place. They'll shelter here through the blow, and when it passes, they'll still be sitting ducks. But storms make men stupid, Mr. Voss. Desperate men in a dying boat do desperate things, and if Eriksen bolts during the rain, you'll chase him into water that kills you both, and the box goes to the bottom, and Meridian writes off another generation of losses on this cursed archipelago." She leaned forward. "Unless the prize is secured before the storm arrives."

"And you're offering to secure it."

"I'm offering myself." She said it lightly, like discussing ballast weights. "My yacht, the Meridian Dawn — sixty feet, new engines, clean title, worth more than everything in that strongbox even before you count the patents' market value. Moored in your anchorage as we speak, with the papers in the cabin safe. Plus me. I'm the one who hired Eriksen. I'm the one whose signature is on the salvage contract. Legally, everything he recovers vests through me. Hold me, and you hold the chain of title."

Silence. Rain began ticking against the canvas overhead, the first scouts of the front.

"Why?" Voss asked, and it was a good question, asked well. "You've spent a fortune engineering this run. Why hand away the prize?"

Because my brother is dead and your company helped kill him and I want your ledgers torn apart so thoroughly that no other nineteen-year-old ever drowns for your balance sheets. Because Felix Eriksen trusted me least of anyone alive and I am about to prove that trustlessness correct in the most spectacular way available. Because a woman can only carry one ghost per decade and I am thirty-eight years old and full.

What she said was: "Because I've seen what happens to people who stay in this fight too long. My brother crewed a survey vessel for these waters. I'd like to leave with my life and let the lawyers sort the rest."

A lie wrapped around a truth — the best kind, the kind that scans true under pressure because part of it is. Voss studied her for a long moment, and she let him see the tiredness, the salt-stained shirt, the shaking hands she'd declined to steady. She let him see a woman at the end of her resources, because that was the character, and she had committed to the role completely.

"The yacht stays where it is," Voss said finally. "You stay aboard it. Osei will post two men. If Eriksen surrenders the box within thirty-six hours, you walk free and we discuss terms. If not—" He spread his hands, a small courteous gesture containing a large threat. "Collateral is only valuable until it isn't."

"Of course." She stood, offered her hand, and he shook it. His grip was dry and precise. "One condition. Nobody touches the crew. Hostages are bad business, but corpses are worse — they attract navies."

"We're aware of how to conduct ourselves."

"I know," she said. "That's what frightens me."

* * *

They walked her back down to the tender through the first real rain, fat drops hitting the sand like thrown gravel. Osei accompanied her personally, hands clasped behind his back, and halfway down the beach he said, quietly, without looking at her:

"The boy on the Halcyon Star. Mateo Varga. My uncle was the bosun on that ship."

Ines stopped walking. The rain ran down her face and she was grateful for it, because it meant she didn't have to control it.

"He was nineteen," Osei said. "I was twelve. My mother cried for a week." He turned and looked at her then, and his careful eyes were not careful at all. "Whatever you're doing, Ms. Varga — and I've watched enough of Mr. Voss's operations to know you're doing something — do it fast."

Then he walked back up the beach, and Ines stood alone in the rain for a moment, undone by the one thing she hadn't planned for: a witness. Proof that the dead were remembered by strangers, that grief had ripples, that the boy who wrote about the food had mattered to people she'd never meet.

She got in the tender. She rowed back out into the lagoon, arms burning, rain hammering the water flat, and she did not let herself think about how easily Osei could have reported his suspicion to Voss instead. Some mercies you accepted without audit, or they stopped being mercies.

* * *

The Meridian Dawn sat at anchor with two Syndicate men aboard, exactly as promised, which meant Ines climbed up her own boarding ladder as a prisoner on her own vessel. One of the guards — young, sunburned, bored — patted her down competently and confiscated the satellite phone from her pocket.

"Anything else?"

"My dignity," she said. "But you can't carry it."

He almost smiled. Good. Bored guards who liked you were slow guards.

Below, in her cabin, she opened the safe and took out the contract folder — the fake one, the decoy she'd prepared in Singapore six weeks ago, full of plausible legalese and a chain-of-title that would take their lawyers days to unpick. Days. That was the real cargo. Not the yacht, not herself. Time, measured in legal confusion, delivered directly into the belly of the beast.

She sat at her desk, the one Mateo's photograph had watched over for fifteen years, and took out the pencil and the waterproof notebook, and began writing in the shorthand Priya had taught her — a navigator's cipher, useless to anyone who didn't know the Kestrel's chart table.

*Two guards, rotation at dusk and dawn. Armory in the aft cabin of the lead boat, portside rack unsecured — saw it myself. Voss expects surrender within 36 hrs; after that he stops negotiating. Storm edge hits tomorrow night. The channel Priya believes in — tell her to trust the soundings, not the chart. And tell Felix—*

The pencil stopped.

Tell Felix what? That she'd done it all for him? That hatred had been the scaffolding and love the building, and she'd knocked the scaffolding down years ago and pretended not to notice what was underneath? That her brother and his mother were two names on the same manifest of the damned, and that she had dragged him across an ocean to make him read it?

He'd hate her. Good. Hate was durable. Hate would keep him alive and angry and moving when gratitude would have made him hesitate, and hesitation, in the next forty hours, would kill them all.

She finished the note, sealed it in the oilskin pouch, and weighed her options for delivery. There was only one: the young guard took his smoke breaks at the stern rail at 0200, facing away from the water, and the Kestrel's dinghy could cross the hundred meters of dark lagoon without an engine. Someone would have to swim for it. Amara, probably — Amara swam like she'd been issued gills at birth, and Amara already knew the plan's outline, and Amara had looked at her this morning with something that wasn't quite forgiveness but was adjacent to it, and adjacent was all Ines had ever been able to afford.

Above her, boots crossed the deck. The guard, making his rounds. The rain thickened against the cabin windows, and out past the reef the storm gathered itself in the dark, patient as a debt.

Ines Varga looked at her own reflection in the black glass — a tired woman in a ruined shirt, prisoner of her own design — and allowed herself, for exactly four seconds, the luxury of fear.

Then she blew out the lamp, lay down on the bunk she might never sleep in again, and started counting the hours until 0200, when the dinghy would come sliding across the water, and the last card would leave her hand.

Thirty-one hours, she thought, as the rain came down harder.

Plenty of time for everything to go wrong.
