## 30. Launch Into the Teeth

The rain came sideways before we even cleared the lagoon.

I stood at the wheel of the Kestrel's sister-sloop — Tomas had pulled her name plate off years ago and never replaced it, so we all just called her *the Girl* — and watched the Syndicate camp shrink behind us into a smear of canvas and lamplight. Somewhere in that smear was Ines Varga, walking into their guns with her hands open, buying us forty minutes with her yacht and her freedom. I didn't let myself think about it. Thinking about it would put my hands back on that wheel and turn us around.

"Channel bearing two-six-five," Priya called from under the spray hood, chart case strapped to her chest like a baby. "If it exists."

"It exists," Amara said. She stood at my shoulder, one hand braced on the rail, the other still wrapped where the agent's knife had opened it. "My grandmother fished it. She called it the Throat."

"Comforting name."

"She also called the reef outside it the Teeth. So." Amara shrugged. "At least she was honest."

The Girl lifted her bow to the first real swell and I felt every plank of her answer it — a long groan running keel to truck, the sound of a boat that had been dead on a beach three days ago and now found herself alive again at the worst possible moment. Tomas had done a miracle below with epoxy and prayer and whatever he'd scavenged from the Kestrel's bones. Miracles have a shelf life. Ours was measured in hours.

Behind us, out in the black, an engine note changed pitch.

"There," Priya said. Not a question. She'd been listening for it since we slipped our moorings.

The gunboat. The same one that had shadowed us for weeks, the one whose captain had stood on the beach this morning with rain streaming off his jaw and offered me a deal dressed up as mercy: the strongbox for clean ledgers, for my crew's lives. He'd said it gently, which was the frightening part. Men who say gentle things with guns behind them have stopped needing to shout.

"He'll wait for the squall line to pass," Amara said.

"No," I said. "He'll come now. Because he thinks we're desperate, and desperate people make mistakes in weather like this." I looked at the sky, where the monsoon had stacked itself into a wall you could almost lean against. "He's right about the desperate part. But he's wrong about whose mistakes those are."

Tomas came up the companionway dripping, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime yesterday. "She'll hold," he said, answering the question nobody asked. "Probably."

"Probably?"

"Felix, I glued a boat together with sailcloth and epoxy in forty hours of rain. 'Probably' is the best word in my vocabulary right now. Cherish it."

I almost laughed. That was the trick of Tomas — he could put a laugh in your mouth at the exact moment you needed something in there besides fear.

"Get forward," I told him. "Weight forward keeps her nose down in the troughs. And keep the pumps honest."

He went. Amara stayed.

"You don't have to steer by memory yet," she said quietly. "We're still inside the lagoon's lee."

"I know what I'm doing."

"You're holding the wheel like it owes you money." She nodded at my hands. White-knuckled. When had that happened? "Felix. Look at me."

I looked. Rain ran down her face and she didn't blink it away.

"Your father sailed these waters for twenty years. Your mother grew up on them. Whatever else they got wrong — and we both know the list is long — they left you more than debts." She touched my arm, light as a gull landing. "Steer like you believe that."

* * *

The lagoon's mouth was a gap in the reef no wider than a church door, and the sea outside it was already standing up in ridges, gray-green and moving fast, the wind ahead of the storm front tearing white smoke off the crests. Priya crouched over her chart with a red flashlight, and I saw her lips moving — not reading, arguing. Arguing with paper.

"Bearing two-six-five holds for four hundred meters," she called. "Then the chart says reef. My chart says reef. Amara's grandmother says channel."

"Grandmothers beat hydrographic offices," Amara said. "Every time. They were here when the offices weren't."

"That's not how charts work—"

"That's exactly how water works."

I brought us through the gap with the engine at half and the wind doing most of the steering for me, and then the open sea took the Girl in its hand and shook her.

I've chased storms for eleven years. I've driven a pickup through hail the size of fists in Kansas, ridden out a waterspout outbreak off Luzon, slept through a typhoon tied to a mooring buoy because there was nowhere left to run. None of it was this. This wasn't weather you drove through; this was terrain. Mountains of water, moving. Valleys between them deep enough to lose a boat and everyone aboard. The Girl climbed the first swell until her rudder breathed air, then dropped down the far side so fast my stomach arrived late, and somewhere below decks Tomas's voice rose in a wordless complaint as something shifted.

"Hull's singing again!" he bellowed up the hatch.

"Singing or screaming?"

"Yes!"

That was the whole conversation. There wasn't time for another.

The gunboat came out of the murk astern, searchlight stabbing through the rain, and I heard the flat crack of something that might have been a warning shot or might have been the storm snapping a halyard. Then a voice on the VHF, warped by static and distance but unmistakable:

*Sloop on the two-six-five heading. Heave to. Final offer stands until the front makes landfall. After that, nothing stands.*

Priya looked at me. Amara looked at me. Even the Girl seemed to look at me, trembling under my hands, waiting to find out what kind of man held her wheel.

"Tell him," I said into the mic, "that the offer expired when he put collectors on my dock in Tonkin. Tell him everything after that was just interest accruing."

I pushed the throttle to the stop.

* * *

Here is the thing about faith: it doesn't feel like certainty. Certainty feels like solid ground. Faith feels like stepping off a cliff in fog and trusting the fog to be a staircase. Every second of the next hour, I steered by faith, and every second I hated it, and every second it worked anyway.

Because the compass was spinning.

My father's compass — the broken one, the one he'd carried home from the archipelago in his coat pocket the year my mother died, the one with the cracked crystal and the card that wobbled on its pin like a drunk trying to stand at attention. I'd wedged it into the binnacle bracket beside the Girl's proper compass, mostly as a talisman, mostly because leaving it ashore felt like leaving a bone behind. And now, as the storm's magnetic chaos closed around us and the good compass began to wander in slow, drunken arcs, the broken one went mad — the card whirling, swinging through full circles, then stopping dead on a bearing that made no sense, then whirling again.

Except.

Except every time it stopped, it stopped pointing the same way. Two-seven-zero. A hair west of the channel Priya believed in and Amara's grandmother had named.

"Priya," I called. "What's your best guess on the Throat's true bearing from here?"

She checked, argued with the chart a moment longer. "Two-six-eight. Maybe two-seventy if the chart's drawn generous."

"And the good compass?"

"Wandering. Ten degrees either side and getting worse. There's iron in this rock, or the storm's playing games, or both."

I looked down at the broken thing spinning in its cracked glass case, my father's last possession, the object I'd kept when I sold everything else, and I understood — not with proof, not with reason, but with the bone-deep certainty that has no paperwork — that he had steered by this. That the wobble wasn't damage. It was character. It was a compass that had learned these waters the way Amara's grandmother had, the hard way, and when everything else lied it pointed true.

"Amara," I said. "Talk to me about the Throat."

Her voice came steady through the rain. "Narrow. Half a cable wide at the entrance, maybe less. Current runs east to west against the swell, so the seas will stack at the mouth. Inside, it bends north — hard bend, you can't see it until you're in it. My grandmother said you don't steer through the Throat. You surrender to it."

"Surrender."

"Let the current take the bow. Fight it and you go into the rock. Trust it and it carries you around the bend like a hand."

Ahead, the reef showed itself in the lightning flashes — a black serrated line where the sea tore itself apart, whitewater exploding thirty feet up, the sound reaching us even over the wind like continuous artillery. Somewhere along that line was a door. The compass said two-seven-zero. The storm said nothing. The gunboat's searchlight swept the water behind us, closer now, patient as debt.

"Tomas!" I shouted down the hatch. "Everything you've got. I need speed at the mouth — enough to punch the current, not so much I can't turn."

"You'll get what physics allows!"

"Physics and I are negotiating!"

* * *

The last thousand meters were the longest of my life.

The seas had organized themselves into something with intent, long green mountains marching in from the southwest, and the Girl surfed them with her seams complaining, spray sheeting over the dodger in solid walls. I steered standing, knees bent, the way you learn to ride a boat in big water — not fighting her motion but borrowing it, using each wave's face to lay the next heading. Behind us the gunboat's light swung and steadied. They were following us in. Of course they were. We were the only thing on this ocean that knew where the door was, and men like the collector would rather follow a ghost through a graveyard than admit the sea had beaten them.

"They're matching our course," Priya reported. "Two cables back."

"Their draft is deeper than ours," Amara said. There was something in her voice I couldn't read — hope, maybe, wearing menace's clothes. "A lot deeper."

"Noted," I said, and did not let myself smile, because smiling at another man's coming wreck is a curse you pay for later. But I noted it. Oh, I noted it.

The compass spun, steadied, spun. Two-seven-zero. Two-seven-zero. In the lightning flashes the reef wall grew until it filled the world, a cliff of black stone and detonating water, and I could see nothing — no gap, no door, no mercy — only rock and ruin as far as the flash reached.

"Felix." Amara's hand found my shoulder. "There. Where the whitewater folds inward. Do you see it? The seam?"

And then I did. A place where the breaking waves didn't break — where they folded into a dark slot like curtains drawing, fed by the current running through instead of against. It looked impossible. It looked like a wound in the reef. It looked exactly like something a woman would name the Throat.

"Two-seven-zero," the broken compass whispered, in its way.

"Hold on," I said, and drove us at the cliff.

* * *

I will not pretend I remember it clearly. Memory in moments like that comes apart into flashes, into sensations without sequence.

The mouth of the Throat took the Girl and shook her like a dog takes a rat. The current grabbed the bow and tried to swing us broadside into rock, and I surrendered — Amara's word, Amara's grandmother's wisdom — letting the stern swing out while keeping just enough rudder to keep us from going all the way around. Water came over the deck in tons. The hull screamed — not sang, screamed, a sound like a violin bow drawn across a rusted saw, and below it Tomas's voice rising through the boards, wordless, urgent, alive. The reef walls closed in on both sides close enough to touch, black stone sliding past in the strobe of lightning, and the channel bent north exactly as promised, a blind turn into chaos, and I turned into it blind because there was nothing else to do, because surrender was the only heading left—

—and then we were through.

Through, and the sea behind us was a roaring wall of white where the gunboat's searchlight wheeled and swung, and the sea ahead was — not calm, nothing was calm, but manageable, survivable, a road instead of a cliff. Rain still hammered. Wind still screamed. But the Throat spat us out into open water on the far side of the reef, and between us and the Syndicate now stood a hundred miles of coral that ate ships for breakfast.

Priya made a sound I'd never heard from her before — half laugh, half sob. Amara gripped the rail and bowed her head, and I thought for one absurd moment she was praying to her grandmother, and maybe she was, and maybe that was the correct navigation instrument all along.

On the VHF, the collector's voice came one last time, stripped of its gentleness now, raw with fury and something underneath the fury that sounded almost like awe:

*You'll run out of sea eventually, Eriksen.*

I keyed the mic. "Maybe. But tonight I've got more sea than you do."

Behind us, the searchlight swung toward the gap in the reef. Toward the Throat. Toward the stacked seas and the blind bend and the rock.

"Tomas," I called down the hatch, "how's our girl holding?"

A pause. Long enough to be honest.

"She's holding," he said. "But Felix — get us somewhere shallow and soft, fast. I'm looking at a seam forward that wasn't talking five minutes ago, and she's starting to sing soprano."

I looked down at the compass. The card had gone still at last, resting on two-seven-zero, quivering slightly, like a heart slowing after exertion. My father's compass. Pointing true through all of it.

Then, as I watched, the card shivered once, twice — and began to spin.
