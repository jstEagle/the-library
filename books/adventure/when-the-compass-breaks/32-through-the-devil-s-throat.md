## 32. Through the Devil's Throat

Priya had never prayed in her life. Her mother used to say that navigation was a form of prayer — you put your faith in numbers instead of gods, and the numbers never lied to you the way gods did. Priya had believed that for thirty-one years.

She was not believing it now.

The channel ahead of them was a lie she had invented herself, stitched together from three days of memory, half a chart she'd drawn from instinct, and one conversation with a dead woman's stories filtered through Felix Eriksen's grief. The Devil's Throat. A gap between two reef walls that no chart showed because no surveyor had ever been mad enough to run it at flood tide. She had told Tomas it existed. She had told Felix it existed. And now, with the Kestrel's patched hull groaning like an old man climbing stairs and forty feet of black water rising behind them, she had to be right or they would all die being wrong.

"Depth under keel?" Felix called from the wheel.

"Nine meters," she said, reading the sounder by feel more than sight. The rain was coming sideways now, so hard it hit the cabin windows like gravel. "But the bottom is coral, not rock. If we touch, we cut. We don't bounce."

"So don't touch."

"That's the plan."

Tomas was below, hands wrapped around the raw patch job he'd finished four hours ago — sailcloth, epoxy, and two stainless plates bolted through the hull where the bilge had been singing its death song since midnight. Every few minutes the engine note changed pitch, and every time it did, Priya's stomach dropped through the deck. That wasn't the engine anymore. That was the ocean talking to them through their own boat, telling them how much time they had left.

Not much.

Behind them, visible only as a smear of searchlight through the rain curtain, the Syndicate gunboat *Meridian Dawn* was coming on. Forty meters of steel, twin diesels, a bow built to punch through weather instead of respect it. The collector aboard her — Osei, the man whose voice had promised them mercy if they surrendered the strongbox — had radioed twice in the last hour. Both times, Ines had answered from the sister-sloop's radio, stalling, lying, buying minutes with words that cost her nothing and might yet cost them everything.

"He's stopped answering," Amara said from the bow, harnessed in, spray jacket streaming. "That means he's done listening."

"That means he's committed," Priya said. "He thinks we're running blind. He thinks we'll founder inside the Throat and he can pick up the box off our drowned bodies at low tide." She almost laughed at that, a small ugly laugh with no humor in it. "Let him think it."

Felix's hands were locked on the wheel, but his eyes were on her, waiting. This was the part she'd rehearsed in her head a hundred times since the island: the moment when everything she knew became everything she said, and the gap between those two things was measured in hull plating.

"Priya," he said quietly. "Talk me in."

* * *

She talked him in the way you talk someone across a rope bridge in the dark — one plank at a time, never letting them look down.

The entrance to the Devil's Throat was marked by nothing. No buoy, no beacon, no white water line on any chart ever printed. It was marked by a single thing only, and Priya had found it three nights ago standing anchor watch on the nameless isle, staring at the way the swell refracted around a shoal headland in the dying light. There was a notch in the eastern reef wall — a V-shaped break in the coral crest where the surf fell differently. Where the waves broke in two directions at once and canceled each other into a flat tongue of water maybe sixty meters wide. The locals on islands further south had a word for water like that. Her grandmother had called it *selat hantu*. Ghost strait. Water that looked calm because something underneath was eating the wave energy whole.

Something underneath. That was the problem. The Throat ran over a drowned riverbed, a fossil channel cut during some ancient lower sea level, and its floor was a sequence of coral terraces stepping down like a staircase — twelve meters, then eight, then fourteen, then six at the narrowest bend before it opened into the deep lagoon behind the barrier. Six meters at the bend. The Kestrel drew three and a half. That gave them two and a half meters of grace at slack-ish tide, except this was not slack tide, this was a monsoon flood tide running against a gale, which meant the depth readings were going to swing wildly and the current through the narrows would be running five knots, maybe six, pushing them toward the northern wall like a hand on the stern.

"You steer for the flat water," she told Felix now, leaning over the chart table, one hand braced against the bulkhead as the Kestrel pitched. "Not the gap. The gap lies. The flat water doesn't. When you see the surface go glassy-smooth in a tongue about a boat-length wide, that's your line. Put the bow on it and hold it."

"And if I lose it?"

"You won't."

"And if I do?"

"Then you'll hear the reef before you see it," she said. "Coral sounds like bacon frying when water runs shallow over it. If you hear frying, come hard starboard and pray."

"I don't pray."

"Then fry quietly."

Amara laughed — a short, sharp bark of a laugh that had no business existing in a moment like this, and Priya loved her for it. Fear made people silent. Amara refused to be silent. She stood at the bow calling depths by eye, reading the color of the water the way her family had read it for generations before anyone handed them a lead line — brown, green, teal, blue-black — translating the reef into language for the helm.

"Green ahead," she called. "Deep green. Good green."

Priya checked the sounder. Eight meters. Amara's eyes and the instrument agreed, and that agreement was worth more than any chart Priya had ever owned. Charts were arguments. Eyes were testimony.

The rain thickened until the world beyond the wheelhouse windows was a gray blur shot through with lightning. Thunder rolled across the water in long flat cracks, and somewhere in that thunder, faintly, they could still hear the gunboat's engines — deeper, heavier, more confident than anything had a right to be in this sea.

"They're following us straight in," Priya said, more to herself than anyone. "They're actually doing it."

"Why wouldn't they?" Felix said. "We're just a leaky sloop. What could we possibly know that they don't?"

"We know something they don't," Priya said. "We know we're going to die if we get this wrong. They think they can't die. That's the difference. That's the whole difference."

* * *

The entrance came up faster than she wanted.

One moment there was nothing but gray chaos; the next, the reef walls rose out of the rain on either side like the shoulders of something surfacing — black coral rock furred with white foam, twenty feet high, closing in until the channel between them was barely three times the Kestrel's beam. The wind funneled through the gap and doubled, screaming, and the rain went from horizontal to vertical in the space of a breath, whipped upward by the compression like spray blown back off a cliff.

"Frying," Amara shouted suddenly. "Port side! Frying!"

Priya felt it too, through the soles of her boots — a vibration, a thin singing in the hull, the sound of water moving fast over coral shallower than the sounder claimed. The flood tide was piling up against the northern terrace, and the deep channel had shifted fifty meters west of where her mental chart said it should be.

"Starboard," she told Felix, and her voice came out steadier than she felt, because that was the job. "Hard starboard. Follow the flat water. It moved."

Felix didn't argue. That was the thing she'd learned about him in the last week, the thing that had slowly replaced her first impression of a man drowning in his own debts: when the moment came, he listened. He put the wheel over and the Kestrel swung, the port rail dipping until green water walked across the deck, and for eight seconds they were pointed straight at the northern wall and Priya's heart was a fist in her throat —

— and then the flat water appeared. A tongue of glassy calm, absurd in all that violence, maybe fifteen meters wide, unrolling ahead of them like a carpet laid down by something that wanted them to live.

"There," she breathed. "There. Bow on it. Hold it."

Felix held it.

The Kestrel entered the Throat proper, and the world changed. The seas outside had been mountains — confused, breaking, coming from three directions at once. Inside the gap the swell compressed and accelerated, and they began to surf. Not gently. The stern lifted and the boat ran, twelve knots, fourteen, the wheel gone light as feather in Felix's hands, the channel walls flickering past on both sides close enough to see individual coral heads, brain coral the size of cars, fire coral glowing yellow-green through the churned water.

"Tomas!" Priya hammered the engine room hatch with her fist. "Talk to me!"

His voice came up the tube, ragged but alive. "Pump's holding! Patch is holding! Don't ask me how, just keep us right-side up!"

"How's the temperature?"

A pause. Too long a pause.

"She's warm, Priya. She's warm but she's turning."

Turning. Meaning the packing gland was heating, meaning the shaft was fighting the current, meaning the engine had maybe minutes of hard running left in it before something seized. Priya closed her eyes for half a second and did the arithmetic she'd been dreading: at twelve knots, the bend was ninety seconds away. At eight knots, if the engine died, the current would take them and put them on the northern wall and that would be the end of the story.

"Ninety seconds," she said aloud, to no one, to everyone. "Ninety seconds of everything we have."

* * *

The bend at the narrows was the place the old fishermen wouldn't speak of, the place Felix's mother had described once, years ago, in a story Felix had carried like a splinter his whole life and finally given to Priya on the beach two nights ago, word by word, his voice shaking. *The water turns left where the land says right,* she had supposedly told him. *You have to believe the water.*

The water turned left. The land — the reef wall, the visible geometry, every instinct Priya owned — said right.

And here was the terrible thing, the thing Priya would carry for the rest of her life whatever happened in the next sixty seconds: she could see it. Standing at the chart table with the rain hammering the windows and the channel walls rushing past, she could see both truths at once. The right-hand passage looked open. Wide, deep-colored, inviting, a corridor of dark blue water leading exactly where a navigator would want to go. The left-hand passage looked like death — a churning chaos of crosscurrents where the flood tide slammed into the ebb eddy and the surface boiled white over what had to be shallow coral.

Every instrument she had agreed with the right-hand passage. Every instrument she had was wrong. The right-hand channel was a trap — a cul-de-sac of deep water that ended in a submerged coral wall two meters under the surface, invisible, patient, the kind of reef that had eaten a hundred boats before theirs and would eat a hundred after. The left-hand chaos was the true channel, the fossil riverbed, six meters of grace hidden under a skin of violence.

Believe the water.

"Left," Priya said.

Felix's head snapped toward her. "It looks—"

"I know what it looks like. Left, Felix. Now."

One heartbeat. Two. His hands moved.

The Kestrel turned left into the boiling water, and for three full seconds Priya experienced the purest terror of her life. The boat dropped into a trough between crosscurrents, the hull slamming, the patched section forward taking the impact with a crack like a rifle shot — Tomas below must have heard it and aged ten years — and then the current took them. Took them the way a river takes a leaf, smooth and total and irresistible, sweeping them around the bend at nine knots with the reef walls blurring past and the surface chaos resolving, miraculously, impossibly, into ordered flow. The fossil channel. The drowned river. It caught them the way a hand catches a falling cup.

Six meters under the keel. Then seven. Then nine.

They were through the bend.

Priya let out a sound she had never made before — half sob, half laugh, ugly and involuntary — and grabbed the edge of the chart table with both hands because her legs had briefly stopped being reliable. Ahead, the channel opened, widened, and the reef walls fell away on either side like gates swinging open, and beyond them, through a thinning in the rain, she could see the lagoon. Flat water. Actual flat water. A mile of it, then the far barrier, then open sea on the other side of the archipelago and freedom shaped like a heading.

"We're through," Amara called from the bow, and her voice cracked on the second word. "We're through, we're through—"

"Not yet," Priya said, because it was true, because the exit was another mile and the tide was still against them and the engine was still dying. But her voice shook when she said it, and everyone in the wheelhouse heard the triumph underneath the caution anyway.

* * *

That was when they heard the gunboat enter the Throat behind them.

They heard it before they saw it — a change in the echo, the deep-throated bellow of twin diesels reverberating off the channel walls, amplified, distorted, filling the gap like the roar of something trapped. Priya spun to the aft window. Through the rain, back up the channel they had just survived, the *Meridian Dawn*'s searchlight swept the walls, arrogant and bright, and the vessel herself came on at speed — too much speed, far too much, forty meters of steel driving into a gap that demanded humility.

"No," Priya whispered. "No, no, don't—"

Osei had watched them turn left into the boiling water. Any sane helmsman would have read that as confirmation the left channel was suicide and taken the right-hand passage — the beautiful, deep, lying right-hand passage. Or he would have slowed, sounded his way through, waited for the tide.

The *Meridian Dawn* did none of these things. She was heavy, and proud, and her captain had spent his career in ships that bullied the ocean rather than listened to it. He followed their wake straight through the bend at speed, trusting that whatever a leaking sloop could survive, his steel could survive better.

Priya watched it happen with the detached horror of someone watching a disaster they had predicted and been powerless to prevent. The gunboat's bow entered the crosscurrent zone. The current took her — took her the way it had taken the Kestrel, but the Kestrel weighed eleven tons and drew three and a half meters, and the *Meridian Dawn* weighed two hundred and drew five. The sweep of the ebb eddy caught her port bow and swung it, just a few degrees, just enough, and her momentum — all that proud, heavy, unstoppable momentum — carried her out of the fossil channel and onto the submerged wall of the right-hand trap.

The sound reached them a full second after the flash of impact, rolling down the channel like thunder answering itself. Steel meeting coral at fourteen knots. The searchlight swung wildly, strobed across the wet rock, went dark. Through the rain Priya saw her heel, saw her bow rise as the reef held her amidships and the sea worked on her stern, saw the running lights flicker, steady, flicker again.

Then the *Meridian Dawn*'s whistle began to blow — long, continuous, the international signal for distress — and did not stop.

Nobody in the Kestrel's wheelhouse said anything for a long moment. The rain drummed. The engine labored. Somewhere below, Tomas was shouting something about the gland temperature, and none of it seemed to matter very much against the sound of that whistle.

"They're on the wall," Amara said quietly, coming back into the wheelhouse, dripping, harness still on. "The reef has her. She's not going anywhere."

"People are on her," Priya said.

"Yes," Amara said. "People are on her."

Felix's hands were still on the wheel, knuckles white, eyes fixed on the opening lagoon ahead — freedom, flat water, the far barrier and everything on the other side of it. Everything they had bled for. The tide was against them. The engine was dying. Every second they lingered was a second borrowed from their own survival, and everyone aboard knew it.

Priya watched Felix look at the lagoon. Then she watched him look back at the whistle sound rolling down the channel, and she saw the exact moment — she would remember it always, the way navigators remember fixes — when the choice was made before it was spoken.

"Come about," Felix said.

* * *

Later, much later, when the survivors were aboard and wrapped in blankets and the storm had spent its first fury, Priya sat in the wheelhouse with her charts spread out and her hands finally, finally steady, and did the thing she always did after a passage: she drew it. The Devil's Throat, from memory, every fix, every correction, the false channel and the true one, the bend where the water turned left. She labeled it carefully in her small precise handwriting, and beneath it she wrote the depth soundings and the tidal notes and the warning about the right-hand trap, so that the next ship to come this way would have what she'd had to build from nothing.

At the bottom of the chart, after a moment's thought, she added a single line:

*First surveyed under duress. Survived by believing the water.*

Through the aft window she could see Felix at the rail, watching the wrecked gunboat settle by the stern in the distance, the whistle finally silent, the Syndicate's power broken on the same reef that had nearly broken them. Tomorrow there would be consequences. Tomorrow Osei and his collectors would be passengers instead of hunters, and ledgers would be torn, and debts would dissolve, and none of it would be simple.

Tonight there was just the chart, drying in the lamplight, and the strange quiet knowledge that the impossible thing had happened, and she had steered them into it on faith, and faith had been enough.

Her mother had been right after all. Navigation was a prayer. You just had to be desperate enough to say it out loud.
