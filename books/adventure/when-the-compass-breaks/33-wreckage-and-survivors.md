## 33. Wreckage and Survivors

The gunboat died screaming.

Felix heard it through the hull first — a long, grinding shriek of steel on coral that traveled through the water and up into the Kestrel's bones like a nerve being pinched. Then the sound arrived properly, rolling across the channel behind them: the crack of the bow shearing, the boom of something large and pressurized giving up, and beneath it all a human sound, thin and scattered, that no amount of engine noise could cover.

He was at the wheel before he'd decided to be. The Kestrel came about in a wide, groaning arc, her patched hull complaining with every degree of turn, and Felix stood there with his hands on the spokes and let himself look back at what had been chasing them for three weeks.

The Syndicate vessel — *Meridian Grace*, the collectors' pride, forty meters of black steel and intimidation — lay broken across the reef line like a dropped toy. Her bow section had driven itself deep into the coral shelf and stayed there, canted at an angle that made Felix's stomach clench even from this distance. The stern had torn free and was settling on its own, listing hard to port, smoke pouring from somewhere below the waterline in greasy brown columns that the wind flattened and dragged east.

And between the two halves of her, spread across a quarter mile of churning white water, were the survivors.

"Dear God," Priya said quietly beside him. She'd come up without him noticing, chart tube still under her arm. "How many?"

"I count..." Felix squinted against the spray. Orange dots, scattered like spilled fruit. "Twelve. Maybe fifteen. Some are moving."

"Some aren't."

"No," Felix agreed. "Some aren't."

Tomas appeared at the companionway, wiping grease off his hands with a rag that had been clean three weeks ago and would never be clean again. He looked at the wreck, then at Felix, and said nothing — which from Tomas was a whole speech. It was Tomas who'd spent the last hour swearing at the bilge pumps, Tomas whose hands had rebuilt the engine twice since the island, Tomas who had every reason in the world to keep sailing and let the sea settle its own accounts.

"They'll die," Tomas said finally. Not a question. A statement of fact, delivered in the flat tone he used for engineering reports. "Water's maybe twenty-two degrees. Swell's building. Hypothermia in two hours, three if they're lucky. Drowning before that if the swell picks them up and slams them into the reef."

"I know."

"And they came here to take everything we have. They'd have put us off the boat in the middle of nowhere if it suited their ledgers." Tomas folded the rag with exaggerated care. "I want you to know I'm aware of the arithmetic."

"I'm aware of it too."

"Good." Tomas stuffed the rag in his pocket. "Then let's not pretend this is a hard decision, because it isn't one, and pretending will only make it feel worse later. Get the rescue gear. I'll get the boat ready."

* * *

They worked in pairs because that was how Tomas insisted it be done, and after everything, nobody argued with Tomas about procedure anymore.

Amara took the skiff with Felix. Priya ran the deck, calling positions through cupped hands over the wind. Ines — stripped of her yacht, her fortune, her carefully constructed empire, wearing a borrowed sweater three sizes too big — stood at the rail with a coil of rope in her hands and an expression Felix couldn't read at all.

The first man they pulled out was young, maybe twenty-five, blue-lipped and shaking so hard his teeth clicked like dice. He grabbed Felix's arm with both hands as Felix hauled him over the gunwale, and for a second their eyes met, and Felix saw the exact moment the kid recognized him — saw the recognition curdle into fear, because this was Eriksen, the debtor, the mark, the man whose file the kid had probably carried in a waterproof pouch along with everyone else's.

"It's alright," Felix said, and meant it, and didn't know why he meant it.

The kid started crying. Felix wrapped him in a blanket and moved to the next one.

They got eleven alive. Eleven men and women pulled from the wreckage of the machine that had been sent to destroy them, hauled aboard a leaking sloop by the very people they'd been sent to break. Two more they found too late, floating face-down among the debris field, and Felix made himself look at each of them for a long moment before Amara gently turned the skiff toward the next signal.

The head collector was the last one.

He wasn't in the water when they found him. He was on the reef itself, wedged in a notch of coral near the exposed shoulder of the wrecked bow section, one leg bent wrong beneath him, one hand still gripping a waterproof case that he refused to release even as the waves broke over him again and again. Felix brought the skiff alongside as close as the coral allowed, and for a moment the two men just looked at each other across four feet of boiling white water.

Felix knew him. Everyone who owed the Meridian Syndicate knew him. Aldous Wren, Regional Director of Collections, a name spoken in port bars the way sailors spoke of storms — with respect, and dread, and the careful vagueness of people who don't want to be overheard. Felix had seen him exactly once before, three years ago, across a desk in a Jakarta office that smelled of orchids and old money, while Wren explained in a voice like warm honey what would happen to Felix's crew if payments lapsed again.

"You're going to leave me here," Wren said. His voice was steady. His hands were not.

"Yeah," Felix said. "I considered it."

Something flickered across Wren's face — not hope, exactly. Recalculation. The collector running numbers even now, even waist-deep in the ruins of his own authority.

"But I'm not going to," Felix said. "Because my father taught me two things about the sea. First: you don't get to decide who deserves saving. The sea already decided, and it decided everyone. Second—" he reached down and took the waterproof case from Wren's bloodless fingers, holding it up between them, "—you never pass up salvage."

Wren actually laughed. It was a horrible sound, half pain and half disbelief, but it was real.

"Your leg's broken," Felix said, hauling him up over the gunwale while Wren hissed through his teeth. "Probably. We'll splint it. And Wren — you're going to owe me now. You understand that? Not the other way around. You're going to owe me your life, and I'm going to remember it, and I'm going to collect."

* * *

Below decks, the Kestrel had never been so crowded or so quiet.

Eleven survivors packed into every dry corner, wrapped in every blanket the crew owned, drinking tea from mismatched cups because Tomas had produced thermoses from somewhere with the smugness of a man who'd been preparing for disasters his whole life. The injured ones laid out on the mess table and bench seats, Amara moving among them with a competence that made Felix wonder — not for the first time — what exactly her archaeology training had covered. Wren occupied the bench by the bulkhead, leg splinted with a boat hook handle and sailcloth, face gray with pain and something worse than pain.

Shame, Felix thought. That was the worse thing. He'd seen it often enough in mirrors.

Felix sat across from him on an overturned crate, the waterproof case on his knees. Around them, the rescued sailors watched with the particular stillness of people waiting to learn their fate.

"You know what's in here," Felix said.

"My documents," Wren said. "Vessel registration. Insurance manifests. My commission papers."

"Your commission papers." Felix turned the case over in his hands. "Which authorize you to seize our vessel, detain our crew, and liquidate our assets. Which list the interest rates you've been charging me — I've read them, Wren, I did the math on the crossing. Do you know what my father's patents alone would have been worth on the open market? Do you know what you were sent to take?"

"I know what I was sent to recover."

"Do you?" Felix leaned forward. "Because here's what I think. I think you're a man who follows orders and keeps good records, and I think somewhere in those records is a number that represents my mother's life, and I don't think you've ever once looked at that number and seen anything but a figure in a column."

Wren's jaw tightened. For a long moment the only sounds were the wind above and the moaning of the injured and the endless percussion of the sea against a hull that had no right still to be floating.

"The Syndicate doesn't see people," Wren said finally. "It sees exposure. Risk profiles. I told them this operation was low-risk. I was wrong. Fourteen of my people are dead or missing because I was wrong." He looked down at his ruined leg, then up, and his eyes were dry and terribly clear. "So do whatever you're going to do. But understand that whatever you take from me tonight, the Syndicate will simply send someone else. I am replaceable. That is rather the point of me."

"Is it?" Felix set the case down between them. "Then here's what replaceable gets you. You owe me your life. All of you do — every person on this boat. And I'm not going to hold it over you the way the Syndicate holds debt over people. I'm going to do something worse."

"What's that?"

"I'm going to ask you to witness what happens next. Fairly. With your own eyes. Because you keep good records, Aldous Wren, and starting tomorrow, you're going to keep them for us."

Silence. Even the injured had gone quiet, listening.

"The patents go to Amara's family," Felix said. "The victims' fund gets established — every family the Halcyon Star disaster touched, including yours, including mine. The Syndicate's claims against this crew dissolve, witnessed and documented, with your signature and your seal. And in exchange, you and your people get safe passage, medical care, and a story to tell your masters that might just keep them off our backs for a while — because the alternative is explaining to them how their regional director lost his ship, his cargo, and his leverage to a debt-ridden storm chaser and a patched-up fishing sloop."

Wren stared at him. Then, slowly, painfully, he began to laugh again — quieter this time, almost wondering.

"You realize," he said, "that mercy is a far more effective instrument of leverage than any threat I have ever employed. In fifteen years of collections, I have never once been on the receiving end of it." He extended his hand — the uninjured one, trembling slightly. "You have yourself a witness, Captain Eriksen."

Felix shook it.

Above them, through the deck planks, the storm went on raging. But down in the crowded, steaming, impossible belly of the little boat that shouldn't have survived, something had shifted — some weight redistributed, some ledger quietly rewritten. Felix looked around at the faces: his crew, his rivals, his enemies, all of them breathing, all of them aboard.

His father's compass was gone — shattered in the Devil's Throat, its glass face cracked, its needle stilled forever. Felix didn't need it anymore. He knew where he was.

Tomorrow they would tear the ledgers in half. Tonight, they would save whoever was left to save.

Somewhere aft, faintly, he heard Priya's voice calling down the companionway — urgent, breathless:

"Felix! You need to see this. There's another boat out there — and it's flying no colors at all."
