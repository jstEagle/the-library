## 34. Ledgers Torn in Half

The collector's name was Oskar Rehnquist, and he threw up twice before we got him aboard.

Tomas hauled him over the rail by the collar of his ruined uniform while Priya and Amara pulled the other two Syndicate sailors out of the water — younger men, deckhands mostly, the kind the Syndicate hired because they had nowhere else to go and no questions to ask. They came up coughing, shaking, one of them crying without seeming to know he was doing it. The gunboat that had chased us through the Devil's Throat lay broken on the reef behind us, its stern half-swallowed, its bow jutting into the morning air like a headstone.

I stood at the wheel of the Kestrel with my hands still trembling from the channel and watched them shiver on my deck, and I felt something I hadn't expected to feel about these men.

Nothing. Or close to it. Not hate. Hate needed fuel, and I'd burned mine somewhere back in the storm.

Rehnquist finished retching, wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, and looked up at me from his knees on wet decking. He was maybe fifty-five, silver at the temples, a man who'd spent thirty years making other people's lives arithmetic. His face was a mess of salt spray and humiliation.

"You," he said. "Eriksen."

"Me."

"You wrecked a Syndicate vessel."

"The reef wrecked your vessel. We just declined to join it." I crouched so we were eye to eye. "You want a blanket and coffee, or you want to keep talking?"

He chose the blanket. Tomas brought three, and hot tea in dented mugs, and none of us said anything for a few minutes while the rescued men drank and shook and stared at the wreckage astern. The monsoon was moving off to the west, dragging its gray skirt across the horizon, and the light coming through behind it was the clean gold of a world rinsed new.

It was Amara who broke the silence, and it was Amara who set the terms of everything that followed, though she didn't know it yet.

"He saved us," she said quietly, nodding at Rehnquist's youngest sailor. "When the boat went over, he grabbed the other two and kept their heads up. Thirty seconds more and they'd have been gone."

The kid — he couldn't have been twenty-two — looked down into his tea like it might tell him how to answer.

I stood up. My knees cracked. Forty hours of rain and a lifetime of debt will do that to a man.

* * *

We anchored in the lee of the nameless island, where the Kestrel sat beached and broken and beloved, and where Ines Varga was still, as far as we knew, sitting in a Syndicate camp trading her fortune for our lives. That thought sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. Every hour we spent here was an hour she was spending alone among collectors with guns.

But the sea doesn't negotiate, and neither did the Kestrel's hull. We couldn't sail anywhere until Tomas pronounced her sound, and Tomas wouldn't pronounce anything until he'd pulled half her planking apart and put it back together. So we had time. Time, and a rescued collector, and a strongbox full of paper worth more than every ship in this archipelago.

I laid it all out on the chart table under the awning we'd rigged on the beach: the patents, the deeds, the letter in my father's cramped hand naming Amara's family as partners — partners betrayed, their shares absorbed, their names erased from the record after the Halcyon Star went down and took eleven people with it, including my mother and Amara's father and, though I hadn't known it until the firelight reckoning two nights ago, Ines's brother.

Rehnquist sat across from me, dry now, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of Tomas's engine room. His two sailors hovered at the edge of the camp, unsure whether they were prisoners or guests. The crew gathered around in a loose semicircle: Priya with her arms crossed, Tomas leaning against the mast step, Amara standing closest to the table, her eyes never leaving the letter.

"I'm going to say this once," I told Rehnquist, "and then you're going to witness something."

"I don't witness things for pirates."

"We're not pirates. If we were, you'd be dead, and your boat would already be stripped." I turned the letter toward him. "Read this. Read all of it. Then tell me what the Meridian Syndicate actually holds."

He read. I watched his face do the thing faces do when a man discovers the ground under him is made of something else entirely. The patents were real. The deeds were real. And the letter — my father's confession, written in ink that had survived thirty years in a sealed box at the bottom of the green dark — named names, dates, amounts. It documented, in the dispassionate language of a dying engineer, exactly how the Syndicate had taken control of the Eriksen-Vasquez survey partnership through fraud, coercion, and finally sabotage-adjacent negligence: pressure applied to insurers, charts doctored, warnings ignored, a ship sent into a storm window her own owners' data said was suicide.

My father hadn't just hidden the inheritance. He'd hidden the evidence. And then he'd spent the rest of his life too ashamed to use either.

Rehnquist set the letter down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

"This is notarized," he said. "This is witnessed. This is—" He stopped. Recalculated. I could almost hear the abacus behind his eyes grinding to a halt. "This is actionable."

"It is," I said. "Here's the action."

* * *

I won't pretend I walked into that negotiation clean. I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the only thing my father left me that turned out to be worth anything.

There was a moment — one moment, standing there with the box open and Rehnquist captive and the power inverted so completely it sang — when I thought about taking all of it. The patents, the fund, the whole fortune. Enough to erase my debts ten times over. Enough to buy a new boat, a new life, a version of Felix Eriksen who never had to flinch at a knock on the door again. Nobody on that beach would have blamed me. Half of them had voted to throw me overboard metaphorically at least twice since we left harbor.

But I'd held my mother's stories in my hands instead of her gold, and I'd steered through the Devil's Throat on memory and trust, and somewhere between those two things the arithmetic had changed. Debt wasn't just money. I'd learned that from the wrong direction, but I'd learned it.

So here's what I proposed, and I proposed it loud enough for everyone to hear, including the two young sailors at the edge of the fire ring, because witnesses were the entire point.

"One. The patents revert to the Vasquez family — to Amara, as the last surviving heir, per the partnership terms in that letter. She licenses them or sells them or burns them; it's hers. Two. A restitution fund, capitalized by the patent portfolio itself, administered jointly, paying out to every family that lost someone on the Halcyon Star. Eleven dead, eleven households. Three. The Meridian Syndicate's claims against me — the debts, the interest, the gunboats, all of it — dissolve. Not because I pay them. Because if they don't dissolve, I take this letter to every maritime court, every insurance regulator, and every newspaper between here and Singapore, and the Syndicate learns what its own medicine tastes like."

Rehnquist's jaw worked. "You're asking me to tear up thirty years of ledgers."

"I'm asking you to survive," I said. "Your boat is on a reef. Your employer's legal position is a house of cards and I'm holding the wind. Your choice is whether you're standing inside the house when it comes down or outside holding the door for the auditors."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we keep you comfortable until Tomas fixes the Kestrel, drop you at the nearest port with your two boys, and file the paperwork anyway. You're not leverage, Rehnquist. You're a witness. There's a difference, and the difference is that nobody hurts you either way."

That last part landed harder than any threat would have. I watched him understand it — watched a man whose entire career ran on fear realize that fear wasn't on the table, which meant the only currency left was reason. Men like him are helpless without fear. It's their native language, and I'd just told him we weren't speaking it.

He looked at the letter again. At Amara. At the eleven names my father had listed, and I saw him find the one that mattered — Vasquez, R., chief hydrographer — and I saw something move behind his eyes that might, in a better man, have been called conscience.

"The Syndicate will send others," he said. "Not for you. For me, if I sign."

"Then sign fast," Tomas rumbled from the mast step, "and come stand next to us. We've got room. Bad food, good company."

Everyone laughed, even Rehnquist, a short startled bark like a man remembering laughter existed. Then he asked for a pen.

* * *

We wrote it on the back of one of Priya's spare charts, because it was the only paper big enough and flat enough, and because there was a poetry to it that Priya appreciated and I didn't fully understand until later: terms of justice written on a chart of waters where the crime happened. Amara dictated the clauses in a voice that never wavered, though her hand shook when she signed. Her family's name restored. The fund established. The victims enumerated — all eleven, each name read aloud, each one a small bell tolling through the camp.

Ines's brother's name went on the list too. Mateo Varga, third engineer. When Amara read it, Rehnquist glanced up sharply — he'd known, then, or suspected, why Ines Varga had been in that camp offering her yacht as collateral. He signed anyway. Maybe because of it.

The dissolution clause was the hardest. Rehnquist argued for an hour — not against dissolving the debts, but against the wording, because wording was the only dignity he had left. In the end we settled on language that gave the Syndicate a fig leaf: claims voided "in consideration of documentary evidence rendering original instruments invalid." Translation, as Priya murmured to me: *you stole it first, so technically you can't collect on stolen goods.* The Syndicate could sell that to its board. Barely. With enough lawyers.

Rehnquist signed. Then he made his two sailors sign as witnesses, and then — this surprised me — he asked Amara to countersign as the injured party, and asked me to countersign as the defrauded party, and when it was done he sat back and looked older and lighter at once, like a man who'd set down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying.

"My superiors will not honor this," he said quietly.

"No," I agreed. "They'll try to bury it. That's why there are copies." I tapped the oilskin packet where Priya had sealed duplicates — one to go to the port authority at Surabaya, one to a journalist Priya trusted, one buried on this island in case the first two never arrived. "And that's why you're going to deliver the first copy yourself. Walking into their office. Alive, promoted by the scandal of having survived, untouchable precisely because killing you proves the letter true."

He stared at me for a long moment. "You've thought about this."

"I've been thinking about nothing else since I was nine years old," I said. "It just took me thirty years to think about it clearly."

* * *

That evening we fed the Syndicate sailors at our fire, and they ate like the starving young men they were, and one of them — the one who'd kept the others' heads above water — told us, unprompted, that he'd been on the gunboat the night it shadowed us off Palawan. That the orders had come from higher than Rehnquist. That there were people in the Syndicate who'd wanted the Halcyon Star's secrets found and destroyed, not recovered, and that Rehnquist himself had been walking a narrow line for years between collecting debts and knowing too much.

Rehnquist heard all of this from across the fire and didn't deny a word of it. He just looked into the flames and said, "The ledgers were never the point. The ledgers were how they owned people. Tear up enough ledgers and the whole machine starves."

Amara sat beside me, the letter safe in its oilskin against her chest, and after the sailors slept she spoke without looking at me.

"Your father stole from mine," she said. "And then hid the proof for thirty years because he couldn't face it. And today you handed me everything anyway."

"He handed it to you. I just carried the box."

"Don't do that." Her voice was gentle but firm, the voice she'd used to talk me through the worst bend of the channel. "Don't disappear inside his ghost. He was a coward at the end. You weren't. Those are different men, Felix, and I've sailed with both of them now — one in a letter and one at a wheel — and I know the difference."

I didn't have an answer for that, so I just nodded, and she let me, and the fire crackled, and somewhere beyond the lagoon the sea breathed in and out like something enormous sleeping.

One problem remained, and it was shaped like a woman who'd traded her fortune for our lives and was at this moment sitting in a Syndicate camp on the far side of the island with nothing left but her nerve.

"We sail tomorrow," I told the crew, "if Tomas says the hull holds."

Tomas, half-asleep against a log, opened one eye. "Hull holds."

"Then tomorrow we collect Ines Varga," I said, "before the Syndicate decides a witness is cheaper dead than paid."

Across the fire, Rehnquist went very still, and I realized he knew exactly what Ines had offered them — and exactly how little her bargain was likely to buy her now that the ledger she'd bet against had just been torn in half.
