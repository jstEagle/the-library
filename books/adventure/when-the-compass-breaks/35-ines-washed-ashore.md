## 35. Ines, Washed Ashore

The tide brought Ines Varga in on her back.

She woke to the taste of salt and the weight of wet silk, flat on her spine in the shallows of the lagoon, the sky above her the color of a bruise healing. Her hands were empty. Her hair had come loose from its knot and fanned out in the water like ink spilled by a careless hand. Somewhere behind her, out past the reef line, her yacht — the *Meridian Rose*, ninety feet of teak and titanium and eleven years of her life — was either sinking or broken or simply gone, and for the first time in longer than she could calculate, Ines Varga owned nothing that anyone wanted.

She lay there and let the small waves rock her, and she laughed.

It came out of her like something escaping a sealed hold — a raw, unlovely sound, half sob, half release, loud enough to startle the gulls off the black rocks. She laughed until her ribs ached, until the laughing turned to coughing and the coughing turned to weeping, and she let that happen too, alone, in the shallows, with no one to perform for. It was the first honest thing she had done in weeks. Possibly years.

Then she got up, wrung out her blouse, and walked up the beach to find out what was left of her plan.

* * *

What was left was this: the Syndicate had taken her at her word.

She had walked into their camp on the third morning of the rain with her hands visible and her chin up, and she had said, *Take me and the yacht as collateral. Let the crew of the Kestrel work. If they run, you own everything I have.* She had watched the head collector — a soft-spoken man named Oduya with the eyes of an accountant and the patience of a fisherman — weigh her offer the way he'd weigh a cargo manifest. He had accepted. He had even, absurdly, shaken her hand.

And then the crew had run, exactly as she'd known they would, exactly as she'd *needed* them to, and the Syndicate had come to collect.

She had spent two days on the gunboat after the wreck, before the rescue, before the inversion of everything — two days in a cabin that smelled of diesel, listening to Oduya's men argue about salvage law and storm damage and whether a drowned gunboat constituted a total loss or a write-down. She had listened to them say her name the way you say an asset. *Varga's collateral covers the hull. Varga's collateral covers the fuel. Varga's collateral covers the interest.* She had sat with her hands folded and let them talk about her in the third person, and somewhere in the second hour she had understood, with a clarity that felt almost like grace, that she had never once in her life been spoken about so accurately.

Oduya himself had come to see her on the last evening, before the ledgers were torn. He had sat down across from her, poured two glasses of the Syndicate's terrible brandy, and studied her for a long moment.

"You knew the gunboat would wreck," he said. Not accusing. Curious, almost.

"I knew it was heavy and proud and captained by a man who'd never run that channel in his life," Ines said. "I knew the Kestrel was light and desperate and steered by a man whose mother taught him these waters before he could read. I made a bet on which hull the sea preferred."

"And if you'd lost?"

"Then I'd have lost everything a day later instead of a day sooner." She lifted her glass. "I've been losing it slowly for eleven years, Mr. Oduya. This was faster. I recommend it."

He had almost smiled. Then he had told her the terms, and the terms were total. The yacht, seized and sold. The accounts in Singapore, Jakarta, and Zurich, frozen and drained. The Varga name on the salvage registry, struck. She had signed each page without reading them, because she already knew what they said, because she had written versions of them herself for other people, in other rooms, in another life.

"You're taking this remarkably well," Oduya had observed, capping his pen.

"I'm not taking anything," Ines said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

They put her ashore at the island two days after the negotiation, on the Syndicate's tender, with a duffel bag of her own clothes and nothing else. No escort. No debt. The papers had dissolved everything — the Syndicate's claims on Felix, the phantom liens, the whole architecture of pressure she had helped build and then helped demolish. Oduya had signed off on it with the detached satisfaction of a man closing a file that had grown too expensive to keep open. A drowned gunboat and a head collector who owed the Kestrel's crew his life had a way of making old debts look like small change.

The tender pulled away. Ines stood on the white sand with her duffel at her feet and looked at what remained.

The Kestrel sat beached at the top of the tide line where they'd run her aground, propped on timbers, her starboard hull open like a dissected specimen. Tomas was inside her to the waist, and she could hear him swearing at a fitting in three languages, a sound so domestic and so furious and so alive that it made her throat tighten. Smoke rose from a driftwood fire pit near the tree line. Laundry — actual laundry, shirts and socks strung between two palms — flapped in the trade wind. Priya had pinned charts to a board under an awning and was annotating them with a pencil held in her teeth. Amara sat at the bow of the half-dismantled sloop they'd escaped in, sanding a rail with long, patient strokes, and when she saw Ines standing there, she didn't stop sanding. She just watched her come up the beach, and her eyes said: *I know what you did. I haven't decided what it was worth.*

Fair enough, Ines thought. She hadn't decided either.

Felix came out of the trees carrying an armload of firewood, and stopped.

For a moment neither of them moved. The wind came off the lagoon and pushed at Ines's damp hair, and she was suddenly, absurdly aware of how she must look — salt-stiff, sunburned, wearing the last of her expensive clothes like a costume from a play that had closed. Felix Eriksen looked, for his part, like a man who had been rebuilt from the keel up. Leaner. Quieter in the eyes. He set down the firewood with exaggerated care, the way a man does when he needs the two seconds it buys him.

"You're supposed to be in Jakarta," he said. "Counting your money."

"I was counting it on the way there." She shrugged. "It counted back."

He crossed the sand toward her slowly, and she braced for it — the anger, the reckoning, the speech about the *Halcyon Star* and the burned charts and the brother she had never mentioned and the eleven years of engineering that had dragged him back to the reef where his mother died. She had rehearsed her answers on the gunboat. She had rehearsed them in the tender. She had been rehearsing them, if she was honest, for most of her adult life.

What she had not rehearsed was Felix Eriksen holding out his hand.

She looked at it. Tanned, scarred at the knuckles, steady.

"You stalled Oduya for six hours," Felix said. "In the storm, with the box in our hold and his guns on us. You lied to a man who had you as collateral, knowing what it would cost. I've had time to do the arithmetic, Ines. I don't like what you did. But I can't argue with what it bought."

"And if I tell you I did it for myself? That my brother was on that ship, and I wanted his ghost settled, and your crew was just the instrument?"

"Then I'd say you're the same liar you've always been," Felix said, "and that the lie doesn't matter anymore." His hand stayed out. "Take it or don't. I'm done keeping score."

Ines looked at his hand for a long moment. Then, before she could stop herself — before the old machinery of calculation could spin up and tell her what this gesture would cost, what it could be leveraged into, what it would look like from the outside — she took it.

His grip was warm and rough and brief. It was the least transactional touch she had received in eleven years, and it undid her more thoroughly than the loss of the yacht.

"God," she said, and her voice cracked on the single syllable. She cleared her throat. "I'm going to need a job."

* * *

They fed her that evening at the fire, and it was the strangest meal of her life.

Not because of the food — rice and tinned fish and the sweet little bananas that grew wild on the windward side, eaten off scavenged plates. Not because of the setting — the fire crackling, the stars coming out over a lagoon so still it held them like a second sky. It was strange because of the way they treated her, which was to say: ordinarily. Tomas asked her to pass the salt and complained to her about the starboard shaft bearing as if she were an engineer, which she was not, and she complained back as if she understood, and he laughed at her. Priya asked her about currents off the Sunda shelf with the focused hunger of a woman who collected other people's knowledge the way other people collected stamps, and Ines found herself giving it freely, and found, to her bewilderment, that she enjoyed it. Amara said almost nothing, but she refilled Ines's cup without being asked, once, and in the grammar of this crew that was practically an embrace.

No one asked what she was worth. No one asked what she could do for them. For the first time since she was a girl in a fishing village on the Mindanao coast — before the scholarships, before the shipping lines, before she learned that everything and everyone was a position to be taken — Ines Varga sat among people who wanted nothing from her at all.

It was terrifying. It was the freest she had ever been.

She found Felix after the fire had burned down to coals, down at the waterline where he'd gone to sit alone, the way she'd seen him go alone every night since she'd arrived. He had something in his hands — the broken compass, she realized. His father's. The housing cracked, the glass gone, the needle loose in its ruin. He turned it over and over like a man worrying a prayer bead.

"Your brother," Felix said, without looking up. "Tell me."

So she told him.

She told him about Mateo, who had been twenty-four and brilliant and broke, who had signed onto the *Halcyon Star* as a junior survey officer because the Syndicate's advance was enough to pay for their mother's surgery. She told him how the letters had stopped. How the Syndicate had sent a settlement offer — insulting, sealed, take-it-or-be-sued — and how she, twenty-six years old and hungry and already learning the shape of the world, had advised them to take it. How she had spent the money building the first boat of her fleet, and how every boat after that had been built on the same keel of guilt, and how she had spent eleven years telling herself that if she got big enough, rich enough, close enough to the Syndicate's ledgers, she would find out what really happened in those waters, and she would make it right.

"And then I found your father's name in a patent filing," she said. "And I found the *Halcyon Star*'s last survey route, and I understood that Eriksen had hidden something aboard her worth killing for, and I thought — there it is. The key to everything. I just needed his son to open the door." She watched the firelight move on the water. "I told myself it was justice. For Mateo. For your mother. For Amara's family. It was, partly. But partly it was just the only kind of ambition I knew how to feel anymore, which is ambition wearing grief's clothes. They look very similar from the inside, Felix. I recommend learning the difference before you're my age."

Felix was quiet for a long time. The compass turned in his hands.

"My father sabotaged the ship," he said finally. "You knew that too, didn't you. Before we ever dove."

"I suspected. The way the survey records were doctored — it had his handwriting all over it. Figuratively." She glanced at him. "I needed you to see it yourself. If I'd told you, you'd have defended him. You'd have had to. He was all you had left of her."

"And now?"

"Now you know he was a man," Ines said. "Who did a monstrous thing for a loving reason, and a loving thing in a monstrous way, and spent the rest of his life burning charts on a beach trying to decide which one he'd done." She looked at him, and let him see her, all the way down, for the first time since a dockside bar in another life. "That's not a villain, Felix. That's just a father. Mine was the same, in his way. Smaller sea, same weather."

Felix laughed — a short, startled sound, the laugh of a man who has been hit somewhere he didn't know was tender. He looked down at the broken compass in his hands.

"She used to tell me stories about these waters," he said. "My mother. The channels, the reefs, the way the light came green through the water in the afternoon. I thought I'd come back here for the money. The patents, the charts, the whole inheritance." He closed his fist around the ruined compass. "Turns out the only thing of hers I actually wanted was the stories. And I didn't even remember I had them until Amara started asking."

"Then you got the whole estate," Ines said, "and I got a duffel bag. We should form a company."

This time he laughed properly, and she let herself laugh with him, and the sound went out over the dark lagoon and came back changed, softer, like something the sea had decided to return.

* * *

She left on the third morning.

There was a supply run — Tomas had bartered repairs for diesel with a copra boat that touched at the island twice a month, and the copra boat's captain, a laconic man named Reyes, had agreed to carry a passenger as far as Balikpapan. Ines packed her duffel the night before, all of it: two shirts, a jacket, a toothbrush, a waterproof bag containing the only documents she owned now, which were a letter of introduction from Oduya — *known to this office; obligations settled* — and, folded into a pocket of the lining where no one would find it unless she wanted them to, a photocopy of a page from her brother's survey logbook that she had carried for eleven years and had never once been able to read without her hands shaking.

She was up before dawn. Of course she was. Some habits outlive their reasons.

Amara was waiting at the tide line.

They stood together in the gray light while the copra boat's anchor light blinked out on the horizon, and for a while neither of them spoke. It was Amara who finally broke the silence, her voice low and even, the voice of a woman who had decided something and was reporting it rather than debating it.

"The letter names my family as partners," Amara said. "Betrayed. My grandfather spent thirty years telling anyone who'd listen that the Eriksens stole from us. My father spent thirty years telling him to let it go. I spent thirty years not deciding which of them to believe." She turned and looked at Ines, and her eyes in the half-light were unreadable and very steady. "You knew. From the beginning. That the box would break us open — him, me, all of it. You built the whole job to make it happen."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Ines considered lying. It was remarkable, she thought, how the old reflex still fired, how the tongue still reached for the polished version. She let the impulse pass through her and out on the wind.

"Because I watched my own family's wreck get settled with money," she said, "and it settled nothing. It just bought silence, and silence is where ghosts get fat." She looked out at the water. "I wanted yours settled in the open. Out loud. With everyone in the room. I didn't care what it cost me, because by then I'd already cost myself everything worth keeping, and I figured — what's one more hull on the reef, if the channel finally gets charted?"

Amara studied her for a long moment. Then she did something Ines did not expect: she reached out and straightened the collar of Ines's jacket — a small, brisk, almost sisterly gesture, the kind of thing done between people who have bled in the same water.

"Reyes will want his coffee," Amara said. "Don't make him wait."

It was, Ines understood, the closest thing to absolution she was going to be offered, and it was more than she deserved, and she took it without argument for the first time in her life.

* * *

Felix walked her to the tender.

The copra boat was a rust-streaked thirty-footer that smelled of diesel and dried coconut, and Reyes watched them come down the beach with the patient indifference of a man who had ferried every kind of human cargo the archipelago could produce. Felix carried her duffel without asking, and handed it up to the deckhand, and then they stood together on the sand in the first true light of morning, and there was nothing left to arrange.

"So," Ines said. "Balikpapan. I hear they need someone who can read a salvage registry."

"You could teach it," Felix said. "Or you could do it honest, for once."

"I'm going to do it honest for exactly as long as it takes to be good at it," Ines said. "Then I'm going to do it well. It turns out those aren't the same thing. Nobody told me."

"I'm telling you now."

"Yes," she said. "You are. That's new."

She put out her hand — deliberately this time, formally, the old gesture emptied of everything transactional and filled instead with what was underneath, which was, she supposed, the only thing that had ever really been between them: two children of the same disaster, standing on opposite shores of the same grief for thirty years, finally meeting in the middle of the water.

Felix took it. Held it a moment longer than the first time.

"Write to us," he said. "Tomas will want to argue about shaft bearings. Priya will want your current data. I'll just want to know you're all right."

"You'll know," Ines said. "I'm going to be more all right than I've ever been, and I'm going to be insufferable about it."

She climbed aboard. Reyes cast off. The little boat swung its nose toward the channel, and Ines stood at the rail — an ordinary rail, on an ordinary boat, with no flag and no registry and no one aboard who wanted anything from her — and watched the island fall away behind them.

The Kestrel sat on her timbers above the tide line, half-skinned, half-reborn, with figures moving around her in the early light. Tomas's voice carried across the water, swearing at something in three languages. Priya's charts fluttered on their board. Amara stood at the bow of the little sloop, sanding, patient as tide. And Felix — Felix stood on the beach with the firewood over his shoulder like a man in a painting of a life she had never once, in all her years of acquiring, thought to want.

Ines Varga raised one hand, not waving exactly. Acknowledging. The way you acknowledge a lighthouse.

Then she turned her face toward Balikpapan, toward the honest work and the hard years and the letter in her lining that she could finally, maybe, someday, read with steady hands — and behind her, on the beach, Felix Eriksen turned back toward the fire, where Amara was setting out the breakfast plates and calling his name across the sand, in a voice that carried.
