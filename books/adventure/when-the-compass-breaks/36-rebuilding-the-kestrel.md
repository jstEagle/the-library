## 36. Rebuilding the Kestrel

The keel was still true. That was the first thing Tomas checked every morning, before coffee, before the sun cleared the ridge of volcanic rock that sheltered their little cove. He'd wade out into the shallows where the Kestrel lay heeled over on her port side like a tired animal, put his palm flat against the keel timber, and run his hand along it stem to stern. True. Still true.

"Talking to her again?" Amara stood at the waterline with two mugs of something that was technically coffee and spiritually an insult to coffee everywhere. She'd taken to rising before him, which he resented on principle. Engineers were supposed to be first up. It was in the unwritten contract.

"Somebody has to," Tomas said, taking a mug. "The rest of you just hit her with hammers."

"We're *building* her."

"You're building her. I'm listening to her." He knocked his knuckles against the hull planking, low down near the waterline, and listened to the dull thud of saturated wood. "She's telling me this section's done. Third plank from the garboard, four feet either side of frame twelve. We can epoxy it till doomsday, it'll never hold a sea."

Amara crouched, squinting along the hull the way she did — like she could read grain the way Priya read charts. "So we replace it."

"So we replace it." He said it lightly, but the words sat heavy in his chest. Replacing meant timber, and timber meant the island had it or they didn't. Three weeks now since they'd beached her, and the island had given them what islands give: driftwood, stubbornness, and just enough luck to keep from starving.

Three weeks since the Syndicate boats had come into the lagoon at dawn. Three weeks since Felix had walked down to meet them carrying the strongbox under one arm like it weighed nothing, when everyone knew it held everything. Three weeks since the collectors had left with the patents and deeds and every scrap of paper worth dying for, and left behind clean ledgers and five people who owned nothing but each other and a broken boat on a nameless beach.

Tomas had expected grief. What he hadn't expected was how light the crew would feel. How they'd laughed that first night, really laughed, around the fire, until Ines — stripped of her yacht, her fortune, her whole glittering armor — had laughed hardest of all and then cried into her sleeve and pretended she hadn't.

He drained the bad coffee and rolled up his sleeves. "Go wake the others. If we want her floated by the end of the month, we need that timber today."

* * *

The timber question solved itself the way most things did on the island: badly, then suddenly well.

They'd been scavenging the eastern shore for a week, hauling driftwood logs that were mostly rotten heart and disappointment. It was Priya who found the wreck — a fishing trawler, old, half-buried in sand on the far side of the point, her ribs exposed like the carcass of some enormous fish. Teak ribs. Forty years soaked and still sound enough to make Tomas's hands itch.

"She's been here longer than we've been alive," Priya said, standing on the trawler's upturned hull. "Feels wrong, doesn't it? Taking apart somebody else's boat."

"It feels right," Tomas said. "It's what boats are for. You sail her until she can't sail, and then she becomes other boats. That's not death, that's just... shipbuilding going in circles." He ran his hand along a rib, testing. "Whoever she belonged to would understand. Anybody who loved a boat would understand."

Felix said nothing, but Tomas caught him looking at the trawler's nameplate, long since scoured blank by sand and salt, and there was something in his face — not grief exactly. Recognition. The look of a man thinking about his father, which these days was less a wound than a habit, the way an old scar itches before rain.

They worked three days dismantling her. Amara turned out to have surgeon's hands for it — she could read a joint, find the peg or the seam holding everything together, and persuade it apart without breaking anything they needed. When Tomas asked where a marine archaeologist learned wreck disassembly, she smiled and said, "Where do you think archaeologists learn? Somebody built all these wrecks once. I got curious about the builders."

By the fourth day they had a sledge piled with curved teak ribs and planking stock, and they hauled it back across the point on rollers, all six of them leaning into the ropes, singing — badly, in three languages, none of them agreeing on the words. Even Ines, who claimed to be above manual labor and had spent two weeks being magnificently useless while her hands healed, took a turn on the rope. Her palms blistered. She didn't complain once, which from Ines was practically a religious conversion.

That night Tomas sat by the fire with his hands wrapped around a mug of the same terrible coffee and thought: *this is the richest I've ever been.* He'd had money once, early days, before the debts. Money had felt like standing on a platform watching trains leave. This felt like being on the train.

* * *

Frame twelve came out on a Tuesday.

Tomas had dreaded it for a week. Cutting out a frame was surgery near the spine — one bad cut and the whole hull lost its argument with the sea. He'd measured it eleven times. He'd drawn chalk lines and erased them. At dawn on the day itself he stood over the Kestrel with the saw in his hand and couldn't start.

"You're doing the thing again," Felix said, dropping down beside him on the shingle.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you treat the boat like she's made of glass and feelings."

"She *is* made of glass and feelings."

Felix grinned. For three weeks the grin had been coming easier, and every time it did, Tomas felt something in his own chest unclench a little more. The man who'd come aboard the Kestrel in chapter one of this mess — wound tight as a winch, flinching at his own name — had been slowly, plank by plank, getting rebuilt too. Nobody said it out loud. You didn't say things like that out loud on a working deck. But everybody saw it.

"Show me where to cut," Felix said.

"You've never cut a frame in your life."

"No. But you have, and your hands are shaking, so one of us should hold the saw and one of us should hold the other guy steady." He put his hand flat on Tomas's shoulder. "Together on three."

They cut it together. The old frame came free with a groan like a man sitting up from a sickbed, and the sound went through Tomas like a bell through water — loss and relief in the same note. They fitted the new rib, salvaged teak, steam-bent over a fire pit in the old way because nobody had brought a proper steamer to a desert island. Amara had known how to build the bending box. Of course she had.

When the new frame went home and the clamps bit down, the whole crew stood around the hull in a half circle, and nobody spoke, and the silence wasn't empty. It was the silence of a room after good news.

"Right," Tomas said finally, gruff, because someone had to break it before it turned into a moment. "Plank three. Who's holding the far end?"

Everyone raised their hands.

* * *

Some nights, when the work was done and the fire was down to coals, Tomas couldn't sleep and would walk the beach alone. It was on one of those nights, in the third week, that he found Amara at the Kestrel's bow with a small knife and a block of the dense black wood they'd found high on the storm line — ironwood, maybe, or something like it. She was carving.

He sat down beside her without asking. On this island, asking had mostly fallen away between them. You just sat.

"What are you making?" he asked eventually.

"A nameboard." She turned the block so the firelight caught the shallow letters taking shape: **K** — only the K so far, but the curves of it were already beautiful, deep and sure. "A boat shouldn't go back to sea unnamed. It's bad luck. And besides—" she paused, the knife resting, "—she needs a new name anyway, doesn't she? After everything."

Tomas frowned. "No, she doesn't. She's the Kestrel."

"Felix might want—"

"Felix doesn't get a vote." He heard his own voice come out harder than intended and softened it. "Look. Boats keep their names. That's the whole covenant. A man buys a boat, wrecks her, sells her, whatever — the name stays. The name is the boat's, not ours. His father named her, way back, before any of us. Before the Halcyon Star, before everything went wrong. Did you know that? The Kestrel was the Eriksen family boat first. Felix grew up on her. So no — she keeps her name. We're not rebuilding a boat. We're giving one back."

Amara looked at him for a long moment. Then she set down the block of ironwood and picked up, instead, a smaller piece of pale driftwood, and began carving that instead. He watched the letters emerge: *Rebuilt by the crew who stayed.* No names. Just that.

"For inside the cabin," she said. "Over the chart table. Where the people who matter will see it."

Tomas nodded and looked out at the dark water, and found that his eyes had gone hot, and decided the wind was responsible.

* * *

The engine was the part nobody talked about, because the engine was Tomas's cross to carry alone.

The Kestrel's diesel had drowned in the final run — salt water in the cylinders, in the injectors, God knew where else. He'd stripped her down to the block in the second week and laid every part out on canvas in the shade of the tarpaulin lean-to, forty years of engineering arranged in rows like bones on a museum table. Half of it was salvageable. Half was rust and prayer.

Priya found him at midnight hunched over the fuel pump, trying to coax a plunger back to true with emery cloth and spite.

"You know," she said, settling onto an upturned crate, "on my grandmother's island, they had a word for this. Fixing something past fixing. It translates roughly as 'arguing with ghosts.'"

"Ghosts are winning," Tomas muttered.

"Mm." She watched him work for a while. "You know what I figured out, lying awake last night? We've all been rebuilding different things. Felix is rebuilding himself. Amara's rebuilding a family. Ines is rebuilding — honestly I don't know what Ines is rebuilding, possibly an ego, possibly a soul. And you—" she tapped the fuel pump, gently, "—you're rebuilding the thing that makes us go. Because that's what you've always been, Tomás. Since before the debts, since the beginning. The rest of us argue about headings. You're the reason we arrive."

Tomas kept his head down over the pump because if he looked up she'd see his face doing something embarrassing. "That's the worst compliment anyone's ever given me," he said.

"I know. I'm very sorry." She stood, stretched, and paused at the edge of the lean-to. "The pump will go. It always goes, when you love it enough. Goodnight, ghost-arguer."

Two nights later, the pump went. He turned it over by hand, felt compression catch, and laughed out loud alone in the dark like a madman. Then he sat down next to the engine and put his forehead against the cold iron and stayed there a long time, the way you'd rest your hand on the shoulder of an old friend who'd come back from somewhere dangerous.

* * *

On the twenty-ninth day, they turned her over.

It took all six of them and a system of levers Priya designed and Ines financed with the last of her practical dignity — meaning she got in the mud and pushed. The hull came off the shingle rollers slow as a whale surfacing, groaned through the arc, and settled upright on her keel for the first time in a month, and the sight of her sitting square and level on the sand stopped every conversation on the beach.

She was ugly. Patched hull, mismatched planking, sailcloth-and-epoxy scars across her flank like a veteran's medals, new teak glowing pale against the old weathered gray. She listed slightly to starboard, which Tomas promised — aloud, repeatedly, and with feeling — to correct. She was the most beautiful thing any of them had ever seen.

Felix walked her length slowly, touching the rail, the mast step, the companionway. When he reached the bow, he stopped, and Tomas saw him mouth something — one word, quiet, to the wood or to whoever used to stand beside him on this deck. Tomas looked away and busied himself furiously with a coil of rope.

That evening they floated her in the shallows at high tide, just to see. She took a trickle of water through a seam that needed caulking, and everyone shouted at once about whose fault it was, and bailed her with buckets, and laughed until their ribs hurt.

Amara nailed the nameboard on at dusk. **KESTREL**, in black ironwood letters, straight and true. Below it, small, the pale driftwood plaque: *Rebuilt by the crew who stayed.*

"To-morrow," Ines announced, producing — from somewhere, from absolutely nowhere, from the same pocket dimension from which she'd produced her yacht and her schemes — a bottle of actual wine, "we discuss where she sails."

* * *

Tomas woke before dawn on the thirtieth day, out of habit, and went down to check the keel.

True. Still true.

But he wasn't alone on the beach. Felix was already there, standing at the waterline in the gray half-light, looking north along the coast where the sea ran out toward open water. He had something in his hand — the broken compass, Tomas realized. The brass case, cracked crystal, the needle that spun wild and pointed true. Felix carried it everywhere now, though it worked for nothing and navigated no one.

"Couldn't sleep either?" Tomas asked.

"Didn't try." Felix turned the compass over in his fingers. "I keep thinking about what comes next. We've got a boat, no debt, no cargo, no job. Priya wants to chase storms for the science institutes. Amara keeps drawing charts of places that need surveying. Ines—" he almost smiled, "—Ines has already drafted three business plans on bark, I swear to God. And I keep standing here wondering what I'm supposed to want now that wanting something won't kill anybody."

"And?"

Felix looked at him. In the growing light his face was open in a way it had never been back in those first days, when every expression was a locked door. "And I think I want to sail her. Today. Just out past the point and back. See if she remembers how."

Tomas clapped him on the shoulder, hard enough to stagger him, engineer-style affection. "Then wake the others. She's not going to remember alone."

They walked back up the beach together as the sun came over the ridge, and behind them the Kestrel rode her mooring lines in the shallows, patched and scarred and named, waiting the way boats wait — patiently, and always ready.
