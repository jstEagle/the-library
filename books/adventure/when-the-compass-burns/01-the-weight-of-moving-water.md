## 1. The Weight of Moving Water

The river was low enough to read its own history that morning.

Jonah Vasquez stood at the wheel of the *Marisol* with one bare foot hooked over a deck plank gone silver with age, watching the water peel back from sandbars that had no business showing themselves yet. Three weeks early, the dry season's bones were already exposed — ribs of pale stone down the channel's spine, drowned trees standing in the shallows like men waiting for a ferry that would never come. She'd run this stretch eleven times in nine years and it had never looked like this. It looked like a river holding its breath.

"Come on, old girl," she murmured, not to the boat but to the water, because the boat was hers and the water was nobody's, and you begged only the things that could refuse you. "Give me a hand here."

The *Marisol* answered the way she always did: grudgingly, then all at once. Forty years of patched steel and a diesel engine that sounded like a man clearing his throat before every important statement, and still she slid off the bar at Puerto Hondo with six inches to spare, her wake folding out behind her in long brown ribbons. Jonah felt the hull shudder free, felt the wheel go light in her hands, and allowed herself exactly three seconds of satisfaction before she started looking for the next thing that could kill them.

That was the job. Not the cargo, not the pay, not the long green miles of it. The job was the next thing. The sandbar ahead, the snag submerged just under the surface where the current made a smooth lie of itself, the fuel gauge that read optimistic on cold mornings. A river pilot who thought about anything other than the next thing ended up as a story other pilots told each other over cheap rum, usually with a laugh in it that didn't quite hold.

She ran the narrows below Cerro Blanco at half throttle, reading the water the way other people read faces. There was a language to it, and she spoke it without thinking anymore: the V of a current seam pointing downstream like an arrow saying *this way is deep*; the glassy tongue of water over a ledge that meant *not so fast*; the ugly boil where two currents argued, throwing up little hills of brown water that collapsed on themselves. Where the surface went quiet and oily, something waited underneath. The river lied constantly, but it lied in patterns, and if you learned the patterns you could hear what it actually meant under the words.

Her mother had taught her that. Or maybe her uncle — the memory had gone soft at the edges, worn like a coin passed through too many hands. Someone had stood behind her on a wheel deck smaller than this one, younger than this one, and said, *the water remembers everything, mija, that's why it never stops moving.* She'd been eight. Maybe ten. The person's face was gone. The sentence remained, lodged in her like a splinter the body grew around.

The water remembers everything. That was why she kept moving. If you never stopped anywhere long enough, nothing could accumulate around you — no debts, no names, no mornings when someone across the market square looked up and said your name in a voice that expected an answer. Motion was a kind of forgetting. She'd built a whole life on it, plank by plank, and the life floated, which was more than most lives managed.

Ahead, the channel bent east around a bluff the color of dried blood, and the wind changed. Jonah smelled it before she understood it — that green metallic edge underneath the mud-and-fish smell of low water. Rain somewhere upstream. Rain in the high country meant the monsoon was thinking about arriving early, and an early monsoon turned every sandbar into a roulette wheel and every tributary into a weapon. She made a note of it the way she made notes of everything: not on paper, never on paper, but in the ledger behind her eyes where she kept currents and debts and the few faces she permitted herself to remember.

*Never stop longer than the job requires.* Rule one. She had others — never carry passengers who ask questions, never take contracts that smell like politics, never sleep in the same bed twice in the same port — but rule one was the load-bearing wall. Everything else leaned against it.

The morning aged. She took the *Marisol* through the long straight below the bluff with the engine at cruising growl, and the country opened up around her — gallery forest leaning out over both banks until the canopy nearly met overhead, vines hanging down into the water like the river was being combed. A kingfisher detonated off a branch, electric blue, gone. Somewhere back in the green a howler monkey started up, a sound like the forest itself grieving, and was answered by another troop a kilometer downstream, grief passing hand to hand through the trees.

Jonah ate standing up, one hand on the wheel: cassava bread, dried fish, coffee from a thermos dented in a place and time she didn't think about. She chewed slowly and watched the banks for woodsmoke, because woodsmoke meant people, and people meant either customers or complications, and both cost time.

There was smoke. Thin, domestic, rising from a bend ahead where a fishing camp straggled along the bank — three stilt houses, nets drying, a dugout pulled up on the mud. As she came abeam, a woman waded out into the shallows and raised both arms over her head, palms out. The old signal. *Ride wanted.*

Jonah slowed against her own judgment. This was how it started, every time. You stopped for one woman with her arms in the air, and the stop became an hour, and the hour became a night moored at their dock, and the night became a meal someone insisted on cooking for you, and the meal became a name they used when they saw you coming next season, and a name was a hook, and a hook was a home, and a home was —

She cut the engine to idle anyway. Because rule one had a corollary she'd never written down, never said aloud, carved nowhere: *unless someone needs the boat.* She hated the corollary. It was the crack in the wall where the cold got in.

The woman was older, gray-braided, hip-deep now, holding up a child — a boy, maybe five, leg wrapped in filthy cloth, face the color of ash. Even at fifty meters Jonah could see the way he wasn't crying. Children cried when they had energy left. This one had spent it.

"Snake," the woman called, when the *Marisol*'s bow nudged close enough. "Three days ago."

Jonah looked at the leg, at the angry red lines crawling up past the knee like slow lightning, and did the arithmetic she'd done a hundred times in a hundred rivers. Nearest clinic: Puerto Hondo, twelve hours back the way she'd come. Next one upstream: San Feliz, nine hours forward, if the shallows held. Her contract was a cargo run to San Feliz anyway — palm oil and salt and machine parts, boring freight, boring money. A detour of zero kilometers. A delay of perhaps one day.

One day. Rule one flexed, groaned, held.

"Get him aboard," she said. "Careful on the ladder. And you — do you have anything of his with blood on it? Cloth, anything?"

The woman stared. "Why?"

"Because I need to know what bit him." Jonah was already moving, hauling her small medical kit from under the chart table, flipping it open. "Brown with a pale belly, or black with yellow bands? Think. It matters."

"Pale belly." The woman climbed aboard with the boy strapped to her back, moving with the economy of someone who had carried children through floodwater before. "It was in the net."

"Okay. Okay, that's survivable." Survivable. The word tasted strange in her mouth, hopeful, and she put it away quickly. "He needs the antivenom at San Feliz and he needs it today. Sit him by the engine — the warmth helps. Don't let him sleep flat."

And that was that. The *Marisol* turned her blunt bow upstream with a snakebit child in her belly and a grandmother on her deck, and Jonah Vasquez stood at the wheel and told herself firmly that this was not stopping. This was the job acquiring weight temporarily. Weight came off. Weight always came off. In four days she'd deliver the boy, deliver the cargo, collect her pay, and be forty kilometers gone before anyone in San Feliz learned her second name, which was Vasquez, which was itself borrowed, which was a longer story involving a fire and a town called Santa Rosa del Agua and a man named Aurelio whose face she had also, deliberately, ground away like a watermark.

Except that some nights the grinding didn't take.

*

The fire came back the way it always did — not summoned, just there, the way weather is there when you open a door.

She was three hours above the fishing camp, running the afternoon stretch with the boy sleeping fitfully against the warm engine housing and his grandmother dozing upright like a bird, and the river had gone golden and slow, and there was nothing to do with her hands, and that was the danger. Empty hands were an open door.

Santa Rosa del Agua had burned on a Tuesday. She knew it was a Tuesday because the market was full — that was the whole design of it, she understood later, though understanding had not helped anyone. The town sat where the Rio Verde met the mother river, and everything moved through Santa Rosa: timber concessions, gold dust, coca, cattle, contraband fuel, and information. Especially information. And Jonah — twenty-six, newly licensed, proud of her first command, in love in the stupid totalizing way of the young — had been seeing a man who worked the radio station. Aurelio. Beautiful man, patient hands, laughed from his chest. He used to bring her coffee at dawn on the dock and tell her she piloted like the river was listening to her.

He had been selling the movement logs. Every boat that cleared at Santa Rosa, every cargo manifest, every pilot's route and schedule — copied, coded, sold upstream to the people who moved product and removed obstacles. He'd been doing it for two years. He told her once, late, drunk on her deck, that he sometimes felt like the river's confessor, that everyone told the water their secrets and he just wrote them down. She'd laughed. She'd thought it was poetry.

Then the Verdemar Consortium lost a shipment and decided the leak was in Santa Rosa, and the consortium did not send investigators. It sent men with jerrycans and a timetable, and the timetable began at the market, midmorning, Tuesday, when the town was fullest, because the point was never really to find the leak. The point was to make sure every pilot, every smuggler, every fixer on two hundred kilometers of river understood that information had a price and carelessness had a bigger one.

Jonah had been upriver, twelve kilometers, taking on fuel. She saw the smoke from the fuel dock and stood very still while the world rearranged itself around the smoke, and then she was running to her boat, and then she was running the *Marisol* — her father's boat, the only inheritance she'd ever wanted — back downriver at full throttle with the fire growing in the windshield like a second sunrise.

She arrived in time to save no one. That was the sentence she carried. Not *she arrived too late*, which implied a race narrowly lost, but the flatter, heavier truth: she arrived in time to watch, from the water, which was the only vantage from which she had ever truly watched anything. The market was a torch. The church roof went up with a sound like tearing cloth. People were in the river — the river that remembered everything, receiving them, carrying some downstream and holding others under. She pulled seven people out of the water that day. She knew the number because she counted them for years afterward, at night, the way misers count coins. Seven. And the eighth had been within reach, a girl on a burning barge, fifteen meters off his bow, and there had been a crosscurrent and a burning raft drifting between them and Jonah had hesitated — one breath, one calculation about her hull, her paint, her planks — and the current took the barge sideways into the fire front and after that there was no girl, there was only steam.

One breath. One calculation. She had chosen the boat.

Aurelio she never found. Alive or dead, ash or fugitive, he simply ceased, and the not-knowing was its own kind of wound, a door that never closed all the way and let a draft through forever. Some nights she imagined finding him. Most nights she imagined not having loved him, which was harder work and less satisfying.

Afterward she had done the only thing her body knew how to do. She had cast off, and pointed the bow away, and kept the bow pointed away for nine years. New name on the registry in every new port. New rules. Never stop longer than the job requires. Never love a man who works near a radio. Never count the people you pull from the water, except that she always counted, always, the miser's habit, the splinter grown over.

Seven. Eight, if you counted the one she owed.

The river went on being golden. The boy coughed in his sleep and his grandmother adjusted the cloth on his leg without waking, and the *Marisol* pushed upstream through the long light, and Jonah realized her hands had gone white on the wheel and made herself loosen them, finger by finger, the way you'd calm a frightened animal.

*The water remembers everything.* Fine. Let it remember. Memory was not the same as debt. She could outrun a memory; she'd been doing it since she was twenty-six. What you couldn't outrun was a name spoken aloud in a voice that expected an answer, a dock where they waited for you, a grave you visited twice. So: no docks, no graves, no answers. Motion. The clean arithmetic of motion.

She almost believed it, most days. Today, watching the smoke of the fishing camp sink behind the bend, she believed it about sixty percent, which was as good as it got.

*

Dusk caught them in the wide marsh reach below San Feliz, where the river braided itself into a dozen uncertain channels and the air filled with birds coming down out of the sky like iron filings toward a magnet. Jonah picked the middle channel by the color of the water — tea-dark meant tannin and depth, milk-brown meant fresh sediment and shallow — and threaded the *Marisol* through with the last of the light, the boy's fever breathing against the engine room bulkhead, the grandmother praying quietly in a language Jonah half-recognized and did not ask about, because asking was a form of staying.

San Feliz announced itself the way river towns do: first a church bell heard across the water, then lights strung along a low bluff, then the smell — charcoal, diesel, frying plantain, sewage, rain-that-hasn't-fallen-yet. The town clung to the bluff above a working dock lit by sodium lamps that turned everything the color of weak beer. Jonah brought the *Marisol* alongside with the economy of long practice, lines out, fenders down, engine off, and the sudden silence rang in her ears like a struck glass.

The clinic was a two-room concrete building up the dock road. Jonah carried the boy himself — he weighed nothing, fever-light, his head rolling against her collarbone — while his grandmother walked beside her touching Jonah's elbow every few steps, not guiding, just touching, the way you touch a railing. At the clinic door a nurse took one look at the leg and shouted a name into the dark behind her, and there were hands, and a table, and a lamp, and Jonah stepped back out of the circle of light because that was where she belonged, outside circles of light.

The antivenom was there. The boy would limp and live. The grandmother found Jonah outside afterward and pressed something into her hand — a woven bracelet, cheap thread, the kind sold to tourists at a hundred docks — and said words in that half-known language, and then, in Spanish, with terrible directness: "You will come back. We will know your boat."

"No," Jonah said, gently, already stepping back toward the dark dock. "You won't. But he'll walk. That's the trade."

She went back aboard and did not sleep. She sat in the wheelhouse with the sodium light leaking through the windows and the bracelet coiled on the chart table like a small dead snake, and she listened to the town breathe around her, and she planned her departure for first light, before the market, before anyone could learn her silhouette. Cargo delivery at dawn, payment, cast off by six. Twelve hours in San Feliz, maximum. Rule one.

But the river had risen overnight — she could hear it, a deeper note under the dock pilings, a fuller voice — and when she checked the gauge at midnight the water had come up nine centimeters in six hours. Upstream rain, then. Early monsoon. The whole basin filling like a lung drawing breath.

At 5:40 in the morning, cargo delivered, payment collected in crumpled notes, she stood at the wheel with the engine idling below her and San Feliz still asleep in its beer-colored light, and she was thirty seconds from casting off when the fixer appeared on the dock.

She recognized the type before she recognized the man: the dry shoes, the dry smile, the way he stood on a wet dock as if the dock should apologize to him. He raised one hand — not waving, beckoning — and in the other hand he held a leather folder, and he said, in a voice pitched to carry exactly as far as her wheelhouse:

"Captain Vasquez. I'm told you're the only fool on this river who'll run the upper Verde in flood season. I have work, and it pays triple, and it's already late."

Jonah's hand rested on the throttle. Rule one stood in her chest like a wall, arms crossed, shaking its head.

Triple, said the folder. Late, said the man. And somewhere under both words, faint as a current seam, the oldest sound she knew: the river, calling her further upstream.

She cut the engine.

"Talk fast," she said.
