## 2. A Contract Written in Rain

The rain found Callao Verde before Jonah did.

She saw it coming from six kilometers out, a gray curtain dragging itself across the river like something being pulled by hand, and she had time — just enough time — to run the Marisol into the lee of the customs pier before the sky opened. The first drops hit the deck like thrown gravel. Then the world went white with water.

Jonah stood at the wheel under the canvas awning she'd rigged in a port two seasons back, watching the town dissolve. Callao Verde was the last real port on this stretch of the river, the place where the maps stopped pretending and started guessing. It was built on stilts and stubbornness: tin roofs rusted to the color of old blood, a market street that flooded twice a year and dried out smelling of fish and diesel, a church whose bell tower leaned like a drunk leaning on a friend. Beyond it, upstream, the country turned green and mean. The monsoon country. The part of the map where rivers braided into a hundred arguments and none of them were wrong.

She'd been here before. Three years ago, maybe four. She'd taken on fuel and flour and left before sundown, because that was the rule, and the rule had kept her alive longer than luck ever had.

*Never stop longer than the job requires.*

Her father had taught her the river, but not the rule. The rule was her own invention, hammered out over years of leaving places just ahead of whatever was coming for her — debts, men, memories, fire. A boat was only safe when it was moving. A woman was only invisible when no one could say where she'd be tomorrow. She had made a religion of departure, and like most religions it demanded everything and explained nothing.

The Marisol knocked gently against the pilings. Forty years old if she was a day, twenty-two meters of riveted steel and bad welds, a cargo deck built for freight and a wheelhouse built for one. Her engine was a marine diesel older than Jonah, temperamental as a mule in August, and Jonah loved her the way you love anything that has carried you through your worst decisions: completely, and without illusions.

"Three days," she told the boat quietly, the way she did. "Fuel, provisions, out. In and out."

The rain said nothing. Rain never did. That was the trouble with rain — it kept all its promises to itself.

* * *

By late afternoon the storm had settled in for the night, and the market street had become a canal. Jonah waded it anyway, because the chandlery wouldn't come to the dock and she didn't trust anyone else to provision the Marisol. Water found the seams of her boots within a hundred meters and stayed there, cold and patient. She bought rice and cooking oil, spare impeller blades, a drum of diesel hauled dripping down a ramp by two boys who charged her double and earned it. The whole time, the rain came down like the sky was settling an old score.

It was in the chandlery doorway, shaking water off her hat, that she first noticed the man watching her.

He was seated at the table by the window where the chandler's wife served coffee to whoever could pay, and he was not drinking his. He was a small man, neat in the way that money makes possible even in a drowned town — dry shoes, dry shirt, a leather folder squared on the table in front of him like a thing that had never been rained on in its life. He had the face of someone who had learned long ago that smiling cost less than meaning it.

Jonah knew the type. Every river had them. They sat in doorways in dry clothes while other people got wet, and they paid for things, and what they paid for always cost more than money.

She turned to leave by the other door.

"Captain Vasquez." His voice reached her across the room without effort, pitched low, carrying the way sound carries over water. "Five minutes. Then I'll bother someone else, and you'll spend the season wondering."

She should have walked out. She knew that the way you know a sandbar is there before you see the water change color above it — some instinct older than sight, reading the current of a room. Five minutes. Everyone who had ever cost her anything had started with five minutes.

But the season had been thin. Two runs canceled because of the shallows, a month laid up waiting for parts, and the Marisol's hull needed work below the waterline that she'd been pretending wasn't urgent since March. Triple pay, he'd said — no, he hadn't said it yet. But men like him never opened their mouths unless the number was worth opening your mouth for.

She crossed the room and sat down across from him, keeping her back to the wall, her hat still dripping onto the floorboards between them.

"You know my name," she said.

"I know boats," he said. "Yours is the only one on this stretch that could make the run I'm buying, and I know captains. Yours is the only one who might say yes." He slid the leather folder across the table but kept two fingers on it, unhurried. "Coffee?"

"I don't drink with men whose names I don't know."

"Mercado." He smiled, and it fit his face like a rented suit. "Everyone calls me Mercado. You'll notice I'm not pretending otherwise."

"What run?"

"Upstream." He said it simply, the way a doctor says the name of a disease. "Past the narrows. Past the mission reach. All the way up to the Sefu expedition camp."

Jonah felt something turn over in her chest, slow and heavy, like the Marisol's engine catching on a cold morning.

Everyone on the river knew about Dr. Imara Sefu. The archaeologist. The one who'd spent six years digging into the flooded ruins up in the monsoon country while every company and government office tried to pretend she wasn't finding what everyone feared she was finding. Sefu ran her expedition the way Jonah ran her boat — lean, quiet, answerable to no one. Twice a week, regular as tide, the expedition checked in on the relay band. Fishermen repeated it the way fishermen repeat everything: *the doctor is still up there, the doctor is still talking.*

Except, Mercado said, laying the folder open on the wet wood between them, she wasn't. Not for eleven days.

"Two missed check-ins," he said. "Scheduled resupply due three weeks ago. The university's people are wringing their hands. The company that insures the dig is asking questions I don't like the shape of." He tapped a manifest written in a careful hand: diesel, medical stores, radio spares, food for twenty. "I need it delivered. You're the delivery."

"The season's wrong," Jonah said. "Pre-monsoon, the upper reaches are a foot deep in channels that move every night. Ask me again in October."

"I'm asking now. Triple rate, plus a bonus on arrival." He named the number, and it was obscene. It was the kind of number that paid off the Marisol's hull twice over and left enough to vanish somewhere far away for a year. Maybe forever. "Half now, half on delivery."

Rain hammered the roof so hard the lamp flickered. Somewhere down the street, water poured off a awning in a sheet, and a dog barked at it, furious and futile.

Jonah looked at the manifest instead of the number. Numbers were bait; manifests were truth. Diesel, medicine, radio parts — ordinary. But at the bottom, in the same careful hand, a single line item that didn't belong: *one sealed crate, do not open, deliver intact.* No weight listed. No contents declared.

"What's in the crate?" she asked.

"Nothing that concerns you."

"That's usually what people say about things that concern me."

Mercado folded his hands. For a moment the smile fell away entirely, and underneath it was something tired and sharp, a man doing arithmetic he didn't enjoy. "Captain, I'll be honest with you, since honesty is cheaper than it used to be. There are people asking after that expedition who are not universities. Men with corporate credentials, moving upriver in boats better than yours. If they reach Dr. Sefu before her supplies do, what happens next won't be in any report. Your job is to get there first. That's all. You don't have to fight anyone. You just have to be faster than people who aren't as good as you."

"And if I'm not faster?"

"Then triple rate won't matter much to either of us, will it."

Outside, the rain eased for a moment, the way it does when it's only gathering breath, and in that moment Jonah heard the river — high, restless, muttering against the pilings like a crowd before a verdict. The water was already rising. She could feel it in the way the Marisol tugged at her lines, impatient, reading the current that hadn't arrived yet but was coming, coming down from hills she couldn't see, carrying everything downhill toward the sea including, eventually, her.

Every instinct she owned said *no*. The same instinct that had gotten her out of a burning town with second-degree burns on her hands and nothing else; the same instinct that had kept her moving for eleven years, port to port, never sleeping two nights in the same bed, never learning anyone's middle name. The instinct said: *triple pay means triple trouble, and trouble upriver in monsoon season isn't trouble, it's a grave with a view.*

But then there was the other voice. The quieter one. The one that sounded like the river itself, saying: *eleven days of silence. Twenty people. And men in better boats than yours already hunting them.*

Greed was simple. Greed she understood; greed was just hunger wearing a suit. It was curiosity that worried her — curiosity was how runners became stayers, how a five-minute conversation became a season, a year, a life. Curiosity was the crack in the rule.

"How much fuel can you put aboard tonight?" she asked.

Mercado's smile came back, and this time it almost reached his eyes. "As much as you can carry."

* * *

She signed nothing. That was the first condition, and Mercado took it with a shrug that told her he'd expected it — men like him preferred captains with no paper trail anyway. Half the money arrived an hour later in an oilskin packet, delivered by a boy who didn't speak and didn't wait. The diesel came by barge in the dark, pumped aboard in the rain, and the supplies followed in crates stenciled with a shipping mark Jonah didn't recognize: a stylized wave inside a broken circle.

She loaded until the Marisol sat low and sullen at the pier, then lower. By midnight the boat was heavy with a season's worth of everything, and the sealed crate rode amidships under a tarp, squat and silent as a coffin. She didn't look at it more than once. Looking at things was how they got into you.

She slept badly in the wheelhouse bunk, which was normal, and dreamed of fire, which was also normal. In the dream the town behind her burned the way it always burned — not quickly, not dramatically, but thoroughly, the way a thing burns when the people who set it have all night and no witnesses. In the dream she was steering away downstream with her hands blistered and the compass spinning, and the compass needle didn't point north anymore, it pointed back at the flames, and it was burning too, the glass gone black, the needle glowing like a wire in a fire—

She woke before dawn with salt on her lips that wasn't sweat, or maybe it was. The rain had stopped. Through the wheelhouse glass, Callao Verde lay steaming under a sky the color of a healing bruise, and the river had come up another half meter in the night. She could tell by the pilings. She could always tell by the pilings.

On the dock, someone was standing looking at the Marisol.

A stranger. Tall, rain-dark coat, a travel bag at his feet like a man who had already decided. He didn't board, didn't call out, didn't wave. He just stood there in the gray light, watching her boat with the particular stillness of a man reading a document he intends to contest — and when he saw her move behind the wheelhouse glass, he didn't look away.

He raised one hand. Not a wave. More like a bid for attention. A man about to ask a question he'd already decided the answer to.

Jonah stood in the wheelhouse with the oilskin packet heavy in her jacket pocket and the river rising under her keel, and she thought: *five minutes. It always starts with five minutes.*

She went out to hear it anyway.
