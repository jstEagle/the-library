## 3. The Stranger at the Dock

The rain in Callao Verde came down like a debt being collected.

Elias Wren stood under the rusted awning of the customs shed and watched it hammer the harbor into gray lace, and he thought: *she would have loved this.* Nadia had loved weather that made itself impossible to ignore. She'd once stood on a rooftop in some border town whose name Elias had never learned and shouted into a thunderhead until the thunder answered, laughing like a woman who had made peace with being small. That was before. Before the letters thinned to postcards, before the postcards stopped, before the radio check-ins from the Sefu expedition went silent like a held breath that never let go.

Eleven days of silence now. He'd counted them the way other men counted money.

He turned his collar up and crossed the street in long strides, because standing still was the one thing he could not afford. Standing still was where grief caught you. Standing still was where you started saying things out loud — *my sister is missing* — in a voice that cracked on *missing*, and men like the fixer who'd pointed him toward this dock smelled cracks the way sharks smelled blood.

The Marisol sat low in the water at the end of the pier, and even half-loaded she looked tired. That was the wrong first impression and Elias knew it immediately, because boats were like people: what looked tired was often just carrying more than it showed. She was maybe thirty meters of river workhorse, steel-hulled with a wheelhouse the color of old blood, her name painted on the bow in flaking white letters that someone had touched up by hand — the *o* slightly too round, done with a brush and patience rather than a stencil. Cargo netting lay coiled on deck like sleeping snakes. A smell came off her of diesel and wet rope and something faintly sweet underneath, resin maybe, or the ghost of some cargo she'd carried years ago and never quite surrendered.

A woman was aboard. He'd known that before the fixer told him — Jonah Vasquez, captain, the only pilot fool enough or hungry enough to take a contract upriver in the teeth of the season. He watched her work now, moving along the rail with a coil of line over one shoulder, and he catalogued her the way Nadia had taught him to catalogue terrain: not for beauty but for use. Strong hands, rope-scarred. A way of walking that kept her center of gravity low, as if the deck might betray her and she'd already made her arrangements for it. Dark hair cut short and practical, going gray at the temples early, the gray concentrated in one patch above her left ear like a streak of lightning frozen mid-strike. She didn't look up when he stepped onto the gangplank. He noted that too. People who didn't look up were either very confident or very practiced at not seeing things they didn't want to see.

"Captain Vasquez." His voice carried over the rain without him raising it much; he'd learned volume in boardrooms and interrogation rooms and had never fully unlearned it. "Elias Wren."

She finished her knot before she acknowledged him. That was its own kind of answer.

"I don't take passengers," she said, to the cleat rather than to him.

"I'm not a passenger. I'm cargo."

That got her eyes. They were brown, nearly black in the flat light, and they went over him with an efficiency that made him feel briefly inventoried — boots, coat, hands, the shape of his shoulders under the wet wool, the absence of any luggage heavier than a leather satchel. He watched her arrive at the obvious conclusion: a man traveling light was a man planning to travel fast.

"Cargo gets weighed," she said. "Cargo gets stowed. Cargo doesn't talk."

"Cargo also doesn't pay triple."

Something moved behind her face — not greed exactly, or not only greed. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman hearing a number she'd been braced against and finding it larger than her armor. "The contract's signed. Manifest's set. I leave before dawn."

"Then I'll stand here in the rain until dawn, and you can watch me do it through your wheelhouse glass, and we can both pretend that doesn't bother you." He took the envelope from inside his coat then — not dramatically, nothing theatrical, just a plain manila envelope gone soft and dark at the corners from the damp — and laid it on the winch housing between them. Rain spattered it immediately, ink-blotted the top edge. "Half now. Half when we find them."

"*When,*" she repeated.

"When." He held her gaze because looking away was how liars died, and he was lying, and he knew the difference between a lie that survived and one that drowned was almost entirely in the eyes. "My sister is on that expedition. Nadia Wren. Cartographer. She missed a check-in, and then another, and the company that hired her has stopped answering my letters. So yes, Captain. When."

It was true, all of it, which was the best kind of architecture for a lie — every load-bearing wall real, only the rooms behind them furnished wrong. Nadia Wren was his sister. Nadia Wren was the expedition's cartographer. Nadia Wren had gone silent eleven days ago. What he did not say was that he'd spent four of those days in the offices of men whose names he'd promised never to speak again, trading favors he'd sworn off years ago, pulling strings that hummed with a tension he recognized from the worst year of his life. What he did not say was that the fixer who'd arranged this passage had asked questions no grieving brother needed answered — *how much does she weigh, this sister of yours, what did she carry, who else knows about the survey* — and that Elias had lied to him fluently and hated himself for how easy it still was.

Jonah Vasquez picked up the envelope. Weighed it in her hand, literally, the way she'd said cargo got weighed. Her thumb moved across the bills inside without opening the flap, counting by thickness, and Elias thought: *she's done this before, taken money from strangers with hard stories.* There was a callus on her suspicion, smooth as river stone.

"This isn't a rescue boat," she said. "I'm running supplies. Rice, fuel, medical kits, radio parts. If your sister's alive, she eats better for a week. If she's not—" She stopped. Not out of kindness, he suspected, but because finishing the sentence cost something and she'd decided not to spend it. "If she's not, the supplies still go. Contract's a contract."

"I understand."

"You don't. Nobody does until the river teaches them." She tucked the envelope into her coat without ceremony, and he understood that the negotiation was over, that he'd been allowed aboard somewhere in the last ninety seconds without a word of welcome, and that the price of that admission was still being calculated. "Bunk's aft, below the galley. You get a hammock, a hook for your bag, and one rule."

"One rule."

"Don't touch anything that looks like it has a purpose. On this boat everything looks like it has a purpose."

"And if I need something?"

"Ask me." Her eyes came back to his and stayed there a beat longer than comfort allowed. "I'd rather be asked than surprised. Surprises on a river get people killed."

"Noted," Elias said, and meant it, and was already planning which rules to break.

* * *

He spent the afternoon learning the Marisol the way he'd once learned hostile offices: by appearing harmless and memorizing everything.

The boat rewarded attention. Below decks she was a labyrinth of good decisions — cargo lashed in tiers against the hull, spare parts labeled in a handwriting that slanted forward like it was in a hurry, a bilge pump rebuilt so many times its casing bore the bright scars of three different wrenches. Nothing matched. Everything worked. It was a vessel assembled by someone who expected to be abandoned by the world and had arranged her dependencies accordingly. In the galley there was a single photograph taped inside a cabinet door, curling with steam-damp: a younger woman, unmistakably Jonah, arm around an older man with a river pilot's forearms and a laugh lines carved deep, both of them squinting into sun on the deck of some boat older and prouder than this one. Elias looked at it for exactly as long as it took to memorize the man's face, then closed the cabinet.

Up top, the wheelhouse smelled of engine oil and coffee grounds and, beneath it, something medicinal — antiseptic, maybe, or the camphor people rubbed into old injuries. Charts were rolled in a rack by the window, and though every instinct told him not to, he drifted toward them the way water drifts downhill.

"They're mine," Jonah said from the doorway, and he hadn't heard her come up the stairs, which frightened him more than he showed. "You want to see one, ask."

"The lower basin," he said, gesturing, keeping his voice level. "I've been studying the route. My sister's letters—"

"Mm." She crossed to the rack, pulled a chart, and spread it on the table with two hands, weighting the corners with a coffee tin and a brass ashtray that held no ash, only a single dried river seed of some kind, pale as a fingernail. The chart showed the reach above Callao Verde: the braided shallows, the narrows, the long lonely stretch where the mapmaker had given up on detail and written, in faded ink, simply *flood country*. "This is where you think your sister is."

"It's where the expedition was headed."

"Expeditions are headed places. Rivers decide where they arrive." Her finger traced the channel marks, and Elias watched her read the water off the paper the way other people read faces — the sandbars marked in pencil, dozens of annotations in that same forward-slanting hand, dates beside them, the pencil notes layered over years like sediment. *She remaps this river every season,* he realized. *She knows it changes and she refuses to trust yesterday's knowledge.* Something about that struck him as both admirable and terribly sad: a woman married to a landscape that divorced her annually.

"Nadia would've liked these charts," he said, and it wasn't even strategy. It slipped out of him with real feeling, and he saw it land — saw Jonah Vasquez glance up sharply, reassessing, recalibrating whatever arithmetic she was running on him.

"She a careful mapper?"

"The most careful person I've ever known. She used to say a bad map was a lie with lines around it."

For a moment — a moment only — something in the captain's face loosened. Then it tightened again, deliberately, like a hand closing. "Supper's at six. You eat what we eat. And Wren—" she waited until he met her eyes, "—the manifest lists nine crates of rice. If tomorrow morning there are ten, or eight, I'll know. I count everything on this boat. Everything."

"I wouldn't dream of it."

"No," she agreed mildly. "You'd dream of something else. That's why I'm watching you."

* * *

Night came down on Callao Verde in sheets, the rain thickening until the harbor lights dissolved into smears and the whole town sounded like it was being rinsed. Elias sat in the galley after supper — beans, rice, coffee strong enough to stand a spoon in — across from a woman who ate steadily and spoke barely at all, and he ran the conversation like a man defusing something.

He gave her pieces of Nadia. True pieces, chosen ones. The rooftop in the thunderstorm. The childhood spent redrawing maps of places that didn't exist, continents invented and ruled by elaborate laws. The scholarship, the fieldwork, the letters home that read like dispatches from a better world. He watched Jonah listen, and he watched her listen *selectively* — head tilted, eyes steady, giving nothing back — and he understood that she was doing to him precisely what he was doing to her: assembling a person out of curated fragments, testing each piece for weight.

"You said the company stopped answering your letters," she said at last. "Which company?"

"Meridian Holdings." The name came out smooth. Too smooth. He heard it land wrong even as he said it, heard the polish on it, and saw her notice the polish.

"Meridian," she repeated, tasting it. "They fund a lot of surveys."

"They fund a lot of things."

"Do you know what your sister was mapping?"

"River channels. Flood extents. Standard hydrographic work." Each word placed like a stone in a crossing, tested before weight committed. "Why? Does it matter?"

"Everything matters on a river." She rose, collected the plates, and paused at the cabinet with the photograph inside — he saw her hand rest on the door of it, lightly, unconsciously, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. "Get some sleep, Wren. We cast off at four. If you're not on deck by five past, I leave without you, and I keep your money."

"That seems fair."

"Nothing about this is fair." She said it without heat, almost gently, and climbed the stairs into the dark, and Elias sat alone in the lamplight listening to the rain and understanding that he had just been warned by a woman who never threatened anyone twice.

He checked his satchel one last time before turning in. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth beneath his spare shirt: the telegram copy, the relay station frequencies, the name of the man at Meridian who had signed the expedition's funding authorization — a name Elias had last seen on a different document, seven years ago, in a different life, attached to a different disappearance that had taught him everything he knew about how companies buried people. And beneath that, folded into quarters, a page torn from one of Nadia's own letters, the ink feathered with damp:

*They've found something in the flood country, Eli. Something the maps forgot on purpose. I'll write more when I'm sure of what I'm holding.*

He had shown that page to no one. Not the fixer, not the consulate clerk, not the woman upstairs whose boat he'd just bought passage on with money that was, in a sense, the enemy's money. Because the fixer had asked his questions with too much interest, and the consulate clerk had written down too much, and Jonah Vasquez — Jonah Vasquez watched him the way you watch a current you haven't decided whether to swim.

Above him, through the deck plating, he heard her footsteps cross the wheelhouse, unhurried, thorough, checking. Then silence, except the rain, except the river muttering against the hull in the dark like something patient.

Elias lay in the hammock with his hands behind his head and did not sleep. He listened to the Marisol breathe — the tick of cooling metal, the groan of mooring lines, the small industrious sounds of water finding its way into every joint of an old boat — and he rehearsed, yet again, the story of the grieving brother, polishing the true parts, sanding the false ones.

At some hour past midnight, the footsteps overhead stopped directly above his bunk and stayed there. Long enough to be deliberate. Long enough to say: *I know you're awake. I know you know I'm awake.*

Then they moved on.

Dawn was still three hours away. The river was rising. And somewhere upstream, in flood country the maps had forgotten on purpose, his sister had held something in her hands that powerful men wanted badly enough to stop writing letters about — and Elias Wren had just handed himself, lies and all, to the only pilot mad enough to carry him toward it.
