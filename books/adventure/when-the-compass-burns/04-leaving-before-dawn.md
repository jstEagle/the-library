## 4. Leaving Before Dawn

The rain came before the light did.

Jonah felt it first as a change in the air pressure, a softening behind her ears like the river clearing its throat, and then as a smell — wet stone and green rot and something almost sweet riding down from the high country where the storm was being born. She stood on the Marisol's aft deck with a mug of coffee going cold in her hand and watched the clouds assemble themselves over the western ridgeline, black and organized, stacking up like cargo that meant to be delivered whether anyone signed for it or not.

Three weeks early. Maybe four. The monsoon wasn't supposed to open for another month, but the river had been lying to her all week — running cloudy when it should have been clear, pushing hard at hours when it should have slackened, carrying debris from tributaries that shouldn't have had water to spare. Rivers lied the way people did: not with words but with details slightly wrong. You had to know which details to watch.

She drank the coffee anyway, bitter and lukewarm, and went forward to cast off.

Callao Verde slept behind her in the gray half-light — a straggle of tin roofs and drying nets and one street of cantinas that never fully closed, all of it slumped against the bank like a drunk leaning on a fence post. She'd been in port eleven days. Too long. The itch had started around day seven, that familiar crawling under her skin that said *go, go now*, and she'd silenced it with engine work and supply lists and the particular discipline of pretending she wasn't counting. Eleven days was nothing. She'd once spent six weeks in a town called Puerto Almendras waiting on a propeller shaft, and she'd left that place so smoothly that the harbormaster only realized she was gone when he came to collect fees and found an empty slip and a paid-up receipt tucked under his door.

That was the trick of leaving done right: no wake behind you. No loose threads. Nobody who could tell a story about the last time they saw you, because there was no last time — just a Tuesday, and then you weren't in it anymore.

She worked the bow lines by feel, coiling and stowing without looking, and told herself the itch was just weather coming. Just the season. Nothing to do with the man sleeping in her wheelhouse bunk because he'd paid too much cash and asked too few questions.

* * *

Elias Wren turned out to be an early riser, which surprised her. Most passengers — and that's what he was, whatever else he was, a passenger with a story about a sister — most passengers slept like ballast until the sun dragged them up. But when she came back through the wheelhouse door at quarter past five, he was already awake, sitting on the edge of the bunk with his long frame folded up like a folding rule, watching the storm light through the glass.

"You didn't tell me we were casting off before dawn," he said. His voice was rough with sleep but his eyes weren't sleepy at all.

"I didn't tell you anything," Jonah said. "You bought a ride, not an itinerary." She hung her oilskin on its peg and took the wheel. "Storm's building west. We want the narrows behind us before it breaks, and I want the confluence before noon. After that the current turns mean and we'll be fighting it for every kilometer."

"The confluence at San Roque," Elias said. "Where the Verde meets the main channel."

It was a small thing. A nothing thing. Any map showed it; any passenger with a chart could have named it. But he hadn't looked at a chart. She'd seen him come aboard with one duffel bag and a leather folder he kept close as a wallet, and neither had been opened since. He'd said it the way a man says his own address — automatic, certain, the knowledge sitting somewhere deeper than paper.

"San Roque," she agreed, and let it go. For now.

He climbed the ladder to stand beside her at the wheel, keeping politely to his side of the space, hands folded. Up close in the gray light he looked worse than he had at the dock — hollowed under the eyes, the kind of tired that sleep didn't touch. Grief, probably. Or guilt. In her experience those two wore the same face more often than not.

"How long have you piloted this river?" he asked.

"Longer than you've been grieving," she said, and immediately regretted it, because it was cruel in a way that invited conversation rather than ending it. He didn't take offense. He just nodded slowly, like she'd confirmed something he'd already written down.

"My father ran barges," he offered after a while. "Inland coal water, nothing like this. I grew up on decks."

"Mm."

"I know enough to stay out of the way."

"That's the first sensible thing you've said since the dock."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, more the memory of one. Then it was gone, and he was looking out at the water again, and Jonah turned her attention to the channel because the channel deserved it and because looking at him made the itch under her skin worse.

* * *

The Marisol dropped down through the last of Callao Verde's moorings and swung her nose upstream, and the town let go of them the way towns always let go of Jonah — easily, indifferently, without a single face at the rail to mark her going. A dog barked twice on some porch and stopped. A fisherman raised a hand without raising his eyes. By the time they cleared the fuel dock, Callao Verde had already forgotten her, and she felt the old comfort of that settle over her like the oilskin she'd hung up: heavy, waterproof, familiar.

Then the river took over, and there was no comfort in it at all.

The pre-monsoon shallows had chewed the lower channel into confusion — sandbars migrating overnight, the marked channel shifted twenty meters east of where the charts swore it lay, brown water sliding over bars in long silk sheets that hid exactly how little depth lay beneath. Jonah read the surface the way other people read print: the darker water was deep, the paler water was lying, the ripple patterns told you where the current stacked up against a bar, and the smell — God, the smell, mud and crushed vegetation and the faint mineral tang of silt turned fresh from the bottom — the smell told you things the eye missed entirely.

She worked the wheel in small corrections, feeling the Marisol answer through her palms, through the soles of her boots, through the deck plates humming against her shins. Forty years old if she was a day, that boat, and honest about everything: every degree of helm, every change in load, every lie the river told. Jonah trusted her more than she'd ever trusted a person, which was a low bar, but a bar nonetheless.

Elias stood beside her and watched. That was fine. Passengers watched. What wasn't fine — what began to collect in her attention like sediment — was *how* he watched. Not the wide-eyed tourist stare, tracking scenery. He watched the water the way she watched it. His eyes went to the ripple line off the point ahead before hers did. When a floating log swung sideways in the current — a dead giveaway of crossflow, of a bar building where none should be — his head turned a half-second before she'd have expected a stranger's to turn.

Half a second. She filed it away.

* * *

An hour out, with Callao Verde gone below the horizon bend and the storm front still grumbling its slow approach across the western sky, Jonah decided to conduct a small experiment. She told herself it was boredom. She told herself it was due diligence — any captain had a right to know what kind of cargo walked around on her deck. Both statements were true, and both were also cover stories, and she knew it, and she ran the experiment anyway.

"Your sister," she said, conversational, eyes on the channel. "Nadia. She was the expedition's cartographer, you said."

"Yes." The word came out level. Careful.

"Cartographer for a scientific outfit. Hydrographic survey, mostly?" She'd gotten that much from the fixer's file, such as it was. "Mapping channels?"

"Tributaries. Yes." A pause. "And some older work. Historical geography. She liked finding where rivers used to run. Where they'd been redirected, buried, built over." Another pause, longer. "She used to say a river's memory is longer than a map's."

"Hm." Jonah eased the wheel over as the channel bent north around a wooded point, the current grabbing the Marisol's stern and swinging it wide. "So she'd have known this stretch. The route your expedition took going up."

"She drew the route maps. All of them."

"Then you'd have seen them."

"I studied them." He said it simply. "After she stopped writing. It was — " He stopped. Started again. "When someone stops answering, you look at the last thing they left you. Her letters dried up in March. So I read her maps instead. Every contour line. Every sounding."

It was a good answer. It was a *very* good answer — specific, emotionally plausible, the kind of detail a grieving brother would produce naturally. And that was exactly what bothered Jonah, though it took her another few minutes of steering to articulate why even to herself.

Because grief didn't organize information like that. Grief rambled. Grief circled back and contradicted itself and broke off mid-sentence. This man's answers came out pre-assembled, load-bearing, each one placed precisely where it needed to be. He wasn't remembering his sister's maps. He was *deploying* them.

"All right," she said. "Test me on something, then. Since you've got her charts in your head." She nodded downstream at a gap in the eastern bank where a side-channel split off toward a wall of cane. "That cutoff there. Navigable?"

Elias followed her nod. He didn't answer right away, and she watched him not-answer — watched him do the arithmetic of what a brother would know versus what he knew, and she saw the moment the arithmetic failed him, because the truth was simply faster than the performance.

"Not at this stage," he said. "Not with the water this low. There's a rock shelf two hundred meters in — Nadia called it the Teeth. It drowns at flood stage and becomes the better channel, actually. In high water the main route silts up and the Teeth are the only way through." He glanced at her. "Why? Are we taking it?"

"No," Jonah said. "We're not taking it now, and we're sure as hell not taking it at flood stage, because at flood stage that whole cane brake is full of snags and the current through the Teeth runs nine knots and eats boats." She let the silence sit for three full seconds, the engine thudding beneath them, the storm-light dimming another shade. "But it's interesting that you know the name of a rock shelf two hundred meters inside a channel your sister's expedition would have passed in about four minutes at cruising speed."

Elias didn't flinch. She gave him credit for that. Most men, caught, would flinch — would laugh, or get angry, or produce some new improvisation. He just looked at her with those hollowed-out eyes and said, quietly:

"You think I'm lying to you."

"I think you're telling me true things in a false order," Jonah said. "Which is worse. Lies are cheap. Ordering costs effort." She adjusted the wheel a degree, more for something to do with her hands than anything. "A grieving brother studies maps because he misses her. Fine. But a grieving brother doesn't know flood-stage hydrology of a cutoff he's never run. That's not mourning. That's briefing."

The river ran on. Somewhere behind them, thunder cleared its throat again, closer now, and the first fat drops hit the wheelhouse roof like thrown gravel.

"What do you want from me, Captain?" Elias asked. No anger. Almost gentle. Which was its own kind of alarming.

"The truth would be a novelty," Jonah said. "Failing that, I'll take consistency. Pick a story and hold it. Because here's what I know about men who board my boat with cash and rehearsed answers." She finally turned and looked at him straight on, and let him see everything the river had taught her face to hold. "They're either running from something or hunting something. And either way, the boat pays for it."

* * *

The rain arrived in earnest ten minutes later, and it ended the conversation the way weather ends conversations — absolutely, without appeal. It came down in ropes, then in sheets, hammering the canopy of the riverbank flat, turning the world beyond thirty meters into gray suggestion. Jonah throttled back, ran the wipers uselessly against water arriving faster than rubber could move it, and navigated by memory and sound: the different drumming of rain on water versus rain on sandbar, the echo-change of the banks narrowing, the deep-water smell strengthening as the channel deepened.

Elias didn't retreat below. He stayed at her shoulder, silent, and after a while — without being asked — he started calling what he heard.

"Cane on the right. Close." 

She'd already heard it. But she noted that he'd heard it too, and that he'd said *right* correctly, facing forward, in the dark, in the noise.

"Foam line ahead, crossing. Bar?"

"Bar," she agreed. "Shifting. We'll swing wide."

They ran the storm like that for two hours, two people passing information in clipped syllables, and it should have been nothing — it was nothing, it was routine, it was the oldest rhythm on the river — and that was precisely what unsettled her. Because it fit. He fit. The way a key fits a lock it was cut for.

* * *

The rain softened to a steady gray drizzle near midday, and the confluence at San Roque opened before them like a held breath released: the green-brown water of the Verde surrendering to the vast, muscular, coffee-dark main channel, the two currents braiding together in a mile-wide turbulence of whirls and boils that marked where the big river's power began. From here upstream, everything would be fought for. Every kilometer purchased with fuel and strain and luck.

Jonah brought the Marisol around the last marker and set her course against the current, and the old boat leaned into it and dug, and the wheel came alive in her hands with the heavy trembling pull of a hull doing honest work.

Elias had gone below to make food — rice and tinned fish, he'd insisted, his turn, and she hadn't argued — and she stood alone at the wheel with the drizzle beading on the glass and let herself think clearly for the first time since the dock.

Here is what she knew. The man had boarded claiming a missing sister, and his grief was real — she believed that much; grief couldn't be faked in the voice, not the small cracks in it, not the way he'd said *she used to say*. But his knowledge was wrong for a brother. He knew the expedition's route the way the expedition's *pilot* would know it. Flood stages. Rock shelves by nickname. Current behavior at the confluence. He knew things that lived only in the body of someone who had run that water, not in the eyes of someone who had read about it.

And there was one more thing, the thing that had been sitting in her chest since dawn like swallowed stone.

The fixer's contract had listed the expedition's manifest. Supplies, instruments, mail. Jonah had read it twice on the first night, the way she read everything twice. And the manifest had said the Sefu expedition had gone upriver in March, standard dry-season ascent, the normal route.

But Nadia Wren's letters — according to her brother — had gone silent in March. Before the ascent was complete. And yet Elias talked about the route past the confluence, the upper reaches, the far water, with the intimacy of a man who'd steered it himself.

Either the expedition had gone farther than its manifest admitted.

Or her brother had.

The Marisol thudded onward against the current, and the storm clouds regrouped over the western ridges, and fifty kilometers upstream, in country where the radio had gone quiet eleven days ago, something waited that both of them were lying their way toward.

Jonah gripped the wheel and kept the boat moving, and told herself — the way she always told herself — that she could still turn around. That the river ran both ways. That leaving was always available, right up until the moment it wasn't.

She didn't believe it anymore. She'd known she wouldn't by the time she cast off. Some part of her had known at the dock, when she'd taken his money.

Behind her, below decks, Elias Wren was cooking rice and tinned fish and keeping his lies warm, and ahead the river rose dark and patient, carrying toward them everything it remembered.
