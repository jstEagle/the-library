## 5. Sandbar Country

The river lied to Jonah Vasquez the way old friends lie — gently, out of habit, with no malice in it at all.

She'd been reading water since she was nine years old, since her father put her hands on the wheel of a boat not much bigger than a coffin and told her the current would tell her everything if she learned its grammar. Brown water meant silt and shallow lies. Green water meant depth but cold feet underneath. A smooth stretch of surface could be six fathoms or six inches; the river didn't distinguish between calm and treachery unless you asked it properly, with your eyes on the ripples and your ears open to the sound the water made over whatever it was trying to hide.

By the third morning upstream, the Marisol was asking questions Jonah didn't like answering.

The pre-monsoon shallows had come early this year. She could feel it in the way the engine labored at RPMs that should have sung, in the brown ribbon of the channel narrowing from a quarter mile to a stone's throw, in the sandbars that had surfaced overnight like the backs of sleeping animals. The charts in her wheelhouse were three years old and might as well have been three centuries. Rivers this far into the basin redrew themselves every season, cutting new channels through the soft earth, abandoning old ones without ceremony, piling sand where last year's pilots had trusted their drafts to the keel.

"Should I be worried," Elias Wren said from the doorway behind her, "that you've been staring at that same bend for ten minutes?"

"You should be worried that I'm going to answer you."

He had a way of standing in doorways that she was beginning to resent — not blocking them, exactly, just occupying them, as though he'd been issued the space by some authority she hadn't met. He held two cups of coffee, which was either an apology or an interrogation technique. She took the cup because it was coffee, not because it was peace.

"The chart says eleven feet through here," he said, nodding at the bend ahead, where the river swung wide around a point thick with bamboo.

"The chart is a rumor." She drank. "Charts are rumors people wrote down before the river changed its mind. Out here the only honest document is the water itself, and even that one edits itself nightly."

Elias leaned against the doorframe and watched the bend come toward them. "My sister used to say something like that. About maps being arguments, not answers."

Jonah kept her eyes forward. "Your sister sounds like she knew rivers."

"She knew paper." Something moved across his face, quick as a fish under the surface. "Paper about rivers."

* * *

The bar took them forty minutes past noon, and it took them honestly, without warning, the way the worst things do.

One moment the Marisol was sliding through what looked like deep green water along the inside of the bend, and the next there was a shudder that started in the keel and climbed the whole length of the hull like a hand dragging up a spine. The engine note dropped. The deck tilted. And then they were still — truly still, in a way boats are never supposed to be still — with the current pushing hard against their port side and the propeller churning brown froth behind them.

Jonah killed the engine before it could tear itself apart on the sand beneath them. In the sudden quiet she could hear the river moving past, patient and enormous, already beginning to pile more sand against their hull.

"Well," Elias said.

"Don't." She was already moving, pulling on her boots, grabbing the sounding pole from its brackets by the wheelhouse door. "Don't say anything encouraging. Encouragement is for after."

She went down the ladder to the main deck and over the side into water that came to her thighs, warm as bathwater and opaque as tea. The pole went down ahead of her: two feet, eighteen inches, fourteen inches. She waded the line of the hull, reading the bottom through her boots as much as through the pole — soft sand here, packed clay there, a ridge of gravel off the bow that explained everything. They hadn't hit a bar so much as the bar had grown up to meet them overnight, a shifting tongue of sediment laid down by yesterday's current, invisible from the wheelhouse, indistinguishable from deep water until it wasn't.

"How bad?" Elias stood at the rail, and to his credit he'd already found the spare line and was coiling it.

"We're on a fresh bar. Maybe sixty tons of us sitting on sand that wasn't here Tuesday." She planted the pole and leaned on it, studying the water downstream, where the channel curled away between two gravel points. "The good news is we're light. We burned most of the fuel coming up. The bad news is the current's going to keep building this bar against our hull every hour we sit here. By tomorrow morning we won't be aground. We'll be part of the landscape."

"What do you need?"

That was the question, wasn't it. What did she need. She looked at him — this stranger with his too-good river knowledge and his grief worn like a coat that almost fit — and she made the calculation she always made, the one that said carry your own weight, trust no one's hands but yours, because other people's hands let go.

But the math didn't work today. Sixty tons against one pair of arms didn't work.

"I need the anchor carried out astern," she said. "Fifty meters, maybe more, straight down-channel. There's a kedge in the lazarette. Can you handle a dinghy?"

"I can handle a boat."

"Everyone can handle a boat until the moment they can't." She hauled herself back over the rail, water sheeting off her, and pointed at the small inflatable lashed to the aft deck. "Take the kedge, take the line, row fifty meters downstream, drop it where I show you, and get back here without drowning. If you drown, I'm leaving you for the catfish and telling everyone you died heroically."

"That's generous."

"It's the best offer you'll get all day."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and somewhere in the middle of it — she couldn't have said when — the suspicion between them wore thin enough to see through, like fog burning off a morning.

It was mechanical work, unglamorous and total. Jonah ran the engine in short bursts while Elias hauled on the kedge line from the stern winch, each pull walking the Marisol a few inches backward off the bar, each burst of prop wash digging sand away from the hull and throwing it downstream. Then the engine would stop, the line would go taut again, and Elias would lean back into the harness with his boots braced against the rail and haul until the rope sang. Jonah worked the bow, driving the spud poles into the bottom and levering them to walk the nose sideways, feeling through the steel shafts the exact shape of the sand holding them — where it gripped, where it was starting to give, where the current had scoured a pocket of deeper water they could fall into if they earned it.

Sweat, river water, diesel, the smell of wet rope. The sun came down through high haze and turned everything the color of old brass. Herons stalked the gravel points, indifferent to their struggle. Once, far off, a troop of howlers started up in the flooded forest and quit again, as if the day were too hot even for outrage.

"Again," Jonah called, and Elias hauled, and the boat groaned and slid four inches, and they both stood breathing for a moment, and then: "Again."

Around the fifth hour, he stopped fighting the rhythm and found it. She felt the change from thirty feet away — the difference between a man pulling a rope and a man pulling *with* something, timing his effort to the boat's give instead of against it. It was the same thing she'd seen in good deckhands, good dancers, good lovers: the ones who understood that strength was mostly listening.

"You've done this before," she said, when he paused to rewrap his palms. His hands were torn raw in two places, and he hadn't mentioned it.

"Done what? Grounding boats?"

"Working water. Real working, not sailing-school working." She tossed him a rag and the tin of tar salve she kept for her own hands. "You read current better than half the licensed pilots I've run with. You knew what a kedge was before I finished saying it. Grieving brothers don't usually know that."

Elias wrapped his palms in silence for a long moment. Downstream, the water slid past the gravel point with a sound like slow applause.

"My father ran cargo," he said finally. "Not here. Farther south, smaller river, different country, same water in the end. Summers, I crewed for him. Nadia stayed ashore with the maps." He looked up, and his face did the thing it did when he was deciding how much truth to spend. "I haven't been on a river in eleven years. But it turns out the body remembers."

"The body remembers," Jonah agreed, and thought: *and so do liars.* Because that was the thing about him — everything he said checked out individually, and together they added up to a man who was slightly more than he claimed, the way a forged bill is slightly heavier than real currency. She couldn't prove it. She could feel it.

But he'd hauled for five hours without complaint, and his blisters were real, and the kedge line was real, and the boat was four inches freer than she'd been at noon.

"Again," she said.

* * *

The Marisol came off the bar at 6:40 in the evening, with the light going amber and the river turning to hammered copper, and the sound of her own keel floating free was so sweet that Jonah had to turn away for a second so Elias wouldn't see her face do whatever it did.

They anchored that night in the lee of the gravel point, in eight feet of water over clean sand, and Jonah declared the day's accounts open. That was when the arithmetic got ugly.

The grounding had cost them more than daylight. When she went below to check the tanks, she found the freshwater situation worse than she'd estimated — the forward tank had been seeping since Callao Verde, a slow weep at a seam she'd patched twice, and the jolt on the bar had opened it further. She dipped the tank with a marked stick and did the numbers twice, hoping the stick was wrong.

It wasn't.

"How much is left?" Elias sat at the galley table with his bandaged hands flat on the wood, watching her face the way he watched everything — openly, hungrily, like a man memorizing a room he expected to lose.

"Two days' worth if we're careful. Three if we're desperate and stupid." She set the dipstick down. "There's a spring I know, up past the narrows, on the east bank. Good water, fast flow. But the narrows are a full day's run, and after the narrows the channel gets serious."

"Serious how?"

"Serious enough that I wanted fresh crew and a dry season, and I have neither." She filled the kettle from the last of the drinking jug — no sense wasting tank water on coffee — and lit the burner. "So tomorrow we make distance. No more stops unless the river forces them. We run till dark, anchor, run at first light."

"And the water?"

"We boil river water tonight and hope my patch holds. And we make the spring." She looked at him across the little galley, lamplight and steam between them, and heard herself say the thing she'd decided not to say: "I need you hauling lines again tomorrow. Whatever you're carrying — whoever you actually are, whatever you didn't tell me at the dock — leave it topside. Down here I need hands, not history."

Something flickered in his expression — hurt, or respect, or the particular exhaustion of a man whose disguises fit worse every day. "And if I said the same to you?"

The kettle began to sing. Jonah poured the water slowly, giving herself the time.

"Then we'd both be working with empty hands," she said. "Lucky for us, mine are only half empty."

He almost smiled. It was the first time she'd seen anything on his face that wasn't grief or calculation, and it changed him completely, the way a single degree of current change changes a whole reach of river. For a moment he looked young, and tired, and like someone's brother.

"Get some sleep," she said. "First watch is mine."

* * *

She took the watch alone on the aft deck, anchored in the dark with the current murmuring against the hull, and let herself think the thoughts she kept locked up during daylight.

The bar had scared her more than she'd shown. Not the grounding itself — groundings happened, she'd taken the Marisol off a dozen bars in a dozen seasons, and the river gave back what it took if you asked correctly. What scared her was the hour she'd spent in the water with her hand on the hull, feeling sixty tons of her life resting on sand that hadn't existed three days ago. Everything she owned floated on that hull. Every escape route, every exit, every version of herself that ended with *and then she left* — all of it sat on a foundation the river could build or erase overnight, and had, and would again.

A boat was just a promise you made to yourself that you could always go. And promises like that needed constant maintenance, or the river collected them.

Across the water, the flooded forest stood black against the last violet of the sky. Somewhere upstream — two days, maybe three, if the narrows allowed — Dr. Imara Sefu and her missing expedition were silent into their eleventh day, and men with corporate credentials were asking after them in towns that were starting to burn. Jonah had seen the smoke once already, years ago, from the deck of a different boat, running downstream with the firelight behind her and a name she couldn't use anymore. She knew what corporate credentials looked like when they finished asking their questions.

Below decks, Elias Wren slept with his blistered hands wrapped in her salve and her rags, dreaming whatever liars dreamed.

She should put him ashore at the narrows. She knew that too. Every instinct she owned, every rule she'd built her moving life on, said it plainly: the man was a complication, complications were cargo she didn't carry, and the narrows would have a landing, and landings had roads, and roads led away.

Instead she found herself thinking about the way he'd hauled — that shift, mid-afternoon, from pulling against the boat to pulling with it. The body remembers, he'd said. She looked at her own hands, cracked and tar-stained, and wondered what hers remembered, and whether remembering was the same as being unable to forget.

The river slid past in the dark, editing itself.

Tomorrow, the narrows. Tomorrow, the spring, and the serious water beyond it, and whatever waited upstream where the radio had gone quiet. She finished her coffee, checked the anchor line twice, and went below to sleep — but she lay awake a long time, listening to the current, and it sounded less like applause now and more like a question the river had been asking her for years, patiently, in its slow brown grammar, waiting for an answer she still refused to give.
