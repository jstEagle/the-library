## 6. Radio Silence

The relay station announced itself before we saw it: a scar of cleared land on the east bank where the jungle had been cut back and burned, and above the stumps a lattice tower leaning like a drunk against the sky. Its guy-wires sang in the wind coming off the water — three thin notes, high and mournful, the sound of something strung too tight. I heard them for a full kilometer before the station itself resolved out of the heat haze: a long low building of corrugated tin gone rust-brown, a rain barrel, a generator shed, and a dog chained to a post who stood up when he smelled us and did not bark.

Jonah cut the Marisol's engine early and let us drift in on momentum, which told me two things about her. First, that she'd been here before — you don't coast into a strange dock unless you already know where the pilings are under the brown water. Second, that she didn't want to be heard arriving. I filed both facts away with all the others I'd been collecting since Callao Verde, the way a man collects stones to throw later.

"You'll want to stay aboard," she said, not looking at me. Her hands moved over the wheel and throttle with the absent precision of someone scratching an old itch. "Relay men talk. A stranger asking questions is a story they tell for a month."

"And a woman resupplying an expedition that's missed check-ins isn't?"

She turned her head just enough that I caught her profile against the glare off the river — the hard line of her jaw, the salt-and-pepper braid coiled at her neck like something kept. "I'm expected to ask. You're not." She killed the engine entirely then, and the silence of the river came up around us like water filling a boot. "Stay aboard, Wren. Or come ashore and be nobody. Your choice."

I chose to come ashore, because being nobody was a craft I'd been practicing longer than she knew.

* * *

The operator's name was Ferro, and he was older than the tower and possibly than the river itself, a man assembled mostly of sinew and sunburn, with eyes the pale washed-out blue of a shirt boiled too many times. He shook Jonah's hand with real warmth — so she *had* been here before, more than once — and gave me a nod that contained no curiosity whatsoever, which I took as a professional courtesy between men who had made themselves unmemorable on purpose.

"Water?" Ferro said. "Fuel? News?"

"News," Jonah said. "Sefu expedition. They've missed two scheduled calls out of the upper basin. I'm running supplies up to them and I'd like to know what I'm running into."

Ferro's face did something small and complicated. It was the look of a man deciding how much truth a customer could afford, and I recognized it from the other side of a hundred counters in a hundred countries. He glanced at me. Jonah said, "He's with me," in a voice that closed the subject like a door, and Ferro shrugged and led us inside.

The radio room smelled of dust, hot vacuum tubes, and the sweetish rot of the river coming through gaps in the floorboards. A desk, a transmitter older than I was, a wall of logbooks going back decades, their spines furred with mold. And pinned above the desk, curling in the damp, a map of the basin — hand-annotated, dense with pencil marks, the kind of living document a man updates for forty years until it becomes his memory made visible.

I looked at it the way you're supposed to look at things you have no business understanding: briefly, hungrily, and without letting your face know what your eyes were doing.

Ferro pulled the current logbook down and wet his thumb to turn pages. "Sefu," he said. "Imara Sefu. Good account. Calls every fifth day, eleven o'clock, this relay or the one at Puerto Hondo depending on weather. Never missed a call in three seasons." His thumb stopped moving. The page lay open under his hand and he looked at it as if it might change if he stared hard enough. "Last transmission was seventeen days ago. Position report, fuel state, all nominal. She even joked about the rain coming early. Then nothing. Eleven days past due now."

"Equipment failure?" Jonah asked.

"At her end? Maybe." Ferro said it the way people say *maybe* when they mean *no*. "That woman maintains her radios like a surgeon maintains instruments. If her set went down she'd have signaled by mirror, by flare, by drum, by sending a porter downriver with a note tied to his hair. Something else happened."

The generator coughed outside and the lights dipped, and in that half-second of darkness I felt the room tilt — not physically, but the way a room tilts when a floorboard of certainty gives way beneath you. Seventeen days. Nadia's last position report had been nominal. Nadia had joked about the rain. My sister, who had once described a monsoon to me in a letter as *the sky finally admitting what it wants*, joking about rain. It was such a small thing, such an ordinary thing, and it undid me more than the silence had. Grief does that. It doesn't wait for the big numbers. It ambushes you with a joke.

"You said men were asking after them," Jonah said.

And there it was — the thing I'd come upriver to hear, delivered in her flat pilot's voice, and my whole body went still in the way I'd trained it to go still, weight settling into my heels, breath dropping into my belly, face arranging itself into the mild blankness of a man waiting out someone else's business.

Ferro closed the logbook. He looked at Jonah for a long moment, then at me, and whatever he saw in my face satisfied him, because he said, "Sit down. Both of you. This isn't a standing conversation."

* * *

He poured coffee that tasted of smoke and river minerals, and he talked with his hands wrapped around his own cup as if holding it together.

"They came six days after Sefu went quiet. Two boats, aluminum, new engines, the quiet kind. Eight men maybe, dressed like fishermen and carrying themselves like soldiers — you know the difference? Fishermen watch the water. These watched the banks." He drank. "One of them comes up the hill, shows me credentials. Laminated card, corporate seal, some name I didn't catch and a title I did: *asset recovery liaison.* Asset recovery. For a scientific survey." His mouth twisted around the words like gristle.

"What did they want?" Jonah asked.

"Everything. Sefu's last coordinates. Her call schedule. Whether she'd shipped anything downriver through me — samples, crates, documents. Whether anyone else had asked after her." He set the cup down. "I told them what I tell everyone: relay operators keep logs, logs are public record, read them yourself. Which is true. What I didn't tell them—" he tapped the logbook with one knuckle— "is that four days before they came, another man asked after Sefu too. Alone. By shortwave, voice only, no name. Knew her check-in schedule to the minute. Asked me to confirm whether she'd called on the eleventh."

The room got very quiet. Outside, the guy-wires sang their three thin notes, and somewhere down the bank the chained dog had begun, very softly, to howl.

"What did you tell him?" Jonah said.

"The truth. That she hadn't." Ferro's pale eyes came up and fixed on me, and I understood with cold clarity that everything he'd said since we sat down had been aimed at this moment, at me, at whatever would happen in my face next. "Here's the thing about that caller. His voice. I've run this relay thirty-one years. Voices come through that speaker stripped of everything but themselves — you can't hide behind a face on the radio, only behind a name, and he gave none. But I knew the accent. Educated. Basin-born, schooled abroad, the way the company men talk. And there was something else in it." He paused, choosing the word with care. "Patience. He wasn't worried about her. Worried men ask questions fast. This man asked slow. Like a man checking on property."

My heart was beating in my ears. Because I knew. I knew before he finished the sentence, the way you know a footstep on the stairs of a house you grew up in. Basin accent, educated abroad, patient. There was a boy I'd grown up with on the delta roads who talked exactly like that — a boy who used to chart the channels in a school notebook while Nadia charted them in hers, racing each other, laughing, two cartographer's children turning the whole watershed into a game. A boy who grew into a man who went to work for the companies because somebody had to feed the family, he'd said, and then kept working for them long after the feeding was done, and long after the families downstream started losing their villages to reservoirs drawn on his maps.

His name was Aurelio Vance on the company letterhead. On the delta, where he'd been born, he had been simply Vance, and Nadia had loved him once, briefly, the way you love the person who taught you that maps can lie.

"That caller," Ferro said, still watching me. "You know him."

"No," I said.

It came out level. It came out almost true. It was the best lie I'd told in years and I hated myself for its smoothness, hated the little machinery of my face that produced it without cost, because Jonah was sitting three feet away watching me with those river-gray eyes that missed nothing on the water and, I was beginning to suspect, nothing off it either.

"Hm," Ferro said, and let it go, which meant he hadn't let it go at all.

* * *

We walked back down to the dock in the late light, the sun going copper behind the burned-over stumps, the river running gold and then bruise-colored in the space of ten minutes. Jonah carried a coil of rope she'd traded for and didn't need; I carried nothing, and felt the weight of that anyway.

"Eleven days dark," she said at last. "Company gunboats asking questions upstream. A second party asking faster ones. And you, paying triple freight to chase a sister who may be none of those things anymore." She stopped at the head of the gangplank and turned to face me fully, and I saw that she was not angry. That was worse. Anger I could work with. What she wore instead was the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. "I'm going to ask you one question, Wren, and you can lie to me if you want. I'll probably never know. But understand that I'm betting this boat, and likely my life, on whatever answer you give me right now. Is there anything about this job — anything at all — that you haven't told me?"

The river moved beneath us, vast and indifferent, carrying everything toward the sea and keeping nothing. Somewhere upstream, in country I couldn't see, my sister was either dead or hiding or waiting, and the man who might know which was a ghost on a radio frequency with a patient voice, and I had just lied to the one person standing between me and all of it.

"Nadia didn't just draw maps," I said. "She found things. Things people buried. It's why the expedition hired her, and it's why she stopped writing home eight months ago." I held Jonah's gaze and told her the truest thing I had, which was still not the whole truth. "Whoever silenced that expedition wanted it silent for a reason. And I think I know the shape of the reason, if not the name."

It was nearly honest. It was honest enough that her shoulders came down a centimeter, and she nodded once, and stepped aboard, and said, "Then we leave before dawn," and went to check her engine.

But as I followed her down into the Marisol's dim hold, past the crates of rice and diesel and medicine bound for a camp that might be a grave, I put my hand flat against the hull and felt the river's pulse through the planking, and I made myself say the name silently, once, the way you press on a bruise to learn how deep it goes.

*Vance.*

Eleven days of silence. A patient voice on the wire. And somewhere ahead of us, in the flooded green dark of the upper basin, my sister's charts pointing at something worth burning villages for.

Dawn was nine hours away, and for the first time since Callao Verde, I was afraid we wouldn't like what it showed us.
