## 7. What the River Keeps

The drowned mission church announced itself an hour before we saw it.

Elias spotted it first — or thought he did. "There's something in the trees," he said, shading his eyes against the flat white glare off the water. "A tower, maybe."

"Bell tower," I said, not looking up from the channel. I'd been running this stretch of the Río Sombra for nine years, and the church was in it the way a stone is in a shoe. You didn't forget it. You just stopped noticing the ache. "Mission of San Cristóbal de las Aguas. Jesuits built it in seventeen-something. River took the valley in the flood of '61. The whole town moved uphill and left God standing in the water."

He lowered his binoculars — good ones, military glass, another item on the growing list of things that didn't fit a grieving brother — and studied the shape emerging from the treeline ahead. "The whole town just left it?"

"The whole town just left it."

The Marisol pushed upstream at a little better than four knots against the current, the old Lister diesel knocking in its housing like a fist on a door that never opens. The river here ran wide and brown and slow, braided with dead channels, and the monsoon clouds had been stacking on the western ridgelines all morning, blue-black and patient, like creditors waiting outside a house they already owned. The air was a wet cloth. My shirt stuck to my back. Somewhere behind us, thunder cleared its throat and thought better of it.

The church came up on the port side, and I throttled down so we'd pass it slow. Not because I wanted to look. Because the water around it deserved respect.

The tower rose maybe eight meters out of the flood, its brick the color of dried blood, crowned with a rusted iron cross that leaned east like a man listening for something. The nave had gone under decades ago, but in the dry years — the years when the river remembered its manners — you could see the roof ridge breaking the surface, and once, in the worst drought anyone could name, the whole bell tower had stood on exposed mud with the bells still hanging in it, green with verdigris, silent. Now, in the high water of a pre-monsoon season already running a meter above normal, the church was mostly suggestion. A dark mass under tea-colored water. The tower. The cross. The tops of two cypress trees that had been in the churchyard when the valley drowned and had spent sixty years learning to breathe through their knees.

"Stop the boat," Elias said.

I looked at him. "We don't stop."

"You're already slowing down."

"I'm slowing down because there's a submerged wall somewhere off that tower that'll open my hull like a letter." I eased the throttle back anyway, until the Lister's knock dropped to a mutter and we were barely holding against the current, drifting sideways in the slow eddy that spun off the drowned nave. "There. Happy?"

He was standing at the rail with his forearms on it, and for a long moment he didn't say anything at all. The water slid past us, brown and opaque, carrying its usual congregation of debris — a whole kapok tree moving with the unhurried confidence of something that had never once been in a hurry, a dead capybara bloated and serene, plastic bottles from a world the Jesuits couldn't have imagined, half a roof, a shoe.

"Are the bells still in it?" he asked.

"Nobody knows. Some salvage company came up in the nineties with diving gear and a permit nobody could find the signature on. Stayed three days. Left with nothing they'd admit to." I lit a cigarette, cupping the match against a wind that didn't exist. "Locals say the bells ring sometimes. Underwater. When the river's deciding something."

"You believe that?"

I let the smoke out slow. "I believe the river's deciding something right now. Look at it. Look at the color. That's Andes water, that is. That's snowmelt and rain from a thousand kilometers upstream arriving early and angry. That water's going to keep coming whether we like it or not, and in two weeks that cross is going to be a meter underwater, and in a month you won't see the tower at all." I flicked ash into the current. "The river keeps what it wants to keep, Wren. Churches. Towns. People. It doesn't ask permission and it doesn't give receipts."

He turned and looked at me then, and I felt the look the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck. He had that quality — I'd noticed it the first day, at the dock in Callao Verde, when he'd counted out cash with a liar's smooth hands — of listening with his whole face. Most people listen with their ears and plan their reply with their mouth. Elias Wren listened like a man reading a document for the forgery in it.

"You talk about it like it's a person," he said.

"What, the river?"

"The river. The bells. The water that decides things." He nodded at the drowned tower, at the cross leaning its long lean. "You've been giving me river facts for three days, and every one of them is true, and every one of them is also about something else. You're not a woman who loves geography, Captain. You're a woman who's using it."

The current nudged the Marisol's bow, and I let it, and I took my time with the cigarette.

"Is that so," I said.

"My sister used to do that," he said. "Nadia. She'd answer a question with a map. I'd ask how she was feeling and she'd show me where the fault lines ran. I thought it was just how cartographers were." His voice did something small and controlled on the name, the way a plank does under a weight it was rated for. "Then I met other cartographers and they were normal, annoying people. So it was just her. It was just — people who'd decided the surface of a thing was safer than the middle of it."

"And you decided to become a man who pries at the middle of things."

"I decided to become a man who finds people." He said it flat, without drama, which is what made it land. "It's what I do. Missing persons. Most of them don't want to be found, and I find them anyway, and most of the time what they're hiding isn't worth the hiding." He looked back at the church. "Sometimes it is. Sometimes the thing under the water is a body."

The Lister knocked. The kapok tree moved off downriver with its cargo of birds, unhurried, going wherever drowned things go. I finished the cigarette and dropped the butt in the bilge bucket because I wasn't raised to feed my garbage to a river that already had enough.

"San Cristóbal," I said. "You want to know why they left the church standing in the water. Everybody asks. They think it's grief, or superstition, or laziness." I nodded at the tower. "It's none of those. The flood of '61 came up over four days. Slow, for a flood. The town had time. The priests rang the bells for four days straight, day and night, telling people to get out, get uphill, take what you can carry. And when the last family was on the hill and the water was in the streets, the priests went up the tower one more time and rang the bells until the water reached the bell chamber. And then they stopped. And the town decided that a church the river wanted was a church the river should have. They built the new one uphill with a bell tower facing the water, so the old bells would know they were remembered." I shrugged. "That's the story. It might even be true."

"But that's not why you know it," Elias said. "That's the brochure. You know it because of something that happened to you."

I looked at him. Rain-warm air, brown water, the cross leaning its lean. Three days on my boat and already he was steering by dead reckoning into water I hadn't charted for anyone in nine years.

"Here's what I know about staying," I said. "I know that every place I ever stopped, I stopped too long. I know that a town is a thing that gets its hooks in you — the regulars at the cantina, the kid who runs your fuel lines, the woman who — " The sentence ended. I let it end. I lit another cigarette off the stub of the first, which is a habit I have when I'm buying time. "I know that staying is just sinking that takes longer. You sit still in one place and the water comes up around you — debts, names, other people's expectations — and one day you look around and you're the church. You're the thing standing in the flood that everybody points at. And the only way out is the one San Cristóbal didn't take." I nodded at the tower. "You leave before the water reaches the bell chamber. You ring the bells while you go. You don't wait around to be drowned."

"That's not a reason," Elias said quietly. "That's a rule. Rules are what people build over reasons."

The current chose that moment to shift — the eddy off the nave letting us go, the main channel reaching out with its brown hand — and the Marisol's bow swung, and I took the excuse. I put the throttle forward and the Lister answered and we came around in a long slow arc that put the drowned church astern.

"Channel's narrow above here," I said, all business, hands and voice doing their work. "Rock bars on both sides, and the flood's coming down to meet us, so we'll have standing water in the narrows — waves that just sit there and hit you from underneath. I'll want you on the bow watching for debris. Wood in that water hits like a torpedo."

"Captain." He didn't move from the rail. "You just told me a true story about a town and a false story about yourself, and you did it in the same breath, and you think I didn't notice."

I kept my eyes on the channel. "Did you."

"The part about the woman." He said it without cruelty, which was worse. "You got to 'the woman who' and the sentence died and you buried it under a cigarette. I've spent eleven years listening to people not-finish sentences about the person they lost or the person they left. I know the sound."

The narrows opened ahead, black rock on both banks, the water between them standing up in those slow grey pyramids that mean trouble. I checked the fuel gauge I didn't need to check. I checked the depth sounder. I did everything except look at him.

"My sister," Elias said, "is probably dead. I've known that since the second missed check-in. I'm not up this river to find her alive. I'm up this river to find out what happened, and to bring back whatever's left of her, and I will lie to whoever I have to lie to in order to do it — I've lied to you, Captain, more than once, and you know it, and we've agreed to pretend otherwise because pretending is the toll on this road." He turned to face the church, the tower already shrinking behind us, the cross going down into the haze. "But I don't lie to the dead, and I don't lie about them. Whatever you left in whatever town you left burning — " and I felt my whole body go still, the way water goes still over something deep — "you can keep it. I just want you to know that I know it's there."

For a long time the only sound was the Lister and the water and the thunder on the ridgelines finally making up its mind.

"Watch for debris," I said.

He watched for debris.

* * *

We ran the narrows through the afternoon, and it was work, real work, the kind that forgives silence. Elias on the bow with a boathook, turning logs, calling depths in a voice that got less city and more river by the hour. Me at the wheel, reading the standing waves, finding the seams between them where the Marisol could pass without getting her teeth rattled out. Twice the hull hit something soft and spinning and we both froze and listened to the planking and heard nothing worse than the river's knuckles rapping, asking to be let in, and being refused.

By late afternoon the narrows opened into a long flooded savanna, and the clouds that had been stacking all day finally came down the valley like a curtain being drawn across the world behind us. The light went the color of old brass. The first drops hit the deck fat and warm and sparse, like the river testing us.

I anchored in the lee of a clay bank, in the cut-off channel where a river bend had been abandoned years ago and now slept in still water under the main flow. Good holding, high banks, one entrance to watch. The kind of place a woman anchors when she's been anchoring in strange water for nine years and is very good at not being found.

Elias cooked. Rice and tinned fish and the hot yellow peppers he'd bought in Callao Verde without asking anyone's permission, and we ate in the wheelhouse with the rain starting in earnest on the roof, a sound like applause from a very large, very indifferent audience.

"Tell me about her," I said. "Nadia."

He looked up, chopsticks — no, fork, he'd brought a fork, of course he'd brought a fork — halfway to his mouth. "You don't want to trade secrets, Captain. You want me to go first so you can decide what mine costs."

"Maybe."

He set the fork down. Outside, the rain thickened, and the clay bank began to run with thin brown veins, and the thunder moved closer with the confidence of something that owned the lease.

"She was the youngest of us and the best of us," he said. "Our father drew charts for the coastal survey — honest work, bad pay — and Nadia used to copy his charts at the kitchen table when she was six, and correct them. Not copy them wrong. Correct them. She'd say, the sandbar moved, Papa, your chart is a year old and the river is not. He'd laugh. Then he'd check. Then he'd be angry, because she was right, and she was six." He smiled, and the smile had a crack down the middle of it. "She went into the interior because the interior is where the blank spaces were. She used to say the blank spaces were the only honest part of any map. Everything else was somebody's claim."

"Somebody's claim," I repeated.

"Everything on a map is a claim, Captain. Borders, names, depth soundings. A map says: this is ours, this is known, this is settled. And Nadia — " he turned the fork over, once, like a man turning a card he didn't like — "Nadia believed the opposite. She believed a map should be a record of what the land is, not what people want from it. Which is a beautiful thing to believe and a dangerous thing to be paid to do, because the people who pay for maps are always paying because they want something." He looked at me. "Your turn."

"That wasn't the deal."

"There's no deal. There's just two people on a boat in the rain, and one of them knows the other one is lying, and the rain is going to last all night." He nodded at the wheelhouse window, at the drowned world beyond it, at the whole brown immensity of the river doing its slow patient work. "You said the river keeps what it wants. Fine. But people aren't rivers. People who keep everything eventually capsize."

I should have thrown him off the boat that night. At the next landing, at the next fuel stop, at the first mudbank flat enough to stand on. I've thrown men off the Marisol for less — for touching the wheel, for asking about the false panels, for a look that lasted a second too long. Nine years of never stopping longer than the job requires, and here was a man asking for the one thing I'd never given anyone, and my hand was on the throttle and I didn't push it forward.

Because — and this is the truth I told myself later, in the dark, in the rain, when lying to myself was the only lying left that didn't cost anything — because he'd said *the woman who* and I had heard it in his voice, the shape of the unfinished thing, and I'd recognized it. He had a Nadia. I had a — 

A town. I had a town. And a man in it whose name I still kept in the same pocket as my knife, and the fire, and the road out, and nine years of river since, and every port a door I left before it closed on me.

"Her name was — " I started, and stopped, and Elias didn't lean forward, didn't move at all, which is how I knew he understood that whatever came out of me next would come out at its own speed or not at all.

"Her name isn't the point," I said finally. "The point is that I stayed somewhere once. Longer than I should have. And I told myself it was different, that I was different, that the rules were for other people. And I was wrong in a way that — " The rain hammered the roof. The Lister ticked as it cooled. Somewhere out in the flooded savanna, something large moved through the shallows and was gone. "There's a town I can't go back to. That's the whole story. That's the truest thing I've said in nine years, and it's still not the truth, and you can take it or leave it."

Elias was quiet for a while. The rain came down. The anchor line hummed a low note in the current, the Marisol swinging gently on her hook like a thing asleep.

"San Cristóbal," he said at last. "The priests rang the bells until the water reached the bell chamber. That's what you said."

"What about it."

"You've been ringing bells for nine years, Captain. Moving, running, never stopping. Every town you leave, you leave loud — you leave owing nothing, owning nothing, known to no one." He picked his fork back up and ate, casual as weather. "But ringing bells isn't the same as being saved. Sometimes it's just noise, so you can't hear the water rising."

I wanted to hit him. I wanted to laugh. I did neither. I took the plates and washed them in the bucket of river water we kept for the purpose, and outside the dark came down complete, and the lightning started walking the ridgelines to the west, long silent flashes that lit the clouds from inside like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"First watch is yours," I said. "Wake me at two. Anything comes up that channel entrance, you wake me before you wake the rifle."

"Understood."

I went below to my bunk, and lay in the dark, and listened to the rain, and did not sleep. Through the deck above me I could hear Elias moving, checking the anchor light, checking the channel, being careful with my boat in the way of a man who understands that a boat is not a boat, it's a life with a hull around it.

And it was near midnight, I think, when I heard him stop moving. When I heard him go to the rail and stand there, and heard the rain change its sound around a standing body.

I came up quietly. I don't know why. Yes I do.

He was looking back the way we'd come, down the long black throat of the channel toward the main river. Toward the drowned church, which was kilometers astern now and invisible, and which he was looking at anyway, the way you look at a grave you passed on the road.

"Light," he said, without turning. "Downriver. Two flashes. Pause. Two flashes."

I went cold in a way the rain had nothing to do with. I took the glasses from the rack and found it — far off, at the edge of the dark, at the limit of what glass and lightning could show: a white light, small and steady, winking. Two flashes. Pause. Two flashes. Deliberate. Repeated. The kind of signal that isn't meant for the sky.

"Signal lamp?" Elias said. "Who's down there?"

I kept the glasses up. Kept my voice level, because a captain's voice is the last thing that sinks. "Nobody I know," I said.

And that was a lie, and we both knew it was a lie, because the signal was the old one, the river-runner's one, the one I hadn't seen in nine years — two and two, *who goes there, who goes there* — and it was pointed not at the church, not at the channel, but directly, patiently, at the Marisol, riding at anchor in the rain with her running lights burning like a woman who has finally, after nine years of moving, stopped.

The lightning walked the ridgelines. The light winked. Two. Two.

Somebody downriver knew my name.
