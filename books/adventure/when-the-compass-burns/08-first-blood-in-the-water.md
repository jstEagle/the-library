## 8. First Blood in the Water

The narrows began where the river forgot how to be generous.

Elias felt the change before he saw it. The Marisol's engine note shifted — a half-tone lower, a strain beneath it, like a singer reaching for a note she wasn't sure was there. The banks, which had been drifting apart all morning into wide brown floodplains dotted with cattle egrets, now leaned inward again, closing like hands around a throat. The jungle came down to the water's edge and kept going, roots submerged, leaves trailing in the current as if the forest were drinking.

"Sit down," Jonah said from the wheelhouse. She hadn't raised her voice. She never did. "And hold on to something that doesn't move."

"What doesn't move on this boat?"

"Nothing," she said. "Pick your poison."

He found a spot on the cargo hatch amidships, wedged himself against the rail stanchion, and watched her work. It had become his habit over the past two days — watching her. Not because he trusted her; he didn't, not entirely, not yet. But because she was the most legible thing on this river. Every other landmark lied. The channel markers were rotted or missing. The charts in the wheelhouse were older than he was, annotated in three different hands, one of them apparently drawn during a fever. But Jonah Vasquez made sense. Her hands on the wheel were a language, and Elias, who had spent his adult life reading maps and the people who drew them, was teaching himself to read her.

She stood with her weight forward on the balls of her feet, shoulders loose, head tilted slightly as though listening to something below the engine noise. Her eyes moved constantly — bank to water to sky to water again — but her body barely moved at all. Economy of motion. A woman who had learned that every wasted gesture was a debt.

Ahead, the river bent hard to starboard around a ridge of black stone, and the water through the gap moved wrong. Elias could see it even from amidships: not flowing so much as *boiling*, the surface stitched with standing waves and white seams, the whole channel constricted to perhaps sixty meters between rock walls that rose sheer and dripping on both sides.

"The Narrows of San Fermin," Jonah said. She said it the way other people named graves. "Six kilometers of bad ideas."

"How bad?"

"You ever thread a needle wearing boxing gloves, in the dark, while someone swings at you?" She adjusted the throttle a fraction. "It's like that, except the needle moves."

* * *

They'd seen the first boat an hour before the narrows.

Elias had spotted it from the bow — a long, low shape holding position near the far bank, maybe two kilometers back, close enough to be traffic, far enough to be nothing. He'd almost mentioned it and then decided against it. River traffic meant nothing out here. Dugouts, barges, fishing boats, all of them moving at their own pace toward their own business.

But then there'd been a second boat, upstream of the first, hugging the shadowed bank where the canopy threw the water into green dusk. And when the Marisol passed the first one, its engine note changed — deepened, picked up, fell in behind them at a distance that never varied. Two hundred meters. Always two hundred meters.

He went up to the wheelhouse. Jonah was already looking at the rearview mirror bolted crookedly to the window frame, a strip of mirror no bigger than a playing card.

"Fishing boats don't run formation," Elias said.

"No," she agreed.

"They've been behind us since the burned village."

"I know." Her voice was flat, unhurried, and somehow that was worse than alarm. "Two boats. Eight men, maybe ten. The big one has an outboard mounted on a swivel — you can see the bracket catch light when they come around bends. That's not for fishing either."

"Pirates? This far up?"

"This far up is where pirates live. Downstream there's police, patrols, radios. Up here there's just us and them and six hundred kilometers of nobody." She glanced at him, and something in her expression made him step back half a pace. "You carrying anything worth stealing?"

"Cash. Some equipment. My sister's letters." He heard his own voice go careful on the last word.

Jonah nodded slowly, turning back to the mirror. "Then they'll want the boat more than the cargo. Boat's worth eight months of what they'd make running timber. And a pilot." Her jaw tightened. "A pilot who knows this stretch is worth killing for."

"So we outrun them."

"In this?" She gestured at the Marisol with her chin — the rusted rails, the patched hull, the engine that coughed when the humidity climbed. "Against two fast boats built for exactly this? No. We don't outrun anyone." She put both hands on the wheel. "We go somewhere they won't follow."

Elias looked ahead at the black stone walls and the boiling water between them and understood, with a slow cold settling in his stomach, exactly what she meant.

* * *

The first shot came at the mouth of the narrows.

It wasn't aimed — or if it was, the aim was a message rather than an attempt. The bullet cracked across the water a meter above the wheelhouse roof and slapped into the stone wall ahead, spitting rock fragments that rang off the deck plates like thrown coins.

Elias hit the deck. When he got his face above the coaming, he saw the lead boat coming on fast now, no longer pretending, its wake a white V cutting across the brown water. Men crouched along its gunwales. One of them stood at the bow with a rifle, working the action with the casual competence of long practice.

"Jonah—"

"I see them." She wasn't crouching. She stood at the wheel as if the bullets were weather, as if the whole thing were a squall she'd steamed through forty times before, and Elias realized with a lurch that maybe she had. "Get below. Take the wheel if I fall."

"If you—"

"*Take the wheel if I fall.*" She looked at him then, fully, and her eyes were the color of the river after a storm — opaque, churning, giving nothing back. "There's a chart under the dash. Red pencil line. If I go down, you follow the red line and you don't deviate, not for rocks, not for bodies, not for me. Do you understand?"

He understood. What he understood was that she had planned for this — that somewhere in the rusted guts of the Marisol there was a chart with a red line drawn by a woman who had always known that someday the past would come up the river after her with guns. That she had traced her own escape route in advance and colored it red like blood.

He crawled to the wheelhouse and stayed.

The second shot took out the port running light. Glass rained across the deck. The third went wide, and then the narrows swallowed them all.

* * *

What happened next, Elias would spend years trying to describe, and never manage it.

The walls closed in. The sound changed — the river's murmur became a roar with texture in it, a grinding undertone, the voice of water dragging itself over stone. The Marisol surged as the current took hold of her hull, and Jonah rode it, feathering the throttle, touching the wheel in movements too small to be called corrections. The boat threaded gaps that Elias's eyes refused to accept existed until they were already behind him. Rocks slid past close enough to read the moss on them. Once, a submerged shelf passed beneath the keel with a shudder Elias felt in his teeth, and Jonah made a small sound — not fear, *annoyance* — and altered course by two degrees.

Behind them, the pirate boat followed.

He heard it before he saw it — the different pitch of its engine, higher, hungrier, echoing off the stone walls until it seemed to come from everywhere. Then it appeared in the churn behind them, wallowing badly in water it wasn't built for, its pilot fighting to hold a line through rapids designed for barges and madwomen. For a moment Elias thought the river itself would settle the matter. The boat struck something, slewed sideways, recovered.

Then the shooting started in earnest.

Bullets cracked off the stone around them. One punched through the wheelhouse window and buried itself in the chart drawer an arm's length from Jonah's hip. She didn't flinch. She was talking now, low and continuous, and it took Elias a moment to realize she was talking to herself — to the boat, to the river, reciting depth and speed and distance like a rosary: *"Easy, easy, third seam on the left, don't look at the hole, look at the water going round it—"*

The channel bent left, then right, then dropped.

That was the part Elias's memory kept returning to afterward: the drop. The river fell away ahead of them — a full meter, maybe more, the water folding over itself in a long silver staircase — and Jonah did not slow down. She opened the throttle.

"We're going to die," Elias said. He said it calmly, conversationally, the way you announce the time.

"Probably," Jonah said. "Hold on."

The Marisol went over the drop like a stone with opinions. The bow plunged, the world turned brown and roaring, the hull slammed the trough below with a blow that threw Elias across the wheelhouse and put a crack in the deck plate he'd hear complaining for weeks. Water came over the rails in sheets. The engine screamed, caught water, screamed again — and then Jonah had the bow up and they were through, surging out of the foam into calmer water beyond, the stone walls falling away on either side, the narrows opening like a fist unclenching.

Behind them, the first pirate boat had stopped.

It sat at the top of the drop, wallowing in the eddy, and even from two hundred meters Elias could see the argument happening on its deck — men gesturing at the rapids, at each other, at the receding Marisol. They were pirates. They were brave enough to shoot at unarmed boats on open water. They were not brave enough — or paid enough — to follow a woman who ran drops like that with a loaded hull and a straight face.

The second boat, the one that had hugged the shadows, never even entered the narrows. It peeled away at the mouth and vanished downstream, and Elias understood that its job had been to turn them back, to herd them into the first boat's guns, and that Jonah had broken the trap simply by refusing to be afraid of the only thing the trap depended on.

* * *

An hour later, anchored in a side slough with the engine ticking as it cooled, Jonah cleaned the rifle she kept wrapped in oilcloth under her bunk.

Elias sat on the hatch cover and watched her. Her hands moved with the same economy she brought to the wheel — bore brush, patches, oil, each motion exact, none wasted. She hadn't spoken since the narrows. Neither had he. The adrenaline was still draining out of him in slow tremors he hid by holding a cup of coffee he had no intention of drinking.

"You knew that drop," he said finally. "You've run it before."

"Twice." She worked the bolt, checked the chamber, reassembled without looking at her hands. "Once going down in flood season like today. Once going up, which is impossible, which is why I did it."

"Why?" He meant more than the drop. He meant all of it — the red-lined chart, the calm under fire, the way she'd stood at the wheel while bullets chewed the air around her head. "Why are you like this?"

She looked up. For a moment he thought she wouldn't answer, and he braced for the deflection, the half-truth, the wall she built out of jokes about the river whenever he got close to anything real.

But the adrenaline had loosened something. Or the narrows had. Or maybe she was simply tired, and tired people tell the truth in fragments.

"When I was nineteen," she said, "I let myself get caught."

The rifle lay across her knees, gleaming. She stared at it instead of at him.

"There was a town. People I — there were people who counted on me, and I stayed when I should have run, because staying felt like courage and running felt like cowardice, and I was young enough to confuse the two." Her thumb moved along the stock, tracing a scratch he couldn't see. "What happened next cost more than running ever would have. So now I have a rule. I don't get caught. Not by pirates, not by companies, not by the past. I run first. I run fast. I run *well*." She finally looked at him, and her eyes were dry and very old. "Today you saw what that costs. Today you also saw why I pay it."

"And if running isn't enough someday? If the thing chasing you is faster than the river?"

Something moved behind her eyes — the same flicker he'd seen at the drowned church, the same door opening a centimeter and shutting. "Then I drown it," she said. "Or it drowns me. Either way, it doesn't take me alive."

She stood, broke the rifle open one last time to confirm it was empty, and stowed it under the bunk. At the wheelhouse door she paused, her back to him.

"Eat something. Sleep if you can. Tomorrow we pass the last relay station before Sefu country." A beat. "After that, there's no one to call and nowhere to run that isn't farther in."

She went forward to check the anchor. Elias sat with his untouched coffee and listened to the river, and thought about a woman who had drawn her escape route in red pencil, and wondered — with the first real fear he'd felt since Callao Verde — what kind of past chased a person so relentlessly that she'd rather ride a waterfall than let it touch her.

Above the trees, the clouds had begun to organize themselves into something with intent.
