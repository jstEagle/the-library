## 9. The Burned Village

The river told them before they saw it.

Jonah had learned to read water the way other people read faces, and all that morning the Marisol's wake had been carrying things downstream that didn't belong to the current: a charred plank, swollen and black as a drowned man's hand. A cooking pot, dented, its handle melted into a silver teardrop. A single rubber sandal turning slowly in an eddy like something deciding whether to come back.

She cut the engine to half throttle and stood at the wheel, one hand on the brass rail, watching the banks.

"What is it?" Elias asked from behind her. He'd spent most of the morning in the deck chair pretending to read a paperback he'd brought aboard at Callao Verde, but she'd noticed the pages hadn't turned in an hour.

"Smoke," she said. "Old smoke."

He came to stand beside her at the rail. "I don't smell anything."

"You won't. Not anymore." She nodded at the water sliding past. "But it does. Char floats longer than you'd think. And look —" she pointed with her chin at a mat of debris caught against a fallen tree on the left bank — "palm thatch. Roofing. That's not flood trash. Flood trash comes down in layers, all seasons mixed together. That's all from one place, all at once."

Elias was quiet for a moment. "A village?"

"Maybe." She didn't believe maybe, and she could hear that he didn't either. She pushed the throttle forward again, but slower now, the way you walk toward a door you're afraid of what's behind it.

* * *

They found it two bends later, on a stretch of high bank where every pilot on this river knew there was a village called San Telmo de Agua — a trading stop, forty-odd families, a stilted market house where you could buy diesel, plantains, and lies about the fishing. Jonah had stopped there twice in five years of running this stretch. The old woman who sold dried fish had once pressed an extra portion on her and refused payment, saying, *You have thin wrists for someone who works so hard,* and Jonah had thought about that stupid kindness for three hundred kilometers.

San Telmo de Agua was gone.

Not damaged. Not flooded. Gone — in the way a sentence is gone when someone has taken a knife to the page. The stilt houses stood or leaned where fire had eaten through their legs, their walls collapsed into black fans of ash and wire. The market house was a skeleton of charred beams against the sky, its tin roof curled back like a leaf in a flame. Everything was the gray-black of burnt wood, except where the rain since had begun to wash it toward brown, and except for one thing: the mud.

The mud was full of footprints. Dozens of them, churned deep, going in every direction and then, finally, all going upstream along the bank trail. Fleeing prints. Prints made by people running with what they could carry.

And crates.

They were scattered along the landing like the contents of a dropped purse — wooden supply crates, some split open, some intact, stamped on their sides with a logo Jonah had seen eleven days ago on a clipboard at the relay station sixty kilometers downstream: a stylized wave inside a hexagon, beneath the words **MERIDIAN HYDROCARBON & INFRASTRUCTURE**.

She brought the Marisol alongside the ruined landing without needing to decide to. Her hands did it. Elias dropped the fenders over the side, and neither of them spoke until the boat kissed the pilings and the engine died and the silence of the burned place settled over them like a second passenger.

"Stay aboard," Jonah said.

"No," Elias said.

She looked at him. He looked back, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before — not the smooth grief-brother mask, not the too-clever questions. Something rawer. His knuckles were white around the rail.

"My sister is upriver," he said. "With people who do *this*." He nodded at the village. "I'm coming."

It wasn't an argument she could win, because part of her wanted the same thing and knew how useless wanting was. She pulled the machete from under the wheel console, checked the fuel in the dinghy outboard, and said, "Then stay behind me and don't touch anything."

* * *

The ground crunched.

That was the first thing, walking up from the landing — the crunch of ash and burnt palm thatch underfoot, fine as snow, rising in little ghosts around their ankles. The air still held a taste of it, faint and chemical underneath, like a candle blown out in a closed room. Under the ash smell was another smell, older and worse, that Jonah chose not to name and saw Elias choose not to name either.

A dog watched them from the shadow of a collapsed canoe shed. It was ribs and eyes, too smart to bark. It watched them pass and did not follow.

"The crates," Elias said. He'd stopped at one near the landing, a heavy-duty shipping crate with steel corners. The Meridian hexagon stared up at them from the wet wood. "These are expedition supplies. Or — no." He crouched, turned the crate with both hands. "Different manifest numbers. These are company stock. Heavy equipment consumables. This one says generator fuel." He looked up at her. "Why would a hydrocarbon company be shipping generator fuel to a village of fishermen?"

"They wouldn't," Jonah said. "Unless they were staying."

The word sat between them. *Staying.* Companies didn't stay in places like San Telmo de Agua. They passed through, bought what they needed, signed what they needed, and left. Staying meant infrastructure. Staying meant a camp, a depot, a forward base. Staying meant they expected to be here long enough that the people already here were a problem to be solved rather than neighbors to be traded with.

She walked on through the ruins, and the village told its story in the grammar of destruction, and Jonah, who had once stood in the doorway of a burning building and listened to a town die, understood the dialect fluently.

The fires had been set deliberately, in patterns. Not spread by wind or accident — started at the load-bearing posts of each stilt house, where the kerosene would soak and climb. She could see the pour-marks in the ash, dark fans where liquid had hit the ground. Every house. The church — a small concrete chapel with a corrugated roof — had been burned too, though concrete doesn't burn; whatever was inside it had. The schoolhouse was the worst. Small desks. A chalkboard still hanging, warped, with half a lesson still ghosted on it in charcoal lines: sums, a child's careful handwriting marching across the board and stopping mid-number, as if the hand had been called away.

Elias stood in the doorway of the schoolhouse for a long time. When he came out his face was composed again, but it was the composition of a man holding a door shut from the inside.

"There are no bodies," he said.

"No," Jonah agreed. "There aren't."

That was the thing she kept circling and couldn't land on. Burned villages in this country usually had bodies, and everyone knew it, and everyone pretended otherwise. But here — nothing. Just the churned mud, the fleeing prints, the debris field drifting downstream. Either the villagers had all run ahead of the fire —

— or they'd been moved first, and the fire was cleanup.

She didn't say that out loud. She didn't have to. She watched Elias arrive at the same arithmetic and watched it land on him like weather.

"The relay station operator," he said quietly. "Eleven days dark. Men with corporate credentials asking after Sefu. And now this." He turned in a slow circle, taking in the whole gray ruin. "This isn't a search for missing scientists. This is a corridor. They're clearing a corridor upriver."

Jonah crouched by one of the crates near the market house ruin. This one had been pried open hastily and left, its contents scattered: tins of diesel additive, a coil of new rope, and — she lifted it out with two fingers — a laminated card, company-issue, printed in three languages. **MERIDIAN PERSONNEL — RESTRICTED ZONE. RIVER ACCESS PERMIT REQUIRED BEYOND KM 340.**

Kilometer 340 was four days upstream. It was also, if Nadia Wren's route notes were worth anything — and Jonah had been studying them in stolen moments since Callao Verde — within thirty kilometers of the last known position of the Sefu expedition.

She handed the card to Elias without a word. He read it, and she watched the same conclusion move through him that had moved through her, cold water finding its level.

"They've claimed the river," he said. "On paper. Like they own it already."

"Paper's how they start," Jonah said. "The fire's how they finish."

* * *

They found the survivor at the far edge of the village, past the chapel, where a line of cassava gardens ran up toward the forest. He was an old man, and he had not fled far enough — or he had come back, which was worse. He was sitting on a stump in the middle of his ruined garden, sorting through the ash of what had been his drying racks, picking out unburnt pieces of net float and setting them in a row beside him with tremendous care, as if the order of the row mattered more than anything else in the world.

He heard them coming and didn't turn around.

"If you've come to finish it," he said in river Spanish, "you're late. And you'll need more men."

"We're not with them," Jonah said.

"Nobody's with anybody. That's the trouble." But he turned then, and looked at them — at Jonah's river-worn jacket, at the Marisol visible through the trees at the landing — and something in his face shifted from defiance to a tired kind of assessment. "Boat woman. I know you. You came for diesel, two years back. You paid fair and you didn't preach."

"I remember the fish," Jonah said. "Your wife sold it."

"Wife's upstream now. With the others. Walking to Puerto Cielo, God give them strength." He went back to his row of net floats. Set another one straight. "Came back for my nets. A man should bury his nets."

"How many came?" Elias asked. "How many men?"

The old man considered him. "Boats first. Two big boats, gray, with a machine on the front that talks — loudspeaker, telling people to gather at the landing. Papers, they said. Registration papers, land titles, fishing permits. Everyone has papers, we've fished here two hundred years, but not the right papers, theirs." He set another float. "Then the ones in matching clothes went house to house. Then the ones with guns went house to house. Then the fire." He shrugged, a small terrible shrug. "The order of things."

"Did they say why?" Jonah asked. "Did anyone say why?"

"Why." The old man almost smiled. "The loudspeaker man said *development corridor*. Said the basin was entering a *new era of managed resource access*. My grandson asked him where we would go." He looked up, and his eyes were dry and very old. "The man smiled and gave him a bottle of soda."

Silence. Somewhere in the ruined village a sheet of tin shifted in the wind and clanged, once, like a bell with nothing left to say.

"They took the young men," the old man said suddenly, as if remembering a debt. "The strong ones. Put them in the boats. Said they were being hired. Porters, laborers, good wages." His hands stopped moving over the nets. "My grandson waved at me from the boat. He was smiling. He thought it was real work."

Elias made a sound low in his throat. Jonah put a hand on the old man's shoulder — light, brief, the only language she had — and felt him lean into it for exactly one second before pulling himself upright again, because pride is the last crop that survives a burn.

"Come with us," she said. "We'll take you upstream. Catch your family."

"And leave my nets?" But he was already standing, joints cracking like green wood, gathering the row of floats into a woven bag with the tenderness other men spend on grandchildren. At the edge of the garden he stopped and looked back once at the ash-gray ruin of San Telmo de Agua, and Jonah watched him do what she herself had done outside a dozen towns: memorize it, so that leaving couldn't take it from him entirely.

Only he wasn't leaving. He was just going upstream. There was a difference, and Jonah felt the difference like a splinter working its way toward skin.

* * *

They cast off an hour before dusk, the old man — Tomás Biya, he was called — seated amidships with his bag of net floats, staring back at the burned village until the bend took it. Elias stood at the bow with the laminated permit card in his hand, reading it again as though the words might rearrange themselves into something less damning.

Jonah held the wheel and did her arithmetic.

Two gunboats. Loudspeakers. Press-ganged laborers. A restricted zone beginning at kilometer 340. An expedition silent for eleven days, led by a woman who had found something in flooded ruins that a company wanted badly enough to burn a village to secure the approach. And somewhere ahead, in that gray corridor of claimed water, Dr. Imara Sefu and whoever remained of her crew — including, possibly, a cartographer named Nadia Wren — were either hiding, captured, or dead.

Below her feet, in the hold, rode six tons of supplies contracted for delivery to that same expedition, and a stranger who lied about why he cared, and a logbook planted aboard her boat by a fixer who played both shores of any river.

Every instinct she owned — every rule carved into her over years of leaving — said turn the boat around. Run downriver ahead of the rain, deliver the old man to his family, take the loss on the contract, vanish. There were a hundred rivers in the world. She had never yet met one she couldn't leave.

Tomás Biya touched her elbow. When she glanced down, he was looking up at her with those dry old eyes.

"You're going to keep going north," he said. Not a question.

Jonah looked at the channel ahead — brown water rising, the sky in the west gone the color of a bruise, the first fat drops of the early monsoon spotting the deck around them. Somewhere up that channel, at kilometer 340, a line had been drawn on a map by men who thought paper could hold a river.

"Yes," she heard herself say.

The old man nodded, satisfied, and settled back among his net floats. Behind her, Elias folded the permit card and put it in his pocket like a man keeping evidence. The rain began in earnest, hissing on the hot ash-smell still clinging to the hull, and the Marisol turned her bow upstream, into the darkening water, toward the line somebody had drawn across it.

Jonah's hands were steady on the wheel. What frightened her was that they were steady — that some part of her, the part that had been running for years, had already known, since the first charred plank came floating down the current, that this river was going to be the one she didn't leave.

The compass on the binnacle trembled, and held.
