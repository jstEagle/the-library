## 11. Monsoon Season Opens

The river told them first.

Jonah had been listening to water her whole life — to the gossip of shallows, the low mutter of deep channels, the particular silence a river makes when it is thinking about something. And for two days the river had been talking in a voice she didn't like: too full, too fast, a brown muscular current carrying whole trees past the Marisol like matchsticks, the banks already softening at their edges, drinking.

"It's early," Elias said, standing beside her at the wheel, watching the water the way she'd taught him to watch it. "The rains. Aren't they early?"

"A week." She didn't look at him. "Maybe ten days."

"Is that bad?"

She let the question hang. Somewhere behind them, over the ridgeline, the sky had gone the color of a bruise going bad, purple-black and swollen, and the air had the thick wet weight of a held breath. The birds had stopped flying. Even the insects had gone quiet, and Jonah had learned long ago that when the small things went quiet, it was time for the large things to find shelter.

"It's bad," she said finally, because lying to him about the river would be a waste of a lie. "The season's opening like a fist. And we're in the middle of it."

* * *

It came at dusk, and it came like a wall falling.

One moment the Marisol was pushing upstream through a gray half-light, the engine laboring against the current, and the next the world simply ended — replaced by water falling not in drops but in sheets, in ropes, in a roaring gray curtain that erased the banks, the sky, the far side of the river, everything. Jonah killed the throttle and let the boat swing with the current, nose downstream, because fighting it in that would be suicide, and eased her toward the lee of a bluff where the overhanging jungle broke the wind's first fury.

The rain hit the deck like gravel thrown by an angry god. It hit the canopy above the wheelhouse like drumming on a coffin lid. It came down the chimney of the stove pipe and put out the fire Elias had just lit, and it came through every seam and joint and riveted plate on the Marisol that thirty years of river work had worked loose, and it began, quietly at first, to collect in her bilge.

Jonah stood at the wheel in the streaming dark and felt it happen. She couldn't have said how she knew — some shift in the boat's trim, some change in the way the deck sat under her boots, some old sailor's sixth sense lodged in her spine. But she knew.

"We're taking water," she said.

Elias looked up from wringing out his shirt. "What?"

"Down in the hold. Listen." And beneath the roar on the roof, if you knew what to listen for, there it was: a sound like a slow kiss, like a mouth against skin, the bilge water lapping at the frames a finger higher than it had been that morning.

They went below with the lantern, and Jonah opened the bilge cover, and the water looked up at them, black and flat and patient, three inches deep where two inches was too much.

"Slow leak," Elias said.

"There's no such thing." Jonah was already pulling the pump handle loose from its brackets, checking the leather cup with her thumb, finding it soft. "There's leaks that haven't decided how fast they want to be yet. This one's thinking about it."

* * *

They anchored in the lee of the bluff and the night became a war fought in shifts.

The rain did not slacken. It settled in, the way the true monsoon does — not a storm that passes but a season that arrives and takes possession, a fact rather than an event. Lightning walked the ridgelines to the west in long silent strides, each flash freezing the jungle into a negative of itself, each thunder rolling down the valley minutes later like the sound of something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Jonah rigged the pump and the bail bucket and a third arrangement she'd built years ago and never named: a canvas chute stitched to a leak in the deck seam above, funneling what came through into a barrel instead of onto the engine. She'd built it in another life, on another river, for another boat, and her hands remembered the work without her.

"You've done this before," Elias said. It wasn't a question. He was soaked to the skin, hair plastered flat, hauling the bucket up out of the bilge with both hands, and even now, even exhausted, there was that watchfulness in him, that ledger he kept behind his eyes. But for the moment the ledger was closed. The water didn't care about anyone's secrets.

"Every boat I've ever had has leaked," Jonah said. "Every boat anyone's ever had. A hull is just a argument with the river, and the river never gets tired."

"How long do we do this?"

"Till the rain stops or the boat wins." She took the bucket from him, poured it out the scupper, handed it back. "One of us bails, one of us rests, and we trade. Four hours. Longer and you start missing things, and a person who misses things at a pump handle drowns."

They traded. Through the black hours they traded, the two of them passing the bucket back and forth like a conversation neither wanted to have out loud, the lantern swinging on its hook, the lightning strobing through the wheelhouse windows, the thunder coming closer and then going away and coming closer again like something circling.

* * *

It was somewhere past the middle of the night — Jonah had stopped tracking time by the clock and started tracking it by the water level, that slow arithmetic of the bilge — when Elias stopped bailing and sat down hard on the engine housing, dripping, and said:

"Tell me something true."

Jonah kept pumping. "The bilge's at two and a half inches. That's true."

"Jonah."

"You're tired. Tired people want true things. Sleep, and in the morning you'll want lies again, and I've got better lies."

He didn't laugh. In the swinging lantern light his face was hollowed out, the shadows under his eyes like bruises, and when he looked at her there was none of the careful charm he'd boarded with, none of the practiced grief. Just exhaustion, and under the exhaustion, something rawer.

"My sister hated the rain," he said. "Nadia. She grew up in a city where it rained eight months of the year and she used to say she became a cartographer of dry places out of spite. And then she took a job mapping the wettest basin on earth." His voice caught on something. "I keep thinking — she would have hated this. This exact rain. And I keep thinking she was somewhere under it, eleven days ago, and nobody answered the radio."

The pump handle rose and fell in Jonah's grip. She let three strokes go by before she answered, and the rain filled the silence for her, hammering on the deck above like a crowd demanding entry.

"You didn't come upriver to resupply anybody," she said. "You came to find out if she's alive. Everything else — the cash, the lies about the manifest — that's just the fare you paid to get aboard my boat."

Elias went very still. The lantern swung; the shadows swung with it.

"Does it change anything?" he asked. "Whether I told you the truth?"

Jonah considered the question honestly, because he'd earned that much, hauling her water out of her hull all night. "No," she said. "I'd have taken you anyway. A man looking for his sister bails harder than a man running supplies." She handed him the bucket. "Your turn."

He took it. But as he turned to the bilge cover he said, quietly, without looking back: "You didn't answer me. I said tell me something true."

And the strange thing — the thing Jonah would turn over in her mind for years afterward, in a life she couldn't yet imagine — was that she almost did. The words were right there, sitting in her chest the way the water sat in her bilge: *I burned a town down. Not with a match. With my silence. I knew what he was doing with what I told him, and I told him anyway, and when the smoke went up I put the river between me and it and I have been putting rivers between me and everything ever since.*

She could feel them there. She could feel the bilge of herself, three inches deep and rising, and the pump handle of speech in her hand, and the choice, the same choice she made every time, in every port, with every stranger.

"Two and a half inches," she said. "That's the truth. Bail."

* * *

Near dawn the leak made its move.

It had been thinking all night — she could feel it, that patient dark arithmetic below — and sometime in the gray hour before light, when Jonah was asleep in the chair by the wheel and Elias had the watch, the river found the seam it had been looking for. Maybe it was a plank working loose. Maybe a seam of caulking, soaked through thirty hours of monsoon, finally gave up its argument. Whatever it was, the bilge went from two and a half inches to five in under an hour, and it was Elias who found it, Elias who came down the ladder two rungs at a time and put his hand on her shoulder and said her name in a voice that had no charm left in it at all, only fear, and the fear woke her faster than any alarm.

"Five inches," he said. "Maybe more. It's coming in fast now."

They fought it. There's no other word for it, and Jonah had never been a woman who reached for softer ones. They fought it with the pump and the bucket and the canvas chutes and finally with their hands, Elias down in the bilge itself to his elbows in black water finding the seam by feel while Jonah ran the pump and shouted the engine over to give him light and power, and the rain came down and the lightning walked the ridges and the river rose around the anchored Marisol, a hand's width an hour, patient as arithmetic.

They found it near the keel, a seam the length of Jonah's forearm, weeping under pressure. Elias held a rag against it while Jonah drove wedges — soft pine, soaked so they'd swell — and hammered them home in the strobing lightning, and when the flow slowed to a seep and the seep slowed to a memory, the two of them sat down on the engine housing side by side, soaked through, shaking, the lantern guttering, and laughed. Not much. Just enough. The kind of laughter that lives on the far side of drowning.

"That," Elias said, "was the worst night of my life."

"No," Jonah said. "It wasn't. But it's in the top ten, and you're still breathing, so drink your water and sit down."

He looked at her then — really looked, the way he'd been looking at her since the dock in Callao Verde, that ledger-keeping look — and for once she didn't mind it. Because she understood it now. He wasn't cataloguing her lies. He was trying to figure out what kind of person bails all night beside a stranger and gives back nothing but water levels.

She didn't know the answer herself. That was the truth she couldn't pump out.

* * *

The rain held through the morning and into the afternoon, and the river kept rising.

By noon the bluff's lee was gone, drowned, and they had to move the Marisol out into the current and work her upstream in short desperate pushes between the eddies, the engine screaming, the whole boat shuddering like an animal in a trap. The channel markers — the few that existed — had gone under. The banks had become rumors. Whole trees rode past them standing upright, roots first, like drowned men walking, and twice Jonah had to throw the wheel hard over to keep from taking one across the bow.

"We can't keep this up," Elias said, hauling on the wheel beside her. His hands were blistered from the bucket handle. He hadn't mentioned them. "The engine can't fight this current for days. Nothing can."

"Then we don't fight it for days." Jonah's eyes were on the water ahead, reading it the way Nadia — wherever she was, if she was — had read her charts. "We fight it in pieces. Eddy to eddy, bank to bank. The river's fast in the middle and slow at the edges. You move like a thief. You move when it isn't looking."

"Is that how you've survived this long?"

"That's how anything survives this long."

But she heard the truth under his question, and she knew he heard it too: *this long* was not the same as *forever*, and the Marisol was old, and the river was rising faster than any season on record, and somewhere upstream, eleven days dark, was an expedition that had stopped answering its radio — and somewhere upstream beyond that, if the fixer's map and the relay operator's nervous silences meant what they seemed to mean, was something else. Something that made corporate men burn villages.

The thought surfaced and she pushed it down, the way you push down anything that floats in a bilge.

* * *

The second night was worse than the first, and shorter, because exhaustion makes nights short. They bailed in shifts again, but the rhythm had changed. Somewhere in the first night's fighting the bucket had stopped being a transaction between two suspicious strangers and become something else — a shared labor, a wordless pact, the oldest kind of trust there is: *I'll hold the light if you hold the seam.* Elias had stopped asking her for true things. Jonah had stopped pretending she didn't notice that he'd stopped. The rain came down. The lightning walked. The bilge stayed at three inches, and three inches, on the Marisol, in that season, was a truce rather than a victory, and both of them knew it.

It was near dawn on the second night, Jonah at the pump and Elias asleep sitting up against the engine housing with his head tipped back and his mouth open, that she went below to check the wedges one more time and found, in the repair kit where it had no business being, a book.

It was a waterproof logbook, canvas-bound, the kind surveyors and river pilots used, and it was tucked inside the kit's false bottom with the deliberateness of a thing planted rather than lost. Jonah knew repair kits the way priests knew scripture. She had packed this one herself, in Callao Verde, item by item, and there had been no book in it.

She stood in the lantern light with the water lapping at her boots and the rain hammering the deck above, and she turned the book over in her hands. It was heavy with use. The cover was stained with river water long dried, and on the flyleaf, in a small precise hand, in ink that had survived its share of weather:

*N. Wren. Field survey — upper basin. Property of the expedition of Dr. I. Sefu.*

*N. Wren.*

Nadia Wren. Elias's sister. The cartographer.

The logbook sat in Jonah's hands like a stone, like a live coal, like a question with teeth. The fixer had put it there. The fixer who'd paid triple, who'd smiled too easily, who'd wanted this boat — this exact boat, this exact route — upriver in monsoon season. He had planted a dead-or-missing woman's logbook aboard, in a place only someone doing emergency repairs would find, at the hour only someone doing emergency repairs would be awake.

Above her, Elias slept. Below her, the river rose. In her hands, the water-stained record of a woman who had charted channels no one else had ever charted, and who had, eleven days ago, gone silent in the flooded country where those channels led.

Jonah closed her hand around the logbook, and the lightning walked the ridgeline, and somewhere upstream, the river carried her toward the answer whether she was ready for it or not.
