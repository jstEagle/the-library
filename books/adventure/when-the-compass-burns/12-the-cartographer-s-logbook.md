## 12. The Cartographer's Logbook

The rain came in the night like a debt being collected.

Elias heard it first as a change in the sound of the river — a hiss beneath the hiss, a whisper beneath the whisper — and woke in the narrow berth below decks with his heart already going too fast, the way it had since the relay station, since the operator had read out the name *Corvane Applied Resources* in his flat, bored voice and Elias had felt the floor of the world tilt a half-degree and say nothing.

He lay in the dark and listened to the monsoon arrive eleven days early.

It was not rain the way he had known rain in the north, in cities, in gutters and downspouts. This was a total event. It fell on the Marisol's deck like fists on a drumhead, fell on the river until the river's voice changed key, fell on the jungle on both banks until the whole world was one wet roaring animal. Lightning lit the cabin in silent white flashes, and in each flash Elias could see the map he'd pinned above the bunk — the expedition's route, inked in his own careful hand — hanging there like an accusation.

Nadia had drawn this river once. Not this exact river — she'd drawn a hundred rivers, dozens, he'd lost count — but she had drawn *rivers*, and she had drawn them the way other people wrote love letters. He remembered her at the kitchen table in the apartment in Puerto Almendras, hunched over a drafting board with a lamp clamped to its edge, her hair tied back with whatever was nearest — a pencil, a shoelace, once a strip of bacon rind she'd forgotten about until he pointed it out and she'd laughed until she cried. She drew rivers from soundings, from satellite passes, from the field notes of pilots and dredge captains and the occasional smuggler she charmed with her terrible Spanish and her absolute, disarming honesty. *A river is a sentence*, she used to say. *Most people only read the words. I want to read the grammar.*

He had told Jonah Vasquez that his sister was dead. That was the lie he'd boarded with, the fare he'd paid. It was easier than the truth, which was that Nadia was missing — eleven days dark, two missed check-ins, a last transmission that the relay operator described, when Elias pressed him with cash, as *garbled, something about a terrace, something about water where there shouldn't be* — and that missing, Elias had learned, was a country with no borders, a country you could wander for the rest of your life without ever being told whether to grieve or hope.

Dead was a closed door. Missing was a door that stood open on darkness, and Elias Wren had been walking through it for eleven days with his hands out in front of him.

The rain kept falling. Somewhere above him, on the deck, Jonah was awake — he could tell by the rhythm of her footsteps, that unhurried pacing she did in bad weather, checking lines he had already checked, checking them again because checking was the only prayer she knew. He had watched her for a week now, the way you watch a lock you intend to pick, and what he had learned was this: Jonah Vasquez moved through the world like a woman walking on a frozen river, testing every step, listening always for the crack beneath the ice. She trusted water more than people. She trusted rope more than promises. And she had a way of looking at Elias — sidelong, at the galley table, over the engine hatch — that said she had not believed one word of his grieving-brother routine and was keeping him aboard anyway, for reasons she hadn't yet shared, and that frightened him more than the pirates had.

He got up, dressed in the dark, and went to help her bail.

* * *

They found the leak at two in the morning, and it was Jonah who found it, because of course it was. Elias had been bailing by lamplight in the aft bilge, passing the dented aluminum scoop back and forth with a rhythm that had become almost meditative — fill, lift, pour, fill — while the river climbed audibly up the outside of the hull, when Jonah dropped down through the hatch with her hair plastered flat and her oilskin shedding water like a dog.

"Move your light," she said, and knelt, and put her palm flat against the planking below the waterline the way a doctor puts a palm on a fevered chest.

Elias had watched her do this before, in the first days, when he'd still been performing helplessness. She read the hull by touch. She'd told him once — one of the few unprompted sentences she'd ever given him, out on the open water with the drowned mission church falling away behind them — that a boat talks to you before it screams. *The Marisol's been talking for years*, she'd said. *I just have to keep the conversation ahead of the shouting.* He had filed the sentence away as evidence of something, though of what he still wasn't sure. Love. Guilt. Both wearing the same coat.

"Here," she said. "Seam's weeping. Not a breach — a weep. She does this when the river's high and the wood swells wrong." Her hand moved along the seam, tracing it, and then stopped. "Hand me the kit."

The repair kit lived in a locker beneath the aft bench — a dented green ammunition box, older than Elias, stenciled with letters in a language he didn't read, packed with caulking cotton, oakum, a lump of pitch, a mallet with a cracked handle, and a roll of waxed canvas patches. Jonah had opened it in front of him twice before, and both times her hands had gone through the same sequence, quick and unthinking, the way a person's hands go through the sequence of a lock combination they've turned ten thousand times.

This time the sequence broke.

Elias saw it happen. Saw her hand stop over the oakum, saw her fingers go still in the lamplight, saw her head tilt by a degree — the smallest possible angle, the angle of a woman listening for a crack under ice.

"What," Elias said.

She didn't answer. She lifted out the roll of waxed canvas patches, and beneath it, wedged flat against the bottom of the locker where no flat thing had any business being, was a book.

It was wrapped in oilcloth, tied with a length of rubber seal-cord, the kind of wrapping you'd use for something you expected to drown. Jonah held it in both hands and did not open it. The lamp hissed. The rain hammered. In the bilge below them the river breathed against the hull, patient, enormous, indifferent.

"That's not yours," Elias said, and heard his own voice come out wrong — too fast, too high.

"No," Jonah said. "It's not."

She turned it over once, slowly, the way she did everything — as if the world were a river and everything in it might shift its weight and ground her. The oilcloth was tied with a knot Elias knew. A surveyor's hitch, double-locked, the knot Nadia used on her instrument cases, on her tent guylines, on the ribbon she'd once tied around his twenty-third birthday present because she said a gift without a proper knot was a gift with an apology already in it.

His mouth went dry. "Open it."

Jonah looked at him then — a long, level look, lamplight and shadow, the look of a woman recalculating a chart. "You know what it is," she said. Not a question.

"I don't know how it's on my boat."

"Your boat." She almost smiled. Almost. "Careful, Wren."

She untied the knot. It came free the way a well-tied knot comes free when the right hands find it — instantly, completely, as if it had only ever been waiting.

The book inside was a waterproof field log, the kind survey expeditions used, hard-bound in yellow plastic gone soft and gray with age and handling, its corners rounded by pockets and packs. There was no label on the cover. There didn't need to be. Elias knew that book the way he knew his own hands. He had bought it for her — Puerto Almendras, the marine supply shop off the fish market, three years ago, a birthday, *you can't keep writing soundings on the backs of receipts, Nadia, it's going to kill you* — and she had laughed and said *something's going to kill me, might as well be good stationery.*

The name stamped inside the front cover, in the shop's cheap gold foil, half worn away, read: **N. WREN — PROPERTY OF — IF FOUND, RETURN TO THE RIVER.**

The river had returned it. Into a locker on a boat he had begged his way aboard, three hundred kilometers from anywhere Nadia had ever been supposed to be.

"Jonah," he said, and couldn't finish.

"I know," she said.

* * *

She opened it on the galley table, weighted the corners with two mugs and the mallet, and turned the lamp up as far as it would go. The rain had settled from assault into siege, a steady roaring fall, and the boat creaked around them like a living thing in its sleep.

The early pages were old. Elias knew them — or knew their kind. Nadia's hand was unmistakable: a small, fast, left-slanting script that looked like it was trying to outrun the pen, annotated in three colors of ink, with soundings and channel notes and margin sketches of birds and boat rudders and, on one page, a detailed and unflattering drawing of a customs official in Barranca Chica labeled *THIS MAN HAS NEVER ONCE LOOKED AT A RIVER.* Elias had forgotten that drawing. Seeing it now, under Jonah's lamp, on Jonah's boat, he had to put a hand flat on the table to keep the world from tilting.

She was alive when she wrote these. She was alive, and laughing, and drawing rude pictures of bureaucrats, and then the pages turned and the ink changed and she was on *this* river, *this* expedition, and the handwriting tightened the way it always tightened when she was excited — the letters leaning harder, the margins filling with arrows.

"Read the dates," Jonah said quietly.

Elias read the dates. The last entries were eleven days old. Two days before the expedition went dark.

He read. And as he read, the rain and the river and the creak of the Marisol all fell away, and there was only the lamp and the page and his sister's voice coming up off the paper like heat off a stone.

*Day 31. Sefu has found it. I don't have the words for what she's found and I'm a cartographer, words are supposed to be my problem. There is a structure under the flood plain at the bend where the old charts show nothing — nothing, the colonial surveys just drew a blank and wrote "marsh." It's not marsh. It's a terrace complex, stepped, seven levels, and the lowest level is a vault. Stone doors. Seals. Pre-colonial, pre-everything. Sefu cried. I have never seen her cry. Tomas cried too, and he pretends he didn't.*

*Day 33. The records in the vault — we've only opened the antechamber — are a survey. An old survey, older than any map I've ever touched. Every tributary. Every hidden channel. Every claim, every grave, every buried thing in this whole basin, drawn by people who read the river the way I've spent my whole life trying to learn to. I stood in that room and understood that I have been an amateur my entire life.*

*Day 34. Ravel asked me today how long the vault survey would take to copy. Asked it twice. Asked it in a way that wasn't a question. I don't like the way Ravel asks things.*

*Day 35. We are moving camp up to the Sunken Terrace proper. Sefu wants the vault mapped before the peak flood. The river is going to take the lower levels — she knows it, she's racing it. Priya is scared. I told her scared is just the body reading a chart the mind hasn't finished drawing. She almost smiled. Good kid.*

*Day 36. Boat came upriver today. Not ours. No flag, tarps over the guns, and the man running it wore a Corvane badge on a lanyard like he'd forgotten to take it off. Sefu told them we're a hydrology survey. The man smiled at me too long. I moved the vault index into my kit. If anything happens to us, whoever finds this should know: the index is the key. The vault without the index is a library with no catalogue. The index without the vault is worth more than anyone on this river should be able to afford.*

*Day 36, later. I don't trust the water anymore. That's a stupid thing for me to write, but the river's wrong here. It rises where it shouldn't. It remembers. Sefu says the old builders engineered the flood plain — that the flooding isn't weather, it's maintenance. That the water is doing what it was built to do. I keep thinking about what it was built to drown.*

The last entry was half a page. The ink was different — a hasty pen, pressed hard.

*Day 37. Ravel is gone. Took the survey skiff before dawn. Sefu's sealed the vault. We're going dark — no more radio, no more transmissions, she says anything we broadcast tells them where we are. If I can, I'll get this log out. There are channels here only I've charted. If I can reach the water, I can reach anyone.*

The page after that was blank. The pages after that were blank. The book's spine was cracked at the last written page, as if it had been shut in a hurry, in the dark, by someone whose hands were shaking.

Elias became aware that he was standing. He didn't remember standing. The galley seemed very small and the lamp seemed very bright and his sister's last written words — *if I can reach the water, I can reach anyone* — seemed to be written on the inside of his own skull.

"She made it to the water," he said. "She must have. The log — someone carried it out—"

"Someone carried it onto my boat," Jonah said.

And there it was. The thing he had been circling for a week, the thing that had been sitting in the bilge of his own suspicion like a body in dark water, finally surfacing into the lamplight.

He looked at her. She looked at him. The rain hammered the deck overhead, and the river climbed, and the Marisol groaned at her moorings like a woman turning in her sleep.

"The repair kit," Elias said slowly. "You said it's always in the same place. You said you could open it blind."

"I can."

"When was the last time you opened it before tonight?"

Jonah's jaw worked. For a long moment she said nothing, and he watched her decide — watched the calculation move behind her eyes the way a fish moves under ice, fast and shadowed and gone. He had learned to read her a little in a week. Not much. A sounding here and there. But he knew what he was watching now: a woman discovering that the ice she'd been walking on had been thin all along.

"Callao Verde," she said at last. "The night before we cast off. I restowed the kit after loading. Everything was where it should be." Her hand closed around the logbook, not hard, but completely, the way she held the wheel in current. "There was no book in that locker, Wren. I would have felt it. I *always* feel it."

"Then someone put it there after."

"On my boat. In a sealed locker. While I slept ten feet away." She said it flatly, but he heard the thing underneath the flatness, the thing that had been underneath everything since Callao Verde since the fixer with the soft voice and the triple pay: *I was played. On my own deck.*

"The fixer," Elias said. "Ostrevant."

"Ostrevant," Jonah agreed.

They stood in the lamplight and looked at each other, and Elias understood, in one cold dropping sensation, that they had both been carrying the same suspicion and had each been waiting for the other to say it first. The fixer had hired Jonah — triple pay, a season no sane pilot would run, a cargo manifest that made no sense for a resupply run. The fixer had let Elias aboard without a single question about his papers, had taken his cash with a smile that had bothered Elias at the time and now curdled in his memory. And somewhere between the dock and the dark, the fixer — or someone working for the fixer, or someone working against the fixer, and Elias no longer had any faith in his ability to tell those apart — had planted Nadia's logbook in the one place on the boat Jonah would only open in an emergency.

*If I can reach the water, I can reach anyone.*

Nadia hadn't just been trying to get the log out. She had been trying to get it to *someone*. And someone had decided that someone was Jonah Vasquez — or had decided that Jonah Vasquez was the best way to get the log to *them*.

"She chose you," Elias said. "Or someone chose you for her. Either way—" He stopped. His voice had come apart. He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes and found them wet and hated it. "Eleven days. She was alive eleven days ago. She was scared and she was brilliant and she was writing about Priya almost smiling, and Ravel was already—"

"Ravel," Jonah said. "The one who took the skiff. You know that name?"

"No." He said it too fast and saw her hear it. He made himself slow down. He had lied to this woman every day for a week, small lies, structural lies, the lies you build a bridge from when you can't build it any other way, and he had told himself they were necessary lies, grieving-brother lies, and now Nadia's handwriting was on the table between them and the lies felt less like a bridge and more like a leak. "I don't know the name. But I know the shape of it. There was a man who used to come to Nadia's talks. A surveyor type. I never got his name. He asked too many questions about her filing systems." It was true, every word of it, and it was also a deflection, and Jonah's eyes told him she knew both things, and filed them, the way she filed everything, in the locker where she kept what she'd deal with later.

The lamp flickered. Somewhere above them, thunder rolled down the river valley like a barrel dropped on stone, and the rain redoubled, and Jonah moved — abruptly, all business, captain again — to check the bilge, to check the seam, to put her palm against the hull and listen to the conversation. But when she straightened up, she was still holding the logbook, and she looked at it for a long moment with an expression Elias had never seen on her face.

It looked, he thought, like recognition. Like a woman reading a letter she'd been sending to herself for years without knowing the address.

"Your sister wrote that the river remembers," Jonah said. "What people want forgotten."

"Yes."

"I've been running from something for eleven years," Jonah said, "and I chose this river because I thought it was the one river that didn't know me." She turned the logbook over in her hands. "Turns out I picked the one river that remembers everything."

Elias didn't know what to say to that. He was not sure it had been said to him. He was not sure it had been said to anyone.

What he knew was this: the logbook was a message, and messages had senders, and senders had reasons, and somewhere downriver in the drowned dark of Callao Verde a soft-voiced man was sitting in an office with rain on his windows, having arranged all of this — the boat, the pilot, the stranger, the season — with the patience of a man setting a table for guests he intended to charge for the meal. And what Elias knew after that was simpler and worse: the last place Nadia had written about was the Sunken Terrace, and the last thing she had written was *if I can reach the water, I can reach anyone*, and the water had reached Jonah Vasquez, and Jonah Vasquez was holding the book, and the river outside was rising faster than any season on record, closing channels, drowning charts, erasing the grammar Nadia had given her life to reading.

The window was closing. Whatever window Nadia had charted, whatever channel she had meant to escape through, whatever chance there was that a woman who read rivers like sentences was still alive at the far end of one — it was closing with the flood.

"We go on," Elias said.

Jonah looked at him across the galley, across the lamp, across her sister's handwriting, and her face was the frozen river itself — still, load-bearing, one crack away from everything.

"You don't give the orders on my boat," she said.

"No," Elias said. "But you're going to say yes."

She held his gaze for three full seconds — long enough for the lamp to flicker, long enough for the rain to fill the silence, long enough for him to understand that whatever came next between them, it would come from the place where she kept the things she'd deal with later.

"Anchor's down till the storm breaks," Jonah said. "Then we talk. You and me. Everything, Wren. No more soundings." She set the logbook down on the table between them, square in the lamplight, like a card played face-up. "Because I've just learned someone's been dealing cards on my boat, and I intend to know every hand that's been played — starting with yours."
