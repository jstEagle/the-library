## 13. Confrontation at Anchor

The anchor went down in a bend of the river where the current slackened against a clay bluff hung with strangler figs, and Jonah chose it for exactly that reason: no one came to a place like this unless they were hiding, and she wanted two hours of dark before they ran the flooded narrows at dawn.

The rain had stopped an hour ago. The whole world dripped. Water fell from the canopy in fat, deliberate drops that struck the Marisol's deck like slow applause, and the river itself had gone the color of strong tea, carrying past them — at a speed that made her stomach tighten — branches, leaves, a dead capybara bloated to the size of a barrel, all of it moving downstream toward the sea with the terrible patience of things that know they will arrive.

Jonah stood at the rail with a mug of coffee gone cold in her hand and watched Elias Wren come out of the cabin.

He'd been below for most of the evening, supposedly reading. She knew better. She'd heard him moving through the hold on quiet feet, heard the false panel behind the spare fuel drums open and close twice. He thought she was asleep. He thought a lot of things about her, and every one of them was wrong, and it was time — three days after finding Nadia's logbook sewn into the leak-repair kit like a letter addressed to someone else's life — to find out how wrong.

"You missed dinner," she said.

"I ate earlier." He leaned on the rail a meter away, close enough to talk over the drip of the trees, far enough to pretend this was casual. "How long do we have before the narrows?"

"Depends on the rain upstream." She turned the mug in her hands. "Depends on a lot of things."

"Such as?"

"Such as what else you've been doing in my hold."

She said it evenly, without heat, the way you'd note a change in the weather, and she watched it land. Watched his shoulders go still in that particular way stillness works on men who are used to being believed. The dripping filled the silence between them, drop by drop by drop.

"I was looking for dry rope," he said.

"There's dry rope in the cockpit locker."

"I didn't want to wake you."

"I wasn't sleeping." She set the mug down on the rail with a small ceramic click. "I haven't slept since I found my repair kit full of your sister's handwriting. So let's not spend the night pretending we're both bad liars when we're both so very good at it."

Elias looked out at the water. The bluff across the channel was black against a sky the color of wet slate, and somewhere back in the flooded forest a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling, once, and then nothing.

"You think I planted it," he said finally.

"I know somebody did. And the fixer in Callao Verde doesn't have hands. He has money and other people's hands." She kept her voice low and level, because shouting would feel too much like feeling, and feeling was cargo she did not carry. "So either you put it there, or you know who did, or you've known since the dock that this whole contract smells like bait — and you climbed aboard anyway and let me steer us upriver into it."

"And if I say none of those things?"

"Then you'll say something else, and I'll stand here listening to it, and we'll both be wasting the last calm night we might get." She picked the mug back up. Drank the cold coffee anyway. It tasted like the bottom of the pot, like burnt patience. "Talk, Elias. Or swim. Those are the options at this anchorage."

* * *

He almost laughed. That surprised him — the laugh rising in him now, here, at the end of everything he'd pretended to be — and he strangled it, because laughing at Jonah Vasquez when she had that look on her face would be like laughing at a storm front.

But part of him wanted to laugh, because she was right, and because some exhausted corner of him had been waiting eleven days for someone to catch him.

"The rope thing was stupid," he said. "You're right. I was in the hold."

"What were you looking for?"

"Honestly? You." He turned to face her fully, and the rain-drip ticked on around them like a clock counting down something neither of them had named yet. "Not — not your body, not your secrets in the way you mean. I've been trying to figure out who you are. Because the fixer told me things about you, Jonah, and none of them added up, and I needed to know which version was true before I decided whether to trust you with the only thing I have left."

"Which is?"

"Nadia." His sister's name came out of him rough, like a stone pulled from a riverbed. "Finding her. Or finding what happened to her. That's all I'm carrying. Everything else is ballast."

"And the logbook."

"I didn't plant it." He held her eyes and made himself not flinch, because flinching would be the end of this conversation and possibly the end of him standing on this deck. "But I knew it existed. Nadia sent me half a journal page eight months ago, from a relay station, before the expedition went dark. Just one page. A sketch of a terrace structure under floodwater, and one line: *If anything happens, find the pilot who never stays.*"

The words went into the dripping air between them and hung there.

"She described you," Elias said quietly. "Riverboat captain. Runs supplies nobody else will run. Leaves towns before anyone learns her name. Nadia said —" his voice caught, and he pushed through it — "she said you were the only person on this river who could get to them without being seen, because you were already invisible. She said you'd built yourself into a ghost, and ghosts don't show up on manifests."

Jonah said nothing. The coffee had gone cold all the way through her, colder than the mug.

"So when the fixer offered me passage on the *Marisol*," Elias went on, "I took it. And then I started asking questions he didn't want asked, and I found out he wasn't just hiring you. He'd hired another boat two weeks earlier — a fast one, shallow draft, company money behind it — and that boat went upriver and came back empty, and the men on it wouldn't talk. And I realized he wasn't trying to rescue the expedition." He watched her face as he said it, watching for the crack. "He was using you to find it. You're the needle, Jonah. He's not paying you to deliver supplies. He's paying you to lead whoever comes next straight to Sefu's ruins — because you'll run any channel, take any risk, and you won't stop to ask why until it's too late."

"That's a story," Jonah said. Her voice was flat as the tea-colored water. "That's a story you tell a woman so she keeps steering north while you do whatever you actually came to do."

"It's a story I can prove. There's a man at the relay station — Ovidio — ask him about the second boat. Ask him who signed its fuel chit." He stepped closer, and she didn't step back, and that small refusal felt like the most important thing that had happened to him in months. "Or don't. Run me off at the next village and steer on alone, and in four days you can find out for yourself whose trap you're sailing into. But I am telling you, on my sister's grave, which I have not even been able to visit because I don't know where it is — I did not plant that book. Someone wanted *you* to find it. Someone wanted you angry at me, or loyal to the mission, or both. Someone is playing this river like a chessboard and we are both pieces, and I would rather be a piece standing next to you than a piece alone."

The bird called again from the drowned forest. Hinge-voice. Once. Silence.

Jonah looked at him for a long time, and Elias understood, in that look, that he was seeing himself weighed — truly weighed, cargo on a scale, jettisonable — and that the terrifying thing was not that she might throw him over. It was that she was good enough at this to make the weighing look like nothing at all.

* * *

Jonah walked away from him.

Not far. Four steps aft, to the wheelhouse door, where she stood with her back to him and her hand flat against the wet wood, and tried to think the way she thought about channels: fast, cold, geometric. Where does the deep water run. What breaks on the surface. What waits underneath.

A pilot who never stays. Eight months ago a woman she'd never met had written those words down and mailed them downstream like a message in a bottle, and the bottle had washed up in Callao Verde in the hands of a fixer with soft palms and triple-rate money, and Jonah had taken the contract knowing — *knowing*, she had told herself it was instinct but it had been knowledge, the animal kind, the kind that lives in the gut below all reasoning — that the job was wrong. Too much pay. Too much urgency. Too smooth a story about radio silence.

She'd taken it anyway. Why?

Because the money was real. Because the route was hard. Because a woman named Imara Sefu had gone silent in monsoon country and some buried part of Jonah had wanted — needed — to believe that going silent was survivable, that people who disappeared into the green could be found and carried home, that running was not the same as drowning.

And because, God help her, some smaller and uglier part had wanted to be the needle. Wanted to matter to the map. Wanted the river to need her the way she needed it.

"You suspected the fixer from the start," she said to the wheelhouse door. "You said it just now like a confession, but it isn't. You boarded my boat knowing the contract was bait, and you let me sail into it, because you needed a pilot who could lie to herself. Isn't that what you said? Needed. Like I'm a tool with a hull number."

"I said it badly—"

"You said it exactly right, that's the problem." She turned around. In the dark her face was mostly shadow and one wet highlight along the cheekbone, and her voice stayed level in a way that Elias found worse than screaming. "Every mile we've come, I told myself I was watching you. Testing you. Sandbar country — I watched how you worked. The rapids — I watched whether you panicked. The burned village — I watched your face when you saw those crates. And the whole time, you were watching me back, taking your own soundings, deciding how much truth I could carry. We weren't crew, Elias. We were two people sounding each other's channels and charting nothing."

"If I'd told you at the dock—"

"At the dock you'd have been a stranger with a story and no proof, and I'd have put you ashore, and you knew it. So you waited. You let me commit. You let me burn fuel and fresh water and take fire in the narrows on a contract you believed was poisoned." She shook her head slowly. "You know what the difference is between you and Ravel-type men, the ones who sell rivers? Nothing I can see from here. Both of you decide what other people are allowed to know. Both of you call it protection."

The word landed on him like a slap, and he deserved it, and he stood there and took it because taking it was the only honest thing left in his inventory.

"You're right," he said.

"Don't agree with me. It makes it worse."

"You're *right*, Jonah." His voice cracked on the second word and he let it. "I used you. I told myself it was different because my reasons were grief instead of greed, but the mechanics are identical — I saw a woman who could do what I couldn't, and I maneuvered her, and I told myself I'd earn the truth later. Later. Always later." He laughed, and this time it came out broken. "Nadia used to say I was a man who'd apologize his way through any wall. She said it like it was charming. It's not charming. It's just cowardice with good manners."

The rain started again — fine, warm, almost invisible, more mist than water, beading on the rails and running down the wheelhouse glass in slow silver threads. Jonah watched it rather than him. Watching water was easier. Water never apologized and never needed to.

"My rule," she said, "is that I never stay longer than a job requires. You know that. Everyone on this river knows it. Do you know why?"

"No."

"Neither do I. Not entirely. There's a town —" she stopped. Started again, quieter. "There's a town I left burning behind me, years ago. I've told myself a dozen versions of why. None of them survive daylight. But I learned something on the way out of that fire, and the lesson was: the moment you care about a place, someone can hurt you with it. The moment you're *needed*, someone can use you. So I stopped being needed. I became a ghost, like your sister said. Ghosts don't burn."

Elias stood very still. He understood he was being handed something fragile, and that the handing cost her more than the finding of the logbook had cost him, and that if he reached for it wrong it would vanish.

"And now?" he said carefully.

"Now I'm anchored in a channel I chose, holding cargo I didn't load, working for a man who admits he played me, on a river that's rising faster than any season on record." She almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, thin as wire. "And the worst part, Elias — the part I'll deny if you ever repeat it — is that I don't want to cut you loose. I want to be angry enough to cut you loose, because anger is simple and I'm good at simple. But you knew Nadia. You have half a page of her handwriting. You're the closest thing on this earth to the woman who drew the map we're following, and I—" the wire-smile faltered— "I have spent eleven days learning to read her markers like scripture, and I don't want to do the rest of it alone. That's the truth. It's ugly. Take it or leave it."

* * *

For a moment neither of them moved, and the mist-rain came down through the dark like the river trying to reach the sky, and Elias felt the whole fragile architecture of the night balanced on what he said next.

He crossed the deck. Stopped an arm's length away — close enough to be trusted, far enough to be believed.

"Then here's mine," he said. "No more later. No more maneuvering. From this minute, everything I know, you know, even the parts that make me look worse. Starting with the worst one." He breathed. "One of the names the corporate men were asking about at the relay station — the operator read it off his visitor log — was a name I recognized. A man who worked with Nadia's first survey team, years ago. A man she trusted. If he's involved with the company, then Nadia may not have been lost by accident. She may have been sold. And I sat on that information for six days because telling you meant admitting I'd been filtering everything, and I was afraid of exactly this conversation."

Jonah studied him through the mist. Looking for the seam in the lie, the tell, the flicker. She was very good at finding seams; it was how she'd survived.

There was no seam. There was just a tired man soaked in warm rain, holding out the last of his honesty like a man holding out his last clean shirt.

"Six days," she said.

"Six days."

"We're even, then. I took a poisoned contract with my eyes open. You sailed on it with yours half-shut." She extended her hand, and he took it, and her grip was a pilot's grip — dry, hard, brief, final. "New terms. Nobody filters. Nobody protects anybody. We run this river with the charts open, both of ours. And when we find whoever planted that logbook in my kit—"

"When we find them," Elias said, "we ask them why."

"No," Jonah said, and now the smile came back, and this one had teeth in it. "*I* ask them why. You hold the lantern. I'm done being the question. From here on, I'm the answer."

She turned to the wheelhouse, and he followed, and neither of them saw — because the mist had thickened and the night had closed like a hand — the single unlit boat that slid past the far edge of the anchorage, riding high and running dark, keeping pace with the current for one long minute before the current took it downstream toward Callao Verde, and the man aboard memorized the *Marisol*'s anchor light, and her position, and the fact that she was heading at dawn into the flooded narrows where a fast shallow-draft boat with company money behind it had already gone and come back empty.

The river rose another centimeter in the night. It always did. It was very patient, and it remembered everything.
