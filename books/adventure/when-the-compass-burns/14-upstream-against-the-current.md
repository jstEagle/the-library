## 14. Upstream Against the Current

The river had turned against them somewhere in the night.

Jonah felt it before she saw it — a change in the Marisol's voice, the engine laboring a half-beat lower than it should, the hull shuddering against water that no longer parted for her but pushed back, sullen and heavy, like a shoulder turned against an old friend. By dawn the current was running brown and fast, carrying whole trees past them sideways, their roots clawing at the air like drowned men reaching for the bank.

"Monsoon's early," Elias said from the doorway of the wheelhouse. He'd slept badly — she could tell by the crease the bench had left in his cheek, by the way he held his coffee mug with both hands as if it might bolt. "The fixer said we'd have another week."

"The fixer said a lot of things." Jonah eased the throttle forward a notch and listened to the engine answer with a sound she didn't love. "Rivers don't read contracts."

She had been running this stretch since before Elias Wren was old enough to shave, back when the channel ran clean and green between walls of bamboo, when you could count the sandbars by heart and the worst thing on the water was a drunk fisherman in a leaky pirogue. Now the banks had vanished. The forest stood knee-deep in its own reflection, and where yesterday there had been a village landing — three posts and a tin roof she'd tied up to a dozen times over the years — there was only brown water moving with the patience of something that intended to outlast everything it touched.

"How far?" Elias asked.

"To the next marker Nadia charted? Six hours. If the engine holds." She said it lightly, the way you say things you don't want the river to overhear. Rivers were superstitious that way. So was she.

He came to stand beside her at the wheel, close enough that she caught woodsmoke and cheap soap under the smell of rain. He didn't touch anything. That was one thing she'd learned about him in nine days of shared deck — he kept his hands off her boat the way a man keeps his hands off another man's wife, respectful and a little hungry. He watched the water instead, tracking the drift of debris, reading the surface the way she'd taught him without meaning to: the smooth glassy tongue where the current ran hardest, the boil and seam where two flows argued, the V of a submerged snag pointing downstream like an accusation.

"You're getting good at that," she said, and immediately wished she hadn't. Praise was cargo she wasn't used to carrying. It sat wrong in the hold.

"I had a good teacher." He glanced at her sidelong. "You never talk about how you learned."

"There's not much to tell."

"That's what people say when there's too much."

Jonah adjusted the wheel a degree, two degrees, feeling the rudder bite into water that fought every inch. Below them, through the deck plates, she could hear the slow tick-tick-tick of the bilge pump doing its work — the leak they'd patched in the mangrove cove holding, but only the way a promise holds when both parties are tired. She let the silence stretch until it should have become awkward, and then stretched it further, because Elias Wren was patient and she wanted to see if his patience had a bottom.

It did. About forty seconds' worth.

"My father ran barges," he said finally. "Coal, on the northern rivers. I grew up thinking water was just a road that moved."

"This isn't a road."

"No." He looked down at the flood rolling past, carrying a dead pig, half a roof, a flowering branch torn from somewhere upstream where someone's garden was now part of the river's estate. "This is more like a country. One that changes its borders every hour."

Jonah almost smiled. Almost. "Your sister would have liked that. A country that moves."

Something crossed his face — grief, or the shape grief leaves behind after it's passed through, like a nail hole in a wall. "She talked about you, you know. In her letters. 'My captain,' she called you. The one who never stays anywhere long enough to be found twice."

"She got that right."

"Did she get anything else right?"

Jonah didn't answer. The engine answered for her, surging as the bow climbed a swell of current and slammed down the other side hard enough to rattle the teacups in their rack. Rain began again, fine and needling, and she switched on the wiper that only worked on the left side of the glass, so the world came to her in halves — clear, smeared, clear, smeared — which was, she thought, about the honest way to see anything.

* * *

They took the first set of rapids at noon.

Nadia's chart showed them as a dotted line through a channel that, in dry season, was probably a staircase of gravel bars; now it was a single churning throat of white water funneling between two ridges that the flood had turned into islands. Jonah stood at the wheel with her feet planted wide and her weight forward, the way she always did when the boat became less a vessel than an argument she was having with physics, and she talked to the Marisol under her breath — *come on, girl, come on, that's it, that's it* — while Elias braced himself against the chart table and watched her face instead of the water.

Later he told her that watching her run those rapids was like watching someone pray in a language only she spoke. She told him it was more like arguing with a landlord. Both of them were lying a little.

The channel bent left where the chart said it would bend left, and dropped where the chart said it would drop, and for eleven seconds the Marisol surfed down a standing wave with her stern kicked up and the propeller singing a note Jonah had only heard twice before and hoped never to hear again. Then they were through, into slack water above a flooded oxbow, and the engine settled back into its laboring rhythm, and the rain came down harder, as if the sky had been waiting to see whether they'd make it before committing fully to drowning them.

Elias exhaled. She realized he'd been holding his breath the whole way down.

"You could have warned me," he said.

"If I'd warned you, you'd have done something stupid like grab the wheel."

"Would I?"

"Everyone does. First time." She throttled back to let the engine cool. Steam curled off the exhaust manifold where the rain hit it, ghosting up around the wheelhouse windows. "There's a pool ahead. We'll anchor an hour, let the current ease. Flood peaks mid-afternoon, drops overnight. You learn the rhythm after enough seasons."

"And how many is enough?"

She looked at him. He was wringing out his shirt hem, watching her with that careful attention he thought she didn't notice — the attention of a man assembling a person out of fragments, the way she imagined he must once have assembled maps out of his sister's scattered survey notes.

"Why do you want to know me, Elias?" she asked. "Really. Not the brother act. Not the cash. What is it you think you'll find?"

He didn't flinch, which she gave him credit for. Most men flinched.

"Maybe the same thing you're afraid I'll find," he said.

* * *

They anchored in the lee of a drowned rubber plantation, the trees standing in rows to their shoulders, their tapping scars underwater, their crowns still stubbornly green. The current here eddied backward along the bank, lazy and confused, carrying leaves upstream in little spirals as if the river had forgotten which direction it meant to go. Jonah cooked rice and dried fish on the deck stove under an awning, and Elias dried the dishes, and neither of them spoke for a long time, because some silences are doors and some are walls and you don't know which until you lean on one.

She leaned on this one.

"There was a town," she said, to the rice pot, because it was easier than saying it to his face. "Up on the upper reaches. San Rafael de las Aguas, if you can believe anyone named it that. Saint Raphael of the Waters. A river town with a church and a customs house and four hundred people who knew my mother's cooking and my father's temper and my business before I did."

Elias went very still, the way you go still around a wild animal that has decided, for reasons of its own, to come near the fire.

"I was nineteen. My father had just died — the Marisol was his, or half his, the other half owed to a man named Aurelio Caz who owned the fuel depot and the warehouse and, as it turned out, most of the town's honesty." She stirred the pot though it didn't need stirring. "Aurelio was kind to me. That's the part I keep coming back to, all these years. He was *kind*. Sat with me after the funeral. Taught me how the freight contracts worked. Told me a woman alone with a boat in that country needed friends, and he was going to be one."

The rain drummed on the awning. Somewhere in the flooded plantation, a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"What I didn't know — what nobody knew — was that Aurelio was selling the river. Not cargo. The river itself. There was a mining concern coming up from the coast, and they wanted to know everything: where the channels shifted, where the villages drew their water, which headmen could be bought, which couldn't. And Aurelio sold them surveys. Sold them names. Sold them my father's old charts, marked up in his own hand." Her jaw tightened. "And when the company came, they didn't come gently. They came with permits and police and a payroll, and when the town wouldn't sign, they came with fire."

"You were there," Elias said quietly. Not a question.

"I was two days downstream, running a load, when it burned. I came back upriver and there was smoke standing over the bend like a tree that had grown overnight, and the church was gone, and the customs house was gone, and thirty-one people were gone with them, and Aurelio Caz was standing on his dock with the company men, shaking hands." She looked up at last. "I could have run him down with the boat. I thought about it. God knows I thought about it. But there were children on that dock too, and I was nineteen and a coward, so I turned the Marisol around and I ran. And I've been turning her around ever since."

The pot steamed between them. Elias didn't reach for her hand, didn't offer comfort in words, and she understood that this too was a kind of kindness — the kindness of a man who knew what it was to carry a name you couldn't put down.

"Nadia found out about San Rafael," he said. "Didn't she. That's why you took this job. Not triple pay. The name Sefu on the manifest, and the route going up past—"

"Past the turnoff for the upper reaches. Yes." Jonah laughed, short and bitter, a sound like the engine misfiring. "Eleven years of running, and the river bends around and hands me the same town twice. That's the joke of it, Elias. You can outrun a place. You can't outrun a watershed. Everything flows downhill eventually. Even the past."

* * *

They weighed anchor at dusk, when the current had eased by a hand's breadth, and pushed on into the purple dark with the running lights burning low and the engine straining against water that never stopped pushing back. Jonah took the first watch. Elias sat with her rather than sleeping, wrapped in a blanket, and she found she didn't mind. That frightened her more than the pirates had.

"Say it again," he said, after an hour. "The part about the watershed."

"Why?"

"Because I've been carrying something too. About Nadia. About why I really think she stopped sending letters. And I figure if you can jettison yours, I can jettison mine." He pulled the blanket tighter. "Ballast. Right? You said a boat runs truer light."

"I said that about actual ballast."

"Same principle."

Jonah watched the black water slide past, threaded here and there with the pale ghosts of drowned trees, and thought about how a confession works — how it's not relief, exactly, not the way the songs promise. It's more like cutting loose a crate you've dragged so long you've forgotten it's cargo. You don't float higher so much as you stop listing to one side.

"Aurelio Caz is alive," she said. "Did you know that? Retired. Coastal villa. They say he goes to mass every Sunday and puts money in the plate like he's paying off a debt in installments." She turned the wheel a degree. "Some nights I chart a course south just to look at him. Some nights I get as far as plotting it. And then the river rises, or a contract comes in, or a stranger boards my boat with his dead sister's charts, and I keep moving instead. Because moving is easy. Moving is the one thing I've ever been excellent at."

"And stopping?"

"Stopping means the smoke catches up." She said it flatly, factually, the way you'd report a depth sounding. "Every town I've left, I left just ahead of it. Callao Verde will burn someday too. Everything burns, eventually. The trick is not being there when it does."

"Is that the trick?" Elias asked softly. "Or is it the trap?"

She didn't answer. Ahead, far upstream, where the channel narrowed toward the highlands and the drowned mission country began, lightning flickered inside a cloud — silent, sourceless, illuminating nothing but itself, like a memory trying to happen.

And then, below the thunder's first grumble, another sound reached them across the water. Faint. Rhythmic. Wrong.

Engines. More than one. Running dark, coming downriver fast — from the direction of the Sunken Terrace, from the direction of everything they were sailing toward.

Jonah killed the running lights and reached for the throttle, and beside her, Elias was already unfolding Nadia's chart against the wheel.
