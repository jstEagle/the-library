## 15. The Company's Wake

The rain came down like a verdict.

Elias Wren stood at the wheelhouse window and watched it hammer the Marisol's deck, turning the river into a field of silver nails. Three days upstream from the burned village, and the water had changed its character entirely — no longer brown but the color of old tea, thick with silt and broken trees, moving with a muscular impatience that made even Jonah glance at it twice. The current had doubled since morning. He could feel it in the way the boat fought, the engine laboring like a heart asked to carry too much.

"She doesn't like this," Jonah said. She wasn't looking at him; she was looking at the river, the way she always did, as if it were a person she'd once loved and now merely tolerated. "Flood current against a loaded hull. Every hour we push, we're spending her."

"Then why are we pushing?"

Jonah's jaw worked. Rain streaked the glass between them, distorting her face into something older than it was. "Because Nadia's markers say there's a cutoff channel two bends ahead that saves us half a day, and because every hour we lose is an hour the company gains." She tapped the chart spread on the table — Elias's sister's handwriting marching across it in confident, precise strokes, depth soundings and channel notes in a script he'd have known anywhere, even blindfolded, even dead. *Watch for submerged fence line at km 212*, one note read. *Villagers drove stakes to pen fish. They'll take your propeller if you don't.* A small drawing of a fish beside it, almost playful. His chest tightened around it like a fist.

"You talk about them like they're already ahead of us," he said.

"They are." Jonah adjusted the throttle a fraction, listening to the engine the way a doctor listens to lungs. "Companies don't move slow, Wren. Slow is for people who have to answer questions. Companies just arrive."

He wanted to argue. He didn't. Three days of shared watches and bailed water and cold food had taught him the shape of her silences — which ones were walls and which ones were doors. This one was a door, barely. She was afraid, and she was telling him so in the only language she allowed herself.

* * *

They ate standing up, rice and dried fish and rainwater caught in a tarp funnel, and it was during that meal that Elias heard it first — or thought he did. A sound beneath the rain. Not thunder. Thunder rolled and retreated. This was steady, rhythmic, mechanical. A pulse.

He set down his bowl. "Stop the engine."

Jonah looked up sharply. "What?"

"Just — listen."

She killed the throttle and the Marisol drifted, slapping gently against the flood, and beneath the rain Elias heard it clearly now: a low diesel thrum, arrhythmic, coming around the bend ahead. Not one engine. Two, maybe, out of sync. And threaded through it, faintly, a generator's whine — the high insect note of machinery meant to sustain people in place.

Jonah was already moving. She cut the running lights, killed the cabin lamp, and had the boat sliding toward the eastern bank before Elias finished standing. Her hands on the wheel were perfectly calm. Her eyes were not.

"Mangrove snag," she murmured, more to herself than to him. "There's a drowned stand off the point — roots like a cage. If I can get us inside it before they round the bend—"

"Who's *they*?"

"I don't know yet." The boat kissed the bank, and Jonah was over the rail with a coil of rope, hauling them into the black lattice of drowned mangrove, the branches scraping the hull like fingernails. Elias grabbed a pole and pushed where she pointed, and together they buried the Marisol in the tangle until only the wheelhouse roof showed, screened by leaves, forty meters of open water between them and the bend.

Then they waited.

Elias crouched under the gunwale, rain drumming the canopy above him, and found his hands were shaking. Not from cold. From recognition. Because he knew that diesel rhythm. He'd spent four years after Nadia disappeared working ports and permit offices and the lobbies of companies whose names were acronyms, and he knew what corporate river traffic sounded like at night: engines run rich, generators humming, everything maintained by contract rather than care. It was the sound of money that expected no resistance.

The bend gave up its secret slowly.

First came the lights — floodlamps mounted fore and aft, cutting white cones through the rain, sweeping the water with the casual arrogance of searchlights. Then the shape itself: a gunboat, forty feet if it was an inch, welded steel, twin engines, a bow ramp lowered into the water like a tongue. A second vessel sat behind it, smaller, a converted barge with a crane arm folded against its superstructure. Both flew no flag. Both needed none.

Men moved on the decks. Elias counted eight, then lost count. Dark uniforms, dark caps, rifles slung across chests with the ease of long habit. At the rail of the barge, a crane swung a crate out over the water and lowered it into the hold of the gunboat with a tenderness that told Elias everything about what was inside.

"Crates," he breathed. "They're loading crates. Out here? There's nothing out here but—"

"The ruins." Jonah's voice had gone flat and quiet, the voice she used when feeling would only be in the way. "Nadia's markers put the Sunken Terrace six kilometers upstream. That crane's been working tonight." She turned her head, and in the dark her eyes caught the distant floodlight and held it, two small hard flames. "They're not searching for your sister's expedition anymore, Wren. They're stripping the site."

* * *

The gunboat anchored midriver, broadside to the current, and the barge tied alongside it, and the two vessels became a single island of light in the drowning dark. The rain kept falling. The river kept rising — Elias could see it climbing the mangrove roots, centimeter by centimeter, patient as arithmetic.

They watched in shifts. Jonah took the first, lying flat along the gunwale with binoculars she produced from a locker Elias had never seen open. He took the second, and it was during his watch that he saw the face.

Not metaphorically. An actual face.

The man came down the gunboat's bow ramp to supervise a crate transfer — tall, fiftyish, gray at the temples, wearing a rain shell with no insignia over a uniform that had plenty. He moved like a man who had never once doubted his authority, and when a younger mercenary fumbled a strap, the man said something short and the younger man flinched in a way that Elias recognized from his own childhood, from schoolyards and from boardrooms, the flinch of someone who has learned that power is arbitrary and proximity is dangerous.

But it wasn't the cruelty that fixed Elias to the spot. It was the profile. The set of the jaw, the deep-set eyes, the way the man tilted his head when he listened — Elias had seen that head tilt in a photograph. In Nadia's apartment, years ago, pinned among her maps: a clipping from a trade journal, a man smiling at some groundbreaking, captioned with a name Elias had forgotten within the week because names meant nothing to him then.

*Voss. Regional Operations Director.*

Nadia had circled the name in red.

His mouth went dry. He lowered the binoculars and found Jonah watching him instead of the boats.

"You know him," she said. Not a question.

"I've seen his face before." The words came out of him like splinters. "In Nadia's things. She kept a file on him. Voss. Some kind of operations director — resource extraction, river concessions. I thought—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I thought he was background. Context. The kind of man you circle in red because he represents everything, not because he's *someone*."

"And now?"

"Now he's standing on a boat six kilometers from where my sister went missing," Elias said, "loading crates out of ruins she charted, and I'm hiding in a tree."

Lightning walked the ridgelines far to the west, silent, counting something. Between flashes, the gunboat's lamps swept the water again, and Elias pressed himself flat and watched the light slide over the mangrove leaves above his head like a hand searching a pocket.

"We should go back," he whispered. "Around them. Find another channel, wait them out—"

"With the river rising?" Jonah shook her head once, a small precise motion. "Every side-channel Nadia marked floods shut by tomorrow, maybe sooner. After that there's one road up this river and it runs past that boat. We slip past tonight or we don't get there at all."

"There," Elias repeated. "You keep saying *there* like you've decided what's there."

"I haven't decided anything." But her voice said otherwise, and they both let it lie.

* * *

They planned it in whispers over the chart, Jonah's finger tracing the black water while lightning counted seconds outside.

"The current's our friend and our enemy," she said. "It'll carry us past them fast — too fast to steer if I'm running the engine loud enough to fight it. So we don't fight it. We drift." Her finger moved to a point just downstream of the anchored pair. "There's a seam out there. Flood rivers have them — where two currents meet, there's a line of slack water, quiet as a hallway. Nadia marked it. Of course she marked it." Something moved through her face, quickly, gone. "We drift down that seam, engines muffled, exhaust underwater, and we're just another log in the flood. Their lamps will be pointed downstream, watching for runners coming *up*. Nobody watches for driftwood."

"And if somebody does?"

"Then we're close enough that they'll hear our hearts before our engine." She met his eyes. "I need you below. Weight low, hands on nothing. If I signal — two knocks on the deck — you kill the bilge pump and you stay absolutely still. Can you do still, Wren?"

He thought about lying. He'd been lying to her since Callao Verde, small lies stacked like sandbags, and she'd known most of them from the start — he could see that now, see it the way you see the frame behind a painting. But this question deserved the truth, or the closest thing to it he owned.

"No," he said. "But I'll do it anyway."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment that honesty had been attempted, which from Jonah Vasquez was nearly a decoration.

* * *

They cast off at the darkest hour, between moonset and the first gray suggestion of dawn, when the rain thinned to a drifting veil and the world reduced itself to sound. Jonah wrapped the exhaust stack in wet canvas and tarps until the engine's voice dropped to a mutter, a rumble felt in the soles of the feet more than heard. She ran it at bare idle, just enough steerage to keep the bow pointed down the seam, and then, at the edge of the lamplight's reach, she cut it altogether.

They drifted.

Elias lay below, in the dark hold, wedged between crates of supplies and the false panel he'd found weeks ago and never mentioned — her other life, her exit life, packed and waiting behind plywood. He put his ear against the hull and listened to the river press against it, a vast breathing thing, and above him, faint through the deck boards, he heard Jonah's footsteps move to the bow and stop. The silence afterward had a held-breath quality. The whole boat was holding its breath.

The gunboat's lamps grew. White cones swept the water downstream, raking the flood, catching debris in their beams — whole trees sailing past upright, their crowns still leafed, like drowned men walking. A floating roof. A dead pig, bloated and turning. The flood was full of the season's wreckage, and the Marisol joined it, invisible by membership.

Through a gap in the deck boards Elias could see a slice of the river, and in that slice the gunboat grew from a suggestion to a fact. He saw the weld lines in her hull. He saw a sentry at the rail, rifle cradled, cigarette ember pulsing. He saw the crane arm of the barge, folded and dripping. And he saw, stacked on the barge's deck under tarps, the crates — dozens of them, stenciled with a logo he couldn't read at this angle but recognized anyway, the same logo from the relay station, from the burned village, the logo that followed suffering like a signature follows a confession.

Twenty meters. Fifteen. The sentry's cigarette glowed and dimmed. Elias could hear voices on the deck above the waterline now, fragments riding the rain:

"—manifest says eleven more loads—"

"—Voss wants the index priority-shipped, nothing else moves till—"

"*Index*," Elias mouthed, and his skin went cold. Index of what? Of everything. Of an archive. Of a vault. Of whatever Imara Sefu had found that powerful men would burn villages to possess.

Ten meters. The current carried them along the gunboat's flank, close enough that Elias could smell it — diesel and canvas and cooked meat, the smell of men fed and paid and unafraid. Close enough to see the wake lapping at their own hull, close enough that if the sentry had looked down instead of out, if the lamp had swung instead of swept—

A plank creaked under Elias's knee.

Small sound. Enormous silence after it. On the gunboat's deck, the sentry's head began to turn toward the water, unhurried, a man with all the time in the world—

And the river saved them. A half-submerged tree came sailing out of the dark on the seam's edge, crown-first, and slapped against the gunboat's hull with a wooden boom that brought three soldiers to the rail, laughing, cursing the river, rifles pointed at nothing. The sentry forgot the plank. The lamps swung to the tree and followed it downstream, chasing a ghost, while the Marisol slid past in the shadow of the distraction, quiet as a debt being forgiven.

Elias didn't breathe again until the lamps were astern and shrinking.

* * *

Dawn found them six kilometers further upstream, tucked in a flooded side-slough while Jonah slept two hours in the pilot's chair and Elias stood watch with the chart and Nadia's markers swimming in front of his eyes.

When she woke, he was waiting with the question he'd been carrying all night.

"The crates," he said. "They were loading them *out* of the ruins. Which means Sefu isn't guarding the archive anymore. Which means either she's dead, or she lost it, or—"

"Or she gave it up to buy lives," Jonah said. She climbed out of the chair, stiff, and stood beside him at the rail, looking upstream at the gray wall of rain where the Sunken Terrace waited. "Or she's trading piece by piece to keep her people breathing. That's what I'd do."

"That's what a runner would do," Elias said, and immediately wished it unsaid.

Jonah took it without flinching. That was the worst part — she took everything without flinching. "Maybe. Or it's what a captain does when the cargo matters more than the boat." She turned to look at him fully, and the morning light made her face unreadable and legible at once. "Either way, Wren — the man with your sister's red circle around his name is sitting on a boat full of her work, six kilometers behind us, and he knows someone's coming. Men like that count their supplies. When the manifest comes up short, they'll come looking."

Ahead, the rain thickened, and through it — faint, almost imagined — Elias saw the first terrace wall rise out of the floodwater, stone-gray and ancient, crowned with vegetation, with smoke drifting from somewhere behind it.

Someone was home.
