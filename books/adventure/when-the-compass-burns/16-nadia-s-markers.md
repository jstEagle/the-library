## 16. Nadia's Markers

The rain had settled into a rhythm by the third day upstream — not the violent arrival of the monsoon proper but its advance guard, a steady gray drumming on the Marisol's cabin roof that Jonah had learned to read like a second engine. It told her how much water the hills were sending down. It told her the river would rise another half meter by morning. And it told her, in the way it softened the edges of the drowned forest around them, that the world she was navigating no longer looked like any world she'd known.

Trees stood to their shoulders in brown water. Their crowns made islands, their trunks vanished into murk, and between them ran channels that appeared and dissolved with every gust of wind pushing surface current across the flood. A pilot without charts would have died out here twice before noon.

Jonah had charts. She had something better.

She stood at the wheel with Nadia Wren's logbook open beside her, weighted down with a coffee mug, and studied the page for the hundredth time since dawn. The cartographer's hand was small and exact, letters upright as fence posts, numbers that never needed deciphering. But it wasn't the writing that held Jonah. It was the drawings — little marginal sketches of landmarks rendered in two or three confident strokes: a leaning kapok tree with a lightning scar, a rock shaped like a sleeping dog, an abandoned dredge bucket hanging from a cable strung between two dead trunks. Beside each sketch, coordinates. Beside each coordinate, a depth sounding taken at some season's particular water level, annotated with corrections for rise and fall.

*Add 2.4 m for June flood stage,* one note read. *Channel shifts east after first heavy rain — verify marker.*

"Verify marker," Jonah murmured. "Sure. Easy."

Elias came up from below with two mugs of the bitter tea they'd been stretching since the sandbar country, and set hers on the chart table without being asked. He'd stopped asking. Somewhere in the last week of bailing water and sharing watches and eating rice out of the same pot, the rituals of courtesy had worn through to something plainer and more durable. He still watched her too closely sometimes. She still caught him doing it. But the suspicion had changed texture — less like two animals circling, more like two people carrying the same heavy thing and not discussing it.

"Where are we?" he asked.

Jonah turned the logbook toward him. "Your sister's channel."

He went still the way he always did when Nadia entered the conversation — a man bracing against a door. His eyes moved over the page, and Jonah watched him read it not as a navigator but as a brother: lingering on the small sketches, the doodled flower someone had drawn in the corner of a sounding table and then half-erased, the tiny notation in the margin of one page that said *E. owes me a drink if this works.*

"She drew that before we left Callao Verde?" Elias said quietly.

"The logbook's been aboard since the fixer gave it to us. She must have planted it with the repair kit." Jonah tapped the flower. "This is older ink. Different pen. She was drawing this channel months ago."

Elias touched the page once, lightly, as if the paper might bruise. Then he straightened and put his captain's face back on — the one he wore when grief got too close to the surface. "So where does it take us?"

"Through the flooded forest, past the gunboat we slipped two nights ago, and up a channel that isn't supposed to exist." Jonah looked out at the water. "According to the survey charts I bought in Callao Verde — bad ones, but official — this whole stretch is solid land. High ground. Terrace country." She let that sit. "Nadia charted a river inside what the maps call a hill."

* * *

They found the first marker an hour later, and Jonah felt it land in her chest like a struck bell.

It was nothing much to look at: a length of rebar driven into a drowned trunk, painted white, with a strip of red surveyor's tape gone pink and ragged from weather. Any fisherman would have passed it without a glance. But it stood exactly where Nadia's logbook said it would stand, at the entrance to a slot of open water between two submerged ridges, and above it — this was the part that made Jonah's hands go careful on the wheel — someone had tied three knots in the tape. Three knots. The logbook's legend page, in Nadia's tidy hand, explained: *One knot = safe at current stage. Two = rising. Three = falling; check soundings again.*

Three knots meant the water here was dropping, which was impossible, because the whole basin was climbing by the hour — unless somewhere upstream a natural sill or collapsed bank was holding back a pocket of the flood, creating a local eddy of lower water, a false calm. Which meant the channel beyond the marker would look invitingly deep and then betray them without warning.

A cartographer who thought to encode the behavior of water into knotted tape — who anticipated that whoever followed her might be running for their life and have no time to take soundings — that was not a technician. That was an artist. Or a prophet.

"She knew someone would come," Elias said behind her. His voice had gone rough. "She built a road and left signs along it."

"She knew *someone* might come," Jonah corrected gently. "She couldn't have known it'd be you. She couldn't have known it'd be anyone." She eased the throttle forward and brought the Marisol's bow around toward the slot. "But she charted for strangers anyway. That's the part I keep turning over."

The boat slid between the drowned ridges. On either side, so close Jonah could have reached out and touched them, the flooded trees rose like the columns of a drowned cathedral, their bark streaked green and black, their branches dripping. The channel was maybe four meters wide. Below the hull, according to Nadia's soundings adjusted for the strange local stage, there were two and a half meters of water over soft mud — enough, barely, if Jonah kept her line true.

And the line was the thing. Because the channel didn't run straight. It bent around invisible features — a submerged fence row, Nadia's notes suggested, remnants of some old homestead; a fallen bridge pier; a stretch where the mud shoaled to a meter and a half on the outside of the bend and fell away to five on the inside. Every bend required committing to the wrong-looking side. Every safe line contradicted instinct.

"Port," Jonah said. "Hard port. Trust me."

Elias threw the wheel over without hesitation, and the Marisol swung her stern across the shallow bar with half a meter to spare, and then the channel opened ahead into a long green corridor and Jonah exhaled for the first time in a minute.

"You've done this before," Elias said. "Run blind water off paper."

"Once or twice." She didn't elaborate. Some cargoes stayed lashed down. But she heard the note in his voice — admiration, carefully rationed — and she found she didn't hate it.

* * *

They threaded the corridor all morning. Twice more they found markers: rebar and tape, and once a length of PVC pipe zip-tied to a branch with a number stenciled on it that matched a station in the logbook. Nadia had surveyed this channel in dry season, apparently — her notes described walking these banks, measuring with a level and a tape measure, arguing with a village headman about whether the old ferry crossing could carry trucks. There were names in the margins. Local names, written phonetically and respectfully: *Don Félix says the water sings here before it rises.* *Old woman at the cassava plot remembers a church bell under the bend — probably the mission bell, see Station 14.*

"She talked to everyone," Elias said, reading over Jonah's shoulder as they idled through a wide pool where the channel briefly relaxed. "Every old man, every kid. Look — she wrote down their stories next to her soundings. Like the stories were data."

"They are data," Jonah said. "Better than sonar, in country like this. An old woman who remembers where the church bell sank knows more about this bend than any instrument ever built." She paused. "Most surveyors write down what instruments say. Your sister wrote down what the river remembers. That's why the company wanted her silent."

Elias looked at her. "You think that's why?"

"I know how powerful men think about maps." Jonah kept her eyes on the water. "A map is a claim. Whoever holds the true map holds everything underneath it — the land, the water, the people who live on both. Your sister didn't just chart a channel. She charted a truth that makes every official map in this basin a lie. Men burn villages for less."

The words came out harder than she intended, and she heard the echo in them — the town behind her, years ago, the fire on the horizon in the rearview mirror of a borrowed truck. Elias heard it too. He had learned to hear those echoes. He said nothing, which was its own kind of courtesy, and went forward to check the bilge.

Jonah stood alone at the wheel with the rain ticking on the roof and understood, with sudden uncomfortable clarity, that she was beginning to love Nadia Wren. Not the way she'd loved people — that door had been rusted shut for years — but the way you come to love a voice on the radio in a storm, a presence that keeps you company in dangerous water. Every page of the logbook made the absent woman more vivid: her impatience (a crossed-out calculation, restarted), her humor (*depth 0.8m — or my pole is crooked*), her stubbornness (the same channel resurveyed three times until the numbers agreed). Nadia had been meticulous and warm and slightly reckless, and she had walked into the monsoon country knowing what men with corporate credentials did to people who mapped truths, and she had gone anyway, because the map mattered more than the danger.

Jonah had spent fifteen years making sure nothing mattered more than her own survival. Sitting beside Nadia's logbook hour after hour, she felt the difference between them like a physical ache.

* * *

At midday the channel delivered them into a place that stopped even Elias's breath.

The trees fell back abruptly and the Marisol drifted out onto a flooded clearing — a plaza, Jonah realized, though it took her a moment to accept it. Stone paving showed through the tawny water in pale rectangles, laid in patterns too regular for nature. Around the edges, walls stood waist-deep above the flood: old walls, mortared without cement, furred with moss, their tops crowned with vegetation that had grown wild for centuries. In the center of the plaza a single stone column rose six meters out of the water, carved top to bottom with figures that the rain and time had softened almost to abstraction — processions of people, maybe, or rivers of symbols, flowing upward.

Nadia's logbook marked it as Station 19. Beside the coordinates, in her hand: *The Old Landing. Pre-colonial. NOT on any government survey. The villagers call it the place where the river was born. Do not anchor on the paving — it is hollow underneath. Moor to the column.*

"Moor to the column," Jonah repeated aloud, half laughing. "Of course. Why not."

"It's a harbor," Elias said slowly. "She found a pre-colonial harbor. This whole flooded forest — it's not forest. It's a city. It was always a city, and the river took it, and everyone forgot."

"And she remembered it for them." Jonah brought the Marisol alongside the column with the tenderness the instruction demanded, passing lines through a weathered iron ring that someone — Nadia? the villagers? hands five hundred years dead? — had set into the stone. The ring held. The boat swung gently at her mooring, rain whispering on the water around them, and for a while neither of them spoke.

Up close, the carvings resolved. Jonah stood at the rail and traced them with her eyes: figures ascending a spiral, carrying things — jars, bundles, children. At the base, half-submerged, the spiral began with figures descending, empty-handed, into water. A story about going under and coming back up. About what the flood takes and returns.

"Elias," she said. "Look at the bottom row."

He leaned over the rail. Water lapped the lowest carvings, and beneath the surface, visible in the afternoon's flat light, more figures continued downward into the dark — deeper than the flood, deeper than the plaza, descending toward something the sculptors had clearly known was there. A chamber. A vault. Whatever Sefu had found.

"They carved the destination into the harbor wall," Elias said. "Directions. For anyone arriving."

"Like your sister's knots in the tape." Jonah shook her head slowly. "People have been leaving markers on this river for a thousand years. Signs for strangers coming after. And your company—" she caught herself, "—their company wants to own the ending of the story."

Elias was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was low. "My sister used to say that a map is a promise you make to someone you'll never meet. That good cartography is just — generosity, formalized." He laughed, wet-eyed. "I thought she was being sentimental. She was being literal."

Jonah looked at him — really looked, the way she rarely allowed herself to look at anyone — and saw that the logbook had done for him what it was doing for her: it had raised the dead woman up out of the abstract and set her living on the deck beside them, impatient and funny and brave, tying knots in tape for travelers she'd never meet.

"We should eat," Jonah said finally, because grief and awe both needed containing and routine was the container she trusted. "Then we push on. According to Station 22, the channel narrows past the old landing, and I want daylight for it."

* * *

They ate rice and dried fish under the canopy, the Marisol rocking gently at her ancient mooring, and it was Elias who broke the comfortable silence, in the oblique way he'd developed for asking about her.

"The town you left," he said. "The one that burned. Was it a mapping thing?"

Jonah set down her tin plate. The rain filled the pause.

"There was a man," she said. "He ran the river cooperative — fuel, cargo contracts, the works. I trusted him with everything. Routes, schedules, the names of every pilot who moved anything worth stealing." She turned her mug in her hands. "He sold it. All of it. To men who wanted to control what moved on the river and what didn't. My routes became their toll roads. Pilots who wouldn't pay had accidents." Her thumb found the old scar along her palm, the one from the night she'd cut a line rather than let a boarding party use it. "I found out on a Tuesday. By Friday the depot was burning — I never learned who lit it, him or them, cleaning up loose ends either way. I was already downstream."

"And you've been downstream ever since."

"I've been *moving* ever since. It's different." She met his eyes. "Moving feels like escape. It isn't. It's just running with better posture."

Elias nodded slowly. "Nadia used to say the opposite. That staying was the hard navigation. Anyone can hold a heading; holding ground, she said, that's the real chart work." He glanced up at the column, the carvings, the drowned city patient under the flood. "Maybe that's why she came out here. To practice."

Jonah looked at the spiral of figures rising out of the water, empty-handed no longer, carrying their bundles upward toward whatever waited at the top. She thought about holding ground. She thought about how the word *home* sat in her mouth like a stone she hadn't swallowed yet.

"Station 22," she said, standing, because some currents you deflect before they drown you. "Let's see what your sister left us there."

* * *

Station 22 nearly killed them, and it was entirely Nadia's fault, and Jonah loved her for it.

The channel narrowed exactly as promised, threading between two walls of flooded brush so close that the brush swept both rails. And then, per the logbook, it made a right-angle turn around a submerged building — *corner post visible at low water, iron, likely a warehouse; DO NOT cut the corner, the floor extends two meters past the wall line* — and it was there, at that turn, moving at bare steerage way, that the Marisol's hull met something that should not have been in the channel at all.

The impact was soft, which was worse than a hard one. A long grinding shudder along the starboard side, wood on wood, and Jonah had the engine in reverse before her mind finished the sentence: *there's a boat under us.*

"Wreck," she called. "Submerged hull, right under the surface. Nadia's notes don't mention it." She was already leaning over the rail, reading the brown water, and there it was — a shadow, a long dark shape lying parallel to the channel, maybe a dugout, maybe a skiff, sunk recently enough that the flood hadn't scattered it. And snagged against it, tangled in its rigging, a scrap of fabric that had once been blue.

Elias went pale in a way Jonah had learned to recognize. Expedition blue. Crew shirts.

"Back us off slow," he said, and his voice was the cartographer's-brother voice, the one that held. "Slow. If we're hung up—"

"We're not hung up. We kissed it." Jonah worked the throttle in increments, feeling the current take the stern, walking the boat backward out of the trap with the delicacy the moment demanded. The grinding ceased. The channel opened. Behind them the sunken shape slid away into murk, taking its blue scrap with it.

For a hundred meters neither of them spoke. Rain on the roof. Engine at idle. The flooded forest pressing close.

"There'll be more of them," Elias said at last. "Boats. People. The company came through here."

"Or the expedition fled through here." Jonah kept her eyes forward. "A sunken dugout cuts both ways, Elias. Someone was running, and someone else made sure they didn't get far. We won't know which direction the story goes until we find the tellers."

Ahead, through the rain-veiled green, the channel bent toward a stretch where Nadia's logbook grew suddenly dense with annotations — soundings taken twice, depths underlined, and in the margin, pressed hard enough to emboss the next page, a single sentence Jonah had been circling all day without reading aloud:

*If you are reading this, do not stop, do not trust the official channel, and whatever you do — do not let them take the index upstream.*

The index. The word from the fixer's planted pages, the word that had started all of this. Jonah felt the river's current strengthen against the hull, as if the water itself were leaning into the sentence.

"Elias," she said quietly. "I think your sister left us one more marker. And I think it's a warning."

Around the next bend, faint through the rain, they both heard it at the same instant: engines. More than one. Coming downstream.
