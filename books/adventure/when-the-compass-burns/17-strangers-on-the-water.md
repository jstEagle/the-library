## 17. Strangers on the Water

Elias saw them before Jonah did, which was a small victory he intended to hoard.

Not that she'd have admitted it. She stood at the wheel with her shoulders set like a woman arguing with the river, one hand on the throttle, the other shading her eyes against the gray sheet of rain coming off the ridgelines. The Marisol pushed upstream through water the color of steeped tea, and everything in the world had narrowed to the channel, the current, the next bend. Elias understood that narrowing. He'd lived inside it for eleven days now — since Callao Verde, since the dock, since he'd stepped aboard this boat with a satchel of cash and a story about a sister that was only half a lie, which made it worse than a whole one.

But the river had taught him things in nine days of running it. It taught him to read the surface the way Nadia used to read contour lines: not for what was there, but for what didn't belong. And on the downstream edge of the flooded point ahead, where the current curled back on itself in a long lazy eddy, something didn't belong.

A shape. Low, dark, riding wrong — too high in the water to be a log, too still to be anything alive.

"Jonah." He didn't shout. He'd learned that about her; shouting made her dig in like a mule. He pitched his voice flat, the way you'd report weather. "Off the port bow. In the eddy."

She turned. For three full seconds she said nothing, and Elias watched her do the arithmetic he'd watched her do a hundred times since they'd left the burned village behind them — risk against reward, fuel against distance, the cost of stopping weighed against whatever the thing in the water might turn out to be. Her rule was never stop longer than the job required. Her rule was never stop at all if she could help it.

Then the rain shifted, and through the gap in the downpour they both saw it clearly: a dugout canoe, riding low, with two figures slumped in it like cargo someone had forgotten.

"They're alive," Elias said. "One of them moved."

"You don't know that."

"I know it anyway."

Jonah's jaw worked. The rain ran off the brim of her hat and dripped from her chin, and Elias thought, not for the first time, that she wore the weather better than most people wore their own skin. "We're carrying supplies for people who may already be dead," she said at last. "Every hour we spend playing rescue is an hour the flood takes from us. You've seen what the water's doing."

"I've seen what the water's doing." He came to stand beside her at the wheel, close enough that he could feel the engine's shudder come up through the deck plates into his boots. "I'm also the one who told you I'd pay for every hour of this boat's time. So bill me."

"This isn't about money."

"No," Elias agreed quietly. "It isn't."

Something passed across her face then — not softness exactly, but the recognition of a debt she couldn't name. She'd been circling it ever since the night of the leak, when they'd bailed side by side until dawn and she'd finally slept two hours against the bulkhead while he kept watch. She owed him nothing. He owed her more than he could calculate. But somewhere between the drowned mission church and the narrows where she'd run rapids that should have killed them both, they had stopped being strangers sharing a boat and become something else, something neither of them would put a word to because putting a word to it would make it fragile.

Jonah eased the throttle back. The Marisol's bow swung toward the eddy.

* * *

They came up on the dugout slow, engine idling, the hull groaning as the current tried to argue with the propeller. Up close the canoe was worse than it had looked from a distance — a hollowed trunk older than either of them, its gunwales scarred, shipping a good four inches of brown water. The two figures lay half-submerged in it.

The first was a man, maybe fifty, gray-bearded, his skin gone the color of old candle wax. He was conscious — barely — and when the Marisol's shadow fell over him he lifted one hand, not in greeting but in warning, fingers spread, as though to say *stop, no further*. His lips were cracked and moving. No sound came out.

The second figure was younger. A young woman, early twenties, curled in the bottom of the canoe with her arms wrapped around her knees, soaked to the bone, eyes open and fixed on nothing. She didn't flinch when the boat bumped alongside. Didn't blink. She looked, Elias thought, like a photograph of herself.

"Ropes," Jonah said, already moving. "Bow line and the stern line. We take the canoe in tow or we lose it — and them with it."

Elias threw the lines. Working together, they hauled the dugout against the Marisol's flank and lashed it, and then came the harder part: getting two half-dead people up over a gunwale slick with rain. The man went first. He weighed almost nothing, which frightened Elias more than any wound would have — a body that light had been starving for days. They laid him on the aft deck beneath the canopy, and Jonah was already there with a blanket and a thermos, and Elias realized she'd done this before, many times, in some other life she never talked about. Her hands knew the work. Warmth first, slowly. Small sips. Don't let them gulp.

The young woman was harder. Not physically — she came up the side almost willingly, like something being poured — but when Elias took her arm she made a sound. Not a scream. A single syllable, bitten off at the root, and her eyes came alive for the first time and fixed on him with such naked terror that he let go and stepped back and raised both hands.

"It's all right," he said, uselessly, the way everyone says it.

"She's not all right," Jonah said, without judgment. "Get her below. Dry clothes. Don't ask her anything yet."

He got her below. She let herself be led, let herself be wrapped in a blanket that smelled of diesel and mildew, let herself be seated on the bench beside the chart table. But when he crouched in front of her and said, gently, "Can you tell me your name?" she only stared at him, and her lips parted, and closed, and parted again, and nothing came. Her throat worked around the silence like a bird beating itself against glass.

Above them, the man was talking. That was the strange thing — the one who'd looked nearest death was the one with words left, while the young woman had none at all.

* * *

Tomas Reyes. That was the man's name, and he gave it in pieces, between swallows of broth Jonah made from a bouillon cube and river water boiled on the little stove. Geologist, he said. Structural survey, Sefu expedition. He said the name Sefu the way a drowning man says the name of a shore.

Elias sat across from him and did not breathe.

"You're from the expedition," he said. Carefully. Keeping his voice level cost him more than the last nine days had. "Dr. Imara Sefu's expedition."

"What's left of it." Tomas laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing bent him double until Jonah steadied the cup against his lips. When he surfaced again his eyes were wet, and not from the coughing. "You're looking for them. Of course you are. Everyone who comes upriver is looking for them." He looked at Elias, really looked, and something in Elias's face must have answered a question Tomas hadn't asked aloud, because the old man's expression changed. Softened. Broke, a little. "You knew one of them."

"My sister." The word came out of him rough, unplaned. "Nadia Wren. Cartographer."

Tomas closed his eyes. "Ah," he said. "Ah, God. Nadia." And then, seeing Elias's face: "Alive. As far as I know, alive. I can't promise you — nobody can promise anybody anything anymore — but alive when we left. Eleven days ago. Twelve? The days don't—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Forgive me. The mind goes soft out there."

Elias found that his own hands were shaking. He folded them in his lap so Jonah wouldn't see, though he suspected she already had; she missed nothing, this woman, and pretended to miss everything. Alive. The word rang in him like a struck bell, and under the ringing was terror, because *as far as I know* was not the same as *alive*, and twelve days in monsoon country was a long time for anyone, even Nadia, even brilliant, stubborn, impossible Nadia who charted channels no one else could see.

"Tell us," Jonah said. She'd taken the seat beside Tomas, close enough to touch, far enough to give him air. She'd wrapped the blanket around his shoulders herself. "From the beginning. Slowly. Eat while you talk."

And Tomas began.

It came out of him in fragments at first, the way wreckage comes off a reef — a piece here, a piece there, no order to it. The rains had come early. The river had come up faster than any season in living memory; the old men in the villages said the water was angry, and Tomas, a geologist, a man of strata and sediment, had stopped smiling at that kind of talk. The expedition had pushed up past the last surveyed tributary following readings, following maps — Nadia's maps, and here Tomas glanced at Elias with something like awe — following channels that appeared on no other chart in existence.

"And then the ruins," he said. "The Sunken Terrace. You've heard of it?"

Jonah's face gave away nothing. "People say there's stone up there that isn't natural."

"People say a lot of things." Tomas drank. His hands were steadier now, wrapped around the warm cup. "It's real. A city, or part of one — pre-colonial, older than anything in the archives downriver, terraces cut into the bluff above a natural harbor that hasn't held water in four hundred years. Except now the river has reclaimed it. The flood brought the harbor back. Dr. Sefu said—" He stopped. Started again. "Dr. Sefu said the water remembered where it used to go."

Elias felt the hair rise on his arms. He thought of what Jonah had told him at the drowned church, her half-truth about why she never stayed anywhere, and how the river seemed to keep what people wanted forgotten. He'd filed it as sentiment then. He wasn't sure anymore.

"We found a vault," Tomas said. "Under the lowest terrace. Sealed. Pre-colonial archive — records, thousands of them, carved and bound and waxed against the damp. Land claims. Water rights. Maps of every hidden tributary in the basin, every buried claim, every boundary that was ever drawn and then erased. Four hundred years of who owned what, and who was made to forget they'd ever owned it." He looked up, and his eyes were clear and terrible. "Do you understand what that is? It isn't treasure. It's power. Power over land, over water, over memory itself. Whoever holds those records can prove — legally, historically, irrefutably — that half the basin belongs to people who were told it never did. Or sell the proof to whoever wants it buried deepest."

The rain hammered the canopy. Somewhere below them, the young woman sat silent in the cabin, and Elias found himself listening past Tomas's voice, listening for her, for any sound, and hearing nothing.

"The chamber," he said. "Is that the vault?"

Tomas frowned. "Who told you about the chamber?"

"Nobody. The word keeps surfacing."

"Then the word is accurate." Tomas set down the cup. "Yes. The chamber. We spent six weeks documenting it. Six weeks, and we might have had another six months, except—" He hesitated, and Elias watched the hesitation, watched the old man weigh something, and watched him decide against weighing it further. "Except we weren't alone in the basin anymore. Boats came up. Not supply boats. Gunboats. Men with corporate credentials asking after us at the relay stations, offering to 'assist' with our logistics. Dr. Sefu sent them away. Twice. The third time they didn't ask."

He fell silent. The engine thudded beneath them, patient as a heart.

"Twelve days ago," Tomas said finally, "Dr. Sefu made a decision. I won't tell you what it was, because I don't fully understand it myself, and because it isn't mine to tell. But it split the expedition. Some of us thought we should pull out — take the records, run downriver, get them somewhere safe. Others believed the vault should stay sealed where it was, that moving it was more dangerous than leaving it. And Dr. Sefu—" He shook his head slowly. "Dr. Sefu chose a third road, and she chose it alone, and she ordered those of us who objected to leave. Not asked. Ordered. Priya and I refused the order in the way that mattered least — we argued — and two days later she sent us downriver with the survey notes and instructions to reach Callao Verde and raise the alarm. She said someone had to carry the truth out even if the truth arrived too late to matter."

"Why you?" Elias asked. "Why send you?"

"Because I'm old," Tomas said simply, "and because Priya had seen too much." His gaze went, unwillingly, toward the cabin hatch. "Because someone had to get her out."

* * *

They anchored that evening in the lee of a flooded palm island, the Marisol swinging on her hook while the rain came down in ropes and lightning walked the ridgelines like something searching. Jonah cooked rice with dried fish and would not hear a word of thanks. Tomas slept like the dead beneath the canopy, exhaustion claiming him mid-sentence, mid-thought, his cup still in his hand until Elias pried it loose.

The young woman ate. That was the day's one miracle. Jonah sat across from her, not speaking, not prompting, just eating her own portion slowly and steadily, and after twenty minutes the girl picked up her spoon and did the same. She ate everything. She ate the way a person eats who has decided to live but cannot yet say so out loud.

Her name, they learned from Tomas before he slept, was Priya Anand. Twenty-three years old. Intern. And the last time anyone had heard her voice, according to Tomas, was eight days ago, on the river, when men in a fast boat had come alongside their dugout in the dark and demanded the survey notes.

"They took the notes," Tomas had murmured, half-asleep. "All of them. Every page. And then they—" His breathing had slowed, thickened. "Priya saw. I got us into the reeds. I got us out. But she saw."

*Saw what*, Elias wanted to ask. But Jonah had touched his wrist — lightly, two fingers, a pressure that meant *not now* — and he had obeyed, and the question had gone down into him like a swallowed stone.

Now, near midnight, he stood at the rail while Jonah checked the anchor line, and the two of them watched the lightning stitch itself along the far ridge.

"Sefu's decision," Elias said. "That's what he called it. What do you think she did?"

Jonah was quiet long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "I think she's a woman who found the one thing in the world worth holding, and I think she's holding it alone, and I think holding things alone is how people die." She tested the anchor line, hand over hand, water streaming off the rope. "Your sister's up there with her."

"Yes."

"And you're going whether I go on or not."

Elias turned to look at her. Rain ran down his face. "Are you asking?"

"I'm telling you what I see." She coiled the line with quick, economical movements. "You've got the look. I know the look. I wore it for years."

"And what happens when we get there?" he asked. "If we find them. If we find her. What then?"

"That," Jonah said, "depends on what Sefu decided."

Below decks, faintly, through the hull, Elias heard a sound he hadn't heard all day. Small. Unsteady. The sound of Priya Anand, alone in the dark cabin, beginning — very quietly, as if remembering how — to hum.

Hope arriving damaged, he thought. But arriving.

He was still standing at the rail when Jonah spoke again, and her voice had changed, gone low and flat, the voice she used for charts and currents and other unforgiving facts.

"Elias. Come look at this."

She was pointing downstream, back the way they'd come, toward the black seam of the southern channel. At first he saw nothing — rain, darkness, the river running high and fast. Then the lightning flashed again, and held, and in that frozen white instant he saw it: far downstream, a wake. Two wakes. Long, straight, deliberate, cutting upriver against the current at a speed no trading boat could manage.

Gunboats. Coming up the same water they'd just crossed.

And behind them, faint but unmistakable on the wind, carried up through the rain from miles south — the smell of smoke.
