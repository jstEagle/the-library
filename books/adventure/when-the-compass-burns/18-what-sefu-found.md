## 18. What Sefu Found

The rain had opinions about fire. It came down in sheets that leaned sideways off the river, and every time the wind shifted, the flames ducked and flattened like something trying to hide. Tomas Reyes crouched at the edge of the lean-to they'd thrown together from torn tarpaulin and a bent outrigger frame, feeding the small fire strips of wet bark he'd dried against his chest all afternoon, one at a time, like a man paying out a rope over a cliff.

The fire was barely bigger than two cupped hands. It didn't matter. In eleven days of running, it was the first thing any of them had managed to keep alive.

Across the coals, Priya sat wrapped in the boat blanket Jonah Vasquez had given her without being asked, her knees drawn up to her chin, her eyes on the middle distance where the rain made the river look like static. She hadn't spoken since the dugout — not since those first broken words on the water: *the chamber, Sefu's decision* — and Tomas had stopped waiting for more. Some silences were wounds. You didn't probe them. You kept them clean and let them close on their own schedule.

Elias Wren sat closest to the fire, turning his hands over the heat as if he could press warmth into them for later storage. He'd been quiet since they'd beached the Marisol in the mangrove cove — quiet in the way of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like the answer to. And Jonah Vasquez stood at the edge of the shelter with her back half-turned to all of them, watching the water rise.

She watched the water the way other people watched a door when they expected someone to come through it.

Tomas knew the type. He'd been expedition geologist for six years; he'd worked with drillers and pilots and smugglers and one memorable woman who claimed to have surveyed the headwaters by balloon. People who lived on rivers fell into two kinds: those who loved the water and those who were fleeing across it. The loving kind talked about the river constantly. The fleeing kind never did, but their eyes went to it every few minutes, checking.

Vasquez was the second kind, and she was the best of them he'd ever seen, and none of that mattered right now, because what mattered right now was that these strangers had come upstream through pirate narrows and rising flood to find an expedition that everyone else had written off as dead — and they deserved to know what they'd found instead.

What *he* had helped find. What had gotten four porters killed and Priya struck dumb and Dr. Imara Sefu bleeding somewhere above the flood line with a wound going bad.

"Tell them," Priya said.

Tomas nearly dropped the bark strip. Her voice was rusted from disuse, thin as wire, but it cut through the rain-hammer on the tarpaulin like it had been waiting eleven days for permission.

Both strangers turned. Even Vasquez looked back from the water.

Priya's eyes stayed fixed on the fire. "He keeps looking at me like I'm going to break if he says it out loud." A breath. "I won't. Tell them what we found. Tell them why they're going to die too, if they stay."

Nobody said anything to that. The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, the river moved with the deep muscular sound of something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Tomas fed the bark to the coals and began.

* * *

"You have to understand what the basin was before the maps," he said. "Not our maps. Anybody's maps."

The rain filled the pauses, which was good, because the story needed pauses. He'd told it once before — to Sefu, the night they opened the outer seal, both of them giddy and stupid with discovery — and even then, half-drunk on adrenaline and warm beer, he'd felt the shape of what he was saying settle over him like cold water.

"Every government survey says this basin has a main channel and maybe forty tributaries worth naming. That's what the colonial maps show, that's what the mining concessions are drawn on, that's what the land titles reference. Forty-odd rivers. Clean lines. Everything accounted for."

Elias nodded slowly. "And?"

"And the basin is lying." Tomas let that sit. "There aren't forty tributaries. There are hundreds. Maybe thousands, counting the seasonal channels and the underground flows through the karst. The old maps weren't incomplete because the surveyors were lazy. They were incomplete because the people who lived here *made* them incomplete."

Vasquez had turned fully around now. She said nothing, but she'd gone very still, the way she did at the wheel when the channel ahead got complicated.

"The delta kingdoms — the ones the chronicles call the River Confederacy — they ran this whole watershed for six hundred years before anyone with a compass showed up. Trade, tribute, flood agriculture, all of it coordinated across distances the colonial administration couldn't manage with telegraph lines. And the reason they could do it—" he tapped the ground beside him, "—is that they held the master record. Every channel. Every hidden passage between watersheds. Every dry-season ford and every place where a river goes underground and comes up again three valleys away. Where the fish spawn. Where the ground is hollow. Where you can move an army, or a cargo, or a whole village, in ways no outsider could follow."

"A hydrological census," Elias said quietly. Not a question. His sister had been a cartographer; some vocabulary survives grief.

"Better than that. A *claim registry*. Because here's the part nobody outside ever understood: the Confederacy didn't just chart the water. They charted who owned what along it. Every buried cache, every ancestral grave site, every treaty boundary, every debt recorded between families and kingdoms — all of it keyed to the waterways, because the waterways were the only permanent thing. Kingdoms fell. Rivers moved slower than dynasties." He shrugged. "Slower. Not immovably. Which is exactly why they built the archive where they built it."

"The Sunken Terrace," Vasquez said. First words she'd spoken in an hour, and her voice came out flat and careful, like she was testing ice.

"The flooded ruins, yes. Except they're not ruins, and they were never meant to be sunken. The complex sits in a natural basin that floods most centuries — the builders engineered for it. Sealed vaults below the high-water line, designed to stay dry behind stone and clay plugs. The city above was the visible part: temples, markets, the works. The part that mattered was underneath, and it was meant to survive everything short of the end of the world." He smiled, and it hurt his face. "It survived the end of its world. It just took five hundred years for anyone to find the door again."

The fire guttered as wind drove rain under the tarpaulin. Tomas leaned forward and shielded it with his body without thinking about it — six years of fieldwork living in his hands.

"Sefu found the entrance eighteen months ago," he went on. "A drainage shaft, exposed by a landslide during the last big flood season. She spent a year and most of her own money confirming it before she brought in the full team. And I want to be clear about what she was, whatever else gets said about her now. She wasn't a treasure hunter. She documented everything. She catalogued the outer chambers properly, she hired local porters from villages whose grandmothers still told stories about the Terrace, she planned to turn the whole thing over to the national institute with the descendant communities holding the interpretive rights. That was the published plan. That was the *real* plan, at the start."

"But?" Elias asked.

"But then we reached the inner vault." Tomas looked at his hands. "And Imara Sefu changed."

* * *

The rain thickened. Out on the black water, lightning flickered inside a cloud like a lamp behind cloth — no thunder yet, just light, silent and wrong. Vasquez glanced at it, then at the river gauge stake she'd driven into the mud that afternoon, and Tomas saw her mouth tighten. The river was up another hand-span since dark. He filed that away with everything else he was afraid of and kept talking, because talking was the only cargo he had left to deliver.

"I want to describe the vault honestly," he said, "because I think people imagine gold when they hear 'archive,' and this wasn't gold. It was worse than gold. It was *paper*. Thousands of documents. Bark-paper codices, lacquered palm-leaf manuscripts, copper plates with stamped inscriptions, knotted cord records — the full range. Five centuries of administrative memory, sealed in clay jars inside limestone chambers, and the dry air down there had kept it legible. Legible, do you understand? Not fragments. Not archaeology. *Reading*. We could read the grain ledgers. We could read the boundary treaties. We could read the water law."

"Water law," Vasquez repeated.

"The rules for who takes how much from which channel in drought years. Enforcement records. Case rulings going back four hundred years." He laughed, short and bitter. "You want to know what power looks like? It isn't a crown. It's knowing, in a bad-water year, exactly which upstream community historically had drawing rights on which tributary, and having the signed record to prove it. The Confederacy didn't rule by force. They ruled by being the only ones who could adjudicate the water. Everyone else had to come to them."

Silence, except the rain. Priya had pulled the blanket tighter. Her eyes were open now, actually focused, and Tomas realized she was listening the way you listen to a diagnosis.

"So the archive maps every hidden channel in the basin," Elias said slowly, working it through. "Which means whoever holds it can find routes nobody else knows exist. Move things — or people — invisibly. And it records every claim, every burial, every treaty. Which means it can prove ownership of land that companies currently hold paper title to. Or disprove it."

"It can unmake the entire legal geography of the basin," Tomas said. "Yes. Every concession, every plantation boundary, every mine claim drawn on those pretty colonial maps — the archive either corroborates it or shows it was theft, with documentation. Land, water, memory. All three, in one sealed room."

"And that's why men with corporate credentials were asking after the expedition at the relay station," Elias said, "eleven days before you went dark."

Tomas looked at him sharply. "How do you know about—"

"Lucky guess," Elias said, in a voice that fooled no one, and Tomas decided — not for the first time since the dugout — that there was a great deal this stranger wasn't telling them, and that he'd interrogate that later, if there was a later.

"Yes," he said instead. "Someone knew. We never figured out how much. But three weeks ago, a survey company's helicopter overflew the Terrace twice in one day. No markings. Then the porters started getting offers — cash, real cash, more than a year's wages — to walk away. Two took it. The rest stayed because Sefu paid them double, which she couldn't afford, which tells you how far past the point of no return she already was." He rubbed his face. "Then Ravel—"

He stopped. The name sat in the firelight between them like a stone dropped in shallow water.

"Ravel," Vasquez said. "Her second-in-command."

"You know the name."

"We've met his work," Elias said grimly. "Burned village about sixty kilometers downstream. Company crates in the ashes. Survivors fled upstream — toward here, presumably."

Tomas closed his eyes. "Then you know better than I do what happened to the villages that helped us." When he opened them, his voice had gone rough. "Ravel was Sefu's logistics man. Fifteen years in the field together. And somewhere between the outer chambers and the inner vault, someone reached him — money, or fear, or the argument, the *reasonable* argument, that a thing like this shouldn't sit in one dying woman's hands. Whatever it was, he sold the route. Sold the porter roster. Sold the location of the supply caches. By the time we understood what was happening, the gunboat was already on the river."

"The porters," Priya said.

Everyone went still. It was the longest sentence she'd offered since the water.

Tomas turned to her. "Priya—"

"They should hear it." Her voice shook, but she kept it level through sheer force, the way you keep a door shut against wind. "They came upriver for this. They should hear all of it." She looked at the fire. "I'll tell the part I saw. He tells the rest."

* * *

But she didn't start right away, and Tomas understood why, and so he filled the silence for her, gently, giving her time the way you give a swimmer time before going in after them.

"There were nine porters when we entered the Terrace," he said quietly. "Good men. Two of them — Mateo and old Anselm — had grandparents from the river villages near the site, and they used to argue with Sefu about what the archive owed those communities. Real arguments, good arguments. Sefu liked them for it. She said an archive that can't survive disagreement deserves to rot."

He stared into the coals.

"When the mercenaries hit the lower terraces, we scattered. Sefu ordered the vault resealed — it takes hours, the clay plug system, and she stayed to run it while the rest of us got out with what we could carry. Mateo and Anselm stayed with her. The others ran the evacuation route through the flooded galleries, and that's where the mercenaries caught the stragglers." His jaw worked. "Sefu got out with two crew members and a wound she won't let anyone treat properly, because she's using the antibiotics on the younger ones. She's been holding the upper terraces for eleven days with three rifles and a radio she daren't use, guarding a door that leads to the only thing in this basin worth killing a province for."

"And the decision," Elias said. "Priya mentioned Sefu's decision."

Tomas exhaled slowly. "The decision is why we're alive and why I don't sleep. Before the attack, Sefu called a council — the senior staff, the porter representatives. She laid it out plainly: the archive was too dangerous to publish, too important to destroy, and too heavy to move fast. Three options. Seal it and bury the entrance under the landslide scree, and let the river keep its secret another five hundred years. Publish everything immediately, copies everywhere, so no single interest could control it — and accept that every land dispute in the basin becomes a war for a decade. Or secure it, negotiate from strength, and decide later who gets access." He spread his hands. "She argued for the third option. Mateo argued for the second. Anselm argued for the first. And the vote deadlocked two-two-two, and then the helicopters came back, and the decision stopped being academic."

"What did she choose?" Vasquez asked.

"She chose to stall." Tomas's voice flattened. "She chose to hold the vault and wait for conditions to change, which is a way of choosing nothing, and nothing is what got people killed. If she'd sealed it, we'd have been gone before the gunboat arrived. If she'd published, there'd be nothing left to steal. Instead she tried to keep every option open, and keeping options open requires holding ground, and holding ground—" he gestured at the rain, the river, the dark, "—holding ground is what the dead are doing now."

The fire had burned low. He fed it the last of his bark strips and sat back, wrung out, emptier than he'd felt since the dugout.

"So that's what she found," he finished. "Not treasure. A mirror. Five hundred years of proof that whoever holds the water holds everything else — and the same temptation sitting in the same room, waiting for whoever opens the door next. The Confederacy kings. The colonial governors. The company. Sefu." He looked at each of them in turn. "Us."

Elias was staring into the fire with an expression Tomas couldn't read — grief and calculation fighting for the same territory. "Your sister," Vasquez said to him, quietly. "Nadia. Was she there? At the Terrace?"

Something crossed Elias's face like a shadow of a bird.

"She was the expedition's cartographer," he said. "She mapped the flooded channels around the site. If anyone charted a way in and out that the mercenaries wouldn't know about—" his voice caught, steadied, "—it would be her."

"And her charts," Vasquez said slowly, "would show whether there's a way to move that archive out by water. Before the river peaks. Before the company comes back."

Before anyone could answer, Priya spoke again — and this time her voice had something in it that hadn't been there since the dugout. Not hope, exactly. Its stubborn cousin.

"Nadia marked the channels," she said. "I carried her stakes. She said if anything happened to her, follow the markers — the red-painted ones, above the flood line. She said—" Priya's breath hitched, and she pushed through it, "—she said the river remembers every secret it's ever been told. She said the markers were how you ask it to repeat them."

The rain hammered the tarpaulin. Out in the dark, the thunder finally arrived, rolling up the valley from downstream like something heavy being dragged closer.

Vasquez had gone still again — that pilot's stillness — and when Tomas followed her gaze out past the shelter, he saw what she was looking at: not the lightning, not the rising river, but the black line of the channel ahead, where the floodwater had begun, almost imperceptibly, to change direction.

"Then we'd better go find those markers," Jonah Vasquez said, "before the river changes its mind."
