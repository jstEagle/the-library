## 19. The Leak Wins a Round

The river told her before the boat did.

Jonah had learned to read water the way other people learned to read faces — early, out of necessity, and with a suspicion that it would lie to her eventually. All morning the Marisol's wake had been wrong. Not dramatically. Not in a way Elias or Priya would have noticed from the deck. But the foam behind the stern was looser than it should have been, spreading instead of folding, and the engine note sat a half-tone lower than the tachometer said it should, and when she put her palm flat against the wheel housing she could feel a tremor that wasn't coming from the shaft.

The hull was drinking.

She didn't say anything at first. Saying things made them true, and there were still eleven kilometers of open channel between them and the mangrove country marked on Nadia's charts — eleven kilometers of brown floodwater moving at better than six knots, carrying whole trees like matchsticks, and if she stopped here she might as well have dropped anchor in the mouth of something hungry.

"Jonah." Priya's voice from the companionway, thin and careful. "There's — I think you should look at the bilge."

"I know."

"You *know*?"

"I've known since the bend below the confluence." She eased the throttle back a notch, feeling for the sweet spot where the leak would slow without letting the current start dragging her stern downstream. "How much water?"

Priya was quiet a moment, doing the arithmetic of fear. "Over the stringers. Maybe eight centimeters in the low spots. Tomas is bailing but he says it's coming faster than—"

"It always comes faster than you think." Jonah cut the conversation off before it could become panic. "Get everything that can't swim up onto the deck. Fuel cans, the archive crates, the medical kit. Elias — where's Elias?"

"Forward. Watching the channel."

Of course he was. The man had developed a habit of standing at the bow rail with his hands folded behind his back like a man at a funeral, watching the water as though it owed him an apology. She leaned out of the wheelhouse and pitched her voice over the rain, which had settled into a steady gray drumming rather than the violence of two nights ago — the monsoon catching its breath between arguments.

"Elias! Get aft. We're taking water."

He turned, and even at forty meters she saw the way his body changed — the grief falling away from him like a coat, replaced by something quicker. He'd been a soldier once, or something adjacent to one; she'd known it since the sandbar, since the way he'd braced under the hull with his shoulders set exactly right. Grieving brothers didn't stand like that.

"How bad?" he called back.

"Bad enough that we're not making the deep channel. There's a cove on the left bank about three kilometers up — mangroves, hard mud underneath. Nadia charted it."

"You're going to beach her?"

"I'm going to *park* her," Jonah said. "Beaching is what happens when you don't choose your ground."

* * *

The cove opened out of the flooded bank like a held breath. From the main channel it was invisible — just another seam of green where the forest came down to drink — but Nadia had drawn it in fine blue ink with a note in her small precise hand: *Hard clay under root mat. Will hold a light hull at half tide. Good cover from upstream sightlines.* A cartographer who thought about escape routes. Jonah was beginning to build a picture of Nadia Wren in her head, and the picture kept surprising her: not the careful academic she'd imagined from Elias's silences, but someone who had looked at this river the way Jonah did, as a thing full of doors.

Getting through the entrance channel was fifteen minutes of the kind of work that aged a pilot. The flood had swallowed the mangrove prop roots to a meter above the first branching, so the trees stood in rows like drowned soldiers, and the water between them moved in slow treacherous coils, pulling toward the main current whenever the wind gapped. Jonah took the Marisol in on momentum alone, engine at idle, rudder answering in that sluggish half-second delay that meant the keel was already dragging silt. Twice the hull kissed the root mat beneath them with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and twice she corrected with a touch of throttle and a prayer she didn't believe in.

Then the cove opened, a pool perhaps sixty meters across, ringed in green shadow, and she slid the bow into the shallows at the far side until the hull grounded with a long shuddering sigh, and killed the engine.

Silence rushed in — the particular silence of the mangrove country, which wasn't silence at all but a hundred small sounds replacing one large one: water dripping from leaves, the tick of cooling metal, somewhere close a bird making a noise like a rusty hinge complaining.

"Well," said Tomas, waist-deep in the bilge, bailing bucket in hand. "We're aground."

"We're *docked*," Jonah said. "There's a difference, and the difference is whether we float again. Everybody out. Now. Every kilo you leave aboard is a kilo pushing the leak underwater."

They waded ashore through thigh-warm water, hauling crates and fuel and the last of their food onto a shelf of exposed root between two great mangrove buttresses. Priya moved like someone relearning how her limbs worked — nine days since she'd spoken more than a sentence, and she worked without being told, without being thanked, eyes always moving to the tree line and back. Elias stood in the shallows beside the hull, and when Jonah came down the ladder into the water beside him, he was already pressing his palms flat against the planking below the waterline.

"Here," he said. "Feel it."

She felt it. Under her fingers, through the wet wood, a cold pulse of pressure — water forcing its way through a seam that had been giving up its caulking by degrees for a hundred kilometers. And near it, worse, a softness in the planking itself, a give like old fruit.

"That's not a seam anymore. That's rot." She swam the length of the hull with one hand trailing, reading it like braille. "Port side, aft of the engine bed. Two planks gone soft, maybe three. The storm strain finished what the years started."

"Can you fix it here? With what we have?"

Jonah looked at him. Water to his chest, hair plastered flat, asking the question the way he asked all his questions now — no longer testing her, just needing the answer straight.

"I can fix anything with enough of the right materials and enough time," she said. "We're short on both. So the answer is: maybe."

* * *

The work filled the rest of the day and gave the afternoon a shape that almost resembled hope.

First they careened her. That was the word her father had used, and using it now, shouting it across the cove, Jonah felt the strange comfort of inherited language — *careen*, from the old sailors' word for a ship turned on her side to show her belly. They ran lines from the masthead to two of the biggest mangrove trunks, waited for the tide of the flood to slacken at midday, then hauled the Marisol over until she lay against the mud at an angle that made Priya gasp and made Jonah's own stomach clench in a way she refused to show on her face. Her boat, her whole world, tipped like a beached whale with her wounds in the air.

The wound was worse than she'd guessed. Three planks, not two, and the middle one had rotted clear through in a patch the size of her spread hand — punky, dark-stained, honeycombed by decades of freshwater worms and the slow chemistry of a hull that had been patched too many times by pilots too broke to do it properly. By her. She'd known about the soft spot for two seasons. She'd nursed it, watched it, told herself next dry season, next yard, next town. Moving water forgave nothing, but it also never demanded payment all at once, and she had built her whole life on that installment plan.

"Next dry season," she murmured, and heard how it sounded.

"What?" Elias crouched at the edge of the mud, holding the lantern they'd rigged under an oilcloth awning because the light under the canopy went green and useless by four o'clock.

"Nothing. Hand me the chisel."

They fell into the rhythm that the sandbar had taught them, the rhythm neither of them named. Elias worked without flinching at the filth or the cold or the leeches that found everyone's skin by dusk; he held what she told him to hold, exactly as long as she told him to hold it, and asked questions only when the questions were useful. Tomas proved to have surgeon's hands and no fear of confined spaces, and spent two hours inside the bilge passing tools and cursing in two languages. Priya kept the fire going and boiled the resin and said nothing at all, and somehow her silence was easier to work around than most people's chatter.

The repair itself was a frankenstein's surgery. Jonah cut away the rotten wood back to solid grain, which left a hole through the hull you could have passed a loaf of bread through, and into that hole they fitted a patch built from everything the Marisol had surrendered: a section of cabin sole, a piece of the old skiff that had died on the sandbar, canvas from the awning, all bedded in pine tar and the last of their epoxy, screwed home with brass screws salvaged one by one from a jar of odds and ends that Jonah had carried for eleven years without ever quite knowing why. When the last screw seated, she pressed her palm against the patch the way she'd press a hand against a child's forehead.

"Hold," she whispered. "Just hold."

* * *

The river climbed anyway.

She noticed it at dusk, the way you notice a stranger in a familiar room. The mooring lines — slack when they'd tied them at noon — had come taut. The root shelf where they'd stacked the crates had narrowed by a hand's width. Down at the water's edge, the brown current slid past with a new note in its voice, deeper, more patient, the voice of something that had decided to keep rising and saw no reason to hurry about it.

By full dark it had come up another half meter, and the cove that had been a refuge was becoming a bowl filling from the bottom. The mangrove roots around them disappeared one by one, the drowned soldiers sinking another degree, and the channel of black water between the trees widened until the far bank was a rumor.

They ate around the fire — rice and dried fish and the last of the good tea, hoarded for morale — and nobody spoke about the water until Priya did, which surprised everyone including, from the look on her face, Priya.

"It's going to reach us," she said. Her voice was rough from disuse, quiet but level. "The floor of the camp. By morning, maybe. If it rises the same rate."

Jonah looked at the fire rather than at her, because looking directly at people while they found their courage could scare it off like a bird. "Probably by morning. Yes."

"So do we move the boat tonight? While we can still see what we're doing?"

"The patch needs six hours to cure before I trust it under load. It's had four." Jonah turned her cup slowly in her hands. "If we float her now and the patch lets go in the main channel, we lose everything — boat, cargo, probably us. If we wait and the water reaches the camp, we get wet feet and a bad night and we float her at dawn on a cured patch."

"And if it rises faster than you think?" Elias asked. Not challenging. Asking, genuinely, the way he'd learned to ask.

"Then we get very wet," Jonah said, "and we float her in the dark, and I do the hardest piloting of my life by lantern light, and we probably still make it. The river doesn't give you good choices, Elias. It gives you choices you can live with. You learn to tell the difference."

He studied her across the fire, and in the orange light his face did something complicated — the grief and the guilt and the growing, reluctant trust all moving at once, weather systems crossing. "You talk about the river like it's a person."

"It is a person. An old one. Old people don't explain themselves, and they remember everything, and they'll drown you as soon as carry you, and either way it isn't personal." She drank her tea. "You learn to stop expecting fairness. That's the whole education."

Tomas laughed softly, a short exhalation. "Sefu says — said — something like that. About science. 'The basin doesn't owe us its secrets.' She used to say it to funders who wanted guarantees." His face closed when he said the name, the way it always did, and the fire popped, and for a moment the whole camp was inside the shape of the missing woman, the expedition reduced to embers and fragments and two survivors who spoke in pieces.

"She sounds like she'd have made a good pilot," Jonah said.

"She'd have hated it. Too much waiting." Tomas smiled at his own memory, briefly, sadly. "But she'd have respected it. She respected anything that couldn't be bought."

* * *

Jonah took first watch, because she always did, and because sleep had been coming to her lately in shallow dishonest installments — twenty minutes here, thirty there, dreams full of fire and running water and a man's voice saying *you'll thank me, Jonah, everybody runs sometime.*

She sat on the root shelf with her back against bark and watched the river rise.

It was a strange thing to watch, if you let yourself really watch it, and she let herself. The water came up the way time came up — invisibly, continuously, proven only by evidence. A floating log that had grounded at sundown lifted free at midnight and sailed away without a sound. The mooring lines tightened another degree, and she paid them out, and tightened again an hour later. Around two in the morning the fire burned down to coals and the rain returned, soft this time, almost apologetic, and the whole cove became a cathedral of dripping green and rising black glass.

Her boat lay heeled over in the shallows, patched, exhausted, alive. Eleven years. Eleven years of moving water, of never sleeping two nights in the same town, of a rule so old it had worn grooves in her like water wears stone: *never stop longer than the job requires.* She'd told herself it was freedom. Some nights, in some anchorages, she'd almost believed it.

But sitting here, watching the flood take the ground away inch by inch, she understood something with the clarity that only exhaustion and rising water provide: the rule had never been freedom. It had been a debt collection schedule. Every town she'd left, every name she hadn't given, every dock she'd cast off from at dawn — none of it had outrun the fire. The fire rode with her. It slept in her bilge like the rot in her planks, and every year the patch over it got thinner, and someday — soon, if she was honest, if she was the kind of honest she demanded of her hulls — it was going to let go in the middle of the main channel, and there would be no cove, no mangroves, no second chance to careen and cut and screw down brass.

The river rose. The lines tightened. She paid them out.

Near dawn, Elias came to sit beside her without being asked, bringing the pot of tea reheated over coals, pouring two cups, handing her one. For a long time neither of them spoke. This was the part of him she'd stopped distrusting: his silence had gotten honest. Early on, his silences had been locked rooms. Now they were just rooms, with windows.

"The patch will hold," he said finally.

"It'll hold."

"And the water?"

"Another meter by tomorrow night. Maybe more. The tributaries are all peaking together — I've seen it once before, the year the mission church went under for good." She wrapped her hands around the cup. "Which means the window Nadia charted through the Terrace channels starts closing. Those submerged passages run on a margin of maybe a meter and a half. After that, they're not passages. They're just river."

Elias was quiet, working it. She could hear him thinking, actually hear it, a kind of friction in his breathing. "So we can't wait for the river to drop."

"The river isn't going to drop. Not for weeks. Maybe not this season." She looked at him. "We go through at peak flood, on a patched hull, following a dead woman's charts, with whatever Sefu's people are still holding up there — or we don't go at all, and everything she found, everything Nadia drew, everything those porters died for, drowns in the record along with the ruins."

"That's not a choice you can live with," he said quietly. "That's a choice you can barely survive."

"Yeah." Jonah drank her tea and watched the east go from black to bruise-colored over the tree line, and felt, beneath her, through the mud and the water and the tired old timbers, the faintest tremor — the Marisol settling as the flood lifted her, testing the patch, asking its question.

The patch held. The boat floated free of the mud with a soft, sucking release, swinging gently on her lines like something waking up.

Jonah stood, joints cracking, captain again in every aching inch of her. "Wake the others," she said. "We sail with the dawn."
