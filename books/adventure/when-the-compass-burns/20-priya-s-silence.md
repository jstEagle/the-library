## 20. Priya's Silence

The mangrove cove smelled of rot and salt and old wood — a smell that, in another life, Priya Anand would have catalogued and forgotten. Here, in the green half-light beneath the canopy, it was the only thing that still reached her. The river had climbed the roots overnight; she could see the tide-line of mud on the trunks, a dirty ring like a bruise around the world's throat.

She sat at the edge of the Marisol's deck with her knees drawn up and her arms wrapped around them, and she did not speak.

It had been nine days now. She knew the number the way she knew tide tables — precisely, uselessly. Nine days since the narrows below the Sunken Terrace. Nine days since the two shots. Nine days since her voice had gone somewhere she could not follow.

Tomas had stopped asking her to talk on the third day. He was a kind man, Tomas, with his field notebooks and his careful questions, but kindness pressed on her like a hand on a wound. He meant well. They all meant well. That was the terrible thing about meant-well: it required something of you. It required a response. And she had nothing to give.

Jonah Vasquez did not ask.

That was the first thing Priya had noticed about the riverboat captain, back when the dugout had come alongside and hands had hauled her over the gunwale and someone had wrapped a blanket around her shoulders — Jonah had looked at her exactly once, taken in the mud and the silence and the way Priya's eyes kept going to the water, and had said nothing at all. No *you're safe now*. No *it's over*. Just a nod, and a tin mug of something hot set down within reach, and then she had turned back to her engine like the world's most important business was the world's most ordinary business.

Priya had drunk the tea. It had been terrible. She had drunk all of it.

Now, in the cove, the boat groaned around her. The Marisol was beached in the shallows, heeled over just enough to expose the hull's wound, and Jonah and Elias Wren were somewhere below with epoxy and canvas and the low, rhythmic argument of two people working toward the same end. Tomas had gone up the bank to check the survey line, moving with the careful economy of a man who had learned, recently, that the world could take things from him. Sefu lay in the stern cabin with her fever, though no one called it that out loud. The word *dying* sat in the boat like a crate nobody would unpack.

Priya watched the water.

It was brown-green and patient, and it carried things: a stripped palm frond, a beetle riding a leaf like a barge, a long ribbon of foam where the current worried at a submerged root. She had studied hydrology for two years before Dr. Sefu had taken her on — had written a paper on sediment transport in monsoon basins that exactly eleven people had read. She had thought she understood water. She had understood nothing. Water was not a subject. Water was a country, and it had been at war with the land since before there were names for either, and it did not care about papers, or expeditions, or two porters kneeling on a bank with their hands behind their heads.

She closed her eyes and the narrows came back, the way it always came back, in the order it always came: the smell of diesel and crushed vegetation; the gunboat sliding out of the mist with its grey paint and its company stencil; the mercenary with the shaved head turning and saying something bored to the man beside him; Mateo saying, in his careful trade Spanish, *we are with the Sefu survey, we have papers*; and then the sound — not loud, not cinematic, just two flat cracks, like branches breaking — and the way the birds had gone up all at once, a whole hillside of birds, as if the forest itself had flinched.

She had run. That was the part that lived in her like a hook in the flesh. She had run before she knew she was running, down the bank, into the flooded secondary channel, into the water that grabbed her pack and tried to keep it, and she had swum with her survey case held over her head like an offering until her arms burned, and she had found the dugout, and she had paddled until Tomas found her, and somewhere in all of that her voice had simply — left. Like a bird going up with the others.

*You could have helped them,* said the thing inside her, in her own voice, in her own accent, the one her mother had tried so hard to sand out of her English. *You had a case full of instruments. You could have thrown something. You could have screamed. You did nothing.*

*I did nothing,* she agreed, silently, for the nine hundredth time. *I did nothing and I lived.*

The rain started again, soft at first, a hiss in the canopy, and then with the sudden commitment of the season, a roar. She didn't move. Rain on a mangrove canopy sounded like applause for something terrible. She had thought that on the second day, and she hated that her mind still made observations, still filed things, still did its little work of turning horror into image, as if the self were a machine that could not be stopped, only broken in one place at a time.

* * *

Jonah came up from the engine room at mid-afternoon, wiping her hands on a rag that had given up being any color but grey.

She was not what Priya had expected a captain to be. Priya's expectations, she admitted, had been formed by novels and one bad documentary: some weathered patriarch with a beard and a wheel. Jonah Vasquez was a woman maybe forty, lean as cable, with a face that did its work behind the eyes and a way of moving through her own boat like the boat had agreed to it. She spoke little and precisely. She had a scar along her ribs, fresh, bandaged, that she never mentioned. And she had a habit — Priya had been watching for days, cataloguing the way she catalogued everything, because cataloguing was the last room of the house that still had lights on — of standing at the bow each evening and looking upstream, at the country she'd come from, for exactly as long as it took to finish a cigarette, and then never again until the next evening.

Today she didn't light the cigarette. Today she came and sat down on the deck an arm's length from Priya, with her back against the rail, and looked at the water.

She didn't look at Priya. That was the trick of it, Priya realized later. She sat down as if she'd always sat there, as if the deck had a Priya-shaped place and a Jonah-shaped place and the two had simply never been occupied at the same time before.

For a long time there was only the rain and the engine-room sounds of Elias below, and the river climbing, a centimeter an hour, patient as arithmetic.

"You count things," Jonah said at last. Not a question. Her voice was low and roughened, a voice used over engines.

Priya's throat worked. It had worked uselessly for nine days, a muscle rehearsing a motion with nothing behind it. She said nothing.

"Nothing wrong with it," Jonah said. "Counting. My father counted fence posts. Out loud. Drove my mother half mad." She turned the rag over in her hands. "You've been counting since you came aboard. Days. Meters on the tide stick. The stitches in that blanket. I do it too, when the boat's in bad water. Gives the mind a rope to hold."

The rain thickened. Somewhere up the bank, a hornbill called, three notes like a dropped hammer.

"I'm not going to ask you what happened," Jonah said. "Tomas told us the shape of it. That's his to tell, and yours, and I'm not going to make you stand in it again by asking. But I'll tell you a thing, and then I'm going to go bail the bilge, because she's taking water faster than I like." She paused, and Priya felt the pause the way you feel a hand extended in the dark — not grabbing, just there. "The first person I ever saw killed, I was nineteen. I stood behind a fuel drum and I watched, and I did nothing, and then I ran, and I ran for a long time after that. Twenty years, near enough." She said it flatly, the way you'd read coordinates. "The doing-nothing doesn't go away. But it gets lighter, if you put it down in front of somebody once. Not for them. For you. So it's in the world instead of just in you. Things kept in the body rot. Things said out loud keep." She stood, joints cracking, and gathered her rag. "Bilge won't bail itself. You know where I am."

She went below. She had never once, in all of it, looked at Priya with pity. Priya understood, in a way that arrived whole and wordless, that this was the whole of the gift. Pity was a debt someone else tried to pay on your behalf, and it made you smaller. Jonah offered no debt. She offered a rope, tied off, not pulled.

Priya sat with the rain for another hour. The light went from green to grey to the blue-grey of early evening. The fireflies came out over the cove, dozens of them, blinking their small green arithmetic. She counted them. She got to forty-one and lost the count and found it again, and somewhere between forty-one and the losing of it, she stood up.

* * *

The stern cabin was warm and close and smelled of diesel, mildew, and the sharp medicinal bite of whatever Elias had been cleaning Sefu's wound with. Dr. Imara Sefu lay on the narrow bunk with her left thigh wrapped, her skin the grey of wet paper, her eyes open and fixed on the overhead beam with the expression of a woman doing long division she did not expect to finish.

She saw Priya in the doorway and did not smile. That, too, was a mercy. Smiling would have required something.

"Anand," Sefu said. Her voice was a ruin of its lecture-hall authority, but the name was exact — surname, always surname, the same as the first day in the field station when she'd handed Priya a core sample and said, *Anand, tell me what this rock has been through.* "You're eating?"

"Yes, ma'am."

The words came out cracked, like a door opening on a rusted hinge, and Priya heard her own voice as if it belonged to a stranger — higher than she remembered, smaller. Sefu's eyes came off the beam and found her, and for a moment the fever in them receded and Priya saw the mind underneath, the famous mind, still working even now, still noting.

"Nine days," Sefu said. "I wondered when it would come back. Voices are like rivers in flood, Anand. They find their old channels eventually." She shifted, winced, and waved at the crate that served as a chair. "Sit, if you're going to sit. Standing in doorways is for ghosts."

Priya sat. Her hands were shaking. She put them flat on her knees and watched them shake, and made herself count the tremors, one, two, three, until the counting did what Jonah said it did — gave the mind a rope.

"Ma'am," she said. "Mateo and Dario."

Sefu closed her eyes. "Yes."

"I saw it."

"I know."

"I ran."

"I know that too," Sefu said, and there was no absolution in it, no forgiveness, only fact, and that was why Priya could bear it. "And I will tell you something, Anand, and you may believe it or not, but it is true. If you had stayed, you would be dead, and the survey would have no witnesses, and the men who did it would have gotten away with it cleanly. You running is the only reason anyone will ever know what happened in that narrows. You are not the coward of that story. You are the record of it." Her breath caught; the infection had her lungs now too, Priya could hear it. "The record is the most dangerous thing on this river. That's why they're burning villages for it. Remember that, the next time the inside of your head tells you that you did nothing."

Priya's eyes were burning. She didn't wipe them. In this cabin, with this dying woman, it seemed important not to perform even the small dishonesties.

"I want to say it," she said. Her voice was steadier now, finding its channel. "All of it. Out loud. Before it rots."

Sefu opened her eyes. "Then say it to the captain," she said. "Not to me. I'm going to be dead soon, and the dead are easy to talk to. The living are the ones worth doing it in front of." She turned her face back to the beam. "And Anand. When you're done — ask her about the fire. She'll tell you, or she won't. But ask."

* * *

Jonah was at the bow, as she was every evening, looking upstream. The rain had thinned to a drizzle. The cigarette was lit this time, its coal the only warm color in the cove. Priya came along the deck on legs that felt borrowed and stopped beside her, and Jonah, without turning, moved the cigarette hand to the windward side so the smoke wouldn't drift into her face.

They stood like that while the river rose. Priya could hear it now, if she listened — not a sound, exactly, but a presence, the deep patient pressure of all that water looking for somewhere to go. She had read a paper once about how a flood is not an event but a decision the watershed makes over weeks. She thought: this is like that. This is a decision I have been making for nine days.

"Mateo Ocampo and Dario Reyes," she said.

Jonah didn't move. The cigarette smoke went up straight in the still air.

"Porters, second contract. Mateo had a daughter in Puerto Alegre he was saving to send to school. Dario was — Dario laughed at everything, he called the leeches *los jefes*, the bosses, because they took a cut of everything you earned." Her voice did not shake. She had expected it to shake, and it didn't, and that was its own strange grief. "On the ninth of the month, at the narrows below the terrace, a gunboat with a grey hull and a company stencil came out of the mist. There were six men visible. One of them had a shaved head and a scar through his eyebrow. Mateo said *we are with the Sefu survey, we have papers*. The man with the shaved head said —" and here it came, the sentence she had been carrying for nine days, hot as a coal, and she set it down in the world, out loud, in front of a witness, exactly as Jonah had promised she could — "he said, *papers don't float.* And then he shot them. Two shots. And the birds went up."

The river took the words and carried them away, and they were in the world now, and they were not only in her.

Jonah looked at the water for a long moment. Then she said, "Papers don't float."

"That's what he said."

"That's what they all say," Jonah said. "In one language or another. I've heard it in three." She dropped the cigarette into the river, where it hissed and vanished, and turned, and for the first time she looked at Priya directly — a long, level look, no softness in it, and no pity either, only a kind of recognition, one runner acknowledging another across a gap of twenty years. "You held your case over your head while you swam. Tomas said. The survey case."

Priya nodded. "It had the channel charts in it. Nadia's charts."

"And you held them over the water like they were worth two lives."

"I didn't think. I just —"

"I know what you just," Jonah said. "You just held what was true over the flood. That's the whole job, as far as I can tell. Everything else is ballast." She looked upstream again, at the darkening country, and her voice dropped into something quieter, almost to herself. "Sefu tell you to ask me about the fire?"

"She did."

Jonah's jaw worked. Downriver, far off, thunder muttered like something turning over in its sleep. When she spoke, it was slowly, each word costing what words cost.

"There was a town," she said. "A long way from here. And a man I trusted, who sold what the river gave him to people who burn things. And a fire. And I left, and I've been leaving ever since, and I'm not going to tell you the rest tonight, because it's not mine to spend all at once." She looked at Priya. "But here's what I'll tell you for free, and you can keep it. The ones who do the burning count on the survivors running. Running is the second half of their weapon. Every town I left behind, I finished their work for them. Every one." She put her hand on the rail, and Priya saw that it was not quite steady, and understood what it cost this woman to say even this much. "We're not running anymore. Not this boat. Not this trip. So whatever's in your head that tells you to be small, to be silent, to be gone — you tell it the same thing I tell mine. Not this time."

The rain had stopped. The clouds were breaking apart in the west, and a single bar of last light came down the cove and lay across the water like a plank bridge to nowhere. In the stern cabin, faintly, they could hear Sefu coughing. Below the deck, the bilge pump cycled, and cycled again — the boat telling them, in its own language, that the river was winning, for now, meter by meter.

Priya breathed. It went all the way down for the first time in nine days.

"The man with the shaved head," she said. "I would know him again. Anywhere. In any light."

Jonah nodded slowly, and Priya saw the thought move through her face like a fish under ice — the thought that a witness is a weapon, and that the river was carrying them, whether they willed it or not, back upstream toward the man who had said *papers don't float*.

"Then get some sleep," Jonah said. "Because come morning, we're going to need every true thing on this boat."
