## 21. The Sunken Terrace

The river changed its mind about being a river two hours before we saw the ruins.

Jonah felt it first in the wheel — a softness in the current, as if the water had stopped arguing with itself and begun to simply pour, one vast muscle flexing all in the same direction. The Marisol's bow swung a half-point starboard without her touching anything, and when she corrected, the rudder answered late, sluggish, like a tired animal.

"Flood pulse," she said, mostly to herself, but Elias was already at her shoulder with the binoculars, and Priya had come up from below with Tomas limping behind her, both of them squinting into the haze like men reading scripture at too great a distance.

"Where?" Priya asked. Her voice was still new to her, rusty from weeks of silence, and she used it sparingly, the way other people spent money they didn't trust.

Jonah lifted her chin toward the wall of green ahead. "Past that bend. Nadia's markers say the channel narrows, then opens." She tapped the logbook where it lay open on the chart table, weighted at the corners with a coffee mug, a wrench, and a brass shell casing she'd never explained to anyone. "Then it opens onto something that isn't a channel anymore."

Elias turned the binoculars east, following the ridgeline where the rain had thinned to a drifting silver gauze. "You've seen them before?"

"No one's seen them." Jonah eased the throttle back a notch, listening to the engine's note change, cataloguing every rattle against the catalogue she kept in her head. "That's the point of them."

* * *

Nadia Wren's charts were unlike anything Jonah had ever piloted by. Most surveyors drew water; this woman had drawn *time*. The pages showed the basin not as it was but as it had been across centuries — old channels ghosted beneath new ones in faded blue, trade routes marked in a careful hand, and everywhere small annotations in three languages, some of which Priya could read aloud and some of which made even her go quiet.

*Here the river took the temple road, 1911.*
*Ferry stone visible at low water only. Do not trust it.*
*My grandmother said the terrace was a crown before it was a ruin.*

"She thought in layers," Elias had said the night before, turning a page with a tenderness he showed nothing else on earth. "Everything that ever happened here is still happening, if you know how to look down."

Now Jonah looked down, and what she saw looking back made her hands go cold on the wheel despite the wet heat.

The channel bent north around a bluff furred with bamboo, and beyond the bluff the forest simply *stopped* — cut off in a clean arc, as though a giant had pressed a bowl into the earth and everything inside it had drowned. Water stretched away gray-green to a horizon broken by shapes that did not make sense at first glance: lines too straight for hills, angles too deliberate for geology. Terraces. Seven of them, maybe eight, rising out of the flood in stepped platforms hundreds of meters wide, each one crowned with the stumps of walls and the black ribs of doorways, so that the whole structure rose from the water like a crown someone had dropped in a puddle and left for the world to forget.

Water poured over the lowest terraces in slow, silent cataracts, spilling from one platform to the next, and where it fell the sound came across the distance to them — not loud, but vast, a hush deeper than any silence Jonah had ever heard. It sounded like the river breathing in its sleep.

"Holy God," Tomas whispered.

"That's not in any chart I've ever seen," Jonah said. "Not any government survey, not any missionary map, nothing."

"It wouldn't be." Priya's voice had gone thin. "Dr. Sefu said — she said the old people downstream called it the place where the water remembers. She thought that was just a story." She swallowed. "It isn't a story."

The Marisol drifted forward into the drowned city's shadow, and the temperature seemed to drop five degrees, the air going cool and mineral, smelling of wet stone and something older — crushed leaves, maybe, or incense, or just time itself given a smell. Birds Jonah couldn't name moved through the flooded treetops that grew from the second terrace, their calls echoing off stone with strange doubled harmonics, as if each bird were singing with its own ghost.

Jonah had run rivers for twenty years. She had shot canyons whose walls had never known a name in any living language, had anchored in bays where the depth-sounding line ran out and kept running, had seen crocodiles the size of dugouts and floods that erased towns overnight. None of it had prepared her for the feeling of approaching something that had been waiting. That was the only word for it. The ruins didn't loom. They *waited*, patient as a held breath, and every meter the Marisol covered brought them closer to the exhale.

"Keep us off the third terrace shoal," she said, and was grateful her voice came out level. "Nadia marked it. The stones are just under the surface and they'll open our hull like a tin can."

Elias was at the rail, gripping it hard enough that his knuckles stood white. He wasn't looking at the architecture. He was scanning the terraces, terrace by terrace, top to bottom, with the systematic hunger of a man searching a crowd for one face.

"Nadia stood up there," he said quietly. "Somewhere on those stones, she stood and drew these charts. She was *here*, Jonah."

"I know."

"You don't know what that—" He stopped himself. Breathed. When he spoke again his voice was rougher. "Eleven days she's been dark. Eleven days, and now I'm looking at the last place on earth she put her feet, and I can't even tell if—"

He didn't finish. Jonah let him not finish. There were things you said to a man at moments like this and things you didn't, and the most useful thing she knew how to give him was the dignity of unfinished sentences.

"We'll find out," she said instead. "One way or the other. That's why we came."

* * *

They took the Marisol in along the northern approach, threading the channel Nadia had marked between two submerged causeways, and the closer they came the more the scale of the place undid them. The lowest terrace alone was larger than Callao Verde's entire waterfront district. Staircases wide enough for twenty abreast descended into the flood and vanished, their steps worn into shallow bowls by feet that had been climbing them for centuries before anyone now alive had been born. Doorways opened off the flooded walls at the waterline, black mouths breathing cold air, and once — Jonah was sure of it, though neither Elias nor Priya would confirm it — something moved in one of them, a pale suggestion of motion, there and gone.

"Fish," Elias said.

"Maybe," Jonah said.

Tomas had gone quiet in the way geologists went quiet, which was worse than fear. He crouched at the rail trailing his fingers in the water and came up with a fragment of pottery no bigger than a coin, its surface incised with a spiral pattern worn almost smooth. He held it in his palm for a long moment, then set it gently on the rail as if returning it to an owner.

"This predates the colony," he said. "This predates *everything* we have records for. Dr. Sefu used to argue with the ministry about settlement timelines. They told her she was dreaming." He laughed, a strange cracked sound. "She wasn't dreaming. She was reading."

The smoke changed everything.

Priya saw it first — she had the eyes of the young and the vigilance of the hunted, and she'd barely spoken since the night by the fire when she'd told Jonah about the porters, in a voice so flat it had frightened Jonah more than tears would have. Now she gripped Jonah's arm and pointed without a word.

Far side. High up, near the crown of the ruins where the seventh terrace met the sky. A thread of smoke, thin and gray, rising steady from behind a collapsed wall, bending with the wind, unmistakable.

Smoke meant fire. Fire meant people. And after the burned village, after the relay station, after the corporate crates stenciled with the same clean logo — smoke meant *them*, or it meant Sefu, and there was no way to know which from this distance, and either answer might kill them.

Jonah killed the engine.

The silence that followed was enormous. The Marisol drifted, rocking gently on the long swells, and the only sounds were the spill of water over the lower terraces and the drip of rain off the canopy and the creak of the boat's timbers settling, and under all of it that vast hush, that breathing of the drowned city, as if the whole structure were listening along with them.

"Options," Elias said.

"We anchor in the lee of the second terrace, out of sightline from the smoke." Jonah was already moving, throwing out the stern anchor, watching the line pay out over water gone suddenly deep and dark. "We take the skiff in close at dusk, land on the fourth terrace stairs, and go up quiet. If it's Sefu, we're friends arriving carefully. If it's the company—" She shrugged. "Then we'll know where they are, and knowing is worth more than ammunition out here."

"And if it's both?"

Jonah looked at him. Rain dripped off the brim of his hat; his face beneath it was drawn and set, and she realized how much weight he'd lost since Callao Verde, how much of him had been burned away mile by mile the way a wick burns.

"Then it's both," she said. "And we do what captains do. We read the water and we pick our moment."

Tomas cleared his throat. "There's something else." He pointed down, past the hull, into the water alongside. "Look."

Jonah leaned over the rail. The water was dark, tea-colored with tannin, but the sun broke through the cloud cover at that moment — a single ragged shaft — and lit the depths below the boat.

Stones. Cut stones, laid in courses, descending into the dark in orderly rows. A street. An entire street, running away beneath them into the flooded deep, lined with the shadows of walls, and here and there the paler rectangles of doorways, and — impossibly, heartbreakingly — the shape of a fountain or a well at the street's center, its basin still intact, holding its little circle of darker water within the greater water like a cup within a cup.

A town. Not a temple complex, not a palace. A town, with streets and wells and doors, and people had lived in it, argued in it, married in it, buried their dead above its flood line and then been drowned anyway, and now it lay down there in the dark doing whatever drowned towns do with their centuries.

"The water remembers," Priya said softly. "That's what the old people said. I thought it was poetry." She shook her head slowly. "It's a warning. Everything the river takes, it keeps. Nothing down there is gone. It's just *below*."

Jonah stared down into the street until the sun-shaft closed and the stones dissolved back into darkness, and she thought — with a chill that had nothing to do with the ruined city and everything to do with herself — about all the towns she had left behind, and whether they too were down there somewhere in her own floodwater, intact, patient, keeping everything she wanted forgotten. Whether you could outrun a thing the way the river couldn't be outrun by its own drowned streets.

"Anchor's set," Elias said. "Skiff's ready."

"Not yet." Jonah straightened, forcing her eyes up from the water to the smoking crown of the terraces, and made her voice into the flat competent instrument it was built to be. "We wait for the light to fail. We eat, we check the leak, we load the rifle and the flare gun and every blade on this boat. And nobody — *nobody* — goes in the water. Whatever's down there has waited four hundred years. It can keep."

* * *

Dusk came early, the way it did in monsoon country — not a sunset but a dimming, the light throttled down like a lamp turned low, until the ruins became a silhouette and the smoke became a darker smudge against a darker sky. By the time they lowered the skiff, the first night insects had started up in the drowned treetops, and the frogs had answered, and the whole basin rang with small urgent voices announcing themselves to the dark.

Jonah took the tiller. Elias sat in the bow with the rifle across his knees, and Priya between them with a paddle she insisted on even though her hands were shaking, because, she said, she was done hiding. Tomas stayed aboard the Marisol with orders to keep the engine warm and the lanterns dark and to run for the channel if they weren't back by midnight — orders he received with the grim relief of a man who had done enough running for one lifetime.

They crossed the flooded plaza in silence, oars muffled with rags, the skiff sliding over streets and doorways and gardens that four hundred years ago had held laughter and cooking smoke and children's feet on those same drowned stairs. Once, directly beneath the hull, a fish rolled — or something did — and the wake of it spread in a slow ring that caught the last gray light and held it, a perfect circle expanding outward over a buried town, and no one in the boat said a word about it.

The fourth-terrace stairs rose out of the water ahead, black stone slick with rain, and Jonah brought the skiff alongside with the gentlest touch of her life, and they climbed.

The stone under her hands was astonishingly smooth, worn by generations of palms, and warm — warmer than the air, as if the ruins kept some slow heat of their own, some banked fire of all that accumulated living. Steps. More steps. Terraces stacked above them like the decks of a ship the size of a mountain, and everywhere the fallen architecture of a civilization that had understood water better than anyone alive — channels and cisterns and spillways carved into every surface, the whole city built as an instrument for conducting the flood, riding it, surviving it.

"They didn't fight the river," Jonah murmured, half to herself. "They built for it."

"Like someone else I know," Elias said quietly, and she chose not to hear it.

On the sixth terrace, they found the first body.

It had been dead for days — a porter, by his dress, laid out with care in the lee of a wall, hands folded, face turned to the sky. Someone had covered him with a poncho weighted at the corners with stones. Beside him, scratched into the stone with a knife point, a single word in Spanish: *ESPEREN.* Wait.

Priya made a small sound and turned away. Elias knelt, touched the poncho's edge, and stood again with his jaw working.

"There'll be more," Jonah said. "Or there won't. Keep moving."

They crested the seventh terrace as full dark settled, and stopped.

Below them, in the great sunken court at the crown of the ruins, shielded from the river side by a curtain wall, a camp glowed — two tents, a tarp strung between columns, a cookfire burning low and disciplined, and figures moving among it. Four figures. Maybe five. Too far to see faces, too dark to see weapons, though Jonah's eye found the shape of a long gun propped against a column and her stomach tightened around it.

And beyond the camp, half-hidden behind a fallen lintel the size of a bus, a doorway — a doorway unlike the others, framed in stone carved with spirals and standing figures, its threshold sealed with a slab that had been pulled aside just wide enough for a person to pass. Lamplight leaked from the gap in a thin gold line. From somewhere inside the dark beyond it came a faint rhythmic sound that might have been digging, or sorting, or prayer.

"Is it them?" Elias breathed. "The company? Is it Sefu?"

Jonah watched the figures move. Watched one pause at the fire and lift something to drink. Watched another walk the perimeter with the unhurried economy of a sentry who expected no trouble — or wanted you to think so.

Then the wind shifted, carrying the woodsmoke across their position, and with it a voice — a woman's voice, low and hoarse and carrying the particular clipped authority of someone accustomed to being obeyed by people smarter than she was, calling out an instruction in a language Jonah didn't know, and hearing it answered.

"That's her," Elias whispered, and his voice broke on it. "*That's her. Imara. That's Sefu.*"

Jonah put a hand on his arm and pressed him down behind the fallen lintel, her eyes never leaving the camp, counting figures again, counting weapons, counting the minutes since the sentry's last circuit, feeling the whole night hold its breath around them — the drowned city below, the sealed doorway leaking its gold line of light, the dying woman's voice drifting up out of the fireglow.

Four defenders. One door. One archive that powerful men would burn villages to possess, and somewhere downriver, two gunboats' worth of reasons to believe they'd come for it.

"All right," Jonah breathed. "Now we learn what the water keeps."
