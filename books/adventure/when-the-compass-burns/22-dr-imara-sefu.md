## 22. Dr. Imara Sefu

The fever had been keeping time for three days now, and Imara Sefu had learned to read it the way she read strata: by its layers, by what each one deposited and what it took away.

In the mornings, when the mist still clung to the flooded terraces like gauze over a wound, the fever was a low hum beneath her thoughts — an ache in the thigh where the machete had opened her, a heat that made the stone under her palms feel warm when she knew it was cold. She could work then. She had catalogued forty-one artifacts from the secondary chamber in those morning hours, her handwriting steady enough, her mind sharp enough to catch her own errors. Discipline was a muscle. She had been flexing this particular one since she was nine years old, since her mother had told her that a brilliant woman would be forgiven nothing, and that forgiveness was overrated anyway.

By midday the hum became a drumbeat. By evening, a flood of its own.

She sat now in the doorway of the upper terrace shrine — the expedition's last defensible position, a chamber of carved basalt open to the river on three sides, its floor raised two meters above the flood line and connected to the lower terraces by a single stair that Ravel had once called *the throat*. She had liked him for that. She had liked him for a great many things, and that was the ledger she could not stop auditing, even now, even with the infection climbing her leg like ivy and two people left alive who answered when she spoke.

Ravel had sold them. She knew it the way she knew carbon dating was approximate and everyone lied about their error bars: not with certainty, but with a confidence so complete it functioned as truth. The gunboats had come on the ninth day, four hours after Ravel had taken the survey skiff "to check the downstream markers." The porters had run. Two had not run fast enough. And Imara had lain in the mud of the third terrace with a machete wound in her thigh, watching men in corporate grey carry crates out of the vault antechamber, and she had thought — with a clarity that the fever had not yet burned away — *I built this. I chose every one of them.*

Below her, the water stretched brown and endless to the tree line. Where the lower city had been there was now only current and the tips of stelae breaking the surface like drowned men's fingers. Eleven days ago this had been the greatest discovery of her career. Now it was a grave with good acoustics.

"Doctor."

Tomas. He came up the stair with that careful flatness he'd adopted since the executions, carrying the tin basin and the boiled water, and behind him Priya moved the way she always moved now — close to walls, close to shadows, a girl trying to become furniture. Imara had tried to draw her out twice and failed twice, and she did not have the luxury of a third attempt. Grief that wouldn't speak was grief that would eventually detonate, and Imara needed every charge accounted for before the company came back.

"Set it down." She pulled back the bandage herself, refusing the flinch, refusing Tomas's hands near the wound. The leg was bad. She had known it was bad for two days and had said nothing, because the moment she said it aloud, someone would have to make a decision about her, and Imara Sefu had spent forty years ensuring that all decisions about Imara Sefu were made by Imara Sefu.

The flesh around the cut had gone the color of storm-bruised fruit, red shading into violet shading into a yellow-white at the margins that she recognized from field manuals and autopsies both. Lymphangitis. Red streaks climbing toward the hip like lightning frozen mid-branch. In Callao Verde they'd have debrided it, packed it, pumped her full of antibiotics and lectures about field hygiene. Here they had boiled rainwater, a first-aid kit two expeditions old, and half a course of amoxicillin she'd been rationing like gold.

"How is it," she said, which was not a question.

Tomas looked at the leg, then at her face, and had the decency not to perform hope. "It's holding," he said. "Barely."

"Barely is a unit of measurement. Write it down." She let him re-dress the wound, her jaw set against the pain, and watched the water instead. Somewhere out there, past the drowned mission church and the narrows where the current ran white over submerged fences, the river went on doing what rivers did — carrying everything toward the sea and remembering nothing. She had always envied that. Rivers got to forget. People were stuck being archives.

* * *

The archive. Even the word in her own head had become a kind of prayer and a kind of accusation.

They had found the vault on the sixth day, behind a wall of fitted stone that Nadia's charts had predicted to within a hand's width — Nadia Wren, who drew channels the way other people drew breath, who had stood in front of the opened wall with tears running silently down her face and said, *"It's a library. Imara. It's a whole civilization's library, and it's been waiting."*

And Imara had felt — she admitted this to no one, she admitted it barely to herself — not wonder first, but appetite. The records inside mapped the basin. Every tributary, every buried claim, every hidden channel and ancestral boundary and water right negotiated across eight centuries before the first colonial map ever lied about this country. Whoever held those records held the plumbing of nations. She had understood that instantly, completely, the way she understood stratigraphy — and she had understood, in the same instant, that there were men upstream who would burn villages for it, and that her name on the discovery meant her name on the target.

So she had sealed the antechamber, distributed the location knowledge to no single person, and gone dark. No radio. No check-ins. Let the fixers and the funders and the corporate vultures believe the expedition lost or dead. She had planned to come back with leverage, with lawyers, with a coalition of universities and basin governments too broad to intimidate.

That had been the plan. The plan had assumed Ravel wasn't for sale. The plan had assumed the monsoon would hold off eleven more days. The plan had assumed Imara Sefu was a woman whose body would obey her will simply because her will had never once been refused.

The fever laughed at that. The fever laughed at all of it.

* * *

On the fourth day of the silence, she heard the engine.

She was lying in the shrine's inner chamber, sweating through the drumbeat hours, when the sound reached her — not the deep diesel churn of a company gunboat but something smaller, older, laboring. A riverboat working hard against the flood current. Her first thought was *mercenaries returning for the rest*, and she was on her feet before her leg finished reporting its objections, pistol in hand, ordering Tomas to the stair and Priya into the inner dark with instructions delivered in a whisper that cost her more than the standing had.

They came up the river side in a dugout first — two figures paddling hard, and Imara put the pistol's sight on the lead figure's chest and held it there through the long seconds of approach, breathing the way the fever allowed, which was badly.

Tomas saw them first through the rain. "Doctor—" His voice cracked open. "It's Priya's— no. It's *them*. It's Wren's brother and—" He turned, and his face was doing something complicated. "There's a pilot. A woman. They came by boat."

*A boat.* Through the narrows, in the opening monsoon, against a flood current that had already eaten two of her survey lines. Either mad or magnificent, and history suggested the categories overlapped.

Imara lowered the pistol halfway and did not lower it further. "Let them climb," she said. "And Tomas — if anything about them reads wrong, you drop the first one and I drop the second. We are not a rescue operation. We are what's left of one."

* * *

They came up the throat of the stair into her light, and Imara Sefu conducted the meeting sitting down, because standing had stopped being negotiable an hour ago, and she refused to meet her legend's end swaying like a drunkard.

The man introduced himself as Elias Wren, and his eyes went straight past her to Priya, and whatever he said next he said to the girl, softly, and Priya — furniture-girl, wall-hugging Priya — crossed the chamber and took his hand, and something in Imara's chest registered the sight and filed it away as *leverage, later, handle gently*.

The woman was another matter.

She stood in the doorway with river water sheeting off her oilskin, gray-eyed, sun-browned, moving with the economy of someone who wasted nothing, not motion, not words, not attention. She looked at the barricade, at the pistol, at the wounded doctor propped against a fallen stela like a queen on a broken throne, and Imara watched her do the arithmetic — *supplies, exits, odds* — and finish it faster than most people finished greetings.

"You're Sefu," the woman said.

"I'm told I am." Imara let the fever rasp show in her voice; hiding weakness cost energy she'd begun to budget like the amoxicillin. "And you're the captain who brought my people upriver through the narrows in monsoon season. Which means either you didn't know better, or you did and came anyway. I find I respect both, and trust neither."

Something flickered at the corner of the woman's mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment between professionals. "Jonah Vasquez. Your fixer in Callao Verde hired me to run supplies. Your expedition missed check-ins."

"My fixer," Imara said, "is a weathervane with a bank account. Whatever he told you, he told you because someone paid him to say it, and whoever paid him wanted either me found or me confirmed dead. Possibly both. Welcome to archaeology's modern era — we've replaced brushes with ledgers." She shifted against the stela, and the leg sent its white flare up through her, and she rode it with her face still, because these strangers would see her still or they would see nothing. "You found the logbook, I assume. Nadia's."

Elias's head came up sharply. "You know about—"

"I know Nadia planted things the way orchids grow on trees — somewhere you'd only find them if you were already looking. If her logbook reached you, it reached you through the fixer, which means the fixer has known about the Sunken Terrace since before you cast off." She let that land, watched it land, watched the glance pass between Vasquez and Wren that confirmed everything. Good. Aligned fears made faster allies than aligned hopes. "Which means the company knows the route. Which means the gunboats you slipped past — you did slip past one, don't bother looking surprised, Tomas counted their generator noise two nights ago — are not the last of them."

The rain thickened against the basalt. Somewhere below, the floodwater licked another centimeter up the drowned stair, patient as bookkeeping.

"So here is where we are," Imara said, and heard her own voice go flat and precise, the lecture voice, the voice that had filled halls in four countries. "I am dying. Not dramatically — administratively. The wound has decided its schedule without consulting me, and I have perhaps days of useful cognition left, fewer of useful legs. I have two crew, one of whom hasn't spoken voluntarily in a week. I have a discovery downstairs that maps every secret this basin ever kept — every channel, every claim, every village's ancient right to its own water — and men upstream who will burn everything between here and the coast to own it. And I have a boat at my dock for the first time in eleven days." She looked at Jonah Vasquez directly, gray eyes into gray eyes, and gave her the truth raw, because lies were for people with time. "I do not intend to surrender that archive. I also cannot carry it. So before anyone proposes heroism, understand: I am ruthless, I am infected, and I am the only person alive who knows which stones in that vault matter and which ones are bait. You need me conscious. I need you capable. Let's establish which of us is negotiating from which position."

Silence, except the rain.

Then Elias Wren stepped forward, and his face had gone through grief and arrived somewhere harder. "My sister died for your secrecy," he said. "Or she will have, if we don't move. So before terms — tell me where she is."

And Imara Sefu, who had audited every soul on her expedition and found Ravel wanting too late, looked at the brother of her cartographer and felt the fever and the guilt arrive together, indistinguishable, and answered him honestly, because honesty was the only currency she had left that hadn't been counterfeited:

"Nadia is alive. She took the index crate and the survey skiff south the night the gunboats came — her choice, her plan, over my objection. If she kept to her own markers, she's ahead of you, not behind." She paused, and let the ruthlessness show through the exhaustion like bone through torn skin. "Now. Shall we discuss what your boat can carry, Captain Vasquez — and how much daylight we have before the company comes back for the rest?"

Above them, thunder rolled down the valley like a door closing, and the river rose.
