## 23. Terms of Survival

The terraces smelled of wet stone and old smoke.

Elias climbed the last flooded stair with water to his knees, one hand on the slick basalt rail that some mason had carved eight hundred years ago — he could feel the grooves of it under his palm, deliberate as handwriting — and came up into a courtyard where the rain fell through a broken roof of carved constellations and pooled around three figures who looked like they'd been expecting death for days and were merely annoyed it was late.

Dr. Imara Sefu sat on an overturned crate with her left leg stretched out before her, wrapped in dressings gone the color of weak tea. She held a pistol in her right hand the way other people hold pens: casually, constantly, an extension of thought rather than a threat. Behind her, a broad-shouldered man Elias took for Ravel stood in the shadow of a colonnade with a rifle slung loose, watching the stairway more than the newcomers. A third figure — a woman with a bandaged arm — kept her position at the far archway, where the floodwater lapped against stone steps that descended into brown murk.

"You're not the company," Sefu said. It wasn't a question. Her voice was dry as the paper she'd spent her life among, and just as flammable. "They announce themselves. They like you to know who's killing you."

"Jonah Vasquez," Jonah said from behind him, water streaming off her oilskin. "I was hired to run supplies upriver. Two missed check-ins."

"And you brought passengers."

"One passenger." Jonah's chin tipped toward Elias. "He invited himself."

"I paid my way," Elias said.

Sefu's eyes found him then, and Elias understood why men twice her size spoke of her in lowered voices. She was small, gray-streaked, sixty if she was a day, and she looked at people the way she presumably looked at core samples — already knowing what was inside them, deciding only whether it was worth mentioning.

"Wren," she said.

His whole body went still. Rain ticked on the flooded flagstones. Somewhere below, thunder rolled along the river valley like furniture dragged across a floor.

"My sister wrote to you," Elias said carefully. "Nadia. I have her letters. I have—"

"I know what you have." Sefu shifted on the crate, and the movement cost her something; Ravel took half a step forward and she waved him back without looking. "She told me about you. The brother who reads maps like novels. The brother who stayed." Something moved through her face — grief, or its fossil record. "Sit down, Wren. You're going to want to be sitting."

* * *

They sat under the broken constellation roof, four of them and a dying woman, while Sefu laid it out with the economy of someone who had rehearsed this argument in the dark for eleven days.

The vault was real. Not gold, not idols, not anything a museum would glass-case — better and worse than that. A pre-colonial archive, sealed beneath the lowest terrace when the first city here drowned, containing the basin's own memory of itself. Survey records. Boundary stones transcribed. Every hidden tributary charted by people who had lived on this water for a thousand years before anyone thought to name it. And threaded through all of it — this was the part Sefu said slowly, watching each of their faces — the land claims. The old ones. The true boundaries of territories that modern maps had erased, redrawn, sold out from under the villages that still remembered, in their bones, where their ground began.

"Do you understand what that is?" she asked. "A company upstream can burn a village because no document says the village exists. This archive says every village exists. Every boundary. Every treaty the colonial governments pretended never happened. It isn't history, Wren. It's ammunition."

"It's a target," Elias said.

"It's both. That's the nature of anything true."

Jonah hadn't spoken. She sat with her forearms on her knees, dripping, her eyes moving — Elias had learned to track where they went — to the water stains on the walls, the flood line, the stairways, the sightlines. Reading the ruin the way she read the river. He filed that away and pressed on, because pressing on was the only thing he knew how to do anymore.

"Nadia found it," he said.

"Nadia found the way in. Three years of survey work, disguised as flood-mapping." Sefu's voice roughened. "She was the best field cartographer I've ever employed, and I've employed thieves, geniuses, and one man who could smell water through limestone. When we opened the vault chamber, she cried. Did you know that? Your sister stood in front of eight centuries of her own profession's ancestors and wept like a child."

Elias's throat closed. He looked down at his hands until he could speak. "Where is she now?"

Sefu didn't answer immediately, and the silence had a shape, and the shape was bad.

"The company came nine days ago," she said instead. "Not soldiers at first — surveyors, engineers, a man named Ostrer who smiled too much and offered me a consulting contract worth more than my university's endowment. Selective access, he called it. We digitize the archive, and certain interested parties receive certain pages. The rest goes into a private vault in the capital." She turned her head and spat, precisely, into the floodwater. "I told him what he could do with his contract. Two nights later they came back with guns. We lost four porters getting out of the lower chambers. Nadia—" 

The pause again. Longer this time. Rain filled it.

"Nadia stayed behind to collapse the entrance tunnel. To buy us time to move the records up here." Sefu's eyes fixed on the middle distance, on something nine days old. "The charges went off. The tunnel came down. She didn't come out."

The courtyard tilted. Elias heard his own breathing, harsh and foreign. Nine days. His sister had been dead or buried alive for nine days while he'd been haggling with fixers in Callao Verde, lying to a riverboat captain about being a grieving brother when grief was the one honest thing he carried.

"She might be alive," he said. "Behind a collapsed tunnel there could be—"

"Elias." Jonah's voice, low. Not unkind. Just true.

"There are air pockets in limestone," he said anyway, because the alternative was not saying it. "Karst systems hold air for weeks. If she got into a side gallery—"

"Then she got into a side gallery," Sefu said, "and she is the single most resourceful person I have ever known, and if anyone in this world could survive nine days underground, it is your sister. I am not telling you she's dead, Wren. I'm telling you I don't know, and that I have not had the luxury of digging." She gestured at the crates stacked in the colonnade shadows — dozens of them, waterproofed, sealed, labeled in her own cramped hand. "Because of that."

* * *

It went wrong, as Elias had known it would, within the hour.

"We evacuate," he said. "All of you, the wounded, the records we can carry — onto the Marisol, downstream, tonight. The company's gunboats are anchored above the narrows; we slipped past them once, we can do it again. You get the archive to Callao Verde, to the university, to journalists, to—"

"No."

The word landed flat and final as a vault door.

"Sefu—"

"Doctor Sefu," she corrected, mildly, which somehow made it worse. "Wren. Sit with me. Look at the water."

He looked. The flood had swallowed the lower terraces entirely; the courtyard they occupied would be underwater within days, maybe hours, if the river kept climbing. The drowned mission church they'd passed two days ago floated in his memory — its bell tower rising from brown water like a drowning man's last gesture.

"In seventy-two hours," Sefu said, "this entire site will be ten meters under. The vault chamber is already sealed by the flood — which, incidentally, protects it better than any wall I could build. The company cannot reach it either. Do you know what that means?"

"That you've won by doing nothing."

"That the river has taken my side." For the first time, something almost like humor crossed her face, dry as dust. "But it also means everything in those crates is the last copy in existence. Once the water takes this terrace, whatever leaves here in the next three days is all there will ever be. And I will not put it on one leaking riverboat captained by a woman who won't tell me her real name and guarded by a man who lied his way aboard." Her eyes flicked between them. "Yes, I know. I've been besieged for eleven days; you think I don't recognize liars? I employ them professionally."

Ravel made a sound that might have been a laugh, quickly smothered.

"So what's your plan?" Elias demanded. "Wait it out? Dig the tunnel yourself with a broken leg? Hand it to Ostrer after all?"

"My plan is that the archive does not move until it can be moved properly — catalogued, copied, distributed so that no single hand holds it. That requires time, equipment, and people I trust. I have none of those things. So I wait, I guard what I can, and I accept that I may die on this terrace with my work intact rather than see it parceled out to whoever has the fastest boat." She lifted the pistol slightly, an emphasis rather than a threat. "That is my term, Wren. The archive stays."

"And the people?" His voice cracked upward and he hated it. "Tomas and Priya adrift in a dugout. Four porters dead. Your leg is rotting off you — I can smell it from here, Doctor. How many lives is paper worth?"

"As many as necessary." She said it without flinching, and that was the terrible thing — she meant it. "You bury knowledge and you bury everyone who ever drew a boundary on a map. You let Ostrer's employers buy the truth and every village on this river spends the next century losing lawsuits to men who never walked the ground. I have watched what happens when documents die, Wren. I grew up in a country that lost its paperwork to a fire, and I watched a generation discover they legally did not exist." Her voice dropped. "I will not be the one who lets it happen twice."

Stalemate. Elias felt it settle over the courtyard like the rain — total, soaking, indifferent. She would not yield the archive. He would not leave without it, or without Nadia, or without some answer that let him sleep. Two immovable objects, and between them a boatload of secrets nobody trusted anybody else to hold.

Ravel shifted in the colonnade shadow. "There's a middle path," he began, smooth as river mud—

"There isn't," Sefu and Elias said together, and even in the wreckage of the moment Elias registered that they agreed on exactly one thing: they didn't trust Ravel's middle paths.

* * *

It was Jonah who broke it.

She'd been silent so long Elias had half-forgotten her — a mistake he suspected people made about Jonah Vasquez professionally, possibly fatally. Now she stood, crossed the flooded courtyard with her rolling gait, and crouched at the terrace edge where the water slid over the lowest steps, dark and patient as a held breath.

"How deep over the sunken channel," she said. It wasn't quite a question. She was reading the current the way Sefu read documents, lips slightly parted, eyes doing arithmetic.

"Which channel?" Sefu asked.

"The one your cartographer charted." Jonah nodded at the waterproof logbook sticking out of Elias's jacket — Nadia's, planted on him by a fixer's careful hands, and none of them had discussed that yet, and he saw Jonah decide, visibly, that now was not the moment. "Markers on the north approach. She charted a submerged processional way — old canal, pre-flood. It runs from the lower terraces out to the main channel."

Sefu went very still. "You've read her survey notes."

"Some of them." Jonah's jaw tightened. "Your expedition diverted through channels only she could have found. I followed her markers upstream for two days. She was good." A pause, and then, quieter, grudging as tribute: "She was better than good. She thought like water."

Something in Sefu's expression cracked — pride and grief in the same fracture. "She did."

Jonah turned to face them all, rain running off her chin, and Elias watched her arrive at the thing she'd clearly been calculating since they'd tied up below. He'd spent a week learning her tells. This one was new: fear. Actual fear, mastered the way she mastered everything, but present.

"Here's the third way," Jonah said. "Nobody surrenders anything. Nobody waits to drown on principle. We move the archive — now, tonight, in relays — by water, through the submerged canal, out to the Marisol before the river closes the window. Then we run it downriver ahead of the peak."

"Through company patrol lines," Sefu said flatly.

"With a leak in my hull, a stranger I don't trust, and a hold full of somebody else's war." Jonah shrugged, and the shrug contained oceans. "Yeah. That's the offer."

"Why would you do that?" Sefu's eyes narrowed. "Triple pay doesn't cover gunboats."

"It doesn't." Jonah looked out at the floodwater, at the drowned world, and Elias saw her looking at something further — every town she'd ever left burning behind her, though he didn't know that yet, wouldn't know it for another week, when she finally gave him the pieces in the dark somewhere upstream. What he saw now was simpler: a woman standing at a door she'd spent her whole life avoiding.

"Because I'm tired," Jonah said, "of arriving everywhere after the fire."

The rain thickened. Thunder walked the ridgelines, closer now, counting down.

Sefu studied her for a long moment — the way she'd studied the vault, perhaps, weighing what was inside against what it would cost to open. Then she looked at Elias, and something in her face shifted, some verdict reached.

"If we do this," she said, "it happens my way. Catalogue order. The index crate moves last and stays in my possession. And if the company comes before we're loaded—" she patted the pistol "—we defend the crates. All of them. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Elias said, before he'd decided to.

"Then we have a window." Sefu raised her voice toward the colonnade. "Ravel! Get Tomas upright and start breaking down the camp. We load at dark."

Ravel peeled away from the shadows, and Elias caught the look that crossed his face — quick, unreadable, gone — and felt it snag somewhere under his ribs like a fishhook. He'd seen that look before, he realized. At the relay station, on the operator's face, when the corporate credentials were mentioned. The look of a man doing arithmetic he hoped nobody would ask him to show.

Below them, the river rose another finger's width against the ancient stone, patient as a creditor.

And far downstream, faint through the rain, Elias thought he heard engines.
