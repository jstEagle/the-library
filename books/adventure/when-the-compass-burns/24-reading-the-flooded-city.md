## 24. Reading the Flooded City

The rain had a rhythm to it now, and Jonah hated that she'd learned it.

Three days of monsoon and the downpour had stopped being an event and become a condition — the constant drumming on the Marisol's cabin roof, the gray curtain beyond the windows, the way sound traveled differently through waterlogged air so that every voice seemed to arrive from farther away than it actually was. She stood at the chart table in the wheelhouse with a hurricane lamp burning low beside her, and she spread Nadia Wren's charts across the scarred wood like a hand of cards she was afraid to look at too closely.

They were beautiful. That was the first thing. Jonah had seen a thousand river charts in her life — hydrographic surveys with their tidy contour lines, pilot guides annotated in pencil by dead men, the crude charcoal sketches traders scratched out over rum. None of them looked like this. Nadia had drawn the basin the way a musician writes music: the channels flowing in confident ink strokes, the depths noted not just in meters but in character — *gravel bar, shifts after rain*; *clay shelf, holds*; *deadfall, three trunks, one moves*. In the margins, in a small precise hand, were observations that had nothing to do with navigation and everything to do with love. *Kingfishers nesting on the leaning kapok — do not anchor near.* *Village of San Roque sells the best coffee on the river. Bargain hard anyway.*

Jonah touched the edge of the paper where a coffee stain had bloomed years ago and been carefully drawn around, incorporated into the map as though the cartographer couldn't bear to obscure even her own accidents.

"You've been staring at that for an hour," Elias said from the doorway.

He was holding two mugs of the expedition's terrible instant coffee, and he set one down within her reach without asking, which was something he'd learned about her somewhere between the sandbar and the burned village — that she didn't answer questions well but she answered gestures fine.

"I'm not staring," Jonah said. "I'm listening."

"To a piece of paper."

"To her." She tapped the margin note about the kingfishers. "Your sister wrote this like she was talking to whoever came next. Not to a survey office. To a person." She looked up at him. Rain hammered the roof. "She knew someone would need this someday. Maybe she hoped it wouldn't be you."

Elias's jaw did the thing it did when grief and anger were fighting for the same ground. He sat down across from her, and for once he didn't deflect, didn't perform the easy stranger who'd forced his way aboard at Callao Verde with cash and lies. He just looked at his sister's handwriting and let it hurt him.

"She used to map our neighborhood when we were kids," he said finally. "Back home. Drew every shortcut, every fence you could climb, every dog that would chase you. Our mother kept asking why, and Nadia said, *Because if you know the way out, you're never trapped.*" His mouth twisted. "I thought it was a game. It was a philosophy."

"It's a good one." Jonah pulled the top chart toward her and weighted its corners with the lamp base, a brass fitting, and the .45 she'd taken off a pirate who'd made the mistake of boarding first. "Now let's see if it keeps us from being trapped."

* * *

The problem was simple and enormous, the way all the best problems were.

Somewhere below them, half-drowned in floodwater, lay the Sunken Terrace — the ruins Dr. Sefu had found, the vault of records that mapped every hidden tributary and buried claim in the basin, power over land and water and memory pressed flat between stone slabs. They'd loaded what they could carry onto the Marisol through the night while Ravel signaled the gunboats and the terrace camp burned behind them. The crates sat in her hold now, forty-one of them, wrapped in tarpaulin, weighing the boat down so deep her gunwales rode barely a hand's width above the brown water.

And the river was still rising.

Jonah had marked the gauge on the terrace pilings twice since dawn. A meter in six hours. At that rate, the flood would peak sometime in the next two days — maybe sooner if the storm cell sitting over the headwaters decided to empty itself all at once. When it peaked, everything changed. Channels that were open now would close. Sandbars would drown and reappear somewhere else. The whole grammar of the river would rewrite itself overnight, and any route she planned today might be a lie by tomorrow morning.

But they couldn't wait for the peak either. Downstream, the company's gunboats were regrouping, and Ravel was out there with the index crate, and every hour they sat anchored in this flooded channel was an hour the mercenaries spent finding them.

"So we go now," Elias said, "through channels that are closing."

"We go now through channels that are *changing*," Jonah corrected. "Closing is just changing with bad manners." She flattened the second chart over the first, aligning the survey marks, and felt the old thing wake in her chest — the cold bright pleasure of a problem that only she could solve. "Your sister surveyed this stretch in dry season. Look here—" she traced a line west of the main channel, "—she marked this as a cutoff, four feet deep in March, impassable. But the flood adds what, eight feet? Nine?"

"Ten," Elias said. "Tomas measured it against the temple stair this morning."

"Ten feet. So the cutoff isn't a cutoff anymore. It's a highway." Her finger moved north along the drowned land. "But it doesn't stay a highway. See how narrow it gets here, between these two ridges? Right now there's ten feet under your keel. When the river peaks and starts to drop, the current through that gap is going to triple. And when it drops another two meters—" she drew a line with her thumbnail, "—it becomes a waterfall. A short-lived one, but you don't get to be short-lived inside it."

Elias leaned over the chart, and Jonah watched him read it — really read it, the way she'd suspected he could since the night he'd answered her river questions too well for a grieving brother. His finger found the gap before she pointed to it.

"The Narrows at Kilometer Forty-One," he said. "Nadia called it the Throat. There's a note — 'do not enter above 2.5 on the staff gauge; the eddy on the east wall will hold you.'" He looked up. "What's the gauge reading now?"

"Four point one."

"So we can't use the Throat."

"We can't use the Throat *on the way out*. We can use it on the way *down*, if we time it right." Jonah pulled the third chart — the one covering the lower basin, the long run back toward the villages — and laid it beside the others until the three sheets formed a single continuous picture of the drowned country. "Here's the shape of it. We leave tonight, on the rising water, through the western cutoff while it's still deep enough to float a loaded boat. That puts us behind the ridge line, out of sight of the main channel, which is where the gunboats will be looking. We ride the flood current north-northwest for about thirty kilometers — no engine, or engine at idle, because the current does the work and engines make noise and noise gets people killed."

"And then?"

"Then we hit the old mission channel." Her finger stopped at a place where Nadia's ink lines converged into a knot of annotations, dense as a wasp's nest. "This is the part I don't like."

* * *

The part she didn't like took the rest of the day to understand, and most of that day she spent standing hip-deep in floodwater on the Marisol's foredeck with the chart board lashed to the rail, watching the drowned city tell her things the charts could only hint at.

The Sunken Terrace hadn't always been sunken. That was the thing Tomas kept explaining, patiently, whenever Jonah asked — the pre-colonial builders had raised their terraces above the seasonal floods, and the floods had risen century by century until the city drowned, and then the river had changed its mind and partially receded, and now the monsoon was drowning it again, and the whole basin was a palimpsest written and rewritten by water that couldn't decide what it wanted to remember. The old mission channel that Nadia had charted was a canal — an actual engineered canal, cut by human hands four hundred years ago to move trade goods between the terrace city and the northern rivers. It had silted. It had been dammed by a landslide Nadia dated in her margins. And now the flood had swallowed the dam, and the canal was running again for the first time in living memory, carrying a ribbon of current through the drowned forest like a vein reopened in an old scar.

Jonah stood at the rail and watched it happen.

The entrance to the canal was invisible unless you knew to look — a seam in the flooded treeline where the current ran contrary to the main flow, dragging rafts of broken vegetation backward against the drift. Where it emerged from the trees, the water boiled around submerged masonry, and she could see the regular pattern of it beneath the surface: cut stones, courses of them, the ghost architecture of a harbor that had died before anyone alive was born.

"Nadia found this in dry season," Elias said beside her. He'd come up without her hearing, which bothered her more than she showed. "When it was a ditch. She wrote — hang on." He unfolded his copy of her notebook pages, protected in a plastic sleeve, and read aloud: "*'Canal entrance at the drowned mole. Silted to perhaps half a meter. If the old dam ever fails, this becomes the fastest route north. Someone should tell the villages.'*" He lowered the page. "Someone should tell the villages. That's how she ended everything. Like she was writing letters to the future."

"The future reads fast," Jonah said. "It's the present that's slow." She gauged the seam of current with a pilot's eye, measuring the angle of the boil, the speed of the debris riding it, the way the floodwater stacked against the eastern wall of the entrance where the old masonry turned the flow. "Half a meter of silt, plus ten feet of flood. Your sister was right. It'll float us." She chewed the inside of her cheek. "It'll also funnel us through four kilometers of drowned forest with overhanging limbs, submerged structures, and a current we can't fight if anything goes wrong. One wrong turn in there and we're wrapped around a tree in moving water, and no amount of courage unwraps a boat from a tree."

"So we don't turn wrong."

"So we send eyes ahead." Jonah turned and looked downriver, toward the mangrove cove where Priya and Tomas stood watch with the one working pair of binoculars. Below decks, Sefu was dying by fractions, refusing morphine because she wanted her mind clear, and Jonah understood that more than she wanted to. "Priya paddled these waters fleeing the mercenaries. She knows every bend between here and the villages better than any chart. Tomorrow she runs the canal ahead of us in the dugout, marks the hazards, and signals us through. We follow her markers at walking speed."

"And if the mercenaries find her first?"

Jonah didn't answer that, because there wasn't an answer that wasn't terrible, and because at that moment Priya herself appeared at the foot of the deck ladder, soaked to the shoulders, with the particular stillness she carried that Jonah had learned to read as urgency rather than calm.

"Gauge on the temple stair," Priya said. "It moved again. Four point six."

Jonah looked at the sky, at the bruise-colored wall of cloud over the headwaters, and did the arithmetic she'd been doing all day, and the arithmetic came back worse each time.

* * *

That evening she called them all into the wheelhouse — Elias, Tomas, Priya, and Ravi, Sefu's remaining crewman, who'd stopped speaking above a whisper since the fire and worked with the mechanical diligence of a man paying off a debt. Through the bulkhead, faintly, they could hear Sefu coughing.

Jonah laid the three charts end to end and weighted them, and told them the plan the way she'd built it, piece by piece, because she'd learned long ago that crews didn't die from bad plans — they died from plans they didn't understand.

"We leave at first light. Not before — I want the light on the water to read the canal entrance, and I don't trust my night eyes in this rain. Western cutoff, engine at quarter, following the deep line Nadia marked. Thirty kilometers of flood current behind the ridge, quiet as a funeral. Then the old canal, with Priya ahead in the dugout setting markers. We come out at the northern rivers below the last rapids, and from there it's open water to the village of San Roque." She paused. "Two days, if the river cooperates. Less, if it doesn't."

Tomas frowned at the middle chart. "Captain Vasquez — the flood peaks tomorrow, you said. Possibly tonight. After the peak, the levels start falling. Won't some of these channels—"

"Yes." She let the word sit. "That's the window. Everything I just described works *because* the river is high. The cutoff floats us now and won't float us in a week. The canal runs now and becomes a mud ditch in a month. The Throat—" she tapped the narrows, "—is passable now and becomes a death trap as the level drops. We are riding a door that is closing, and the only question is whether we're through it before it shuts." She looked around the wheelhouse, at each face in the lamplight. "I've spent eleven years running from places. This is the first time I've ever run *toward* one, and I intend to arrive. Questions."

There was one, of course. There was always one.

"What happens," Ravi said quietly, "if the window closes while we're inside it?"

Jonah looked down at Nadia's charts, at the precise loving hand that had drawn escape routes for people she'd never meet, and she thought about doors, and windows, and all the times in her life she'd left a room a moment before it burned.

"Then we stop being a boat," she said, "and start being a very crowded island. And we wait for the water to give the land back. It always does. Eventually." She rolled the charts and tied them with the cord Elias offered. "Get some sleep. All of you. First light comes early, and the river doesn't wait for anybody — including me."

They filed out. Elias lingered, the way he did, and stood a moment looking at the coffee stain Nadia had drawn around on the chart's corner.

"She'd have liked you," he said. "She didn't like many people."

"Don't say that like it's past tense," Jonah said. "We haven't gotten her back yet. Or her name cleared. Or Ravel's teeth kicked in. Plenty of reasons left for her to dislike me."

Something that was almost a smile crossed his face, and he went below.

* * *

Alone in the wheelhouse, Jonah did what she always did before a hard run: she walked the boat.

Foredeck to stern, lantern in hand, checking everything twice — the lashings on the archive crates, the spare fuel drums wedged amidships, the patch on the hull that had held through the worst night of bailing she'd ever done and would hold through one more day because it had to. The Marisol sat heavy and low in the black water, riding like a barge, riding like a promise. Eleven years this boat had been her one fixed point, the only address she'd ever kept, and tomorrow she'd ask it to carry a drowned library through a closing door.

At the stern rail she stopped and looked back the way they'd come. Far downstream, past the drowned terraces, the rain had thinned enough to show the horizon, and on the horizon — or maybe only in her imagination, or maybe in the imagination the river gave everyone eventually — there was a smudge of light that wasn't lightning. Steady. Artificial. The kind of light a gunboat makes when it thinks no one is far enough upstream to see it.

She counted seconds between it and the next flash of real lightning, and the math said: closer than yesterday.

Jonah went back to the wheelhouse, lit the lamp over the chart table, and unrolled Nadia's charts one more time. Outside, the rain resumed its rhythm, and the river rose in the dark, patient as a creditor, and somewhere below the temple stair the gauge climbed toward numbers no pilot wanted to say out loud.

First light, she told the water. First light, and we run.
