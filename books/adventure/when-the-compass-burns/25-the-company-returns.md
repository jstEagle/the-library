## 25. The Company Returns

The rain had a rhythm to it by the third day, and Elias Wren had learned to hate rhythm. Rhythm meant pattern, and pattern meant the storm had settled in for the long siege — and somewhere downstream, he was certain, men were using that same patience against them.

He stood under the tarp they'd rigged across the upper terrace of the Sunken Terrace ruins, watching water sheet off the ancient stone in silver curtains. Below him, the flooded city spread out like a drowned crown, its terraces stepping down into brown water that had swallowed everything below the third level. Statues that had once watched over processions now kept their vigil over current and debris. Somewhere beneath all that water lay the vault Dr. Imara Sefu had given two years of her life — and nearly her last breath — to find.

Behind him, inside the chamber they'd cleared of rubble, Sefu slept in fits. The infection in her leg had spread despite Priya's careful dressing changes; her fever came in waves now, each one higher than the last. Tomas sat beside her, mopping her brow with cloth wrung from rainwater, his hands steady in the way of a man who had run out of terror and settled into something quieter and worse.

Ravel stood at the terrace edge, binoculars raised toward the downstream bend.

Elias had been watching Ravel for an hour without quite knowing why. There was nothing overtly wrong with the man. He was efficient, competent, the kind of second-in-command every expedition needed and few deserved — broad-shouldered, gray at the temples, with a voice that stayed level no matter what the river threw at them. He had carried Sefu up two terraces when her leg gave out. He had organized the watch rotations, rationed the food, kept the two remaining porters working through their grief for the companions who hadn't made it out.

And yet.

It was small things. The way Ravel checked the downstream approach more often than the upstream one, though the company's gunboats would come from Callao Verde, from downstream. No — Elias corrected himself — that was exactly right. That was what a vigilant man would do. But Ravel checked it *hopefully*, if such a thing could be said of a glance. There was a quality to his waiting that didn't match the others'. Tomas waited like a man dreading a blow. Priya waited like a woman who had already been struck and was braced for the next one. Ravel waited like a man listening for footsteps he expected to recognize.

Elias had spent enough years around liars to know the architecture of anticipation.

"You should sleep," Ravel said without turning. His voice carried easily under the rain's percussion. "You've had four hours in three days."

"So have you."

"I'm used to it." A pause. The binoculars swept the bend again. "Your sister used to say the river keeps everyone's schedule but her own."

Elias went very still. "Nadia told you that?"

"Everyone knew Nadia's sayings." Ravel lowered the binoculars, and in the gray light his face was unreadable, which Elias was beginning to understand was itself a kind of tell. "She was the only person on this expedition who never seemed afraid of it. Of any of it."

*Of any of it.* The words landed strangely, like a key testing locks it wasn't cut for.

Before Elias could decide what to make of that, a whistle came from the lower terrace — two short, one long. The porter on watch. Elias's stomach dropped before his mind caught up, because he'd learned the signals on the first night, and that one meant *vessel, downstream, unknown.*

Jonah appeared at the stairhead as if the whistle had summoned her from sleep, though Elias knew she'd been below with the Marisol, nursing the leak that was slowly winning its war against the hull. Her hair was plastered dark with rain, her eyes already doing the thing they did — reading the water, reading the light, reading him.

"Report," she said, and the porter's voice floated up: two boats, midriver, running dark, maybe six kilometers down. Running *dark*. In this rain, at this hour, with this current — nobody ran dark unless they didn't want to be seen until it was too late to run themselves.

"They found us," Elias said.

"Or they were always coming," Jonah said, "and we just confirmed the address." She turned to Ravel. "How many approaches does this terrace complex have from the water?"

"Three that matter. The main stair, the submerged causeway on the north side, and—" he paused, calculating, "—a channel through the collapsed gallery that floods at high water. Nadia charted it."

*Nadia charted it.* Again. Elias filed it away with the other splinters.

"Then we move the crates tonight," Jonah said. "All of them. Down to the Marisol, through the causeway, while the rain covers the sound. If those boats reach the main channel by morning—"

"They won't," Ravel said. "Not in this current, not at night. Even they won't risk the shoals below the bend in the dark." He said it with such certainty that Elias felt the hair rise on his arms, because certainty like that wasn't caution. It was *knowledge.* You only spoke that way about an enemy's decisions if you trusted the enemy to behave.

Jonah looked at Ravel for a long moment. Elias watched something pass across her face — not suspicion yet, but its ancestor. The instinct of a woman who had survived by reading water and men with the same eye.

"You're very sure of their seamanship," she said mildly.

"I've read their incident reports." Ravel smiled, thin and professional. "Corporate fleets train for open water, not flood-current shoals. It's a documented weakness."

*Incident reports.* A fixer in Callao Verde had used almost the same phrase. Elias's mind, which had been circling the truth for days like a bird over a kill site, suddenly went still.

* * *

They worked through the dark hours, and the work was terrible and holy at once.

The archive crates came down the terraces in relays — Elias and Tomas hauling on ropes slick with rain, the porters moving with the grim economy of men who had buried friends and intended to bury their work instead. Each crate held the memory of a civilization: land grants, tributary charts, burial records, claim registries, the whole legal skeleton of a basin that predated every border drawn since. Power over land, water, and memory itself — that was what Sefu had found, and that was why villages burned.

Sefu herself insisted on supervising the first descent. They got her down four steps before her fever took her legs out from under her, and it was Ravel who caught her, Ravel who carried her back up, Ravel whose face showed exactly the right amount of anguish.

*Exactly* the right amount. Elias thought about that while he hauled rope. Grief that fit perfectly was grief that had been rehearsed.

By the deepest hour of night, eleven crates lay lashed under tarps in the Marisol's hold, riding low in the black water of the cove where Jonah had beached her. The leak pump chugged its stubborn rhythm. Jonah moved among the crates checking lashings, and Elias crouched beside her in the dripping dark.

"I need to tell you something," he said, "and I need you to hear all of it before you react."

Her eyes came up. In the faint gray of the storm-light she looked carved out of the same stone as the ruins — patient, weathered, immovable.

"Ravel knows things he shouldn't," Elias said. "The company's route preferences. Their patrol timing. He quoted incident reports from a fleet that shouldn't have public records. And when the watch signal came, he was already facing downstream. He'd been facing downstream for an hour."

Jonah was quiet for a moment. Rain drummed the tarp above them like fingers on a coffin lid.

"I know," she said.

"You *know*?"

"I've known since the burned village." She tested a lashing, pulled it tighter. "A man doesn't survive that long in the bush by being careless about sightlines. He was watching the approach road before I ever saw smoke. And at the relay station—" she glanced at him, "—you flinched at a name too, Wren. Don't think I missed it. We're all carrying cargo."

"That's different."

"It always is." She stood, rolled her shoulders, and looked out at the black river. "Here's what matters. If Ravel's selling us, he'll want the boats to find us calm and cooperative. Which means our best play is to look exactly like what he expects — and be somewhere else entirely when the curtain goes up."

"And if you're wrong about him?"

"Then I've insulted an honest man," Jonah said, "and honest men forgive that easier than dead ones."

* * *

Dawn came the color of old pewter, and with it came the gunboats.

Elias heard them before he saw them — the wrongness in the river's voice, engines pitched low against the current, throttled down to a murmur that didn't belong to any fisherman. Then they rounded the bend in the gray half-light, and his mouth went dry.

Two of them. Military-surplus hulls, gunmetal gray, riding heavy. Mounted amidships on each: a machine gun under a rain cover, the barrel angled casually toward the terraces. On the lead boat's wheelhouse, stenciled in white, the same logo from the relay station, from the burned village, from the crates in the pirates' wake — the stylized current inside a broken circle. Meridian Extraction Group. The name meant nothing and everything, the way corporate names do: bland as water, hungry as fire.

The boats did not charge. They came on with the unhurried confidence of predators that already know the kill is arranged, and swung wide, and anchored — anchored, in a flood current that should have made anchoring suicidal — precisely in the channel mouth below the main stair.

*Precisely.* Elias's blood turned to ice water. Nobody anchored there by luck. Somebody had told them the bottom was clean, the holding good, the depth exact.

Somebody inside the walls.

On the lead boat's deck, a figure raised a megaphone. The voice that crackled across the water was pleasant, reasonable, almost bored.

"Dr. Sefu. Dr. Imara Sefu. We know you can hear this. We are not here for violence. We are here for property — specifically, property belonging to the Meridian Extraction Group, misappropriated during your survey. Return it, and everyone walks away. Every person on these terraces boards our boats dry, fed, and free. That is a promise backed by shareholders, not sentiment."

Tomas made a sound low in his throat. Priya had gone white as the foam on the pilings. Upstairs, faintly, they could all hear Sefu trying to rise, and failing, and cursing in three languages.

"Dr. Sefu," the megaphone continued, patient as erosion, "we have counted your people. Seven. We have counted your boats. One. We have counted your options. Zero. Come down the stairs. Bring the crates. No one needs to drown today."

*Seven people,* Elias thought. *One boat.* The numbers were right. The numbers were exactly, precisely right — and the cold that had started in his stomach climbed into his chest, because there was only one way the man with the megaphone could count so well.

He turned and looked up the stairs.

Ravel was standing at the terrace rail, visible, deliberate, his hands loose at his sides. Not hiding. Not reaching for a weapon. Watching the gunboats with an expression Elias could finally name, because he'd seen it in boardrooms and border towns and the faces of men counting money that wasn't theirs:

Relief.

"You," Elias said. His voice came out flat and strange, someone else's voice. "You signaled them."

Ravel didn't deny it. That was the worst part — he didn't even perform denial. He simply turned, and the rain ran down his competent, trustworthy, gray-templed face, and he said, in the level voice that had organized watches and rations and rescues for weeks:

"The expedition was dead the day Sefu opened that vault. She just refused to read the autopsy. Porters died because she thought secrecy was armor. It isn't. It's just a slower way to bleed." He nodded toward the gunboats, almost gently. "I've bought everyone here a future. All it costs is stones with writing on them. Stones, Wren. She was going to get people killed for *stones*."

Below, the megaphone voice called again, sweeter now, the tone of a man whose partner has just confirmed the merchandise is ready: "Dr. Sefu? Your colleague tells us you're reasonable. Shall we discuss terms?"

Elias looked at Jonah. She was already moving — down the stairs, toward the Marisol, toward the eleven crates of a drowned empire — and over her shoulder she threw back one word that was less an answer than a battle standard:

"*Terms.*"

The word cracked across the water like a whip, and the negotiation — whatever shape it was about to take — had begun.
