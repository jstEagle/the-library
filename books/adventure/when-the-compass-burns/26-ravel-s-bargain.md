## 26. Ravel's Bargain

The rain had a sound like applause, Ravel thought, if the audience were enormous and indifferent and had been clapping for ten thousand years. He stood under the tarpaulin strung between two broken columns on the upper terrace of the Sunken Terrace, listening to it hammer the flooded city below, and he let himself feel, for one clean moment, entirely justified.

Below him, in the lamplit dark of the barricaded hall, Dr. Imara Sefu was dying by inches and calling it principle.

He could hear her voice through the stone — low, cracked, still carrying that authority that had made forty people follow her into a drowned civilization in the wettest season anyone could name. She was arguing with the pilot woman again. Jonah Vasquez. The river rat with the scarred hands and the eyes of someone who had already left every room she'd ever stood in. Even now, even here, at the top of the world's water, the woman radiated departure. Ravel had watched her for three days and catalogued her the way he catalogued everything: assets, liabilities, exit routes.

Vasquez would run. Eventually. Everyone ran. That was not cynicism; that was fieldwork. Eleven years with the Meridian Consolidated Group had taught Ravel that the only difference between people was the price at which their running became rational. Sefu's porters had run when the shooting started. The geologist Tomas had run. The intern had run so fast she'd forgotten how to speak. And Sefu herself —

Sefu didn't run. That was her flaw. Not her virtue. Her flaw.

Ravel adjusted the tarpaulin where the wind worried it, and thought about the crate.

* * *

They had found the vault on the nineteenth day, down beneath the waterline of the old ceremonial stair, behind a wall of fitted basalt that the flood had spent four centuries trying to reach. The chamber inside was dry. Dry, in the middle of all that drowning — sealed so perfectly by its builders that the air inside still smelled of resin and smoke, as though the priests who stacked those records had only just stepped out. Rows upon rows of wrapped bundles. Bark-paper codices. Clay tablets in sleeves of waxed cloth. Maps — Nadia Wren had wept over the maps — charts of tributaries that no government survey acknowledged, boundaries of land grants older than any nation that claimed the basin, records of who had owned what, who had buried what, who had moved rivers to hide what they'd taken.

Power, Ravel had thought, standing in that dry amber light. Not knowledge. Knowledge was what you told students. This was power — the kind that came with a chain of custody, the kind that lawyers and ministers and men whose names never appeared in newspapers would empty accounts to hold.

And Sefu had looked at it — he would remember this until he died, the exact angle of her headlamp, the rain of dust motes through the beam — and said, "Nobody sells this. Nobody owns this. We document it, we copy it, and we give it back."

Give it back. As if the dead had ever wanted anything given back. As if the basin's villages — the ones that burned, the ones that flooded, the ones that emptied out year by year into the cities' desperate margins — needed another archive instead of leverage. Sefu talked about memory like it was bread you could hand out. But memory without power was a eulogy. Power without memory was just theft with better stationery. Ravel had spent his whole career in the space between, and he knew which side of that equation kept people alive.

The porters were alive because he had kept them alive. Or most of them. Two hadn't run fast enough when the mercenaries came through the narrows, and yes — yes — that was on his ledger too, he wasn't a fool, he knew what he'd done when he passed the expedition's position downstream in a coded burst from the relay station's spare handset. He had weighed it. He weighed everything. Two lives against thirty, against the archive itself, against the simple arithmetic that armed men do not leave because a doctor asks them nicely. If Sefu had told him her plan — if there had *been* a plan beyond barricade and pray — maybe the arithmetic would have come out differently. But she kept her counsel the way she kept the vault: locked, on principle, above the waterline of everyone else's judgment.

So he had made the call alone. The way captains make calls alone. The way pilots make calls alone. The way, he suspected, the woman Vasquez had once made some call alone, in some burning town behind her, that sent her running upriver for a decade. He recognized the look. He'd seen it in mirrors.

* * *

Footsteps on the wet stone. Ravel didn't turn. There were only five of them left on the terraces now — Sefu, Ravel, the engineer Okonkwo, the pilot, the brother. The brother was coming. He always came at this hour, after the lamp-oil check, when the camp went quiet enough for conscience.

"Elias," Ravel said, without turning. "You walk like your sister. Heavy on the outside edge. She did it too, going down the vault stairs."

"Stop saying her name." Elias Wren stopped three meters off, at the edge of the tarpaulin's shelter, rain sheeting off his shoulders. He held the carbine low but not carelessly. Three days ago Ravel would have called him a grieving amateur. Three days ago, before the gunboats appeared downstream and Ravel's signal fire answered them, grief had finished its work on the man and something harder had set in its place. "I want to know why."

"Why." Ravel laughed, and heard how thin it came out. "Everyone wants why. It's the cheapest question there is. Why does the river rise? Why did the company burn Callao Verde's sister village? Why did your sister die?" He turned then, and let the man see his face, let him see that there was no mask left worth tearing off. "Why doesn't keep anyone warm. Let me tell you *what*, instead. What happens tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that, if we keep doing what Imara wants."

"The archive goes to the villages—"

"The archive goes to the bottom of the river, if the pilot gets her way, and copies go to universities that will publish footnotes for twenty years while Meridian's survey crews finish mapping every claim those documents describe. You've seen their boats. Two gunboats now. There'll be four by the end of the week — I know how they stage, I signed the requisitions once, before I understood what I was signing. They don't need the vault, Wren. They need *time*. Time to dredge, time to bribe, time to burn the last villages that remember the old boundaries. Every day we sit up here being principled, some minister downstream signs away a watershed he's never seen, using maps that say nothing belongs to anyone." He stepped closer, into the rain-shadow, close enough to see the rain beading in the man's beard. "Your sister charted half those hidden channels. She knew exactly what was in that vault. Ask yourself why she stayed with an expedition that planned to bury it."

Elias's jaw worked. Rain ran off his chin. "Nadia believed in Sefu."

"Nadia believed in *Nadia*," Ravel said, gently, cruelly, because cruelty was the only honest tool left in his kit. "She believed in being the one who found things. And she was. God help me, she was brilliant. But belief doesn't stop bullets, and brilliance doesn't float a boat, and when the shooting started in the narrows, the plan — the whole of Dr. Sefu's plan — was to stand in a doorway and die near her work. Two porters are in the river because of that plan. Priya hasn't spoken since because of that plan. And if we're still here when the river peaks, none of us will be anywhere at all."

"You could have told us. About the gunboats. Before."

"Yes," Ravel said. "And you'd have voted. And the vote would have been to stay, because hope votes to stay, and then we'd all be dead instead of merely doomed." He spread his hands. "I chose. Alone. That's what I'm offering to keep choosing — except next time, not alone, and not for them."

* * *

Here was the thing Ravel had learned in eleven years of corporate salvage work, the thing no one said aloud in the seminars: corruption was not a fall. It was a translation. Every value translated into every other value, at some exchange rate, and the only sin was pretending you weren't trading. Sefu traded lives for principles and called it integrity. The pilot traded presence for absence and called it freedom. Ravel traded the unownable for the survivable and refused to call it anything but what it was.

Meridian had offered him a number before he ever turned. That was the part nobody understood about betrayal — it arrived first as a courtesy. A regional director, a voice like oiled walnut on a satellite line: *Dr. Ravel, the Group has no interest in confrontation. The Group has interest in access. Name a figure for consultation services.* And Ravel, standing in the relay station with the rain coming down and the expedition's radio silence stretching into its second week, had asked himself the question that separated him from better men, or joined him to worse ones, depending on the decade doing the asking:

*Who can actually protect these things?*

Not Sefu. Sefu couldn't protect her own wound. Not the villages — the villages couldn't protect a cooking fire from a company match. Not the state, which had sold the basin's mineral rights in lots before Ravel was born. Institutions failed upward; that was their function. The only thing that had ever protected anything, anywhere, was interest aligned with custody. Someone had to own the past or it got bulldozed. That wasn't cynicism. That was maintenance.

So he'd named a figure. A large one. And he'd told himself — and this was true, he needed it to be true — that he would use the money to buy the expedition's survivors out of the country, that the number included extraction costs, that he was converting an unprotectable treasure into protectable lives. Some of that was even real. The rest was the rain sound, the applause, the enormous indifference — the story a man tells himself so he can sleep in a wet camp full of people who trusted him.

Two porters. He saw them sometimes when he closed his eyes. Mateo, who sang off-key. The boy, Anselm, seventeen, who'd asked Ravel on the third day whether the ruins were haunted and laughed at his own question. They were in the river now, under the brown water, under the drowned church spire, under everything. And Meridian's director had not mentioned them again, because to Meridian they had never existed, and that — that erasure, that smooth administrative forgetting — was the thing Ravel told himself he was fighting, even as he fed it.

He was tired of feeding it. That was the truth he'd carried up the stairs tonight, the thing he meant to put on the table like a card he'd been holding face-down for eleven years.

* * *

"Sefu's dying," Elias said. It wasn't a question. The infection had gone past the knee; everyone smelled it now, the sweet wrongness under the woodsmoke.

"In four days, maybe five. The pilot knows it. The engineer knows it. Which means in four days this camp has no authority, no plan, and two gunboats." Ravel let the rain fill the pause. "So listen to me, Wren. Listen, because I'm only going to say this once and then I'm going to do it whether you help or not. I have a channel to the regional director. Real — not the signal fire theatrics, an actual frequency, an actual name. I can trade. Not the whole archive — I'm not stupid enough to think they'd honor that, and I'm not clean enough to pretend I wouldn't try anyway. One crate. The index. The catalogue of what exists, nothing more. In exchange: safe passage for every living person on this terrace, medical evacuation for Sefu, and a written commitment — witnessed, I'll make them witness it — that the vault stays sealed and un-dredged for five years. Five years is enough time for the villages to copy what matters. Enough time for your sister's charts to get into a hundred hands. We don't win. We *don't lose completely*, which is the only outcome available to people in our position, and anyone who tells you different is selling a funeral."

"And the index leads them to everything else eventually."

"Everything else is at the bottom of a rising river behind four centuries of basalt. They'll spend those five years looking and find silt." Ravel held the man's eyes. "Or — alternative ending — we refuse, the company comes up those stairs tomorrow night, and we find out how many of us can swim. Those are the endings, Wren. There is no third one where everybody's brave and nobody bleeds. I've read that book. It ends with the village burning."

For a long moment Elias said nothing. The rain applauded. Somewhere below, thunder rolled along the terrace walls like furniture being dragged across eternity, and the lamplight in the barricaded hall guttered and steadied.

"You really believe it," Elias said finally, and there was something in his voice Ravel hadn't expected — not disgust. Recognition. "Don't you. You believe every word. That's the worst part."

"I believe what the bodies tell me," Ravel said. "You should try it. Grief makes idealists of everyone else. It made a realist of me."

"No." Elias shifted the carbine to his other hand, and his voice dropped into something quiet and final. "It made a runner of my sister, right up until the day she stopped running and charted a channel into a place no one was supposed to go. People surprise you, Ravel. That's the thing your arithmetic can't carry." He stepped back into the rain. "I'm not helping you. But I'm not stopping you either. I want to watch you make the call. I want to see if you flinch."

Then he was gone, down the wet stairs toward the lamplight, and Ravel stood alone under the tarpaulin with the applause of the rain and the weight of the radio handset in his pocket like a stone he'd chosen to swallow.

* * *

He waited until the camp's sounds settled — Okonkwo's snore, the pilot's wakeful silence from the boat deck far below, Sefu's labored breathing gone shallow with fever-sleep. Then he climbed to the highest terrace, where the old observatory stump stood against the sky, and took out the handset, and watched the lightning walk the ridgelines like a man reading a map by flashes.

Five years. Sealed vaults. Safe passage. He rehearsed it once, twice, the way you rehearse a lie you need to survive contact with a listener trained to hear lies. And underneath the rehearsal, honest as a knife in the ribs, sat the other thought, the one he'd been outrunning since the relay station:

That the director would say yes to all of it, warmly, reasonably, and mean none of it — and that Ravel had known that from the beginning, and pressed the transmit key anyway, because a man who has already fed two porters to the river might as well learn whether he's a monster or a midwife, and there was only one way to find out.

The frequency opened with a hiss, like the river itself clearing its throat.

"This is Consultation Seven," Ravel said. "I'm ready to talk terms."

Downstream, in the dark, two gunboats turned their bows upstream into the flood.
