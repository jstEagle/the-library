## 27. Night Crossing

The rain came down like a verdict.

Jonah stood at the rail of the Marisol and watched the Sunken Terrace dissolve into the storm — the drowned crown of it, the pale stone steps rising out of black water, the torches Sefu's people had lit along the upper gallery now hissing and drowning one by one. Lightning walked the ridgelines to the east, slow and patient, like something counting. The thunder arrived seconds later, rolling up the river valley as if the whole basin were a drum being struck from below.

"Wind's backing around," Elias said beside her, shouting over the rain without quite shouting. He'd learned that much about her in three weeks: she hated noise that wasn't necessary. "Northeast. It'll come straight down the channel."

"It'll come straight down everything," Jonah said. "There's no *around* tonight. There's only through."

Below them, in the flooded forecourt of the ruins, the first relay was already moving. Priya stood waist-deep on a submerged stair with a crate balanced on her shoulder — a girl who hadn't spoken for eleven days, now hauling the memory of an entire civilization on her back because Jonah had asked her to and then listened when the words finally came. Tomas was beside her, gray-faced, favoring the leg he'd wrecked in the dugout, passing crates hand to hand up the line. Two of Sefu's remaining crew waded the shallows with rope tied around their chests, anchors of flesh against a current that wanted everything.

And at the top of the line, wrapped in oilcloth that did nothing for her fever, sat Dr. Imara Sefu herself, directing the loading of her life's work with the flat, imperious calm of a woman who had already decided she was dead and found the fact merely inconvenient.

"Vasquez." Her voice carried even through the rain, dry as paper. "How many trips?"

"As many as the river gives us."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have." Jonah turned to face her. Up close, Sefu's skin had gone the color of old tallow, and the wound dressing on her thigh had bloomed dark again despite everything Tomas knew. But her eyes were still lit from inside, still burning with that terrible administrative clarity that had gotten half her expedition killed. "The Marisol sits low already. Every crate we take is water we can't ship later. I'll run four loads tonight if the leak lets me. Five if the river's kind."

"The river is not kind," Sefu said. "It never has been. That's why we're doing this." She looked past Jonah, out at the black water where two gunboats waited somewhere downstream, their engines cold, their searchlights dark, Ravel's signal — whatever form it took — not yet given. Or already given. No way to know. "Load the index last. If we lose everything else, the index tells them where to dig."

"I know what the index is worth."

"Do you?" Sefu's gaze came back to her, sharp as a surveyor's transit. "You're a pilot, Vasquez. You move things. You don't decide what they mean. Tonight you're deciding what they mean. Feel the weight of that."

Jonah felt it. She'd been feeling it since the burned village, since Priya's voice breaking open in the mangrove cove, since the moment she'd looked at a map of submerged channels and realized that Nadia Wren had drawn the only true picture of this drowned country anyone would ever have. The weight wasn't the crates. The weight was that once these records left the vault, they could never go back to being nobody's business.

"First relay," Elias called from below. "Coming across."

* * *

They worked by feel.

No lights — lights were a signature, and signatures got boats burned. Jonah had killed the Marisol's running lamps at dusk and now navigated the hundred meters between the terrace landing and her hold by the lightning's strobe and the sound of water on stone. Each time the sky flashed white, she read the channel: the drowned balustrade on the port side, the sunken colonnade standing just proud of the surface off the starboard bow, the deep lane Nadia's charts promised ran clean between them. Between flashes there was only rain and the engine idling low, and Jonah's hands on the wheel, and the knowledge — earned over twenty years of rivers — that the water remembered its shape even when no one could see it.

Elias worked the deck. He'd stripped to a soaked shirt, muscles standing out along his arms as he hauled crates up from the relays and stacked them forward, low, and tight, keeping the weight centered the way she'd shown him. He'd stopped asking questions somewhere around the third hour. Now he just moved, and Jonah found she could read him in the dark the way she read the channel — by rhythm, by the sound of his boots on wet planking, by the small grunts of effort that told her where he was without looking.

"Crate coming," he called, and she felt the boat heel slightly as forty kilos of pre-colonial archive swung aboard.

"Forward and down. Mind the seam."

"There's water over the seam plates already."

"I know."

"How much do we have?"

"Ask me at dawn."

That was the truth of the night, the truth under all the other truths: the Marisol was dying by inches and everyone aboard knew it. The leak she'd nursed since the monsoon opened had grown teeth during the beaching at the mangrove cove, and the repairs there — good enough for calm water, good enough for hope — were failing now under flood current and overload. The bilge pump ran constantly, a wet mechanical heartbeat below decks. When Jonah had checked an hour ago, the water in the bilge was above her boot soles and rising, and the pump was losing ground by centimeters per hour.

Four loads. Maybe five. Then the arithmetic of the hull would finish its argument.

The second relay crossed at midnight. The third came hard after, in the worst of the squall, when the wind drove the rain sideways and the lightning stopped flashing and started stuttering, and for ten minutes Jonah piloted blind on memory alone, feeling the current shove the stern toward the colonnade and correcting with throttle and prayer. Priya made that crossing twice, carrying crates through water that reached her ribs, and when she finally collapsed onto the Marisol's deck Jonah knelt beside her and put a hand on her shoulder and said nothing, because that was the language the girl understood.

"You should leave me," Priya whispered, teeth chattering. "I'm slow now."

"You're cargo," Jonah said. "I don't leave cargo."

Something moved behind the girl's eyes — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live someday, if she lived.

* * *

It was Tomas who saw the light.

He was lashing the fourth stack when he went still, head cocked, and Jonah followed his stare downstream to where the rain blurred everything into a single moving gray. At first there was nothing. Then — there, gone, there again — a green spark, low on the water, maybe two kilometers down the channel. Steady. Deliberate. Not lightning; lightning didn't repeat itself in patterns.

Three short. Three long. Three short.

"Somebody's signaling," Elias said quietly.

"Somebody's answering," Jonah said, and the bottom dropped out of the night.

Ravel. It had to be. Somewhere on the terraces or in the flooded galleries, the man who'd sold the expedition's position was giving the gunboats their heading — and the gunboats were acknowledging receipt. Which meant they were moving. Which meant the window that had seemed like hours was now minutes, and every crate still sitting in the vault was a crate the company would own by morning.

"Sefu!" Jonah's voice cracked across the water. "How many crates remain?"

A pause, long enough to be an answer. Then Sefu's voice, thinner than before, ragged at the edges: "Eleven. And the index."

Eleven crates. One trip, if the hull held. One trip, if the gunboats were slow. One trip, if the river — which was not kind, which had never been kind — decided to grant a dying boat one more crossing.

"Everyone across," Jonah said. "Now. We load and we run."

They ran. There was no other word for it. The relay became a chain of bodies moving through floodwater at a pace that was almost falling, crates passed hand to hand with the slick desperation of people bailing a sinking thing with buckets. Elias waded the shallows hauling double loads. Tomas limped the line with his leg screaming. Sefu refused to be carried and instead directed from her chair, calling weights and order with a voice that frayed a little more with each crate, until the last of the eleven went aboard and only the index remained, sitting in its oilcloth at the top of the stairs like a small black heart.

Sefu put her hands on it.

For a moment — one flash of lightning, one held breath — Jonah saw the doctor weigh it. Saw her look at the vault entrance behind her, at the flooded archive she'd guarded with blood and other people's blood, at the thing she'd built her whole self around. Twenty years of scholarship. Power over land, water, and memory. All of it reducible, in the end, to one box a strong man could carry.

"Dr. Sefu," Jonah said gently. "The boat."

"Yes," Sefu said. "The boat." She lifted the index and handed it down the line, and her hands shook, and whether from fever or grief Jonah couldn't tell and didn't ask.

* * *

The Marisol wallowed when they cast off, riding a hand's width lower than she'd ever ridden in Jonah's keeping. The bilge water sloshed at knee height below decks. The pump had stopped gaining ground entirely; now it was just making noise, a faithful animal bailing an ocean with a teaspoon.

Jonah took the wheel and pointed her bow downriver, toward the split channel, toward the village, toward whatever the morning held. The current took them instantly — the flood had climbed another half meter since dusk, and the river now ran like something chased, boiling around the drowned colonnade, dragging at the hull with fingers of brown water.

Behind them, upstream, a light flared on the upper terrace. Not torchlight. Electric. White and searching.

"They've arrived," Elias said. He'd come to stand at her shoulder, soaked through, breathing hard. In the next flash of lightning Jonah saw his face and read it easily: fear, yes, but underneath it something harder. His sister had charted these channels. His sister had died — or worse — somewhere in this country, and the men whose light was sweeping the ruins she'd mapped were the men who'd silenced her. "How long until they see us?"

"We're downwind and unlit and the rain's a curtain. Ten minutes if they're careless. Less if they're not." She eased the throttle forward and felt the engine answer with a sound like a cough. "But they'll find the camp empty and the vault open, and they'll know which way we went, because there's only one way."

"Then we'd better be fast."

"We'd better be *lucky*. Fast is optional."

The river rose to meet them. That was the thing about monsoon country that no chart fully captured: the water didn't stay where you put it. Channels that Nadia had drawn as narrow shifted and merged; sandbars became shoals; shoals became drownings. Jonah steered by the charts in her head and the charts in Elias's hands — he'd taken to holding Nadia's logbook open under an oilskin, reading depths and markers aloud while Jonah translated them into rudder and throttle — and slowly, crate by groaning crate, the Marisol gathered speed and slid downstream into the dark.

The leak kept its counsel for twenty minutes. Then it changed its mind.

Jonah heard it before she saw it: a new note in the pump's labor, a wetter sound beneath the engine, and then Elias, at the companionway, saying very quietly, "Jonah. You need to come see this."

She wedged the wheel and went below with a lantern. The bilge water had come up past the floorboards' seams. It was black and oily and it moved with the boat's motion, and at the aft end, where the hull curved up toward the transom, water was entering in a thin, steady sheet — not a trickle anymore, not a seep, but a seam opened wide, drinking the river in gulps.

"We're taking on faster than the pump can push out," Elias said. "Maybe twice as fast."

Jonah crouched and put her hand in the water. Cold. Rising. She thought of the arithmetic: distance to the split channel, roughly six kilometers; speed against current, perhaps four knots; time, ninety minutes. Time for another thirty centimeters of river inside her boat. Maybe forty.

"We make it," she said, "or we swim with the archive."

"That's your plan?"

"That's my *hope*. Plans are for mornings."

She went back up and drove the boat harder. The engine protested — a knocking deep in its chest that she'd diagnose tomorrow if there was a tomorrow — and the Marisol leaned into the current like a tired animal leaning into a harness, and the rain came down and the lightning counted and somewhere behind them the white searchlight swept the empty terraces and found nothing but stone and rising water.

At the halfway mark, Priya came up on deck unasked and began bailing with a bucket. No one had told her to. She simply appeared, small and stubborn, throwing river back into the river, and after a moment Tomas joined her, and then Elias, and the four of them formed a bucket line that accomplished almost nothing and meant everything.

Two kilometers from the split channel, the searchlight found them.

It came suddenly — a blade of white cutting through the rain from astern, pinning the deck, turning the world into a photograph: crates, bucket line, Jonah's hands on the wheel, Elias's face turned back toward the light like a man looking at his own execution. Then the light swept away, hunting, and came back, and stayed.

"They've seen us," Elias said.

"No," Jonah said, watching the light waver, watching it lose them in the rain and find them again. "They've *seen the river*. They haven't seen us yet. Get below — all of you — get low, get quiet. If they come alongside, they'll shoot first and board second, and I need you alive to bail."

"And you?"

"I need to be exactly where I am."

The gunboat's engine reached them a minute later — a deep, diesel growl, bigger than theirs, faster than theirs, closing. Jonah did the math she didn't want to do: they couldn't outrun it. Not loaded, not leaking, not against this current. The gunboat would run them down in open water and take everything, and the company would have its archive, and Priya's silence would have been for nothing, and Sefu's death would buy nothing, and the river would keep its secrets for the men who could afford dredges.

Unless the river had other plans.

Jonah looked ahead, into the rain, to where the split channel waited — the place where the flood had torn the river into braids, where Nadia's charts showed a narrow passage between drowned pilings and a sandbar that was now, thanks to half a meter of extra water, either safely submerged or a killing trap, depending on things no one could know until they were already committed.

The gunboat's searchlight steadied on them. A voice came across the water, amplified, male, bored: "*Unidentified vessel. Heave to. This is a corporate security operation. Heave to or we will fire.*"

Elias surfaced from below, dripping. "Jonah—"

"I heard them."

"What are you going to do?"

Jonah Vasquez, who had spent twenty years running from every fire, every name, every anchored thing, put her hand on the throttle and felt the old itch rise in her chest — the itch that said *run*, that said *open water*, that said *you've escaped worse than this*. And then she looked at the bucket line, at Priya's small stubborn shoulders, at the crates stacked low and tight, at the index crate with its small black heart of paper, and she found that the itch had changed its tune.

Not *run*. Not anymore.

"*Hold on,*" she said, and shoved the throttle to the stop, and turned the Marisol's bow toward the split channel, and the night opened up before her like a door.
