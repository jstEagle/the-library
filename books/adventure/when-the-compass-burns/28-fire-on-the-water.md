## 28. Fire on the Water

The rain stopped an hour before midnight, and in the sudden silence Elias Wren heard the thing he had been dreading all evening: engines. Not one engine — two, maybe three, running low and idle somewhere below the drowned terraces, the way men idle their motors when they want to be heard only by each other.

He was on watch at the eastern parapet of the Sunken Terrace, wedged between two carved serpents whose heads had worn smooth as river stones over however many centuries they'd been keeping this vigil. Below him the floodwater lay black and flat as poured tar, holding a broken mirror of the clouds. The storm had passed over and left the air rinsed and trembling, every sound carrying across the water like a confession shouted from a mountaintop.

There it was again. A diesel cough. The soft knock of something metal against something metal.

Elias counted to thirty before he moved. He had learned that much from Jonah Vasquez — that panic was a luxury purchased with other people's lives, and that a man who counted first was a man who could still think when the counting ran out. Then he slid down from the parapet, took the flooded stair three steps at a time, and went looking for her.

He found her where he always found her these days: in the lower gallery of the vault antechamber, sleeves rolled, hands black with lamp oil and mold, directing the loading of crates by the light of two hurricane lamps. The archive of the Sunken Terrace was coming apart into forty-one wooden boxes, each one wrapped in waxed canvas and numbered in Nadia's handwriting — no, not Nadia's, he corrected himself, Sefu's, though the resemblance was uncanny, the same slanted, impatient script, as if cartography bred a particular kind of penmanship. Twenty-eight crates were already down at the waterline, lashed under tarps beside the dugouts. Thirteen remained.

"Engines," Elias said. "Below the east channel. More than one."

Jonah did not stop moving. She handed a crate down to Tomas, who passed it to Priya, who stowed it in the second dugout with the economy of someone who had done nothing else for six days. "How far?"

"Half a kilometer. Maybe less. They're idling."

"Then they're waiting for full dark to finish setting." She wiped her hands on her thighs and looked up at him, and in the lamplight her face was all planes and decision. "Ravel's signal worked, then."

It was not a question, so Elias didn't answer it. Ravel had been gone since dusk — slipped away from the camp during the last shift change, and everyone had known what his going meant, and no one had said it aloud, because saying it aloud would have required deciding what to do about it, and there had been no time to decide anything except how to move faster.

"Sefu," Elias said.

"She knows." Jonah turned toward the inner chamber. "She's known since she saw him counting the boats instead of the crates."

* * *

Dr. Imara Sefu sat propped against the vault door itself, which was a slab of basalt older than any nation, carved with rows of glyphs that the expedition had spent four years learning to read and would now spend one night trying to save. Her right leg was bound in dressings that had been white once and were the color of tea leaves now, and her skin had gone the gray of old paper, but her eyes were still lit from inside, still burning with the particular fever of a woman who has spent her whole life being right and cannot stop now, even dying.

"You should go," she said, before Elias could speak. "All of you. Take the twenty-eight and run."

"We're not leaving thirteen crates," Elias said.

"You're not leaving *thirteen crates*," she repeated, and something almost like a smile touched her mouth. "Nadia said you'd be like this. Stubborn past the point of sense. She wrote it in a letter I wasn't supposed to read. *My brother argues with rivers,* she said. *He thinks if he's loud enough the water will apologize.*"

Elias felt the words go through him like a blade finding the gap in armor. Four months since the letter came telling him Nadia was missing. Three weeks since he'd stood on the dock at Callao Verde and lied to a riverboat captain about why he needed passage upstream. He had carried his sister's name through all of it like a coal cupped in both hands, feeding it silence, and here was a dying stranger handing him back a piece of her voice he hadn't known existed.

"She said that?" His voice came out wrong.

"She said worse." Sefu closed her eyes briefly. "Get the rest loaded, Wren. Argue with me later, if the river lets either of us live that long."

He turned to go, and her hand caught his wrist — surprisingly strong, the grip of a woman who had hauled herself up temple walls on two good arms while the third member of her expedition bled out below.

"Wren. If it comes to fire — and it will come to fire — the index matters more than anything else in the vault. Crate one. Everything else is body; the index is the spine. Without it the rest is just paper in dead languages." Her fingers tightened. "Do you understand me? Whatever happens, whoever lives — the index does not fall into the company's hands, and it does not burn."

"I understand."

"No," she said softly, letting him go. "You understand the words. Pray you never have to understand the choice."

* * *

They got nine more crates to the waterline in the next hour. It was brutal, wordless work — passing the weight hand to hand down stairs slick with rain and moss, the lamps hooded, the only sounds breath and wood and the distant mutter of the river climbing its banks. Priya worked without speaking, as she had spoken hardly at all since the narrows, but her eyes moved constantly, cataloguing, and twice Elias caught her looking downstream toward the dark where the engines waited, and once he caught her looking at Ravel's empty bedroll with an expression he decided not to name.

Tomas counted under his breath as they worked. He was a geologist and he counted everything — steps, crates, heartbeats, it seemed — and his murmured numbers became the rhythm of the night. *"Treinta y dos. Treinta y tres."* At thirty-seven, Jonah called a halt.

"That's it. We're out of time." She stood at the top of the water stair, listening. The engines had changed. The idle had become a steady, purposeful thrum, and beneath it — fainter, higher — the whine of an outboard. "They're moving. Two boats up the east channel, one swinging wide to the west shallows. They mean to come at us from both sides."

"The west shallows won't float a gunboat," Tomas said. "Not even in flood."

"They're not bringing a gunboat into the shallows." Jonah's voice was flat and terrible. "They're bringing fire."

Elias understood one heartbeat later, and the understanding arrived in his stomach before his head. The western approach to the terrace ran through a stand of dead cane and half-drowned bamboo, dry above the waterline, brittle as tinder after a week of wind between storms. A boat through that cane, trailing flame —

"Get the crates to the dugouts," Jonah said. "Now. All of them. Elias — the Marisol."

"What about her?"

"She's moored in the lee of the north terrace, and she's the only thing we have that floats heavier than a log. If we can get the archive aboard and run the north channel before those boats close—" She stopped. Looked at him. And for the first time since he'd known her, Jonah Vasquez looked uncertain. "If we can't, then everything we've bled for ends on this mud tonight."

"Then let's not end," Elias said, and went down the stair two at a time.

* * *

The first flare went up while he was still untying the Marisol's stern line.

It rose from behind the western canebrake — a hissing red star that climbed, hung, and burst, and by its light Elias saw the whole geography of the trap laid bare: the long black sheet of the flood, the terraces rising like the vertebrae of some drowned god, and out in the west shallows a low boat running fast through the cane, and the cane already burning behind it. Fire took the dry stalks the way rumor takes a village — silently at first, then all at once, a running orange tide that threw shadows fifty meters high and turned the falling rain-scented night into something out of the oldest stories, the ones about the end of the world.

*"Fuego!"* Tomas's voice, cracking. *"¡Se prendió el carrizal!"*

They came out of the vault chamber at a run, hauling the last crates on improvised stretchers of tarpaulin and pole, and the world had gone entirely red. Heat pressed against Elias's face from a hundred meters away. Ash began to fall like grey snow. Somewhere in the burning cane a shot cracked, then another — wild, or warning, or simply the mercenaries shooting at the dark because the dark was all they could see.

"The dugouts are exposed!" Priya shouted, and it was the most words Elias had heard from her in days. "The east bank — they'll see—"

"They'll see ash and tarps," Jonah said. She had appeared at his shoulder with the calm of a woman standing in the eye of her own private storm. "Load the Marisol. Dugouts stay hidden until we're clear. Move!"

They moved. Crates went over the gunwale and into the hold, into the false panels, into every cavity the Marisol possessed — and Elias, hauling crate eleven, thought absurdly of the night he'd found her secret compartments and understood that the boat had been built for exactly this, for flight, for hiding precious and dangerous things, for the life of a woman who never stayed. The boat had always known what Jonah wouldn't say.

Crate twelve. Crate thirteen.

Sefu came down to the waterline on Tomas's arm, hopping, gray-faced, refusing to be carried. She stopped at the foot of the stair and looked back up at the vault entrance — the great basalt slab, the glyph rows glowing orange in the firelight, the dark doorway that held four years of her life and, buried deeper than that, the memory of every person who had ever kept those records safe through plague and conquest and flood.

"Close it," she said.

"Doctor—" Tomas began.

"Close it. Flood the counterweights. If we can't take it with us, no one takes it at all." She turned to Elias, and the firelight made her eyes look like coals in a face of ash. "Wren. Where is crate one?"

His blood went cold. He turned, scanning the hold, the tarps, the stacked canvas-wrapped shapes — and counted. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen. All present. But crate one — the index, the spine, the thing she had gripped his wrist about — the space where it had been stacked at the head of the line was empty, and the tarp that had covered it lay folded neatly aside, deliberately, the way a careful man folds a blanket when he doesn't want to wake anyone.

"Jonah." His voice didn't sound like his own. "Where's crate one?"

She was already turning, already reading the empty space the way she read water — instantly, totally, seeing the shape of what was missing and what it meant. Her gaze went up the stair, across the terrace, to the eastern edge where the ground fell away toward the flooded channel and the black water beyond.

And there — a flicker. A lantern shuttered and uncovered and shuttered again, down at the base of the lower terrace, near the old canoe landing. Once. Twice. Deliberate. A signal answered, far out on the water, by a single brief bloom of running lights.

"Ravel," Jonah said.

* * *

What happened next happened in pieces, and Elias would spend the rest of his life reassembling them and never get the sequence quite right.

He remembered shouting Ravel's name into the fire-lit dark and hearing, faintly, laughter — or maybe just the roar of the cane fire playing tricks. He remembered Jonah shoving the last crate deep into the hold and slamming the panel with her palm like a wound being closed. He remembered Sefu trying to climb the stair toward the vault alone, because the counterweight mechanism needed two hands and she would not ask, and Tomas catching her, and her striking at him — actually striking, a dying woman spending her last strength on fury — and then her leg folding under her, and the sound she made, which was not a cry but a short, bitten-off exhale, as if the infection that had been eating her for a week had chosen this exact moment to collect its debt.

He remembered Priya. That was the piece that would haunt him longest. The girl had gone still at the edge of the light, staring down at the canoe landing where the lantern had flashed, and her face in the fireglow had been the face of someone watching a nightmare walk upright out of sleep and put on a human face. She had seen men executed on this river. She knew exactly what Ravel's signal had bought, and for whom.

And he remembered the moment the second flare went up — not from the west this time but from the water, from the lead gunboat now sweeping into the open flood below the terraces with its searchlight blazing — and the beam swung across the Marisol's deck and found them all, pinned like insects, and a voice amplified to inhuman volume rolled across the water:

*"DR. SEFU. THE TERRACE IS SURROUNDED AND BURNING. SEND OUT THE RECORDS AND YOUR PEOPLE LIVE. YOU HAVE ONE MINUTE."*

Sefu was on the ground at the foot of the stair, propped against the basalt, laughing. It was a horrible sound, wet and delighted, the laugh of a woman who has finally been offered terms she can refuse with her whole heart.

"One minute," she repeated. "They give me one minute. I have given this basin four thousand years of minutes." She fumbled at her coat, and Elias dropped to his knees beside her, and she pressed something into his hands — a oilskin packet, small, heavy with paper. "The duplicate sheets. Nadia's copies of the index. She made them in March; she trusted no one, least of all Ravel. Half the index, incomplete — but half a spine is better than none." Her grip crushed his fingers. "The original goes with Ravel unless you take it back. Do you hear me? Whatever it costs. Some things cost less than they look, and some things cost more, and you do not get to know which until afterward."

Above them the minute expired. The searchlight snapped off. And the gunboat's bow gun spoke — a single round, high, deliberate, smashing into the upper terrace a hundred meters from the vault door, showering the stair with stone fragments, a promise written in explosives.

Jonah's hand closed on Elias's collar and hauled him upright.

"We're leaving," she said. "Now. Everyone in. Sefu, I'm sorry — you don't get to die on their schedule."

The Marisol's engine caught on the third try, coughing, flooding, catching — that old Lister diesel grumbling awake like a dog too loyal to know it was outmatched. The lines came off. The bank slid away. And behind them, as the burning cane reached the terraces' root and the flames climbed the ancient dry timber of the excavated scaffolding, Dr. Imara Sefu's life's work went up in a column of orange fire visible, Elias knew, from ten kilometers in every direction — a beacon, a funeral pyre, and a message.

But the crates were aboard. Twenty-nine of thirty. And the thirtieth was moving away through the dark in the bottom of a canoe, toward a gunboat, in the hands of a man who believed that everything in the world belonged to whoever could carry it off.

Elias stood at the stern rail with the oilskin packet against his chest and watched the fire shrink behind them, and felt something in himself finish hardening, the way steel finishes in a quench. He had come up this river for answers about his sister. He had found instead a war, a dying woman, a stolen index, and a pilot who ran from nothing anymore.

Ahead, the north channel waited — narrow, unmarked, rising by the hour, charted only in a dead cartographer's waterproof hand.

"Hold on," Jonah said, to no one and everyone, and drove the Marisol into the dark.
