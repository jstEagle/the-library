## 29. The Running Fight

The first shot came out of the morning mist like a wasp finding the one gap in a screen door.

Jonah heard it before she understood it — a flat crack, then the *spang* of lead biting into the Marisol's rail not two hand-widths from her elbow. Splinters stung her cheek. She didn't flinch. Flinching cost time, and time on this river had just become the only currency that mattered.

"Down!" she roared into the wheelhouse. "Everybody down!"

Behind her, crates of archive records shifted as Elias threw himself flat among them. Priya was already small against the deckhouse wall, hands over her ears, and Tomas crouched low with Sefu's head cradled in his lap below, where the fevered doctor muttered at things none of them could see. The Marisol rode heavy — heavier than Jonah had ever taken her downriver — and every meter of freeboard they'd lost to the flood current was a meter more of hull for the gunboats behind them to aim at.

She looked back once. Two wakes, white and arrogant, knifing through the brown water maybe eight hundred meters astern. The lead boat had a mounted autocannon on its bow, its barrel swinging in slow, patient arcs, the way a man swings a lantern looking for something he already knows is there.

Eight hundred meters. At full flood, running light, the gunboats would make twelve knots. The Marisol, loaded to her rails with another civilization's memory, would be lucky to make nine.

Jonah did the arithmetic she'd been doing since the terrace disappeared behind the smoke: nine against twelve, down a river with no cover, no darkness left, no tricks but the ones the water itself would give her.

"All right," she said quietly, to the boat, to the river, to whatever was listening. "Let's dance."

---

She took the Marisol off the channel's spine first.

Every pilot knew the deep water ran the outside of the bends, that the safe route hugged the cut banks where the current did your work for you. Every pilot also knew that was exactly where a pursuer would expect you to run — straight lines, honest water, a fair chase. So Jonah swung wide instead, into the inside of the long bend where the flood had spread itself thin across a drowned sandbar field, water so shallow it hissed rather than roared, braided into a dozen silver threads between bars of wet sand.

The Marisol groaned. Her old hull found the bar with a shudder that traveled up through the deck plates and into Jonah's boots, and for three terrible seconds they were plowing mud, engine screaming, propeller chewing silt.

"Hold," she said through her teeth. "Hold—"

Then the bow lifted, found the thread of deeper water between two bars, and they were through, sliding sideways across the shallows while the gunboats, committed to the main channel, fell away behind like dogs losing scent.

Elias appeared at the wheelhouse door, gray-faced, holding onto the frame. "What are you doing?"

"What they won't." She jerked her chin at the chart pinned beside the wheel — Nadia's chart, the one with the pencil annotations in that precise, patient hand. "Your sister drew these bars. Every one of them. She marked which threads hold water at flood stage and which are traps." Her finger traced the line she'd chosen, a wandering stitch through the braid. "I'm running her map."

"She surveyed this in dry season."

"And I'm reading it in flood. That's the trick of it." Jonah felt something loosen in her chest, some knot she'd been carrying since Callao Verde. "A chart isn't the river, Elias. It's what the river was. You have to know how much it's changed and which way. Your sister knew that better than anyone I ever met, and I never even saw her face."

For a moment Elias said nothing. Then he braced himself against the doorframe and stayed there, watching the water ahead of them with new eyes, and Jonah understood that he was seeing his sister alive again in every penciled depth-sounding, every careful question mark where Nadia hadn't been sure. Grief has many rooms. This one, at least, had windows.

---

The second gunboat didn't follow them into the braid. It held the main channel, running parallel, its crew tracking them through binoculars, waiting for the drowned fields to end and the channels to merge again. Patient men. Paid men. The worst kind, because they didn't hate you; they just had a job, and the job was you.

Jonah used the time.

"Ravel signaled them from the terrace," she said, loud enough for all of them. "Which means they know our heading but not our plan. They'll guess we run for the delta — open water, witnesses, safety. So that's what they'll guard. The lower river, the narrows past San Cristóbal, everything with a straight shot to the sea." She glanced back at Elias. "So we don't go there."

"Where else is there?"

"The side-channels." She tapped the chart. "Nadia marked three. Flood cuts — places where high water opens routes that don't exist any other time of year. They're not on any company chart, because until this week, they weren't there at all." A grim smile touched her mouth. "We're going to sail down roads that were born last Tuesday and die next Friday. Nobody can blockade a road that doesn't exist yet."

Tomas crawled up from below, Sefu's pistol tucked in his waistband, looking like a man who'd been handed a snake and told it was a rope. "And if one of those roads dies early?"

"Then we're aground in a flooded forest, and they pick us off at their leisure." Jonah turned back to the wheel. "So let's not let it die early."

---

They ran the first flood-cut at midday.

It opened off the main channel behind a screen of flooded scrub oak — a gap in the green that only showed itself when you were already inside it, water pushing through a corridor of drowned trunks with the smooth, muscular certainty of something that had done this before and would do it again. The Marisol threaded it at half throttle, Jonah standing at the wheel with her whole body reading the current, feeling the boat answer her hands the way a good horse answers a rider who has stopped giving orders and started having conversations.

Twice the corridor narrowed to less than a boat's width of clear water. Twice she felt the current try to set them against a submerged trunk, and twice she countered with the wheel and a burst of throttle, the engine note rising like a held breath.

Priya watched from the deckhouse roof, and somewhere in the middle of the second narrowing, Jonah heard the girl laugh — a short, startled sound, quickly smothered, but real. First sound she'd made since the dugout. Jonah filed it away like a coin saved.

They came out of the cut into a long, quiet lagoon where the flood had drowned an old oxbow, still water mirror-flat except for the wake unrolling behind them. For ten minutes there was no sound but the engine at idle and the drip of water from the canopy.

Then, far behind, faint as a memory: the thud-thud-thud of a diesel working hard.

"They found where we left the channel," Elias said.

"They found where we *could have* left the channel." Jonah killed the engine entirely and let the Marisol drift. "There are four ways out of that lagoon. They'll check them one at a time, and they'll do it slow, because they're scared of grounding worse than they're scared of us. Fear makes you careful, and careful makes you slow." She looked at the sky, reading the light. "We've got maybe two hours of that. Then we move."

---

They moved in the rain.

The monsoon arrived over the western ridgeline like a debt collector, a purple-black wall that ate the horizon in minutes, and Jonah welcomed it the way she'd never welcomed weather in her life. Rain flattened the water, erased wakes, turned the world into gray wool. In it, the gunboats' advantages — speed, guns, numbers — counted for less, and hers counted for more, because hers lived in her head and her hands and thirty years of a river that kept changing its mind.

She ran the second flood-cut blind, by sound and feel, with Priya posted at the bow calling depths in a voice that grew steadier with every call. "Two meters! One and a half! Clear left! Log, log, starboard!" The girl had a river voice. Jonah noted that too.

The bullet found her at the exit.

The third gunboat — the one they hadn't seen, the fast patrol launch that must have cut upriver through the night to get ahead of them — was waiting where the cut spilled back into the main channel, tucked under the cut bank in the rain-shadow of a dead fig tree. Jonah saw it exactly one second too late: the shape resolving out of the gray, the muzzle flash stuttering.

"Hard down!" She threw the wheel over and slammed the throttle forward, putting the Marisol's superstructure between them and the bank, and the rounds came anyway — a tearing rip of automatic fire stitching across the stern, punching through the empty fuel drums lashed aft, blowing splinters off the wheelhouse door.

One of them found her.

Not clean — a graze, a hot line drawn along her ribs like someone dragging a coal across her side, and then the wet warmth spreading under her shirt, shocking at first, then almost gentle. She'd been shot before, or near enough. The body remembers the grammar if not the sentence.

"Jonah!" Elias was suddenly there, one arm around her, hauling her down behind the wheel as a second burst walked across the water where she'd been standing.

"I'm flying the boat!" she snarled, and shoved him off, and stood back up.

Because here was the thing, the true thing, the thing she'd never said aloud to anyone: in that moment, with blood soaking her shirt and the river trying to kill her from both ends, Jonah Vasquez was not afraid. She was *awake*. Every sense lit and tuned, the whole gray world sharp as etched glass, and her hands moving on the wheel with a knowledge deeper than thought. This was the room she'd been born into. This was the only place she'd ever been completely honest.

The launch was coming after them now, its engine howling, its skipper hungry — too hungry. He'd seen the hit. He thought they were crippled, or nearly, and greed makes pilots stupid.

Jonah gave him what he wanted.

She let the Marisol stagger — backed off the throttle, let the boat wallow, let the smoke from the punctured drums curl aft like a wound smoking. Ahead, maybe six hundred meters, the channel bent hard around a point of black rock, and beyond the bend, if Nadia's chart told the truth, lay the third flood-cut: a narrow slot between two ridges where the river split around an island and the eastern channel, in flood, ran fast and shallow and treacherous over a shelf of submerged rock.

No sane pilot would take it loaded. No sane pilot would take it at all.

The launch closed to two hundred meters. Fifty meters of open water to the bend. She could hear the other gunboat behind now too, both engines converging, the trap closing exactly the way a trap closes when the prey runs true.

"Elias." Her voice came out level, almost gentle. "Get everyone holding onto something. Tell them whatever you want. Tell them we're winning."

"Aren't we?"

"We're about to find out."

She waited until the launch committed to the straight — until its skipper had put his bow down and his throttle up and there was no taking it back — and then she swung the Marisol around the black point so hard the boat lay over on her beam, cargo shifting below with a crash, and drove straight at the slot.

The eastern channel took them like a throat.

Water that had been pushing them along now *pulled*, the flood current funneling between the ridges at speeds the chart's little arrows could only suggest, and the Marisol surfed down the chute with the wheel gone light in Jonah's hands, steering half by rudder and half by faith. The rock shelf rose beneath the hull — she felt it, a grinding shiver through the keel, the sound of paint and prayer being sacrificed together — and then they were over it and through, spat out into the lower pool spinning slightly, wallowing, alive.

Behind them, the sound.

The launch's skipper had followed. Of course he had. But he'd come in fast and fat and confident, and the shelf that had kissed the Marisol's keel caught his hull square amidships at fourteen knots. The impact carried across the water like a door slamming in a cathedral. His bow reared, stalled, and the flood current, which does not negotiate, took him sideways and folded him against the rocks with a shriek of tearing metal. Men went into the water. The river took two of them before Jonah could even think about stopping, and she didn't stop, because stopping meant dying, and the man who'd shot her could swim or not as the river decided.

The second gunboat rounded the bend, saw the wreck, saw the slot, and slowed.

Slowed. Hesitated. And in that hesitation, Jonah read the rest of the day: they would go around, the long way, and lose hours, and by then—

By then she intended to be a rumor.

---

They ran until the light went copper and then gray, through country no chart but Nadia's admitted existed. The wound kept her honest — every breath pulled a hot wire tight along her ribs — but she flew the boat anyway, because there was no one else, and because flying the boat was the only prayer she knew.

At dusk they tied up in a drowned gallery forest, hull hidden among flooded trunks, engine cold, and Jonah finally let Elias peel her shirt back and look at the damage.

"Graze," he pronounced, after cleaning it with river water and the expedition's dwindling medical kit. "Ugly. Not deep. You'll live to be stubborn about it."

"That's the plan."

He worked in silence a moment, taping the dressing, and then said, without looking up: "You smiled. When they were shooting at us. I saw it."

Jonah watched the rain dimple the black water. Somewhere out in the flooded forest, a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"Yeah," she said. "I did."

"You want to tell me why?"

She considered the question the way she considered most questions — turning it over, checking it for hooks. But the day had taken something out of her, some last layer of the lacquer, and what came out was closer to truth than anything she'd given him yet.

"Because out there, today, I wasn't running *from* anything. I was running *for* something. Cargo. People. A promise I made to a dying woman." She flexed her hand on the wheel, watching it obey. "Thirty years I've been lightening the boat, Elias. Throwing things overboard so I could go faster. Today I was heavy, and heavy was better."

Elias tied off the bandage and sat back on his heels. "Sefu asked me something similar, before—" He stopped. Started again. "She asked what I'd do with my sister's maps once this was over. I told her I didn't know. I think I lied."

"Most people lie about that one."

"Do you? Know what you'll do?"

Jonah looked downstream, where the dark was coming up out of the south like a tide, carrying with it the smell of the delta, the sea, the whole wide escape she'd spent her life mistaking for freedom. The wound ached. The boat sat heavy around her, full of a dead woman's archive and a living girl's laughter and a stranger who'd become, somewhere in the running fight, something else.

"I know what I won't do," she said. "That's a start."

---

They made the last leg in the dark, running without lights on water that glowed faintly with phosphorescence stirred up by the flood, each wake a smear of cold fire. Priya spotted the village lights first — a scatter of orange points on the left bank, upstream of the great split where the river divided around the drowned hills, and Jonah felt something turn over in her chest that had no name she wanted to use.

She knew those lights. Knew the village behind them. Knew the old woman who traded dried fish and the boy she'd once taught to throw a cast net, years ago, in another life, when she'd stopped there for three days repairing a seized impeller and left before anyone could learn her name.

Three days. She'd allowed herself three days, and it had felt like theft.

Now she came back with a burned terrace behind her, a hole in her side, a boat full of secrets, and two gunboats somewhere in the dark behind them learning the long way around.

"Jonah." Elias's voice, quiet, at her shoulder. "Those lights. Is that—"

"Yeah."

"You've been here before."

"I've been everywhere before." She eased the throttle down and let the Marisol drift toward the bank, toward the lights, toward the thing she'd been outrunning for thirty years without knowing its name. "That was always the problem."

The current took the last of their way. The boat slid in among the moored dugouts, silent as a leaf landing, and somewhere on the shore a dog began to bark, and then a lamp flared in a doorway, and a figure came down to the water's edge holding the light high — an old woman, small and straight-backed, peering out across the black water at the boat that had come out of the storm.

Jonah stood at the rail, hand pressed to her bandaged ribs, and waited to find out whether the river had brought her home or merely brought her to ground.

The old woman raised the lamp higher. Squinted. And then her voice came across the water, cracked and certain, saying a name Jonah hadn't heard spoken in eleven years:

"*Marisol.*"

Not the boat. The woman. The captain.

Someone here remembered.
