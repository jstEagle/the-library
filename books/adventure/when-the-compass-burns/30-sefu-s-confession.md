## 30. Sefu's Confession

The Marisol's hold smelled of diesel, river rot, and the particular sweetness of a wound that had gone past saving. Imara Sefu had spent forty years cataloguing smells — the mineral breath of limestone caves, the alkaline sting of old paper, the copper-and-citrus tang of a dig site freshly opened — and she had always believed that a trained nose could name anything. But this smell, rising from her own body, from the place below her ribs where Ravel's knife had found her in the dark of the terrace camp eleven days ago, resisted naming. It was not the smell of dying, she decided. It was the smell of a lie finally decomposing.

She lay on a pallet of folded tarpaulins wedged between crates, and the crates were the problem. The crates were everything.

The boat moved beneath her with the patient, muscular rhythm of a vessel working against current, and each swell of the engine transmitted itself through the deck into her spine, into the wound, into the fever that had taken up residence behind her eyes like a second, brighter sun. She had refused the last of the antibiotics. Priya had argued. Elias Wren had argued harder, in that flat, careful way of his, as though grief were a language he'd learned to speak without an accent. She had listened to both of them and then told Jonah Vasquez to keep the pills for someone who could still be saved, and Jonah — that remarkable, closed woman — had looked at her for a long moment and put the bottle away without a word. There was a mercy in that. The mercy of being believed about your own body.

The rain came down on the deck above in sheets, in fists, in the long drumming rolls of a monsoon that had arrived a month early and showed no interest in restraint. Below the waterline she could hear the leak — a soft, insistent hiss, like the river whispering through a crack in the world, and the bilge pump answering it in stubborn, mechanical syllables. The Marisol was dying too, she thought. Everything on this river was dying or leaking or burning. And yet the boat held. The boat kept pushing upstream against a current that wanted it gone.

She had been a woman who pushed against currents her whole life. It was worth considering, now, at the end, whether she had ever pushed in the right direction.

Footsteps on the ladder. Not the crew's easy clatter, not Priya's quick light scramble. These were deliberate, weighted, the steps of someone who had thought about whether to come down and had decided yes the way you decide to open a door you know is cold behind.

"Dr. Sefu." Jonah Vasquez's voice, low, pitched under the rain-noise. "Tomas says you asked for me."

"I asked for the captain," Sefu said. "I'm glad it's the same person."

Jonah came into the lamplight. The woman was younger than her reputation — or perhaps her reputation had simply never been written down, Sefu reflected, which was its own kind of youth. She had the stillness of people who had practiced being still so long it had become a kind of armor. There was a bandage at her ribs where the mercenary's bullet had grazed her in the running fight, and she favored that side without appearing to. Her hands were river hands: scarred, capable, restless even at rest, as if they were always holding a wheel that wasn't there.

"Sit," Sefu said. "Unless captains don't sit in their own holds."

"Captains don't sit much." But Jonah lowered herself onto an upturned crate, and Sefu watched her do it — watched the way she chose the crate with the flat top, the way she placed it so she could see both the ladder and the hold's dark end. A woman who had learned the hard way what corners cost. Sefu had spent her career reading landscapes. She read this one now, this woman, and what she read frightened her, because it was a map of everything Sefu herself might have become if she had never been given a university, a grant, a name that opened doors. A woman made entirely of exits.

"You're dying," Jonah said. Not cruelly. Simply, the way she'd say the river's rising.

"I'm dying," Sefu agreed. "The knife was filthy, the antibiotics were few, and I have spent my life spending my body like a man spends a fortune he doesn't believe is his. So. We have perhaps a day. Perhaps two if the fever is lazy." She smiled, and knew from the way the smile landed that it was not a comforting smile. "I didn't ask you down here for last words, Captain. Last words are for people whose stories are finished. I asked you because yours isn't, and because I'm about to make it worse."

Jonah said nothing. She was good at nothing. Sefu had known generals, ministers, two heads of state, and a cardinal, and none of them could hold a silence the way this river pilot held one — not as absence but as pressure, a hand laid flat on the conversation.

"You've seen the crates," Sefu said.

"I've seen the crates."

"Then you've seen the seals. The archive vault at the Sunken Terrace held eleven hundred sealed cylinders. Clay and wax and lacquer, some of them older than any alphabet that's ever been taught in a classroom in this basin. Maps. Deeds. Water-rights. Testimonies. Records of every tributary, every buried claim, every village boundary that the colonial surveys rewrote and the companies since have burned." Her voice was going rough; the fever made her eloquent and she resented it. "Four hundred and six of them are on this boat. The rest —" She stopped. The rest were ash, or sinking, or in the hands of the man who had escaped with the index. "The rest are gone."

"The index," Jonah said. "Ravel's crate."

"The index." Sefu closed her eyes. Behind them, the fire on the terraces played again, orange against black rain, and Ravel's face as he turned away from her — not triumphant, which she could have hated, but *reasonable*, which she could not. "You want to know what's in the four hundred and six that made a company burn a village to get them. I'll tell you what's in them. Power, Captain. Not the vulgar kind. Not gold. The other kind. The kind that decides whose grandfather owned which bend of which river, whose well was dug by whose great-great-grandmother, whose land was taken and by whom and when and under what signature. With those records, the villages of this basin could take back everything. Every stolen acre. Every poisoned well. Every lie on every land title from here to the mountains."

"And without them?"

"Without them, the companies keep everything, and the river forgets, and in a hundred years no one will even know there was anything to remember." She opened her eyes. The lamp flame swam. "That's the speech. That's the speech I gave my crew, my funders, my own reflection. And every word of it is true, Jonah Vasquez, and every word of it is also a lie, because of what I was going to do with it."

The rain hammered. Somewhere above, Elias Wren's voice murmured to Priya, low and continuous, a man standing watch over a dying stranger because his sister had died in the same cause and he had nowhere else to put his hands.

"I had offers," Sefu said. "You don't run an expedition into monsoon country for three years without learning who's watching. There were men in Callao Verde — the same fixer, I'd wager, who put you on this river — who understood exactly what the vault contained, and they didn't want it burned. Burning is for peasants' records. They wanted it *curated*. They wanted access. And they were prepared to pay for it in a currency a woman like me could never earn honestly: endowment. A research institute. My name on a building. The archive preserved — oh, beautifully preserved, climate-controlled, catalogued — with a reading room that admitted exactly the people who could pay, and never, ever, the villages whose names were written in the clay." She laughed, and the laugh tore something in her chest, and she rode the pain down like a diver. "I hadn't said yes. I want you to know that. I want it carved on whatever stone they give me, if they give me stone: *she had not yet said yes*. But I had not said no either. I had kept the door open. I had told myself it was leverage — that I could trade selective access to selective buyers and use the money to protect the whole. That I would be the one holding the key, so the key would stay clean. Do you know what that is, Captain? That's the oldest sentence in the language of corruption. *I would be the one holding the key.* Ravel said it to me once, almost word for word, the week before he sold us. I should have heard myself in him. I didn't. That's my confession. Not that I betrayed them. That I was on the road to it, and the road was paved, and comfortable, and I had already walked far enough to know the way."

Jonah had not moved. But something in her stillness had changed — the way water changes before it freezes, Sefu thought, nothing visible, everything different.

"You're telling me this because I'm a stranger," Jonah said at last. "Dying people confess to strangers."

"I'm telling you this because you're the only person on this boat who has ever run." Sefu pushed herself up on one elbow, and the hold tilted, and she let it. "Tomas will tell you I'm a hero. Priya believes it, God help her. Elias needs me to be a hero or his sister died for nothing. They'll all tell you the archive is sacred and must go to the universities, the ministries, the *proper hands* — and every one of those proper hands has a reading room with a lock on it, Captain, and every lock has a price. I'm the only one down here who will tell you the truth, which is that the archive is not sacred. It's a weapon that happens to be shaped like the past. And weapons don't care who picks them up. They only care who's holding them when the moment comes." She reached out and caught Jonah's wrist — her own hand shockingly light, bird-boned, already half-ghost — and felt the captain's pulse jump under the skin and settle, disciplined, obedient. Even her heartbeat took orders. "So here is what I'm giving you, Jonah Vasquez, and I'm giving it to you because you're a runner, and runners know what it costs to carry things. I'm giving you the choice. Not the crew. Not a committee. Not a ministry with a seal. *You.*"

"Me." Flat. Wary.

"You. When I'm gone, there will be a vote, because Elias is a decent man and decent men vote. But votes can be argued with, and I am about to lose my ability to argue. So I'm telling you first, and telling you plainly, and you can do with it what you like." Her grip tightened, what was left of her strength funneled into five fingers. "You can take the four hundred and six cylinders to the capital and become the most powerful woman in this basin inside a year. You can sell them, and never pilot another leaking boat, and buy back every debt you've ever run from. You can give them to the universities and let the lock-keepers decide who remembers. You can copy them for the villages and sink the originals where no dredge will ever reach — and yes, Wren will propose that, and it's a beautiful proposal, and it erases the past instead of selling it, and maybe erasing is just selling to no one." Her breath caught. She rode it. "Or you can do the thing I was too proud and too frightened and too *flattered* to do, which is to put them in the hands of the people whose names are written in the clay, all of them, uncurated, unlocked, and let the river decide what it keeps. Every one of those roads is a road I stood at the head of. I chose none of them. I chose the doorway and stood in it and called it caution." She let go of Jonah's wrist. "I'm not asking you to be better than me, Captain. I'm asking you to be *different* than me. It's a lower bar than it sounds."

For a long moment the only sounds were the rain, the pump, the engine's patient labor, the leak's soft hiss — the river, everywhere, whispering through the cracks in everything.

"Why me?" Jonah said finally, and her voice had a crack in it, hairline, the kind you'd miss if you hadn't spent forty years reading surfaces for what ran beneath. "You've known me three weeks."

"Three weeks is longer than most people know anyone." Sefu sank back onto the tarpaulins, and the fever-sun behind her eyes flared, and she spoke through it because she had very little time and the truth was heavy and she wanted it set down before her arms gave out. "Because I watched you at the Split Channel. I was half-dead in the hold and I felt that boat turn, and I felt the current take us, and I felt you *choose* — not once but a hundred times a minute, reading water no chart has ever mapped, and every choice was for the people aboard. Not for the cargo. Not for the archive. Not for the legend of Jonah Vasquez, if there is one. For the people." She looked up at the beams, at the dark between them where the rain's percussion lived. "I chose for the work. Ravel chose for himself. The company chooses for the company. Everyone in this story has chosen for something, and the something is the whole of them. You choose for people, Jonah. You've been running from that fact for years, I think — it's a heavy thing to be, the one who chooses for people — but the river keeps washing you up against it." Her eyes found Jonah's and held them, and the fever made the lamplight swim, but the words came clear, cold as deep water. "The archive will test you the way the rapids tested you. It will offer you everything you've been running toward and everything you've been running from, in the same crate, with the same seal. And no one will be able to tell you what's right. That's what power is, Captain. That's the whole of it. It's the moment when no one can tell you what's right, and you find out what you are."

Jonah stood. She stood slowly, one hand pressed to her grazed ribs, and looked down at Sefu for a long time — and Sefu, who had spent her life excavating the past out of the earth, had the sudden vertiginous sense of being excavated herself, of lying at the bottom of a trench while someone above read her strata, layer by layer: the ambition, the fear, the open door, the comfortable road, the key she had kept polished in her pocket for a buyer she'd never let herself name.

"I won't decide tonight," Jonah said.

"No," Sefu agreed. "Runners don't decide in the room where the question's asked. They take it upriver and turn it over for a hundred miles. I know the type." She smiled, and this time it was, briefly, a real smile. "Decide before the river peaks. After that, the channels close, and whatever you've chosen, you'll be standing in it."

Jonah nodded once and went to the ladder, and paused there, one hand on the rail, her back to the dying woman.

"Sefu." She said the name without the *Doctor*, and it sounded, in the iron dark of the hold, like something being returned — a tool handed back to its owner, or a debt forgiven. "The villages. When you told yourself it was leverage. Did you ever actually open the cylinders? Read what's in them?"

"Some," Sefu said. "One, mostly. The oldest one. It's a boundary record from a village on a river I can't name, because the name is in a language with maybe two hundred living speakers. It lists who planted which grove. Who dug which well. Who is buried under which stone." She swallowed against the dryness. "It ends with a line the translators argued over for a month. The best reading is: *the water remembers, so we write, so the water doesn't have to.*"

The lamp guttered. Above them the rain redoubled, and the Marisol pushed on against the current, and Jonah Vasquez climbed out of the hold without another word, and Sefu lay back in the dark with her confession finally set down like a crate on solid ground, lighter by exactly its own weight, and listened to the river whisper through the cracks, and waited to see what the woman who chose for people would do with the only power she had ever been offered honestly.

* * *

The fever took the shape of the terrace camp that night.

She was back there in the dream, or the memory, or whatever place the dying keep for their unfinished business: the rain, the orange light, the sound of crates being carried down to the water. In the dream she stood at the vault mouth with her hand on the last seal and Ravel beside her, reasonable as ever, saying *we can't save it all, Imara, we can only save what we can protect, and protection has a price, and someone has to set the price* — and in the dream she had answered him, finally, the answer she had not found in eleven days of waking: *then we set it at nothing. We give it away. The only price that can't be bought is the one no one pays.*

She woke to lamplight and to Priya asleep against the crate beside her, the girl's hand resting on Sefu's arm without permission or apology, and to the sound of voices on the deck above — Jonah's low and level, Elias's sharper, the two of them arguing in the rain about something with the weight of a future in it. The index, probably. The vote. The roads.

Sefu could not make out the words. She found she did not need to.

She had set the choice down where it belonged, in the hands of a woman who read water the way scholars read clay, and the fever was pulling her under again, gently now, the way the river takes a boat whose captain has finally let go of the wheel — and her last coherent thought, before the dark came down warm and total as floodwater, was of a village on an unnamed river, and groves planted by hands two thousand years gone, and the line in the clay that she had carried across three continents and one burning terrace and was now, at last, ready to stop carrying:

*The water remembers, so we write, so the water doesn't have to.*

Above her, the argument on deck went quiet. Then Jonah's boots crossed to the ladder, and paused, and did not come down — and the Marisol held her heading, upstream, into the closing dark.
