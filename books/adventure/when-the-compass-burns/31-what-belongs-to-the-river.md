## 31. What Belongs to the River

Sefu's voice had gone thin as paper by the time she finished, but the words themselves were heavy as ingots. She lay propped against a crate in the Marisol's flooded hold, her bandaged leg stretched out before her, and she watched Jonah with the patience of a woman who had spent months underground reading dead languages — the patience of someone who knew that what she'd said could not be unsaid.

"Think about it," Sefu said. "That's all I ask. Not tonight. Not even tomorrow, if the river gives us that long. But think."

Jonah didn't answer. She climbed the ladder to the deck instead, because the hold smelled of bilgewater and blood and the sweet rot of Sefu's wound, and because there were things a captain did with her hands when her mind refused to be still: check the mooring lines, test the wind, count the crates lashed under tarpaulin like coffins waiting for their turn.

The rain had eased to a fine gray drizzle. The river ran high and brown and patient, carrying branches and whole trees south toward the sea, and somewhere downstream two gunboats were regrouping, and somewhere upstream the Sunken Terrace burned its slow smoke into the clouds, and here in this mangrove cove, wedged between roots older than any map, Jonah Vasquez stood on the deck of a dying boat and tried to understand what it meant to own a thing that every powerful man in the basin would kill for.

* * *

She made coffee on the little spirit stove because her hands needed work, and while it boiled she let herself do the arithmetic.

The archive was real. She had carried three of its crates aboard with her own arms, had felt their weight — not much more than paper and oilcloth, really, forty kilos each at most, light enough that a strong man could carry one away under his shirt if he tore it open. But inside those crates was the shape of the whole basin: every hidden tributary, every buried claim, every name scratched into stone by people who had understood water better than anyone alive now understood it. Land titles older than the republic. River rights no government had ever acknowledged. The kind of knowledge that turned a company into a country, or a country into a company.

Sefu had laid it out plainly, the way dying women do, having no more use for decoration. Sell access — selective, careful, priced high — to the governments that would pay for stability, or to the universities that would pay for truth, or to whoever else came with clean enough hands. Enough money to vanish. Enough leverage to never run again. A new life anywhere on earth, under any name she cared to choose, with lawyers and distance standing guard over her sleep.

*Anywhere,* Sefu had said. And Jonah had heard the word the way you hear a door opening onto a room you once lived in.

Because that was the old dream, wasn't it. That was the compass needle pointing north, always north, always away. One more job, one more season, one more river, and then enough — enough to stop, enough to be safe, enough to finally set down whatever it was she'd been carrying since the night the sky over San Cristóbal went orange and she stood in the shallows watching her whole life burn behind her and felt, for the first time in years, nothing but relief.

She poured the coffee. It tasted of char and river water, which was to say it tasted like home, and she hated herself a little for thinking so.

* * *

San Cristóbal. She hadn't let herself say the name in eleven years, and now it came up like floodwater finding an old channel, filling her without asking permission.

A town of four thousand souls strung along a bend of the Río Blanco, where the water ran green through cane fields and the church bell cracked on the hour and nobody locked anything. She'd been twenty-six. She'd had a boat then too — the *Alba*, a fat old cargo scow she'd bought with three years of wages, and a berth at the municipal dock, and a woman named Rosa who sold fruit by the landing and laughed with her whole body, and a friend named Aurelio who ran the fuel depot and knew everything about everyone, the way friendly men do.

Aurelio. That was the wound's true name, though she'd spent a decade calling it something else.

He'd been the one who told her about the survey crew coming upriver, the men with the corporate credentials and the polite questions, mapping the basin for the water concessions. He'd been the one who warned her, weeks later, in a voice gone strange: *They're going to need someone who knows the channels, Jonah. Someone local. There's good money in it.* And she'd said no, because she'd seen what those concessions did to villages downstream, and she thought that was the end of it.

It wasn't the end of it. It was the beginning. Because Aurelio hadn't just asked her — he'd answered for her. He'd given them the charts she kept in his office for safekeeping. He'd shown them where the *Alba* docked, where Rosa's stall stood, which houses belonged to which families, and when the company's men came back to clear the "obstructions" from the concession route, they came knowing everything, courtesy of a friend's loose tongue and a payment Jonah never saw and never wanted to see.

The fire took the east quarter first. Then the cane fields. Then the church, whose bell cracked one last time in the heat, a sound she still heard sometimes in thunder. Forty-one people died, though the official count said nine, because official counts are written by the survivors' enemies. Rosa survived — Jonah found out years later, married, three children, a stall in a market town two hundred kilometers away — but Jonah had never dared go look, because what do you say? What possible sentence in any language?

So she ran. Of course she ran. Everyone runs from the scene of their crime, and hers was this: she had trusted a man, and her trust had been the map.

After that, the rule. Never stop longer than a job requires. Never learn names past the ones on a manifest. Never let a town get familiar enough to burn. Callao Verde, Puerto Hondo, Santa Ana de las Aguas — she could list them like stations of a cross, every port where she'd tied up just long enough to load and cast off before anyone could ask her to stay, before anyone could hand her a chart to keep safe, before she could love anything that fire could find.

And now Dr. Imara Sefu, bleeding out in her hold, was offering her the one thing she'd told herself she wanted all these years: enough money and enough distance to make running unnecessary. To make safety permanent. To buy her way out of the story entirely.

And Jonah sat on her own deck with cold coffee and understood, finally, with the terrible clarity the river grants at dusk, that the offer was a lie. Not Sefu's lie — Sefu believed it, the way drowning people believe in ropes. It was the lie Jonah had been telling herself since San Cristóbal: that running was a strategy, that motion was safety, that there existed some far shore where the past couldn't swim.

There was no far shore. She'd checked. She'd spent eleven years checking, and every port was the same port, and every dawn was the same dawn, and the only thing that had ever actually changed was the depth of the water under her keel.

* * *

Elias found her there after full dark, when the rain had stopped entirely and the frogs had started up in the mangroves, a sound like the world clearing its throat.

He didn't say anything at first. He lowered himself onto the crate across from her, and for a while they just listened to the river move — that vast, muscular, indifferent sound, the sound of something doing exactly what it was made to do, going exactly where it was made to go.

"Sefu talked to you," he said finally.

"She talked to everyone. Dying people get talkative."

"What did she offer you?"

Jonah looked at him. In the dark his face was mostly shadow, but she could read him well enough now — six weeks on the water will do that, will teach you a man the way you learn a channel, by the hazards he shows and the ones he hides. He was thinking about Nadia. He was always thinking about Nadia; it lived in him like ballast. But tonight there was something else underneath, something he was carrying carefully, the way you carry a full cup through rough water.

"She offered me a new life," Jonah said. "Anywhere I want. Money, leverage, protection. The whole catalog."

"And?"

"And I'm sitting here drinking bad coffee instead of packing."

Elias almost smiled. "I keep waiting for you to run. You know that? Every day since Callao Verde, some part of me has been braced for it — waking up and the *Marisol*'s gone and I'm standing on some dock holding a bag of cash nobody wants anymore." He turned his cup slowly in his hands. "But you didn't run when the pirates found us. You didn't run when the village burned. You didn't run when Ravel signaled the gunboats and every sane instinct must have been screaming at you to cut the lines and take your chances alone." He paused. "You stayed. Every single time, you stayed. So I've stopped believing the story you tell about yourself."

"Which story is that?"

"The one where you're a runner." He said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "Runners don't spend a night bailing seawater for strangers. Runners don't memorize a dead woman's survey markers because it matters to *her*, not to them. Runners don't come back for people." He looked up. "I think you've been running from the idea that you're allowed to stop. That's different. That's not a direction. That's just fear wearing a compass."

The words landed in her chest like stones dropped down a well — she heard them fall a long way before they hit anything. She wanted to be angry. Anger was the old tool, the reliable one; anger had kept her moving for eleven years, had burned bridges ahead of her so she'd have no choice but to cross the next one. But the anger wouldn't come. Maybe she'd used it all up hauling crates through the rain, or maybe Elias Wren, professional liar, had simply gotten too good at telling the truth.

"You don't know what I've done," she said quietly. "What following me costs. Ask the town of San Cristóbal what my trust is worth. Ask the forty-one."

Elias was silent a moment. "Nadia used to say something," he offered at last, staring into the black water. "When we were kids. When I'd get scared of losing things." He turned the phrase over like a stone, testing its weight. "She'd say: the river doesn't lose anything. It just carries it somewhere new."

"That's not comfort. That's hydrology."

"It's both." He set down his cup. "Get some sleep, Captain. Whatever you decide — decide it rested."

He went below, and Jonah stayed.

* * *

The river spoke to her that night, the way it sometimes did in the small hours, when the engine was off and the rain had stopped and there was nothing between her and the water but a hull of riveted iron.

Not words. Rivers don't use words. But meaning, the way meaning lives in current and eddy and the particular silence where a snag waits just under the surface. She had learned to read it as a child, before she could read letters — her grandfather's hand on the tiller, saying *feel that, mija? The water remembers where the bank used to be. It always remembers. You just have to listen.*

The water remembers.

She thought about the drowned mission church they'd passed in chapter after chapter of this journey, its bell tower rising from the flood like a finger pointed at heaven, and how the river had swallowed it a century ago and still marked it on no map but held it in every pilot's memory. She thought about the Sunken Terrace, drowned and forgotten and keeping its records anyway, patient as sediment, until a woman brilliant enough came looking. Nothing the river takes stays lost. It just goes somewhere new, and waits.

And she thought about the archive, sleeping in its crates ten feet from where she sat. Wealth, Sefu had called it. Leverage. A new life anywhere.

But that was the trap, wasn't it — not the corruption of it, though God knew that was real, but the *anywhere*. Anywhere was just another word for nowhere. Anywhere was the eleventh port town, the twelfth, the thirtieth, the same coffee and the same strangers and the same rule: never stop longer than a job requires. Sefu was offering her the chance to keep running forever with the bills paid, and she had nearly mistaken it for freedom.

What belonged to the river, the river kept. And what did Jonah Vasquez belong to?

She walked the deck, slow, touching things. The rail her father had re-welded. The wheel with its worn spot where her palm fit like a signature. The engine hatch, dented where the pirate's bullet had kissed it. Eleven years of iron and diesel and salt, the only constant witness to her life, and she loved it the way you love a scar — not despite the damage but because of what the damage proved.

And beyond the rail, across the black water, the mangrove cove gave way to a rise of ground, and on the rise, dim in the starless dark, she could just make out the shape of the village where they'd sheltered — the one that remembered her. Where the elders had come down to the landing two days ago and an old woman named Doña Estrella had taken Jonah's face in her two brown hands and said, *Vasquez. The pilot girl. You brought us medicine in the dry year, '09. We prayed for you after. We never knew where you went.*

We prayed for you after.

Eleven years of running, and somewhere behind her, all that time, people had been praying. Not cursing. Praying. She had built her whole exile on the belief that she was a contagion, that everything she touched caught fire, that the kindest thing she could do for any place was leave it. And here was a village that had kept her in its memory the way the river keeps a sunken church — not lost, just carried somewhere new, waiting.

Maybe that was the answer. Maybe nothing she loved had ever burned because she loved it. Maybe towns burned because men with credentials and concessions burned them, and the only power her absence ever had was to make sure no one was left who knew the channels well enough to fight back.

Well. She knew the channels now. Better than the company did. Better than anyone.

Jonah stood at the rail until the sky went the color of wet ash, and the river rose beneath her, brown and patient and remembering, carrying everything it had ever taken toward some sea that would give it all back as rain.

She didn't have an answer yet. But for the first time in eleven years, she noticed, the needle wasn't trembling.

* * *

Dawn came gray and dripping. Below decks, Sefu's fever had climbed through the night, and Priya sat beside her with cool cloths, and Tomas was rigging a stretcher sling from cargo netting, and Elias stood at the foot of the ladder with a question already forming on his face.

Jonah came down before he could ask it.

"We're not selling it," she said. "Any of it. Not to governments, not to universities, not to saints. We copy what can be copied and we sink the rest where no dredge will ever reach — and I know the place. There's a blue hole off the old mission channel, sixty meters down, cold as a grave. The river keeps what belongs to it." She looked around the hold, at the fevered legend, the grieving brother, the silent girl, the geologist with his broken glasses taped together. "But that's a decision for all of us, not for me, and not for a dying woman's guilt. So we run for the village. All of us. And we put it to the people who'll have to live with what we choose."

Sefu opened her eyes. They were bright with fever and something else — something that might have been relief, might have been surrender, might have been the last light of a woman letting go of the thing she'd killed for. "The villages," she whispered. "Imara, you fool. It was always supposed to be the villages."

"Then rest," Jonah said. "We'll carry it the rest of the way."

She climbed back up into the gray morning, and her hands went to the mooring lines, and they were steady.

Downstream, half a day's run through the flooded channels, smoke was rising from the village landing — too much smoke, the wrong color, thick and black and oily, the kind that comes from engines and generators and things that burn hot on purpose.

Jonah stood at the wheel and felt the old itch rise in her palms, the compass pulling, the voice that had saved her life a dozen times whispering *run, run, there's still water enough to disappear.*

She gripped the worn spot on the wheel until her knuckles went white, and pointed the Marisol's bow straight at the smoke.

"No," she said aloud, to the river, to the burning towns behind her, to herself. "Not this time."

And she opened the throttle.
