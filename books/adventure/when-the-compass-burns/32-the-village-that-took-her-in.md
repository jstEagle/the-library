## 32. The Village That Took Her In

We came down out of the split channels at dusk, the river running bronze and slow around us, and I thought at first the lights ahead were fireflies. Then they resolved into lanterns strung along a palisade of blackened bamboo, and behind them the low shapes of houses raised on stilts above the waterline, and behind those, faint as breath on glass, the green shoulders of hills going dark.

Jonah stood at the wheel with her ribs wrapped and her face gray, and she did not say anything for a long time. The Marisol's engine had been coughing since the fight — a wet, metallic rattle that sounded like a man clearing his throat before saying something final — and we had been running on prayer and bailing buckets for two days. Sefu lay below decks in the hold we'd cleared of crates, fevered, her wound gone from angry red to a color I didn't have a name for and didn't want to learn. Priya sat beside her because Priya had appointed herself to that post without anyone asking, and no one was going to ask her to leave it.

"Kwai Landing," Jonah said finally.

I looked up from the chart table where I'd been pretending to study Nadia's markers, though by now I knew them by heart, could draw them blind, could probably draw them in my sleep, which is where they lived most nights anyway. "What?"

"The village." She nodded at the lanterns. "It's called Kwai Landing. Or it was." A pause, and then, quieter, almost to herself: "I haven't been back in nine years."

Tomas came up the companionway ladder, dripping rain off the tarp he'd rigged over the aft deck, and heard the last part. He looked at the village, then at Jonah, then at me, and raised one eyebrow in the way he did when he was deciding whether a question was worth the energy. Tomas rationed questions like a man rationing ammunition.

"You know these people?" he asked.

"I traded here." Jonah's hands moved on the wheel, small corrections, the boat breathing under her. "Medicines downstream, dried fish upstream. Rubber tapping knives. Salt." She said the list like an inventory, flat and quick, the way she said everything that touched her past — as if speed could keep it from catching. "The headman's name was Odumbe. There was a woman who mended nets who used to feed me breakfast every morning I was there whether I wanted it or not. Her name was—" She stopped. Her jaw worked once. "I don't remember her name."

That was a lie, and I knew it was a lie, because I had learned by then to read the specific texture of Jonah Vasquez's lies: they came fast and smooth, while her truths came slow and cost her something visible. This one had cost her. She remembered the name. She had simply buried it so deep alongside everything else she'd buried that digging it up felt like opening a grave.

"She'll remember yours," I said.

Jonah shot me a look that would have opened a hull. But she didn't deny it, and that was its own kind of confession.

* * *

They saw us before we reached the palisade. Of course they did — a wounded riverboat limping out of the flood channels at dusk, listing two degrees to port, trailing the smell of burnt paint and gun smoke, is not a subtle arrival. Lanterns swung along the wall. A drum started somewhere, low and arrhythmic, not a warning rhythm exactly but not a welcome either; something between, a question beaten out in wood.

Jonah brought us in slow, using the current instead of fighting it, and we slid along the outside of the palisade until a voice called out from the gate — an old voice, cracked but carrying, in the trade tongue first and then, when nobody answered, in three other languages I didn't know.

Jonah answered in the fourth.

I don't speak it. I never learned what language it was, though later I'd hear more of it than I expected and come to love the sound of it, all consonants and river-smooth vowels. What I understood was the effect. The drum stopped. The voice on the wall went silent for three full seconds. And then it said a word — one word, repeated twice, rising the second time like a question that already knew its answer —

— and people started coming down to the water.

They came with lanterns and they came with spears and some came with both, which told you everything about the season. They waded out onto the mud flats below the gate, dozens of them, and the light caught faces old and young, and in the middle of them all, leaning on a boy's shoulder, was a small round woman with white hair cropped close to her skull and a net-mending needle still stuck through the knot of her hair like a flower.

Jonah killed the engine. In the sudden silence I heard her exhale, long and controlled, the way she exhaled before running rapids.

"That's her," she said. "The net-mender."

"You remembered," I said.

"I remembered everything," Jonah said, and there was something raw underneath it, something that hadn't been sanded smooth yet. "That's the problem."

* * *

They didn't let us dock at the main landing. They made us come in through the fish gate on the north side, which I understood later was a courtesy — the main landing was where strangers arrived, and we were not being received as strangers. The old woman whose name turned out to be Mama Yewa — I got it finally from Priya, who had been listening harder than any of us — stood in the shallows in the rain and took Jonah's face in both her hands the moment Jonah stepped off the gangplank, and looked at her for a long time, and said something soft in that fourth language.

Jonah answered. Her voice broke on the second word.

I looked away. It seemed like the kind of thing a person should be allowed to do without witnesses, and also I found that my own eyes were doing something inconvenient, and Elias Wren does not cry in front of river pilots, dead sisters' ghosts, or anyone.

What I saw instead, looking down the mud street, was the village taking stock of us the way villages do — quickly, completely, without pretense. They saw Tomas's burned hands and Priya's silence and the way she flinched from the torchlight. They saw Sefu carried ashore on a stretcher of lashed poles, delirious, calling out coordinates to no one. They saw the bullet graze along Jonah's ribs when her shirt shifted, and they saw the Marisol sitting low in the water behind us, wounded, leaking, flying no flag.

And they did not ask a single question about any of it. Not that night.

Mama Yewa fed us first. That was clearly the law of the place: wounds after food, questions after wounds. We ate smoked fish and cassava paste and a bitter greens soup that tasted like the forest floor smells, sitting on a floor of packed earth inside the headman's house while rain hammered the thatch overhead like applause. The headman was not Odumbe anymore — Odumbe had died four years back, they told us, in his sleep, a good death, the best kind — but his daughter, a broad-shouldered woman named Adaeze with her father's patience and none of his slowness, ran things now, and she ran them well. She listened to our story with her hands folded, all of it, the expedition and the ruins and the archive and the gunboats and the battle in the split channel, and she interrupted only twice: once to send a runner out into the rain, and once to ask how many mercenaries remained alive.

"Two boats wrecked," I said. "Maybe eight men unaccounted for. And Ravel."

At the name Ravel, Adaeze's face did something small and cold. "The one who sold his own people."

"He'd argue it differently," I said.

"They always do." She unfolded her hands. "You will stay. All of you. The boat can be beached and patched — my uncle's sons know hull work, they patch the trading barges every season. Your doctor needs things we have and things we don't. Tomorrow the runners go upstream to the mission at Falaba for antibiotics." She said it the way she presumably said everything, as fact rather than offer, and I watched Jonah open her mouth to object — I knew that mouth, I knew the exact shape of the objection forming, *we can't impose, we need to keep moving, staying is how people get hurt* — and I watched Mama Yewa, without even turning her head, put one hand flat on Jonah's knee.

Jonah closed her mouth.

I will think about that hand on the knee for the rest of my life, I think. How such a small weight could stop a woman who had outrun gunboats.

* * *

They gave us a house. An actual house, empty, on the east row where the stilt foundations ran deepest — a widow's house, Adaeze said, standing empty since the last flood year, and we could use it as long as we needed. Priya and I carried Sefu up the ladder-ramp together, Tomas walking ahead with the lantern, and laid her on the sleeping platform under a mosquito net that smelled of smoke and camphor. Her fever-talk had settled into murmurs. Once, surfacing, she gripped my wrist with shocking strength and said, "Did the index make it," and I said yes, ma'am, it made it, and she said, "Then burn the rest if you have to, Wren, promise me," and I said, "Sleep, Doctor," because I wasn't ready to have that conversation with a dying woman or anyone else.

Priya stayed. She arranged herself cross-legged at the foot of the platform with her arms around her knees, and when I hesitated at the door she said, "Go," in a voice that had recovered some of its architecture since the night she'd finally spoken to Jonah, and so I went.

Outside, the rain had thinned to a drifting mist. The village glowed. Lanterns everywhere, warm orange, doubled in the floodwater standing between the house rows so that the whole settlement seemed to float on light. Somewhere a guitar was being played badly and cheerfully. Children — actual children, barefoot, shrieking — chased each other through the shallows past ten o'clock at night, and no one told them to stop, because the day had been frightening enough and joy was being issued as medicine.

I found Jonah at the edge of the east row, sitting on the lowest step of a stilt ladder with her boots off, her feet in the floodwater, looking out at nothing. Or at something. With Jonah it was hard to tell the difference.

"You should be sleeping," I said.

"So should you."

"I slept on the run downriver. You didn't. You steered thirty-one hours."

"Somebody had to." She scooted sideways on the step, which from Jonah was an invitation written in gold leaf. I sat. The water was blood-warm around my ankles. Mist beaded on my forearms.

For a while neither of us spoke. The bad guitar worked through something that might have been a song. A dog trotted past with a whole fish in its mouth, proud as a general.

"When I left here," Jonah said, "it was raining like this."

I kept still. I had learned that Jonah's confessions were like catfish — they came to you if you held bait steady and made no sudden moves.

"There was a man," she said. "Not — everyone assumes a lover, when I say it that way. He wasn't. He was older. He ran the trading post here, and he taught me charts. Real charting, survey-grade, before I ever met a cartographer like your sister. He was the first person who told me I had a gift for water." Her jaw tightened. "And he was the one who sold the river. Channel depths, government patrol schedules, which villages were hiding what. Sold it to the dredging consortium, back when they were just getting their teeth in. Three villages got burned out because of his ledgers. Not this one — this one paid him off, or maybe he liked it here too much, I never found out. But I found the ledgers. I was living aboard the Marisol at the trading post dock, and I found the ledgers, and I confronted him, and—" She stopped. Restarted. "There was a fire. The trading post burned. I don't talk about what happened between finding the ledgers and the fire, and I'm not going to start tonight. But I left that morning, and I've been leaving mornings ever since. Nine years. Every place I've dropped anchor, I've been counting the hours until I could cut loose. It's not a life, Elias. It's a holding pattern."

"And this place?"

She was quiet long enough that the mist had time to soak through my shirt.

"This place remembers me," she said finally, "and it doesn't want anything." Her voice came out strange, half wonder and half grief, as if the two had gotten tangled in her chest and she couldn't sort which was which. "Every other place that ever knew my name wanted something out of me. Cargo, silence, distance, a favor, a debt. These people fed me breakfast for a week when I was twenty-three years old and I never gave them a thing except fair prices and help hauling nets, and they kept the memory for nine years like it was worth keeping. Do you understand what that does to a person? To be remembered kindly? To be remembered at all?"

I thought of Nadia. I thought of how I had spent four years remembering her in fragments — her handwriting on survey sheets, her laugh in a port bar, her last radio check-in, eleven days late and forever — and how each fragment had been a hook, dragging me upriver toward answers. Being remembered as a wound. And here was Jonah, remembered as a kindness, and it undid her worse than any wound ever had.

"It terrifies you," I said.

"It terrifies me," she agreed. "Because you can run from anything that wants something from you. You can't run from—" She gestured at the village, the lanterns, the bad guitar, the children asleep now, the whole warm floating constellation of it. "This doesn't chase you. It just waits. It says, *the door's open whenever.* And some rotten part of me wants to walk through it and nail the door shut behind me, and another part of me is already mapping the exits."

"Both parts are you," I said. "You don't have to evict either one."

Jonah looked at me sidelong. In the lantern light her eyes were the exact color of deep water over sand, and for once there was no calculation in them, no pilot reading my draft and my cargo. Just tiredness, and something underneath the tiredness that might, in better light, in a different life, have been hope.

"When did you get wise?" she asked.

"About a week after I stopped lying to myself," I said. "So, recently. Don't get used to it."

That earned me the ghost of a laugh, and then, unexpectedly, the real thing — short and rough, startled out of her, the first true laugh I'd heard from Jonah Vasquez since I'd known her. She pressed the heel of her hand against the graze on her ribs like it hurt to laugh, and maybe it did, and laughed anyway.

* * *

Morning came up gold and loud. The village had decided overnight, in whatever parliament villages convene while their guests sleep, that we were theirs now, and the evidence was everywhere. Someone had mended the tear in the Marisol's awning. Someone else had left a basket of fruit on the widow-house steps. Two teenage boys were already down at the fish gate arguing over the best way to beach a boat our size, and when I came down they recruited me without ceremony, and by noon half the village was hauling on lines and rollers and singing some work song with a beat that made the labor feel like dancing, and the Marisol came up the ramp and settled into her cradle of logs and palm trunks like a tired animal lying down.

Jonah supervised the hull inspection with her arms crossed, wincing, letting Uncle's sons find things wrong with her boat that she plainly already knew about, and I watched her face as they pronounced the stern seam beyond patching — watched her absorb it, nod, and look out at the river for a long moment with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to.

Sefu woke clear-headed in the afternoon, drank the antibiotic brew Mama Yewa's sister prepared, and immediately began issuing instructions, which Adaeze absorbed with folded hands and then overruled, gently, completely, on medical grounds. Sefu glared. Adaeze smiled. It was like watching two generals discover a mutual respect neither would admit to.

And in the evening, at the headman's house, Adaeze called us together — me, Jonah, Tomas, Priya, Sefu propped upright on cushions and too weak to argue her way out of attending — and set a clay lamp between us, and said:

"You brought war to our water, and you also brought the reason for it. My runners say the company men are regrouping at the confluence. They are angry, and angry men with guns count badly. So before you rest another night under our roofs, you will tell us what is in those crates you saved, and why it is worth burning villages for — and then we will decide, all of us together, what Kwai Landing does next."

She looked around the circle, and her eyes rested last on Jonah.

"Everyone speaks," she said. "Even the captain who keeps leaving. Especially her."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft from the door, steadied, and burned.

Jonah looked at the floor. Then at the roof beams. Then, at last, at the faces waiting — and I saw her take the longest breath of her life, and begin.
