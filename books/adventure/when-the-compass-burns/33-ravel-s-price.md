## 33. Ravel's Price

The fever had broken in the night, and Ravel woke to the smell of woodsmoke and river mud, lying on a woven mat in a stranger's house while an old woman pressed a cool cloth to his forehead with hands that had no business being so gentle. He had come ashore half-drowned, coughing up brown water, delirious, mumbling about crates and channels and the price of everything. They could have let him die on the bank. Instead they had carried him here, to the house of the woman who smelled of fish and smoke, and fed him broth he hadn't earned.

He should have felt gratitude. What he felt instead was the cold arithmetic of opportunity, turning over like river stones under his boot.

* * *

Three days earlier, Ravel had been a man with a future.

He had been second-in-command of the Sefu expedition for two years, and in that time he had learned what Dr. Imara Sefu would never admit: that knowledge hoarded is knowledge wasted, that an archive locked in a vault beneath a drowned city serves no one but itself. He had watched her sit on the greatest cartographic discovery of the century — every hidden tributary, every buried land claim, every secret the basin's rivers kept — and do nothing with it except guard it. As if guarding were the same as serving.

When the company men had first approached him — through a fixer in Callao Verde, through intermediaries so layered they might as well have been ghosts — Ravel had told himself he was being pragmatic. The company had gunboats. The company had money. The company could extract the archive, copy it, and put it to work: roads, leases, surveys, development. Sefu wanted to be a saint. Ravel wanted to be useful. There was a difference, and history always favored the useful.

Then the expedition went sideways. Porters executed at the terrace camp. Priya fled into the flood. Sefu barricaded herself above the vault entrance with a rifle and a dying woman's stubbornness. And Ravel, who had sold their position for a promise of protection, discovered that the company's idea of protection was a crate of index cards and a man left standing in the smoke.

So he had run. Not toward the company — toward the river, toward the one place he knew Jonah Vasquez would eventually go, because he had watched her for two years and understood something essential about her: she was a woman who ran from places, which meant she could be predicted by the places she'd already been. The village downstream had traded with her once. The elders remembered her kindness. If Jonah Vasquez was going to stop anywhere in this drowned world, it would be there.

He had found them. Or rather, the flood had delivered him — spat out of a side channel, half-dead, into the arms of people too decent to let a drowning man drown.

Now he lay on the mat and listened to the rain hammering the thatch and did the only thing he knew how to do: he counted.

Two gunboats anchored three kilometers downstream, past the split channel, holding position since dawn. Forty-one mercenaries between them, give or take the ones lost in the running fight. One crate recovered from the Marisol's wreckage — burned, waterlogged, but the index intact enough to matter. And somewhere in this village, or sunk in the channel where no dredge could reach it, the rest of the archive.

Forty-one guns against a village of fishermen and farmers and one ex-captain with graze healing along her ribs.

Ravel smiled at the thatch. It wasn't even a fight. It was a negotiation waiting for someone brave enough to open it.

* * *

He was walking again by the second day, moving slowly through the village with the careful gait of a man cataloguing exits. The villagers watched him the way you watch a snake cross your path — not with hatred, exactly, but with the particular stillness of people who know what snakes do.

He found Jonah at the barge, mending net with Priya's younger cousin, teaching the girl the knots without seeming to teach. She looked up when his shadow fell across the planks, and her face did something complicated — not surprise, because she had known he'd survived; Elias had told her, grimly, the night they'd pulled him from the water. Something closer to recognition. The look you give a debt you'd hoped was forgiven.

"Captain Vasquez." He spread his hands, palms out, the gesture of a man with nothing concealed. "I owe you my life. Twice now, if we count the terrace."

"You owe me nothing." Her voice was flat as the flooded plain. "You owe two porters their lives, and you can't pay that."

"No," Ravel agreed quietly. "That debt I can't pay. But I can pay others." He glanced at the girl, who had gone still over her knots, then back at Jonah. "Walk with me. Please. What I have to say concerns everyone in this village, and I'd rather say it to you first."

She didn't move for a long moment. Then she set down the net, said something soft to the girl in the river dialect, and walked with him to the end of the dock, where the water lapped against the pilings and no small ears could reach.

"The company knows where the archive sank," Ravel said. "Not precisely — the channel closed after the peak, the silt has shifted, Nadia Wren's markers are gone or moved. But they know the stretch of river. Five kilometers, maybe six. And they know the village sheltered us." He let that settle between them like silt. "There are two gunboats below the split channel right now. I've seen them from the ridge. They're waiting for orders, and the orders depend on what happens next."

"What happens next," Jonah repeated.

"I can make those orders go away." Ravel turned to face her fully, and here it was, the seduction laid bare, the argument he had polished all the way downriver. "I still have standing with them. My credentials, my reports, my name on the extraction contract. If the archive surfaces — if it's handed over, cleanly, publicly, with the village's cooperation documented — then this village becomes a footnote. A cooperative community. There might even be compensation. Contracts for transport, employment for the young men, a clinic—"

"A clinic." Jonah's laugh had no humor in it at all. "You're offering blood money with a stethoscope."

"I'm offering the only version of this story where nobody burns." Ravel's voice stayed level, reasonable, almost kind. "Captain, I've seen what these men do when they're denied. I stood in a village upstream that said no. You saw it too — the burned one, on the way up. That was a *yes* that came too late. I'm trying to get your yes in before the matches come out."

"And if we say yes? Hand over four hundred years of records that map every claim, every theft, every hidden tributary in this basin — hand them to the same company that burned that village and shot those porters?"

"Then the records belong to someone with the power to protect them. Which is more than they have now, rotting in the mud."

"They don't rot in the mud," Jonah said. "They wait. There's a difference."

Ravel studied her — the graze along her ribs, the hands that had spent weeks learning to be still and were now gripping the dock rail hard enough to whiten the knuckles. He had thought he understood her. He had thought she was a runner, and runners always took the exit when it was offered.

But she wasn't running anymore. God help them all, she wasn't running.

"You should talk to your elders," he said finally. "All of them. This isn't your choice alone, Captain. When the company comes — and they will come, with or without me — everyone will look at whoever's standing at the front. Right now, that's you." He paused at the head of the dock, and for one unguarded moment something almost human crossed his face — exhaustion, maybe, or the ghost of the man he'd been before the arithmetic ate him. "I'm sorry it's you. Truly. But the river doesn't ask permission either."

* * *

The elders gathered at dusk in the longhouse, and Ravel was made to stand outside while they talked, which he understood completely. You didn't invite the snake into the council of mice.

He heard fragments anyway, carried on the wet wind: *the burned village*, *the porters*, *the gunboats*. And once, sharply, an old man's voice — the one called Mateo, who had traded with Jonah years ago and never forgotten it — saying something that sounded like *she stayed, didn't she?*

Inside, Jonah stood before the fire with the whole village looking at her, and told them the truth. All of it. The archive, the company, the gunboats, the offer. She did not soften it and she did not dramatize it. She spoke the way she piloted — reading the current, naming the rocks.

"They will burn this village if we refuse," she said. "Some of us will die. Maybe many. I won't lie to you about that, and I won't pretend I can promise otherwise. The other road — handing over the archive — buys safety, maybe. For a while. Until the company needs something else this river has, and comes back for it the same way." She looked around the circle of faces, old and young, the fishermen and the grandmothers and the children asleep against their mothers' shoulders. "I've run from every hard place I ever landed. I'm not asking you to trust me because I'm wise. I'm asking you to trust me because I finally stopped."

Old Mateo rose. He was ninety if he was a day, and he walked to the fire with the deliberation of a man who had buried a wife and two sons and outlived every argument he'd ever lost.

"When the captain came here years ago," he said, "her boat leaked and her money was short, and she fixed our ferry rope in the dark before she left, and never said a word. We found out after." He turned to the room. "A person shows you who they are when nothing is owed. She has shown us. Now the question isn't whether we trust her. The question is whether we are the kind of village that sells its river."

The vote, when it came, was not unanimous. It never is. Two families with young children voted for the bargain, and no one shamed them for it, and Jonah met their eyes and nodded slowly, carrying that weight too. But the village held. The answer was no.

And outside in the dark, Ravel heard it, and turned, and walked down through the rain to the riverbank where the company's signal lamp would find him.

* * *

He signaled at midnight. Three long flashes, two short. The arrangement he'd made with the boat commander before he ever came ashore — the contingency, the fallback, the honest man's dishonest insurance. *If they refuse, I'll signal. Then you come.*

By morning the gunboats were in sight.

They came up the main channel in the grey light, engines low, riding the flood current with the arrogant ease of things that had never been told no by a river. Villagers lined the banks in silence — not fleeing, not hiding, just standing, the whole population of the place arranged along the waterline like a living seawall. Jonah stood at the center of the dock with Elias on one side and old Mateo on the other, and behind her, stacked openly on the barge, forty empty crates painted to look full.

Ravel stood at the gunboat rail beside the commander, a mercenary named Ostrer with a face like a closed ledger, and watched Jonah Vasquez watch him, and felt the strange double vision of a man seeing both futures at once.

"Last chance," Ostrer said. "Tell them to stack the real crates on the dock by noon, and we motor away. Nobody burns today."

"They've already answered," Ravel said.

"Villages answer twice. Once with words, once with ash."

The gunboats slowed at the mouth of the split channel — the wide fork where the flood had carved the river in two, the left branch deep and navigable, the right branch choked with submerged pilings from some drowned structure, invisible under the brown water, marked on no chart but Nadia Wren's. Ravel knew the channel. He had helped survey it, two years ago, back when he was still a man who believed knowledge should serve whoever paid for the survey.

He leaned toward Ostrer and pointed left. "Deep water. Take the left branch. The right is shoaled — you'll ground for certain."

Ostrer looked at him for a long moment. Then at the village. Then at the left branch, wide and brown and deceptively calm.

And Ravel felt it again, that cold arithmetic, except that this time the numbers wouldn't resolve. Because he had pointed left, and left was safe, and safe meant the gunboats lived to make their offer with fire instead of words. But he had also, in pointing, done the last honest thing available to a dishonest man: he had told the truth about the river, and the truth about the river was that it changed.

The flood had shifted the silt three nights ago. The left branch — surveyed in dry season, mapped in low water — was no longer the deep one.

Jonah knew. He could see it in the set of her shoulders on the dock, in the way she stood with her weight forward like a pilot reading water she had already decided to trust. Nadia's charts, copied in Elias's careful hand, had been updated by every fisherman in the village who had watched the flood remake the channel overnight. The company's maps were two years old. The river's maps were three days old.

Ravel opened his mouth — to warn them, to correct himself, to shout *the channel has moved* — and found that he had already chosen, weeks ago, on a night in the smoke at the terrace camp, and that every choice since had only been the long echo of it.

The lead gunboat swung into the left branch.

For ten seconds, the only sound was the engine and the rain.

Then the bow lifted, hard, as if the river had slapped it — a submerged piling taking the hull amidships with a crack that rolled across the water like the end of the world arriving early. The gunboat lurched, slewed, began shipping water through a wound you could hear. Behind it, the second boat reversed hard, engines screaming, and grounded anyway on a bar that hadn't existed yesterday.

On the dock, Jonah Vasquez raised her arm — and the village moved.

Dugouts poured from the reeds on both banks. Fishermen with grappling lines. Young men with the pilings' true positions memorized in their bodies, shouting directions to each other across the water. Elias wading chest-deep with a boat hook, dragging a stunned mercenary out of the flooding wreck because that was the kind of man he was even now. And at the center of it, calm as ballast, the captain who had stopped running, calling the currents by name.

Ravel stood at the rail of the crippled flagship and watched his future sink in three meters of brown water, and understood, with the clarity of a man watching the last door close, that he had never once been on the side of the river.

Ostrer grabbed his collar, spit-sprayed, screaming over the chaos: "You said LEFT—"

"I know," Ravel said.

And downstream, through the rain, the sound of the second gunboat's engine died to a gurgle, and the villagers closed in from both banks like the river itself deciding whose side it was on — and Ravel, dripping, disarmed, hauled out by the same gentle-handed old woman who had once wiped his fevered brow, looked up at Jonah Vasquez standing over him on the dock.

She looked tired. She looked sad. She did not look surprised.

"You knew the channel had shifted," he said. It wasn't a question.

"Nadia charted it. The river finished the job." Jonah crouched, forearms on her knees, close enough that he could see the graze along her ribs, the salt lines of sleepless weeks. "You gave us three days, Ravel. Three days to move the crates, warn the villages, rig the channel. Why?"

And Ravel, who had built his whole life on the principle that every man has a price and every price can be found, opened his mouth to lie — and heard himself say, in a voice cracked raw by river water and something older than regret:

"Because I watched them shoot the porters, and I told myself it was necessary. And I have been telling myself that ever since, and the sentence keeps getting heavier, and I wanted—" His voice failed. Started again. "I wanted to put it down. Just once. Before the end."

Jonah studied him for a long moment. Then she stood, and nodded to the fishermen holding his arms, and said the sentence that would follow him into whatever came next:

"Tie him to the barge post. He rows with the work crews until the archive's safe. After that—" she looked at the ruined gunboats, the drifting smoke, the village standing whole on its banks, "—after that, the village decides. Not me. Not him. Them."

* * *

But the day was not done with anyone yet.

Because as the villagers swarmed the grounded second gunboat, as Elias cut loose its fuel line and old Mateo's grandsons stripped it of weapons with the efficiency of people who had waited their whole lives for this exact morning, one of the mercenaries broke from the treeline on the far bank — a runner, young, fast, carrying a radio — and slipped into the mangroves heading downstream.

Toward the third gunboat. The one that had waited below the split channel, the reserve, the one Ravel had not mentioned because he had genuinely believed it had withdrawn.

It had not withdrawn.

Elias spotted the runner at the same moment Jonah did. Their eyes met across the chaos of the captured deck, and Jonah was already moving, already shouting for a dugout, already reading the current the way she read everything — as a question with exactly one right answer, and no time left to find it.
