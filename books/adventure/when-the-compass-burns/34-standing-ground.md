## 34. Standing Ground

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and in the silence that followed, Jonah could hear the river thinking.

It was a sound she'd known all her life — the low, muscular mutter of water deciding where it wanted to go — but here, in the flooded basin below the village of San Telmo de las Aguas, it had a different quality. A hunger. The channels that Nadia Wren had charted thirty years ago and that the flood had resurrected overnight were no longer lazy backwaters. They were throats. And somewhere downstream, two gunboats were coming up those throats with Ravel standing on the deck of one of them, pointing the way.

Jonah stood on the village's rickety observation platform — a fish-drying rack the elders had lent her without being asked, which was its own kind of answer — and watched the light come up gray and bruised over the water. Behind her, the village smelled of woodsmoke and wet thatch and frying plantains, because Elder Mabika had insisted that everyone eat a proper breakfast before the dying started. She had argued with him for exactly four seconds before understanding that this, too, was a form of standing ground.

*They will come for the archive,* Ravel had said through the megaphone, his voice carrying across the water with the smooth confidence of a man who had never once doubted he'd be paid. *Give it to us and the village is spared. You have until midday.*

Midday. As if the flood kept appointments. As if mercy did.

"You're going to say no." Elias came up beside her, moving carefully on the wet bamboo. He had Nadia's logbook under his arm like a breviary. He always carried it now; Jonah had stopped pretending not to notice. "You're going to stand on this platform and tell two hundred people to bet their houses on your piloting."

"I was going to ask them first."

"Were you?" He looked at her sideways, and there was something in his face that wasn't quite challenge. It was closer to hope, handled carefully, the way you'd handle a bird with a broken wing. "Because I've been watching you since Callao Verde, Captain, and I've never once seen you ask permission for anything."

Jonah didn't answer right away. Below them, the village was waking into purpose. Mabika's grandsons were hauling dugouts above the flood line. Old Ferreira, who mended nets and had once, years ago, traded Jonah a coil of rope for a story, was already lashing planks across two canoes to make a work platform. Children who should have been frightened were being put to work instead — carrying machetes, counting crates, doing the small essential labor that made a place feel defended rather than doomed. Nobody had organized them. They had simply seen her walk out of the elders' meeting at first light with a chart in her hand, and they had understood.

That was the thing about being asked to stay, Jonah was learning. It came with obligations you couldn't outrun, because you weren't running anymore.

"Ravel thinks I'm still the woman he met at the relay station," she said finally. "The one who takes the contract and leaves before the shooting starts. He's offering me the same deal I've taken my whole life — keep moving, keep breathing, let other people burn." She turned the chart over in her hands. Nadia's ink lines had gone soft with damp, but the channels were still legible: the Split Channel, the drowned cacao terraces, the sunken pilings of the old ferry landing where the current ran like a whip. "He's wrong about me. Maybe he's been wrong longer than anyone knows."

"And if you're wrong about yourself?"

"Then we all drown together," Jonah said, "and at least we'll drown facing upstream."

Elias laughed — a short, startled sound, as if it had escaped custody. Then he unrolled the logbook to the page he'd marked and held it out to her, and the morning turned to work.

* * *

The council convened on the floor of Mabika's house, because the village had no table big enough for an argument this size. Jonah spread Nadia's charts on the woven mats and weighted the corners with machete handles, and she told them the truth, all of it, because she had learned in the last three days that these people could smell a half-truth the way catfish smelled blood.

"The company wants what's in those crates," she said. "Maps. Records. Every hidden tributary and buried claim in this basin, written down by people who lived here before anyone drew a border. With that paper, they own the water. Without it, they only rent it. Ravel has offered to spare San Telmo if we hand it over." She let that sit. "I won't lie to you. If we refuse, they may burn this village whether we fight or not. I've seen what they did to the last village that stood between them and paper."

An old woman near the wall — Jonah thought her name was Doña Rosa, though she'd only ever known her as the woman who sold smoked fish at the landing — spoke without raising her hand. "The soldiers burned Kawayan in March," she said. "My sister's son ran. He ran all the way to the coast, and they found him there anyway, because a man who runs can be followed." She looked around the room. "A village that stands has to be *found*. There is a difference."

"There is a difference," Mabika agreed. He was very old and very thin and he sat with the straight-backed patience of a man who had buried three floods' worth of neighbors. "Captain Vasquez. Years ago you brought us medicine during the cholera season and would not stay for thanks. We remember this. Now you bring us a war instead of medicine, and you offer to stay for it." His eyes were dry and bright. "It is not the gift we would have chosen. But it is yours to lead, if we choose to follow. So tell us — how does a village fight two gunboats?"

Jonah looked down at the charts, and the answer rose in her the way the flood had risen — not gently, but completely.

"It doesn't," she said. "The river does. We just have to be the ones holding the map when it decides."

She turned the chart so they could all see it, and began, for the first time in her life, to explain herself.

* * *

The Split Channel was Nadia's masterpiece, though she'd never lived to know it. Where the main channel bent east around the drowned cacao terraces, the flood had reopened an old distributary that branched and braided through what had once been plantation land — three threads of current separated by fingers of submerged ground, invisible from the surface except as subtle changes in the water's color and texture. Nadia had charted them all, depth by depth, season by season, in handwriting so precise it looked printed. The northern thread was deep enough for a loaded barge. The middle thread would float a shallow dugout and nothing else. The southern thread ran over the ruins of the old ferry landing, where iron pilings stood just beneath the surface like the ribs of some enormous drowned animal.

Any pilot coming upriver in flood conditions would take the northern thread, because it looked widest and calmest, and because the middle and southern threads showed no water movement at all — slack water over shallows, reading as mudflat or bank.

Unless you knew. Unless someone had taught you, the way Nadia's charts had taught Jonah over three sleepless nights, and the way Jonah was now teaching eleven villagers in borrowed dugouts.

"Paddle out to the third marker and stop," she called, standing balanced in the bow of the lead canoe with a pole in her hands. "Don't touch the water past it. Feel that?" The young man — Mateo, Mabika's eldest grandson, sixteen and fearless in the way that made elders pray — held his paddle blade flat against the surface and his eyes went wide. Beneath the canoe, faint but unmistakable, the current tugged. The northern thread, pulling even here, even behind what looked like solid ground. "That's the seam. Water moves where the bottom drops. Where it doesn't move, the bottom's close enough to open a hull. From the gunboat, this whole finger looks like one bank. From underneath, it's three rivers wearing one disguise."

Mateo grinned. "Like us."

Jonah blinked at him. "What?"

"We look like one thing," he said, paddling back, easy and strong. "We are three things. Village, river, and you."

She had no answer for that, so she gave him the next marker instead, and spent the rest of the morning teaching children to read seams.

By noon the plan had flesh. The archive crates — what remained of them after the fire, after Ravel's theft, after Sefu's death — went up the hill to the church foundation, wrapped in oilcloth, guarded by women with machetes and one very old shotgun that Ferreira swore had been fired twice in forty years and missed both times. The village itself would appear abandoned: fires cold, boats hidden in the mangroves, livestock moved inland, shutters closed. Let Ravel see an empty village and think his bargain had been refused by flight. Let him come up the northern thread confident, hunting, his guns pointed at shorelines that held nothing.

And let the Marisol be waiting for him in the wide water below the split, riding at anchor with her lamps lit, looking for all the world like a lone boat whose captain had finally decided to run — cargo aboard, engine warm, a prize too good to pass up.

"They'll want to take her intact," Elias said, when she laid that part out. He was rigging the lamp brackets himself, working with the careful hands of a man who had learned boatwork in a hurry and was proud of every scar it had given him. "A captured pilot, a seized hold, proof that the resistance was one woman and one rotten boat — that's worth more to Ravel than sinking us. He'll come alongside. He'll board."

"He'll board," Jonah agreed. "And while he's boarding, the flood does the rest. The northern thread bends hard to starboard below the second terrace — Nadia marked a crosscurrent there that runs against the main flow at this stage of the rise. Any boat that comes through fast, with its weight forward and its helm answering to a pilot who doesn't know the seam—" She shrugged. "The river takes the wheel."

"And if he doesn't take the bait? If he shells the village from the main channel and never comes up the split?"

Jonah looked at the Marisol where she lay against the bank, patched and scarred, her paint gone the color of old driftwood, her engine ticking as it cooled. Twenty-two years. Two continents of river. Every town she'd fled, every name she'd shed, all of it carried in that leaking hull like ballast.

"Then she burns for nothing," Jonah said. "But Ravel didn't chase me three hundred miles of floodwater to shell an empty village from a safe distance. Men like him need to *see*. He'll come. He needs my face to make his surrender real."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and the village worked with them, and Jonah discovered that being believed in was a heavier cargo than anything she'd ever hauled.

Ferreira and his sons sank brush barriers in the middle thread — not to block it, but to complicate it, to fill a fleeing gunboat's options with snags. The women of the fish-drying racks knew the southern thread's pilings better than any chart, having snagged nets on them for a generation, and they walked Jonah along the bank marking each one with cut cane stakes visible only from the water side. Priya, who had not spoken for eleven days and then had spoken only to Jonah, worked beside her in silence for an hour before saying, quietly, "The porters used to joke that the river would protect the archive. That it swallowed the mission church to hide it." Her voice was still rough, like a tool left out in the rain. "Maybe they were right."

"Rivers don't protect anything," Jonah said. "They just don't take sides. That's better than most armies."

Tomas, the geologist, turned out to have a surveyor's eye and a head full of numbers, and together he and Elias recalculated the crosscurrent timing against the river's rise — a meter since yesterday, another half-meter expected by nightfall. "The seam strengthens as the water climbs," Tomas said, tapping the chart. "By midnight, the counter-current below the second terrace will run at nearly three knots against the main flow. Anything coming up that bend will be fighting its own wake."

"Then we want him there at midnight," Jonah said.

"No," Elias said slowly, and then, with the first real smile she'd ever seen on him, "we want him there whenever he arrives. We just have to make sure he arrives angry enough to hurry." He nodded toward the Marisol. "Lamps lit. Engine idling loud. And maybe—" he hesitated, then committed— "maybe a fire on her stern deck. Something that looks like panic. Something that says *she's loading to run right now, catch her before she slips away.*"

Jonah stared at him. "You want to set my boat on fire."

"A little," Elias admitted. "Controlled. Contained. Dramatic."

"She's the only home I've had in twenty years."

"I know." He said it gently, without pity, the way he'd learned to say everything since the confrontation at anchor, since the logbook, since the long nights bailing side by side. "That's why it has to be you who lights it. Not me. Not the river. You — choosing what she's for."

Jonah turned away and looked at the water for a long time. The flood had climbed the bank another hand's width since morning. Somewhere downstream, past the drowned terraces, past the wrecked gunboat from the battle they'd already survived once, Ravel was making his own preparations, certain that greed was universal and fear was total and that everyone, given enough pressure, chose to run.

He wasn't entirely wrong. She had run from a burning town with a man's blood on her hands and a name she'd buried in the ashes. She had run from Callao Verde, from Puerto Almendras, from a dozen landings where staying one more day would have meant answering questions she couldn't survive. Running had kept her alive. It had also kept her alone, and alone was a country she knew every road of, and she was done living there.

"All right," she said. "Fire on the stern. But I pick the spot, and I pick the hour, and if the wind turns, we drown it with buckets if we have to." She looked back at Elias, and something passed between them that neither of them named — the particular trust of two people who had lied to each other thoroughly enough to earn the truth. "Get the lamp oil. And Elias — thank you for not asking me if I'm sure."

"Would it help?"

"Not even slightly."

"Then I'll save us both the breath." He shouldered the oil can and headed up the bank, and Jonah stood alone on the shrinking shore with her boat and her chart and the biggest decision of her life already made, waiting only for the dark to justify it.

* * *

Dusk came early under the storm clouds stacking up over the western ridgelines, and by full dark the village of San Telmo de las Aguas had become a ghost of itself.

From the water, it looked abandoned: shuttered houses, dead fires, dugouts dragged into the mangrove shadow, not a lantern showing. Only the Marisol told a different story — anchored in the wide pool below the Split Channel, lamps burning yellow at mast and stern, a small orange fire crackling on her aft deck where Jonah had built it from split bamboo and lamp oil in a steel tray, contained, controlled, feeding on itself. Smoke rolled off her in the windless air like a signal flag. *Come and get me. I'm leaving. Last chance.*

Jonah stood at her own wheel in the wheelhouse shadow, watching the fire she'd lit reflect off the black water, and felt strangely calm. Below decks, the hold was empty except for sandbags arranged to trim her bow-heavy — heavy enough to sit deep, to handle sluggishly, to look like a boat straining under stolen cargo. The engine idled at half-throttle, loud in the night, a heartbeat broadcast to anyone listening.

On the banks, invisible in the mangroves and the flooded terrace vegetation, forty-one villagers waited with machetes, ropes, the old shotgun, and eleven dugouts positioned to seal the channel mouths once the trap closed. Mateo held the northern seam markers. Doña Rosa held the southern pilings. Mabika, who had refused every plea to stay on the hill, sat in the lead canoe with a lantern hooded under his coat, because he said a village's elder should witness what the village decided.

Elias crouched at the rail beside her, a signal lamp dark in his hands, watching the downstream dark. "You've gone quiet," he murmured.

"I'm listening to the river."

"What's it saying?"

Jonah listened. The flood muttered against the hull, patient and immense, carrying silt and secrets and the memory of every drowned thing in the basin. Far off, thunder walked the ridgelines without hurrying. And beneath it all — faint, rhythmic, still miles out but closing — the sound she'd been waiting for.

"Engines," she said. "Two of them. Diesel, running rich. He's pushing hard." She checked the sky, the wind, the fire on her own deck. "He took the bait, Elias. He's coming up the main channel, and he'll turn into the split right about the time he convinces himself I'm about to bolt."

Elias exhaled slowly. In the firelight his face looked younger and older at once. "Nadia would have loved this," he said. "Her charts, saving a village."

"Her charts, and a captain who finally stopped running." Jonah put her hand on the wheel — the same wheel she'd gripped through rapids and pirate chases and the long drowning dark of the monsoon — and felt it steady under her palm, as if the boat understood. "When they round the second terrace, kill our lamps. All of them. Let the fire do the talking."

"And then?"

Jonah watched the far bend of the channel, where the black water bent around the drowned cacao rows, and waited for the gunboats to come into view.

"And then," she said, "the river takes the wheel."

Out of the darkness downstream, a searchlight bloomed.
