## 35. The Battle of the Split Channel

**Jonah**

The rain came down like a verdict.

Jonah stood at the wheel of the Marisol — what was left of her, a scorched hull with one working engine and a hold full of crates that men with guns would burn villages to take — and watched the two gunboats come up the main channel like knives being drawn. Their running lights cut through the sheeting water, twin green eyes, patient as predators that had never once gone hungry.

Behind her, on the bank, the village waited in darkness. No fires. No lanterns. Two hundred people who had known her name for six years and never asked why she only stayed three nights at a time, now armed with machetes, fish spears, and a plan she'd drawn in the mud of Elder Maren's floor while the rain hammered the thatch like applause.

"You're sure about this," Elias said beside her. Not a question, really. He'd stopped asking questions somewhere around midnight, when she'd shown him the channel charts and he'd understood what she meant to do — not flee, not fight fair, but *invite*.

"No," Jonah said. "But I'm sure about them." She nodded at the green lights. "They think I run. Everyone thinks I run. That's the only weapon I've got left, so we're going to spend it."

The Marisol's engine coughed, caught, settled into its dying-animal rhythm. She'd patched the hull twice since the cove, stuffed the worst leak with tarred canvas and prayer, and every hour the river climbed another hand's width, brown and muscular, shoving at her keel like it wanted the boat for itself.

"Signal Priya," Jonah said.

Elias raised the shuttered lamp and clicked it twice toward the eastern bank, where the flooded side-channel mouth lay hidden behind a curtain of drowned bamboo. Twice, from the reeds, the lamp answered. Priya was in position with six villagers and the dugouts loaded with brush bundles — the false cargo, the bait for the bait.

"And Tomas?"

A single click from downstream, near the old fish weir. Tomas and the young men with the cables.

Jonah exhaled. Her ribs ached where the bullet had kissed her in the running fight, a hot wire of pain with every breath, and she found she was grateful for it. Pain kept you honest. Pain said *you are still here*.

The gunboats were a kilometer out and closing. She could hear their engines now, diesel-deep, arrogant, engines built by men who believed no river would ever refuse them.

"Elias," she said quietly. "If this goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If it goes wrong, you take the index crate and you go north with Priya and you don't look back. You've spent enough of your life looking back at rivers."

He didn't answer right away. Rain ran off his jaw. "You first," he finally said. "Prove it can be done."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then she turned the wheel and pointed the Marisol's bow straight at the gunboats, dead center down the main channel, alone, lit by nothing but lightning — the most conspicuous thing on the water, a wounded boat flying no flag, running *toward* the enemy.

Let them read that. Let them believe the story they already told about her: that Jonah Vasquez always broke the wrong way, always fled, always chose motion over ground.

The lead gunboat's searchlight snapped on and found her, white and total, pinning her to the river like a moth.

"There you are," Jonah murmured, and opened the throttle.

* * *

**Ravel**

Ravel stood on the deck of the lead gunboat with the rain soaking through his company-issue slicker, and felt the particular pleasure of a man watching a debt come due.

He'd known Vasquez would run north. He'd counted on it. He'd *shaped* it — the torching of the terrace camp, the timing of the pursuit, even the mercy of letting her reach the village, because a runner needs a reason to stop running only long enough to catch her breath, and then the current takes her again. He'd offered the elders terms an hour ago through a megaphone: the archive, or the village burns. Simple arithmetic. Villages had burned before. The basin was full of ash that used to be names.

And now here came the Marisol, alone, charging down the main channel like something out of a fool's dream — no escort, no guns, just a burnt hulk and a woman with a reputation for escaping things.

"She's making a break for it," said Okonkwo, the mercenary commander, squinting through the rain. "Straight at us. Madness."

Or a diversion, Ravel thought. But he looked at the water — the flood-swollen main channel, wide and deep and fast, the only navigable route — and saw no diversion possible. The side channels were drowned, yes, but drowned channels were walls of current; even Vasquez couldn't sail a leaking scow up a waterfall. He'd studied her file. He knew her limits. He'd helped write half of them himself, back when his knowledge had been worth money instead of a rope.

"Run her down," Ravel said. "But keep her intact. The crates matter more than the corpse."

The searchlight held her. The gunboat's bow rose onto the flood current, plowing a white shoulder of water, accelerating. Behind them the second boat matched course, half a kilometer back, lights blazing.

Ravel gripped the rail and let himself imagine it finished: the crates in the company's hands, his percentage, a city with streetlights, a life where no one knew what he'd traded away. Every betrayal is a purchase, he told himself, the way he'd told himself for years. You're not selling your soul. You're buying a door.

Ahead, the Marisol suddenly turned — hard, all at once, wrenching sideways across the gunboat's bow — and vanished into the rain on the northern bank, into the black seam where the flooded bamboo stood.

"Where did she go?" Okonkwo shouted.

Ravel stared at the place she'd disappeared. The drowned side-channel. The one Nadia Wren had charted. The one he'd dismissed because no pilot alive would take a boat into current like that.

No pilot except one who had decided, for the first time in her life, not to run.

"Follow her," Ravel said, and heard his own voice go thin. "Okonkwo. *Follow her.*"

* * *

**Elias**

From the reed bank, Elias watched the lead gunboat turn into the split channel after her, and his heart went into his throat and stayed there.

It was working. God help them, it was working.

He crouched in the mud with Ravel's own field glasses — taken off a dead porter's body at the terrace camp, irony upon irony — and tracked the searchlight as it swung into the narrow water between the drowned bamboo stands. The channel looked innocent from above: a broad silver ribbon of floodwater cutting through what had been, three weeks ago, a shortcut the village used at low water. But Jonah had made him walk it at dusk, wading chest-deep with a sounding pole, and she'd shown him what the flood had hidden.

The old pilings. Dozens of them, from some failed logging operation forty years gone — ironbark posts driven deep into the channel bed, rotted off just below the surface, invisible under brown floodwater, scattered across the channel like the teeth of a broken comb. And beneath them, the channel narrowed twice, and the current funneled through those narrows at a speed no engine could fight and no hull could survive sideways.

"A river doesn't need to be deep to kill you," Jonah had said, tapping the mud map. "It just needs to be *fast*, and it needs one thing you didn't expect. The pilings are the thing they won't expect. They'll be reading depth and current. Nobody reads for sunken wood."

Now the searchlight swept the channel, found nothing but rain, and pushed deeper. The gunboat's engine note changed — higher, straining, the pilot pushing against the funnel of current between the first narrows.

Elias clicked the lamp once. Downstream, at the fish weir, Tomas answered.

The cables were old mooring wire, salvaged from the Marisol's own spares, stretched taut just below the surface across the main channel behind the gunboats — not to stop them, but to make sure that whatever came out of the split channel came out *crippled*, and couldn't turn around clean. Behind the second gunboat, the villagers' dugouts slipped silently into the current, carrying fire.

Elias moved. Six villagers with him, along the ridge path above the channel, following the sound of the searchlight generator's whine. His hands shook, and he let them. Fear was just the body telling the truth.

Below, the gunboat's light swung wildly — and then stopped swinging, and the night filled with the sound of tearing metal.

* * *

**Jonah**

She heard it hit before she saw it happen — a sound like a giant's knuckle cracking, ironbark post meeting steel plate at eighteen knots of flood-assisted speed.

The Marisol was already through the second narrows when the lead gunboat found the first piling. Jonah had taken her through the same teeth minutes earlier, threading the gaps she'd memorized in the dark, feeling the current grab her hull and *shake* her, the whole boat groaning like a living thing, water over the gunwales, Elias's voice in her head saying *prove it can be done*. She'd proved it. Barely. The port propeller had clipped a piling and now beat the water with a lopsided stutter, and the leak had torn open again, and none of it mattered because behind her the night was coming apart.

The searchlight beam whipped skyward, crazily, and died. Metal shrieked. Through the driving rain Jonah looked back and saw the gunboat slewing sideways across the current, impaled, its bow crumpled around a black post that stuck up out of the water like a grave marker. The river poured over its deck. Men were shouting. A flare went up, red and useless, lighting the rain like blood in water.

The second gunboat, half a kilometer back, saw the flare and did the smart thing — slowed, reversed, tried to swing back to the main channel.

Into Tomas's cables.

The wire took the gunboat's propellers the way a snare takes a rabbit. There was a grinding, splintering roar, and the boat went dead in the water, drifting, its engines screaming a high mechanical death rattle. And out of the dark on either side came the dugouts, silent as herons, six paddles apiece, brush bundles burning now, hurled aboard the crippled deck, fire catching on fuel drums and canvas and everything else a war machine is made of.

Jonah brought the Marisol around in the wider pool below the narrows and just — watched. Watched the machine that had chased her across four hundred kilometers of river come apart in the rain, undone not by guns but by geography, by memory, by a village that had never forgotten where the old logging posts were sunk.

"We did it," Elias breathed beside her. He'd come down from the ridge; she hadn't seen him arrive. "Jonah. We did it."

"Not yet." She pointed.

Downstream, at the mouth of the split channel, a longboat was pulling hard for the far bank — one man rowing with the desperate, ugly efficiency of someone who had rowed for his life before. Even at this distance, even in the rain, she knew the set of his shoulders.

Ravel. With a crate in the bow.

"He grabbed the index," Elias said, flat with fury. "Off the wreck — he must have had it with him the whole time—"

"He had it since the camp," Jonah said. "He was always going to save himself first. That's the one true thing about Ravel." She spun the wheel. The Marisol's damaged propeller thrashed the water. "Hold on."

* * *

**Elias**

They came at the longboat from two sides, the way herons hunt — Jonah driving the Marisol down the current line, cutting off the open river, while Elias and four villagers slid out of the reeds in a dugout, paddling without a sound.

Ravel saw the Marisol first and put his back into the oars, and for a moment it was a race, man against current against boat, and Elias understood with sudden clarity that Ravel would row until his heart burst rather than be taken, because Ravel knew exactly what the village did to traitors, and worse, what the *river* did.

Then the dugout slid out of the rain directly in his path, and Ravel's oar caught water wrong, and the longboat slewed.

Elias stood up. The dugout rocked. Rain fell into his eyes and he didn't blink.

"Nadia drew these channels," he said, and his voice carried strange and clear across the water. "Every one of them. She mapped this river so people could *live* on it. And you sold it to men who burn the people."

Ravel looked up at him, soaked, panting, the crate clutched against his chest like a child. And Elias saw it — saw the thing he'd been refusing to see since Callao Verde. Ravel wasn't a monster. He was tired. He was a tired man who had made a religion of survival and called it wisdom.

"They were going to die anyway," Ravel said. "All of them. Sefu, the porters, your sister — the river kills everyone eventually. I just chose which side of the water to stand on."

"The river doesn't choose sides," Elias said. "That was always your mistake."

He stepped across and took the crate. Ravel didn't fight him. That was the strangest part — he simply let go, sat back in the flooding longboat, and put his hands up, almost relieved, like a man setting down a load he'd carried too many years.

The villagers bound him with mooring line, gently, the way you tie anything you might have to carry a long way.

* * *

**Jonah**

Dawn came gray and washed-out, the rain thinning to a fine cold mist, and the split channel looked almost peaceful — if you didn't look at the wreckage.

The lead gunboat lay heeled over against the piling field, half-drowned, its superstructure blackened. The second had burned to the waterline and grounded on the shallows, a smoking skeleton. The river was already reclaiming both, folding them into itself, and in a week the flood would take the rest and no one upstream would ever admit they'd lost anything at all.

The villagers worked the banks in the mist, pulling mercenaries from the water, binding wrists, dragging salvage ashore. Someone was singing — low, wordless, a work song Jonah had heard here six years ago and never forgotten. Old Maren stood at the channel mouth directing traffic with a fish spear like a marshal's baton.

Jonah sat on the Marisol's rail, soaked through, ribs screaming, and watched the index crate come ashore in Elias's arms. He set it down in the mud at her feet like an offering.

"That's it," he said. "Everything. The vault records, minus what burned, plus this. The whole map of the basin — every hidden tributary, every buried claim, every name the companies paid to have erased." He looked at her. "Sefu will want to decide. If she's still—" He stopped.

"She's still alive," Jonah said. "I checked at first light. Dying, but stubborn about it. Like everything else on this river."

Elias laughed — a short, cracked sound, half sob. Around them the village was waking properly now, fires being lit, the smell of smoke that was only breakfast. Children were already down at the wrecks despite everything, daring each other closer, and an elder was hauling them back by their collars.

Jonah looked at the crate. Inside it was enough power to buy a country, or enough truth to free one. Inside it was every temptation she'd ever run from, sitting in the mud at her feet, waiting for her to pick it up.

Instead she picked up a bailer, and started bailing out the Marisol, because the old girl had earned another day, and because some things you do with your hands while your heart catches up.

Elias watched her a moment, then crouched, pulled the bailer from her grip, and took a turn himself.

"You know they're going to ask you," he said, nodding at the village — at Maren, at the children, at the whole wet, smoking, singing world of it. "What happens now. They're going to ask *you*."

Jonah looked downriver, where the flood was carrying the last of the smoke away, and where, far off, she could just make out a third boat coming upstream through the mist — small, unarmed, flying no colors she recognized.

"I know," she said.

The boat grew closer. And on its deck, someone raised a white cloth, and began to wave.
