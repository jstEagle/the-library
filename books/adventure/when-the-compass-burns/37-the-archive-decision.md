## 37. The Archive Decision

The rain had finally thinned to a fine gray mist by morning, and through it the village looked like something drawn in charcoal and then half-erased — rooftops dripping, smoke rising crooked from cooking fires, the river running high and brown and patient beyond the palisade. Elias stood at the edge of the elders' longhouse with his arms crossed against the damp, watching men haul the last of the gunboat wreckage out of the split channel. A child had already claimed a piece of painted hull for a sled. Somewhere behind him, inside the longhouse, the archive waited in its crates like a sleeping animal that might yet bite.

He had not slept. None of them had, really, but he felt it differently than the others — as a kind of ringing silence where Nadia used to be. For three years his grief had been a compass needle spinning uselessly, pointing everywhere and nowhere. Now it pointed downriver, toward the drowned ruins, toward the flooded chamber where her charts had led them, and it did not spin anymore. It simply hurt, clean and specific, the way a wound hurts when the dressing comes off.

Inside, they were going to decide what to do with the most dangerous collection of paper in the basin.

He went in when Priya touched his elbow. She didn't speak — she still spoke rarely, spending words like coins she couldn't earn back — but she had developed a whole grammar of gesture, and this one meant *they're waiting for you*.

* * *

The longhouse smelled of woodsmoke and wet thatch and the camphor the elders burned against fever. Crates were stacked along one wall, forty-one of them salvaged from the Marisol before she burned, their stenciled numbers blurred by spray. Water had taken others. Everyone knew it; no one said it twice.

Jonah sat near the fire with her ribs wrapped, her hands around a clay cup she wasn't drinking from. She looked, Elias thought, like a woman who had set down something enormously heavy and hadn't yet learned what her shoulders were for without it. Across the fire, on a cot dragged close to the warmth, lay Dr. Imara Sefu.

She was dying. There was no polite word for it anymore. The infection had climbed past what Tomas's antibiotics could argue with, and she had stopped pretending otherwise two days ago, which — knowing Sefu — was the most honest thing Elias had ever seen her do. Her skin had gone the color of old paper. But her eyes were still lit from somewhere, and when the elders finished their opening courtesies and the room settled, she was the one who spoke first.

"I called this council," she said, and her voice carried despite everything, the voice of a woman who had spent thirty years making rooms listen. "Not the village. Me. Because what is in those crates belongs to no one in this room, and everyone in this room wants it, and I include myself." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "Especially myself."

Elias watched the villagers' faces. Old Mama Ilsa, who ran the fish weirs. The headman, Dario, whose son had died holding the channel pilings against the second gunboat. Young men with machete scars still fresh. They had bled for paper they'd never seen. They had earned the right to want it.

"Three days ago," Sefu continued, "I would have told you the answer. Three days ago I had an answer I'd been carrying since the Terrace — that this archive should be controlled, rationed, sold carefully to people who could be trusted to use it slowly. Selective access. I even had buyers in mind." Her gaze found Jonah. "I told your captain some of this. She'll tell you the rest if you ask her."

Jonah didn't look up from her cup. "You told me," she said quietly. "That's enough."

"So my answer is compromised," Sefu said. "Which is why I'm not giving one. I'm asking for a vote. Every person in this room who fought for these crates, or buried someone who did. One voice each. And whatever you decide—" she paused, and Elias saw her hand tremble on the blanket until she stilled it with will alone, "—I will die agreeing. That is the only inheritance I have left to give, and I give it now, in front of witnesses, so that no one can spend my name arguing against you after I'm gone."

The fire popped. Rain ticked against the thatch like small fingers.

Dario stood. He was a broad, deliberate man who moved like the river he'd fished all his life, and when he spoke it was in the trade tongue, slow, so that everyone including Elias and Priya and the two remaining expedition crew would follow every word.

"Before we vote," he said, "we should know what we are voting on. Not the papers themselves. The question underneath them." He turned, surveying the room. "My father told me stories about the time before the companies came. He said the old kingdoms kept records too — who owned which bend of the river, which families held which tributaries, where the salt roads ran, where the dead were owed. He said the companies burned those records because a people who cannot read their own claims cannot make them." He gestured at the crates. "Now we are told those records survived. In there. Every hidden channel, every buried claim, every debt this basin ever owed itself. Power over land, water, and memory. Dr. Sefu said it herself, weeks ago, standing on the Terrace with the water rising."

"She also said," put in Ravel's former cook, a wiry woman named Beatriz who had stayed loyal, "that whoever holds it can protect us. That the next company to come upriver won't come with two gunboats. It'll come with twenty, unless we have something to bargain."

"And look what bargaining bought us," said Dario's sister, an elder named Ondine, her voice like gravel washed smooth. "Ravel bargained. Look where Ravel is." Ravel sat bound in a storage hut across the compound, awaiting whatever justice the village decided he'd earned. Nobody in the longhouse looked toward the door, but everybody thought about it; Elias felt it move through the room like a current under calm water.

Tomas rose next, leaning on a walking stick, his leg still splinted from the dugout days. "I am a geologist," he said. "I have spent my life reading stone, and I will tell you what stone says: nothing lasts except what is buried well. Empires leave their names on the surface so the next empire can scratch them off. What survives is what goes underground." He looked at the crates a long moment. "I helped Dr. Sefu open the vault. I have read some of what's in there. And I will say plainly what I think, because she asked for plainness: if any one government, any one company, any one *village* holds this archive alone, within ten years it will be a weapon. Within twenty, someone will have forged new pages into it. Within fifty, children will kill each other over claims their great-grandparents never made. Paper remembers longer and more cruelly than people do."

"Then burn it," said a young man near the door — Mateo, who had lost two brothers in the channel fight. His voice cracked on the words. "Burn it and let the river take the ash."

"No," said Priya.

Everyone turned. She almost never spoke in council; the sound of her voice landed in the room like a dropped cup. She stood with her hands pressed flat against her thighs, and Elias saw her gather herself the way she must have gathered herself in the mangroves, hiding, listening to men talk about killing her friends as though discussing cargo weights.

"Burning it burns them again," she said. "The porters. The scribes who wrote it. The kingdoms that kept it safe for six hundred years while everything else was taken." Her chin lifted. "They hid it once so it would survive. If we burn it, we finish what the companies started. I won't vote for that. I'd rather sink it."

"Sink it," repeated Mama Ilsa, tasting the word.

"Copy it first," Priya said. "Everything that's left. Copy it as many times as there are villages and schools that want a copy — here, upstream, downriver, the university at Callao Verde, anywhere. Let no one hold the only one. Then take the originals out into the deep channel where the flood scoured the bed, weight them, and let them go. No company can dredge the whole basin. No company can buy what nobody owns."

Silence. The fire worked at its logs. Elias realized he was holding his breath.

It was, he thought, exactly what Nadia would have proposed. Not because Nadia had been sentimental — she had been the least sentimental person he'd ever loved, precise as a blade — but because she had understood maps the way priests understood scripture: that a map's power lived in being *read*, and that hoarding a map was a kind of blasphemy against everyone who'd walked the ground before you. His sister had charted the Sunken Terrace because she believed the water's memory belonged to the people who lived beside it. Priya, who had watched men die for that belief and gone mute with the weight of witnessing it, had just spoken it aloud for her.

Jonah set down her cup. When she spoke, her voice was low and rough, the voice Elias had heard her use only twice — once over Nadia's logbook, once at the grave-digging above the flood line.

"I've run from every place I ever stopped," she said. "Twenty years of moving, and I'll tell you what I learned about carrying things. Whatever you carry, you carry for whoever's chasing you. You don't get to choose. The day you pick up something valuable enough, somebody downstream starts planning how to take it, and you become just the route between them and it." She looked at the crates. "I burned yesterday so this wouldn't happen. Half the archive went up with my boat, and I grieved the boat, not the paper. But the paper that's left — if we keep it, we're keeping a target with our name on it. If we sell it, we're Ravel with better manners. If we copy it and sink it—" she exhaled, "—then it stops being treasure. It becomes riverbed. And riverbed doesn't negotiate."

Sefu had closed her eyes during all of it. Now she opened them, and Elias saw that she was smiling faintly, the smile of a professor watching students surpass the syllabus.

"You understand," she said to the room, "what you'd be doing. You'd be dissolving power instead of transferring it. That has been tried perhaps five times in the history of the world, and it has succeeded perhaps never, because there is always someone in the room who believes they would wield it wisely." Her eyes moved, deliberately, to Elias. "Someone young. Someone grieving. Someone with a claim."

Heat climbed his neck. Every face turned to him.

His sister's charts were folded against his chest right now, under his shirt, where he'd carried them since the Terrace. Her handwriting. Her survey marks. The last thing on earth she had made. And beneath the grief, honest as a stone in a shoe, sat the other thing: the thought that if *he* held the archive — if he curated it, edited it, decided who learned what — then Nadia's death would mean something permanent. That he would be her executor, her monument, her continuing voice. That he would matter.

He had been carrying that thought for three days like contraband. Sefu had just searched his pockets in front of everyone.

"My sister drew the charts that got us here," he said. His voice came out steadier than he expected. "She died — we think she died — keeping this out of the wrong hands. And I've spent three days noticing how easy it would be to convince myself I was the right hands." He took the folded charts out from under his shirt and laid them on the crate nearest the fire, smoothing them flat with both palms, an act that cost him more than the bullet graze on Jonah's ribs had cost her. "So. My vote. Copy everything. Give copies to anyone who asks, freely, forever. Sink the originals where the flood buried the old riverbed, deep, past any dredge line. And no index — no master list saying what survived and what burned. Let even us not know exactly what we gave away. That's the only way it stays free. A secret shared completely stops being leverage."

"The index crate Ravel stole," Beatriz said sharply. "We recovered it. It lists everything."

"Put it in the deepest crate," Elias said. "Let it sink with the rest. If we keep the index, we've kept the key, and keys are the first thing people kill for."

Ondine laughed — a short, surprised bark. "The gringo has learned to let go faster than most men learn to hold on."

They voted then. Dario called each name in turn, and each answered in the trade tongue or a nod, and Elias counted as the room went: copy and sink, copy and sink, copy and sink. Mateo, who had wanted to burn it all, voted last, and after a long moment said, "Copy and sink — but I carry a crate to the boats myself. For my brothers." Even Beatriz, who had argued for bargaining power, ended with a shrug that meant yes. Priya voted with her whole body, standing, both hands raised like a child in a schoolroom, and several people smiled for the first time in days.

Unanimous. Eleven voices, plus Sefu's, given from the cot in a whisper: "Agreed. Witnessed. Done." She said it formally, in the cadence of a contract, and Dario repeated it back so the room could witness the witnessing, and that was that. Twenty years of scholarship, six hundred years of hidden kingdoms, three governments' worth of buried claims — dissolved in eleven minutes by a fishing village with wet feet.

Elias had expected to feel loss. Instead he felt the opposite: lightness, terrifying and clean, like stepping off a sandbar into deep water and finding he could swim.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon.

Copying was the slow part — there were no presses here, only paper, ink, and hands. Sefu's surviving crew, Tomas, Priya, Beatriz, the mission-literate among the villagers, even the schoolteacher's fourteen-year-old daughter, who had the best hand of anyone and was fiercely proud of it. They set up trestle tables under the longhouse eaves and passed pages down the line like buckets at a fire. By dusk there were three complete sets and a fourth half-made; by tomorrow there would be seven. Copies would go out with the next trading boats — to the mission upstream, to the cooperative at Puerto Hondo, to the university in Callao Verde where, Elias noted with grim satisfaction, the fixer Marquez still had offices. Let Marquez try to buy what every clerk in the city could read for the price of a library card.

The sinking would happen at dawn, at the scour hole below the split channel, where the flood had dug the bed down past forty meters. Dario's divers knew the spot; they moored their fish traps there because the nets came up clean of silt. Forty-one crates would go down wrapped in oiled canvas, weighted with the gunboat's own ballast iron, which Elias found fitting in a way he couldn't explain and didn't try to.

He spent the evening on the tables himself, copying a section of hydrographic surveys, and it was there, somewhere past midnight with his wrist aching and the lamp low, that he found the page in Nadia's hand.

Just one. A working sheet, tucked into a folder of older material — her quick sure strokes, the little north-arrow she always drew as a fish, the margin note in her cramped slanting script: *If anyone finds this, the water knows the way better than we do. Follow it home.* Below it, a sketch of the Terrace from above, the drowned crown of it, and a single word he recognized as her shorthand for *finished*: a circle, closed.

He sat very still for a long time. The lamp hissed. Down the table, Priya glanced over, read his face the way she read everything now, and quietly moved the inkwell away from his elbow so he wouldn't knock it over, and said nothing, which was the kindest sentence anyone had spoken to him in years.

*Follow it home.*

He copied the page twice — one for the archive, one for himself, folded flat against his chest where the original had ridden — and then he carried the original to Jonah, who was sitting outside the longhouse in the mist, keeping the night watch nobody had assigned her because she no longer had a wheel to stand at and didn't know what to do with her hands.

"She left instructions," he said, and handed it to her.

Jonah read it by lamplight, twice. He watched her thumb move over the little fish-shaped arrow, and watched something in her face unclench that had been clenched since Callao Verde, maybe since before Callao Verde, maybe for twenty years.

"Follow it home," she said at last, testing the words like a pilot tests a channel with a sounding pole. She looked out at the dark river, high and brown and glittering faintly with reflected stars, and Elias saw her actually consider it — not flee the idea, not flinch, but stand in it and let it stand in her. "Your sister," she said, "gave better directions dead than most pilots manage alive."

"That was her gift."

At dawn they would sink the vault. At midmorning, if Sefu lasted — and Tomas said she was spending her last strength on purpose, rationing it for this, the way she rationed everything — they would bury her on the hill above the flood line, in ground that had never yet come up to meet the coffin. Elias had already chosen his shovel.

But that was dawn. For now the mist thickened, the lamps burned, the copying tables filled page by page with the basin's stolen memory going out into the world in a hundred honest hands, and Elias Wren sat down again at the trestle, dipped his pen, and began, line by careful line, to give his sister's work away.
