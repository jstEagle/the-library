## 38. Burials and Names

The rain stopped on the third morning after the battle, as if the river had finally decided it had said everything it needed to say.

Jonah stood at the edge of the burial ground above the flood line — a shelf of red earth where the village had laid its dead for longer than anyone could count, marked not with stones but with trees. A sapling for each grave. The hillside was a forest of small histories, some grown tall and wide, their roots holding the slope against a century of floods.

They had chosen a ceiba for Sefu. It was already in the ground, a whip-thin thing no taller than Priya, staked and tied with red cord. Around its base, the villagers had heaped river stones in the old way, and old Marta — the one who remembered Jonah from the trading years, whose hands were knotted like driftwood — had pressed a bowl of maize and a cup of aguardiente into the earth beside the roots.

"She liked her coffee bitter," Elias said quietly beside Jonah. "She used to say sugar was for people who hadn't earned the taste."

Jonah huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Sounds about right."

Below them, the village went about the business of living. Smoke rose from cook fires. Children ran along the levee they'd spent two days rebuilding, hauling baskets of clay. The split channel had done its work — both gunboats lay broken in the shallows downstream, half-sunk, being picked apart by salvage crews who moved through them like patient ants. Ravel sat in a locked storeroom beneath the church, waiting for whatever authority would eventually come upriver to collect him, and Jonah found she could not summon a single feeling about that worth naming.

But today was not for Ravel. Today was for the doctor.

* * *

The whole village came. That was the thing that undid Jonah, standing there in the wet grass with her ribs still wrapped where the graze had scabbed and torn and scabbed again — the whole village came, every soul, and most of them had never met Imara Sefu. They came because the expedition had been theirs too, in a way. Because the archive that now slept in copies across four villages and two mission schools had come out of this basin, out of this water, and belonged to the people who lived along it. Because a woman had died refusing to let powerful men own the map of their own home.

Tomas spoke first, his arm still in a sling, his voice cracking on the second sentence and steadying by the fourth. He told them about Sefu's stubbornness — how she'd once made the whole team re-survey a channel three times because the numbers didn't sit right with her, and she'd been right all three times. How she drank her coffee bitter. How she'd carried a photograph of her mother's village, drowned by a dam built off falsified surveys, and how every mile she mapped was a mile mapped so that no one else's home could be stolen with a lie.

Priya spoke last. She had spoken little since the dugout, but she stood straight at the graveside in a borrowed dress the color of river clay, and her voice came out clear and low.

"She told me once," Priya said, "that a map is a promise. That whoever draws the lines is promising the future what the past looked like." She looked down at the thin ceiba tree. "She kept her promise. Now we have to keep ours."

Then the elders did the naming. Old Marta stepped forward and spoke Sefu's name aloud — full name, Dr. Imara Naledi Sefu — and then the village answered it back, all together, one voice rolling down the hillside and out over the brown floodwater. It was the custom here, Jonah understood: the dead were not let go until the living proved they would carry the name. Say it back, or lose it forever.

Two hundred voices said *Imara Naledi Sefu*, and the river took the sound and kept it.

Jonah's throat closed. She thought of every town she'd slipped out of under darkness, every name she'd left behind unspoken, every grave she'd passed without stopping because stopping was the first step toward staying and staying was the first step toward burning. She had run from funerals her whole life. This one she stood inside of, feet planted in the mud, and let it hurt.

Elias's hand found her shoulder. She didn't shrug it off.

* * *

That evening, in the long gold light before dusk, Jonah found Elias on the church steps with Nadia's charts spread across his knees.

He'd recovered them from the Marisol's cabin — what remained of the Marisol's cabin — dried page by page over the cook fire, weighted flat between boards. The ink had bled in places. The paper had warped into soft waves, like something that had learned to swim. But the lines held. Nadia's lines always held.

Jonah sat down beside him without asking. That was new, too. A month ago she'd have circled twice before sitting anywhere near another person's grief.

"She drew this one freehand," Elias said, tapping a chart of the flooded channels below the Sunken Terrace. "No instruments. Look at the margin notes." His finger traced the tiny script in the corner: *Current reverses at ebb — locals call it the river breathing. Trust the breath, not the gauge.* "She talked to people. That's what none of the company surveyors ever did. She sat with fishermen and ferrymen and old women who remembered where the water used to go, and she wrote it all down like it mattered more than her soundings." He laughed, wet-eyed. "Because it did."

"You're going to stay," Jonah said. It wasn't a question. She'd watched him all week — watched him teaching the salvage crews to read the wrecked gunboats' charts, watched him walking the levee with Marta's grandsons, showing them how to hold a compass, watched him sleep without checking the door.

"I keep telling myself I should go back," Elias said. "There's a life in the city. There are people who'll want to hire me now — everyone will want to know what happened up here, and I'm the man with the story." He folded the top chart closed, gently, the way you'd close a coffin lid. "And then I think about teaching some kid to draw a line that keeps a village from drowning, and I can't imagine leaving. She'd have stayed, you know. If she'd lived. Nadia. She'd have stayed and argued with the elders about projection methods until they threw her in the river."

Jonah smiled. "Sounds like you knew her well."

"Better late than never." He looked out at the water going copper and rose. "I spent years angry at her for disappearing into this country. Turns out she wasn't disappearing. She was arriving. Took me a burning boat and a flooded city to figure out the difference."

The words landed somewhere deep in Jonah's chest, in the place where the Marisol still smoldered. Arriving. She turned the word over like river glass.

"Elias," she said. "When you teach them — teach them the margins too. The local names, the old channels. Half of what killed those mercenaries is knowledge that isn't written anywhere official."

"That's the whole curriculum," he said.

* * *

Three days later, the elders came to her.

She was working on the barge — the village's old cargo barge, beached and rotten-bowed, which she'd begun tearing apart and rebuilding plank by plank because her hands needed work and the work needed doing. Sawdust in her hair, tar on her forearms, the good smell of fresh-cut cedar covering the bad memory of smoke. She didn't hear them cross the yard; she only turned around and there they were, five of them, Marta at the center, standing in the sawdust like a delegation from another century.

"Captain Vasquez," Marta said. The title was deliberate. Everyone here knew her name now — knew all of it, the running and the fire and the man she'd trusted, the whole confession she'd jettisoned somewhere upstream in the dark. Knowing it, they'd chosen the title anyway.

"Marta." Jonah set down her adze. Her heart was doing something strange and slow, like an engine turning over in cold weather.

"The council has talked," Marta said. "Not quickly. We are not quick people." A ripple of dry laughter among the elders. "We have watched you since you came. We watched you fight for us. We watched you bury your boat and not look back at her. And we remember, twenty years ago, a young woman on a smaller boat who fixed our ferry winch for nothing and taught our boys to read the sandbars, and then vanished before we could thank her properly."

Jonah said nothing. There was nothing in her mouth but the taste of cedar and old shame.

"We are asking you formally," Marta went on, "to stay. Not as a guest. As a neighbor. As a teacher, if you'll take it — there are children here who need to learn the water, and old men who need someone to argue with about channels. And when the barge is finished, if you want a berth on her, the village will build you a house above the flood line, on the hill, where you can see everything coming." She paused, and her voice gentled. "We know what you lost. We know why you never stayed anywhere. We're asking anyway. Staying is not the same as being caught, Captain. You should know that better than anyone, after what you did to those gunboats."

The yard was very quiet. Somewhere behind the church, a rooster complained about the heat. The river breathed against the levee, in and out, in and out.

Jonah looked at her hands — scarred, tarred, steady. Hands that had held a wheel through rapids no sane pilot would attempt, that had bailed a leaking hull all night, that had buried a woman and burned a boat and broken a man's grip on a crate of stolen history. Hands that had never once, in forty years, been asked to hold anything permanently.

Every town she'd ever left rose up in her: the burning skyline behind her stern, the radio towers receding, the faces she'd refused to memorize because memorizing was the first step toward mourning. She had believed, her whole moving life, that staying was a kind of drowning. That the current was safer than the bank.

But the current had nearly killed her a dozen times this season, and the bank had fed her, and named her, and asked her — formally, with sawdust on the ground and the whole weight of custom behind it — to belong.

"Yes," Jonah said.

It came out rough, half-voice, and she cleared her throat and said it again, louder, the way the village had answered Sefu's name back to the sky.

"Yes. I'll stay."

Marta smiled with all her remaining teeth and took Jonah's tarred hand in both of her knotted ones, and the other elders came forward one by one, and each of them said her name — *Jonah Vasquez* — and waited for her to say it back, and she did, every time, until the name felt less like a mask and more like a mooring.

* * *

That night she climbed the hill alone, past the burial ground, to the spot where the house would stand. The flood had dropped a meter in three days; the river was pulling itself back inside its banks, revealing mud-scoured fields, drowned fences, the bones of the ordinary world. Down at the waterline, the blackened skeleton of the Marisol lay where the fire had left her, half-beached, being slowly taken apart by villagers who'd asked permission first — permission, for her boat, as if it were hers to give away rather than just hers to lose. Some of the planks would become a roof beam for the school. Elias had promised it.

Jonah sat down in the wet grass where her front door would someday face the water.

A month ago — a lifetime ago — she'd have been gone by now. The job done, the pay collected, the horizon calling with its old sweet lie: *keep moving, keep ahead, keep nothing.* She had outrun every fire, every debt, every grave. And what had it bought her? A boat of ashes and a name nobody knew.

Now there was a grave on the hill with her doctor's tree growing out of it. A man on the church steps teaching children to draw promises. A barge taking shape under her hands, plank by plank, with nowhere to go and no reason to go anywhere. A village that had said her name back to her until it stuck.

The compass in her pocket — the old brass one, the one she'd carried through every burning town — she took it out and opened it. The needle swung, trembled, hunted for north the way it always had, restless as she'd been.

Then it settled. Steady. Pointing at nothing in particular, pointing at everything that was here.

Jonah closed the compass and put it away, and for the first time in forty years of moving water, she did not check the horizon for what might be coming.

Which was fortunate, perhaps, or perhaps not — because the next morning, a white launch with a university pennant rounded the downstream bend, flying a flag of inquiry, and slowed as it passed the village, and a voice called across the water asking, politely, whether anyone here might know the way upriver.
