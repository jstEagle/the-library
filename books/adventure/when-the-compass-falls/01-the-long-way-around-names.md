## 1. The Long Way Around Names

The dead man's wedding ring came out of the river mud at four in the afternoon, and I held it up to the light the way you're supposed to hold anything small and gold: like it might still mean something to somebody.

"Loft," called Marisol from the far bank, using the name I'd been renting for eleven months. "You got anything?"

"A button," I said, and pocketed the ring. "Brass. Civil War era, probably."

She made a note on her clipboard without looking up. That was the arrangement. Nobody looked too closely at Edmund Loft, salvage tech, third-tier certification, references that checked out just thoroughly enough to be boring. I'd built the name the way my father taught me to build an anchor placement—three points of contact, none of them load-bearing alone, the whole system invisible once it's set. He'd taught me a lot of things I spent a decade pretending not to remember.

The ring went into the finds tray with the buttons and the buckle fragments and the corroded spine of a pocket watch. Somewhere downstream a county archivist would catalog all of it under *Site 44-LO-117, Contractor: Meridian Salvage Group*, and nowhere in that paperwork would appear the name Nakamura, which was the entire point of me.

I've gotten good at being nobody. It's a trade like any other. You learn the tells that make people curious—a too-clean tent, hands that read terrain before their owner does—and you sand them off. You learn to tie your knots slightly wrong, then correct them when no one's watching. You learn to let other men be loud around fires so the quiet one never gets remembered. Ten years of practice and I could walk through a crew of forty and leave less impression than weather.

My father could not have done this. Kenji Nakamura walked into rooms like a man who expected his name to already be there waiting for him. It usually was. Expeditions named peaks after him. Journals put him on covers. There was a plaque—I have reasons to know about plaques—in a waystation in the Meridian Range with his name cast in bronze, and for ten years I had arranged my whole life so that I would never again stand in front of bronze with my father's name on it and feel what I felt.

The river kept doing its work. So did I.

* * *

The letter arrived on a Thursday, which is how trouble always announces itself: on an ordinary day, wearing ordinary clothes.

Marisol brought it out to the site herself, which should have been my first warning. Mail came to the crew through a post office box in town, sorted by the foreman, delivered in a rubber-banded bundle that smelled like diesel and other people's lives. A hand-delivered envelope meant someone had paid attention to where I actually was, and there were perhaps six people alive who knew that, and I did not like any of the arithmetic.

"For you," she said, and her eyebrows did a small editorial dance. "Fancy paper."

It was fancy. Heavy stock, cream, no return address—which was its own kind of signature, because people who want to be found print return addresses, and people who want you to know exactly who they are while pretending otherwise do not. The handwriting on the front was slanted, economical, the pen pressed hard enough to emboss the paper. I recognized it before I recognized the name inside, because I'd spent portions of my childhood watching that handwriting across negotiation tables and expedition manifests and, once, scrawled across the back of a photograph of my father, in words I was too young to read and old enough now to recite.

*E. Loft.*

She'd used the alias. Which meant she knew it was an alias. Which meant—

I stood there in the river shallows holding an envelope that weighed maybe an ounce and felt like ballast, and the water moved cold around my calves, and ten years of careful anonymity sat down inside my chest like a passenger who intended to stay.

"You going to open it or frame it?" Marisol asked.

"It's probably a bill."

"Bills don't come on paper like that." She shrugged and waded back toward the screens. "Your funeral, Loft."

Inside: one page, typed except for the signature. No salutation worth the name—just *Mr. Loft*—and then terms laid out with the clean brutality of someone who has spent thirty years buying things other people didn't want to sell.

*A six-week contract. Crew of eight, your selection. Technical mountaineering, high altitude, remote concession. Compensation triple your current rate, plus completion bonus equal to two years of the same. Permits secured. Departure within the month.*

And then, the line she had clearly written last, after the rest was done, because it was the only line that wasn't business:

*I know whose son you are. Come argue with me about it. —V.K.*

I read it three times. The river talked the whole while, patient, indifferent, the way rivers are about the problems of things that float on them.

Here is what you need to understand about Vesper Kline, if you never had the misfortune of growing up in the shadow of her arguments with my father. She was the other half of a rivalry that mountaineering magazines treated like entertainment and both participants treated like scripture. Two of the best route-finders of their generation, orbiting the same ranges, publishing rebuttals to each other's survey papers, racing each other to firsts neither would admit they were racing toward. My father won the one that mattered—the Meridian ascent, the shrine find, the cover story, the career that bought our house and killed him—and Vesper Kline lost it so completely that she more or less vanished from the field. I was fourteen. I remembered her only as a tall woman with a voice like a drawn blade who once told me, at some fundraiser, crouching to my eye level with terrifying sincerity, that my father was the most honest liar she had ever met.

I hadn't thought about that sentence in years. Now it arrived whole, word for word, the way buried things do when somebody finally digs near them.

Because that was the thing about the letter. Not the money—though the money was real, and I was not so pure that I didn't feel its gravity. Not even the threat of exposure, though that was there too, folded into *I know whose son you are* like a knife wrapped in a napkin. The thing was the destination. Six weeks. High altitude. Remote concession. There was exactly one range in this hemisphere with permits running on that kind of clock and a reputation bad enough that every crew who'd survived it had sworn it off in writing, some of them, rumor said, with witnesses.

The Meridian Range. The expedition that took my father and two others and gave back a legend, and left the survivors scattered like dropped gear across the decades—Ruth Alvarez tending ropes in the Cascades, telling anyone who'd listen never to ask her about it; the rest gone quiet or gone entirely. The mountain where my father's compass had last pointed at something he believed in hard enough to die above the snowline for.

She wasn't hiring a salvage tech. She was hiring a ghost, and she wanted me to know she knew.

I finished the day's grid like a professional. Screened the silt. Logged the buttons. Gave Marisol my finds tray with the ring in it and watched her log it as *unidentified band, possible modern loss*, which was fine, which was accurate, which was none of my business anymore. Rings come out of rivers. Names come off rings. People remarry. The water keeps everything and keeps nothing, depending on how you look at it.

That night in my tent I did not sleep, which was standard, but I also did not burn the letter, which was not.

Here is the inventory of my options, as I lay there listening to the river count its money:

One. Ignore her. She'd exposed me already or she hadn't; either way, silence cost me nothing today and possibly everything next season, because a woman who finds you once can find you twice, and Vesper Kline had built a career out of not letting go of things.

Two. Burn the letter, move counties, build a new name. I'd done it before. It gets easier each time, which is the most damning thing I can say about myself. But the names were running out the way unclimbed routes run out, and somewhere behind me was a trail of borrowed surnames stretching back a decade like cairns marking a path away from something rather than toward.

Three.

Three was the bonus. Three was six weeks of wages that would buy me another five years of invisibility, funded by the one mountain I had promised myself, in the specific and binding language of grief, that I would never touch again.

I want to tell you I saw the trap whole. That I understood, even lying there in the dark with her cream-colored paper against my chest, that Vesper Kline had priced the contract precisely so that refusing it would cost me more than accepting—that she had studied me the way she'd studied my father's routes, looking for the weakness, and found it, and it wasn't greed. It was that she'd figured out the one sure way to make me come to the Meridian Range was to make it look like my own idea.

But I didn't see it. Or I saw it and decided I was smarter than the shape of it. Both versions of that night exist in my memory, and I've stopped trying to determine which one is true. My father used to say the mountain doesn't care what you believe, only what you do. He said a lot of quotable things. Most of them were carved on his headstone by people who never tested them.

In the morning I wrote two letters. One accepted the contract, listed my terms—crew selection, final say on route decisions above base camp, and a clause, unusual for a hired hand, granting me veto authority over any deviation from published protocols. Professional paranoia. Nothing personal.

The second letter was shorter. It went to a post office box in a town in the Cascades, to a woman I hadn't spoken to in nine years, and it said only:

*Ruth. They're pointing a crew at the Meridians. I think I'm going. Tell me not to, or tell me what I'll need. Either answer is fine. Just answer.*

— E.

I mailed them both from the gas station in town, standing at the drop box longer than a man should stand at a drop box, holding the letters over the slot like a diver holding his breath.

Then I went back to the river and dug up other people's past for one more week, meticulous, invisible, a man named Loft with steady hands and no history, while somewhere east of me a woman who had hated my father for thirty years read my acceptance and began, I have no doubt, to smile.

The passes wouldn't hold open long. Neither, it turned out, would I.
