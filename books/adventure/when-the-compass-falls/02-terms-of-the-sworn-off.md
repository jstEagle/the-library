## 2. Terms of the Sworn-Off

The roadhouse was called the Tin Lantern, which was optimistic, since it had neither tin nor a working lantern—just a neon sign missing its L, so that from the highway it read TIN ANTERN, glowing pink over a gravel lot full of trucks and one rented sedan with government plates nobody had noticed yet.

I'd been there an hour before anyone else arrived, which was my habit and my armor. I took the corner booth where the wall met the window, back to the room, face to the door. You learn these things young if your father's name is on enough plaques in enough mountain towns: sit where you can see them coming. Not that anyone was coming for me. Nobody had come for me in ten years, and I'd worked hard to keep it that way, the way other men work hard at their golf swings.

The waitress knew my order by the second visit, which was already one visit too many. Coffee, black, and whatever pie wasn't cherry. She brought huckleberry and said, "You waiting on the Kline people?"

"Waiting on pie," I said.

She looked at me the way waitresses look at men who lie badly about small things, which is to say she filed it away and moved on. That's the trouble with small towns and small lies—they're the only ones you can't get away with.

* * *

They came in waves, the way crews always do. First the gear rats, hauling duffels that smelled like ten seasons of other people's mountains. Then the specialists, moving quieter, carrying their expertise in their shoulders. Then Ruth Alvarez, who carried hers in her hands—scarred, square hands that had probably tied off more lives than most surgeons save—and stopped dead three steps inside the door like a woman who'd walked into a room and forgotten why.

She saw me in the corner. I raised my coffee an inch. She didn't raise anything back.

Ruth and I went back eleven years, which is to say we went back to before, though neither of us had ever said so out loud. We'd worked four contracts together under my borrowed surname—Quintana, if you're wondering, a name I'd picked off a gravestone in a town where nobody would ever check, chosen because it sounded like a man who fixed things rather than found them. Ruth had never asked why a rigger with my hands was working salvage digs in the lowlands for half his market rate. Some silences are gifts. Hers were the expensive kind.

"You're early," she said, sliding into the booth across from me without being asked.

"I'm always early."

"You're always hiding," she said, "and early is a good place to do it."

Before I could decide whether to be offended, Dev Okafor came through the door with a map tube under one arm and a laptop bag that had never once been near a laptop—he kept his field notebooks in it, forty pounds of graph paper and pencil, because he trusted the grid more than he trusted God. He was twenty-six and drew contour lines the way monks illuminated manuscripts, and he was the only person on any crew I'd ever worked with who rerouted a trail for a reason and could tell you the reason to the meter.

"Nakamura." He grinned, then caught himself. "Sorry. Quintana. Habit."

"It's fine," I said, and it wasn't, but not for the reason he thought.

He'd slipped once before, six months ago, called me Nakamura in front of two other crew members, and I'd watched their faces go blank—just another foreign-sounding name in a trade full of them, nothing to snag on. But Ruth's face hadn't gone blank. Ruth's face had done something small and terrible, a flinch folded inside a blink, and I'd known then that she knew, or suspected, or remembered something she couldn't yet say. Old-timers are like that. They keep whole expeditions in their heads like shipwrecks in a harbor, hulls down, masts showing.

The rest filtered in: the Okada brothers, who climbed like water finding its level; Priya Raghavan, our medic, who treated frostbite the way some people treat houseplants, with grim daily attention; Tomás Ferro, who could read ice the way Dev read terrain; and a handful of seasonal hires whose names I made a point of learning and forgetting in equal measure, because names are how you start belonging to people, and belonging to people is how they find you.

By seven o'clock there were fourteen of us crammed into a roadhouse built for thirty, and the pie was gone, and the neon outside buzzed its broken word at the dark, and Vesper Kline walked in like weather.

* * *

I want to be fair here, which is difficult, because fairness requires distance and I have none. But you should understand what she looked like before you judge what I did next.

Vesper Kline was sixty-one years old and moved like a woman who had decided, decades ago, that speed was for people with somewhere better to be. Silver hair cut short as a verdict. A field jacket older than half my crew, patched at the elbows with what looked like deliberate contempt for fashion. She crossed the room without scanning it, which told me she'd already scanned it—from the parking lot, through the window, while I'd been watching the door like an amateur. Her eyes passed over me once, weightless, and moved on.

That should have been my first warning. People look at strangers. People don't look at you when they've already decided what you are.

She set a leather folio on the bar, turned to face us, and did not buy a round, which the crew noticed and forgave before she spoke, because the room had already tilted toward her the way rooms tilt toward money.

"You know my reputation," she said. No microphone, no projection—she simply assumed the acoustics would cooperate, and they did. "Some of you have worked for me. Most of you have worked against me, or considered it, or been warned off it by people who lost money betting I'd fail. All of that is accurate. I'm expensive, demanding, and litigious. I'm also the only client currently offering what I'm about to offer, and I'll be gone in twenty minutes."

Tomás muttered something appreciative. Even Ruth leaned forward a degree, and Ruth didn't lean for anyone.

Kline opened the folio. "The Meridian Range."

The room changed temperature. I don't mean that poetically—I mean fourteen people stopped moving at the same instant, the way a forest goes quiet when something large passes through it, and the silence had a specific shape, and the shape was a name nobody said.

"The Meridian Range," she repeated, "the high approach, the Corridor of Falls, and everything above it. Full traverse to the upper basin. Permits are issued through the Northern Concession Office and expire in six weeks, after which the season's first snows close the passes until spring. We leave in nine days. The contract pays double your standard rate, with a completion bonus—" she named a figure, and I watched Priya Raghavan, who owned a house with a leaking roof, close her eyes briefly like a woman hearing good news and bad news in the same sentence. "—that I trust makes the double redundant."

Dev's pencil was already moving. I could hear it, that soft graphite whisper, sketching the range from memory. He was twenty-six. The Meridian disaster was nineteen years old. To him it was history, a case study, a plaque somewhere. To him the name meant elevation profiles and a fascinating failure of group decision-making under altitude stress.

To Ruth Alvarez it meant something else entirely.

I watched her hands. They'd gone still on the tabletop—not relaxed-still, held-still, the way you hold a rope when the load shifts. She was looking at the middle distance, at nothing, at nineteen years ago, and her face had closed like a shop pulling down its shutters ahead of a storm.

"And the objective?" Tomás asked. "What are we climbing for?"

"A survey," Kline said. "Geological, glaciological, archaeological. The concession office wants the upper basin mapped before the spring assessment. There are rumors of historical sites above the tree line. Shrines, cairns, markers—the usual folklore. If we find them, we document them. If we don't, we document that too." She closed the folio. "Either way, the record gets made, and you all get paid."

It was clean. It was plausible. It was, I would later understand, the most carefully constructed lie I'd ever heard delivered in a roadhouse, and I grew up on expedition briefings, which are their own genre of fiction.

Questions came fast after that—insurance, evacuation protocols, radio coverage, the ratio of guides to clients—and Kline answered them all with the flat efficiency of a woman reading her own obituary and finding it acceptable. I stayed in my corner and drank coffee that had gone cold and did arithmetic I didn't want to do.

Double rate. Completion bonus. Six weeks of work, then a winter with the account actually above zero for the first time in three years. And the Meridian Range, which I had sworn off at nineteen years old in a hospital corridor, along with everyone else who'd survived it—all of us scattered now, retired or dead or, in Ruth's case, sitting forty feet away pretending to study a napkin.

Sworn-off wasn't a legal term. It was a promise fourteen survivors had made in a motel parking lot while the search helicopters were still flying, a promise that had held for two decades mostly because grief is a strong adhesive. And here was Vesper Kline peeling it up like old tape, offering money, and here was every one of us calculating.

Here was me calculating.

* * *

She found me afterward. Of course she found me afterward. I was outside, because crowds and I have an arrangement, and she came through the roadhouse door into the cold with no coat, which in this country is either toughness or a statement, and with Vesper Kline it was both.

"Quintana," she said. "Your file says structural rigging, high-angle rescue, eight years lowland salvage. That's a strange résumé for a man with your hands."

"My hands?"

"They're climber's hands. Salvage work gives you crane hands, dockworker calluses. Yours are rope-worn across the palm and the index finger. You've spent years on thin line." She said it the way a jeweler identifies a stone—no admiration, just classification. "Also, you sat facing the door, you counted the exits twice, and when I said the words 'Corridor of Falls,' your coffee cup stopped halfway to your mouth for one point five seconds. Everyone else reacted to the bonus. You reacted to the route."

The neon buzzed. Somewhere down the highway a truck ground through its gears.

"I'm careful," I said. "Careful people face doors."

"Mm." She looked out at the dark, at the black shoulder of hills to the east where the Meridians lived behind their foothills like a debt behind a polite letter. "My father climbed with yours," she said, conversationally, terribly. "Did I mention that? Kenji Nakamura. Nineteen seventy-eight, the Karakoram survey. My father carried his tent stakes. He talked about him for years—said Nakamura was the only man he ever met who could navigate by feel, no instruments, whiteout, and come out within a kilometer of his mark."

The cold went through me and settled somewhere behind the sternum, where it intended to stay.

"Small world," I said.

"Enormous world," she agreed. "Full of coincidences. Well. Think about the contract, Quintana. Nine days." She turned to go, then paused, and for just a moment the efficiency dropped away and I saw underneath it, the way you see bedrock through a landslide scar. "Everyone who was on that mountain in ninety-nine signed away their right to go back. Every one of them. Do you know what that kind of unanimity costs a person? It cost me everything, and I wasn't even there." She pulled her collar up. "I'm told the pay really is quite good."

She walked to her truck. I stood in the parking lot of the Tin Lantern with its broken pink word burning overhead, and I did the thing I was best at, the thing I'd practiced for ten years in borrowed names and lowland mud:

I told myself a story.

The story was simple. The story was: it's just money. Double rate, completion bonus, six weeks, and then I'm out, and if we go up the high approach I'll steer us west around the upper basin the way I steered every crew around every place that hurt, quietly, competently, one detour at a time. Nobody climbs toward a shrine they're not looking for. I'd keep them off it. I'd keep myself off it. I'd take the money and manage the geography and come home unchanged, which is the promise every liar makes to himself right before the mountain starts making edits.

Inside, the crew was signing. I could see them through the window, bent over Kline's folio one at a time—Priya first, thinking of her roof; the Okadas together, thinking of each other; Dev last, reading every clause twice because he was Dev, and signing anyway, because even Dev had a number.

Ruth didn't sign. She stood at the end of the bar with her arms crossed, watching the others put their names down like witnesses at something, and when Kline approached her with the pen, Ruth shook her head once—not no, exactly. Not yet.

Then she looked up, through the glass, directly at me, and held it. Ten seconds. Fifteen. Long enough that it stopped being a glance and became a message, and the message had no words in it, only weight—the weight of a parking lot nineteen years ago, of helicopters, of a promise made by people too wrecked to make promises well.

I went back inside and signed.

Vesper Kline watched me write *E. Quintana* in my careful, anonymous hand, and something crossed her face too fast to name, and she said, "Welcome aboard," and poured herself a drink at last, as if a transaction had concluded.

Nine days, I told myself. Six weeks of season. One detour west, one quiet winter, one more year of being no one in particular.

Outside, far to the east, behind the hills, the Meridian Range stood with its passes still open, counting.
