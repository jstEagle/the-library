## 3. What the River Keeps

The Kettle River doesn't run so much as it sulks—wide, brown, patient, the way water gets when it's been carrying silt and secrets for ten thousand years and has stopped expecting anyone to notice. I noticed. I always notice this river. It's the price of growing up on its banks: you learn its moods the way other children learn their mothers'.

We crossed it at the old ford on the second morning out from the roadhouse, six of us and eleven mules' worth of gear, and I stood in the shallows up to my knees and let the cold climb my shins while Dev Okafor took his bearings off the far bank like a man reading scripture.

"Current's slower than the survey says," he called back. He was twenty-four, all elbows and enthusiasm, with a field notebook he treated better than his own boots. "Somebody's numbers are wrong."

"Somebody's numbers are usually wrong," Ruth said behind me, and I felt her eyes on my back like a hand pressed flat between my shoulder blades. She'd been quiet since the roadhouse. Quiet in the specific way of a person who has decided when she's going to speak and is merely waiting for the terrain to catch up.

I waded out the far side and didn't look upstream. Upstream, two miles as the raven flies, the river bent north around a gravel bar where a boy once learned to read water because his father believed a man who couldn't read water had no business calling himself an explorer. The boy's name was Edmund Nakamura, and he was buried so deep under a borrowed surname that sometimes I had to dig for him myself.

"Ed—" Dev caught up, dripping, cheerful. "Which way after the ford? The concession map shows the trail cutting east along the ridge."

"The map's optimistic." I wrung out my socks with the efficiency of long practice. "Ridge trail washed out in the spring melt three years ago. We go south along the river through the willow flats, pick up the game trail above the oxbow."

He frowned at his map, then at me, then at the willows, which were doing their best impression of an impenetrable green argument. "That adds what, half a day?"

"Half a day we'd lose anyway trying to find a trail that isn't there."

It wasn't even a lie. That was the craft of it—the good lies are ninety percent true, and the remaining ten percent you bury where only you know the coordinates. The ridge trail *had* washed out. But there was a lower route, a good one, that would have kept us east of the river and a full day clear of a certain bend in a certain valley where the willows gave way to scree and a man named Kenji Nakamura had last pitched a tent under a sky that was about to teach his son everything he knew about endings.

I went south. Nobody argued. That's the thing about being the quiet one who knows the country: people follow you and call it their own idea.

* * *

The willow flats were misery, which helped. Misery keeps a crew honest and closemouthed. By midday we were all wearing the same expression—that flat, mud-splattered resignation that passes among working people for camaraderie—and nobody had breath left over for questions about routes.

Kline rode point on the lead mule like she'd been born in the saddle, which I doubted, and looked at the country like she was pricing it, which I also doubted, because Vesper Kline didn't look at anything the way normal people did. Normal people look at a valley and see a valley. Kline looked at it and saw a debt.

"You've been here before," she said, falling in beside me without preamble. She had a way of arriving that made it seem like she'd always been there and you'd just failed to notice.

"Salvage work comes where the rivers flood," I said. "Floods move artifacts. Artifacts pay."

"Mm." She watched the water. "You walk this ground like you're visiting someone."

"I walk every ground like that. It's called not wanting to twist an ankle."

Something almost like a smile moved across her face and decided against it. She dropped back to harass Dev about contour intervals, and I put another mile between myself and the conversation, and the river ran alongside us, keeping its own counsel, the way rivers do and fathers don't.

My father talked constantly. That's the part nobody tells you about the dead—not the silence, but the echo. Ten years gone and he still narrated my every step. *Loose rock, Eddie, test it. Cloud building over the divide, we should make camp early. You pack your fear at the bottom of the load so it carries easiest.* He'd been right about all of it, which was the problem. A man who's right about loose rock and weather turns out to be right about bigger things too, and then everyone believes him, and then belief becomes weight, and weight becomes the reason two men follow him up a mountain they should have come down from.

I tested the next stretch of bank out of habit. Solid. Thanks, Dad.

* * *

We made camp that night on a bench above the oxbow, and if any of them wondered why the site was so conveniently perfect—flat, drained, firewood within reach, water a stone's throw—they didn't say so. Of course it was perfect. I'd slept on it before. I'd slept on it at nine years old with my father's bedroll beside mine and the whole sky doing its indifferent glittering act overhead, and I'd slept on it at nineteen, alone, on the way down from identifying a body, though that time I hadn't really slept so much as lain there listening to the river refuse to explain itself.

Dev set up his plotting board by firelight, transferring the day's track onto his master sheet, and I watched him do it sideways, the way you watch a dog you suspect is smarter than advertised. He plotted our line faithfully—all of it, including the detour—and then he sat back and looked at the shape of it, and I saw the moment the shape told him something.

A good cartographer reads lines the way a doctor reads a pulse. Our track had a stutter in it. South when it should have gone east, a jog that served no drainage, no grade, no logic a trail crew would recognize. On paper it looked like nothing. To a certain kind of eye it looked exactly like what it was: a man walking around a grave.

Dev looked at the map. Then he looked at me across the fire. Then he looked back at the map, and picked up his pencil, and drew in tomorrow's proposed route—the correct one, east along the ridge spine, straight toward the range—and left a gap where the decision would go.

"Your call on the line," he said mildly, to the fire or to me, hard to say. "You know this country better than the sheets do."

"Do I?"

"Nobody walks willow flats like that unless they've got a reason or a grudge against shortcuts." He rolled his maps with the tenderness other men reserved for infants. "Either way, I'll follow the pencil."

He turned in early. I stayed up and fed the fire sticks it didn't need, and thought about how the young ones always find out first, because they haven't yet learned which questions are expensive.

* * *

Morning came gray and smelling of snow that hadn't committed yet. We broke camp in the efficient silence of people who've been cold together, and I gave the order I'd been rehearsing since the roadhouse without letting myself hear the words in my own head.

"We cut east today," I said. "Up through the burn scar, along the spine. We'll gain the shoulder of the Meridian approach by tomorrow night if the weather holds."

Ruth paused halfway through cinching a load. Just a pause, half a second, a hitch in the machinery. She knew the burn scar. She knew what lay on the far side of it, in the narrow valley where the scree fans out below the first walls. She'd walked out of that valley eleven years ago with frostbite and a story she'd been telling carefully ever since—or not telling, which amounts to the same discipline.

She finished cinching the load and said, "Burn scar's open going. Good choice," and her voice was perfectly level, and I understood that she was giving me something: the fiction, held up between us like a tarp against rain. You could take shelter under it together or you could stand out in the truth and get soaked. For now, we stood under the tarp.

The burn scar was ten years old, black snags standing in the new green like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down and been absorbed. Fireweed everywhere, purple and reckless. My father would have loved it—loved the botany of ruin, the way the land refuses to hold a grudge. *Nothing stays dead here, Eddie. It just changes jobs.* I kept my eyes on the route and my hands busy with the rope and let him talk, because arguing with him in front of the crew was a habit I couldn't afford.

By noon we were on the spine, and the Meridian Range came up over the world ahead of us like a verdict. White already in the high couloirs, early and wrong, the season leaning into winter ahead of schedule. Somebody behind me whistled. Somebody else said something about the permits and the clock, and Kline said something back in her ledger-keeper's voice, and the machine of the expedition rolled forward on its rails.

And below us, to the south, hidden by a fold of the ridge, the valley with the bend in the river slid past under our feet. Half a mile east of the trail. An hour's walk if a man wanted to break away, which no reasonable man would, and which I found myself calculating anyway—the way you calculate an old phone number, automatically, uselessly, your fingers remembering a shape your life has outgrown.

There was a cairn there. My father's crew built it when they came through on the approach, the same approach, the same season, eleven years earlier. I'd built half of it myself, a kid along for the summer, stacking stones while the adults argued about the route up the icefall. My father had put something inside it—I never asked what; expedition men put things in cairns the way other men put things in drawers—and the last time I'd seen it, the cairn had been whole, and so had everybody in the party, and the future had still been a place instead of a direction.

I didn't look south. I'd gotten very good at not looking south. It was practically a skill; I could have taught a seminar.

But Dev, walking beside me with the compass in his hand, glanced that way once—a quick, involuntary check, the way you glance at a door you heard close—and then looked forward again and said nothing, and adjusted nothing, and matched my pace exactly.

That was the trouble with Dev Okafor, I was beginning to understand. He noticed the detour. He noticed the gap he'd left on his map. He noticed that I never once looked at the most beautiful view in the valley, and he filed all of it away in whatever meticulous interior archive he kept, alongside contour intervals and current speeds and the wrong numbers in somebody's survey.

He said nothing. Young men who say nothing are either wise or dangerous, and at twenty-four they're usually both.

* * *

We camped that night on the shoulder, and the wind came off the Meridians carrying the first real cold of the season, and I lay in my bag listening to the tent fabric snap and thinking about inheritance.

Money skips generations cleanly enough. Land does too, more or less. But a route—that's different. A route is knowledge worn into a body, and you can't disown it any more than you can disown your own gait. Every switchback I chose, every campsite, every instinct about where the water would be and where the rock would lie—my father had installed all of it, patiently, over summers, the way you train a compass needle. And now here I was, leading six strangers toward the mountain that killed him, navigating by instincts he'd given me, avoiding his grave by reflexes he'd taught me, and the whole apparatus of my anonymity turned out to run on his engineering.

You can change your name. You can't change your dead reckoning.

Somewhere out in the dark, the river kept running north around the bend, past the cairn, keeping whatever it keeps. Rivers are the only honest archivists I know. They take everything and surface it eventually—boats, bones, letters, the occasional reputation—and they never editorialize. Someday, probably, the river would give up whatever my father sealed in that cairn, the way it gives up everything, to some flood season, some stranger, some kid wandering the gravel bars.

I'd always assumed I'd be long gone by then. Lying there in the snapping dark with the Meridians rising white against the stars, I realized with a feeling I couldn't quite name that I was no longer sure which direction gone was.

In the morning, Dev handed me the day's route sheet for approval, and at the bottom, in his small precise hand, he'd written a note in the margin where the track crossed the ridge spine: *Local knowledge confirmed. Source: E.M.*

E.M. Not my name. Not the name I'd signed the contract under, either—he'd abbreviated it, smoothed it, done me the courtesy of a fiction. Or maybe he'd done himself the courtesy of a question he wasn't ready to ask.

I initialed it, and we broke camp, and the mountain waited ahead of us with the patience of all large indifferent things, and behind us, two miles south and a decade deep, the river went on keeping what it keeps.
