## 4. Kline's Ledger

People think obsession looks like fire. It doesn't. It looks like a ledger.

Mine is a green cloth-bound notebook, bought in 1987 from a stationer in Kathmandu who has since died, whose shop has since become a phone accessories kiosk, and which has survived four moves, two divorces—one mine, one the mountain's—and one very determined attempt by a former graduate student to throw it into a river. The river attempt was poetic. I retrieved it with a fishing net and a borrowed truck, and I have never entirely forgiven the student, though to be fair I have never entirely forgiven anyone, which is the whole point of this chapter of my life, the one I'm living now, the one with the Meridian Range at the end of it.

The ledger has columns. That's what people never understand about a grudge done properly: it is not a wound you nurse, it is an account you keep. Debits and credits. Dates. Weather. Who said what, and who was standing close enough to hear it, and whether they nodded.

Kenji Nakamura has occupied the debit column since March 1994.

I was thirty-one then, which is the age at which you still believe the world is a meritocracy with weather. I had eleven years of fieldwork behind me, two papers that people actually cited, and a third in draft that was going to settle the question of high-altitude cultural transmission in the Meridian corridors—the shrine question, the one that made and unmade careers. I had a permit. I had funding. I had, God help me, a reputation for rigor, which in those days I thought was armor.

Nakamura had charm. That's the short version. The long version is that he had charm, a grant officer's ear for what funders wanted to hear, and a habit of arriving in places just after I'd done the tedious work of making them possible. He didn't steal my permit—permits aren't stolen, they're outmaneuvered, and the concession board that year had three members who owed him favors from the Annapurna years. He didn't steal my funding—the foundation simply called, very apologetic, and said they'd decided to consolidate their high-altitude program under a single principal investigator, and wouldn't it be wonderful if I joined his team as field coordinator.

Wouldn't it be wonderful.

I declined. I watched from the valley, from a borrowed telescope and a borrowed tent, as his expedition went up into the Meridian in the last good window of the season. I watched the weather turn. I watched six men come down instead of nine.

And I watched, three months later, as the world learned the name Nakamura attached to the words *shrine* and *Meridian* and *discovery*, in that order, forever.

* * *

Here is what I have spent thirty years unable to prove, and unable to stop trying to prove, which are the two conditions that define a ledger rather than a grievance:

The shrine, as described in his published account, does not fit the geography.

I have been over his route description the way other women go over love letters. I have walked the lower corridors myself, twice, in seasons when the passes allowed it, and the chamber he describes—a vaulted hall, he wrote, "cathedral-scaled," with "a colonnade of frozen offerings" and "a light like a held breath"—cannot exist where he says it exists. The ice geometry is wrong. The meltwater channels don't feed a space that size. I have the survey data. I have the photographs of the corridor mouths. I have a scale model in my office in Denver that my second husband referred to, before the divorce, as "the shrine to the shrine."

But here is the thing. Here is the thing that keeps the ledger open.

The lower markings are real.

I have seen them. Survey marks, old ones, cut clean into the ice walls of the approach caves, in a hand that is disciplined and strange and—this is the part I have never said aloud to anyone, not to the concession board, not to the ethics committee, not to the therapist I fired for suggesting I "consider what the mountain means to me"—familiar. Nakamura documented markings. The question was never whether he found something. The question is what he found, and what he made of it, and whether the man who came down that mountain in 1994 with three fewer companions and one immortal reputation told the truth about the difference.

Because there is a third possibility, and it is the one that has kept me awake in tents on four continents: that he found exactly what he described, and then, under the pressure of funders who had paid for a cathedral, described it bigger. That the truth was real and small and human, and he buried it under scale the way you bury anything—carefully, with good intentions, telling yourself the myth will serve the site better than the fact.

Or that he lied outright, and there is nothing up there at all but ice and the bones of the men who went up to check.

Either way, my career died in the gap between those two possibilities. When I challenged the account—politely, then less politely, then in a paper that the journal accepted and then, after two phone calls from somewhere, unaccepted—the field closed around him like water around a stone. Rigor, I learned, is armor only when you're winning. When you're losing it's called bitterness, and it ages worse.

So I stopped challenging and started buying.

* * *

The roadhouse meeting went the way I designed it to go, which is to say the way all my meetings go now: I let the money do the talking and I watch the faces.

Six weeks of permit. A bonus that made the old-timers sit up straighter. And the Meridian, named plainly, no euphemism, because I wanted to see who flinched.

The Alvarez woman flinched. Ruth Alvarez—yes, *that* Alvarez, the one from the '94 support team, the one who came down with the retreat party and never went back and never, in thirty years, gave a single interview, though three documentarians and one very persistent podcaster tried. She looked at the route map the way you look at a photograph of someone you buried, and then she looked at me, and then she said nothing, which from a woman her age is the loudest sentence in the room.

I hired her anyway. You want the people who flinch. The ones who flinch are the ones who know where the bodies are, and I mean that with as little metaphor as I can manage.

But it wasn't Alvarez I was watching. It was the salvage man.

* * *

The one calling himself Edmund Marsh. Salvage and recovery, lowland permits, a résumé that reads like a man trying to be forgettable: competent, unglamorous, no high-altitude firsts, no expeditions worth naming, ten years of digging up other people's wrecks in valleys where nothing taller than a crane exists.

I have read ten thousand résumés. I have never before read one that was so aggressively, so *professionally* unremarkable. It's a skill, being unremarkable. Most people can't do it. Most people can't help leaving fingerprints—the inflated verb, the name-dropped expedition, the photograph of themselves looking windswept. This résumé had been sanded. Sanded by someone who understood exactly what a résumé is for, which is concealment, and who had practiced.

And then there was the name. Marsh. Edmund Marsh.

Nakamura had a son. This is not secret knowledge; it's just unfashionable knowledge, the kind that lives in the back pages of old expedition reports and in the memory of people like Alvarez. A boy, born in the late seventies, who appeared once in a photograph in a climbing magazine—Kenji holding him up on his shoulders at base camp, both of them squinting into the sun, the boy's face half-turned away. The boy was named Edmund. After the article about the shrine discovery, the family withdrew. The widow gave no interviews. The son vanished from the record entirely, which, in the climbing world, is not something that happens. It is something that is *done*.

Edmund Marsh, who handles a rope like he was weaned on one. Edmund Marsh, who at the roadhouse, when I spread the route map, looked at the Meridian approach corridor for exactly one and a half seconds too long, and then looked away with the particular discipline of a man closing a door on a room he knows every inch of.

I have spent thirty years reading men who want something from mountains. I know the hunger when I see it. But this wasn't hunger. This was grief with its collar turned up.

So I did what I do. I opened the ledger.

* * *

I tested him on the third day of the approach trek, at the river crossing, in the hour of flat grey light when everyone's guard is lowest because everyone is cold and no one is thinking about anything but their feet.

The detail I chose is this: in 1994, Kenji Nakamura's team crossed the Meridian headwaters not at the bridge—the bridge that every map, then and now, marks as the crossing—but a kilometer downstream, at a gravel bar that floods out by mid-June. I know this because I interviewed the retreat party afterward, or tried to; Alvarez talked to me for eleven minutes on a bus station bench in 1996 and then stood up and walked away, and in those eleven minutes she mentioned, without knowing why it mattered to me, that they'd crossed "at the bar, not the bridge, because Kenji didn't trust the bridge." It is a detail that appears in no published account. It appears in no map. It exists in one dead woman's memory, one retired woman's eleven minutes, and my ledger.

A man who knew that crossing was either Kenji himself, one of the four who died, or someone who had sat at Kenji's knee and been told the stories. And Kenji's stories, by every account, were not told at knees. Kenji kept his field knowledge the way he kept everything: close, and slightly larger than life.

So at the crossing I stopped the column and said, loudly, to no one in particular: "Bridge looks rotten. We'll cross at the bar, downstream."

And I watched Edmund Marsh.

Here is what I saw, and I have replayed it enough times that I can now describe it frame by frame, which is either expertise or illness, and I have decided, at my age, not to distinguish: his hands stopped moving on the pack strap. Not froze—stopped, deliberately, the way a man stops a clock so it can't be heard ticking. His eyes went downstream before his head did. He knew where the bar was. Not *a* bar—*the* bar. And then he said, in the flat voice of a hired hand: "Bar's probably flooded this early."

It wasn't. I knew it wasn't; I'd had my scout check the gauge readings three weeks ago. The bar was high and dry and wide as a road.

He knew it wasn't flooded, and he said it was, because he did not want us crossing there, and he did not want to say why, and the why was a kilometer downstream and thirty-one years deep.

I let it go. I said, "Fine, the bridge," and we crossed on the rotten bridge like ordinary fools, and I wrote it in the ledger that night by headlamp, in my small square hand: *E.M. knows the crossing. Knows the bar. Lied to avoid it. Conclusion pending.*

Conclusion pending. Thirty years of pending. My whole life has become a pending column.

* * *

You will want to know what I would do with the truth, if I get it. People assume a woman like me wants vindication—wants the world to say *Vesper was right, Nakamura was a fraud, give her back her career.* And yes. Fine. Yes, I want that. I have wanted it so long it has worn a groove in me like a boot path, and I walk it in my sleep.

But I will tell you the truest thing in this ledger, the entry I have never written down because writing it would make it real in a way the green notebook cannot contain:

I am almost afraid he told the truth.

Because if the shrine is real, cathedral and all, then Kenji Nakamura beat me fair and square, with better luck and better weather and a better grant officer, and my ruin was not a crime but a misfortune, and misfortunes don't have anyone to blame. And a grudge with no one to blame is just a life, examined. I don't know if I can survive that at sixty-two. I have built thirty years of architecture on the assumption of his guilt. Take away the guilt and the whole building comes down, and I am standing under it.

So I need the ice caves. I need the chamber or the absence of it. I need the markings read by someone who can read them, and I need the truth the way a debt collector needs payment: not for the money, but to close the book.

And if the boy on my crew is who I think he is—then he is not an instrument, whatever I told my scout, whatever I told myself. He is a witness. The only living witness who might know which version of his father came down that mountain. And I have bought his contract, and pointed him at the place that killed his father, and told myself it's just business.

It's just business. I wrote that in the ledger too. Even the lies get columns.

* * *

We made base camp below the Meridian on the ninth day, in the blue hour before dark, and the mountain did what mountains do to the people who come back: it stood there, indifferent as arithmetic, while we raised tents in its shadow.

The snow came overnight. Early—too early, the kind of snow that makes old climbers quiet and young ones cheerful. I stood outside my tent at dawn with the ledger in my coat pocket and watched it come down on the high passes like a curtain being drawn across the first act, and I thought: *six weeks. The permit gives us six weeks, and the mountain may not.*

Alvarez was already up. She was standing at the edge of camp looking up at the route with her hands in her pockets, and when she saw me she didn't nod, didn't speak, just looked at me for a long moment with an expression I have spent thirty years learning to read, because I saw it last on a bus station bench in 1996.

It was the expression of a woman about to tell me something I would not want to hear.

She started toward my tent. And behind her, coming out of his own tent into the falling snow, hair white with it already, was Edmund Marsh—Edmund *something*—looking up at the mountain with his face open in a way I had never once seen it open, and I understood, with the certainty of a column finally balancing, that whatever Alvarez was about to say, and whatever the boy was about to do, my ledger was about to get its most important entry yet.

I went to meet them both.
