## 5. First Snowline

The snow came in the night like a rumor nobody wanted to confirm.

I woke in my tent to a sound I'd been dreading for two weeks—the soft, granular hiss of flakes against nylon—and lay there doing the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the roadhouse. First week of September. The Meridian passes historically held until mid-October. Historically. Mountains have been breaking their own records lately the way athletes on something suspicious do, and none of it in our favor.

Base camp sat in a bowl of gray scree at 3,400 meters, six tents, a cook fly sagging under wet canvas, and the Meridian Range rising above us like a jury that hadn't yet decided. Through the tent door I could see the high col where the route bent west into country that had killed my father, wearing its first white like a man trying on a suit for a funeral he's hoping isn't his own.

I got up, made coffee, and told myself the day would be ordinary.

This is a skill of mine, telling myself things. Ten years of practice. I can tell myself a lie so smoothly it arrives with references.

* * *

The morning was spent doing what base camp mornings are for: arguing with gear. Kline had bought equipment that photographed well and performed adequately, which is to say we spent three hours re-rigging the fixed lines she'd had her scout stash—more on that betrayal later, though at this point in the story I didn't know about it yet, and there's a particular misery in looking back at a version of yourself who was still allowed to trust people.

Dev Okafor worked beside me on the rope coiling, humming something off-key, drawing maps in the frost on a supply crate with one finger when he thought I wasn't watching. The kid had a cartographer's affliction: he couldn't look at terrain without wanting to improve it. He'd already redrawn Kline's approach route twice in his notebook, each version shaving hours off through shortcuts that existed only if you ignored everything the terrain was saying.

"Those gullies," I said, nodding at the east flank where he'd penciled a line. "They funnel. You'd be climbing in a rockfall chute by ten a.m."

He looked up, wounded. "You've been here before?"

"I read," I said. "There are accounts."

"There are accounts of everything." He blew on the crate, erasing his map. "Doesn't mean anybody's actually walked it."

No, I thought. Nobody's walked it. Not all the way. One party went far enough to matter and came back short by exactly one father.

Ruth Alvarez passed behind us carrying a coil of static line, and I felt her eyes on my back the way you feel weather change before you hear it. She'd been watching me since the roadhouse—not hostile, not warm. Watching me the way an old guide watches a client who claims experience: patiently, waiting for the mountain to conduct the interview.

She'd said almost nothing in two weeks. That should have comforted me. Instead it sat in my chest like a stone I kept forgetting to put down and then remembering, always at the worst moments—when I tied a friction hitch without thinking, when I read the cloud deck over the col and knew what it meant, when Dev asked me a question about ice anchors and I answered with a specificity that belonged to a different name.

The snow thickened around noon. By two it had stopped pretending to be weather and become a statement.

* * *

Kline called a briefing under the cook fly at four, standing with her back to the range as if daring it to interrupt her.

"Sixty percent accumulation on the upper route," she said, tapping a tablet that showed satellite imagery two days old, which in mountain time might as well be cave paintings. "It's cosmetic. It'll settle or slide off. Either way we move on schedule."

"Snow doesn't 'slide off,'" Ruth said from the back. "It loads. Then it decides."

"It decides based on what's underneath it," Kline said. "And underneath it is competent climbers on a fixed schedule. Permits expire in thirty-one days. We are not renegotiating with a concession office through a storm window."

I watched the crew absorb this. Marek, our rigger, nodded along because Marek nodded along to anything with numbers in it. The Torres twins exchanged the glance they used instead of sentences. Dev wrote *31 days* in his notebook and underlined it twice, and I understood him a little too well—he wasn't counting down to the summit. He was counting down to being someone who'd done this.

"And if it keeps snowing?" I asked.

Everyone looked at me. I hadn't meant to say it out loud. That happens more than I'd like.

"Then we climb in it," Kline said, "because the alternative is explaining to four investors why their money is buried under a tent in a bowl eating rations."

"That's not the alternative," I said. "The alternative is going home. It's always the alternative. It just gets more expensive every day you don't take it."

The fly was quiet enough that I heard the snow ticking on canvas. Kline studied me with those pale eyes that had been taking inventory of me since the roadhouse, and I had the distinct sensation of a lock testing a key it suspected didn't belong to the door.

"You have concerns, Mr. Quist," she said, using the surname I'd worn for a decade like a coat with someone else's initials in the collar. "Log them. In writing. Otherwise we climb on schedule."

"I'll log them," I said, and left the fly before I could log anything else.

Behind me I heard Ruth's voice, low and dry: "Give the man a minute, Vesper. Snow makes some people honest."

* * *

Dinner was freeze-dried ambivalence. Afterward the crew crawled into their tents and the mountain turned the volume down, and I stayed outside because I'd rather be cold alone than warm among people I was lying to, which is a sentence I'd have laughed at ten years ago and now just described my Tuesday.

The clouds had torn open in the west. The Meridians stood exposed under a hard moon, and the new snow on the upper faces caught the light and threw it back silver, and I stood there looking at the exact shoulder of rock where—according to a report I'd read forty times, according to Ruth Alvarez's silence, according to a compass I still carried in a sock at the bottom of my kit—my father had made his last radio call.

*"Weather's turning. We're pushing for the chamber tomorrow. If we're not back in three days—"*

He never finished the sentence. The tape ends there. I've thought about that a lot, how the most important sentence of his life ran out of tape. There's probably a lesson in it about recording your important sentences early.

"You knew him."

I didn't jump. I want that noted. But my hand did close around the thermos like it was going to defend me, and Ruth stepped out of the dark between tents with two cups of something steaming and the expression of a woman who had rehearsed this conversation for two weeks and discarded every polite version.

"Knew who?" I said.

"Don't." She handed me a cup anyway. Tea, mostly sugar, the kind mountaineers drink as medicine. "Don't do the thing where you spend five words buying yourself an hour. I've got frostbite scars older than your act."

I drank the tea. It gave me somewhere to put my face.

"Your hands," she said. "The way you rack gear. Left over right, always, even tired. The way you read the col this morning—you looked at it like you were checking on somebody. And the detour on the approach." She let that sit. "Three kilometers out of our way, past a bench of boulders below the south ridge, and you wouldn't meet anyone's eyes for a day after. I know that bench, Edmund. I helped carry a stretcher away from it."

The name landed in the middle of my chest and stayed there, glowing.

"How long," I managed.

"Since the roadhouse. You signed the waiver left-handed and your signature started with a stroke nobody makes unless they learned to write it hiding it." She shrugged, an old woman's shrug, economical. "Also you look like him. Around the mouth. He made the same face when he was wrong and wouldn't admit it."

"He was rarely wrong," I said, and heard myself defending a ghost out of pure reflex.

"No," Ruth agreed. "He was rarely wrong. That was the problem. A man who's rarely wrong stops checking." She looked up at the range, at the silvered shoulder. "I was on the original expedition. Twenty-two years old, the only woman, the only one they let carry the med kit because carrying the med kit meant staying in the middle of the line where nothing could happen to me. I was there when the vote came to turn around. I voted to turn around. I've had thirty years to decide whether that makes me the sensible one or the coward, and the answer changes depending on the year."

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because you were going to keep pretending until the pretense got someone hurt, and I'm too old to watch that movie again." She turned to face me fully, and up close her eyes were the color of glacier melt, that gray-green that looks gentle until you remember what it does to rock. "Three days. You tell the crew who you are, in your own words, before we leave this camp. Or I tell them. And I'll tell them less kindly than you will, because I'm angry, Edmund. Not at you. At him. At all of us. At the whole stupid economy of famous men and the people who survive them."

The wind came down off the col then, a preview, carrying grit and cold. Somewhere above us the new snow was settling onto old snow, learning its weight.

"What happens in three days if I don't?" I asked. "We're eleven days from the next resupply. You'd gut the crew mid-expedition over—a name?"

"Over a name." She said it slowly, tasting it. "Yes. Because it's not a name. It's the reason half this crew took the contract and the other half will walk off when they find out they weren't told. Marek idolizes the man. Dev has your father's route maps taped inside his notebook—I've seen them. You think this is your secret. It stopped being yours the day Kline bought the crew and pointed them here." She drained her tea and set the cup on a rock with the care of someone who leaves no trace anywhere, ever. "Three days. Then the snow decides whose story it gets to bury."

She walked back toward her tent, small and straight-backed against the wind, and I stood there holding a cooling cup and doing the thing I do best, which is telling myself things.

*I can manage this,* I told myself. *Three days is enough time to build a version of the truth that fits in a briefing. I'll frame it. Contextualize. Lead with competence, follow with contrition—*

And then I stopped, because I heard my father's voice in the framing, that smooth funders' cadence he must have used when he exaggerated the shrine for men in suits, and I understood that Ruth hadn't given me a deadline. She'd given me a mirror, and three days to stop liking what it showed.

* * *

I didn't sleep. Around midnight I dug the compass out of the sock at the bottom of my kit—brass case, hairline crack across the bezel, needle that wobbled a degree east of true and had wobbled there my whole childhood, so that my father's sense of north was permanently, privately, slightly wrong.

I held it in the dark and listened to the snow start again on the fabric above me, soft and patient, the way secrets accumulate. First flakes, then load, then the mountain deciding.

Three days. Thirty-one days of permit. One crew that trusted a stranger named Quist, one rival who'd bought us all, one dead man's route going silver above us in the dark.

Outside, the storm found its rhythm, and I lay there understanding, finally, the true cost of a secret: it doesn't stay the size it was when you buried it. It grows. Every day you keep it, it eats another day of trust, and by the time you dig it up it's big enough to bury you.

Two days and a wake-up, I thought. And then the first flakes of a different kind of avalanche began to fall, because at dawn, when I crawled out to check the lines, there were boot prints between my tent and the cook fly that no one in our crew owned—

small boots, fresh, heading uphill toward the col,

and Vesper Kline was already standing over them, smiling like a woman whose mail had finally arrived.
