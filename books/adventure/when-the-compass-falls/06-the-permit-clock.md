## 6. The Permit Clock

I keep the maps the way other people keep diaries. Every line I draw is a confession of where I've been and what I believed when I got there, which is why I draw them in pencil first and ink them only after the ground has agreed with me. The Meridian Range had not yet agreed to anything. It sat on my table at base camp like a debt collector—patient, inevitable, unimpressed by my excuses.

We'd been there four days. Four days of rigging tents against wind that came off the icefield with opinions, of hauling loads to the moraine cache, of watching the snowline creep down toward us each morning like it was testing how close it could get before we flinched. Ruth Alvarez moved through camp the way she did everything: without wasted motion, without wasted words, and with a look that suggested she'd already read the last page of whatever book you were pretending to write.

Edmund—*Nak*, as he'd let us call him, a name so thin it was practically a pseudonym itself—spent those days doing what he always did. Working. Checking anchors twice. Repacking loads so the weight rode right. He was the kind of quiet that you only notice when it stops, and then you realize it had been holding up part of the ceiling.

Vesper Kline noticed everything else.

That's what I liked about her, in the way you like a storm front you can see coming from forty miles off. She circled camp each evening with her ledger under her arm, and I mean an actual paper ledger, leather-bound, corners soft with handling, because Vesper Kline trusted nothing that could be remotely wiped. She'd stop at each tent, ask two questions, write down three answers, and move on. By day three she knew our boot sizes, our rations preferences, and, I suspected, which of us slept facing the door.

On the fourth morning she asked me for a supply run.

"Two of you," she said, tapping the manifest. "You and Nakamura. Down to Kessel Station, pick up the fuel drums and the fresh rope order, confirm the heli slot for week six." A pause, precise as a piton. "And check on our permits while you're there."

"Anything wrong with the permits?"

"I don't know yet," she said, which was the most honest thing she'd said all expedition, and therefore alarming.

* * *

Kessel Station was four hours down-valley, a concession town that existed for one reason and had never pretended otherwise. It had a fuel depot, a regulator's office, a saloon that doubled as a gear exchange, and a population that changed entirely every season like the ice on the river. The buildings were all corrugated steel and optimism. The mountains behind it didn't look back.

Edmund drove. I navigated, which was redundant—the road went one direction—but it gave my hands something honest to do. We talked about nothing for the first hour. Rope stretch. Fuel ratios. The particular way this valley funneled weather down its throat at night. Edmund Nakamura was easy to talk to precisely because he never talked about himself; conversation with him was a one-way valve, and I hadn't yet wondered why the pressure only flowed one direction.

The regulator's office smelled of toner and old coffee, and the man behind the counter wore the expression of someone who had been disappointed by professionals his entire career.

"Nakamura and Okafor," Edmund said. "Meridian concession, Kline party. We're here about a flagged file."

The man's eyebrows performed a small, expensive dance. "Flagged. Yeah." He turned to his terminal with the reluctance of a man opening a trapdoor he'd rather not stand on himself. "Your access authorization came back with a hold on it. Third-party inquiry. Somebody filed a question about your route permits—specifically the upper corridor easements—and until it's answered, the office can't countersign your high-altitude passes."

"Filed by whom?"

"That's not—" He caught Edmund's look and reconsidered his morning. "It's anonymized through the regional office. Standard whistleblower channel." He said *whistleblower* the way you'd say *mold*. "Look, these things happen. Competing concessions, conservation groups, somebody's ex-wife. Nine times out of ten it's nothing. The inquiry just has to be resolved before your window closes, and your window—" he checked something, wincing, "—closes in thirty-eight days."

Thirty-eight days. Six weeks had become five and change while we were hauling fuel drums, and now a stranger's anonymous letter had its thumb on the scale.

"What does resolution require?" Edmund asked. His voice was level. I remember noticing that—how level it was, how deliberately, like a man keeping a full glass steady while walking on scree.

"A site visit, or documentation. Someone from the regional office goes up, or you produce survey records substantiating the route claims." The man shrugged. "Neither of which is happening fast in shoulder season."

We walked out into the light with the whole shape of the problem between us, unspoken. Thirty-eight days. A route that needed every one of them. And somewhere behind us, a piece of paper with no name on it, quietly sawing at the rope.

Edmund stood in the street for a moment, looking up-valley at the range, and his face did something I couldn't read. Not anger. Something older than anger. Like a man hearing a song he'd hoped to forget.

"You okay?" I asked.

"Fine," he said. "Let's get the drums."

* * *

It was on the drive back, somewhere past the second river crossing, that I started doing the arithmetic I should have done in the office.

A third-party inquiry. Anonymized. Filed recently enough to catch us before the climb but after the money was spent. Who benefits? Not the regulators—they hated paperwork more than anyone. Not the concession office. Which left competitors, or—

Or us.

The thought arrived ugly and stayed. Seven people on this crew. Old alliances folded through it like fault lines: Ruth and Kline went back further than either admitted; the two brothers on rigging had climbed with Kline's outfit for a decade; I was new; Edmund was newer. And Kline herself, who had bought our contract outright, who wanted the Meridian corridor with a hunger none of us had ever been given a reason for.

"Dev," Edmund said.

"Hm?"

"You're doing the thing where you stare at the windshield like it owes you money."

"Just thinking about the permit."

"Don't," he said. Then, softer: "Not yet. Wait until we know what we're looking at."

Which was reasonable advice, and I took it, and it changed nothing, because by the time we rolled into base camp the sun was going down and Vesper Kline already knew.

* * *

She met us at the tent line with the radio log in her hand. She'd called Kessel herself, an hour before we arrived—of course she had; she'd probably called before we'd finished loading the truck—and whatever the regulator told her, it had rearranged her face into something I'd never seen on it before. Not fear. Vesper Kline didn't do fear. It was the look of a woman who has found a knife in her own kitchen and is trying to remember everyone who's visited.

"The office says an inquiry was filed nine days ago," she said, without greeting. "Nine days. That's before we broke camp at the roadhouse."

"Could be coincidence," one of the brothers offered.

"Nothing about this concession is coincidence." She turned, scanning the circle of faces the firelight gave her. "Somebody on this mountain, or somebody talking to somebody on this mountain, sent a letter to the regional office asking questions about our upper-corridor easements. Questions specific enough to freeze the countersignature. That's not a hiker's curiosity. That's somebody who has seen our route plan."

Silence. The fire cracked. Somewhere up-slope, snow let go of a rock and hissed into the dark.

"So here's how this works," Kline continued, and her voice went smooth and cold, the voice of the woman who'd built an outfit and lost it and built it again. "Nobody leaves camp. Nobody touches the radio without me standing there. And anybody who knows anything about that letter has tonight to say so privately, because if I find out later, it'll go differently."

"That's not how you build trust," Ruth said.

Ruth Alvarez had been sitting slightly outside the firelight the whole time, the way she did, mending a strap with a patience that looked like indifference. Now she set the strap down.

"Trust," Kline said, "is what you have left after you count everything else."

"Then count this." Ruth's voice didn't rise. It never rose; it just got heavier, like load shifting on a haul line. "You bought this crew's contract in a week. You pointed us at a range half these people can't name a route on. You won't tell us why the shrine matters to you beyond the word *legacy*. And now the first time something goes sideways, you decide it's theft from inside the tent." She shook her head slowly. "I've been on expeditions that ate themselves, Vesper. They all started exactly like this. Somebody says *one of you did it*, and everybody starts doing arithmetic about everybody else, and by the time the real trouble comes, nobody will tie in to nobody."

"This isn't the same—"

"It's always the same. The mountain changes. People don't."

Kline's jaw worked. For a moment—one long, flickering moment—I thought she might actually answer Ruth honestly, might say something true about why the Meridian mattered, and I leaned forward without meaning to, hungry for it.

She didn't. "The offer stands," she said instead. "Tonight. Privately." And she walked to her tent with the ledger under her arm like a shield.

The fire burned on without her. People drifted apart in twos and threes, conversations dropping to murmurs, eyes doing the thing eyes do when everyone has just been told to suspect everyone. I watched the brothers confer by the gear tent, heads together. I watched one of the cooks glance at me and then away, too quickly, and felt the cold little draft of it.

That's what suspicion does. It doesn't need evidence. It just needs permission.

* * *

I found Edmund at the map table, running a headlamp over the upper corridor, and I said the thing I'd been carrying since the truck.

"For what it's worth—I don't think it was you."

He looked up. In the lamplight his face was tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "You don't know that."

"No," I admitted. "But here's what I know. Whoever filed that letter knew the route plan. The route plan lives in three places: Kline's ledger, the office copy, and your head. You've redrawn that corridor four times since the roadhouse, refining the approach, and every version's been better than the last, which is the opposite of what sabotage looks like. Saboteurs make things worse. You make things *inevitable*." I shrugged, suddenly embarrassed by my own certainty. "Also, you rerouted us around that bench above the river on the trek in, added half a day, and never said why, and I've decided that whatever the reason was, it wasn't the kind of reason a man betrays a crew over."

He'd gone very still. "You noticed the detour."

"I'm a cartographer. Noticing detours is the entire job." I hesitated. "Why *did* you reroute us?"

For a second I thought he'd tell me. Something moved behind his face—a door opening onto a lit room, then closing. "Old campsite," he said finally. "Bad ground. Flash flood history. Wasn't worth the risk."

It was a good answer. It was even probably true. It was also the answer of a man who had rehearsed it, and I filed that away next to the level voice in the regulator's office and the way he read terrain like scripture, and I decided—not knowing why, which is the honest way to decide most things—that I would take his side anyway. Loyalty isn't a conclusion. It's a wager. I'd made mine on the trek in, watching him place his feet, and the permit letter wasn't going to make me fold a hand I'd already paid into.

"Kline's going to want statements," I said.

"Then I'll give her one." He rolled the map closed with both hands, careful, deliberate. "Dev. If it comes to a vote about whether I stay on this crew—"

"It won't."

"—vote what you know, not what you feel."

"That's terrible advice. Everything worth voting on, you feel first."

Something happened at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The memory of one, maybe, kept in storage. "Get some sleep, Dev."

* * *

Sleep didn't come for any of us, I think. The wind came down off the icefield around midnight and made the tents talk, and under the wind-talk there was the other sound, the human one: boots on frozen ground, someone checking lines, someone standing too long outside someone else's tent. Trust, fracturing along old alliances, exactly the way Ruth said it would. I lay in my bag and drew the camp in my head—allies as contour lines, Kline's influence as a drainage divide, and wondered which watershed I'd wake up in.

Near dawn the wind dropped, and I heard Ruth's voice outside, low, and Edmund's answering it, and though I couldn't make out the words, I caught the tone: two people discussing a weight they'd both carried separately and were only now discovering they shared.

At first light, Kline emerged from her tent with red-rimmed eyes and the ledger, and announced that the supply run had confirmed the heli slot, the fuel was staged, and the inquiry would be answered with documentation—she'd produce the survey records herself, she said, and her smile had teeth in it. "Whoever wrote that letter wanted to slow us down," she told the assembled crew. "They've failed. We leave for the ice bridge tomorrow."

Nobody cheered. Everybody watched everybody.

And it was then, breaking camp, coiling rope with hands that wouldn't quite warm, that I saw Edmund pause at the edge of the moraine and look up at the high corridor—not the way a climber looks at a route, appraising, calculating, but the way a man looks at a house he grew up in and hasn't entered in years.

Behind him, unnoticed by everyone but me, Ruth watched him watch the mountain, and her face held something I could only call grief with a deadline attached.

Three days, I thought, without knowing why I thought it. Whatever Ruth was waiting for, she'd given it a number.

Tomorrow we climbed onto the ice.
