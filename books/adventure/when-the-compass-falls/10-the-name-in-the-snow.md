## 10. The Name in the Snow

I want it on the record that I did not go snooping.

This matters to me. In my family, snooping is a moral category. My mother once returned an unopened envelope to a neighbor because it had been misdelivered and she could tell from the weight that there was money inside, and she talked about that envelope for years the way other people talk about pilgrimages. I was raised to believe that what people keep hidden is theirs, full stop, even when what they keep hidden is obvious, even when it's practically humming.

So understand: I found the plaque because the storm made me.

We'd been pinned at the waystation for two days by a wind that seemed personally offended by our presence, and the depot—the mid-mountain one, not the halfway house further up; cartographers are precise about these things—was a timber box the size of a chapel with all the pews removed. It had been built by some mining concern in the fifties, abandoned, then adopted by the climbing community the way stray dogs adopt a porch. There were bunk frames, a rusted stove that Ruth coaxed back to life like she was persuading an old man to take his medicine, and along one wall, shelving. And on the shelving, under a tarp stiff with age, cached gear from expeditions going back decades. That's normal. Waystations are libraries of other people's ambitions. Every rope coil is a sentence somebody wrote about themselves.

Ruth sent me to inventory the cache because we were short on fuel tablets and because, I think, she wanted me out of the main room where Kline and Edmund had been conducting a conversation that wasn't a conversation. You know the kind. Two people circling each other with words so careful they might as well have been handling nitroglycerin. I'd been watching them for days and pretending I hadn't, which is its own kind of inventory work.

The tarp came off in a cloud that tasted like mouse droppings and 1970s nylon. Ropes gone brittle. A primus with a cracked tank. Tin caches, labeled in grease pencil, most of them illegible or boring: *fuel*, *spare stakes*, *DO NOT EAT (R.M.)* — whoever R.M. was, message received, forty years late.

And then a wooden crate, cedar, the kind survey equipment used to travel in, with brass corners gone green. The lid was stenciled with an expedition name I recognized before I finished reading it, because you don't grow up wanting to be a mountain cartographer without knowing the Meridian Range's whole sad liturgy.

*NAKAMURA EXPEDITION — MERIDIAN SURVEY — 1974.*

My hands went still on the lid. Not dramatic-still. The stillness of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't want to finish.

Because here's the thing about names: I'd been carrying Edmund's wrong one for six weeks. *Edmund Marsh.* He'd said it casually at the roadhouse, first day, like a man handing you a business card he'd printed himself, and I'd written it in the crew ledger in my neat cartographer's hand and never thought twice. People climb under borrowed names. Debt, divorce, disowned inheritances—you hear all three stories eventually, usually around a campfire, usually less than half true. I'd filed Edmund Marsh under *private history, do not open* and gotten on with contour lines.

But Nakamura. Nakamura was not a private name. Nakamura was a *public* name, a textbook name, the name stamped across the most famous survey in modern mountaineering history, the find that put the Meridian Range on every map printed since, the shrine discovery that got written up in journals and magazines and at least one documentary narrated by a man whose voice sounded like velvet being folded. My father had a copy of the field report. I'd read it at fourteen, sitting cross-legged on the floor of our flat in Lagos while rain hammered the shutters, and I had decided, somewhere in those pages, what I wanted my life to be about. Finding true things and drawing them honestly. That's not an exaggeration. That is a fourteen-year-old reading a legend and rearranging his entire future around it, and I have never once regretted it until this exact moment, standing in a cold shed holding a dead man's crate.

Because the Nakamura expedition ended in the ice. Four men went up past the corridor; one came down. And the son—

The son was on the crew manifests. I knew that too. Everyone who cared about the range knew that. The boy who survived because he'd been sent down with the retreat party, who grew up and vanished from the record so completely that the documentaries always had to say *his surviving son declined to comment,* which is a phrase that sounds respectful and means *we couldn't find him and he wouldn't have talked anyway.*

Edmund Marsh had hands that moved like they'd been trained by someone obsessive. Edmund Marsh read terrain the way I read it, except faster, except like he'd already seen it. Edmund Marsh rerouted us around a valley on the approach with no explanation, and I'd noticed—I notice everything, it's my job and my affliction—and said nothing, because everyone's allowed one unexplained valley.

I stood there with the crate in my hands and let the arithmetic finish.

Stenciled on the side, under the expedition name, smaller: *PROPERTY OF H. NAKAMURA. IF FOUND, RETURN TO CONCESSION OFFICE, MERIDIAN GATE.* And below that, added later in different paint, in a hand that was looser, younger, hurried: *E.N.*

E.N. Edmund Nakamura. Who had signed the cache himself, presumably, at nineteen or twenty, before the mountain took his father and he took a new name and buried both.

I should put the crate down. That was the thought. My mother's voice, actually, arriving at altitude through forty years and four thousand kilometers: *what people keep hidden is theirs.* Put it down, Dev. Inventory the fuel tablets. Go back inside and be a man who didn't know.

I put the crate down.

Then I picked it back up, because I am apparently not entirely my mother's son, and lifted the lid.

Inside: a theodolite, salt-crusted and beautiful, its brass still holding a ghost of polish. A leather case of plotting instruments. And wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom, a commemorative plaque—a casting, bronze, the kind a concession office strikes when an expedition succeeds and everyone wants something to nail to a wall. It hadn't been nailed anywhere. It had been carried up and cached, which told its own story, though I didn't read that story yet. I read the inscription instead.

*IN RECOGNITION OF THE NAKAMURA MERIDIAN SURVEY AND THE DISCOVERY OF THE HIGH SHRINE. HIDEKI NAKAMURA, LEADER. WITH THE PARTY: R. ALVAREZ, T. OKONKWO, S. BRENNAN.*

Four names. One leader, three companions. Two of whom I'd never heard of, because history keeps only the headline. One of whom was currently sitting thirty feet away in the main room drinking tea like her joints were the only thing she'd ever lost.

And no Edmund. Because Edmund had been sent down. Edmund had been *saved*, if you can call it that, and had spent whatever remained of his life deciding whether it was a rescue or an exile.

I set the plaque back exactly as I'd found it. I closed the lid. I squared the crate to the shelf the way I square everything, because precision is how I pray, and I sat down on a bunk frame in the cold room and did not move for a long time.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about meeting your hero's child: you don't feel awe. Awe is clean. What you feel is more like vertigo—the specific kind, from a ridge, when you realize the slope you've been trusting has a drop on the other side of it.

Because I had built a small cathedral to Hideki Nakamura at fourteen, and I had, without admitting it, transferred a wing of that cathedral to Edmund over six weeks of watching him work. The way he checked knots he'd already checked. The way he listened to the wind change two minutes before the rest of us heard anything. The way he'd saved Dev Okafor—saved *me*—out of that crevasse with an improvised haul system that belonged in a manual, executed by a man who supposedly packed crates for a living. I had lain awake after that night thinking: *whoever Edmund Marsh really is, he learned this somewhere serious.*

Well. Somewhere serious had a name, and I'd known it since I was fourteen, and it had been sleeping in the next bunk over calling itself Marsh.

The hurt part was stupid and I knew it was stupid even while feeling it. He owed me nothing. A man's name is his own property, and mine is Dev Okafor, and I have never once explained to a stranger why I measure everything twice. But hurt doesn't consult the intellect. Hurt just sits there like a stone in your boot, and you walk anyway, and every step reminds you.

What curdled it into something worse was the questions. They started that evening and would not stop.

If he'd wanted anonymity, fine—but why take this contract? Why come back to the range that ate his father, under a false name, working for Vesper Kline, of all people, a woman whose grudge against the Nakamura name was famous enough to have its own weather system? Did he know who she was? Of course he knew. So he'd walked into it with his eyes open, which meant either revenge or penance or something I didn't have a category for yet.

And the reroute. The valley on the approach. I'd marked it in my notes at the time—*unexplained detour, +4 km, E.M. gave no reason*—and now the note read differently. We had walked four extra kilometers around his father's last camp, and he had let us believe it was about the river crossing, and I had believed him, and the believing now felt like something that had been done to me rather than something I'd chosen.

That's the part I hated myself for. Hero worship curdling isn't really about the hero. It's about discovering that your own trust had a blind spot shaped exactly like the thing you wanted to be true.

* * *

Ruth found me in the cache room near midnight. She didn't announce herself; she just appeared in the doorway with a candle stub, silhouetted, looking like a painting of herself.

"You've been in here a long time," she said.

"Inventory takes as long as it takes."

"Mm." She came in, set the candle on the shelf, and looked at the cedar crate. Just looked at it. Then at me. Ruth Alvarez has eyes that make you feel audited. "Fuel tablets," she said. "Find any?"

"Two tins. Mostly dud."

"Shame." She picked up the candle again. At the door she stopped, without turning around, and said: "Some things in these caches belong to the mountain now. Some things are waiting for a particular person. Sometimes it's hard to tell which is which." And she left, and I understood that Ruth Alvarez knew exactly what I'd found, and had probably known where it was for forty years, and had decided—like me—to say nothing.

Which meant the secret had a population of two now. Maybe three. Maybe the whole crew was a congregation of people politely not mentioning the elephant, and only Edmund thought he was carrying it alone.

* * *

The next morning the storm broke the way storms do up there—all at once, like a door slamming open—and we struck camp and moved. I worked. That's the honest truth of it: I worked harder than usual, because work is where I keep the parts of myself I don't want conversing with others. I shot bearings. I updated the route log. I recorded the waystation in the ledger, and when I got to the line for *cache contents* I wrote *survey instruments, assorted, deteriorated* and did not write *one bronze plaque, inscribed, heavy as a verdict,* and felt the omission sit in my chest like swallowed ice.

And I watched him.

I'm not proud of it, but there it is. I watched Edmund Marsh break camp with the same meticulous hands, and I tried to see the boy who'd signed that crate, the nineteen-year-old who'd carried a commemorative plaque up a mountain and then buried it in a shed instead of nailing it to any wall. What does a person do with a trophy for a day that killed his father? You can't display it. You can't throw it away—or you can, but you don't, because throwing it away would mean deciding what it meant, and he clearly hadn't decided yet. So you cache it. You leave it at altitude, in the cold, where things keep. You leave it the way you leave a question you're not ready to answer.

He caught me watching once. Mid-morning, on the rise above the depot, wind at our backs, the range opening up ahead of us white and enormous. He looked over—he has a habit of checking on each of us in rotation, like a lighthouse—and for a second our eyes met, and I felt my face do something it shouldn't have. Not accusation. Nothing so useful as accusation. Something more like grief, which is much harder to hide, because grief leaks.

His gaze held a beat too long. I saw him register it. I saw him decide not to ask—which was, I realized, exactly what I'd been doing for six weeks, and exactly what Ruth had been doing for forty years. This crew ran on a currency of unasked questions. We were rich in it. We were drowning in it.

"Good ice ahead," he said. "Dev, I'll want your readings on the slope angle past the shoulder."

"Already have them," I said. "Thirty-one degrees. Firm under the new snow."

"Of course you do." Almost a smile. And God help me, the warmth in it landed anyway, right through the hurt, because that's the trap of heroes: they stay warm even after you find out they're made of ordinary flawed human material, and ordinary flawed human material is so much harder to stop loving than marble.

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of a moraine, and I lay in my bag listening to the wind work the flysheet, and I finally admitted the real question to myself, the one underneath all the others.

Not *why did he lie.* That one answered itself; grief lies, I've lied to strangers about smaller things than a dead father. Not *is he who I think he is*—the crate had settled that beyond argument.

The real question was: *what happens when Kline finds out?*

Because she would. I'd watched her watch him for weeks with the patience of a woman defusing something. She tested him constantly—little probes dressed as casual talk, details about the old routes, about the original expedition, and I'd thought she was just an obsessive historian of her rival. Now I understood she was fishing, and that Edmund had been dodging hooks for six weeks with a skill that suggested long practice. But nobody dodges forever. And when she landed him—if she landed him—it wouldn't be gentle. Whatever she wanted from this mountain, she wanted it *through* the Nakamura name, and the name was standing right there wearing a borrowed coat.

Should I warn him?

I turned the idea over. Warning him meant telling him I knew, which meant confessing to the crate, which meant the whole architecture of polite silence coming down on both our heads at eleven thousand feet in a season running out of days. Not warning him meant letting him walk blind toward a woman who had bought our contracts the way you buy tools.

In the end I did neither. I did what cartographers do when the map runs out: I kept walking and kept recording.

Because the next morning, breaking camp, I saw him stand for a moment facing up-valley—toward the corridor, toward whatever was left of the shrine, toward whatever his father had found or claimed to find—and I saw his mouth move. Just slightly. Just once. Like a man saying a single word to the wind, too quiet for anyone to catch.

And I thought: *he's getting ready to use it. The real name. He's practicing.*

The permits expire in days. The passes will close behind us. And somewhere above us, in the ice, waited the thing that had made and unmade two reputations before I was born.

I tightened my pack straps, shot one last bearing, and followed Edmund Nakamura up the mountain.
