## 11. Halfway House

The depot announced itself the way old men announce themselves at parties: by taking up more space than anyone expected and offering no apology for it.

We'd been climbing for six days when the ridge opened onto the shelf where the Meridian Expedition Company had built its mid-mountain waystation, back when companies still built things on mountains instead of just extracting them. It was a squat timber box bolted to the rock like a barnacle, half-swallowed by decades of snowpack, with a corrugated roof that had rusted the color of dried blood and a door that hung at an angle no carpenter would defend. The original expeditions had used it as a staging point. Nobody had used it for anything since.

Nobody, it turned out, was a word I should have interrogated more carefully.

"Smoke," Dev said, and I felt the word land on the whole crew like a dropped pack.

He was right. A thin gray thread rose from the stovepipe, straightening in the still air, and my stomach performed a maneuver I can only describe as a controlled descent without a rope. Smoke meant fire. Fire meant people. People meant someone had climbed this route ahead of us, through terrain we'd spent six days treating as unbroken, and had done it recently enough to keep a stove fed.

Ruth stopped walking. I heard her stop—the particular crunch-and-silence of a woman making a decision with her boots—and when I turned, she was looking at me with an expression I'd been dreading since base camp. Not accusation. Worse. Confirmation.

"You knew," she said. Not a question. Ruth Alvarez didn't waste questions on things she'd already priced.

"I knew as much as you did," I said, which was true, and which was also the kind of truth that curdles in the mouth, because I had known *something*. I'd known Kline ran this operation like a chess problem. I'd known the permits, the timeline, the bonus structured to make refusal feel like cowardice. I'd known all of it and climbed anyway, telling myself the oldest lie in the mountaineering book: *I'm just here for the money.*

The lie was getting heavy. Everything up here got heavy. That was the one law the Meridian Range enforced without appeal.

* * *

We approached the depot the way you approach anything that might be a problem: spread out, quiet, with Dev hanging back to guard the sledges and Ruth moving with a economy that reminded me, not for the first time, that she'd been on this mountain before, in a year when the mountain took more than it gave back.

The door was ajar. Inside, the stove was burning low and steady—someone had banked it properly, the way you bank a fire when you mean to come back. Two bunks had been cleared of debris. A coil of modern rope hung by the door, dry and unfrayed. And on the table, weighted under a carabiner, sat a sealed cache box with a logistics tag still bright and plastic and obscene against all that old timber.

The tag bore three initials, stenciled in the tidy corporate font of Kline Expeditionary Logistics: **V.K.**

"Well," said Tomas, our rigging man, who had a gift for stating the obvious at the moment it became unbearable. "Well, hell."

I opened the box because someone had to. Inside: high-altitude rations, a spare burner, two canisters of fuel, a medical kit with the seal broken and re-taped, and a route log—a proper one, hardbound, with entries in a hand I didn't recognize. The last entry was dated nineteen days before our permits even began.

*Route viable to the col. Icefall stable but shifting on the east margin. Marked cairns at intervals. Scout proceeding to lower caves. Will report by radio on schedule.*

Lower caves. My hands went still on the page. The lower ice caves were above the corridor of falls, in terrain we'd budgeted nine days to reach. This scout had been there. Had stood where my father had stood, or close to it, and had written about it in the flat administrative language of a man doing a job, the way you might describe a church or a crime scene.

"Edmund." Ruth's voice. "The radio."

There was a radio on the shelf. Of course there was. The scout had reported on schedule; Kline had known every step of our route before we'd taken it, had known the icefall's mood, had known—my eyes went back to the log, to the phrase I'd already read three times—had known about the caves.

The stove ticked. Snow squeaked under someone's boot outside. And I stood in a waystation my father's generation had built, holding evidence that the woman who'd bought our contract had never once needed us to find anything. We weren't climbers to her. We were instruments—calibrated, insured, expendable—sent to do the dangerous part of a job her scout had already scouted, so that if the mountain kept anyone, it would keep us.

I'd spent ten years being nobody's instrument. I recognized the feeling the way you recognize an old scar in a mirror.

* * *

Kline arrived forty minutes behind us, moving fast, which told me she'd known exactly what we'd find and had timed her arrival to be present for the discovery—or rather, to be present for the moment after the discovery, when the anger was still too cold to be useful.

She came through the door with snow on her shoulders and her chin up, and I'll grant her this: she didn't flinch. Vesper Kline had a face built for boardrooms and inquests, and she deployed it now at full capacity.

"You went through my cache," she said.

"Your cache," Ruth repeated. She said it the way you'd handle a live wire. "On a public route. Stocked before our permits opened. By a scout you sent up *alone*, in early season, through the icefall you told us was unassessed."

"The icefall *was* unassessed. Now it's assessed. You're welcome."

"You used him as a probe." Dev had come in behind her, and there was something in his voice I'd never heard there before—not the easy warmth he offered everyone, not the hero-worship I'd caught him aiming at me when he thought I wasn't looking. Something harder. "One man, solo, early season. What was the contingency if he'd gone into a crevasse, Ms. Kline? A strongly worded memo?"

"The contingency was that Marcus Voss is the best solo alpinist I've ever hired and he knew the risks he was being paid for." She said it evenly, but I watched her thumb press white against the table's edge. "Every one of you knew the risks too. You signed the same documents. Nobody on this crew read a single line about safety that didn't apply to him."

"That's not the same," Dev said.

"No," Kline agreed, and for a moment something moved behind her eyes—something old and tired and furious. "It isn't. He had less company."

The silence that followed had texture. I let it sit. Then I set the route log on the table between us, open to the last entry, and watched her read the words *lower caves* in her own scout's handwriting while the whole crew watched her read them.

"Tell them," I said. "Or I will, and I'll be less generous."

"Generous." She almost laughed. "You want generosity from me, Mr.—" and there it was, the half-second hitch, the name she'd almost said, the name she'd been not-saying since the roadhouse. Ruth saw it. Dev saw it. I felt it pass through the room like wind through a tent.

"You sent him to the caves," I said. "You already know what's up there. So the question the rest of us are asking—and you'll answer it, Vesper, because the permits, the bonus, all of it, that was the leash, and the leash just broke—is what exactly did you hire six people to walk in front of?"

She looked at the log for a long time. Outside, the light was doing the thing high-altitude light does in late afternoon, going from gold to steel without an intermediate stop. When she finally spoke, her voice had dropped its boardroom finish, and what was underneath was worse. It was sincere.

"I hired you to confirm it," she said. "Voss found markings. Survey marks, old ones, high in the caves. He couldn't document them properly—his hands were too cold, his camera failed, and he was alone and smart enough to know his limits. But he saw enough. And if those marks are what I think they are, then everything I have spent thirty years saying about this mountain, and about the man who claimed to find a shrine here, is either going to be proven—" she stopped. Recalibrated. "The world will want more than one witness's word. It will want a crew. It will want names. That's what I hired."

"You hired ballast," Ruth said quietly. "Witnesses you could spend."

"I hired *credibility*," Kline snapped, and then, with visible effort, took the anger back down like a climber taking up slack. "You want the ugly version? Fine. The ugly version is that no institution on earth will reopen an expedition file on the word of Vesper Kline, because Vesper Kline is, per the record, a disgraced fraud who accused a beloved dead man of fabrication and couldn't prove it. The ugly version is that I have been right for thirty years and it has been worth exactly nothing, because being right requires evidence and evidence requires—" she gestured at the room, at all of us, at the whole rigged apparatus of respectability—"*witnesses*. People the world believes. So yes. I bought a crew. I bought a route. I bought the son of the man who ruined me, though he doesn't know I know, and—" she caught herself, one beat too late.

The stove ticked. Dev looked at me. Ruth had been looking at me for days.

"Though he doesn't know you know," I said.

"Edmund." Ruth's voice, low. A warning, or permission. Hard to say.

Kline and I looked at each other across the route log, across thirty years of her grudge and ten of my hiding, and I understood suddenly that she had known since the roadhouse—since before, maybe, since the letter, since she'd bought our contract specifically because I was on it—and that every test, every pointed question, every moment of her watching me work had been her turning the evidence over in her hands.

"Say it," I told her. "You've been carrying it around like contraband. Say it in front of everyone, or I will."

"Edmund Nakamura," she said. "Son of Kenji Nakamura. Who led the Meridian expedition in the year the mountain killed him and two others, and came back—at least, his notes came back—describing a shrine that no subsequent survey has ever located. The find that made his name." She said it flatly, without triumph, and somehow that was the cruelest part. She hadn't wanted the reveal. She'd wanted the *site*. The reveal was just a door she'd had to walk through. "I needed his son on this mountain because if anyone alive can read those marks, can confirm the route, can authenticate what's in those caves—it's the man his father taught to survey before he could ride a bike."

Dev made a small sound. I didn't look at him. I would have to, eventually. Not yet.

* * *

They gave me an hour, which I didn't ask for and didn't refuse. I took it outside, on the shelf of rock below the depot, where the wind came off the col with a sound like a crowd deciding something.

The truth was I'd been rehearsing this moment for ten years and had never gotten past the first line. *My name is Edmund Nakamura.* Four words. I'd said them to Ruth at base camp in the dark, and she'd said *I know, I was there*, and that had been the whole ceremony. Now it was out in the open air, and the strange thing was how little it changed. The mountain didn't care. The wind didn't care. My father had climbed under this same sky carrying a name he'd spent his whole life making mean something, and the mountain had killed him anyway, and then let the world argue about what he'd found.

I thought about what Kline had said—*the man his father taught to survey*—and felt the strange double weight of it. She'd hired my grief. She'd weaponized my inheritance. And the worst part, the part I kept turning over like a stone looking for the clean side, was that it had worked. I was here. I'd led them here. Every detour I'd taken to avoid my father's last camp, every quiet reroute, every night I'd told myself it was just money—I'd been walking a route she'd drawn, toward a verdict she'd already decided she needed, and I'd called it free will because the pay was good.

But.

There was always a but. It was the only thing I'd inherited from him that I actually liked.

But the scout's log said *lower caves*, and Kline had said *markings*, and neither of them—not Voss, not Vesper, not thirty years of her obsession—had stood where I was going to stand and read my father's handwriting in the ice. Whatever Kline wanted from those caves, she wanted it as a verdict. I was going to get something else. I could feel it the way you feel weather coming: my father had left something up there, and it wasn't a shrine, and it wasn't proof, and it wasn't for her.

I went back inside. The crew was waiting in the kind of silence that has weight and temperature. Dev sat with his arms crossed, and when our eyes met he didn't look away, and what was in his face wasn't betrayal exactly. It was the expression of a man who has just learned the map he's been carrying was drawn by someone with a reason to lie, and is deciding, right now, in real time, whether that makes the map useless.

Ruth stood by the stove. She'd put water on. That was Ruth—grief, rage, apocalypse, there'd be tea, because the body still needed things and pretending otherwise was how people died on mountains.

"Permits expire in nine days," I said. "The scout's log puts viable route to the col, and the icefall's east margin is shifting, which means the west margin—the one we're on—is the one that holds. Kline sent a man ahead of us because she wanted witnesses, not leaders." I looked at her. She held my look. "Fine. Then she can have both. We push for the caves, we document everything, and what comes down the mountain comes down in the open—every finding, every record, every name. Including hers. Including mine. Including my father's."

"That's not what I contracted," Kline said.

"No," I agreed. "You contracted ballast."

The stove hissed. Somewhere above us, past the col, past the corridor of falls, the ice held whatever it held, and the first flakes of the season's real snow had begun to come down—small, dry, patient, like the mountain counting us.
