## 12. Split Route

The permit clock ran out in four days, and the mountain knew it before we did. You could feel it in the way the light came off the ice—thinner, more transactional, like the sun had started keeping its own ledger and we were overdrawn.

We held the vote at the mid-mountain depot, which was less a building than a rumor of one: a corrugated shelter bolted to the rock, half its roof peeled back by some winter with opinions. Kline stood at the far end with her arms crossed, wearing the expression of a woman who had already counted the votes and found them disappointing. Ruth sat on a supply crate with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she wasn't drinking. Dev hovered near the stove, radiating the particular anxiety of a young man who has recently learned his hero is a stranger and hasn't decided yet whether that's better or worse.

Two of us were hurt. Osei's ankle had turned on the descent from the ice bridge—a bad turn, the kind that swells overnight into something you can't argue with—and Marta's fingers hadn't come back from the cold properly. She kept flexing them like she could negotiate with the frostbite, and the frostbite, being frostbite, was not negotiating.

"Here's the arithmetic," Kline said. "The concession expires Friday. After that, anything above this line is trespass, and trespass means confiscation, fines, and my name dragged through whatever's left of it." She said *my name* the way other people say *my child*. "The shrine site is two days' hard push. The descent is one day if the weather holds, three if it doesn't. We can't do both."

"We can split," Dev said.

"We can *die* split," Kline corrected. "But yes. That's the option on the table."

She laid it out flat, no varnish: anyone who wanted out could walk down with Osei and Marta, no penalty, no questions. Anyone who stayed was staying past the paper edge of legality, into the part of the map where the only law is what your legs will agree to. She didn't dress it up as glory. I'll give her that much. Vesper Kline has never once tried to make a bad idea sound good; she just stands next to it and dares you to notice how bad it is.

"I'll take the injured down," said Tomas, our rope gun, who had been quietly competent for six weeks and wanted nothing except to keep being so. "No offense to anybody's shrine."

"None taken," Kline said, in a voice suggesting several offenses taken.

That left the question hanging over the rest of us like weather.

* * *

Ruth voted first, which surprised nobody who'd been paying attention, and nobody had been paying attention longer than Ruth Alvarez.

"Staying," she said. She looked at her tea instead of at any of us. "I walked off this mountain once already. Forty years ago. I'm not doing it twice."

"You don't owe this mountain anything," I said.

"That's exactly why," she said. "Debts you can pay off. This isn't a debt."

I understood her completely and wished I didn't. There's a kind of guilt that doesn't want absolution—it wants a return trip. Absolution is a door closing. A return trip is a door you get to stand in front of, cold air coming through the gap, telling yourself you're only looking.

Dev voted stay next, fast, before he could think better of it. "Staying." Then, quieter, glancing at me: "Somebody should document it properly. If we find anything." He'd been saying things like that for two days now—half field notes, half test. Checking whether I'd flinch at the word *properly*. I hadn't flinched. I was saving up all my flinching for later, apparently, because I was about to spend the whole account at once.

Tomas, Osei, and Marta would go down. Five of us would go up. The vote took four minutes. The silence afterward took considerably longer.

Kline looked around the shelter, counting heads like a woman auditing a theft. "Five," she said. "Against a storm window of seventy-two hours, on an unmarked route, past the legal line." A pause. "Good."

It was the most honest thing she'd said in weeks. Not *thank you*, not *brave choice*—just *good*, the way you'd say it about a hand of cards. Vesper Kline doesn't see people; she sees positions on a board. I used to think that made her monstrous. Standing there, watching her recalculate our odds with something that might have been tenderness if you squinted hard enough through the wrong end of a telescope, I decided it might just be the only way she could carry what she carried. You can't grieve a career for thirty years and still look at people straight on. Something has to give, and usually it's the looking.

* * *

We spent the evening packing for the push, and packing is its own kind of confession. Everything you leave behind is a thing you've admitted you can't afford to want. I went through my kit twice, weighing each item against the altitude it would have to be carried to, and ended up with a pile of discards that told a story: spare socks, the second stove, a paperback I'd been pretending to read since base camp, and—the hardest one—a coil of rope I'd been carrying for reasons I hadn't let myself examine.

Except I did examine it, sitting there under the broken roof while the others slept. It was my father's rope. Or rather, rope of his vintage, bought at his recommendation, from a shop he'd written about in a letter I still had in a box in a storage unit in a city I'd been avoiding for a decade. I'd brought it because bringing it felt like loyalty. I left it behind because leaving it felt like honesty. The mountain doesn't care what brand hauled your father up its face. The mountain cares about weight.

I kept the compass, though. Of course I kept the compass. Some weights you carry precisely because they're heavy.

* * *

Morning came gray and windless, which every mountaineer knows is not good news but merely postponed news. We got the walking wounded moving by seven. Marta hugged each of us in turn, quick and fierce, and then held onto me a beat too long.

"Come back down," she said. "Both halves of you."

I laughed, because it was either laugh or answer the question underneath the question, and I wasn't ready to answer it. I'm still not sure I was ready an hour later when the descent party dropped below the shoulder and out of sight, and it was just the five of us and the route and the enormous indifferent white.

Kline set a pace that was technically suicidal and practically correct. We moved on glacier, then on rock, then on the blue-gray ice of the upper tongue where the route stopped being a route and became a negotiation. Ruth climbed like punctuation—short sentences, no wasted motion, a period at the top of every pitch. Dev climbed like a man reading. He'd stop at features, photograph them, mutter coordinates. Somewhere in the last two days he had transformed from a kid who was excited to be here into a professional who intended to survive being here, and I suspected—I knew—which of us he blamed for the tuition.

We roped up on the icefield. Kline led, I took second, and for six hundred vertical meters we moved in a rhythm so clean it felt almost like forgiveness. That's the trap of climbing, if you let it be. The body solves problems the heart refuses to touch, and for a while you mistake the quiet for peace.

At the col below the shrine approach, we stopped to eat, and Kline looked at the sky, and the sky looked back with an expression I didn't like.

"Sixty hours," she said. "Maybe fifty-five."

"Then we move now," I said.

"We've been moving now."

"Then we move *more* now."

She almost smiled. On her face it looked like a crack in a dam—interesting, and slightly alarming, and suggestive of large forces behind it.

* * *

It happened at the start of the final push, on the shoulder where the route bent east toward the corridor. Kline called a halt to check the horizon, and Ruth took the chance to say, quietly, in that voice she uses for things she's decided are true:

"When we get up there, boy, you're going to have to tell them."

"Tell them what?" Dev asked, twenty feet away, not even turning around. Which meant he knew. Which meant he'd known for days, and had been waiting—not for the information, but for me to hand it over myself.

The wind picked at our backs like it was hurrying us along. I looked at the route ahead, at the corridor of falls where meltwater had frozen mid-descent into a staircase of blue glass, at the place where my father had last been a living man instead of a story people argued about. And I thought about what Ruth had told me above the tree line—that he'd sent the retreat party down and pushed on, that survival and abandonment had shaken hands on this exact shoulder and neither one had washed since.

My father chose the mountain over the exit. For ten years I'd chosen the exit over everything, and called it prudence, and signed other men's names to my work so that no one would ever look at mine and see him standing behind it.

There are worse inheritances than a flaw. The worse one is the hiding.

"Dev," I said.

He turned. Ruth turned. Even Kline turned, though she pretended to still be studying the horizon, and I understood in that moment that she had known longer than any of them—had probably known since the roadhouse, since the letter, maybe since before the letter—and had bought my crew's contract partly to watch what I'd do with the truth when it finally caught me. Vesper Kline doesn't run experiments. She runs *evidence*.

"My name isn't Edmund Marsh," I said. "It's Edmund Nakamura. Kenji Nakamura was my father."

The words came out easier than I'd expected, which offended me slightly. Ten years of construction, and the demolition took nine seconds.

Dev's face did several things in sequence—confirmation, hurt, awe, and then a fourth thing I couldn't name until later, when I realized it was relief. He'd been carrying the secret for days, alone, and you could watch the weight transfer across the space between us like a load shifted between climbers.

"I know," he said. "The plaque at the waystation. Your father's expedition photo." A pause. "You rerouted us past his last camp on the approach. I checked the old survey maps that night."

Of course he had. I'd hired a cartographer and then lied to him with terrain, as if terrain were somehow exempt from the rules.

"Why hide it?" he asked, and it wasn't hostile. It was genuinely puzzled, the way only a young person can be puzzled by cowardice—having not yet committed enough of their own to recognize the architecture.

"Because his name opened doors I wanted shut," I said. "And closed doors I wanted open. Mostly because—" I stopped. Looked up the route. "Because everyone who says his name wants something from him. The truth, the myth, the vindication, the ruin. I didn't want to be another one."

"And now?"

"Now I'm going up there to find out what he actually did," I said. "Not what any of us need him to have done. And I'd rather do it under my own name than borrowed one."

Kline finally turned from the horizon. Her eyes were bright and terrible and, for one unguarded instant, young.

"Took you long enough," she said.

"You knew."

"I've known since you tied off that crevasse anchor with a Nakamura friction hitch," she said. "Your father invented it. He wrote it up in a journal that cited *me* as the failed comparison case." She smiled without warmth. "I've spent thirty years reading everything he touched. You tie knots like him. You walk away from things like him, too—or you did."

The wind gusted, hard, from the west, carrying the smell of snow that hadn't fallen yet but had filed the paperwork.

"Fifty hours," Ruth said, checking nothing in particular, meaning everything.

I clipped into the rope. My name sat inside my chest like a stone I'd finally agreed to carry instead of hide, and I want to be honest about this: it did not feel like liberation. It felt like a burden, willingly shouldered, which is the only kind worth having. The mountain ahead of us held whatever my father had found or fabricated or both, and somewhere in the ice caves above, his handwriting was waiting to speak in his voice, and I was going to have to listen as his son instead of as a stranger, which is infinitely harder.

"Edmund," Dev said, testing it, trying it on. Then, grinning despite everything: "Lead on, Nakamura."

I led.

Behind us, the first flakes of the early storm came down the col like scouts sent ahead of an army, and above us the corridor of falls glittered in the failing light—blue, vertical, and sealed at the top by something that was going to make the climb up look like the easy part.
