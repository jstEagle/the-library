## 13. The Corridor of Falls

There are two kinds of cold, my grandfather used to say. The kind that makes you move faster, and the kind that makes you stupid. He was a fisherman off Lagos before he was anything else, and he said it the way men say things they've paid for.

By the second hour in the corridor, I had stopped being able to tell which kind I was feeling.

The push team was five: Edmund leading, Ruth on his rope, Kline behind her with the grim economy of a woman who'd bought this mountain and intended to collect, me in fourth position with the survey gear, and Tomas Reyes sweeping. We'd left the others at the depot at dawn—Halvorsen and Priya walking the injured out with the descent sled and most of our collective good sense—and now we were committed to a slot of ice no wider than a two-lane road, walled on both sides by frozen waterfalls stacked like organ pipes, blue-green and groaning.

That's the thing nobody tells you about ice. It talks. All day, all night, a low conversational creak, like a house settling, like something enormous turning over in its sleep and deciding, for now, not to wake.

"Pitch four," Edmund called back, his voice flattened by the walls into something intimate and strange, as if he were speaking directly into my ear from thirty meters up. "Mixed climbing. Watch the rock band on the left—it's loose. Trust the ice instead."

Trust the ice. Sure. The ice that was actively refreezing behind us.

I want to be precise about that, because it matters to what happened later. When we'd come through the lower corridor that morning, meltwater had been running down the walls in ropes, dripping off the seracs in a steady percussion, *tink tink tink*, like a clock counting down something we hadn't agreed to. By noon the percussion had slowed. By one o'clock it had stopped. And by the time Edmund anchored pitch four and Ruth started up, the water we'd walked through—the shallow stream bed at the corridor floor, the seeps between the falls—had gone glassy and still, sealing over like a wound closing.

Ruth noticed first. She always noticed first; she just rationed out what she did about it.

"Dev." She didn't turn around. She was standing on the ice ledge below Edmund's anchor, hands on hips, looking back down the way we'd come. "Come look at this."

I came. I looked. Where the corridor narrowed half a kilometer below us, there had been a gap—a chimney of open air between two falls, maybe three meters wide, that we'd squeezed through with our packs turned sideways. It was still there. But the edges of it had thickened. The water sheeting down both walls had frozen mid-pour, building outward, and the gap had gone from three meters to something closer to two since breakfast.

"It does that every afternoon," Kline said behind us. She'd climbed up while we stood there, silent as ever on her feet for a big woman, and now she stood with her arms crossed, breath steaming. "Freezes at night, thaws by midmorning. Standard diurnal cycle."

"And if the temperature doesn't come back up?" Ruth asked. "If that front the forecast flagged arrives early?"

Kline's jaw did something small and hard. "Then we're climbing fast."

*We're*, I noted. Not *you*. Somewhere between the depot vote and here, Vesper Kline had quietly reclassified herself from owner to crew member, and none of us had been consulted. I decided not to point it out. There's a time for pointing things out, and it is generally not when you are standing under several thousand tons of frozen waterfall with a storm front somewhere over your shoulder.

Edmund rappelled down to us, boots first, upside down for a moment against the blue wall, and I watched him read the corridor the way he read everything—not looking at it so much as listening to it. Ten years I've been drawing maps, and I can tell you that some people look at terrain and some people *interrogate* it. Edmund interrogated. His eyes went down the corridor, then up, then to the sky, which was doing something I didn't love: going the flat, pearly, featureless white that means weather has stopped being a place and become a lid.

"We camp at the top of pitch six," he said. "Tonight's freeze will set the lower pitches solid—that's actually good for the descent, if there is one. Tomorrow we make the upper corridor in one push."

"If there is one," Ruth repeated, dry as chalk. "You heard him, Vesper. If."

"I heard him." Kline looked at Edmund, and for a moment neither of them blinked, and I got the distinct sensation of watching two people conduct an entire negotiation in a language consisting entirely of eye contact. Then Kline shouldered her pack. "Lead on, Nakamura."

She said the name lightly. Casually. Like it was nothing.

Except Nakamura wasn't the name on any of our contracts. The name on our contracts was Ed Marsh, route consultant, a man whose references checked out in the way that carefully built things check out. And except that three nights ago, at the Halfway House depot, I had found a brass expedition plaque bolted inside a cache locker—*MERIDIAN RANGE SCIENTIFIC EXPEDITION, 1974, E. NAKAMURA, FIELD LEADER*—and I had done the arithmetic, and the arithmetic had a face, and the face was currently chalking up for pitch five ten meters above my head.

I hadn't told anyone. I don't fully know why. Partly because Ruth clearly already knew—you don't spend thirty years not talking to a man unless you know exactly who he is. Partly because Dev Okafor, cartographer, twenty-six years old, had spent his entire professional life with a photograph of E. Nakamura taped inside his field kit lid, next to his grandmother's picture, because his grandmother had worked the 1974 expedition's base camp kitchen and used to say that Nakamura was the only muzungu who ever learned to cook ugali properly. Hero worship, my mother would have called it, and warned me about it, and been right.

But mostly I hadn't told anyone because watching Edmund climb was like watching someone speak a language fluently after pretending for years to be mute, and I wanted—I needed—to understand why a man that good had spent a decade being no one.

So I kept the plaque in my chest pocket where the map case usually lived, and I climbed.

* * *

Pitch six put us on a shelf the size of a tennis court, walled on the north by a fall we named the Organ—Ruth named it, actually, "because it looks like one and it moans like one"—and open to the south, where the corridor bent east toward the upper mountain. We got tents up in the last flat light. The temperature fell the way it does up high, all at once, like a trapdoor opening, and by full dark the mercury on my kit thermometer read minus nineteen and dropping.

It was during first watch that I heard the sound.

Not the Organ's moaning—that I'd gotten used to, a low bass note that rose when the wind funneled through the falls. This was different. Thinner. Rhythmic. Coming from up-corridor, around the bend, where the shelf gave onto a field of broken seracs that even Edmund's headlamp couldn't fully interrogate.

A whistle. Three notes, repeated. Weak, but deliberate. The distress signal from the concession office's own protocol card, the one Kline had made us memorize in week one, back when we were all still pretending this was a job and not a reckoning.

I woke Edmund. I didn't decide to; my hand was on his shoulder before my brain finished the risk assessment, which tells you something about where my loyalties had gotten to without my permission.

He listened for exactly one repetition, then said, "Kline. Your scout?"

She was already sitting up in her bag, already awake, which answered its own question. Her face in the lamplight did something complicated—hope and dread fighting for the same real estate—and then she was out of the tent and pulling boots on with hands that weren't quite steady.

"He went up eleven days ago," she said. "I told you he'd gone to ground in the lower valley."

"You told us he'd gone home," Ruth said, zipping her jacket with terrible calm. "You told us a lot of things, Vesper."

"He's alive," Kline said, and her voice cracked on *alive* in a way that no ledger, no contract, no decades-long grudge could account for. "Eleven days. In this. He's—" She stopped. Recalibrated. Put the mask back on, but late, and we'd all seen underneath. "Three-note signal. That's the protocol. He's mobile enough to signal."

"Or he cached a whistle and something else is blowing it," Ruth said.

Nobody liked that sentence. Nobody said so either.

Edmund was already roping up. "Four of us go. One stays with the gear—Tomas, you've got watch. Short rope spacing, it's broken ground. Dev, bring the medical kit and the bivy sack. Ruth—"

"I'm coming," Ruth said, in the tone of a woman informing a mountain of a fact it had failed to grasp.

Edmund looked at her for a moment. Something passed between them—thirty-some years' worth of something—and then he nodded, and we went up into the dark.

* * *

We found Anselm Petrie forty minutes later, wedged in the lee of a serac like a letter slipped under a door.

I'll describe him honestly, because what I felt deserves the context. He was alive. That was the miracle part, and it took a moment to land, because everything else about him argued for the opposite conclusion. He was curled around his own core heat, both hands bare and blackened to the second knuckle, face frost-nipped gray-white across the cheeks and nose, lips split, eyes crusted. His parka was expedition grade but he'd lost his pack somewhere—no sleeping bag, no stove, no tent. Eleven days. At altitude. In temperatures that had spent three of those nights below minus twenty-five.

And he was talking. Had been talking, apparently, for some time, to the serac, to himself, to whoever came. His voice was a ruin, a whisper dragged over gravel, but it ran in loops, and the loop was this:

"The markings. The markings in the ice. They're inside. They're *inside* the ice, do you understand, they're not carved on it, they're *in* it, deep, deep in the blue, you have to get the light low, you have to get the sun low and look *through*—"

"Anselm." Kline dropped to her knees beside him, and I watched Vesper Kline—owner, buyer, instrument-maker—take the man's ruined hands in hers as gently as if they were made of paper. "Anselm, it's Vesper. Look at me."

His eyes found her and focused, barely. "You sent me to find the approach. You didn't say—" A cough that wracked his whole frame. "You didn't say the mountain was *marked*. She marked it. The whole route, it's surveyed, it's *hers*, it's—"

"She?" Edmund asked. Quietly. Too quietly.

Petrie's eyes slid to Edmund and something flickered there—recognition or delirium, impossible to say. "Nakamura," he whispered. "The old notes. The old notes say Nakamura. They're everywhere. Every pitch, every anchor point, every safe line through the icefalls—he surveyed it all, he mapped the whole corridor, and the shrine chamber, the chamber is—" His voice climbed, fraying. "It's *in* the ice. The markings are IN the ICE—"

"Shock," Ruth said, already cutting away his bootlaces with the calm of a woman who had done this in worse places. "Hypothermic, dehydrated, frostbite third degree at minimum on both hands, probably the feet too. He's been breathing his own CO2 in this hollow, that's half the babbling." She looked up at Edmund. "He's not wrong, though. Whatever he saw, he saw it."

"He's been up here eleven days," Kline said. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a woman carrying something breakable. "The approach I gave him was three days' work. What was he doing?"

"Reading," Edmund said.

One word. And the way he said it made the hair stand up on my arms under two layers of fleece. Because it wasn't a guess. It was recognition. Edmund Nakamura knew exactly what his father's survey marks looked like in low light through blue ice, and he had known since before Petrie ever whistled, and possibly since before any of us signed anything.

Kline looked at Edmund. Edmund looked at Kline. And I stood there holding the medical kit, holding the plaque in my chest pocket, holding a dead man's name in my mouth like a stone I couldn't swallow or spit out, and understood that everyone on this shelf except poor frostbitten Anselm Petrie was keeping the same secret from the same people for different reasons, and that the mountain had arranged us into this exact configuration on purpose, the way ice arranges itself into a crevasse: patiently, and with intent.

"We carry him down?" Tomas's voice crackled over the radio from camp, tinny and hopeful.

Edmund considered. I watched him consider—watched his eyes go down the corridor, where the refreeze had been working all evening, sealing our retreat pitch by pitch; watched them go up, toward whatever waited above the serac field; watched them rest, finally, on Petrie's blackened hands.

"Down is closing," he said. "And he needs a stove and a warm bag within the hour, not within six. We take him up to camp, stabilize him tonight." A pause. "Tomorrow we go where he was going."

"Edmund." Ruth's voice, low. "The permits expire in four days. The front is—"

"I know what the front is doing, Ruth."

"Do you also know what *you're* doing?" she asked, and there was no cruelty in it, only the weariness of a woman who had buried one Nakamura already and recognized the family resemblance in how a man stands at the mouth of a corridor.

Edmund didn't answer right away. He looked at Petrie—at the wreck of a man Kline had sent up this mountain alone, ahead of us, as a disposable instrument—and then at Kline, who bore the look without flinching, which was somehow worse than flinching.

"I'm doing what the marks say," he said finally. "They go up. They've always gone up."

* * *

We carried Petrie into camp near midnight, strapped into the bivy sack like cargo, his whistle—found clenched in his teeth, God help us, he'd blown until his lips froze to the metal—still hanging from its lanyard. We got him into Ruth's tent with heat packs and warm sugar water, and Ruth took first medical watch, and Kline sat outside her own tent for a long time afterward, alone, facing up-corridor, not moving, a monument to whatever it is a person becomes when the thing they've chased for forty years turns out to have been waiting with the lights on.

I couldn't sleep. So I did what I always do when I can't sleep: I drew.

Sat in my tent with the map board on my knees and charted the day's ground by headlamp, the pitches, the anchors, the serac field, Petrie's hollow. And when I finished the corridor, my pen kept going, upward, into the blank space above the seracs where my own survey ended and the unknown began—and I found I'd drawn, without deciding to, a small neat square, and labeled it.

*Chamber?*

Underneath, before I could stop myself, in letters I immediately wanted to cross out:

*What did he find? What did he say he found? Which one is true?*

Because that was the question now, wasn't it. Not whether the shrine existed—Petrie's babbling, the marks in the ice, Edmund's face had settled that. The question was the one Kline had crossed a lifetime to ask and Edmund had crossed a decade to avoid, and tomorrow, past the serac field, past the last of the known ground, one of them was going to get an answer.

Outside, the wind shifted. Came around to the north. Carried with it the first fine grains of the front, ticking against the fly like sand, like a clock, like fingers drumming.

The Organ moaned. The ice talked. And somewhere above us, in the blue dark, the markings waited—inside the ice, deep, deep in the blue—for the light to lie down low enough to read them.
