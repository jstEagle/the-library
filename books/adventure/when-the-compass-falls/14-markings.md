## 14. Markings

The ice caves announce themselves before you see them. There's a sound the mountain makes when it's hollow underneath—a kind of held breath, a resonance in your boot soles that says *careful, there's nothing under this but a rumor of floor*. I'd heard it once before, twenty years ago and forty pounds lighter, standing at my father's shoulder while he tapped the ice with his axe handle like a doctor percussing a chest.

I didn't tell anyone I recognized the sound. That was becoming a habit with me: recognition, then silence. My whole adult life arranged as a series of things I knew and didn't say.

We'd come up through the corridor of falls behind Kline's scout—Marek, frostbitten to the wrists, babbling half-sense about markings inside the ice—and now we stood at the cave mouth while our headlamps argued with the blue dark. The entrance was a slit in the glacier's flank, maybe shoulder-width, breathing cold air outward in slow pulses, like something enormous asleep.

"Airflow's good," Ruth said, which was her way of saying *it won't kill us immediately*. She'd been saying things like that since the corridor. Her version of comfort had always been arithmetic.

Kline stood apart, working her gloved fingers, watching me. She'd been watching me for days, and I'd been pretending not to notice, which is its own kind of performance—one I was getting tired of staging.

"Edmund," she said. Not Nakamura. Not the borrowed surname from the contract. Just Edmund, said quietly, like a key trying a lock.

"After you," I said, and ducked into the mountain.

* * *

The lower caves were cathedral country. Our lamps found walls of ice so deep a blue they looked lit from inside, ancient pressure bubbles hanging in them like trapped weather. The floor sloped gently upward, polished by meltwater in some earlier century, and the sound of us moved ahead of our bodies—six sets of boots echoing back down the throat of the glacier, so that we sounded like twelve people, or twenty, an expedition instead of what was left of one.

Dev kept close to my shoulder. He'd been keeping close since the waystation, since the plaque, though he still hadn't said anything, and I hadn't asked him what he knew. Some conversations you postpone because you're not sure either party will survive them.

"Markings should be ahead," Kline said. "Marek came out raving about them. Symbols on the walls."

"Ancient ones?" Dev asked.

"That's what we're here to find out."

That was true, in the way Kline's statements were always true—the way a ledger is true. It recorded exactly what she wanted recorded and nothing else. I'd spent two weeks on this mountain learning to read her the way I read terrain: every sentence a route with a hidden crevasse in it.

The passage narrowed, widened, narrowed again. Then Ruth, ten meters ahead, stopped so abruptly Dev walked into her pack.

"Oh," she said. Just that. One syllable carrying thirty years.

Her lamp was fixed on the wall to her left, and when mine joined hers, I understood the syllable completely.

Survey marks. Dozens of them. Chiseled into the ice with a rock hammer and filled with something dark—ash, probably, or crushed charcoal from a stove—so they'd hold against refreezing. A survey line running up the wall in careful increments. Elevation notations beside each mark. And above them, at the height of a man's reach, initials cut deep and confident:

**T.N.**

My father's hands. I knew them the way you know a voice on the phone after one word. The *T* with its flat crossbar, the *N* whose diagonal always overshot the corner slightly, because he chiseled fast and never went back to correct himself. He used to say corrections were for men who had time to be wrong twice.

"That's not ancient," Dev said softly. "Those are modern survey marks. Those are—" He stopped. I felt him stop, felt the sentence die in his mouth, because he'd seen the plaque too and he was doing his own arithmetic now.

"When were these dated?" Kline asked, and her voice had gone very controlled, the control of a woman holding a door shut against a flood.

Ruth ran her thumb near—not over—the letters. "The original expedition came through here in September. Twenty-two years ago." She didn't look at me. She was giving me the courtesy of not looking at me, which on this mountain was nearly the same thing as an embrace.

Kline turned. Her headlamp caught me full in the face, and I watched her watch me, and I understood that whatever happened next would happen without words, because some recognitions are too large for speech.

She'd suspected. Of course she'd suspected—she'd bought a crew contract specifically to acquire a man named Edmund Reyes who handled a rope like a surgeon and rerouted treks around campsites no map marked. She'd tested me in the lowlands with a detail only a Nakamura would know, and I'd answered it correctly, and she'd filed the answer away like evidence. But suspicion is one thing. Suspicion is a hypothesis. What she was looking at now was data: the son of Thomas Nakamura standing in front of his father's handwriting, reading it.

And I was reading it. That was the thing I couldn't have explained if she'd demanded an explanation. To anyone else those marks were archaeology. To me they were dictation. I could hear him. *Elevation 4,180, ice sound, take the left branch, the right one breathes warm and warm means water and water means collapse.* Twenty years dead and still issuing route advice, still chiseling fast and never correcting, still talking to whoever came after him—which, it turned out, had always been going to be me. He just hadn't known it yet. Or maybe he had. Maybe that's why a man marks a wall in the middle of nowhere: not for science, but on the off chance that someday the only person who could hear him would walk by.

"You knew this route," Kline said. Not a question. An entry in the ledger.

"I grew up on it," I said. "Secondhand."

She held my gaze for a long five seconds. Then she turned back to the wall, and said nothing else about it, and that silence was louder than any accusation she could have mounted. It said: *I know, and I'm choosing not to spend this knowledge yet.* It said: *whatever this costs you, I intend to collect it later, at a time of my choosing.* Vesper Kline never discarded leverage. She banked it.

But there was something else under it too, something I almost missed because I wasn't looking for mercy from her. When she turned away, her hand came up briefly to her own sternum—a small gesture, unconscious, the gesture of a woman steadying herself against a verdict she'd been awaiting for decades. She hadn't come all this way for a shrine. She'd come for proof. And the proof had just introduced itself to her by its first name.

* * *

We followed the survey line upward.

It climbed the cave system in stages, my father's marks appearing at intervals like mileposts—each one chiseled, ashed, annotated. His notation system was idiosyncratic; he'd invented it himself and taught it to exactly three people, of whom I was one and Ruth, I realized as I watched her navigate by it without hesitation, was another. Ice density ratings. Slope stability codes. Little hieroglyphs for wind direction and snow load. A whole private language pressed into the walls of a dying glacier, addressed to no one, or to everyone, or—to be honest about it, which was the mood the caves were putting me in—to me.

"He documented everything," Ruth murmured, walking the line with her lamp low. "Every hundred meters. Even coming back down. Look—" she pointed to a mark where the numbers ran in reverse order, descending. "He surveyed the retreat too. Man wouldn't leave a mountain unmeasured even while it was trying to kill him."

"That sounds like him," I said, and heard my own voice do something strange on the second word.

Dev had gone quiet in the particular way of a man doing math he doesn't like. I could feel his questions stacking up behind his teeth: *Why does Ruth know this code? Why do you? Why did you flinch at that last mark like it spoke to you?* He was a cartographer. He dealt in lines between points. Sooner or later he was going to draw the line between me and that wall, and I couldn't blame him for it, and I dreaded it anyway.

The passage opened into a chamber, and the chamber stopped us all.

It wasn't large—maybe the size of a chapel, which felt less like coincidence than like commentary—but the ice overhead had formed into vaulting, natural ribs of frozen meltwater arching toward a keystone of clear ice, and through that keystone the storm-light outside came down diffused and pale, so the whole room glowed like the inside of a lantern. The air was utterly still. Our breath hung around us and refused to disperse, as if the room wanted to keep even that.

At the far end, the survey line terminated in a final mark, larger than the others, and beneath it a full annotation block—two dozen lines of chiseled text, dense as scripture.

Nobody moved. Then Kline said, "Read it," and her voice cracked on the second word, very slightly, a fissure in the ledger.

So I stepped forward, and I read my father's last field notes aloud in a hollowed-out glacier, to the woman who'd ruined her career hating him and the old woman who'd survived him and the young man who'd just figured out whose blood was standing in front of him.

*Chamber confirmed. Structure is genuine—construction predates any regional culture we can assign, or the ice has done something we don't understand, which is also possible and I hate it. Markings consistent throughout. This is real. Whatever happens next, write this down: this is real.*

Then, below, in smaller characters, the chisel strokes tighter, angrier:

*Funders want scale. Funders want a temple. This is a room and a cairn and a man's honest work and it is enough, it should be enough, but it will not be enough for them and I am tired. Recording what is here. Will decide on the rest at base camp.*

*Will decide on the rest at base camp.*

I stopped reading. The words hung in the lantern light, in our breath, in the terrible stillness.

Because I knew what he'd decided at base camp. Everyone in the climbing world knew what he'd decided at base camp. He'd come down off this mountain and announced a shrine of impossible antiquity and improbable grandeur, and the announcement had made his reputation, and the reputation had made mine unbearable, and the exaggeration had planted the seed of the accusation that had ended Vesper Kline's career when she called it a fabrication and got called a liar for her trouble.

Except it wasn't a fabrication. It was a decision. Made by a tired man in a tent, with funders circling, with a real discovery in his notebooks that he feared wasn't enough. He'd documented honestly and reported dishonestly, and both acts were sitting side by side in the ice like two halves of one man, and I was the only person alive positioned to read both.

"That's it," Kline whispered. "That's the whole case. He wrote it down. He *wrote it down*." She laughed—a single dry note with no humor in it, the laugh of a woman watching a twenty-year grudge dissolve into something worse, which is understanding. "Do you know what I would have given, twenty years ago, for one sentence like that? One line in his hand saying *this is real but I lied about the rest*?" She pressed her palm flat against the ice beside the annotation, carefully, not touching the letters. "One sentence. It would have saved everything."

"It would have ruined him," Ruth said quietly.

"Yes," Kline said. "I know." And something in her face suggested that the knowing was new, and heavy, and not entirely welcome.

Dev was looking at me. Not at the wall—at me. At the way I'd read those notes, at the way my voice had carried the cadence of a man I'd never let myself imitate aloud in twenty years. His headlamp beam wavered, and I saw him arrive, finally and completely, at the end of his arithmetic. He didn't say anything. He just nodded, once, small, like a man confirming a position on a map he'd already suspected he was standing in.

The family resemblance, I thought. All this time I'd hidden behind borrowed surnames and careful anonymity, and the whole time the truth was legible in how I read a chisel stroke. You can falsify a name. You can't falsify inheritance.

"We should document everything," I said, and my voice came out level, which cost me. "Photograph every mark. Cast the annotations. Before the season closes this place."

Kline was still looking at the wall. At the last line. *Will decide on the rest at base camp.*

"He chose the myth," she said.

"He chose survival," Ruth said. "There's a difference, and it took me thirty years to learn it."

Above us—or beyond us, deeper in, wherever the mountain kept such things—the ice ticked and settled, the glacier shifting its weight like a sleeper turning over. And then, faint but unmistakable, from the direction of the upper caves, from the direction of the shrine chamber none of us had reached yet: a low, resonant groan, long and structural, the sound the mountain makes when it's deciding whether to keep its rooms.

Everyone looked up. Everyone except Kline, who was still reading my father's handwriting, and me, who was listening to it, and somewhere between the two of us the truth finally got loud enough that neither of us could pretend anymore.

"The upper chambers," I said. "We need to see them before the mountain changes its mind."

Ruth checked the storm clock on her wrist and said nothing, which from Ruth was a scream.
