## 17. Closing Weather

The storm came early the way debts always do—before you're ready, at the worst possible hour, wearing a face you almost recognize.

I'd been watching it build for two days. A bruise on the western horizon that Dev kept calling "just cloud" and Ruth kept not calling anything at all, which was worse. Ruth's silences had a vocabulary all their own. This one said: *you know what that is, boy, and you know what it means.*

By the third watch of the night it arrived. Not gradually, the way weather is supposed to introduce itself. It came down the corridor like something with an appointment to keep, and by dawn the wind was doing ninety through the gap below the shrine chamber and the temperature had dropped thirty degrees and every man and woman on that mountain understood, in the wordless way climbers understand things, that the window had closed.

We were pinned in the ice caves with a real discovery crated in canvas, a frostbitten scout who wouldn't stop talking about markings nobody else could see, and six weeks of permits that now mattered about as much as last year's calendar.

And Vesper Kline stood at the mouth of the cave looking out at the white, and I watched her do the arithmetic.

I'd seen her freeze before—not like this, not literally—but I'd seen the moment when a person's certainty meets something it can't argue with. Back at the roadhouse, when Ruth went quiet over the route map. On the glacier, when my rescue of Dev exposed hands that knew too much. Each time, Kline had recovered inside a minute, because Kline's whole life was recovery, was the art of turning ruin into leverage.

This time she didn't recover.

"The corridor," she said. Her voice was level. That was the frightening part. "The corridor is still an option."

"It iced shut at four this morning," I said. "Ruth checked. The meltwater refroze behind us coming up, and the front added another meter of verglas on top. You could see it from here if the visibility weren't zero."

"We have ice screws."

"We have eleven ice screws and a corridor that's now a vertical pipe of black ice. We'd need forty and a week." I kept my voice flat too. Two flat voices make a strange kind of music. "It's not an option. It hasn't been an option since the front landed."

She turned from the cave mouth then, and her face in the lamplight was something I wasn't prepared for. Not anger. Not calculation. It was the look of a woman standing in the wreckage of a fifty-year plan, holding the blueprints, understanding for the first time that the building was never going to exist.

"There has to be a way down," she said.

"There is one way down."

"No." She said it fast, the way you say no to a diagnosis. "No. The east face is unclimbed. Unclimbed means unclimbed, Nakamura, it doesn't mean *difficult*, it means nobody has ever—"

"I know what it means."

"Do you?" She stepped toward me and the lamplight moved across her face and I saw how old she'd gotten in the last twelve hours, how the obsession that had kept her young the way a fire keeps iron hot had finally burned through to the cold metal underneath. "You've been on this mountain five minutes. Your father couldn't climb it either. Your father couldn't climb his way out of a paper bag without falsifying the—"

"Vesper." Ruth's voice, from the back of the cave. Just the name. Just once.

Kline stopped. And in the silence I heard what Ruth already knew and I was only beginning to understand: that Kline wasn't attacking my father anymore. She was attacking the east face, the storm, the permits, me—the entire universe that had conspired to hand her proof she never wanted to hold.

Because the chamber had settled nothing. That was the cruelty of it. The shrine was real and small and human, and my father's notes were honest about what he found and dishonest about what he claimed, and both of those facts sat in the same crate now, strapped beside the scout's frostbitten silence, and neither one of them gave Vesper Kline the verdict she'd spent her life earning. There was no villain. There was just a man who'd found something true and sold it bigger than it was, and a woman who'd built a cathedral of grievance on the foundation stone, and the cathedral was coming down in a snowstorm on an unclimbed face in a country where the passes were closing behind them.

"We rest today," I said. "All of us. We eat, we melt snow, we check every piece of gear twice. Tomorrow, first light, we go down the east face."

"You don't get to decide that."

"I'm deciding it anyway."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about command: it isn't a thing you take. It's a thing that gets handed to you at the worst moment of everyone's life, wrapped in circumstances nobody would choose, and the only ceremony is that people start looking at you instead of at each other.

They started looking at me somewhere around noon.

Not all at once. Dev first—he'd been watching me differently since the waystation, since the plaque with my father's name on it, and whatever war that discovery had started inside him, it had apparently resolved itself into a kind of fierce, wounded attention. He looked at me when Kline spoke, the way you look at a referee waiting for a call.

Then Ruth, who had earned the right to lead this crew three times over and knew it, and who looked at me anyway, deliberately, and nodded once. *Your father sent the retreat party down,* she'd told me above the tree line, carrying her guilt like a second pack for fifty years. *Now you carry them.* I don't think she meant to say it out loud. I think it came out of her the way heat comes off stone.

Even the scout, half-delirious in his bag, turned his frost-nipped face toward my voice when I gave instructions.

Kline saw it happening. That was the worst part. She saw the center of gravity move and she stood there in the middle of the cave with her arms crossed and her jaw set, and I watched her decide—consciously, expensively—to let it happen, because even her pride understood that the alternative was dying on a mountain arguing about jurisdiction.

But she wasn't finished with me. Of course she wasn't.

At dusk she found me checking anchors by lamplight, and she crouched next to me in the blue shadow of the cave entrance, and for a long time she said nothing at all. Outside, the storm hammered the ice like it was owed money.

"My hands are shaking," she said finally.

I looked. They were. Fine tremors, constant, the kind that aren't fear exactly—they're the body's ledger of everything the mind refuses to count.

"Cold does that," I said, giving her the exit.

"It's not cold." She flexed her fingers, watched them shake, watched them like they belonged to someone she was auditing. "Fifty years, Nakamura. Fifty years I've known exactly what happened on this range, exactly who did what, exactly what I was owed. I had it all written down. Every deposition, every retraction, every funding letter your father ever signed. I could recite them." Her voice dropped. "And now I'm standing on top of the truth and it doesn't say what I spent my life making sure it would say, and my hands won't stop shaking, and tomorrow we go down a face no human being has ever descended, and I find that I—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I don't know how to follow."

"I know," I said. "I spent ten years learning how to disappear. Following is easy compared to that."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not supposed to be. It's supposed to be true." I coiled the rope in my lap, slow, letting my hands do something useful while the conversation did its work. "Here's what I've got, Kline, and it's not much. Tomorrow we descend nine hundred meters of virgin ice on eleven screws and whatever Ruth can build out of spite. Somebody falls, we catch them or we lose them. The storm gives us maybe eighteen hours before the next front. That's the whole situation. There's no room in it for who bought whom, or whose name goes on what, or whether your fifty years were wasted. They weren't wasted. They got us here. That's all any of it ever does."

"And if I refuse to go?"

"Then you stay here with the crate and the scout and Ruth will leave you a stove and I'll come back for you in spring, and you'll have had fifty years of being right about everything, alone, in an ice cave." I looked at her. "You won't refuse."

"No," she said, after a while. "I suppose I won't." And then, so quietly I nearly missed it under the wind: "Your father would have ordered me. He'd have loved ordering me."

"Yeah," I said. "He would have." I tied off the coil. "I'm not him. That's the whole point of me."

* * *

First light came gray and windless, which on a mountain is either mercy or ambush, and we didn't waste time finding out which.

Ruth built the first anchor—a buried deadman in the nevé above the lip, backed up twice, tested until she trusted it with her whole weight and then some. She worked in silence, efficient as breathing, and when she finished she stood and looked down the east face for a long moment.

"Hell of a view," she said. "Shame about the drop."

Nine hundred meters of ice and rock, pitched in shelves, broken by seracs that hung over the route like bad decisions. Nobody had ever looked at it from the top with intent to survive. We were about to be the first data point.

I set the order myself. Dev second, because he was the best technical climber among us and because keeping him close kept him alive. Ruth third, running sweep on each anchor. The scout in the middle, lowered like cargo, half-conscious and apologizing to no one in particular. Kline last but one, ahead of me, where I could reach her.

She noticed. Of course she noticed.

"You're putting me on a short leash," she said.

"I'm putting you where my arm is long. If you slip, I catch you. If I slip, you're the one who calls it." I met her eyes. "That's not a leash, Vesper. That's trust with a rope attached."

Something moved across her face—too fast to name, gone before it landed—and she clipped in without another word.

The first pitch was the easy one, if you can call sixty meters of near-vertical water ice easy. Dev led it beautifully, placing screws with the economy of someone who'd stopped wasting motion years ago, and I brought up the rear hauling the scout's sled-bag and trying not to think about the fact that every anchor we placed was a promise we might not be able to keep on the way past.

By midmorning we were three pitches down and the mountain had shown us its opinion: a serac calved off the flank two hundred meters east, distant as thunder, close enough to remind us the route was alive and indifferent.

Then the weather changed its mind again.

It came in from the south this time, fast, a wall of spindrift riding a wind that appeared between one pitch and the next. Visibility dropped to a rope length. Then less. Dev's figure at the stance below blurred into a suggestion, a ghost in orange shell fabric.

"Stance secure!" His voice came torn thin across the gap. "Anchor's good! Send them down!"

I sent the scout first, lowered slow through the white, counting the meters against the rope marks. Then Kline.

She froze at the edge.

Not metaphorically. Not the other kind of freezing, the internal kind I'd watched her fight all yesterday. Her boots were planted at the lip, her hands locked on the anchor rope, and the wind was pulling at her hood and she was looking down into a whiteness that contained, as far as her eyes could tell, nothing at all—no stance, no Dev, no bottom, no evidence that the world continued below the place where she stood.

"Nakamura." My name came out stripped of everything. No rival, no employer, no history. Just a name, called into weather, by a frightened person. "Nakamura, I can't see him."

"He's there. I can hear him."

"I can't—" Her breath was coming wrong, fast and shallow, the rhythm that ends in bad decisions. "Fifty years, Edmund. Fifty years and I never once—this is not—I climbed everything, I climbed *everything*, and I can't see the—"

"Vesper." I moved to her, clipped my sling to her harness beside the main line, put my gloved hand over hers on the rope. Through two layers of fabric I felt the shaking again, worse than last night, the full audit of a lifetime conducted by her own fingers. "Listen to me. You don't have to see him. You have to feel the rope. The rope knows where he is. Your job is the next ten feet, and my job is everything after that. Can you give me the next ten feet?"

The wind screamed. Somewhere below, faintly, Dev's voice: *"Kline? You coming?"*

She closed her eyes. I watched her do it—watched her reach down into that iron discipline that had carried her through ruin and obsession and fifty years of being right, and pull out not certainty, not leverage, not a single one of the weapons she'd sharpened her whole life, but just the plain animal willingness to put her weight on a rope held by other people.

"Ten feet," she said.

"Ten feet," I agreed.

She leaned back over the void, into the white, into the nothing her eyes insisted was there, and she weighted the rope, and I fed it through the brake, smooth and slow, ten feet at a time, and she did not freeze and she did not fall and somewhere in the middle of the fourth pitch she stopped shaking.

* * *

We made the col at the base of the headwall as the light died, six hundred meters down, three hundred to go, and nobody proposed pushing on because everybody understood the arithmetic of darkness and ice.

We dug in. Two tents crushed into a shelf barely wide enough for the courtesy, the scout zipped into both bags, the stove hissing its small stubborn argument against the night. Ruth melted snow with the patience of a woman who had survived worse by refusing to be impressed by it.

Kline sat against the tent wall with her hands wrapped around a cup she wasn't drinking, and after a while she said, to no one, to the nylon, to me:

"On the original expedition, your father stood right about where we're sitting. Did you know that?"

I hadn't. Ruth's eyes flicked up from the stove, and I saw her realize it at the same moment I did—that this shelf, this col, was ground her feet had crossed fifty years ago, going down, leaving him behind.

"He sent us down from here," Ruth said quietly. "Me and Tomas and the others. Stood right there and told us the weather was turning and the smart money was low." She stirred the pot. "Weather was fine for three more days. He just wanted fewer witnesses."

"Or he wanted the ones he loved to live," I said.

Neither of them answered that. The stove hissed. Outside, the wind worked at the tents like a creditor.

Kline looked at me across the cup, and in the lamplight her face had lost something overnight—not the obsession, that was burned in too deep to melt—but some load-bearing part of the certainty around it. What was left looked, unexpectedly, like a person.

"When we get down," she said, "there are things that have to be decided. About the record. About names."

"I know."

"You'll want things I can't give."

"Probably."

"And I'll want things you shouldn't."

"Definitely," I said. "So we'll fight about it in a warm room, like civilized liars."

For the first time since the roadhouse—maybe for the first time in the fifty years anyone present could account for—Vesper Kline laughed. It was a small sound, rusty, startled out of her like a bird from cover, and it died almost immediately, and she looked almost embarrassed, and Ruth smiled into the pot where nobody was supposed to see.

I lay back against the pack and listened to the storm renegotiate terms with the mountain, and I thought about my father standing on this exact shelf sending the people he loved downward, and I thought: *that's the job, then. That's the whole inheritance. You send them down alive and you carry what it costs.*

Sleep took me like a debt collector.

I woke once, deep in the black hours, to a sound I couldn't place—metal on ice, rhythmic, patient, somewhere below us in the dark. Not wind. Wind doesn't keep time.

I crawled to the tent flap and looked out, and far down the east face, in the blue-black dark, there was a light. Small, steady, moving. Descending.

Someone was climbing our route ahead of us. Downward. In the storm. In the dark.

And they were moving like they knew every anchor we'd placed.
