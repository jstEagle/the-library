## 18. East Face Descent

There is a particular sound a rope makes when it comes tight around a human being who has just decided to fall. I'd heard it once before, on a training wall in Colorado, from a man who weighed nothing and fell beautifully. This was not that sound. This was the sound of eighty kilograms of Vesper Kline's hired cartographer discovering that the east face of the Meridian Range does not negotiate.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, and the mountain hates impatience more than it hates anything else.

* * *

We stood at the top of the corridor at four in the morning, in the blue-black hour Edmund called "the honest hour," because nobody lies at four a.m. with the wind chewing on them. Behind us, the ice caves had sealed themselves like a throat swallowing. Ahead of us, the east face dropped away for eleven hundred meters of rock and rime and rumor—the face no one had climbed because no one had ever needed to leave the Meridians by it before. The mountain had always been a destination. Now it was an exit.

Edmund crouched at the lip with his headlamp off, reading the face by starlight the way other people read menus. He'd been doing this all night—standing at edges, going quiet, letting the mountain talk first. Somewhere in the last two days he had stopped being the quiet hired hand with suspiciously good knots and become something else, something the crew orbited without discussing. Ruth had known for weeks. Kline had figured it out in the ice caves—I saw her watch him read his father's handwriting, and I watched her decide to say nothing, which told me everything. And me? I'd found the plaque at the waystation three days ago. E. NAKAMURA, EXPEDITION LEADER, MERIDIAN SURVEY. I'd carried the name around like a stone in my boot, waiting for him to say it out loud.

He'd said it at the vote. Just like that. "I'm Edmund Nakamura. My father led the expedition that died up here. I'm asking you to follow me anyway."

And we did. Which says less about him than it does about us, probably.

"Rap stations every sixty meters," he said now, voice flat and even, the voice of a man who had rehearsed this in his head so many times the rehearsal had worn grooves. "V-threads through the ice where there's ice, bolts where there isn't. We've got fourteen anchors' worth of hardware and eleven hundred meters of face, so we recycle everything. Nothing gets left behind except what we can't help." He looked at each of us in turn. "Kline, you're second. Dev third. Ruth anchors. Nobody moves until the person below calls clear. Questions?"

"I have one," Ruth said. She held up her right hand, flexing fingers gone stiff and gray at the tips since the corridor. Frostbite is patient. It waits its turn. "When we get down, somebody's buying me a drink with actual whiskey in it, not whatever Kline packed."

"Two drinks," Kline said. Her voice came out scraped. She hadn't slept. None of us had, but Kline wore it differently—she wore it like a debt she intended to collect from the mountain personally. "I'll buy the bottle."

"That," Ruth said, "is the first sensible thing you've said since base camp."

* * *

The first three hundred meters went almost well, which on the east face is the same as going badly, just with better manners.

I went over the lip third, into air so cold it felt structural, like descending through the inside of a bell. The rock under my tools was verglas over granite—glass over bone—and every swing had to be placed like a signature: confident, or not at all. Below me the valley floor was a white absence. Above me Edmund moved with a fluency that still startled me, even after everything. Ten years of hiding, and the hiding hadn't rusted him. If anything it had kept him sharp, the way a blade stays sharp in a drawer.

"Station," he called, and the word fell past me into the dark.

By the fifth rappel the storm found us. It didn't arrive; it assembled—first a wind with opinions, then spindrift pouring off the face like sand through an hourglass, then the whole sky closing into a white rotary that erased the difference between up and down. I clipped into the station and pressed myself against the rock and felt the mountain breathing through my jacket.

"Edmund." Kline's voice, shredded by wind. "Visibility's gone."

"Then we go by feel." He appeared beside her, a shape with a headlamp halo. "Short ropes. Twenty meters between climbers, no slack. Call every move."

"You're describing a blind descent of an unclimbed face in a storm."

"I'm describing Tuesday," Edmund said, and I laughed—a stupid, barking laugh that the wind stole immediately—but it did something to the rope of tension running through all of us. Loosened it half a turn. That's a thing I learned about leadership up there, watching him: it's mostly comedy performed at the correct moment, by a person who has already done the terrifying math.

We went. Station to station, anchor to anchor, the world reduced to twenty meters of rope and the sound of your own breath turning to frost inside your hood. Ruth below me, steady as ballast. Kline above, moving worse than she should have—not weak exactly, but rigid, a woman whose body had remembered something her mind refused to name about faces and falls and expeditions that don't come home. Every time she reached a station she gripped the anchor with both hands and stayed there a beat too long before unclipping.

I pretended not to notice. The mountain notices enough for everyone.

* * *

It happened at the eighth station, seven hundred meters down, where the face steepened into a headwall and the ice thinned to a varnish.

Later I would reconstruct it a dozen times, the way you replay a car crash frame by frame looking for the exact moment physics made its decision. Here's what I have: the storm had thickened. My hands were in the bad place—not painful anymore, which is the warning sign, pain being the body's way of saying it still expects you to live. I'd switched to my backup tools an hour ago because the picks on my primaries had dulled to suggestions. And the station Edmund had built was a V-thread in ice that turned out, when weighted, to be sitting over a hollow.

He tested it twice before he trusted it. I watched him test it. That's the part that still sits wrong in my chest—he did everything right, and the mountain simply lied.

The thread blew as I weighted the rope.

Not dramatically. That's the horror of it. No crack, no thunder. Just a soft crunch, like biting through the crust of snow into nothing, and then the world tilted and the face swung away from me and I was falling with eight meters of rope paying out behind me and no anchor above and the next station forty meters below and my hands too cold to feel the brake.

I remember thinking, with absurd clarity: *so this is the sound.* The sound I'd heard on the training wall. Except this time I was the weight.

The rope came tight.

Except—it shouldn't have. There was nothing above me to come tight to. But there was someone beside me, someone who had reached the station a minute earlier and clipped in short, someone whose gloved hand had closed on the trailing rope and wrapped it twice around her forearm and her harness's friction hitch, someone who weighed less than me and had no business holding me and did it anyway.

Vesper Kline caught me on a munter hitch and her own skeleton.

The jerk nearly took her over the edge with me. I heard her grunt—a real sound, animal, involuntary—and then the rope burned through her wrap and caught, and we were both just hanging there in the white rotary, me dangling below the blown station, her stretched along the anchor line with her boots braced against rock, holding the entire arithmetic of my life in her forearms.

"Hold," she said. Not to me. To herself. A woman negotiating with her own tendons.

"Kline—" My voice cracked. "Let the hitch take it, don't hold it with your—"

"I know how a hitch works, Okafor." Through gritted teeth. Through the storm. Her arms shaking visibly now, tremors running up into her shoulders. "Get your feet on the rock. Climb to me. Now."

I got my feet on the rock. Verglas, glass over bone, but terror finds purchase where skill can't. I climbed those eight meters in maybe forty seconds, which is a personal record I never want to attempt again, and when I reached her she was still holding the wrap, wouldn't let go until Edmund arrived from above and transferred the load to a fresh anchor he built in ninety seconds flat, drilling into living rock while the storm tried to peel him off the face.

Only after I was clipped to the new station—only after Edmund's hand pressed my shoulder once, hard, saying without words *you're here, you're here*—did Kline let go.

She sagged against the anchor. Her forearms, when she pushed back her sleeves later, were striped red where the rope had run. Rope burn through two layers of glove and jacket. She looked at the marks the way she'd looked at her father's ledger photographs in the roadhouse: like evidence.

"Why?" I asked her. It came out smaller than I meant it to. "You spent a decade hating his family. You bought us like equipment. Why'd you catch me?"

She was quiet long enough that I thought the wind had eaten the question. Then she said, "Because you're not his family. You're yours." A pause. "And because on the corridor, when the scout was babbling about the markings, you were the only one who didn't look at me like I was the reason we were up here. You looked at the route." Another pause, shorter. "That's not forgiveness. Don't mistake it. It's just... accurate bookkeeping."

From above, Edmund's voice, level as a spirit bubble: "Station's good. Next rap, twenty meters, stay tight. Dev—you're climbing with me from here."

"Yes," I said. And then, because the honest hour apparently lasts all day on the east face: "Thank you."

"Don't thank me," Kline said, already rigging her descender with those striped forearms, not looking at me. "Thank the hitch. I just held on. Anyone would have."

Which was a lie, and we both knew it, and it was the most generous lie anyone had told me in years.

* * *

We lost the crating at the ninth station.

It wasn't dramatic either—that seems to be the theme of the east face, catastrophe arriving with library manners. The artifact crate, the shrine fragments Edmund had insisted on documenting and Kline had insisted on removing, rode in a haul bag slung beneath Ruth. At the ninth station the wind gusted to something beyond measuring, a shove from the whole sky, and the bag pendulumed out from the wall and slammed the rock and the sling abraded through on a granite edge sharper than anything has a right to be at that altitude.

We watched it fall. All five of us, hanging on the face, watched the bag tumble end over end into the white, taking with it the padded crates, the foam, the strapping, the careful catalog of a discovery that would have rewritten a region's history if it had survived the trip.

Nobody screamed. That was the strange part. Ruth said, quietly, "Well." Kline made a sound I couldn't classify. Edmund watched the bag vanish and then turned back to the wall and said, "Field notes are intact. That's the primary record. The fragments are corroborating evidence—we photographed everything in situ, we have rubbings, measurements, Kline's scout's testimony. The stones themselves were always going to be argued about. The notes aren't arguable. They're handwriting."

He said it evenly, but I was climbing directly below him by then, per his order, and I could see his hands on the anchors. They weren't even slightly steady. He'd strapped the field notes to his chest that morning—his father's final notebook, wrapped in plastic, bound flat against his sternum under his hardshell—and when he talked about them his voice did something it never did otherwise. It softened. Like he was talking about a heartbeat.

"The notes come down or none of us do," Ruth said. "Agreed?"

"Agreed," said Kline, and for once there was no ledger in her voice. Just agreement.

* * *

The last two hundred meters were the worst, because hope is heavier than fear and it pulls differently on a rope.

The storm began to spend itself somewhere below eight hundred meters, fraying into gusts and gaps, and through the gaps we could see the glacier tongue at the base of the face, and beyond it the treeline, and beyond that the valley, and I swear the valley looked fictional. A place from a map. A place where whiskey existed and fingers had ten joints apiece and no one had ever heard of a V-thread.

Edmund built the final anchor himself, a monster of equalized threads backed up with the last two bolts in the rack, and he made each of us check it, and then he went last, which surprised no one. Leaders go last. It's in some unwritten manual. He descended into the lightening afternoon with his father's notes strapped over his heart, and when his boots hit the glacier he stood there a moment longer than he needed to, head bowed, one gloved hand pressed flat against his chest.

Ruth reached him first. She took his hand off his chest and held it—her frostbitten fingers and all—and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the last eleven hundred meters hadn't already said.

Kline walked apart, as she does. But I saw her stop, ten meters out, and look back up the face they'd just come down, the whole impossible vertical mile of it, and I saw her mouth move. I was close enough to catch it, barely:

"Better books than mine."

I decided not to have heard it. Some things you keep for people whether they want you to or not.

* * *

Camp went up in the willows at dusk, and it was the ugliest, most beautiful camp I've ever helped build. Stove hissing. Boots steaming. Ruth's hand in a pot of lukewarm water, wincing and swearing with tremendous creativity. Edmund moving through it all with the field notes finally unstrapped, drying them page by page over the stove like a man handling scripture, or letters, or both.

I sat down next to Kline, because apparently I have no survival instincts left.

"Your arms need dressing," I said.

"They need whiskey first. Priorities."

"Ruth called the whiskey."

"Ruth called *a* whiskey. I'm calling the medical one." But she let me wrap them, sat there staring into the stove flame while I wound gauze around the rope burns, and just before I tied it off she said, without preamble, "In the ice caves. When Nakamura read his father's notes. You knew by then, didn't you. What he was. Who."

"Yeah," I said. "Since the waystation."

"And you didn't tell me."

"He hadn't told anyone. It wasn't mine to give."

She considered that for a long time. The stove popped. Somewhere in the willows a bird made a sound, insane, optimistic, alive.

"When we get down," she said finally, "there's going to be a reckoning. About the shrine, about his father, about what gets published and what gets buried. I've wanted that reckoning for thirty years, Okafor. I've organized my entire life around it." She flexed her bandaged hands, testing. "And tonight I find I can't remember why it mattered more than the fact that all of us came off that face."

"It's not either/or," I said. "It never was. That's kind of the whole lesson, isn't it? Both things true at once. His father documented the shrine honestly *and* exaggerated it. You were ruined *and* you sent a scout ahead of us. You hate the Nakamuras *and* you caught me."

Her mouth twitched. Almost a smile. On Vesper Kline's face, an almost-smile registers like a seismic event.

"Go to sleep, Okafor."

I crawled into my bag. From across the fire I could hear Edmund turning pages, slow and careful, and Ruth muttering inventory, and the wind in the willows doing its best impression of a gentler mountain. We'd lost the artifacts. We'd nearly lost me. We'd gained, God help us, each other—which sounds like the tagline of a terrible film, and I stand by it anyway.

I was almost asleep when Edmund spoke, quietly, to all of us or no one:

"There's a town at the trailhead. Concession office, telegraph line, the works." A pause, the sound of a page turning. "And when we get there, Kline and I are going to have a conversation about what the record looks like. Both records. All of them."

Kline, from the dark: "You think I'll agree to that?"

"I think," Edmund said, "you held a man's life on your forearms today for eight meters of verglas. I think you already know what the record looks like. You're just deciding whether you can afford it."

Silence. Long silence. Long enough that I opened my eyes and saw her silhouette against the embers, motionless, her bandaged hands folded like a woman at a trial awaiting verdicts.

"No," she said at last. "No, Nakamura. I don't think I can afford it."

A pause.

"But I suspect I'm going to do it anyway."

The fire settled. The willows whispered. And somewhere north of us, behind walls of new snow, the Meridian Range closed its passes for the winter, keeping—as it always had—its own accounts.
