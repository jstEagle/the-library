## 1. The Weight of Moving On

The tide was out and so, more or less, was Silas Dravenmoor's ambition.

She stood at the rail of the *Merry Adeline*, a charter fishing boat with a personality somewhere between a workhorse and a sulking teenager, and watched six tourists from Birmingham discover that the sea does not care about your itinerary. One of them—a man in a rain jacket the color of a highlighter—was feeding his breakfast back to the gulls. The gulls were grateful. The man's wife was not.

"Rough patch," said Devi Okafor, the *Adeline*'s actual captain, appearing at Silas's elbow with two mugs of tea that tasted mostly of salt and optimism. "You want to take us through the shoals on the way back? Give them something to look at besides their own regrets?"

"Happy to." Silas took the mug. "Though honestly, watching them regret things is my favorite part of the job."

"You're a cruel woman."

"I'm an underpaid woman. Cruelty is free."

Devi laughed and went to console the highlighter man, who had begun apologizing to the sea in a formal, almost diplomatic register. Silas leaned on the rail and let the wind do what it always did: take her thoughts out one by one, examine them without comment, and hand them back lighter.

Three years in Gullwrack now. Three years since Kernvall, though she measured time less in years than in small mercies: no photographs in the local paper, no name on any manifest that mattered, no questions from Devi beyond the ones a friend asks about your sleeping habits and then wisely abandons. In Gullwrack she was simply "Silas," which did boat repairs, knew the tides better than the harbormaster's charts did, and had once talked a panicked family off the sea stacks in fog so thick it seemed less like weather than like a decision the world had made about them.

It was not nothing. It was also not a life. It was a life-shaped space she occupied carefully, the way you'd occupy a room where the floorboards were rotten—you learned which spots held weight and you never, ever danced.

* * *

The letter came on a Tuesday, which should have been her first warning. Nothing good had ever arrived in Gullwrack on a Tuesday; the mail boat ran Thursdays, and anything earlier meant someone had paid extra, and people only paid extra when they wanted something.

She found it wedged in the door of her rented room above the chandlery, thick cream paper in an envelope heavy enough to have opinions. No return address. Her name written across the front in a hand so confident it looked typeset: **S. DRAVENMOOR**.

Not *Ms. Silas*. Not the alias she'd been using for boat bookings. Her full surname, the one that had been in every newspaper from here to the southern capitals for one terrible month three years ago.

She stood in the doorway holding it for a long moment, listening to the gulls argue over the fish market scraps below, feeling the particular cold that starts behind the sternum and works outward.

Inside the room, she set it on the table, made tea she didn't drink, and picked it up four times before opening it on the fifth.

Two pages. A letter, and a bank draft.

The letter was brief, courteous, and precise in the way of a man accustomed to being obeyed. An expedition. Short notice. A glacier to the north—the Vardensk, though he didn't say the name outright, just described its position with the loving accuracy of a collector cataloguing a specimen. A sea cave beneath it, exposed only at extreme low tide, twice more before calving season sealed the coast for the year. He required a guide with ice experience. He required discretion. He required her specifically, though how he'd known where to find her was a question the letter declined to answer, elegantly, the way expensive stationery answers things.

Triple her usual rate. Triple what she'd have called a fantasy rate, if she'd ever allowed herself rates above subsistence.

And beneath the letter, a bank draft for half the fee up front. Enough to clear what she owed Brannock the smuggler—she thought of him as a smuggler because he preferred the word *logistics consultant*, and she preferred to be accurate—and enough for a year of rent besides, with room left over for a new pair of boots, which hers had been requesting in writing for some months.

She read the letter twice. Then she folded it, put it back in the envelope, carried it to the small iron stove in the corner, and held it over the open grate.

This was the ritual. She'd done it twice before in three years—once with a magazine that wanted an interview about "the tragedy that reshaped northern guiding," once with a university offering a teaching post that came with a background check. Both times she'd held the offer over the flame until the heat crept into her fingers, and both times she'd felt something in her chest unclench, like a knot giving way, like a body remembering it could breathe.

Freedom, she told herself, smelled like burning paper.

The draft, unfortunately, did not burn. Bank drafts are notoriously resistant to symbolism. She held the letter over the fire until the corner began to brown, and then she looked down at the draft lying fat and smug on the table, and then she thought about Brannock's face—not threatening, never threatening, just patient, the patience of a man who owned several boats and needed to own exactly one more—and she took the letter away from the stove and beat the small fire out with a dish towel.

"Well," she said to the room. The room, which had heard worse, said nothing back.

* * *

That evening she walked the harbor wall, which was what passed in Gullwrack for thinking. The town did its best work at dusk: the fishing fleet coming home in loose formation, lights pricking on along the quay, the smell of frying fish asserting itself over everything else like a landlord collecting rent. Old Marta waved from the chandlery door. Silas waved back. This was the whole of her social calendar most weeks, and she had convinced herself it was enough, the way you convince yourself a coat still fits by not moving your arms.

Here was the arithmetic she couldn't escape:

Owed to Brannock: a sum she preferred to think of as *a boat*, because he did, and the boat was collateral, and boats are heavy things to owe.

Rent: due in eleven days.

Prospects otherwise: repairing nets, mending hulls, guiding tourists to look at seals. Honest work. Thin work. Work that kept her invisible, and invisibility, she had learned, was a currency too—it just bought very little, and spent fast.

Against that: triple pay. Half up front. A glacier she knew by reputation, a cave she knew by rumor, and a client who knew her real name, which meant he knew everything else, which meant—

Which meant this was not an opportunity. Opportunities don't find you through locked doors. This was a hook, baited with exactly what she needed, dropped into exactly the water she swam in. Someone wanted her out there. And the truly humiliating thing, standing on the harbor wall with the wind pulling at her collar, was how little that frightened her compared to the alternative. Fear of exposure was an old companion; she'd grown calluses. But debt was new, and new fears bite harder.

There was another arithmetic underneath, the one she didn't let herself do. The Vardensk Glacier. Northern waters. She had promised herself, three years ago, on the worst day of her life, that she would never go north again. North was where Wren Halloway had died—avalanche, the reports said, body unrecovered, the mountain keeping what mountains keep. North was where Silas had led seven people onto a slope she'd misread, and where the inquiry afterward had used words like *negligent* and *reckless* and, once, memorably, *unfit*.

If she went north, she would be going back toward the ghost. And some traitorous, exhausted, honest corner of her had missed the ghost every single day.

"Decision made?" asked Devi, materializing beside her with the uncanny timing of harbor cats.

"I'm considering a job."

"A job. You? I thought you were retired at thirty-four."

"Temporarily retired. Indefinitely temporarily."

Devi followed her gaze north, past the breakwater, to where the horizon went dark and cold. "Bad idea?"

"Probably."

"Taking it anyway?"

Silas looked at the water. Somewhere out there was a boat she owed a smuggler, and further out, past all reckoning, a mountain that owed her a body and a truth, in whichever order it pleased.

"Yeah," she said. "I'm taking it anyway."

Devi nodded slowly, the nod of a woman filing information away for later use as ammunition. "Then you'll want your boots resoled before you go. Marta will do it tonight if you ask nicely. And Silas—" she paused, and for a moment the harbor-cat humor dropped away, and what was underneath was older and kinder, "—whatever's up there that's calling you, try to come back from it. People do that, sometimes. They go looking for a thing and forget they're allowed to bring themselves home."

"That's grimly specific."

"I'm a captain. We're professionally grim." Devi clapped her on the shoulder and strolled off toward the fish fry stand, calling back over her shoulder: "Ask Marta nicely! She likes biscuits!"

Silas stayed on the wall a while longer. Above her, the first stars were coming out over the water, indifferent and clean, and she thought about the letter's last line, the one she'd read three times without deciding whether it was courtesy or threat:

*I trust you will find our terms difficult to refuse. That is rather the point.*

She turned the bank draft over in her hands one final time, watching the harbor lights slide across its surface like weather over ice.

Then she went to buy biscuits.
