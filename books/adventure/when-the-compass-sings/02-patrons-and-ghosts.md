## 2. Patrons and Ghosts

The letter had said the Harrow Hotel, suite four, at seven o'clock, and that I should ask for Mr. Vane. The letter did not say why a man with money enough to rent the best room in Gullwrack Harbor would want me, of all people, standing in his doorway smelling faintly of bait brine.

I'd spent the afternoon trying to talk myself out of going. This was not difficult. I was very good at talking myself out of things; it was practically a trade skill. But the letter sat on my table like a debt of its own, and debts have a way of collecting interest whether you acknowledge them or not.

So I put on my one clean shirt—the blue one, the one that made me look like a person who occasionally ate vegetables—and walked up from the docks through streets still dripping with evening fog.

* * *

The Harrow Hotel had been built by a man who believed that more was more, and his descendants had done nothing to correct him. Brass everywhere. A carpet that seemed to be actively trying to swallow visitors. The desk clerk looked me over with the particular expression reserved for women who arrive alone and smell of fish, then pointed me up the stairs without being asked, which meant Vane had described me to him. That should have been my first warning. A man who knows what you look like before you've met him has already decided what you're worth.

Suite four was warm in a way no room in Gullwrack had any right to be. A fire crackled behind a brass screen. Maps covered every horizontal surface—coastal charts, glacial surveys, tide tables annotated in a small, impatient hand. And in the middle of it all stood Aldous Vane.

He was perhaps fifty-five, silver at the temples in a way that looked arranged rather than earned, wearing a waistcoat the color of good port wine. He crossed the room toward me with both hands extended, as though I were a long-lost cousin rather than a stranger he'd found through channels he probably shouldn't admit to.

"Miss Dravenmoor." His voice was warm honey poured over something harder underneath. "I can't tell you what an honor this is."

"That's usually my line," I said. "I'm the guide."

"And what a guide." He steered me toward a chair without asking if I wanted to sit, which I did anyway, because the fire was doing things to my bones that I approved of. "Do you know how many people I wrote to before someone gave me your name? Eleven. Eleven letters, and ten came back either refused or unanswered. You are a difficult woman to find, Silas Dravenmoor."

"I work at it."

He laughed—genuinely, I thought, though men like him practice laughing until it's indistinguishable from the real thing. He poured two glasses of something amber without asking, pressed one into my hand, and settled across from me like a man settling in for the evening's entertainment.

"You'll forgive my enthusiasm. I've been chasing this particular dream for twenty years." He swept a hand at the maps. "You know the legends, of course. Every child on this coast does."

"The Siren Cave," I said. "Sings when the tide goes out. Sailors follow the sound and never come home. My grandmother used it to keep us off the ice."

"Legends are sediment," Vane said, leaning forward. "They're what settles out when truth sits still long enough. Something under that glacier *sings*, Miss Dravenmoor—or echoes, or resonates, or whatever the geologists want to call it—and something made it sing. Carved stone carries sound in ways natural ice doesn't. I've spent half my fortune proving that much. Now I need someone to get me inside before calving season closes the door for another year."

I turned the glass in my hands. "There are other guides."

"There are other guides," he agreed pleasantly. "None of them available on nine days' notice. None of them willing to work a route that isn't on any chart. And none of them—" he smiled, and for just a moment the charm thinned like ice over moving water, "—none of them with your particular experience reading country that wants to kill you."

It was a compliment shaped like a knife. I drank instead of answering.

"The pay is triple the standard rate," he continued. "Half now, half on completion. Nine days. In and out before the second tide window shuts. What could be simpler?"

Nothing about a man who says *what could be simpler* is ever simple. But I thought of the letter from Marlowe the smuggler sitting in my drawer, the one with the figure at the bottom that made my stomach fold in on itself. Half now would clear it. Half now would mean I owed nobody anything, which was the closest thing to freedom I'd felt in three years.

"I'll need to see the crew manifest," I said. "Before I commit."

Something flickered across his face—annoyance, quickly papered over. "Of course. Standard caution. I admire it." He produced a folded paper from the map table and handed it over. "Six of us total. Myself, my man Corwin, two porters hired out of Stonnaway, a camp cook, and—"

I stopped reading.

The fourth name on the list was printed in the same neat type as the rest, but it might as well have been carved into my chest: **TOMAS REYES**.

* * *

I don't remember setting down the glass. I remember the sound it made against the side table—too loud, too careful—and I remember Vane watching me set it down.

"You know him," Vane said. It wasn't quite a question.

"We've met."

"Ah." He refilled my glass uninvited. "Then perhaps you also know he comes highly recommended. Ice man, rescue certified, excellent references. Though I gather his references and yours share a certain... history."

The room got very small. The fire popped. Somewhere below, the hotel's front door opened and closed, letting in a breath of cold harbor air that didn't reach us.

Three years ago, Tomas Reyes had stood in a hearing room in Kernvall and told six strangers exactly where our rope team had been when the cornice failed. He'd been the base-camp radio operator. He'd read back my own position reports in a flat voice while Wren Halloway's family sat in the front row with their faces like closed doors. He hadn't lied, exactly. He'd told the truth in the shape most useful to the insurers who were paying for the inquiry, and the shape they wanted was a woman who'd pushed too high too fast because she had something to prove.

I hadn't gone back to Kernvall after that. Or anywhere near anyone who'd been there. I'd built a life out of towns too small to hold newspapers and jobs too small to hold attention, and here was a piece of that old life sitting on a hotel manifest in neat black type, hired by a man who watched me now with the patient interest of someone waiting for a lock to turn.

"You knew," I said. "When you wrote to me. You knew he'd be on this expedition."

Vane spread his hands, all innocence, all warmth. "Miss Dravenmoor. I hired the best crew I could assemble on short notice. If two excellent mountaineers happen to share a past, well—" he smiled, "—the mountains are full of coincidences. Surely you of all people believe that."

I stood up. The chair scraped. My heart was going hard and stupid against my ribs, and every instinct I owned was screaming the same thing it had screamed for three years: *leave, leave now, leave first, leave clean.*

"I'm sorry for your time, Mr. Vane," I said. "Find yourself another guide."

"Half payment is five hundred crowns," he said mildly, to my back.

I stopped. God help me, I stopped.

"Payable tonight, on signature," he went on, as if discussing the weather. "I understand you have certain obligations in this town. Small town, Gullwrack. People talk. Dockmasters especially." He let that sit a moment, then rose and crossed to the window, hands clasped behind his back, a man admiring a view he already owned. "I'm not threatening you, Silas. I want to be very clear about that. I simply need a guide who won't flinch when the ice gets interesting, and everything I've heard says you don't flinch. Prove me right. Take the money. Lead us to the cave. And in nine days we part ways, richer and even, everyone's history staying exactly where histories belong—in the past."

I stood there with my hand on the door latch, feeling the cold iron under my fingers, and I understood two things with terrible clarity.

The first was that Aldous Vane had not stumbled onto my name. Ten refused letters, he'd said. Eleven written. Which meant someone had given him the eleventh address, which meant someone in my careful little invisible life had sold me, or he'd dug me out himself, and either way the anonymity I'd treated as armor was tissue paper.

The second was that five hundred crowns was not just money. It was Marlowe's ledger closed. It was the dockmaster paid off. It was a boat ticket out of Gullwrack whenever I chose, to wherever I chose, with no one holding a marker against my name. Three years of running, priced and payable tonight.

Paranoia said *walk*. Paranoia had kept me alive this long, and I owed it a great deal.

But paranoia, I realized, standing in that gilded room with a collector's eyes on my back, only works if you're running *from* something. And lately—God help me—I'd started to suspect I was mostly running in place.

I let go of the latch.

"One condition," I said, turning around. "On the ice, my word is final. Not yours, not Reyes's. Anyone who argues with a call I make gets left at the last safe camp with the cook and the spare tent. Triple rate includes obedience, Mr. Vane. That's the price."

For a moment—just a moment—something moved behind his charm, quick and cold as a fish under ice. Then it was gone, and he was beaming again, extending his hand across the maps.

"Done," he said. "Welcome aboard, Miss Dravenmoor. I have a feeling this expedition will be one for the books."

Later—much later—I would think often about that sentence, and about how right he was, and how neither of us guessed in what way.

That night I signed his paper, took his draft to the dockmaster's office before it closed, and watched Marlowe's debt vanish from a ledger with a scratch of the clerk's pen. Walking back along the quay in the dark, the harbor lights shivering on black water, I made myself a promise: nine days. Get them in, get them out, take the money, disappear deeper.

And then, because promises to myself had never once held, I looked up at the north horizon, where the glacier lived behind its wall of cloud, white and silent and keeping whatever it kept—

—and somewhere out there, faint as memory, I swear the air hummed.
