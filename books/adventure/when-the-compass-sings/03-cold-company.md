## 3. Cold Company

The glacier announced itself three days before they saw it.

Silas felt it first in her teeth—a low pressure settling over the coast like a hand pressed flat against the sky—and then in the birds, who had stopped flying inland and begun circling the water instead, as if even they understood that something enormous was waiting to the north and it was better not to be beneath it when it spoke.

"You're smiling," said Priya Chandra, the team's photographer, falling into step beside her on the shingle beach. "That's either a good sign or a very bad one."

"Both," Silas said. "The barometer's falling faster than I'd like, but the wind's backing around to the southwest, which means we'll get a dry window tomorrow. And the birds—" she nodded at the wheeling gulls— "are feeding heavy. They only do that before weather they intend to sit out."

Priya squinted at her. "You read birds."

"I read everything." Silas adjusted the strap of her pack. "Out here, everything's talking. Most people just don't speak the language."

"And what's it saying now?"

"That we should make the moraine camp by dark and be grateful for it."

It was the longest sentence she'd volunteered in two days, and Priya looked quietly pleased about it, the way people do when a shy dog finally takes a treat from their hand. Silas hadn't meant to be shy. She'd meant to be invisible. But invisibility, she was learning, was difficult to maintain when you were the only person on the expedition who knew which snow bridge would hold a man's weight and which would fold him into a crevasse like a letter into an envelope.

The crew was nine, counting Vane. There was Priya, who photographed ice the way other people photographed children—tenderly, as though it might grow up someday. There were the Okafor twins, Emeka and Adaeze, riggers by trade, who bickered constantly and moved in perfect unison, which Silas found privately hilarious. There was Dr. Lena Marsh, the archaeologist Vane had retained for reasons he kept vague, a small fierce woman who carried more books than clothing and had already asked Silas eleven questions about glacial recession that Silas had answered with varying degrees of patience. There was the cook, Bram, a large gentle man whose stew had single-handedly raised morale on the second night from funeral to tolerable. And there were the porters, four local men hired out of the last village, led by a quiet, watchful fellow named Ivo who watched Silas the way she watched the ice—which is to say, continuously, and without sentiment.

And there was Tomas Reyes.

Tomas walked at the back of the column and said little, except to her, and what he said to her was never kind. He'd been hired as safety officer—the irony of that was not lost on her—and he performed his duties competently enough, checking harnesses, testing anchors. But every evening, without fail, he found a way to stand near her while she worked and let fall some small stone of a remark.

*The Kernvall incident,* he called it. Always *incident*, never *disaster*, never *the night six people went onto an icefield and only four came back*. As though it had been a fender-bender. A spilled drink.

She could have hated him for it. Some part of her did. But mostly she just endured him the way she endured bad weather: by knowing it would pass, and by refusing to give it anything to push against.

***

The moraine camp went up at dusk in the lee of a ridge the color of old iron. The glacier itself was still hidden behind the shoulder of the mountain, but its presence filled the valley the way a sleeping animal fills a room—you couldn't see it, but you could feel the weight of its breathing.

Bram produced stew. The twins argued about anchor placement until Adaeze won, which she did every time, and Emeka conceded with theatrical wounded dignity, which he also did every time. Dr. Marsh sat cross-legged on a crate reading by headlamp, muttering about basal till. It was, Silas thought, almost a pleasant evening, in the way that a knife is almost a spoon.

"Tide charts?"

Tomas had appeared beside her fire-ring without sound, which was a skill Silas respected and resented in equal measure. He nodded at the papers spread across her knees.

"Navigation," she said.

"On paper." He crouched, unhurried, and picked up the top sheet before she could stop him. His eyes moved across the columns of figures. "These are lunar tables. Spring tides." He looked up. "You're timing our approach to the moon."

"I'm timing our approach to the water. The moon's just how the water keeps its calendar."

"The cave opens twice," Tomas said. "Before calving season shuts the whole coast down. That's what Vane told us. That's why we're moving fast." He set the sheet down, carefully, deliberately, the way a man sets down a card he intends to win with later. "Under six hours each time, isn't it? That's what the fishermen say. Six hours, twice, and then the glacier starts throwing itself into the sea and nobody goes near that shore again for a year."

"Then we'd best not waste any of them arguing," Silas said.

He smiled—not warmly; nothing about Tomas Reyes was warm—but with a certain professional appreciation, the way one duelist appreciates another's footwork. "You know what I can't figure out, Montrose?" He used her surname like a tool he'd brought along specially. "Vane pays triple rates for a disgraced guide nobody else will touch, and everybody on this crew pretends not to know why. But I know why. I was there, after. I read the reports." He stood, brushing grit from his knee. "The Kernvall incident. Four survivors. Two dead. One guide who walked away before the inquiry finished taking evidence."

"I didn't walk away," Silas said quietly. "I was carried. On a stretcher. You'd know that if you read past the summary."

Something flickered across his face—there and gone, too quick to name. "Get your charts in order," he said, and walked off toward his tent.

Priya, who had witnessed the exchange from three meters away with the frank delight of a woman who has been starved for entertainment, wandered over and sat down uninvited. "I don't like him," she said.

"He's good at his job."

"So are rattlesnakes." Priya accepted a bowl of stew from Bram without looking away from Silas. "What happened at Kernvall? Really. You don't have to tell me. But everyone's carrying their version of it around like luggage, and luggage gets heavy."

Silas stirred her stew. Steam rose between them, smelling of salt and dried fish and Bram's heroic improvisation with a curry brick.

"A cornice collapsed above the route," she said. "Not where we were. Where we were going. Six of us were roped together below it. My partner—Wren Halloway—was leading. The avalanche took the slope out from under the rope team. Wren and one client went down with it." She heard her own voice and was faintly surprised by how level it was. Competence as armor worked on tone, too. "The inquiry wanted someone to blame. The insurance company wanted someone to blame. The clients' families wanted someone to blame. I was the guide. Guides are where blame pools, like water at the bottom of a slope."

"And was it your fault?"

Silas looked at her. "If I answer that honestly, you'll have a version of events you'll carry around like luggage. Are you sure you want it?"

Priya held her gaze for a long moment. Then she said, "No. Not yet. When you're ready." And she got up and went to photograph the last violet light on the ridge, leaving Silas alone with her stew and the strange, disorienting realization that someone on this expedition had just offered her the one thing no one had offered her in three years: the benefit of the doubt.

***

She stayed up after the camp slept.

This was habit older than Kernvall—the night hours belonged to her, had always belonged to her, the quiet time when the mountains stopped performing and simply existed, vast and indifferent and honest. She boiled water for tea she wouldn't drink and spread her charts out by lamplight, weighting the corners with stones.

The tide tables for the Siren Cave coast were a mess of contradictions, half rumor and half arithmetic. The fishermen of the last village had given her times that disagreed with the almanac by forty minutes; the almanac disagreed with the naval survey by twenty more. So she'd done what she always did: she'd taken all three sources and reconciled them against the thing none of them measured, which was the behavior of the sea itself. She'd spent two evenings at the last village watching the water drop against the harbor pilings, marking the levels with chalk, comparing what she saw to what the papers claimed. The sea, unlike committees, did not lie. It was merely difficult to interview.

Her reconciliation put extreme low tide at 0440 on the third morning from now, and again roughly a fortnight later—two windows, each under six hours, and then the calving season would begin in earnest and the whole coastline would become a shooting gallery of falling ice. After that, the cave would keep its secrets until next summer. Or forever. Glaciers were not sentimental about deadlines.

*Six hours,* she thought. *Twice.* It wasn't much. It was exactly enough, if nothing went wrong, and nothing ever went wrong, except always.

She turned to the second chart—the one she hadn't shown anyone—and studied the bathymetric lines Vane had provided. The approaches to the cave were a maze of drowned rock and shifting gravel bars, and the channel marked on Vane's chart ran through water that, according to her own chalk-marks on the harbor pilings, would be forty centimeters shallower than he believed. Which meant his timetable was wrong. Which meant if they followed his schedule, they'd arrive at the cave mouth with the tide already turning, and the sea does not negotiate.

She made the correction in pencil. Small, neat, decisive. Then she sat back and looked at it and felt the familiar cold settle into her chest—not fear, exactly, but its cousin: the knowledge that tomorrow she would have to tell Aldous Vane that his plan was wrong, and Aldous Vane did not strike her as a man who enjoyed being wrong.

Behind her, a boot crunched on scree. She closed the second chart before she'd fully decided to—reflex, pure reflex—and turned.

It was Ivo, the porter. He stood at the edge of the lamplight with a thermos and an expression of careful neutrality, and he said, in slow, accented English: "The water at the point. It turns forty minutes before your book says." He set the thermos down beside her, a peace offering or a test. "My father fished that coast thirty years. If your numbers match his, you are the first guide in a long time worth following."

Then he was gone, back into the dark, leaving Silas alone with the tea, the charts, and the uncomfortable warmth of being seen—and the colder knowledge, arriving right on schedule, that somewhere behind her, Tomas Reyes had been awake too. She'd heard his tent flap move ten minutes ago. She'd heard it stop moving directly behind her, where the light fell.

Where the lamplight threw her shadow long across the scree—

—and where, if a man stood in that shadow, he could see every line of the chart she'd tried to hide.

The glacier breathed somewhere in the darkness, a sound like a door opening very slowly in a very large house.

Two days to the first window. And now someone besides herself knew exactly when it opened.
