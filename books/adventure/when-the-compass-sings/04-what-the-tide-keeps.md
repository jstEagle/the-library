## 4. What the Tide Keeps

The tide chart did not lie, but it did not tell the whole truth either.

Silas had explained it to them three times on the walk down from the moraine camp: the sea pulled back from the glacier's foot twice a day like a man lifting a curtain to check the weather, and for a little under six hours it held that curtain up before letting it fall again. What the chart could not convey was the sound — the long wet exhale of the water withdrawing through channels it had spent ten thousand years carving, a sound like the world clearing its throat before saying something important.

"Six hours," Aldous Vane repeated, consulting his pocket watch with the reverence other men reserved for scripture. "And we are early. I do admire punctuality in the face of geology."

"You'll admire it more when the geology starts keeping its own schedule," Silas said. She was crouched at the lip of the shingle beach, reading the ice as she'd read it all week — the way she read everything, silently and slightly ahead of everyone else. The glacier wall rose out of the exposed seabed like the prow of a blue-black ship, streaked with ash from some eruption older than any map she owned. At its base, where the water had been an hour ago, a dark seam opened into the rock. The Siren Cave. Or the mouth of it, anyway. Legends were always bigger than their doorways.

Tomas Reyes stood apart from the group, arms crossed, watching her rather than the ice. He'd been doing that for three days. It was getting under her skin the way a splinter does — small, ignorable, until you pressed on it by accident.

"The floor will be slick," she said to the team generally, standing and brushing grit from her knees. "Headlamps on inside. Nobody wanders. If I say the word *turn*, you turn, no questions, no souvenirs." She looked at Vane on that last word, and he smiled his collector's smile, which was the smile of a man who intended to take souvenirs or die trying.

"Lead on, Ms. Dravenmoor," he said. "The tide is our clock, and I intend to spend every minute of it."

*Ms. Dravenmoor.* Not her real name, but close enough that only two people on this expedition knew the difference, and one of them was dead. She turned toward the cave mouth before her face could do anything about it.

* * *

Inside, the world changed key.

The passage widened after fifty meters into a cathedral of stone and old ice, and when the four headlamp beams swept across it, even Tomas made a sound — half breath, half word — that Silas chose generously not to identify. Stalactites hung in ranks like the pipes of some drowned organ. The walls glistened black and green, veined with mineral light that caught the beams and threw it back in slow, oily rainbows.

"It's real," whispered Priya Nair, the team's surveyor, a compact woman who had said perhaps forty words total since base camp and seemed to consider each one a budgetary decision. "Vane, it's actually real. You weren't exaggerating."

"I never exaggerate," Vane said happily. "I merely arrive first."

Silas let them have their wonder while she did her work. Floor conditions first: the stone was barnacled up to shoulder height, marking the tide's high reach, and below that line it was glazed with a film of silt that would turn treacherous the moment anyone got clever. Air movement next — there was a current, faint but steady, flowing *out* of the deeper passage. That meant another opening somewhere. Caves breathed; you just had to notice whether they were inhaling or exhaling. This one was exhaling, slow and cold, smelling of stone and something older than stone.

And then there were the marks.

She saw the first one by accident, her beam snagging on a groove in the wall at eye height. She stopped. Raised her lamp. A spiral, cut deep into the rock, worn smooth at its edges by time or touch. Around it, smaller marks — chevrons, dots arranged in arcs, lines that might have been waves or might have been a mountain range seen by someone who loved mountains enough to argue with them.

"Priya," she said quietly. "Come look at this. Tell me what you see."

Priya came. Looked. Was silent for so long that Vane drifted over, then Tomas, then the porter, Anselm, who was carrying more rope than any man should be asked to love.

"These aren't recent," Priya said finally. Her voice had gone careful, the way voices do when they're handling something fragile. "The patina — the mineral redeposition over the cuts — that's centuries. Maybe more. Silas, there's no record of settlement on this coast before the whaling era. Nothing permanent. Nothing that *carved*."

"There is now," said Vane, and his voice had changed. The charm was still in it, but underneath the charm was hunger, plain as a lit window in a dark house. He ran his gloved fingers above the spiral without touching it, a man restraining himself from touching. "Do you understand what this means? Someone stood here and made meaning. Before maps. Before names. Before anyone thought this coast was worth a name." He laughed, low and delighted. "I've spent eleven years chasing rumors of this place. Eleven years, and men at clubs telling me the Siren Cave was a sailor's fable, a hole in the water and nothing in the hole."

"Fables usually have a body count," Tomas said. His voice was flat, but Silas caught the glance he flicked at her — quick, assessing, gone. Testing her. She kept her face neutral and moved her lamp along the wall.

More marks. More spirals, spaced with a regularity that stopped looking decorative and started looking deliberate. She counted paces between them without deciding to. Forty-two. Then forty-two again. Then forty-two.

"They're spaced," she heard herself say. "Evenly. That's not art. That's a *path*."

"A path to what?" Anselm asked, and his voice echoed back at him from the dark, sounding suddenly younger.

Nobody answered, because nobody knew, and because the passage ahead bent left around a buttress of ice fused to the rock, and because Vane was already walking toward it with the confidence of a man who had never once been the person a cave decided to keep.

"Stay behind me," Silas said, and took the lead.

* * *

The second chamber was smaller, drier, and wrong.

Not wrong the way danger is wrong — she checked for that first, always, out of habit and history both. No unstable ceiling, no hidden crevasse under the silt, no carbon-monoxide stillness in the air. Wrong the way a room is wrong when someone has lived in it. There was a flat shelf of stone at waist height, swept clean of debris. Against the far wall, sheltered from the drip line, sat a stack of items arranged with intention: a tin box gone to rust-bloom, a coil of fishing line, three candles stubbed to different heights, and a folded square of oilcloth that crinkled, when Priya knelt and opened it with surveyor's delicacy, around a journal.

The journal was modern. That was the wrongness. Everything else in the chamber belonged to some unmeasured past, and this — this was bound canvas, machine-stitched, its cover bleached gray by years of damp. Dates were inked on the spine in a hand that slanted hard to the right.

Silas did not move toward it. She made herself stand very still and let the others look first, because her heart had begun doing something complicated and she needed a moment to negotiate with it.

"Someone's been here," Anselm said. "Recently. Within—"

"Years," Priya said. "Look at the candle lengths. Different visits. Many visits." She lifted the journal, weighed it in her hands, and — credit where due — looked to Silas before opening it. "Should we?"

It was Vane who answered, stepping forward with his hand already out. "We absolutely—"

"It stays with the survey inventory," Silas said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "It gets photographed, catalogued, and read in order, in front of witnesses. That's how evidence works."

"Evidence of what?" Vane's eyes glittered. "A castaway? A hermit? Ms. Dravenmoor, if there is a human story in this rock, it belongs to—"

"To whoever wrote it," Silas said, "until proven otherwise. And to whatever authorities exist once we're off this ice. That was the agreement, Mr. Vane. Documentation, not salvage."

Something passed across Vane's face — fast, quickly smoothed away, but she caught it, and she thought Tomas caught it too. He was watching Vane now instead of her, and there was a new set to his jaw that she recognized, because she'd worn it herself once, in another country, in another life.

Priya opened the journal to the first page and angled her lamp.

The handwriting hit Silas like a hand around her throat.

She knew that hand. She had known it across camp tables and route plans and letters sent from three towns over, letters that started *Dear Silas, stop being stubborn* and ended *yours, W*. A hand that slanted hard to the right, with the crossbars of the T's drawn long, like a person reaching for something.

Wren Halloway's handwriting. On a page dated fourteen months after the Kernvall avalanche. Fourteen months after Silas had stood at a memorial service and watched a coffin lowered into ground that held, as far as any rescue team could prove, nothing at all.

The room tilted. She put her palm flat against the cold wall and breathed, once, twice, the way she'd taught younger guides to breathe when the mountain moved under them. *You don't panic. You assess.*

"Silas?" Priya's voice, distant. "Are you all right? You've gone white."

"Air's thin," Silas managed. "Keep reading. Read it aloud. All of it."

While Priya read — entries about fish and tides and the strange kindness of dry stone, entries that grew more fluent, more settled, more *alive* with each passing month — Silas drifted, ostensibly checking the chamber's rear passage. Ostensibly. In truth her eyes had snagged on something at the shelf's edge, half-tucked beneath the tin box, glinting dull orange in the lamplight.

Rust. The bloom of it unmistakable. And stamped into the corroded face of the carabiner, still legible beneath the decay, a logo she would know blindfolded, in the dark, at the bottom of the sea: a stylized peak split by a lightning stroke, and beneath it the word **KERNVALL**.

Her company's mark. Her expedition. The gear that had gone into the ice with seven people, one of whom was supposed to be in a grave two countries south of here.

Silas looked over her shoulder. Priya was reading. Vane was photographing the markers with barely contained ecstasy. Anselm was repacking rope. Tomas was watching the tunnel mouth, doing his job, doing it well.

She picked up the carabiner. It was lighter than it should have been, rust having eaten most of its argument. She turned it once in her palm, feeling the ghost of the gate mechanism, stiff with salt and years.

Then, before her conscience could finish drawing up its objections, she closed her fist around it and slid it into the inner pocket of her jacket, against her heartbeat, where it lay like a coal that refused to go out.

Because there was a question the journal hadn't answered yet, and she needed to be alone with it before anyone else asked it out loud:

If Wren Halloway had walked out of an avalanche that killed everyone else — why had she never come home?

Behind her, Priya turned a page and went quiet. When she spoke again, her voice had changed entirely.

"Silas. The last entry. It says—" She stopped, swallowed. "*Someone else has been coming here. I've heard them on the ice. If you're reading this, don't wait for the second tide.*"

The glacier, far above and outside, groaned — long and low, like something turning over in its sleep.

Silas's hand pressed flat against the carabiner in her pocket, and the dread that bloomed there was, for the first time in five years, tangled hopelessly with hope.
