## 5. The Man Who Wasn't Buried

The second chamber opened like a held breath.

Silas felt it before she saw it—the way the air changed against her face, warmer by a degree or two, moving instead of dead. In ice caves, moving air meant a way through somewhere, or at least a chimney to the world above. She raised her fist and the team stopped behind her, headlamps swinging.

"Air's live," she said. "Stay off the walls here. The meltwater's been working this section for centuries."

"How can you tell?" Priya Chandrasekaran asked—Vane's archivist, the only member of the crew who seemed genuinely interested in what they found rather than what it might sell for.

"Smell it." Silas pulled her balaclava down and breathed. Salt, stone, and beneath both something green and mineral, like a garden under snow. "The sea's in here somewhere. And where the sea gets in, things grow."

They followed the passage down over shelves of blue ice worn smooth as river stones, and then the ceiling lifted all at once and they stood in a cathedral of rock and frozen water, their lamps swallowed by darkness that had never once known daylight.

It was Priya who found the alcove first—or rather, who found the light.

"There," she whispered, pointing to a seam high on the far wall where a thin blade of grey gleamed. "Is that—is that *fabric*?"

Silas crossed the chamber with her heart doing something complicated against her ribs. The alcove was cut into the living rock above the ice line, sheltered from the worst of the damp, and someone had made a home of it. A groundsheet, patched twice with sailcloth. A spirit stove, its burner furred with rust but whole. Crates stacked with a neatness she knew in her bones, because she'd spent three years teasing Wren Halloway about it—*you alphabetize your freeze-dried rations, you absolute lunatic*—

She stopped walking.

"Dravenmoor?" Tomas Reyes was somewhere behind her. His voice sounded very far away. "What is it?"

The crates were stamped with the logo of the Kernvall Alpine Survey. The same logo as the carabiner in her pocket, which suddenly weighed as much as an anchor. And beside them, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, sat a tin document box—the kind surveyors used for logbooks—and on top of it, weighted down with a smooth white stone, a journal.

She didn't touch it. Not yet. She stood there with her lamp shaking slightly despite herself, and let the others come up around her, and heard Vane's sharp intake of breath when he saw the stamp.

"Well," Aldous Vane said softly. "Well, well."

"It could be anyone's," Tomas said. "Salvage. Another expedition."

"The Kernvall Survey lost four people nine years ago," Priya said quietly. She'd done her reading; of course she had. "Avalanche on the eastern col. Two bodies were recovered." She looked at Silas. "Two were not."

Silas reached out and picked up the journal.

Her hands remembered how to do it even while the rest of her went somewhere cold and quiet. She'd held Wren's field journals a hundred times—borrowed them, argued with them, read passages aloud around campfires in a terrible impression of Wren's clipped, precise voice until Wren threw a mitten at her head. She knew the weight of them. She knew the way Wren pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages.

She opened the cover.

The handwriting was Wren's.

* * *

**14 March. Day 9 since the col.**

Leg is bad but mending. I have stopped expecting anyone to come and started planning as though no one will, which is either wisdom or surrender. Found this chamber on day 4. The tide empties it twice daily. There is dry rock above the ice line and the air moves, which means there is a way out that does not require the col. I will find it when the leg allows.

I keep writing to you, Silas, though you will never read this. It keeps me sane. Or some version of sane. The version that talks to ghosts.

Silas read it standing up, which was fortunate, because if she'd been sitting her legs would not have agreed to hold her afterward.

The dates ran backward in her mind like water finding a crack. Nine years ago, March, the avalanche on the eastern col. She had been two hundred meters downslope when it came, roped to Dev Anand, and the mountain had taken Wren Halloway and Marcus Oyelaran between one heartbeat and the next. They had searched for six days. The insurers had searched for four. Then everyone had signed the papers and gone home, and Silas Dravenmoor had spent nine years learning how to be a person whose best friend was a closed casket question mark.

And here was Wren's handwriting, marching forward through the years. Day 9. Day 22. **Day 40: caught three fish in the tidal pool. The leg holds. I have named the seal. This is possibly a warning sign.**

"She survived," Priya breathed. "Oh my God. She *survived*, and she walked down into—"

"Read the last entry," Silas said. Her voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice.

Priya took the journal gently from her hands and turned to the final pages. Her lamp trembled too now. She read aloud, slowly, because the handwriting had changed—looser, faster, the letters of someone writing quickly by bad light.

* * *

**2 November.**

Someone has been coming here.

I thought at first I was imagining it. Nine years alone will do that; I talk to a seal, after all. But the evidence doesn't imagine itself. Three weeks ago one of my fish traps was emptied and reset wrong—I set my traps left-handed since the shoulder injury, and whoever reset it did it right-handed. Last week a ration tin vanished from the outer cache. Yesterday I found boot prints in the mud past the second chamber. Size eleven. Modern sole pattern. Not mine.

Nine years, and in the last three months, someone else has found the cave. Twice I've heard voices echoing down the ice tunnels when the tide is low—too distorted to make out words, but there were two of them, maybe three. They don't come deep. They take things from the outer chambers and leave.

I don't know if they know I'm here. I don't know if they care. What I know is this: whatever they're taking, they're taking it *out*, which means they have a route I haven't found, or they have a boat, or they have permission from someone who should not be giving it.

If anyone reads this: the markers in the third gallery are mine. I carved them over four winters. They are not decoration. Follow the spiral marks toward moving air and you will find the upper exit. Do not trust the low passage after rain.

And if the person coming here reads this: I see your boot prints. I count your thefts. Come to the fire sometime and explain yourself, or stop taking my traps, because winter is coming and I am done being generous.

—W.H.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a long moment. The sound of the glacier filled the silence—that vast, slow groaning, like a ship turning over in its sleep.

"Boot prints," Vane repeated. His voice had shed its charm entirely; underneath it was something quick and hungry. "Modern soles. Someone's been raiding this site." He turned to Silas, and his eyes were bright with avarice and calculation in equal measure. "You knew. You saw the carabiner yesterday and you said nothing."

"I said the cave predates any known settlement," Silas said. "That's true."

"You said nothing about *her*."

"I didn't know about her." The words scraped coming out. "Nobody knew. We buried empty coffins."

Tomas had taken off his glove and was running his bare fingers along the edge of the journal's pages, frowning. "These entries span years. She's been living here nine years? Off fish traps and—" he gestured at the crates "—survey rations?"

"And whatever the sea gives up," Silas said. Her mind was moving fast now, grief and hope shoving each other aside like drunks in a doorway. Wren alive. Wren, nine years alone in the dark with a bad leg and a seal for company, carving navigation marks into the rock for strangers who might someday need them. Wren, whose last written words were a complaint about stolen fish traps.

Wren, who had been writing letters to a ghost, and the ghost had just walked into her home wearing a headlamp.

"We have to find her," Priya said. "Now. Tonight."

"The tide's coming back in four hours," Silas said. "We go deeper, we follow the spiral marks, and we do it fast and quiet. If those boot prints belong to whoever's been raiding this place, they may still be using these tunnels."

"Or," Vane said, and his smile returned like frost forming, "we secure the artifacts first. The journal alone is worth—" He caught himself, recalibrated. "The journal is *priceless*. Historical documentation of survival against impossible odds. And the crates, the equipment, the markers—"

"They're hers," Silas said.

"They're *evidence*, Dravenmoor. Of a discovery that will redraw every map of this coast. And you—" he stepped closer, lowering his voice so the others had to lean in to hear, which was of course the point "—of all people should understand the value of controlling what the record says. Your name isn't on any manifest for this trip. Interesting, that. Almost as interesting as why a washed-up harbor guide gets triple rate to lead a collector to a cave she clearly already knew about."

The chamber seemed to tilt. Somewhere behind her, Tomas made a small sound—not quite a word.

Silas held Vane's gaze and thought about the carabiner in her pocket, stamped with a logo from a dead expedition, and about a journal that ended with a woman alone in the dark counting someone else's boot prints, and about the fact that the tide waited for no one's bargains.

"The artifacts stay where they are until we find her," Silas said. "Then we discuss it. Tide's turning in four hours, Mr. Vane. I'd spend them wisely."

She turned and walked toward the third gallery, following the faint spiral carved into the rock, and behind her she heard the crew fall in—one set of boots eager, one reluctant, one silent—and beneath the groan of the glacier, very faintly, carried on that living thread of moving air:

a sound that might have been the sea.

Or might have been a whistle. Four notes, rising.

The signal she and Wren had invented a lifetime ago, for *come find me*.
