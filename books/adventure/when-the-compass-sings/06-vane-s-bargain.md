## 6. Vane's Bargain

The storm came in off the water like a debt collector, and Silas felt the same cold arithmetic in her chest that she always did when weather turned: what can I save, and what will it cost me?

They had retreated to the tents above the tideline, the first window closed behind them, six hours spent inside the Siren Cave with wonders none of them had words for. Carved markers older than any map. Stone channels worn smooth by hands that predated every settlement in the archives. And one rusted carabiner with a logo she knew better than her own heartbeat.

She'd pocketed it before Priya's headlamp swept past. A small theft. The smallest lie of her life, and already it sat in her chest pocket like a coal that wouldn't cool.

Now the wind screamed down from the glacier and the camp stank of wet wool and stove fuel, and Aldous Vane was being charming about it, which was somehow worse.

"Extra rations all around," he announced, dealing out chocolate bars like a man who had never once been told no by weather. "We've earned them. Six hours in the oldest room on earth." He unwrapped his own bar and ate it in two bites, which ruined the elegance somewhat. "Silas, you were extraordinary in there."

"I read a tide chart," she said.

"You read *that* tide chart," he said, "which is not the same thing." He settled onto the supply crate across from her, knees nearly touching hers in the cramped mess tent, and smiled the smile from their first meeting—the one that made him look like a man who collected friends the way he collected artifacts. "Tell me about Kernvall."

The tent went very quiet. Outside, Priya was arguing with the generator; Tomas's footsteps crunched somewhere near the ice edge, pacing his nightly circuit. Rain hit the canvas like thrown gravel.

"I don't know what you mean," Silas said.

"Of course you don't." Vane produced something from his coat—a folded printout, soft at the creases from handling—and laid it on the crate between them with the tenderness of a man setting down a winning card. "I mean this. Expedition manifest, three years ago. Two guides listed. One guide unaccounted for. One guide who walked out alone and never gave a statement that satisfied anyone." He tapped the paper. "You have your mother's jaw, Ms. Dravenmoor. It's a distinctive jaw. I collect distinctive things."

Her mouth had gone dry. "You hired me because—"

"Because you're competent? Obviously. Because you're cheap and discreet and desperate enough to say yes to short notice?" He shrugged, apologetic, a man confessing to a minor social faux pas rather than an ambush. "All true. But mostly because when we find something impossible in that cave—and we have—I wanted the one person alive whose judgment about impossible things is already compromised. Whose discretion isn't a virtue. It's a *need*."

The stove ticked. Somewhere beyond the canvas, the glacier groaned, long and low, like a ship changing its mind.

"You've got the wrong woman," Silas said. "I'm nobody. Ask anyone in Harrow Point."

"I did, actually. For three days before I wrote that letter." His eyes were kind, which was the cruelest part. "Nobody there could tell me anything except that you paid cash, fixed boats beautifully, and watched the tide tables like other people watch the news. That's not anonymity, Silas. That's camouflage. And camouflage only works on people who are hiding."

She thought about the letter. She thought about burning it, the match held so close the paper curled, and how rent had won, and how the smuggler's debt had won harder. Every step of the last month rearranged itself in her memory into a corridor with a door closing at the far end.

"What do you want?" she asked. Her voice came out flat and steady, guide's voice, and she hated how useful it was even now.

Vane leaned forward. Up close, the charm thinned and she could see what ran underneath it—not cruelty exactly, but appetite, enormous and patient, the thing that had bought his way onto three continents. "Tomorrow is the second window. The last one before calving season. Six hours, maybe less if the shelf goes early—it's been talking to us all day, haven't you heard it?" He said it lightly, but his hand had gone still on his knee. "In that cave is a collection that would end every argument about the peopling of this coast. Universities would fight for a decade over the right to study it. Museums would empty their vaults. And if it sits here another season, half of it drowns or gets sealed under a hundred tons of ice."

"So you want to take it."

"I want to *rescue* it," he said, with genuine feeling, which frightened her more than greed would have. "Quietly. Before the authorities arrive and spend four years filing paperwork while the sea eats it. You know how bureaucracy moves. You've been running from it for three years."

"And you need me because—"

"Because I don't know the ice. You do. Because my crew follows you now, not me—you've seen it, don't pretend you haven't. Priya checks with you before she checks with me. Even Reyes, God help him, watches your hands when the wind shifts." He spread his palms. "And because if anyone ever asks how Aldous Vane came to remove artifacts from a protected site, the answer will be that his disgraced guide misled him. Poor man. Deceived by a woman with a falsified name and a dead partner and a history of abandoning expeditions."

The word *abandoning* landed where he'd aimed it. She kept her face still. Three years of practice.

"Wren didn't abandon anything," Silas said quietly.

"No," Vane agreed. "Wren Halloway died. Presumably. The mountain says so. The insurers say so. The families of four other people say so, and they've said it to your face, haven't they? At the hearings?" He tilted his head. "Grief makes people cruel. You'd know."

Outside, Priya killed the generator. The sudden silence rang. In it, Silas heard the glacier again—that deep, grinding syllable, repeated, like something practicing a word.

"If I refuse," she said.

"Then tomorrow morning I make a satellite call, and by the time you've led us home, there are journalists in Harrow Point and investigators at your landlady's door, and every guide's association on two coasts has your real name pinned to the Kernvall file. You'll never work again. Not quietly, anyway." He said it without heat, almost gently, laying out the weather forecast. "Or—and I genuinely prefer this option—you help me carry out what matters, we all get rich enough to forget each other, and your secret stays exactly as buried as it's been. Everyone keeps what they came for. Isn't that what guides do? Get people safely to what they want?"

*Get people safely to what they want.* Her father's phrase, or near enough. She wondered, distantly, whether Vane had researched that too, whether he knew the saying had been carved on her father's boat, whether there was any corner of her left that wasn't in his folder.

"The markers aren't yours to take," she said.

"The markers are going to be under forty meters of ice by June. Sentiment doesn't float, Silas."

"They're not markers," she heard herself say, and stopped.

Vane went still. "What did you say?"

Too late. The coal in her chest pocket burned through the fabric. She looked at the stove, at the rain, anywhere but his face, and understood she had just handed him the second knife.

"Not markers," he repeated softly. "You know what they are. You recognized something in that chamber. Something specific." He rose, unhurried, and crouched beside her pack where it leaned against the tent wall, and before she could move he'd lifted out the journal—Wren's journal, the one she'd taken from the alcove, the one whose dates marched straight through the avalanche and out the other side into a spring that should never have existed.

"That's mine," she said, and her voice cracked on it, and she despised herself for the crack.

"It's evidence," Vane said. "Of what, I wonder." He weighed it in his hand, not opening it, watching her instead, reading her the way she read cloud banks. "Three years ago you lost a partner. Tonight you're sitting on her handwriting and lying to my face about carvings. Which means either you found a forgery—which would be remarkable—or—" He let it hang. He was too intelligent to finish the sentence. Finishing it would give her something to deny.

"Give it back," Silas said.

"In the morning," Vane said, tucking it inside his coat, "you and I go in first. Ahead of the others. We take what matters, we come back before the crew knows, and everyone walks away whole—including Wren Halloway's reputation, including yours, including poor Mr. Reyes, who I suspect would be devastated to learn what his colleague has been hiding." He paused at the tent flap, silhouetted against the grey churn of the storm, and for one moment the charm dropped entirely and she saw the collector underneath, the hunger wearing a gentleman like a coat. "Sleep well, Silas. You've always struck me as a woman who makes the right choice around dawn."

The flap fell shut behind him.

She sat alone in the ticking dark, hands shaking, doing the arithmetic she always did: what can I save, and what will it cost?

The answer arrived with the sound of boots on gravel—Tomas Reyes ducking into the tent, shaking off rain, freezing when he saw her face.

"Guide," he said slowly. "Why does Vane have a journal?"

* * *

He didn't sit. He stood with his back to the tent pole, arms crossed, water dripping off his hood onto the floorboards, and Silas realized with a strange clarity that she had never actually looked at him before—not properly. She'd looked at the testimony, at the courtroom, at the man-shaped reason her name was mud in three valleys. She'd never looked at the tired eyes above the frost-burned cheeks.

"He took it," she said. "From my pack."

"Your pack." His voice was careful. "Why is Wren Halloway's expedition journal in your pack?"

The question hung there, and Silas understood that whatever she answered next would decide everything. Lie, and lose the only ally inside the camp. Tell the truth, and hand Tomas Reyes the power to destroy her twice.

She was so tired. Three years tired. And the glacier outside was talking, and the tide would turn in nine hours, and somewhere past the icefall a chamber full of impossible carvings waited with its impossible dates, and she had run out of corridors.

"Because the dates don't stop," Silas said. "Tomas. The entries keep going. Past the avalanche. Past the day she died." She pulled the folded printout of the manifest from where Vane had left it, and set it on the crate between them, next to the space where the journal had been. "And someone else has been coming to that cave. Someone who isn't us."

Tomas stared at the paper. Then at her. The rain filled the silence, patient as a witness.

"Vane wants us in the cave first tomorrow," she went on, quieter. "Him and me. To strip it before the others wake. And if I refuse, he burns what's left of my life." She laughed, short and joyless. "So. You've testified against me once already. Consider this your chance to hear both sides."

Something moved through Tomas's face then—something old and exhausted, the look of a man carrying weight he'd stopped noticing until someone pointed at it. He lowered himself onto the crate across from her, slowly, like his knees had opinions.

"The insurers coached me," he said. "Did you know that? Three weeks before the hearing. Sat me in a hotel room in Kernvall town with a lawyer who kept using the word *negligence* like it was a hammer." He rubbed his jaw. "I told myself it was the truth. I told myself for three years it was the truth."

"Tomas—"

"Shut up a minute, guide. Let me get through this before I lose my nerve." He exhaled. "Here's what I think. I think Aldous Vane didn't hire a guide. I think he hired a hostage. And I think—" his eyes flicked toward the flap, toward the storm, toward the glacier muttering in the dark, "—I think whatever's writing in that journal is the reason he's really here. And I think we need to see those pages before he does."

Silas looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the wind shifted, and beneath it—faint, rhythmic, unmistakable—came a new sound from the direction of the icefall. Not a groan this time.

A crack. Long. Clean. Like the first syllable of a sentence the mountain had been waiting three years to finish.

Tomas heard it too. They looked at each other in the lamplight.

"That's early," he said.

"That's very early," Silas said, and reached for her boots.
