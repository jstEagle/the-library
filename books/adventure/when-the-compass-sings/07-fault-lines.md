## 7. Fault Lines

The camp slept in the blue half-light of a glacier night, and Silas did not.

She had waited until Vane's snores rose from his tent in their self-satisfied rhythm, until the cook fire burned down to orange eyes, until even the wind seemed to lose interest. Then she'd taken Wren's journal to the far side of her tent, propped the headlamp against her pack, and begun to copy.

Not steal. Copy. There was a difference, she told herself, and the difference was that she intended to give everything back. The journal was evidence—of what, she didn't yet know, but evidence behaved badly around collectors. Aldous Vane looked at old things the way other men looked at unattended wallets.

*Day 41,* she wrote, matching Wren's slanted hand stroke for stroke into her own notebook. *The fish are gone but the tide still brings things. Found a boot today. Not mine.*

Silas's pen stopped. She pressed her wrist against her mouth to keep the sound in. *Found a boot today. Not mine.* Only Wren would write grief like an inventory item. Only Wren would make you laugh about it two years too late, alone, at one in the morning, on the wrong side of the world.

*Day 44. Someone has been in the lower chamber. Wax drippings, not mine. I've moved my supplies and marked the walls so I can find my way in the dark. If they come again, I'll see them before they see me.*

So it was true. Someone else had been coming here—and Wren had been watching for them, patient as stone, for who knew how long. Silas copied faster now, the lamplight trembling with her hand.

"You always were a terrible liar," said a voice behind her, "but an even worse forger."

She was on her feet before the sentence ended, journal clutched to her chest like a child, headlamp knocked askew so the light swung wild across the ice. Tomas Reyes stood at the edge of her lamp's reach, hands visible, empty, the universal grammar of *I'm not here to hurt you.* His breath fogged between them.

"How long," she said.

"Since you left the fire. You're quiet, Dravenmoor, but not that quiet." He nodded at the journal. "I'm not going to take it."

"Everyone says that right before they take it."

"Fair." He crouched slowly, deliberately, and sat down on an ice block like a man settling in for weather. "Then I'll say something else. Sit down, Silas. Please. I've been rehearsing this conversation for three days and if you run I'll have to do it all over again to your back, which is worse for both of us."

She didn't sit. But she didn't run either, and after a moment he took that for the concession it was.

"I testified against you," he said. "Kernvall. You know that."

"I was there."

"You weren't in the room." He rubbed his jaw, and in the swinging light Silas saw how tired he looked—not the tiredness of the trail but of some older, heavier cargo. "I told the inquiry what the insurers' lawyers told me to say. That you deviated from the route. That you ignored the forecast. That Wren Halloway's death traced back to your judgment." He exhaled. "None of it was true. And I've known it wasn't true every day since."

The wind filled the silence, because Silas couldn't.

"Why?" Her voice came out scraped raw. "Why would you—"

"Because Meridian Assurance held my brother's medical debt. Forty thousand. They bought it up when his clinic folded, and then they offered me a trade: my testimony against the paper." He laughed, short and joyless. "They dress it up nicely. 'A statement consistent with the findings of our internal review.' As if they'd found anything. As if anyone had looked. They needed someone to blame who couldn't afford lawyers, and you—" he gestured at her, at the ruined name, the hidden manifests, the whole geography of her exile— "you were perfect. Disgraced in two countries already. No one would fight for you."

"They were right," Silas said quietly. "No one did."

"No one could afford to." He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "I signed because of Diego. I've regretted it since the ink dried. And then Vane puts out a call for guides, and your name is on the list—the fake name, but I've read enough forged documents to know handwriting under pressure—and I thought, well. Here it is. The bill coming due."

"So this is blackmail after all."

"It's the opposite of blackmail, you stubborn woman." For the first time something warm cracked through his voice—exasperation, almost affection. "I'm telling you because you deserve to hear it from me, not from Vane. Because Vane knows. All of it. Why do you think he hired us both? A blackmailed guide and the man who buried her—he's building himself a crew of people with hooks in them. Leverage is cheaper than loyalty."

Silas lowered herself onto the ice block across from him, the journal still against her ribs. The lamplight steadied as her hand did.

"He offered me triple rate," she said. "Short notice. I should have known no one pays triple for a walk."

"Nobody pays triple for anything honest." Tomas studied her a long moment. "What's in the book?"

And there it was—the fault line, running straight through the middle of her. She could lie. Lying had kept her alive for two years; she was good at it in the way that drowning men are good at holding their breath. Or she could hand a man who had once sold her out the last true thing she had.

"Read it yourself," she said, and passed it over.

He read by the tilted lamp, lips moving slightly, and Silas watched his face do what hers had done: confusion, then dawning horror, then something that looked almost like awe.

"This is Halloway's hand," he said. "This is—these dates go past the avalanche. Past the recovery operation. Past the memorial service, Silas. I attended the memorial service." He looked up. "She was writing about the memorial service. In past tense. From inside a cave."

"She's alive."

He closed the journal carefully, the way you close a door on a sleeping person. "That's not possible."

"The dates are possible. The handwriting is possible. The carabiner in my pocket is possible." She pulled it out, let the lamplight find the Kernvall stamp. "Someone survived, and someone else has been visiting this cave, and Vane wants to strip it bare before any authority on earth sees a single artifact. Those are the facts. What we do with them is the only question left."

Tomas was silent for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had changed—lighter somehow, as though a weight had been set down and he hadn't yet learned to walk without it.

"When we get out," he said, "I'll recant. Publicly. On the record. Whatever it costs."

"It might cost you Diego's care."

"Diego deserves a brother who can look at himself in the mirror more than he deserves the treatments I can't look at myself while paying for." He shrugged, awkward, embarrassed by his own honesty. "Besides. Maybe the truth pays better than Meridian does. It certainly can't pay worse."

Silas laughed—a real laugh, rusty, surprising them both. "You always were an optimist."

"I testified against a mountain guide and lived. I contain multitudes."

* * *

From Tomas Reyes's point of view, the next forty-eight hours unfolded like watching a rope team come apart in slow motion—which, given his profession, was a thing he had seen, and never forgotten.

It started small. Vane began issuing orders that bypassed Silas entirely: Priya to catalog artifacts by value rather than location, Marcus to rig a sledge for "specimen transport," the cook to lighten the food stores to make weight for stone. Small orders, reasonable-sounding orders, each one a single degree off true. It was the navigation trick every bad leader Tomas had ever known used—never steer hard enough that anyone notices, just keep bending, keep bending, until the destination has changed and it's too late to argue.

By the second evening, the crew had split along lines nobody had drawn aloud. Priya and Marcus sat with Vane at dinner, laughing at his stories, their eyes going to the crates. The cook, old Halvard, sat pointedly apart, and when Vane asked him to reduce rations again, Halvard said, "We eat like mountaineers or we don't climb like mountaineers," and that was the end of that conversation.

Tomas watched Silas navigate it all without saying much. She fixed Priya's crampon strap. She re-rigged Marcus's sledge knots properly, silently undoing Vane's shortcuts. She checked the tide tables three times and marked the final window in red: tomorrow, dawn, six hours, and then the sea took the road away until calving season ended—and possibly forever, the way the ice had been talking lately.

After dinner he caught her arm near the gear line.

"The final window," he said. "Vane wants the full load out. Every crate."

"I know."

"He'll order it mid-crossing. He'll order it when the ice starts moving, because that's when he'll panic, and panicked rich men don't do math." Tomas glanced toward Vane's tent, where lamplight glowed and Vane's voice rose and fell in cheerful phone conversation—satellite phone, the only one in camp, calls nobody was invited to overhear. "When he gives that order, someone has to say no. Out loud. In front of everyone."

"That someone will be me."

"I know that too." He hesitated. "The question is whether anyone follows you. Priya's got a daughter's tuition in those crates, figuratively speaking. Marcus owes Vane's foundation his whole career. You need them to choose you over their own arithmetic, and you haven't given them a reason yet."

Silas looked out at the glacier, blue-white under the strange bright dark, calving somewhere deep in its throat with a sound like a door closing in another house.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "I'll tell them the truth. All of it. Kernvall, the testimony, the journal, Wren—all of it. Let them choose with the whole board showing."

Tomas nodded slowly. "That's either the bravest or the stupidest—"

"Don't finish that. I've heard both versions."

But as they turned in, as the camp settled and the glacier groaned through its long cold dreams, neither of them mentioned the other sound—the one beneath the groaning, rhythmic and faint, carried up from the sea cave below.

Footsteps. Patient as stone. Coming up the old marked way, toward the light of their fires.

Someone had been watching the camp. And tonight, at last, they were done waiting.
