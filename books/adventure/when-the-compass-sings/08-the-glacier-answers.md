## 8. The Glacier Answers

The tide turned at 3:40 in the morning, and the glacier answered an hour early.

Silas stood on the black shingle below the ice face, headlamp beam cutting a cone through sea fog, and listened to it—the deep, wet groan rolling up from somewhere inside the glacier's gut, like a whale mourning in another ocean. She had heard that sound twice before in her life. Once above Kernvall. Once in her dreams for six years after.

"Everyone off the beach," she said quietly.

Vane was already halfway to the cave mouth, two porters behind him hauling sledges wrapped in oilcloth. He turned, and even at forty meters she could see the shine of his smile. "The charts say we have until ten."

"The charts say a lot of things." She pointed her chin at the glacier. "That sound means the serac field above the entrance is shedding weight. It doesn't mean it's falling yet. It means it's *thinking* about it. We go in fast, we work fast, and we are out of that chamber by eight. Not nine. Eight."

Tomas appeared at her shoulder, breath steaming. He'd barely slept—she'd seen his lamplight burning past two—but his voice was steady when he said, "You heard her. Move."

And they moved. That was the thing Silas noticed, the thing that lodged under her ribs like a splinter: they moved because Tomas said so, and because she'd said so first, and nobody looked to Vane at all. The collector noticed it too. She watched him notice, watched something cold settle behind his charm like frost forming on glass.

* * *

Inside, the Siren Cave did its trick again. The tide pools caught their lamplight and threw it back doubled, tripled, until the walls seemed hung with constellations. The carved markers ran along the left wall in their ancient procession—spirals, chevrons, the long dotted lines that Silas now understood were routes, someone's careful map of safe passage drawn centuries before anyone thought to name this coast.

Wren's alcove waited at the back, exactly as they'd left it three days ago: the supply cache, the journal, the small cairn of stones Wren had stacked with her particular crooked precision. Silas touched the top stone as she passed. It was warm. Or she imagined it was. Six years of imagining made it hard to tell the difference.

They worked. Vane directed the packing of artifacts with the tenderness other men reserved for infants—carved bone toggles, a bronze fitting green with age, the wax-sealed fragments they'd catalogued on the first visit. The porters loaded sledges. Priya Kaur, the expedition's photographer, knelt at the tide line photographing the markers before the water rose over them, and Silas found herself standing beside her, watching the sea creep up the wall toward carvings older than any nation either of them could name.

"You think anyone's still alive down there?" Priya asked, meaning the people who'd made the marks. "I mean—the tradition. Someone taught someone taught someone."

"I think the sea keeps what it wants," Silas said. "And gives back what it owes. Eventually."

Priya glanced at her sidelong. "That's either very wise or very grim."

"It's usually both."

Then the glacier spoke again, and this time it wasn't thinking.

The crack came from directly overhead—beyond the ceiling of rock and ice, but close, horribly close—and it went on far longer than any crack should, a tearing like the sky unzipping. Dust sifted down through the lamplight. The tide pools shivered into rings.

"Out," Silas said. No quiet this time. "OUT. Drop what you're carrying. Everyone to the markers, single file, NOW."

They ran. Behind them the entrance flared white—not light but sound given a color, the concussion of tons of ice striking the shingle where Vane's sledges had sat ninety seconds earlier. The pressure wave hit them like a hand. Silas shoved Priya flat against the wall, covered the photographer's head with her own body, and felt grit rain down her collar like the world's coldest confetti.

When the echo died, they regrouped at the third marker, coughing, counting heads. All present. All alive. Silas played her lamp toward the entrance and felt her stomach drop through the floor of the world.

The route out was gone.

Not gone—*changed*. Where the low corridor had run, a jumble of fresh icefall now sealed the lower half, blue-white blocks the size of houses, still steaming faintly in the lamplight. Above them, the old exit arch remained open, but reaching it meant climbing fifty feet of fresh collapse that would not stop moving for hours. Maybe days. And the tide was already turning, black water sliding up the shingle toward their boots.

"We can't climb that," said one of the porters—a big, gentle man named Okafor who had carried more loads than anyone and complained about none of them. His voice shook. "That ice is alive. It'll shift under us."

"He's right," Tomas said. "We need the upper tunnel. The one the markers show."

"The markers show a route *through*," Silas said slowly. "Not out. Through the glacier, along the spine, to the far side of the point." She'd studied Wren's copied pages until 3 a.m., and the final entry had circled one line of dots over and over, pressing hard enough to tear the paper: *the long way is the only way, if you're ever reading this, take the long way.*

"There is no far side," Vane said. "There's nothing there but the calving face."

"There's a shelf above the calving face," Silas said. "Wren mapped it. There's a ramp of old ice, stable, that comes up onto the headland. It's four hours. Maybe five."

"And the artifacts?" Vane's voice had gone very soft, which Silas had learned was worse than loud. He gestured at the sledges, the oilcloth bundles, the crates already half-loaded. "Six years of my life. A discovery that rewrites every textbook on this coast. You want to leave it here for the tide?"

"I want everyone breathing at the end of the day."

"The tide will destroy them! Do you understand what—" He stopped himself, drew breath, and when he spoke again the charm was back, slick and terrible. "Silas. Think carefully. If we abandon these, there's no expedition. There's no discovery. There's just a group of people who nearly drowned in a hole. And you—you especially—need this expedition to be worth something. Don't you?"

He let it hang there. *Or I tell them all who you are. What you did.* He didn't say it. He didn't need to.

Silas looked at the sledges. Then at her crew—Okafor with his shaking hands, Priya cradling her camera like a child, young Devi whose first job this was, Tomas standing with his arms crossed and his jaw set like a man who had already decided something expensive.

She thought about Kernvall. About the moment on the icefall when the right call and the paid call had diverged, and how she'd spent six years paying interest on choosing wrong. About Wren's journal, and the line pressed so hard into the paper it tore: *take the long way.*

"No," Silas said.

The word came out calm, and it surprised her, how calm. Fear was still in her—fear was everywhere, filling the cave like the rising tide—but fear, she had learned, was just fuel that hadn't been lit yet.

"We leave everything. Sledges, crates, all of it. We carry food, rope, lamps, and each other. That's the manifest."

"Silas—" Vane began.

"That's the manifest," she repeated, louder, "and anyone who disagrees can wait here and negotiate with the ocean. Vane, I'm telling you as your guide: those blocks above us are going to move again before noon. When they do, anything under them goes with them. Including us. Including your precious textbooks." She stepped closer to him, close enough to see the sweat freezing at his temple despite the cold. "And here's the other thing. Go ahead. Tell them who I am. Tell them everything. Because I've been dead in every way that matters for six years, and I am *done* letting men like you hold the shovel."

The cave was silent except for the sea.

It was Okafor who broke it, unbuckling his sledge harness and letting the load slide to the shingle with a crash that echoed like applause. "Long way," he said. "Point me at it."

Devi followed. Then Priya, laughing a little wildly, setting her camera bag down with exaggerated ceremony, like a queen abdicating. One porter hesitated—Halvard, the quiet one, who Vane paid double—and looked between his employer and the ice wall for a long moment.

"My daughter's getting married in June," Halvard said finally, to no one in particular, and dropped his harness too.

Vane stared at them, and for one naked moment Silas saw what lived underneath the charm: not greed, exactly, but terror—the terror of a man who had built his whole self out of acquisitions and had nothing left when the crates were empty. "You'll regret this," he whispered. "All of you. That history will drown."

"Maybe," Silas said. "But we won't." She turned to the wall, found the fourth marker, the beginning of the dotted line climbing into darkness. "Rope up. Single file. Tomas, you're my second—call the spacing. Priya, keep count of heads out loud, every hundred steps. We move on my word and stop on Tomas's."

"And me?" Vane's voice cracked on it.

"You walk in the middle," Silas said, "where I can see you. Move."

* * *

The upper tunnel took them up through the glacier's throat. Old ice pressed close on both sides, glowing faint blue-green where their lamps touched it, shot through with bubbles of air frozen a thousand years ago. The dotted markers continued—someone had carved them *inside* the ice passages, which meant someone had come this way often, which meant—

"Don't say it," Tomas murmured beside her. "Don't say it yet. Climb first. Hope after."

But Silas was already doing the arithmetic she couldn't stop doing: supplies cached deep enough to survive seasons. A journal with dates running years past the avalanche. A warning that *someone else has been coming here*. And now a route through the glacier, maintained, marked, kept passable by hands that knew what they were doing.

Hands like Wren's.

Behind them, the glacier groaned again, longer this time, and somewhere above, tons of ice began its slow argument with gravity. Silas put her hand against the ancient wall, felt the mountain's pulse through her palm, and led them onward into the dark—toward the long way, the far side, and whatever was waiting there, alive or otherwise, at the edge of the calving face.
