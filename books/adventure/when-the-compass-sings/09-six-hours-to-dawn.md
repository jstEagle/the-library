## 9. Six Hours to Dawn

The glacier spoke first.

It was not a groan this time, nor the long complaining creak Silas had learned to ignore over twenty years of ice work. It was a *crack*—a single pistol-shot report that echoed down the cave's throat and came back doubled, tripled, like something enormous clearing its throat before it sang.

"Everyone out of the water," she said quietly. "Now."

They had been in the cave for barely ninety minutes. The second tide window had opened at 2:40 in the morning, black and glassy, and they'd come through it in single file with headlamps bobbing, Vane at the rear carrying more rope than anyone needed because he liked the look of equipment. Now the water at their feet was beginning to shiver.

"That was just settling," Vane said. His voice had the bright, reasonable tone of a man talking himself into something. "Glaciers settle. You told us that yourself, Dravenmoor."

"I told you glaciers *talk*," Silas said. "I never said they were polite." She pressed her palm flat against the tunnel wall. The vibration coming through it was wrong—too rhythmic, too deep. Somewhere above them, along the calving face, a section of ice the size of a cathedral had decided its time was up. The only question was whether it would fall before or after they were out.

Tomas appeared at her shoulder, breathing hard from the forward scout. "Exit tunnel's still open," he said. "Barely. There's meltwater coming through the ceiling like a sieve."

"Then we move fast and we don't stop to admire anything." She turned to face the crew—Priya, the quiet geologist; Halloran and his bad knee; young Devi who hadn't stopped shivering since hour one but hadn't complained once either. "Leave what you're carrying except ropes and lights. We go light or we don't go."

Vane's pack did not leave his shoulders. It bulged with the carved markers, the bronze fittings, the journal pages he'd torn out when he thought no one was looking—the whole stolen history of the place, wrapped in oilcloth against his spine.

"Mr. Vane," Silas said. "You heard me."

"And I'm ignoring you." He smiled, and it was almost charming, the way a knife is almost a tool. "These artifacts are worth more than every life on this expedition combined. That's not cruelty, that's arithmetic. I'm happy to carry my own weight out. I'd simply rather carry the weight of history too."

"The weight of history is going to get us all killed."

"Then perhaps you should have thought of that before you took my money." He said it lightly, but everyone heard the blade under it. Everyone knew now what she was. What she'd done. Or what they believed she'd done.

Silas looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked past him—at Priya, who had quietly set down her sample case; at Halloran, who was watching Vane's pack with an expression usually reserved for rot in a climbing anchor.

"You can stay with him if you want," she said to the crew. "But I know the way out, and he doesn't. And the tide doesn't care how much anyone paid."

Devi picked up her rope. Then Priya. Then Halloran, muttering something about rich men and arithmetic. Tomas fell in beside Silas without a word, which was its own kind of vote.

Vane stood alone in the lamplight for three heartbeats. Then, softly, so only she could hear: "You'll regret choosing them over me, Dravenmoor. People always regret choosing the living over the dead."

"Not in my experience," she said. "In my experience, it's the other way around."

He turned and walked deeper into the cave, into the dark beyond their lamps, alone with his relics and his arithmetic.

She let him go. God help her, she let him go—and she would think about that choice for the rest of her life, whichever length that turned out to be.

* * *

The exit tunnel was worse than Tomas had described.

Meltwater sheeted down the walls in silver ropes, and the ceiling had dropped a full arm's length since they'd come in. Ahead, where the passage should have bent toward the sea door, there was instead a wall of fresh rubble—blocks the size of trucks, fused with slush, still ticking as they settled.

"No," Devi said. Just that. "No, no, no."

"Yes," Silas said. "Just not here." Because she had spent the last two days studying the carvings—not as artifacts, not as treasure, but as *lines*. Lines that repeated. Lines that branched. Lines that, when she finally stopped seeing them as decoration and started seeing them as a map, resolved into something unmistakable: a route. Arrows, essentially. A thousand years old, cut by hands that had also been trapped in this dark and had also needed a way out.

"Wren found it," she said aloud, mostly to herself. "Wren must have found it years ago. That's why the markers deep in are fresher-cut. Someone's been maintaining them."

"Maintaining them?" Halloran said. "Who maintains stone arrows in a glacier cave?"

Silas didn't answer. Her throat had closed around the answer days ago, and it hadn't left yet.

They climbed. The alternate route ran upward through a series of chambers the old mapmakers had marked with spirals—a spiral meaning climb, she'd realized, a zigzag meaning descend, a circle-within-circle meaning *shelter here, wait for the water*. The ice groaned overhead in long descending notes, and twice the passage behind them filled with falling debris, and once Devi slipped on blue glass-smooth rock and Tomas caught her by the hood of her jacket and hauled her back with a strength none of them had credited him with.

"You're good at this," Silas told him later, hauling herself up beside him onto a ledge.

"My mother made me take dance lessons," Tomas said. "Twelve years. I hated every one until right now."

Despite everything—despite the water rising behind them and the mountain coming apart ahead—someone laughed. It might have been Silas.

* * *

They found the third chamber at what Silas judged to be four hours into the six-hour window, which meant they were either going to make it or they were going to die knowing exactly how late they were.

And there, in the chamber, was a fire ring.

Not ancient. Not a thousand years old. Recent—ash still gray, a tin cup set neatly beside it, fish bones arranged with an odd, careful tidiness. And on the wall above it, freshly cut into the ice-glazed stone, a new marker: the same spiral language as the old ones, but with one addition. A small figure. A stick-person, crude and certain, standing beside the route-arrow with one arm raised.

Pointing back the way they'd come.

"Oh," said Priya softly. "Oh, someone's *here*."

Silas's headlamp found the far side of the chamber, where a low alcove opened into deeper dark—and in that dark, something moved.

It came out slowly, badly, favoring one leg, wrapped in layers of patched fleece and seal-hide that had clearly been animal and clothing both at different points. Hair gone white-blond and matted. Face hollowed, weathered, bearded in a way that made gender briefly a mystery. It held a sharpened piece of glacial iron in one hand the way a soldier holds a rifle, and it planted itself between them and the alcove with its weight forward, ready.

"Don't," it rasped. The voice was ruined, rusted from disuse. "Don't touch the markers. Don't touch the—"

Silas pulled off her glove. With her bare hand, numb fingers shaking, she drew in the frost on the rock beside her: a single symbol. The old Kernvall team sign. Two peaks and a falling star.

The figure went absolutely still.

"Say something," Silas whispered. "Say anything. Say my name if you remember it. Say it wrong, even. I don't care."

The creature—the man—the *survivor* lowered the iron blade. His eyes, pale as winter water, fixed on her face, and traveled it slowly, feature by feature, like a man reading a letter he'd waited years to receive.

"...Sparrow," he said. The old nickname, from the old days, from a rope team that had trusted each other with their lives. "You got *old*, Sparrow."

Silas Halloway-Dravenmoor, who had not wept in front of another human being in nine years, sat down on the frozen floor of a dying glacier and wept like rain.

Wren Halloway knelt in front of her, awkward, half-feral, alive, and put his arms around her with the careful gentleness of someone relearning how.

"I wrote to you," he said into her hair. "Every day, in the journal. I wrote that if you ever came back, I'd tell you it wasn't your fault. The avalanche. It wasn't—I saw it start, Sparrow. I saw where you were standing. You couldn't have—"

"I left you," she said. "I testified—I let them say—"

"I know. I read the newspapers they left in supply drops. Took me four years to walk far enough to find any." He pulled back and looked at her, and beneath the ruin of his face was still Wren—all of him, the stubbornness and the terrible kindness. "We're going to have a lot to talk about. Preferably somewhere warmer. Is that a crew behind you? Did you bring a *crew*?"

"They're mine," Silas said, and was surprised to find she meant it. "More or less. Long story."

"There's a tide coming," Tomas said gently, from the entrance. "And a mountain coming down faster."

* * *

They were in the final passage, Wren limping between Silas and Devi, following his own markers home, when the glacier sang.

Not a crack this time. A roar—the whole calving face letting go at once, a sound like the sky tearing, and the floor of the world tilted and shook. Ice dust exploded through the tunnels in a blinding white breath. Silas flattened the team against the inner wall and counted seconds, the way you do, waiting to learn whether the roof would hold.

It held. Barely. When the dust cleared, the way behind them was gone—sealed under forty feet of fresh collapse—and the way ahead was open, glowing faintly with pre-dawn blue light from the sea mouth.

And in that blue light, ahead of them, a figure was running.

Vane. Alone, pack bouncing, oilcloth tearing, relics spilling behind him across the ice as he sprinted for the shrinking gap between the sea door and the advancing tidewater. He had waited, Silas understood dimly—he had hidden and watched them pass, and then run for the exit at the last possible moment, arithmetic to the end.

"VANE!" she screamed. "THE SHELF! THE SHELF'S GOING!"

He glanced back—just once, just long enough to see her pointing up, to see the fracture line racing along the ceiling above the sea door like lightning written in stone.

He kept running.

The shelf came down with a sound Silas would hear for the rest of her life. Not a crash—an *exhale*, vast and almost gentle, the mountain setting down its burden. When the spray settled, the sea door was half-blocked, the water was surging in through the remaining gap, and Aldous Vane was nowhere among the living.

His pack floated back to them on the flood, oilcloth torn, one carved marker turning slowly in the blue water like a compass needle that had finally found north.

Silas caught it. Held it. Looked at Wren, at Tomas, at the others crowded behind her in the rising light.

"The gap's still open," she said. "Sixty seconds of swimming, maybe less. Everyone stays roped. Everyone goes *now*."

Above them, dawn was arriving—six hours exactly, to the minute, since they'd entered. The glacier, having sung, fell silent.

And somewhere beyond the ice, faint and growing, came the sound of engines. Rescue boats. Someone had reported the calving early.

Someone had sent help.

Silas looked at Wren, who was squinting toward the sound with an expression she hadn't seen on him in nine years: hope, unguarded, ridiculous, alive.

"Well, Sparrow," he said. "Race you to the boats."
