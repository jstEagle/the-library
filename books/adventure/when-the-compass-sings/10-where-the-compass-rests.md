## 10. Where the Compass Rests

The rescue station at Hafnarskál smelled of diesel and dried fish and, faintly, of the antiseptic they'd used on Wren's hands. Silas sat on a bench too short for her legs with a blanket around her shoulders she hadn't asked for and couldn't seem to put down, and across from her, a woman named Inspector Ásta Jónsdóttir turned a page in a notebook that had already eaten six pages of Silas's confession.

"Start again," Ásta said, not unkindly. "From Kernvall. Slowly this time. I'm not writing a headline. I'm writing what happened."

So Silas told it slowly.

She told about the storm window they should never have pushed, and how Wren had wanted to turn back and Silas—hungry for the summit, hungry to be *someone*—had overruled her. She told about the cornice that let go, and the rope, and the arithmetic of weight that no one ever teaches you in guide school: that two people on one line is a conversation, and one of them always ends up doing more of the talking. She told them she'd cut nothing, decided nothing, that the mountain had simply taken Wren and left Silas holding a rope end like a question with no answer.

And then she told the part she'd swallowed for four years—that the insurers hadn't believed an accident so clean. That they'd needed someone culpable, and when Silas wouldn't say what they wanted, they'd found Tomas Reyes instead, a young porter with a sick mother and a mortgage shaped like a noose, and they'd fed him just enough of the story to testify to it. That his testimony had been bought, and her silence sold, and between the two transactions the truth had gone missing entirely, like a carabiner dropped into a crevasse.

Ásta wrote all of it down. When Silas finished, the inspector was quiet for a long moment, then said, "You know this doesn't end neatly. Vane's estate will fight every word. The insurers will lawyer up before their coffee cools."

"I know."

"And you could have stayed quiet. You were very good at staying quiet." Ásta's eyes flicked up. "Why stop?"

Silas looked toward the infirmary door, where a nurse had just passed carrying a basin, and thought about the honest answer, which was not noble at all. "Because I got tired of being a ghost," she said. "Ghosts don't get to keep anything."

* * *

It took eleven days for the world to decide what to do with her.

Eleven days of statements and counter-statements, of a lawyer from the maritime authority who kept calling her "Ms. Dravenmoor" in a tone usually reserved for weather warnings, of journalists camped outside the station like gulls waiting for a fish toss. One of them ran the story under the headline GHOST GUIDE RETURNS FROM THE ICE, which Silas found almost flattering until she read the second paragraph and discovered the reporter believed she'd been *dead*, a confusion she chose not to correct because watching the correction ripple through three news outlets was the most entertainment she'd had in years.

Tomas read the articles aloud to her in the canteen, doing voices. He did the insurers' spokesman as a man gargling gravel. He did the tabloid as a woman who had clearly never been colder than a heated pool.

"'Sources close to the expedition,'" he intoned, "'describe Dravenmoor as a changed woman.'"

"You're my sources?"

"I'm your only source. I described you as 'less likely to punch me than she used to be.' They cleaned it up."

"They should have kept it. It's accurate."

He grinned, then set the paper down, and something in his face went careful. "They're reopening the Kernvall file. Properly, this time. Ásta says with your statement and mine together—" He stopped. Rubbed the back of his neck. "I want to say it right, so let me say it once. What I did was wrong, and knowing they made me do it doesn't make it mine any less. But I carried it alone, and you carried yours alone, and that was the whole trick of it, wasn't it? They only worked because we were both by ourselves."

Silas turned her tea mug a slow quarter-circle on the table. "Yes," she said. "That was the whole trick."

"So." He stuck out his hand, rough-palmed, rope-scarred, absurdly formal. "Not by ourselves anymore."

She shook it. Her throat did something complicated that she disguised as a cough.

* * *

Wren was allowed visitors on the twelfth day, which everyone agreed was typical of her—arriving back from the dead and still making people wait.

Silas found her propped up in the infirmary bed with her hair cut short and ragged where frostbite had made the decisions for her, her left hand wrapped thick as a boxing glove, and her eyes—the same eyes, that was the unbearable thing, the same sharp gray eyes—tracking Silas across the room with an expression caught somewhere between recognition and suspicion, as if Silas might be a hallucination the frost had left behind.

"You look terrible," Wren rasped.

"You've been eating glacier moss for four years. You don't get a vote."

Wren laughed, and it cracked halfway through into something else, and then Silas was sitting on the edge of the bed being held by one good arm while a nurse pretended very hard to be interested in a chart. Neither of them said anything useful for a while. There wasn't anything useful. Four years of grief and one week of impossible hope don't compress well into sentences.

Eventually Wren pulled back and studied her. "The journal," she said. "You read the last entry."

"The warning. About someone else coming to the cave." Silas hesitated. "Vane. It was Vane. He'd been sending scouts for years—he knew the markers were old, older than anyone guessed, and he wanted the site stripped before anyone official heard about it. He hired me because of Kernvall. Because he thought guilt was a leash."

"And instead you bit him."

"Glacier calved on him, actually."

Wren absorbed this. Outside the window, the harbor lay flat and silver, and somewhere beyond it the great white shoulder of the glacier sat doing its slow geological bookkeeping, indifferent to everything it had nearly buried and everything it had, against all odds, given back.

"Why?" Wren asked quietly. "Why did you come back? To the cave. You could have walked away from Vane's letter. Nobody would have known. That was the whole point of the life you'd built—no name, no manifest, no past. Why hand it all back?"

Silas had expected the question. She'd rehearsed answers on the walk over—something about duty, something about the families owed the truth—and she discarded all of them now, because Wren had spent four years surviving on honesty and would smell a polished lie through plaster walls.

"Because running was easy," she said. "That's the thing nobody tells you. Disappearing is the easiest work there is. Every year it got simpler—one less letter answered, one less name on one less list. And every year it cost more, and I paid it, because paying felt like penance and penance felt like I was still doing something for you." She looked at her own hands. "Coming back for you wasn't brave. Anyone would have. Staying afterward—that was the first brave thing I ever did. Staying in the room when the truth came out. Staying when my name was in the papers. Staying here, now, with you looking at me like I might still be the person who got you killed."

"You're not," Wren said. Simply, the way she'd once said *rope's fixed* or *weather's turning*. A fact, delivered at altitude. "The person who got me killed wouldn't have come back at all. Or worse—she'd have come back and taken Vane's money and told herself a story about it later." She nudged Silas's knee with her foot. "You stayed. So stay."

"That's the plan."

"Good." Wren settled back against the pillows, and a tired, crooked smile surfaced—the old one, the one that used to mean trouble was being planned. "Because I've been reading the survey reports they brought me. Those markers in the cave? Ásta's archaeologist thinks they're a route system. Hundreds of years old. Someone lived through winters in that ice, Silas. Someone drew maps for the people who came after." Her eyes glinted. "And they're going to need a guide who can read ice and isn't afraid of a little bad history. Someone with a name. On the manifest. In ink."

Silas felt something loosen in her chest—a knot she'd been carrying so long she'd mistaken it for a bone.

"In ink," she agreed.

* * *

Spring came late that year, the way it does when it has something to prove.

On the morning the ferry brought the first survey team to Hafnarskál, Silas stood on the dock with a clipboard in her hands—an actual clipboard, with her actual name printed at the top in actual ink, above a list of six names belonging to people who had chosen, out of everyone they might have hired, to be led by Edmund Montrose's most infamous ghost. Tomas stood at the rail checking knots he already knew were sound, because some rituals keep you human. Wren sat on a crate with her bandaged hand in her lap and the sun on her face, arguing cheerfully with the archaeologist about whether the third marker chamber was navigational or ceremonial, a debate Silas suspected would outlast them all.

A tourist kid, maybe ten, tugged his mother's sleeve and pointed. "Is that her? The one from the cave?"

"It is," Silas said, before the mother could shush him. She crouched to his level. "And in about twenty minutes, it's the person who's going to tell you exactly how many layers you're underdressed by."

The boy grinned. His mother mouthed *thank you*. Somewhere behind her, Tomas snorted.

Silas straightened, checked the sky—it was going to hold, mostly, the way spring skies hold, which is to say with opinions—and called the team in. Six faces turned toward her, open and expectant, and not one of them flinched at her name.

"All right," she said. "Listen up. Today we go slow, we go smart, and nobody touches anything older than their grandmother. Questions?"

Wren raised her bandaged hand. "Can I come?"

"Not yet."

"Next trip?"

Silas looked at her—at the gray eyes, the crooked smile, the whole impossible returned fact of her—and felt the compass in her chest swing, settle, and rest on a bearing she recognized at last as home.

"Every trip," she said. "Now let's move. The tide won't wait, and neither will the ice."
