## 1. Miles, Not Years

There's a trick to leaving a place before it leaves a mark on you, and I've had twenty years to perfect it. You never unpack past the second layer of the bag. You learn the names of three locals and no more. You pay cash, tip well, and remember that every town is a room you're only borrowing until the work runs out.

The delta country taught me this early. It's a place built on impermanence—channels that rewrite themselves every flood season, islands that exist on last year's maps and not this year's water. Out here, staying put is a kind of arrogance. The river doesn't recognize leases. So when people ask me how long I've been in the territory, I give them the honest answer: long enough to know which channels lie.

I take the jobs nobody wants. That's not false modesty; it's a business model. The lucrative contracts go to firms with helicopters and government ties. What falls to freelancers like me is the work that requires someone willing to be uncomfortable for weeks at a stretch: boundary surveys in marshland where the ground truth changes daily, elevation checks on tributaries that appear and vanish with the rains, re-surveys of parcels whose previous surveyors quit or drowned or simply stopped answering their radios. I'm good at it because I don't mind being alone, and I don't mind being wet, and I have no ambitions that require me to be anywhere else.

My boat is a thirty-year-old aluminum skiff I've patched so many times the hull reads like a scarred face. My camp kit fits in two dry bags. My library is six books, rotated seasonally. When a client asks where I'm based, I say "wherever the work is," and they nod, because in this country that's a respectable address.

The name Nakamura appears on none of my paperwork. I made sure of that twenty years ago, when I turned eighteen and signed my first contract under "Felix Mercer," a name borrowed from a dead man I invented on the spot. It wasn't hard. Nobody checks. Freelance surveyors are hired for their hands and their boats, not their lineages. And if anyone ever did check—if some curious clerk traced the surname back through the archives—they'd find Dr. Kenji Nakamura, celebrated archaeologist, author of eleven books, subject of two documentaries, holder of more honorary degrees than I have fingers. They'd find his famous excavations, his famous theories, his famous face on the cover of a textbook I was assigned in school and refused to open.

They'd find me too, eventually. The son who walked out at seventeen and never came back. There are articles about that, too, buried deep—the human-interest angle, the estrangement, the prodigal who wanted nothing to do with the family trade. I've never read them. I know they exist the way you know there's a splinter in your palm: by the way everything else hurts around it.

For twenty years I've kept the two lives separate the way you keep oil and water separate—carefully, constantly, and with the understanding that one good shake will ruin everything. No permits with my real name. No references from my father's colleagues. No mention of him in interviews, because I don't give interviews. When clients ask about my training, I say I apprenticed on delta surveys, which is true, if you don't ask whose crew I apprenticed on.

It was his crew. For five summers, between the ages of twelve and seventeen, I was the kid on Dr. Kenji Nakamura's dig sites who hauled buckets and learned to read contour lines before he learned to drive. He was brilliant. Everyone said so, and everyone was right, and that was part of the problem. Brilliant men make poor fathers. They mistake their children for apprentices, and apprentices for instruments, and instruments for things that can be calibrated to purpose. By the time I was fifteen I understood that my father loved archaeology the way other men love God—totally, unreasonably, with a willingness to sacrifice anything placed on the altar. I understood that I was not exempt from the category of *anything*.

So I left. Not dramatically. I didn't slam doors or deliver speeches. I finished a field season, packed my gear, and told him I wasn't coming back next summer. He looked at me across the mess tent with an expression I've spent two decades trying not to remember—not anger, not hurt, but something worse: *disappointment in the data*. As though I were a survey result that hadn't matched the hypothesis. As though I'd failed to confirm something he'd already published.

He wrote to me, after. Letters, at first, then cards, then nothing. I kept the letters in a box I never opened and finally lost in a move I don't remember making. Somewhere along the way the silence became mutual, then habitual, then total. Twenty years. Long enough for a child to become a stranger twice over.

I tell you all this now, at the start, because what happened this spring won't make sense otherwise. Or maybe it will make too much sense, which is its own problem.

* * *

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, at the supply depot in Corvane, which is as close to a home address as I keep—a mail drop behind the chandlery where I store dry goods and pick up client checks. The depot man, Silas, held it out to me with the particular expression he reserves for correspondence that looks official. Thick envelope. Heavy paper. A return address I didn't recognize, in a city I'd never worked.

"Estate something-or-other," Silas said. "Sign here."

I signed. I stood there in the doorway with the envelope in my hand for longer than was reasonable, the way you stand at the edge of cold water knowing you're going to go in anyway. Then I walked down to the seawall and opened it there, in the wind, where whatever was inside would have to compete with gulls and weather for my attention.

It was from a firm of solicitors. My father was dead. Had been dead for six weeks—heart failure, alone, in a hotel room in a city where he'd been lecturing. Sixty-eight years old. The letter was formal, efficient, almost gentle in the way that legal language is gentle: *we regret to inform you*, *you are named in the will*, *please find enclosed*. There were details about probate I skimmed and a phone number I knew I wouldn't call. And there was one item, listed among the bequests, that stopped my eye the way a submerged rock stops a boat:

*One (1) unopened field journal, 1998–2003, to be delivered intact to Felix Nakamura.*

Unopened. Sealed, apparently, by his own hand, with instructions that it reach me exactly as it was. Not catalogued. Not transcribed. Not read by the estate, the university, or the archivists who would have fought each other for the privilege. A field journal covering the years I'd left—1998 to 2003, the span of my absence—and sealed shut, addressed to the son who'd walked away from it.

I sat on the seawall for a long time. The tide came in around the pilings. A barge went past loaded with gravel, and the wake rocked the moored dinghies, and none of it required anything from me, which was the whole appeal of the view.

Grief is a strange cargo when you haven't declared it. I kept waiting to feel something with a proper name—sorrow, relief, regret, any of the standard inventory. What I felt instead was a kind of pressure, like weather building offshore. He was dead. The fact sat in my chest with a weight I couldn't account for, because surely you can't mourn a man you spent twenty years refusing to think about. Surely the heart doesn't keep accounts on debts you've sworn are settled.

But the heart keeps terrible books. That's the thing nobody tells you. I'd spent two decades telling myself the story of the clean break, the rational exit, the son who simply chose a different life. And underneath that story ran another one, quieter, that I'd never let surface: that I hadn't left *him*, exactly. I'd left before he could finish deciding what I was worth. I'd left the way you leave a poker game when you're losing—before the last hand reveals how much you actually owed. Every job I took that nobody else wanted, every season I declined to extend, every town I left just as it started feeling like somewhere—I'd built a whole life out of the mechanics of departure. Leaving wasn't my habit. It was my alibi.

And now the man himself was gone, and the alibi had expired, and all that remained was a sealed journal and a question I'd been outrunning since I was seventeen: *what did he want to say to me?*

I didn't burn it. I want that on the record, even if the record is just this page. There was a moment, walking back from the seawall, when I genuinely considered it—dropping the envelope into a drum of burning brush behind the chandlery, watching the wax seal curl, letting the river take the ash. It would have been clean. It would have been consistent with twenty years of policy. Some part of me, the part that still flinched at his name in conversation, argued for it with real eloquence.

But I've seen what fire does to paper out here. I've recovered enough waterlogged documents from abandoned camps to know that words survive better than the men who write them. And something in me—some stubborn, unsentimental grain, the same grain that makes me good at this work—refused to destroy the only artifact my father had ever addressed directly to me. Whatever was in that journal, he'd sealed it for *me*. Burning it would have been a reply of sorts, and I found I wasn't ready to send one.

So I read nothing. That was the compromise I struck with myself on the seawall, and I've kept to it: the journal stays sealed. I put it, envelope and all, into the bottom of my gear bag, beneath the spare chart case and the emergency rations, wrapped in a dry bag I use for nothing else. It rides there now, weightless and heavy at once, the way unresolved things ride in a boat. I know it's there. I feel it against my back when the bag sits wrong on my shoulders. I tell myself it's just paper. I know better.

* * *

Three days later I took a contract upriver—a channel verification on the Serevan tributary, two weeks' work, poorly paid, requested by a government liaison office that normally wouldn't return my calls. I almost turned it down. The money was thin and the timing was bad; the monsoon was building in the hills, and every experienced pilot in the delta was pulling boats to high ground. But the work was remote, and remote was what I needed. Grief, untreated, does best in empty country, where there's nothing to remind you of itself.

I told myself that anyway. It's the kind of lie a man tells fluently after twenty years of practice.

I loaded the skiff at dawn: fuel drums, survey kit, the tarp, the food, the journal riding silent in the bottom of the bag like ballast I hadn't asked for. Silas watched me cast off and asked, as he always asks, when I'd be back.

"Two weeks," I said. "Maybe less."

"And after that?"

"After that, wherever the current puts me."

He didn't press. Silas has known me eight seasons and has never once asked why I never stay. I think he understands without asking. There are people in the delta who've made peace with the water's impermanence, and people who've made a religion of it, and I suspect he's filed me under the second heading without judgment.

The Serevan opened ahead of me in the early light, brown and slow and full of the season's first silt. I ran the channel from memory, checking markers against the chart, and for a few hours the work did what work does—it filled the hands and quieted the head. A kingfisher worked the shallows. The rain held off. I ate lunch standing up, as always, because sitting down felt like settling.

It was near dusk, on the third day upriver, when the radio crackled with a call that didn't belong to any of my frequencies. A government liaison, the voice said, requesting Felix Mercer. A survey team on the Serevan had missed two scheduled check-ins. Four days of silence. Was I anywhere in the vicinity, and would I be willing to assist?

I looked at the chart. I looked at the sky, where the monsoon was stacking itself over the hills like a debt coming due. I thought about the sealed journal in my gear bag, and the father inside it, and the twenty years of distance I'd maintained at considerable personal cost.

Then I keyed the mic, because four days of silence on a flooded tributary is not a riddle you leave for the river to solve.

"This is Mercer," I said. "Give me their last known position."

What I didn't say—what I couldn't have said, then—was that the coordinates they gave me were thirty kilometers above the camp where I'd find the marker stamped with my father's initials, older than my estrangement, waiting in the mud like a signature on a letter I'd spent my whole life refusing to open.
