## 2. The Empty Landing

The liaison's name was Okonkwo, and he had the particular pallor of a man who spent his life indoors apologizing on behalf of other people's decisions. We met in a government field office that smelled of mildew and carbon paper, where a ceiling fan turned with the weary conviction of something that had long ago stopped believing in the wind.

"Mr. Mercer," he said, shaking my hand like he was checking it for contraband. "The Serevan survey team. Six personnel. They missed their scheduled check-in four days ago. Then again yesterday."

"So they're either dead or they've gone native," I said. "Which is it you're hoping for? The paperwork's lighter on the second one."

His mouth did something complicated. "We're hoping for a radio malfunction."

"Radio malfunctions don't happen four days running on a tributary with relay towers every twenty kilometers. I know. I installed two of them." I let that sit. "You want someone to go up there and confirm what you already suspect, so the ministry can start deciding whose career this costs."

"I want," he said carefully, "someone willing to go up there at all. The monsoon is three days out, maybe less. Every licensed guide in the district has declined."

"Every licensed guide has a family," I said, "or at least a landlord who'd notice. I have neither. That's my entire qualification, and you should know I'm aware of it."

He slid the contract across the desk. I read it the way I read everything—looking for the exits. Standard terms. Hazard waiver. A confidentiality clause broad enough to drive a barge through, which told me the ministry already knew something they weren't putting in writing.

One line caught me. *Personnel roster available upon request.*

"Give me the roster now," I said.

He hesitated, then turned a folder toward me. Six names. A geologist, two technicians, a medic, a junior cartographer. And at the top, in bold: **E. ODUYA — TEAM LEADER**.

Something about the name snagged, the way a hook snags on a sunken log—you feel it, you can't say what. I let it go. Names are the kind of thing you learn not to collect in my line of work. You collect coordinates, water levels, the draft of your boat. Names belong to people who stay.

"Fuel and provisions at the Kessel depot," Okonkwo said. "You'll retrace their route from the confluence. Mark anything anomalous."

"Anomalous," I repeated. "That's a generous word for six missing people."

"It's the word I'm authorized to use."

I signed. Not because I wanted the work—though I did, in the way a man wants the next horizon, which is to say reflexively—but because the Serevan tributary was a place I'd been avoiding for eleven years, and avoidance, I've found, is just a slower way of arriving.

* * *

The confluence at dawn was the color of milk tea, the two rivers arguing about which one was really the river. I loaded the boat alone, which is how I preferred it: fuel drums, tarp, the aluminum case with the survey instruments, and—because I am not a sentimental man, whatever else I am—the field journal from my father's estate, still unopened, still sealed in its oilskin, riding at the bottom of my gear bag like ballast I couldn't explain to customs.

I hadn't burned it. I'd thought about burning it, standing over the stove the night the letter came, and what stopped me wasn't sentiment. It was the suspicion that my father had engineered even his death to make me do something, and I refused to let the old man's last move be a checkmate. So the journal rode along, unread, a hostage I refused to negotiate with.

The Serevan narrowed as I worked upstream, the banks closing in, jungle leaning over the water like eavesdroppers. Four days of rain had come and gone ahead of the monsoon proper, and the river ran high and brown and muscular, shrugging my hull around like it was deciding whether I was worth the trouble.

I found their first camp by midafternoon. Or rather, I found where it had been: a flat shelf above the flood line, the grass flattened in rectangles where tents had stood, fire-blackened stones stacked with the tidy geometry of people who expected to come back and didn't.

The second camp was thirty kilometers up, at a bend where the tributary split around a drowned island, and I reached it as the light went the color of old brass.

I knew before I tied off that something was wrong. Not wrong like violence—wrong like a room where the furniture has been arranged for a photograph.

Their gear was stowed. Not scattered, not abandoned, not torn apart by animals or weather. *Stowed.* Packs lined up under a tarp, weighted with stones against the wind. The tarp itself lashed down with proper knots—bowlines, not granny knots. Someone with field discipline had done that, and done it calmly, because panicked hands tie granny knots.

The cookfire was cold. Not days-cold, not rained-out. Cold the way a fire is cold when someone raked it flat and doused it deliberately, and then—this was the part that raised the hair on my arms—someone had set the kettle on the flat stones beside it. Upright. Empty. Put away.

Four days of rain. I looked at the ground and the ground looked back at me, and the ground was wrong. The mud around the camp was a churned, featureless brown, the kind of surface that records everything—a boot, a knee, a dragged sack, a body. Four days of rain should have written all over it. Six people, packing out or running or being taken, leave tracks. Animals come to abandoned camps. Something, anything, should have crossed that mud.

It was blank. Smooth as poured concrete, as if the camp had been lifted out of the world rather than left in it.

I stood there a long time, letting the river talk, because when the evidence goes quiet you learn to listen to the water instead. The Serevan said nothing useful. Rivers are terrible witnesses; they're always washing the scene.

I worked the camp the way I'd been taught—not by my father, whatever else he'd taught me without meaning to, but by twenty years of doing this. The tarp knots: right-handed tiers, two of them, working together. The kettle: dented aluminum, ministry issue, dry inside. The fire ring: three days cold at most, which meant the camp had been struck *after* the last rain, after the second missed check-in. After the world started looking for them.

They hadn't vanished during the storm. They'd packed up, in order, in daylight, after everyone knew they were missing.

That's when I stopped thinking of it as a search and started thinking of it as an errand. Somebody had come back for their gear. Somebody had tidied the scene. And whoever tidies a scene in the delta country, four days into a monsoon, is not a grieving family member. Grief doesn't tie bowlines.

* * *

I made camp myself, because night on the Serevan is not a suggestion, and I cooked rice over my own fire and did not look at the other fire ring across the clearing, which is a thing I recommend to no one. Grief you refuse to name doesn't stay put. It gets up in the dark and walks around and stands in other people's cold cookfires, and you find yourself thinking: *six people, and every one of them somebody's brother, somebody's son,* and then you think about the letter in the solicitor's envelope, and the journal in your bag, and you put the thought down like a hot pan.

In the morning I found the marker.

I'd gone down to the waterline to check the boat, because the river had risen overnight—half a meter, maybe more, the tributary drinking ahead of the monsoon—and the water had pulled back from the mud and left the bank scoured, peeled clean like an old floor.

The marker was set in the mud at the base of a strangler fig, half-exposed, bronze gone green with age. A survey benchmark. Standard pattern, standard alloy, the kind my trade has hammered into every continent's bones for a century. I'd seen ten thousand of them.

I'd never seen one with those initials.

**K.N. — SURVEY PARTY 3 — 19** and then a year worn to a ghost, the last digit eaten by forty years of flood silt.

Kenji Nakamura. My father had stood on this exact bank—this bend, this drowned island, this fig tree—decades before I was old enough to hold a staff, and driven a benchmark into the delta mud, and the river had kept it for him all this time, and now it handed it back to me like a debt called in.

I crouched there until my knees ached. The mud was cool through my trousers. Somewhere upstream, thunder was organizing itself into something with intent.

Here's what I knew, in the way you know things in the pit of you before the thinking catches up: the missing team's route ran through country my father had surveyed before I was born. The ministry's confidentiality clause. The tidied camp. The kettle put away. And now this—a signature in bronze, older than my estrangement, waiting in the mud like a letter I'd spent my whole life refusing to open, and the river had finally gotten tired of holding it.

My father had died three weeks ago in a city I hadn't visited in twenty years, and I had not gone to the funeral, and I had told myself that was cleanliness. But the dead don't stay where you put them. They wait in the mud. They wait at bends in rivers. They wait until you're thirty kilometers past the last point of safe retreat, with a monsoon coming down the valley and six missing people whose camp was erased by hands too calm to be afraid.

The first fat raindrops hit the water around me, big and deliberate, like the sky testing its aim.

I pried the benchmark loose—it was evidence, I told myself, though evidence for what, I couldn't have said—and stowed it in the boat beside the unopened journal, my father's two messages to me, one in bronze and one in ink, riding together in the dark of my gear bag.

Then I heard the oar.

Not mine. A second boat, coming up the channel through the building rain, rowed hard and rowed angry, and standing in the bow, a woman with a dry bag slung across her chest and an expression that suggested she had bribed, argued, and possibly threatened her way onto my river, and had not enjoyed a single minute of it.

She brought the skiff alongside mine before I could decide whether to be annoyed, and shouted over the rain, in a voice that brooked no committee:

"Which one of you is Mercer? I need to talk to the man who's pretending to be Mercer."

I looked at her, and at the boat, and at the benchmark dripping in my gear bag, and understood that the river had decided the search was no longer mine alone.

"My name is Mercer," I said.

"Then you'd better start rowing," she said, "because I know what my brother found out here. And so, I think, do you."
