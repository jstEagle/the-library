## 3. A Stranger's Oar

I found him at the supply depot the way you find anything in that country—by standing very still and watching what moved.

The depot was a corrugated shed at the edge of Port Serevan, all rust and diesel and men shouting numbers at each other, and Felix Nakamura was the only person in it who wasn't shouting. He stood at the counter with his back to me, signing for fuel with the unhurried hand of a man who had done this ten thousand times, and I remember thinking: *there*. That's the one. That's the man whose name is on none of the permits.

Because that was the thing. I'd spent four days pulling threads since Emeka missed his second check-in, and every thread led to the same strange knot. The government liaison had hired a search guide—a good one, apparently the only one willing to go up the Serevan tributary before the monsoon broke—but the man's name appeared nowhere. Not on the contract copy I'd photographed through a clerk's window. Not on the fuel requisition. Not on the boat registration, which listed the vessel as belonging to a company that, when I called it, turned out to be a fish cannery that had closed nine years ago.

A man that invisible is either hiding from something or working for someone. In my experience the two categories overlap more than anyone likes to admit.

So I watched him load his own supplies—no deckhand, no porter, everything handled twice, checked once—and when he carried the last crate down the ramp toward his boat, I stepped into his path.

"Felix Nakamura."

He stopped. He didn't startle—that would have been too easy, too human—but something behind his eyes recalculated, quick as a compass needle swinging off north and then pretending it hadn't. "You have the advantage," he said.

"Mara Oduya." I let the name sit there. Nothing. No flicker of recognition, which meant either he genuinely didn't know it or he was better than I thought. "Emeka Oduya is my brother. He leads the survey team you've been paid to find."

"I know who you are." He set the crate down on the planking between us, deliberate, like a man building a small wall. "The liaison mentioned family had been asking questions."

"Family has been asking questions for six days. The liaison has been answering them for five." I looked past him at the boat—a broad-beamed river craft, older than me, patched in three places with the kind of competence that doesn't advertise itself. "You're leaving tomorrow at first light."

"Sunrise, yes."

"Then we should discuss terms tonight, because I'm coming with you."

He picked the crate back up. It was, I understood later, his way of ending conversations—pick up the object, resume the task, let silence do the refusing. But I'd grown up with Emeka, who used the same trick, and I knew how to walk alongside a moving wall.

"You're not on the manifest," he said.

"I am now. I'm a hydrological consultant."

"You're not certified in this district."

"I am certified in three others, and the district office stamped my papers an hour ago for a fee I won't be itemizing on any expense report." I kept pace with him down the ramp. "Your government wants its survey team found quietly. A grieving sister asking to consult on flood behavior ahead of monsoon season is quiet. It's practically decorative."

He reached the boat, set the crate in the hold, and finally turned around. Up close he had the look of a man weathered on purpose—sun-cracked, gray at the temples before fifty, hands that had done decades of work without ever once being shown off. His eyes were the problem. They were patient eyes. Eyes that had learned to wait out storms, arguments, and, presumably, sisters.

"Ms. Oduya," he said, "the tributary is going to try to kill us. That's before we account for whatever happened to your brother's team. Adding an untrained passenger doesn't improve anyone's odds."

"I'm not untrained. I map water for a living. I know how a channel lies, how a floodwall fails, how a river changes its mind overnight. Your charts are two years old and the monsoon is early. You need me more than I need you, and we both know it, because if you didn't, you'd already have walked away instead of standing here explaining yourself."

That landed. I saw it land—the smallest shift in his stance, weight settling back onto his heels. People who want to leave don't explain themselves. People who want to leave are already gone.

"The rumors," he said.

Good. He wasn't going to pretend he hadn't heard them. "Yes. The rumors. That Nakamura's son works both sides of the river. That jobs go missing when he's near them. That artifacts move downstream in boats nobody registered." I folded my arms. "Tell me they're false."

"They're false."

"That was fast."

"It was true first." Something moved across his face—not quite a smile, more like the ghost of one that had died young. "My father's name opens doors and closes them. I keep mine off paper so it does neither. If that reads as suspicious to people who've never needed to disappear, I can't help that."

*People who've never needed to disappear.* I turned the phrase over and found the hook in it. "Needed," I said. "Not chosen."

His jaw tightened, just once. "Terms," he said, changing the subject with the grace of a man shoving furniture in front of a door. "You pay your own way—supplies, your share of fuel. You follow my orders on the water without debate. And when we find your brother—or when we find what's left—you do your grieving on your own time. I can't run a boat and a mourner at once."

It was a cruel thing to say, and I want to be honest about what it did to me, because honesty is the only currency I had left that week. It hit like cold water. Not because it was cruel, but because it was practical, and practicality in the face of Emeka missing felt like betrayal. My hands shook, and I made fists of them where he couldn't see.

"And if we don't find him?" I said. "What's your rate for failure?"

"There isn't one. Nobody's paying me enough for this job to cover failure." He said it flatly, without self-pity, and somehow that was the first thing he'd told me that I believed completely. "We leave at sunrise. Be here at half past, or I go without you."

"You won't."

"No," he agreed, after a moment. "I won't. Your brother's team took readings along the lower channels that I'll need, and you're the only other person who's seen their route maps." He picked up the next crate. "Half past, Ms. Oduya."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat in my rented room above the chandlery with Emeka's last letter open on my knees and read it until the words wore smooth.

*The water's doing something impossible up here, Mara. Channels that aren't on any chart. Stone showing through where there shouldn't be stone for another hundred years. I'll write it all up properly when we're back—*

The letter ended there, mid-thought, the way letters do when their writers don't know they're writing their last ones. Below it, in the margin, in the smaller handwriting he used for things he wasn't sure he believed: *Found a survey marker today with initials that made the old man on our crew go pale. K.N. Ask me about it sometime.*

K.N. Kenji Nakamura. The famous one. The dead one, now—I'd read the obituary three weeks ago, buried on page eleven, the archaeologist who'd spent forty years hunting a city the delta kept swallowing. And his son was the only guide willing to go look for my brother, on a tributary where my brother had found a marker stamped with the dead man's initials.

I don't believe in coincidence. I believe in currents. Everything in that country moves by water, including secrets, and secrets pool in the low places.

At half past midnight I packed. Hydrological instruments, because I was legitimately those things. Emeka's route maps, copied twice, originals hidden separately. And at the bottom of the bag, wrapped in oilcloth, the letter—because I am my father's daughter too, and my father taught me that you carry the evidence even when carrying it hurts.

* * *

He was early. Of course he was early. When I came down the ramp at twenty-five past, the boat was fueled, squared away, and Felix Nakamura was crouched in the stern doing something unnecessary and precise to a coil of rope, the way men do when they want their hands busy so their faces stay honest.

"You're early," I said.

"So are you."

"I slept on the boat question all night and decided yes."

Something almost crossed his face. Almost. "Stow your gear amidships. Anything loose goes overboard in the first real water."

I stowed it. I took the bow seat because I wanted his back where I could watch it, and I think he understood that, because he said nothing about it, which was itself a kind of answer. He cast off, swung the nose upstream, and the depot began to shrink behind us into its own rust and noise.

For the first hour neither of us spoke. The river ran brown and heavy, pre-loaded with the monsoon still gathering somewhere over the highlands, and I watched the banks slide past and tried to read the water the way I'd read Emeka's handwriting—for signs of strain, for places where the surface lied.

It was Felix who broke the silence, eyes still forward.

"The marker," he said. "Your brother's crew found a marker with K.N. on it. You were going to ask me about that eventually, so ask now. I'd rather it happen while my hands are full than while I'm trying to sleep."

I looked at the back of his head for a long moment. Rain-smell was coming up the river ahead of us, faint but unmistakable, the sky loading its weapon.

"My brother wrote to me about it," I said. "He said it made an old man on his crew go pale. He said he'd ask me about it sometime." I paused. "There isn't going to be a sometime, Mr. Nakamura. So you're going to tell me why your dead father's initials are carved into a riverbank where my brother vanished—and you're going to tell me whether the rumors are true, the real ones, the ones about what your father was hunting."

Felix's hands stayed steady on the wheel. Steadier, I noticed, than his voice.

"Those are two different answers," he said, "and I only owe you one of them."

Ahead of us, the tributary opened its mouth, and the first rain began to fall.
