## 4. Water Over Stone

**FELIX**

The river changed its mind three times before noon.

I'd been running the Serevan tributary for eleven years, and I could read it the way some men read weather—by the color of the water, the lean of the reeds, the particular silence a channel makes when it's about to lie to you. But the monsoon had been sitting on the highlands for two days now, and the river was drinking everything it was given, and a drunk river is an unreliable narrator.

Mara Oduya stood at the bow with her arms crossed, watching the water like it owed her money.

"Slow down," she said.

"We're fine."

"Slow *down*, Nakamura."

I throttled back. The boat settled into the current with the sullen obedience of anything that has been told what to do by someone who might be right. Ahead of us the channel split around a mudbank that hadn't existed last season—or rather, had existed underwater, where I didn't have to think about it. The left fork looked better. Wider, deeper green, a clean tongue of current sliding between the flooded fig trees.

"Left," I said.

"No." She didn't raise her voice. She said it the way you'd tell a man his fly was down. "Right."

"The right fork narrows to nothing past the bend. I've run this stretch since before you—"

"Since before me, yes, you've mentioned." She crouched at the gunwale and trailed two fingers in the water, then held them up, watching how fast the droplets ran off. "Feel that? Water's cold. Two degrees colder than it was this morning at the depot. Cold water means new water, and new water means it's coming off the plateau, not through the lowland channels." She pointed left, at my beautiful wide green fork. "That's not a channel anymore. That's a spillway. It's filling a basin behind those trees that doesn't have an outlet. We go in there, we don't drown—we just stop. Forever. In a boat full of your charming personality."

I looked at the left fork. It looked like every good channel I'd ever trusted.

"Hydrology," she said, "is just the study of rivers lying to people who aren't paying attention."

I turned us right. The right fork narrowed exactly as I'd promised, but under Mara's direction we threaded it—she read the surface the way I never had, not as a path but as a sentence, every riffle and boil a word. Past the bend it opened again into the main stem, and behind us, if I glanced back, the left fork was already a lake where a river used to be.

She didn't say *I told you so*. That was somehow worse.

"Where did you learn that?" I asked, because silence felt like a debt.

"My mother farmed rice on a floodplain my whole childhood. You learn or you lose the crop." She sat back against the console, finally out of the wind. "You learn or you lose the crop," she repeated, quieter, like she was hearing it in another voice. Emeka's, maybe. I was starting to understand that everything she did, she did twice—once for herself and once for the brother she was dragging up this river by sheer refusal.

We made good distance after that. I hated how much of it was her doing.

* * *

**MARA**

He cooked dinner the way he did everything else: competently, silently, and as though being observed were a tax he resented paying. Rice, dried fish, chili paste from a tin that had seen more seasons than I had. He handed me a bowl without looking at me, which took a specific kind of skill.

"So," I said. "Kenji Nakamura."

The spoon stopped moving for maybe half a second. Most people wouldn't have caught it. I'd spent six years in flood monitoring stations catching things—a gauge twitching before the crest, a reading that lied by a millimeter right before it lied by a meter. I knew what a flinch looked like when it was trying to pass itself off as nothing.

"What about him?" Felix said.

"You know what about him. The man found Aksum's lower city. The man they named a lecture hall after at three universities. My brother had his monograph on delta settlement patterns in his kit bag for ten years—the spine was held together with tape." I stirred my rice. "And your name is Nakamura, and you've never once mentioned it, and when the liaison at the depot said the survey team was following coordinates from an old Nakamura field map, you went white as the foam on a weir."

"I didn't go white."

"You went the color of that tarp. Which is white."

He ate. The fire cracked. Somewhere across the black water something large moved without hurry, the way large things do when they own the night.

"There are thousands of Nakamuras," he said finally.

"And there's one Kenji, and one lecture hall, and one monograph my brother loved, and one guide on this river whose father wrote it." I set my bowl down. "I'm not asking for your life story. I'm asking why a man who clearly knows this water better than anyone would take a job retracing a route his father charted forty years ago, and pretend the name on the chart means nothing to him."

For a moment I thought he'd answer. His jaw worked around something—not words; words came easy to him and cheaply, deflections mostly. It was something older than words.

"It means nothing to me," he said, and stood, and carried both bowls to the wash bucket, and that was the end of the interview.

I lay awake in the boat afterward listening to the rain start—fat, scattered drops hitting the canopy like a slow drumroll, the overture to something with a longer runtime than either of us wanted to admit. And I thought about my brother, who talked constantly, who narrated his own surveys like a nature documentary, who would have gotten this man talking inside an hour. Emeka would have known what to say to Felix Nakamura.

I just knew what to watch. And what I watched, all night, was a man sleeping curled toward the gear bag like it was the warmest thing aboard.

* * *

**FELIX**

Day three, the sky gave up pretending.

It didn't rain so much as relocate—an entire ocean deciding the highlands were no longer a suitable home. We ran the last twelve miles to the second camp site with the spray coming sideways and the world reduced to gray water and gray air, and I'll say this for Mara Oduya: she didn't complain once. She just got louder, shouting headings over the engine noise, and once, when a floating log the size of a church pew swung at us out of the murk, she grabbed the tiller with me and we hauled the nose around together, close enough that I could feel her breath, and neither of us said anything about it after, which was its own kind of conversation.

The camp appeared out of the rain like a rumor. Same neat stowage as the first site. Same cold fire ring. And wedged under a stone at the base of a drowned fig tree, a waterproof pouch that made my chest go tight before I even touched it, because I knew the make—I'd grown up watching my father pack that exact brand into that exact kind of pouch, waxed canvas, brass clips, built to survive anything except being opened by the wrong person.

Mara opened it with hands that shook only a little.

Her brother's journal. She read the first page standing in the rain, and I watched her face do several things in sequence, none of them private, and I turned away to check the bilge because some griefs you shouldn't have to perform in front of a stranger.

Then she said, "Felix," in a voice I hadn't heard from her yet, and I turned back.

She was looking past the camp, past the dead fire, at the ridge line above the floodplain—where the rain had stripped a curtain of vegetation off something that should not have been there. Stone. Cut stone, dressed and fitted, running in a long pale line along the ridge like the spine of something enormous surfacing to breathe.

"That's not natural," she said.

No. It wasn't. And the geometry of it—the spacing, the batter of the walls, the way the causeway met the waterline—it was a language I'd been taught before I could ride a bicycle, by a man whose initials I'd found stamped on a survey marker two days ago, and every instinct I had said *look away, get back in the boat, let the river keep it.*

But Mara was already wading toward the shore with her brother's journal clutched to her chest, and the rain was thickening into something with intent, and behind us, far downstream, I could have sworn—though I'd never swear to it—that I heard an engine that wasn't ours.

I killed the motor and followed her in.
