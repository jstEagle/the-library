## 5. The Drowned City

The river told us where to look before we knew we were looking.

I'd been reading water for twenty years—the way a current braids around a snag, the way silt hangs in the slow water like breath held underwater—but I'd never seen a river lie the way this one was lying. The Serevan's main channel ran brown and fast to our left, but off to the right, through a gap in the drowned cane, there was a stretch of water moving wrong. Too smooth. Too green. Water that had found something it couldn't push past and decided, with the patience of all water, to go over instead of around.

"Slack water," Mara said, before I said anything. She'd been standing at the bow for an hour, one hand on the rail, watching the banks like she expected them to confess. "That's not a backwater. That's impounded flow. Something's damming it."

"Or something's under it."

She turned and looked at me, and I wished I'd kept my mouth shut, because there it was again—that look she gave me when I accidentally knew too much. Like she was adding another line to a ledger she kept on me. *Nakamura. Knows rivers. Knows stone. Won't say why.*

"Take us in," she said.

I took us in.

* * *

The second camp sat on a shelf of high ground that used to be a riverbank and was now, temporarily, an island. The flood had eaten everything below it; you could see the old waterline as a band of pale mud three feet up the tree trunks, and above that, the ground was still dry enough to hold our boots without sucking at them.

The camp was neat. That was the thing I noticed first, before the fear arrived. Neatness is worse than mess. Mess means panic, means running, means people who at least got to run. Neatness means someone folded their world up carefully and then stopped existing.

Tents staked and guyed, rainflies drawn tight. A tarp strung over the cooking area, pots washed and nested. Emeka Oduya's team had broken camp the way disciplined people break camp, and then they had walked into four days of monsoon rain and left no tracks, because there were no tracks. The mud around the campsite was unbroken. Not even birds had crossed it.

Mara stood very still in the middle of it, her arms wrapped around herself, and I watched her do the arithmetic every sibling of a missing person does: *he was here, he was fine, he was organized, and then—*

"He wouldn't leave gear," she said. Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice of someone holding a door shut from the inside. "Emeka would sooner leave a kidney. He's fastidious about equipment. It's insufferable."

"I noticed."

"You haven't met him."

"No," I said. "But I've met his tents."

That got half a sound out of her—not quite a laugh, more like a laugh that had been interrupted by grief and never resumed. She moved through the camp the way I'd have moved through it, checking the logical places: under the tarp, inside the tent flaps, beneath the cook kit. I checked the illogical ones, because twenty years of survey work teaches you that people hide things where fear tells them to, not where sense does.

I found it behind the fire ring, wedged into a crack in a slab of stone that shouldn't have been there at all.

Stone. On a riverbank in delta country, where the geology is all silt and patience. A dressed block, edges cut square, surface pitted by a century of submersion—and stamped near one corner, faint but legible, with a surveyor's mark. Three initials.

*K.N.*

My father's initials. My father's mark, cut into a stone that had been underwater since before I was born, which meant my father had been here, or someone using his system had, and either way the past had just reached up out of the mud and taken hold of my ankle.

"Felix." Mara's voice came from inside the nearest tent, strange and tight. "Felix, come here."

She was crouched over a floorboard she'd pried up, and in the cavity beneath it lay a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with wax, the kind of waterproofing river people use and government surveyors don't bother with. She unwrapped it with hands that shook only a little, and inside was a journal. A cheap spiral notebook, the kind that costs less than lunch, wrapped like a relic.

Emeka's handwriting on the cover. And below it, in smaller letters: *If I don't come back, this needs to reach someone who isn't bought.*

We went back to the boat to read it. Some things you don't read standing over a cold campfire in a place that swallows people.

* * *

Mara read aloud. I ran the boat into the slack water channel and anchored—properly anchored, two lines, because I didn't trust the river anymore—and she sat across from me in the cockpit with the flashlight in one hand and her brother's words in the other, and the afternoon went gold and then grey around us while she read.

The early entries were routine. Soundings, water levels, complaints about the food. Then, on the fourth day:

*"Bathymetry's gone strange. Channel drops away under us—soundings read eleven meters where charts say four. Rios thinks instrument error. It's not instrument error. There's structure down there. Regular. Dressed stone. We dropped a line with a weight and it came up with mortar in the joints."*

Mara looked up at me. "Dressed stone," she repeated, as if I might have missed it.

"Keep going."

*"We've located it properly now. A complex, submerged since the last century's floods—the old maps call it a trading shrine, but the maps are wrong. It's big. Terraced platforms, a causeway, standing stones in rows. The floodwaters dropped during the dry season and exposed the upper terrace for the first time in living memory. First time in a hundred years anyone has stood on it. Rios cried. I'm not ashamed to say I understood him."*

Her voice caught on that sentence, just slightly, and steadied itself.

*"There are carvings. Astronomical alignments—the whole complex is oriented to a solstice rise that doesn't match any published survey of the region. Which means the published surveys are wrong, or incomplete, or suppressed. I've requested the historical archives through the ministry liaison, Okonjo, and he's stonewalling. He asked me today what it would take to make our report 'simpler.' I told him the truth doesn't come in simple."*

Mara lowered the journal. "Okonjo," she said. "He signed our search authorization. His office hired you."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"Mara—"

"*Keep going,*" she said, and it wasn't about the journal anymore, and we both knew it, and I let it go because the light was dying and the first real rain had started ticking against the canopy and I wanted to hear the ending before whatever was coming arrived.

She read on. The entries shortened. The tone changed. Someone was visiting their camp at night—footprints, a skiff with no lights, supplies moved. Rios wanted to pull out. Emeka wrote that they'd stay one more day to finish documenting the alignment, because documentation was the only protection the site had: *"Once it's recorded publicly, they can't strip it quietly. Paper is armor. We just need one more clear day—"*

The next entry began normally and ended mid-word. No date after it. Just:

*"Lights on the water again. Not the barge. Voss says he's with the ministr"*

And nothing. The pen had lifted mid-syllable and never come back down.

The rain picked at the canvas overhead. Somewhere upstream, thunder cleared its throat.

"Voss," Mara said.

"There'll be a Voss in every version of this story," I said. "There always is. The man who arrives right after the discovery and right before the disappearance, carrying paperwork and good intentions."

"You think he has them."

"I think your brother hid that journal under a floorboard and waterproofed it like a man who expected his camp to be searched. I think he wrote 'someone who isn't bought' because he'd already priced everyone around him. And I think a team that leaves no tracks in four days of rain didn't walk anywhere." I heard my own voice come out level and cold and hated it a little. "But that's tomorrow's problem."

"What's tonight's problem?"

As if the river had been waiting for its cue, the bilge alarm—a cheap thing I'd installed myself and never once trusted—went off with a chirp like a dying bird.

* * *

Here is what I will say about bailing water through the first night of a monsoon: it concentrates the mind.

The hull had been holed somewhere in the false channel that morning—we'd run over submerged debris, probably a fence post, possibly a roof beam, and I'd felt the knock through the keel and filed it under *later*. Later had arrived, as later does, all at once and in the dark. The take was slow but honest: a steady seep coming in faster than the automatic pump liked, faster, after an hour, than the pump could argue with.

So we bailed. Mara on the bucket, me on the hand pump, taking turns, the flashlight clipped to the canopy throwing our shadows huge against the rain curtains. The storm had come down on us like a decision. Not the polite prologue we'd been getting for days—this was the monsoon proper, the wall of it, rain so dense the river disappeared and we floated on weather. Lightning turned the world white once every few seconds, and in each flash I saw the same impossible thing: water stretching flat and endless in every direction, and out of it, dark and vast, the shoulders of the drowned city.

The upper terrace stood maybe four feet above the floodline, a long black spine of stone. In the lightning flashes I could pick out the rows of standing stones Emeka had described, tilted like teeth, and the raised mass of the central platform beyond them. A hundred years underwater, and here it broke the surface like something surfacing to breathe.

"It's beautiful," Mara shouted over the rain, pausing with the bucket halfway to the gunwale. She wasn't talking about the masonry.

"Yeah," I said. "That's usually how you know it's going to cost you."

Water came over the transom in a lazy, casual way, like it owned the place. I got the pump going again. My arms burned. Mara's hair had given up entirely and hung in ropes, and she was laughing—actually laughing, low and ragged, the laugh of a person who has passed through fear into some cleaner country beyond it.

"We're going to sink," she said.

"Not tonight."

"You can't know that."

"I've been sinking slowly for twenty years," I said, hauling on the pump handle. "You develop a feel for the rate."

She looked at me across the flashing dark, water streaming off her chin, and something in her face shifted—the ledger closing, maybe, or a page turning. Whatever she was going to say next got cut off by the bilge alarm again, shriller now, and by the sight that made us both stop moving.

Lightning held the world open for a full two seconds, and in that window I saw the standing stones clearly—clearly enough to see that one of them had fallen, or been pushed, and lying in its place on the terrace, half-tangled in torn netting, was a shape that was not stone.

A body. Or a tarp wrapped around something the size of a body. Anchored down with line, weighted with rock, left on the highest ground the flood allowed—as if whoever put it there had wanted it found eventually, by someone, but not yet.

The dark came back. The rain kept falling. And the bilge alarm screamed on, patient as everything else in this country, telling us exactly how much time we had left.
