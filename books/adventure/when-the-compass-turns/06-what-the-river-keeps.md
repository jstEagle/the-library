## 6. What the River Keeps

The bilge pump died at eleven minutes past midnight, which I know because I was watching the time the way you watch a wound—hoping it will stop bleeding on its own and knowing it won't.

"Hand me the bucket," Felix said. He didn't shout it. That was the thing about Felix Nakamura that I was only beginning to understand: he didn't raise his voice for anything short of drowning, and even then he'd probably just gesture.

I handed him the bucket. The rain came down like a verdict. We were tucked into the lee of a drowned temple wall—the irony of sheltering behind a ruin that had already lost its argument with this river was not lost on me—and the boat sat low in the black water, taking on more than we could argue back out of her.

Bailing is honest work. There's no room in it for pride or pretense or the careful distance two strangers maintain when they're both hiding something. You scoop, you throw, you scoop again. The river takes what it wants and gives back nothing but weight.

Felix bailed with the rhythm of a man who had done this before, many times, alone. I noticed that about him too. Everything he did looked rehearsed for an audience of one.

"Read," he said.

"What?"

"The journal. Your brother's journal. Read it out loud." He nodded at my pack, where Emeka's notebook sat wrapped in its waterproof sleeve like something sacred. "If I'm going to bail until dawn, I need something to think about besides the hole in this boat."

So I turned on the flashlight, clamped it under my chin like a child telling ghost stories, and opened my brother's handwriting to the storm.

* * *

Emeka wrote the way he talked—fast, certain, with the spelling of a man who thought faster than his pen could follow. Reading him felt like hearing his voice, and that was a mercy and a cruelty at once. I hadn't heard his actual voice in five weeks. I'd been telling myself five weeks meant nothing, that government survey teams missed check-ins all the time because of weather and broken radios and bureaucratic sloth.

His entries said otherwise.

*Third day up the Serevan. The flood has dropped further than anyone expected—old man Okonkwo says the water hasn't been this low since his grandfather's time. And the water is hiding something.*

*We found stone today. Not river stone. Cut stone. Steps, maybe, running down off the ridge and under the waterline. Dr. Adeyemi is beside himself. He keeps saying "a century, at least a century submerged."*

I read that part twice. Around us the rain hammered the canopy, and Felix threw bucket after bucket over the gunwale, and somewhere out in the dark the river breathed against the ruined wall like something patient.

*Fourth day. It's not just steps. It's a complex—a causeway, chambers, carvings under the silt. Adeyemi thinks it may connect to the pre-delta trade routes everyone wrote off as legend. This is the find of a career. This is the find of several careers.*

Then the handwriting changed. Not the letters—the pressure. Emeka pressed harder when something frightened him. I'd seen it in his school notebooks, in his letters home from university. The pen became a weapon when he was afraid.

*I don't like the new arrangement. Voss's people arrived yesterday—"salvage consultants," per the ministry liaison, though nobody filed paperwork I've seen. They have dredging equipment. Dredging equipment does not accompany a hydrological survey. I asked Okafor about it and he laughed at me. Okafor has never laughed at me in twelve years of friendship. Either the money is very good or he is very scared.*

My throat closed. I kept reading, because Felix had asked me to read, and because stopping would mean looking at what I was reading.

*They started pulling artifacts last night. By flashlight, no permits shown, no cataloguing. I photographed everything—camera's hidden in the second aid kit, if anything happens to me. If anything happens to me. Why did I write that? Because I watched two of Voss's men look at me the way you look at a problem you haven't solved yet.*

"I'm going to stop you there," Felix said quietly.

"You wanted me to read."

"I did." He set the bucket down. Water sloshed around our ankles, cold and insistent. "Keep reading."

*Last entry. The liaison came by at dusk. Told us the check-in schedule was being moved up, that we'd be extracted early, that the site would be "handled administratively." Handled. Like a spill. Like a body. I told Emeka—no. I'm telling myself now. If someone finds this: the site is real, the looting is real, and the cover comes from inside the ministry. Check the extraction orders against the permit registry. Someone signed for equipment that was never requisitioned. Follow the signatures.*

The entry stopped mid-word. A single letter, half-formed, trailing off the line like a man pulled away from the page.

I sat there with the flashlight shaking in my hand and understood, finally and completely, why the campsite had been too clean. Why there were no tracks in four days of mud. Why the gear was stowed and the fire was cold and the whole place looked less like a disappearance than like a stage set struck between acts.

"They didn't vanish," I said. My voice came out flat and strange. "They were moved. Collected. The ones who mattered anyway." I looked up at Felix. "Someone on that team was working for them from the start. Okafor, probably. Maybe the liaison. Maybe—"

I stopped, because Felix had gone very still, and stillness on that man was louder than shouting.

* * *

Here is what I knew about Felix Nakamura at that point, bailing water out of a dying boat in a monsoon:

He had no last name on any permit I could find. I'd checked—I'd bribed a clerk in the depot office and gone through three years of river licenses while Felix loaded fuel drums twenty meters away. Every document said *F. Nakamura, contract surveyor,* and every one of them had been flagged, quietly, by someone, with a note that read *clearance pending.* Pending for years. Nobody's clearance stays pending for years unless somebody wants it to.

He flinched at his father's name. I'd tested it twice, casually, the way you test ice—*Dr. Kenji Nakamura, wasn't he involved in the delta excavations back in the day?*—and both times Felix had answered with a flatness so total it was practically a confession.

And he had known, the moment I showed him the survey marker stamped with those initials, exactly what it meant. I'd watched his face do something complicated and then go blank, and blankness, I have learned, is what people build when they're hiding a house.

"Your clearance," I said. "It's never come through, has it. Because someone is keeping it frozen. Because someone knows who you are."

"Mara—"

"And your father spent thirty years hunting sites like this one. Sites the river took. Sites that surface once a century." I stood, ankle-deep in river water, holding my brother's journal like a torch. "And a salvage crew with ministry cover shows up at a site that surfaced three weeks ago, and you happen to be the only guide willing to run the search, and I am supposed to believe that's coincidence?"

The rain filled the silence between us. Felix looked at the bucket, at the water rising past the second plank, at the dark wall of the drowned city keeping the worst of the current off our hull. When he finally spoke, he didn't perform calm. He just sounded tired, in the way of a man setting down something heavy he'd carried so long his arms had forgotten their shape.

"My father got a letter about this region eight months ago," he said. "A colleague of his found references to a submerged complex on the Serevan in some colonial-era archive. He wanted to come look. His health wouldn't allow it. So he asked me." A humorless breath. "He asked me to be his eyes. First time in twenty years he'd asked me for anything."

"And?"

"And I told him no." Felix picked the bucket back up, scooped, threw. Scooped, threw. "I told him I don't work for Nakamuras. I work for whoever pays, and nobody pays for legends. Two weeks later he was dead, and the estate sent me his field journal, and I put it in my bag without opening it because opening it felt like—" He stopped. Started again. "Because I've spent twenty years making sure nothing of his could get into me. And then your brother's team found the exact thing my father died wanting to find, and the government hired me to search for them, and I told myself it was a job. Just a job."

"Is it?"

"No." He met my eyes for the first time since the pump died. "I think someone used my father's research to find this site. I think they used it fast and dirty, before anyone legitimate could, and I think when your brother's team stumbled onto the operation, they couldn't decide whether to buy them or bury them. And I think they let me onto this river because either I'd find nothing—or I'd find something, and then I'd be a Nakamura standing in the middle of it, and that would be useful to them. Leverage. A scapegoat. Take your pick."

The words landed one after another, and I let them, because I'd spent six days suspecting and suspicion is cheap; proof costs more.

"You're Kenji Nakamura's son," I said. "The son. The one who walked away. Everyone in the field knows the story—they just don't know the son changed his name to a first initial and disappeared into the wetlands."

"I didn't change my name. I just stopped using it." Another bucketful over the side. "There's a difference. The difference is that changing your name means you want to be someone else. I just wanted to be no one."

"That's insane."

"It's worked beautifully for twenty years."

"Has it?" I gestured—at the boat, the storm, the journal, the whole flooded catastrophe of the week. "Look around, Felix."

He looked. And something in his face gave way—not dramatically, nothing so kind as drama. It just settled, like sediment finding the bottom, into an expression I recognized because I'd worn it myself since the day Emeka missed his second check-in. The face of a person who has been holding a door shut and finally admits what's pushing on the other side.

"He wrote to me every year," Felix said. "Birthday, New Year. Same envelope, same handwriting. I never opened them. I kept them. All of them. In a box, in storage, in a town I don't live in anymore." He laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "Twenty years of never answering, and I still kept every letter. What does that make me?"

"His son," I said.

The water was above the third plank now. Somewhere beneath us, the hull groaned—a long, structural complaint, the sound of a boat renegotiating its terms with the river.

"We're sinking," I said.

"Not yet."

"Felix. We are actively, measurably sinking, and the nearest dry land is a temple roof, and the nearest help is a hundred kilometers downstream, and the people who took my brother's team have a gunmetal skiff and a ministry leash." I held up the journal. "You just told me they may have let you find this. Which means they know where we are. Which means—"

"Which means we can't stay here." He was already moving, pulling the tarp, checking the fuel line with hands that knew the boat better than the boat knew itself. "The chamber in Emeka's charts—the one above the flood line, on the east face. If we can reach it before the hull goes, we get out of the water and out of sight."

"And if the hull goes first?"

"Then we swim for it." He glanced at me, and for one unguarded second there was something almost like warmth under all that iron. "You're a hydrologist, Mara. Read the current. Tell me where it wants us to go."

I read the current. The current said east, toward the stone, toward whatever my brother had touched last.

I told Felix where to steer, and the boat leaned into the dark, and behind us the river rose to take back everything it had ever lent—including, I suspected, the truth about who had signed for what.

The last thing I saw before we left the lee of the wall was a light far upstream. Not lightning. Lightning doesn't hold position.

It was coming downriver toward us, steady as a signature.
