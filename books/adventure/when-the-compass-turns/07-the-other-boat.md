## 7. The Other Boat

The rain had a rhythm by then, and the rhythm was *everything*.

It came down in sheets so thick that the river and the sky stopped being separate things, and our little boat sat in the seam between them like a stitch that had come loose. Mara bailed. I steered. Every twenty minutes we swapped jobs because both of them were terrible and fairness seemed important when you were drowning slowly.

That's when I heard the engine.

Not ours — ours didn't have one anymore, or rather it did, but starting it felt like asking a question I wasn't sure I wanted answered. This was another engine, deeper, idling somewhere upstream behind the curtain of rain. A sound with intent behind it.

"Mara."

She looked up from the bail bucket, water streaming off her braids. "I heard it."

"Could be the barge."

"Could be." She said it the way you say *could be* when you already know it isn't.

The skiff came out of the gray like something surfacing from deep water. Gunmetal hull, no registration numbers, no markings at all except the particular clean absence that marks things that don't want to be identified. It rode low and heavy in the flood, which told me before I saw inside it that it was carrying weight. Boats that are looking for people ride light. Boats that have already found what they were looking for ride heavy.

There were four men aboard. The one at the wheel was broad without being soft, maybe fifty, with the kind of face that has been rained on in enough countries to stop having a nationality. He raised a hand — friendly, unhurried — and called across the water in English flattened into nowhere:

"You folks need help?"

And here is the thing about me: I have spent twenty years making myself unremarkable. I know how to be nobody. I can stand next to a man and let him look straight through me, and it's a skill I've polished like river stone. But recognition doesn't care about skill. Recognition happens in the body, below the level where you keep your secrets.

Because I knew that voice. Not the man — the voice. I'd heard it through a radio handset three days ago, playing back the missing team's last check-in for the government liaison, a recording someone had *cleaned*, and underneath the static there had been a second voice, calm, giving instructions to a man who was supposed to be leading an official survey. I hadn't placed it then. I placed it now.

"Rescue," I said, keeping my tone flat as silt. "We're fine. Hull's holed but she's floating."

The man smiled. His name, he offered, was Voss. He said it the way men say names they've worn out — casually, like a coat.

* * *

He brought his skiff alongside us with the ease of someone who'd done it a thousand times in worse weather, and that ease was its own kind of message. Rescue crews work fast and nervous. This man worked slow. He hooked a line to our gunwale himself, and while he tied it I looked past him into the hold of his boat, and my stomach did the thing stomachs do when they understand something before the rest of you does.

Tarps. Under the tarps, shapes. A tripod case with a government asset tag half scraped away — but not all the way, because scraping takes time and whoever did it had been interrupted or careless. A transit case stenciled SEREVAN HYDROLOGICAL SURVEY. And a duffel bag I recognized, because I'd seen its twin at the empty landing: the standard-issue kit bags the survey team carried, one of them still bearing a name tape in faded thread.

*O. Adeyemi.*

One of the missing men. His bag was riding in the bottom of a salvage skiff, dry, packed neat as laundry.

I didn't say any of this out loud. Saying it out loud would have been true, and true things, delivered to a man like Voss on open water, tend to become problems. Instead I did what I do. I became furniture.

Mara, though — Mara went the other direction entirely, and I will spend the rest of my life admiring her for it. She let herself be seen. She pulled her hood back, let the rain mat her hair down, and put on grief the way other people put on life jackets: quickly, completely, and with total commitment. Her eyes went wet on command. I genuinely could not tell you how much of it was performance.

"Please," she said, and her voice cracked in exactly the right place. "You've been upriver? We're looking for my brother. Emeka Oduya. He was with the survey team — they missed their check-ins, four days now—"

Voss's face performed sympathy beautifully. It was like watching two mirrors face each other. "Terrible business," he said. "Whole river's been turned over looking for them. You two shouldn't be out here alone." He paused, and the pause had weight in it. "Where are you headed?"

"Downstream," I said. "To the confluence. Report in."

"To who?"

"The liaison office."

"Mm." He looked at me then, really looked, and I felt the look go through me like a sounding line — down, down, checking depth. "You're the contractor. Nakamura, right?"

And there it was. My name, spoken aloud on the water, by a stranger, in the rain. Twenty years of leaving that name off every permit, every manifest, every camp register, and this man said it like he'd been carrying it around waiting for the chance.

"No," I said.

His eyebrows rose a fraction. "The permits list a Nakamura."

"Permits list a lot of things." I shrugged, and the shrug cost me everything I had, because shrugging while your pulse tries to climb out of your throat is a specific kind of acting. "Name's Felix. I pilot boats."

Voss studied me for a long moment. Rain ran off his jaw. Whatever he decided, he decided it privately, and I understood that whatever it was, it wasn't settled — it was deferred. Men like him don't make decisions in front of witnesses. They make them later, in the dark, where decisions are cheaper.

"We'll take your line off," he said finally. "Weather's turning worse. Get downstream safe." Then, almost as an afterthought, almost gently: "If you see anything up here — wreckage, camps, anything — best to leave it be. Floodwater moves things around. Easy to get hurt poking at it."

It was not advice. It was a map of intentions drawn in the shape of courtesy.

* * *

They cast us off and the gunmetal skiff held position for a full minute, engines idling, watching us pull away. I counted the minute. I counted it because counting kept my hands busy and my hands needed to be busy, because if they weren't busy they were going to shake, and shaking hands on a tiller in monsoon current is how you die honestly instead of dishonestly.

Then the skiff turned upstream. Toward the ruin. Toward the drowned city and whatever was left of the survey team.

"They have their equipment," Mara said quietly. She'd dropped the grief from her voice like setting down something heavy. "Their bags. That's Olu's kit — Olu Adeyemi, he never went anywhere without—" She stopped. Her jaw tightened. "Rescue teams don't collect personal effects and pack them neatly under tarps. Salvage crews do."

"I know."

"And you lied to him."

"I lie to everyone. It's my best quality."

"Felix." She looked at me across the bail water sloshing in the bilge, and her expression wasn't anger. It was worse. It was the look of someone doing arithmetic. "He called you Nakamura. He knew the name before he ever saw your face. Which means someone told him. Which means someone has been tracking you — or tracking your father's name — since before we ever launched."

The rain filled the silence between us, drumming on the hull, on the water, on the whole drowned world.

She was right, and I had known she was right since the moment Voss opened his mouth, and knowing it changed nothing about how cold it made me. My father had spent forty years hunting something in this delta. He was dead now, three weeks dead, and the first person to speak his name aloud in my hearing was a man riding a boat full of stolen government gear. The hunt hadn't died with him. It had just changed hands, and apparently the new hands had a list, and my name was on it twice — once as the son, once as the guide.

"So what do we do?" Mara asked. "Run?"

"Running's what I'm good at," I said. "But no. Not yet."

I nodded upstream, toward the gray wall where the skiff had gone. "They're heading for the ruin. If any of that team is alive, they're alive near the ruin, and Voss is going to find them first." I looked down at the bilge water swirling around our ankles, at the boat that was dying under us one liter at a time. "We can't outrun them and we can't outgun them. But we know these channels better than they do, and we know where the survivors' second camp was, and—" I hesitated, because the next part was the part I'd been avoiding saying out loud even to myself.

"And?"

"And there's a barge." I pointed downstream, to where the freight channel bent south. Twice a week, weather permitting, the supply barges came up from the delta towns hauling diesel and rice, and they carried radios, and radios reached people who couldn't be bought. "When we passed the cutoff yesterday I saw smoke on the water. There's traffic down there. If we can signal a barge — get an official witness, an official record — then Voss's whole operation becomes a liability instead of a convenience. Men like him can murder two people on a flooded river and call it misadventure. They can't murder two people in front of a barge crew and a satellite log."

Mara was quiet for a moment. Rain ticked against the hull.

"You want to flag down a cargo barge in a monsoon."

"I want to *not disappear*," I said. "Those are different things."

She almost laughed — a short, hard sound, gone as fast as it came. "My brother used to say something like that. *Don't disappear, Emeka, whatever else you do.*" She wiped rain off her face with the heel of her hand, and when she looked at me again the grief was still there but it had hardened into something usable. Something with edges. "All right. Signal the barge. But Felix — if we're doing this, we're doing it all the way. No more half-truths between us either. When we make landfall, you tell me the rest. All of it. What your father was hunting, and why a salvage crew knows your name."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair. It's necessary. Fairness is for people with time."

* * *

We ran downstream through the worst water I've ever put a hull into, and I've put hulls into some water. The tributary had stopped being a river and become a moving lake with opinions, and every channel marker was underwater, and I steered by memory and by the color of the current — brown water runs shallow, green-black water runs deep, and water that looks smooth is lying to you.

Behind us, maybe a kilometer back, the gunmetal skiff followed.

It didn't chase. Chasing is loud and committed. It *followed* — patient, matching our speed, staying just far enough back to stay ambiguous. Letting us know. Letting us think about it. Because that's the thing about men like Voss: they understand that fear does most of their work for them, that a witness who spends six hours marinating in dread will often solve your problem for you by panicking, or wrecking, or simply deciding not to testify.

So I let myself be afraid, briefly, thoroughly, the way you let a fever break. And then I got angry instead, because anger steers better.

Mara spotted the barge first — a low black shape crawling up the freight channel through the rain, running lights doubled against the weather. I cut across the flooded saddle between the tributary and the main channel, riding water that shouldn't have been navigable, and when we came alongside the barge's leeward flank I stood up in the pitching boat and fired three flares into the air, one after another, red stars burning holes in the gray.

A figure appeared on the barge deck. A horn sounded — two blasts, questioning.

"Vessel in distress!" I shouted across the water, and then, louder, making sure every syllable landed like a stake driven into ground: "Requesting escort to the confluence! Two persons aboard, witnesses to a salvage operation on the Serevan tributary! Names logged — Felix Nakamura, Mara Oduya!"

Mara stared at me. I had just done the one thing I had sworn off for twenty years. I had said the name. Out loud. On the record. Attached myself to it permanently, in front of witnesses, in ink that doesn't wash out.

On the barge, someone wrote it down.

Behind us, the gunmetal skiff slowed, hung back at the edge of visibility, and did not turn away.

We had become cargo now — the dangerous kind, the kind that has to be accounted for. And somewhere upstream, in the drowned rooms of a city the river had kept for a hundred years, Voss was closing on the last of the missing team, and I had a horrible, certain feeling that we were racing him for more than testimony.

The barge horn blasted again, and this time it was answering.
