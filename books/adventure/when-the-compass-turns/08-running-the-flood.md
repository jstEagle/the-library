## 8. Running the Flood

**FELIX**

The barge horn answered us, and then the river took the question back.

I've run water that would make a grown man weep. I've threaded gorges in Nepal where the guidebooks say *not recommended for anything with a heartbeat*. But I have never — in twenty years of taking jobs no one else wanted — piloted a boat through rapids that did not exist yesterday.

That's what monsoon does to the Serevan tributary. It invents geography overnight. Channels that were ankle-deep trickles at dusk become churning brown throats by midnight, and the old maps are suddenly fiction. The drowned city's causeways, submerged for a century, now stood just proud enough of the flood to shred a hull like wet paper.

"Voss is coming," Mara said. She didn't shout it. She'd stopped shouting somewhere around the third hour of bailing and had settled into a flat, terrible calm that scared me more than the storm did.

"I know."

"He has four men and a boat that isn't leaking."

"I noticed."

"And we're running *toward* him."

I put the tiller over hard and didn't answer, because she was right, and because the answer was Emeka. Voss was closing on the last of the missing team, and every minute we spent being sensible was a minute he spent getting there first. Rescue was a word he wore like a coat. Underneath it he was a man tidying up loose ends, and three government surveyors were very loose ends indeed.

The rain came sideways now, needling through the torn canopy, and the channel ahead lit up white with foam. I couldn't read it. That was the truth of it — twenty years of reading water, and this water was written in a language invented an hour ago.

"Mara." I held out my hand without looking away from the dark. "The charts."

She had them wrapped in oilcloth inside her jacket, against her body like a second heartbeat. Emeka's hand-drawn charts — the ones he'd made sneaking up tributaries his own survey wasn't authorized to touch, because Emeka Oduya had never met a rule he respected more than a question. She'd been studying them all evening by flashlight, lips moving, memorizing.

She pressed them into my palm and kept her fingers on mine a moment longer than the transaction required.

"He marked the deep channels," she said. "Pencil lines. He said the river remembers its old bones even when it floods — the water goes deepest where the stone used to be."

"The drowned city's streets," I said. "He navigated by a ghost."

"He navigated by his sister's brother," she said, and something in her voice cracked down the middle, quick as ice, and then sealed again. "Follow the pencil."

So I did. I ran a flooded river at night by the pencil marks of a missing man, while his sister crouched in the bilge calling out depths from a chart drawn in a dead man's handwriting — no. Not dead. I refused the word. *Missing.* Missing meant the story wasn't finished, and I had built my whole life on unfinished stories being easier to walk away from.

The boat hit the first causeway ledge at 11:40 by my watch. I felt it come — a smooth green shoulder of water ahead, too smooth, the lie rivers tell before they show their teeth — and I swung us hard along Emeka's pencil line, and still the hull kissed stone with a sound like a knuckle cracking. Something below decks shifted. The leak, which had been a polite trickle, became a conversation.

"We're taking water," Mara called.

"I know."

"You keep saying you know."

"It's the only thing I'm sure of!"

And then — because the night hadn't humiliated me enough — the engine coughed.

* * *

**MARA**

The engine died the way Emeka used to fall asleep mid-sentence: abruptly, rudely, with unfinished business everywhere.

Felix got the boat sideways into an eddy behind a flooded fig tree, which was either brilliant seamanship or dumb luck, and in the dark I honestly could not tell you which, and neither, I suspect, could he. Water came over the transom in a lazy, proprietary way, like a landlord inspecting property he already owned.

We had perhaps twenty minutes. Maybe less. The gunmetal skiff was upstream, and Voss had lights, and we had a dying boat and a flashlight with a failing battery and a set of charts drawn by a man who might be —

No. I did that math all day and it never came out different. *Missing.*

"Options," Felix said. He was bailing with a fuel can cut in half, working steadily, not panicking, and I hated how much steadiness looked good on him. "One: we drift downstream and pray the barge finds us. Two: we beach here and hide in the mangroves until morning, and hope Voss passes us by."

"And three?"

He lifted the flashlight and swung the beam across the water to our left, where the rain had thinned for one merciful moment.

Stone. A wall of it, black and streaming, rising out of the flood maybe thirty meters off our beam. And near its top, above the flood line, a dark rectangle: a doorway, or a window, or a mouth.

"That's the complex," Felix said. "Emeka's coordinates put the main chamber right about there. The upper rooms were never flooded — that's why the site stayed secret. The river kept everything below and left the attic dry."

"You want to climb into a ruin," I said, "in the middle of a monsoon, pursued by armed men."

"I want to not be floating in a colander when they get here. Yes."

It was a bad plan. It was the kind of plan Emeka would have loved, which should have been a warning, but instead it felt like a hand on my shoulder. I took an oar.

* * *

**FELIX**

Getting to the wall nearly killed us twice, and I'll be honest about the arithmetic: the river would have won if the ruin hadn't helped. There was a stair under the water — Emeka's pencil line ran straight up it, because of course it did; the man had charted a staircase he'd never seen dry — and the current funneled along it instead of across it, and we rode that funnel like a rail until the bow ground against stone and Mara was out and hauling the line and I was throwing our one dry bag up to her into the dark rectangle above.

The chamber inside was low-ceilinged and utterly black and smelled of cold silt and bat. My flashlight found carved walls — figures, processions, a river god with a crown of reeds, worn smooth by a century of damp air — and I registered all of it with the part of my brain that wasn't busy shaking.

Then the light caught something modern.

A crate. Government stencil. Then another. A tarp. A camp stove, cold. And against the far wall, three men sitting up out of sleep with their hands raised, blinking into the beam like things surprised underground.

One of them said, in a voice scraped down to gravel: "Are you Voss?"

"No," I said.

The middle one started to cry. Quietly, the way you cry when you've been crying for days and have gotten efficient at it.

Mara's flashlight swept past me and locked on. "Which one of you is Oduya's team?" she said, and her voice was steel over a fault line. "Where is Emeka Oduya?"

The tall one on the left lowered his hands slowly. "You're — Mara? He talked about you. Said if anyone came looking, it'd be you." He swallowed. "I'm Deng. Survey team. We're the only — " He stopped. Started again. "There were six of us."

The chamber went very quiet except for the rain on the stone above and the river chewing at the walls below.

"Voss's crew pulled us out of the water four days ago," Deng said. "Our boat went over at the second camp. Two of ours drowned. They fished the rest of us out — I thought God, at first. Then we saw what was in their hold." His face did something complicated. "They didn't kill us. That's the worst part. They *need* us alive. Dead men can't sign statements saying they saw nothing. Living men who want to go home to their families will say anything they're told."

"They're keeping you as witnesses against yourselves," Mara said.

"They feed us. They check on us twice a day. They took Emeka's journal off him the first night and he hasn't spoken since — not properly. He just draws." Deng nodded toward the corner, and my light found a figure curled against the wall, knees up, a pencil stub moving on the back of a ration carton.

Mara made a sound I will hear for the rest of my life.

The figure looked up. Even in the bad light I knew the face, because it was hers, ten years younger and hollowed out. Emeka Oduya stared at us across a drowned king's chamber, and then he said, in a voice like a door opening after a long time:

"Told you the river remembers its bones."

Mara crossed that chamber faster than any flood.

* * *

**MARA**

My brother was lighter than he should have been. That was the fact my hands reported first, before joy, before relief: *he is too light.* Four days of looter rations and silence. I held his face and made myself look at his eyes and they were clear, at least, clear and furious, which for Emeka is healthy.

"How many guns?" Felix asked Deng, all business, giving us the room the way some people know how to do.

"Two rifles between the four of them. They come at dawn and dusk. Boat's moored on the far side of the causeway." Deng hesitated. "They're expecting us to be here. If we're gone at dawn check—"

"They'll know someone else found you," Felix finished. "And they'll know exactly who, because we just signaled a barge with your position." He said it evenly, but I saw his jaw work. Our signal had saved these men from drowning in silence — and painted a target on the whole chamber. Everything we did now cost something. That was the lesson of this whole river.

Emeka pushed himself up against the wall and held out the ration carton he'd been drawing on. "Give this to your father," he said to Felix.

Felix went still. "What?"

"Dr. Nakamura. You're his son — don't argue, Voss bragged about it, thought it was funny, keeping the famous man's boy in the dark." Emeka tapped the carton. "These chambers match a site in his published notes from '89. The one everyone says he fabricated. He didn't fabricate it. He found it, and someone buried the report, and he spent thirty years being called a liar for it." Emeka's eyes were bright and feverish and absolutely certain. "I found his original field coordinates in the archive. That's why I really ran this survey. Your father was right, Felix. He was right the whole time, and they ruined him for it."

I watched Felix take the carton like it might detonate. Twenty years of flinching at a name, and here it was handed to him in pencil on cardboard, vindicating a man he wouldn't even mourn out loud.

Down below, the river rose another inch against the stair, patient as a debt collector.

And out on the black water, faint but unmistakable, an engine changed pitch — slowing, searching, coming around the causeway.

Dawn was hours away. Voss's dusk check was not.

"They're early," Deng whispered.

Felix looked at the doorway, at the water, at Emeka's carton in his hand — and I watched him decide, right there in the dark, that he was done being his father's ghost.

"Then we don't wait for dawn," he said.
