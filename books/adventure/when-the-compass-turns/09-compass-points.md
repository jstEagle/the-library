## 9. Compass Points

The satellite phone sat in the hands of a man who hadn't spoken in nine days, and it was the most beautiful thing I'd ever seen.

His name was Okafor—Dr. Adaeze Okafor, according to the tag still clipped to his collar—and when the three survivors had stumbled out of the stone chamber's dark toward our flashlight beams, he'd looked at me like I was either salvation or a hallucination brought on by eating river fish raw. The other two, a young tech named Priya and a quiet geologist whose name I never did catch properly, were in worse shape. But Okafor could talk. Okafor could talk the paint off a boat.

And Okafor had a satellite phone with half a charge and no idea what to do with it.

"Give it to me," I said.

Voss's skiff was out there somewhere in the rain-dark, running without lights. We'd heard the engine an hour ago—a low, patient sound, like something that knew exactly where we were and saw no reason to hurry. The flood had swallowed the causeway approach; the ruin rose out of black water like the back of a sleeping animal, one chamber above the surface, and we were all of us standing inside its ribs listening to the storm try to take the roof apart.

Mara crouched beside her brother. Emeka Oduya was alive—that was the miracle we'd been handed along with everything else—but his ankle was broken and he'd lost weight the way people lose weight when they've spent nine days deciding not to hope. He'd hidden from the salvage crew by climbing into the flooded lower galleries, breathing through a reed, while Voss's men walked ten feet above his head discussing shipment schedules. Then he'd found the other survivors hiding deeper in, and they'd made a pact of silence and stagnant water.

"You're Nakamura's son," Emeka said again. It wasn't a question this time. It was more like a diagnosis.

"Unfortunately."

"He talked about you." His voice was gravel and disuse. "The survey team. Before—" He stopped. Looked at the water coming through the doorway in long gray ropes. "Before the plant among us reported what we'd found."

I didn't have anything to say to that, so I took the phone.

The field journal came out of my bag last. Twenty years I'd carried it unopened—well, four days, technically, but the weight of it was twenty years, because my father had written *Felix* on the flyleaf in handwriting I'd have known blind, and some things you can't unread even before you read them.

I opened it now because there was no other move left. Voss would be here within the hour. The barge I'd signaled two nights ago was a day out if the pilot believed me, which was a coin flip. And three survivors plus a hydrologist plus me were going to die in a drowned temple unless someone made the truth louder than the money burying it.

My father's journal was a map of obsession. Forty years of entries, coordinates cross-referenced against colonial surveys, flood records, oral histories he'd collected from elders whose grandchildren probably still lived along this tributary. Page after page of a man chasing a city the river had taken, and in the last third—the part written after I left home—the entries changed. Shorter. Sadder. And then, near the end, a full survey of this site: the drowned complex, its true extent, its age. Not treasure. Not a tomb. A trade capital, pre-colonial, intact beneath the silt for a century—the kind of find that rewrites textbooks and makes governments very nervous about who controls the narrative.

He'd known where it was. He'd known for years. And he'd sat on it, or been prevented from publishing—I couldn't tell which, and the distinction suddenly mattered less than the fact that Voss's crew was stripping it with government paper over the operation, and my father's own documentation could prove both the site's significance and the fraud of the salvage permits.

"The coordinates," Mara said, reading over my shoulder. Her voice had gone very quiet. "These match Emeka's charts."

"Of course they do." I thumbed the phone awake. "He found what my father mapped. My father just never told anyone he'd mapped it."

"Why?"

That question deserved better than the answer I had, which was *because he was my father*, so I didn't give it. I gave her the truer one instead: "Because telling the world means letting the world in. And he taught me everything he knew about keeping people out."

The phone caught signal on the third try. Half a bar, flickering, the storm chewing at the connection. I started broadcasting—not a call, a broadcast, because calls get cut off and broadcasts get recorded. I read the permit numbers from Emeka's journal into the phone. I read the shipping manifests Okafor recited from memory, dates and tonnage, the private company's name that kept appearing behind shell registrations. I read my father's coordinates and described the site in the flat professional language of a licensed surveyor, and then, because the moment seemed to demand it, I said my full name into the record.

"Felix Kenjiro Nakamura. Son of Dr. Kenji Nakamura. Witnessing."

Mara's hand found my shoulder. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to. Saying my name out loud felt like stepping off a ledge I'd been circling for twenty years, and the strange thing was that I didn't fall. The name just hung there in the wet air, mine, attached to me, witnessed.

The upload bar crawled. Seventy percent. Eighty.

Then the engine sound stopped.

* * *

They came up the flooded causeway in two boats, six men, lights cutting the rain into white blades. Voss stood in the bow of the first one with a rifle slung casual as a fishing rod, and he had the decency to look annoyed rather than theatrical, which I appreciated. There's nothing worse than a villain who enjoys himself.

"Nakamura," he called across the water. "You're making this complicated."

"That's generally considered my best quality."

"I have a rescue contract too. Paperwork. As far as anyone official is concerned, I found you and the missing team, and there was an accident during extraction." He let that sit. Rain drummed on stone. "Four bodies recovered. Tragic. Nobody asks questions about flood country."

"You'd need five bodies," Mara said, stepping forward, "and you only brought six men. Bad math."

I loved her immediately and completely and put it away for later, because later was a country I was learning might actually exist.

Voss's men fanned onto the causeway's exposed spine, boots slick on ancient stone, and the geometry of it turned simple and terrible: them between us and the boats, the water rising, the phone at ninety-two percent. Okafor pulled Priya back into the chamber. The geologist started praying in a language I didn't know, quietly, like a man doing paperwork.

Emeka tried to stand. His broken ankle folded under him and he went down hard on the causeway stones, and Voss's nearest man moved toward him—not fast, not threatening, just moving, the way you move toward cargo—and something in my chest closed like a fist.

Later, I would try to reconstruct the decision and fail. There wasn't one. There was just the space between Emeka falling and the man reaching him, and my body filling it, because for twenty years I had walked away from every space like that one, and I was done. Done being a rumor. Done being a name withheld. If the compass of my life pointed anywhere now, it pointed here, at a stranger's brother, in the rain, on stone older than every border on any map I'd ever surveyed.

I hit the man at the waist. We went down together on wet rock, and his rifle cracked once into the sky, and then the causeway edge—which had held a city for a thousand years and a flood for nine days—decided it was finished holding us.

Stone tilted. Water took us.

The flood wasn't cold so much as total. It got into everything at once, eyes and ears and the space behind my ribs, and the current had the strength of the whole watershed behind it, every tributary and hillside funneling past this one point. I lost the man I'd tackled. I lost down. I found Emeka—or he found me, a hand closing on my jacket, both of us turning in the brown roar like leaves in a gutter.

I have done a lot of things in water. I have surveyed channels at flood stage and swum rapids I had no business swimming. None of it prepared me for holding another person's head above the surface while my own went under, and none of it prepared me for how little I minded. There was a arithmetic in it that finally balanced: twenty years of exits weighed against one entrance, and the entrance won.

Then a rope hit the water. Then Mara's voice, furious and precise, calling the count of a haul like she was reading a gauge. Then hands—Okafor's, the geologist's—dragging us up the tilting stone as the causeway shed its last dry meters, and I came out of the river coughing half of it back, alive, with Emeka's grip still locked on my jacket like he'd decided drowning me was not on the agenda either.

The phone was gone. Taken by the water, along with my father's journal, which I had been stupid enough to carry into a fight.

But the upload had hit one hundred percent before the causeway broke. I'd watched it happen with the rain streaming down my face and Voss's men thirty feet away, and whatever server was on the other end of that flickering signal now had everything: the permits, the manifests, the coordinates, the name.

Voss stood at the edge of the retreating causeway, rain hammering his shoulders, and for a long moment nobody moved. Then the far channel lit up—running lights, searchlights, the deep horn of a barge that had believed me after all—and behind it, smaller, faster, the government patrol launch that had been riding the barge's wake the whole way in.

Voss's crew looked at the lights, looked at their boss, and did the same math everyone does eventually. Two of them simply sat down on the stones and raised their hands. The rest backed toward the skiffs, and Voss let them go, because a man like Voss understands that loyalty is a salvage operation too—you strip what's valuable and abandon the hull.

He looked at me once across the water. Not hatred. Assessment. Filing me somewhere for later.

Then the authorities were everywhere, loud and official and late, and Mara was kneeling next to me on the wet stone with her hand pressed flat against my chest as if checking that the heart had stayed in.

"You idiot," she said. "You absolute—"

"Yeah," I said. "I know."

And the strange thing, the thing I kept turning over while the patrol officers shouted and the barge swung its lights across the ruined city rising black out of the flood, was that I wasn't thinking about the evidence uploaded or the arrest unfolding or even the journal sinking into the current with forty years of my father's handwriting dissolving into the river that had taken his city.

I was thinking that I'd said my name out loud, in front of witnesses, and the world hadn't ended.

It had, in fact, just gotten considerably larger.

Mara squeezed my jacket where the river had tried to keep it. "When we get off this rock," she said, "you're going to tell me the rest. All of it. Starting with why your father never published."

I looked at the water where the journal had gone down, and for the first time in twenty years, the answer to a question about my father didn't feel like a door closing.

"It's a long story," I said. "And apparently I'm staying."
