## 10. Where the Water Settles

The tribunal room in Port Serevan smelled like mildew and old paper, which struck me as fitting. Everything official in this country eventually came back to water damage.

I sat through three days of testimony with my hands folded like a woman who had never once considered throwing a punch, while the survivors told their stories one at a time. Emeka went second. He'd lost weight in the stone chamber—everyone had—but his voice came out level and clear, and when he described how Voss's crew had sealed them into the ruin with a week of rations and a warning to stay quiet until "the work was finished," I watched two of the tribunal members stop writing and just look at him.

That was the moment I understood it would hold. Not because the evidence was clean—it wasn't; half the salvage company's paperwork had conveniently drowned—but because Emeka testified the way he'd always done everything, without decoration, and the truth needed no decoration. It just needed someone willing to say it out loud.

Felix sat in the back row for all three days. He didn't testify. He'd handed over his father's journal, the survey markers, the photographs of the stripped chambers, and then made himself into furniture. That was his talent, after all. Twenty years of practice.

But he stayed through every session. He stayed when the salvage company's lawyers called him a disgruntled contractor with no permits and no name on any registry. He stayed when they implied—carefully, legally—that a man who hid his own identity might have reasons worth investigating. He stayed, and on the third day, when the tribunal chair asked if anyone else wished to speak, Felix Nakamura stood up, walked to the front of the room, and said, "My name is Felix Kenji Nakamura. My father mapped this site forty years ago and was silenced for trying to report it. Ask me anything."

He answered questions for four hours. I counted. I was watching the clock because watching him was unbearable and necessary, the way watching a man set down something heavy is—you want to help, and you know you can't, and you watch anyway.

Afterward, outside, in the wet heat of late afternoon, he lit a cigarette with hands that weren't quite steady and said, "So that's what my name sounds like in public. Loud."

"Deafening," I said. "You'll want earplugs next time."

He almost smiled. Almost. We were getting there by increments, the way the river gets somewhere—not directly, not quickly, but always moving.

* * *

The last page of Kenji Nakamura's journal was dated eleven days before his death. Felix read it aloud to me on the veranda of the survey station, six weeks after the flood, because he said reading it silently felt like keeping a secret again and he was finished with secrets. His voice did something strange on the second sentence and then steadied itself.

*To whoever finds this—and I have stopped pretending it will be anyone but Felix.*

*I mapped the Serevan complex in my thirty-ninth year. The government buried the survey. My colleagues were reassigned, discredited, one of them worse than discredited. I chose my reputation over the truth and told myself it was strategy. It was cowardice wearing strategy's coat.*

*My son saw it before I did. He was nineteen. He said, "If we don't say it now, we never will," and I sent him away instead of answering. He has spent twenty years making sure no record anywhere carries both our names. I understand why. I have never forgiven myself for it, and I don't ask him to forgive me either.*

*I ask only this: stop running. Whatever you are running from, it is smaller than what you are running past. I learned that too late to be useful to anyone. Be useful to someone. Stay somewhere long enough for the place to know you.*

*The compass metaphor is yours, not mine. You always hated mine. Good. Keep hating it, and point it home anyway.*

Felix closed the journal. The river moved below the veranda, brown and unhurried, carrying its usual cargo of silt and branches and things nobody asked it about.

"He waited until he was dead to say it," Felix said. "Very like him. Timing was never his strength."

"You're joking."

"I'm absolutely joking." He looked out at the water. "Mostly."

I let the silence sit for a while, because some silences are load-bearing. Then I said what I'd come to say, the thing I'd been drafting and discarding for two weeks like a bad survey line.

"The documentation project runs through the dry season. Eight months. Proper mapping, proper preservation assessment, proper everything—the tribunal ordered it, which means it's funded, which means it's real." I turned the page of the notebook I wasn't looking at. "Emeka is leading it. He asked for you by name. So did I, but Emeka's the director, so his asking counts more."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you say no, and I spend eight months being professionally annoyed with an empty chair."

He laughed—a short, surprised sound, like he'd forgotten the mechanism. "You've thought about the empty chair."

"I've thought about a lot of chairs." I closed the notebook. "Felix. I'm not asking you to stay forever. I'm asking you to stay long enough that leaving becomes a decision instead of a reflex. There's a difference. I've watched you leave things your whole adult life from a distance of about four meters, and it looks exhausting."

"It is exhausting," he said quietly. "For the record. It's very exhausting."

"Then rest."

He didn't answer right away. Below us, a barge went by, loaded with gravel, its wake slapping the pilings in slow strokes. Somewhere across the compound, Emeka was arguing with a supplier about waterproof crates, loudly, in two languages, and the sound of it was so ordinary and so alive that I saw Felix hear it too—saw something in his face unclench that had been clenched so long I'd mistaken it for bone structure.

"Eight months," he said.

"Through the dry season. After that, you're free to vanish, and I'll only be moderately insufferable about it."

"That's a lie and we both know it."

"It's a generous lie. Those are the foundation of every good working relationship."

He looked at me then, fully, the way he'd started doing sometime during the flood—like a man checking whether the ground would hold. "Mara. Why does it matter to you?"

Because my brother came back from the dead and you carried him. Because you opened a journal you'd been afraid of for twenty years and read it out loud to a woman you'd known six weeks. Because I have spent my life around water and I know stillness when I finally see it.

"Because the site deserves good documentation," I said, "and you're the best surveyor in the delta, and I'm a hydrologist, not a sentimentalist."

"Liar," he said, using my own word against me, and I decided to allow it.

From Felix:

The bag went under my bunk at the survey station the night I arrived, same as it had gone under bunks and beds and floors in fourteen countries. Old habit. Weight in the wrong place means you can move in ninety seconds, and ninety seconds had been the margin between me and every consequence I'd ever dodged.

I unpacked it on a Tuesday morning in October, without ceremony, because ceremony would have meant admitting it was a decision and I wasn't ready to grant myself that much credit yet.

Two shirts. A rain shell with a torn pocket. The journal, soft-cornered now from handling. A compass my father gave me at nineteen that I'd carried for twenty years without once letting it mean anything. A photograph I'd forgotten was there: the two of us at a dig site, me scowling, him laughing at whatever I was scowling about. I stood there holding it longer than a grown man should stand holding anything.

Then I put the clothes in the locker. The journal went on the shelf, spine out, where anyone could see it. The compass I kept in my jacket pocket, because old habits die slower than new ones, and because Mara had already caught me checking it twice and I preferred she think it was navigation rather than prayer.

She found me at midday on the causeway, the exposed part, where the stones of the drowned city broke the surface like the backs of sleeping animals. She had a clipboard and a look of organized menace.

"We start grid mapping tomorrow," she said. "Sector one. You'll be working under my brother, who will enjoy that immensely."

"He'll be insufferable."

"He's already insufferable. This gives it structure." She looked out over the water, which was low and clear for the first time since I'd known her, the dry season pulling the river back like a held breath released. Beneath the surface, maybe a meter down, you could see the tops of columns. In another month they'd stand in open air, and the city that had spent a century underwater would spend a season in the light, and people would come and write its name correctly in reports.

"Eight months," I said.

"Through the dry season." She glanced at me sideways. "And then?"

And then. The old answer rose up on schedule—*and then I'm gone, and then I'm a name on no permit, and then I'm ninety seconds from nowhere*—and I let it rise and watched it find nothing to attach to. The bag was empty. The locker was shut. The compass pointed, as it happened, due north, toward the station, toward the noise of Emeka arguing with a supplier, toward a shelf with a journal on it and a chair at a table that someone had, apparently, already assigned to me.

"Then I'll probably stay," I said. "Ask me again in the dry season."

"I intend to ask you every day," Mara said, and wrote something on her clipboard, and walked back along the causeway without waiting, certain as a survey line, and I followed her because for the first time in twenty years the direction I was already facing and the direction I wanted to go were the same.
