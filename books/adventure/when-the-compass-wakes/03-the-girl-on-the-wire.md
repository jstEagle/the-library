## 3. The Girl on the Wire

Isla Quill had been listening to the company's secrets for three years, and in all that time she had learned one rule above every other: the wires never lied, but they never told the whole truth either.

She sat at the relay desk of the Meridian Central Office with her back straight and her hands loose, the way Mr. Tetch liked his operators to sit — attentive, obedient, invisible. The evening traffic clicked through her fingers like water through a sluice: freight manifests, pay orders, a complaint about a stalled grader on the Kestrel line. Ordinary wire. Safe wire. She transcribed it without reading it twice.

Then, at nine minutes past eight, the eastern circuit opened unbidden.

That was the first wrong thing. The eastern circuit was Vell's private line — director's traffic only, sealed with a cipher that ordinary operators weren't supposed to be able to read. Isla wasn't supposed to be able to read it. But she had spent two years copying fragments into the margins of her prayer book, matching them against public dispatches until the cipher gave way the way old mortar gives way, grain by grain, and now she read it as easily as her own name.

The key chattered. She wrote it down because her hands wrote things down the way other people breathed, and then she looked at what she'd written, and the room went cold around her.

*PRIORITY — DIRECTOR'S EYES ONLY. SUBJECT: HOLLIS, J. (DECD.). EFFECTS UNRECOVERED AT SCENE. BELIEVED CARRIED NORTHWARD BY UNKNOWN PARTY. REWARD POSTED — FIVE HUNDRED DOLLARS. DETAIN AND SEARCH ALL RAIL EMPLOYEES OF THE NIGHT MAINTENANCE DIVISION. CORLISS EN ROUTE.*

Below it, a second message, sent four minutes later:

*IF MAP RECOVERED INTACT, BURN OPERATOR. NO WITNESSES ON THIS TRAFFIC.*

Isla read *burn operator* twice. It did not mean fire, necessarily. In company language it rarely meant anything so clean.

She sat very still. The lamp above the desk hummed. Somewhere down the hall, Mr. Tetch was arguing with a clerk about overtime, his voice rising and falling like weather. The night switchboard blinked its patient green eyes at her, and the whole vast nervous system of the Meridian Rail Company lay open under her fingertips, carrying its orders through the dark, and for the first time in three years she understood exactly what kind of body she had been part of.

Hollis. She knew the name. Every operator on the eastern circuit knew it by now — the prospector who'd gone up the Sable headwaters with company papers and come down dying, or hadn't come down at all, depending on which rumor you bought. The reward notice had crossed her desk an hour ago, addressed to every station master in the district. Five hundred dollars. That was more than she made in a year.

And someone had carried Hollis's effects northward. Someone on the night maintenance division.

There was only one man on the night maintenance division who worked the closed lines alone.

* * *

She knew Wren Fairweather the way you know a figure in a photograph — by reputation and by wire. Debt ledger flagged red three times in two years. Father dead, mother gone, no kin on record. A signal engineer good enough that the company kept him despite the debt, because good signal men were scarce and desperate men were reliable. Desperate men didn't run.

Except now he had something worth running with, and Corliss was en route, and Corliss did not detain and search. Corliss concluded matters.

Isla finished her shift at eleven. She walked out through the switching yard the long way, past the coal chutes where the night wind came off the mountains smelling of iron and snowmelt, and she let herself think about what she was doing, because she had promised herself she would think it through honestly or not do it at all.

Here was the honest version: she wanted out. She had wanted out since the day she decoded a requisition order for dynamite marked *SABLE GORGE — RESIDENCE CLEARANCE*, and understood that the towns going under the reservoirs were not accidents of engineering but line items. She had stayed anyway, because staying was safe and safe was a habit, and because a girl alone with no trade but the wire had nowhere to go that the wire didn't reach. The Meridian owned the rails, the river rights, the telegraph concessions, half the land offices, and — though it kept this off its letterhead — most of the law between here and the coast.

But she could read their ciphers. And they had just put a dead man's name and a live man's murder in front of her, and asked her, implicitly, to be the kind of person who did nothing about it.

Mr. Tetch would say it wasn't her affair. Mr. Tetch said everything wasn't her affair; it was how he'd survived thirty years inside the machine. Maybe he was right. Maybe the smart move was to finish her shift tomorrow, collect her pay, and let the night maintenance man take his chances with five hundred dollars' worth of trouble.

But she had watched the wires carry the drowning of three towns in flat, orderly prose — *RESERVOIR LEVELS NOMINAL, DISPLACEMENT PROCEEDING ON SCHEDULE* — and she had typed those words herself, once, with her own quick clever hands, and there is a kind of knowledge that sits in you like swallowed glass. You can pretend it isn't there. You cannot digest it.

Wren Fairweather had the map. Corliss was coming. And Isla Quill, operator second class, was done being the company's nervous system.

She turned toward the boarding houses on Lamp Street, walking fast, her coat collar up against the wind.

* * *

Mrs. Halloway's boarding house stood at the end of Lamp Street with its porch light burning behind fogged glass, one among forty identical houses the company had built for its workers and rented back to them at rates calculated to keep them grateful for the privilege. Isla had checked the tenant rolls weeks ago — checking things was her disease — and knew Fairweather took the corner room upstairs, the one facing the yard.

She did not knock on the front door. Front doors got remembered. Instead she went around the side, past the woodpile, to the kitchen entrance, and knocked the pattern every yard worker knew: two, pause, three. Shift change.

A woman answered — Mrs. Halloway herself, small and gray and sharp-eyed, a candle in one hand. "Kitchen's closed."

"I'm looking after a message for your boarder. Fairweather. Night maintenance." Isla held up the envelope she'd prepared, blank but official-looking, company stock paper she'd liberated from the office. "It's marked urgent. I'm not supposed to leave it with anyone but him."

Mrs. Halloway looked at the envelope, then at Isla, and Isla watched her decide — the way people in company towns always decided — that whatever this was, asking questions was more dangerous than not. "Corner room. Top of the stairs. He'll likely be awake; he's always awake." She stepped aside. "You want a candle?"

"I've got my eyes," Isla said, and climbed the stairs.

The hallway smelled of lamp oil and boiled cabbage. Under the last door, a thin line of light lay across the floorboards, and beneath the light, shadow: someone standing, not sitting. Waiting.

She raised her hand to knock, and the door opened first.

He filled the frame — tall, work-shouldered, dark-haired, with the hollowed look of a man who had been expecting trouble and rehearsed for it all evening. His eyes went from her face to the envelope to her hands, cataloguing, and Isla realized with a small cold shock that he was deciding whether she was a threat, and that he was probably right to.

"Mr. Fairweather," she said quietly. "We should talk somewhere that isn't a doorway."

"Who sent you?"

"The eastern circuit," she said. "Nine minutes past eight tonight. Director's cipher." She watched the words land. He didn't understand them yet — she could see that — but he understood *director*, and he understood *cipher*, and his hand tightened on the door edge until the wood creaked. "May I come in? There's a name in that message I don't think you want shouted down a stairwell."

A long moment. Then he stepped back, and she slipped past him into the room.

It was bare the way rooms are bare when a man expects to leave them: bed made tight, kit bag by the wall, a single chair, a cold stove. On the table, a bundle wrapped in oilskin, and beside it a folded map already half-unrolled, and Isla felt her pulse jump, because there it was — the thing five hundred dollars and a man named Corliss were hunting — sitting out in lamplight like it was harmless.

Fairweather saw her look. He moved the oilskin over the map, deliberate, a question in it.

"My name's Isla Quill," she said. "I work the evening relay at Central. Three years. And tonight I read a message that says the company knows a dead prospector's effects left the mountain with an unknown party, and they've posted five hundred dollars, and they're searching every man on the night maintenance division." She paused. "They also know you, specifically. Your name was in the follow-up. Fairweather, W., night signals. Corliss is coming for you, and Corliss doesn't arrest people, Mr. Fairweather. Corliss resolves them."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from the window. Neither of them moved.

"You read their private cipher," he said at last. His voice was low, roughened, the voice of a man who hadn't spoken since morning. "How?"

"Two years of copying scraps and doing sums nobody assigned me." She shrugged. "The company thinks its operators are typewriters with ears. They forget typewriters can count." She nodded at the covered map. "Hollis. Jacob Hollis. He was Vell's own surveyor once — that was in the file traffic last month, before anyone knew he was missing. Whatever he found up there, the director wants it buried so deep even the memory of it rots. And you're holding it."

"I'm holding a promise," Fairweather said. "A dying man made me—" He stopped. Recalculated. She watched him decide how much to give a stranger, and watched him lose the argument with himself, because there was no one else. "He made me carry it upstream. To the source of the Sable. I don't even know what's there. I know what it costs me to stay — the collectors have me to the last cent, and now this. And I know what it costs me to go, if what you say is true."

"It's true." She said it flatly, and let him hear that she didn't soften things. "So here's my offer, and I'll make it once. You can't get out of this city on the main lines — they're sealed at both yards, and by morning every station master between here and Kestrel Bend will have your description. But you're a signal engineer, and there's the old Ashford spur. Decommissioned eleven years. It runs north along the river bluffs for sixty miles before it washes out, and it has one working handcar shed at mile twelve, and the patrol schedule for it crosses my desk every Tuesday." She held his eyes. "I can read the company's mail, forge its transit papers, and tell you where its eyes are blind. What I need is passage. Out. Anywhere the wire doesn't reach. You're going upstream anyway."

Silence. The house ticked and settled around them. Downstairs, faintly, Mrs. Halloway's candle scraped into its holder.

"Why?" Fairweather said finally. "Five hundred dollars is a year of your wages. You could walk downstairs right now, send one message, and buy yourself a new life. Why warn me instead?"

It was a fair question, and he asked it without accusation — the question of a man who had been sold to often enough to check the price tag. Isla thought about the dynamite requisition. About *DISPLACEMENT PROCEEDING ON SCHEDULE*. About three towns with church bells still registered on the county rolls, sleeping under sixty feet of company water.

"Because I've been the wire," she said. "Three years of carrying their orders, telling myself the words weren't mine, that I just moved them, that moving a thing isn't the same as meaning it." Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "Tonight the wire carried an order to burn whoever read it. That's me, Mr. Fairweather. That's the thanks the machine gives its own hands. So no — I'm not selling you. I'm getting off the line while I still have legs."

Something shifted in his face. Not trust — trust was too far a currency for one conversation — but recognition. The look of a man hearing his own arithmetic done by someone else and finding the sum matched.

"They'll know it was you," he said. "When I vanish and you vanish, they'll pull the traffic logs. They'll find your shift, your key rhythm. Operators have fingerprints, same as anyone. I've heard Tetch brag that he can identify every man on his floor by their sending."

"He can," Isla agreed. "Which is why, as of tonight, I stop sending like myself." She allowed herself the smallest smile. "I've been practicing someone else's fist for a year. A slow, sloppy operator named Brennan who works the Granger substation and drinks on duty. If the logs get pulled, Brennan had a very busy evening."

Fairweather stared at her. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a brake releasing, as if the mechanism of it hadn't been oiled in years. "You've been impersonating a drunk telegraphist in anticipation of fleeing the territory."

"Impersonating a drunk telegraphist in anticipation of *opportunity*," she corrected. "There's a difference. Opportunity requires nerve."

"And nerve requires a reason." He looked down at the oilskin bundle, and when he spoke again the rustiness was gone, and what remained was quieter and heavier. "A dying man bled out on my tracks tonight and made me promise. I told him yes because it was easier than watching him die unanswered. I don't know what's under those mountains. I don't know why Vell fenced off sixty miles of river. But I know the company paid collectors to hound me for a debt I can't clear, and I know it posted blood money on a dead man's belongings, and I know it's sending Corliss." He met her eyes. "That's reason enough to find out the rest."

"Then we have an arrangement," Isla said.

"We have a beginning of one." He extended his hand across the table, calloused palm, engineer's grip. "I don't trust easy, Miss Quill. Ask anyone. Ask my creditors."

"Good," she said, shaking it. "Neither do I. We'll distrust each other thoroughly. It's the foundation of every lasting partnership."

Outside, far off across the yards, a locomotive whistled the midnight section — long, long, short — and beneath it, almost lost, came another sound that made them both go still: the rapid clop of hooves on Lamp Street, several horses, moving fast, then slowing.

Fairweather reached the window in two strides and put his eye to the gap in the curtain. Isla joined him, close enough to feel the tension running through him like current through copper.

Four riders had drawn up outside Mrs. Halloway's. Lantern light swung across the porch. At their head sat a broad man in a gray company coat, unhurried, the way a man is unhurried when he owns the street he's standing on, and even at this distance Isla knew the silhouette from the office gossip — the enforcer the clerks wouldn't name above a whisper.

Corliss. A day early. The wire had lied about his schedule, or he had.

"He's early," she breathed.

"He's checking the boarding houses," Fairweather murmured. "Door to door. Starting at the end of the street." His jaw worked. "The back stairs. Kitchen. Mrs. Halloway—"

"—will swear you were asleep and I was never here, because she decided an hour ago that not knowing is safer than knowing," Isla whispered. "But we can't be here when they climb those stairs. The coal chute behind the woodshed opens onto the alley. I walked past it coming in."

For a heartbeat neither moved. Then Fairweather swept the map and oilskin into his coat, snuffed the lamp with his fingers, and in the sudden dark Isla heard him exhale — not fear exactly, but the sound a man makes stepping off a ledge he's already committed to.

"Mile twelve," he said softly. "Tuesday's patrol schedule. Tell me everything you know about that spur, Miss Quill, because starting now, we're on it."

Down below, boots hit Mrs. Halloway's porch, and a fist began to pound the front door.
