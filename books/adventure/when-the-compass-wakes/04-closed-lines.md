## 4. Closed Lines

The handcar sat where the company had forgotten it, which was to say exactly where Wren had left it three years ago.

It stood at the mouth of the old Kestrel Spur, behind a fence of woven wire that had rusted down to lace, under a tarp that had gone gray as a dead man's skin. The spur itself ran west out of the Meridian yards like a vein nobody bled anymore — two ribbons of steel vanishing into weeds and coal smoke haze, climbing toward the hills where the Sable River came down out of country no map in the city still bothered to draw honestly.

Wren crouched at the fence line with his collar up and his heart going like a piston with a cracked wrist pin. Beside him, Isla Voss crouched too, small and coiled, her telegrapher's bag strapped tight across her back, watching the yard with eyes that had spent years reading dots and dashes and had learned, apparently, to read men the same way.

"Two watchmen," she murmured. "One by the coaling tower, one walking the north fence. The walker turns at the water crane every eleven minutes. I timed him twice last night."

"You timed him."

"I time everything." She said it without pride, the way another person might say *I breathe*. "Eleven minutes is enough if you're quick."

Wren looked at the handcar — the teeter-totter bar, the little iron wheels furred with rust, the tool locker he'd padlocked himself back when he'd still believed the company might promote him instead of burying him. He'd maintained that car through the long months when the Meridian Rail Company still pretended the western lines mattered. He knew its weight to the pound. He knew that the left pump handle had been replaced with stock from a different batch and sat a quarter-inch proud of the right, so that pumping it felt like rowing a boat with one longer oar. He knew the sound it made rolling over a fishplate joint: *chunk-chunk*, like a heartbeat with a stutter.

He knew machines. It was the only thing he'd ever known well enough to be worth anything, and tonight it was going to buy him his life.

"Eleven minutes," he said. "Then we go."

* * *

They'd made the plan in his boarding house room at three in the morning, whispering over the oilskin map spread across the bed like a patient on a table, while downstairs Mrs. Halloway's clock ticked and the house snored around them.

Isla had laid it out with the economy of someone who'd once routed messages for an entire division office. The main line west was watched — of course it was; Vell's collectors knew Wren Fairweather had something worth killing for, and they'd be sitting on every ticket window and freight platform between Meridian and the mountains. But the Kestrel Spur was decommissioned. Struck from the schedules. The company kept a token patrol on the first four miles because the spur passed the reservoir works, but past the fourth milepost the rails went unwatched for thirty miles of empty country, all the way up to where the line simply stopped, sawn off and abandoned, at the edge of the gorge country.

"They closed it six years ago," Isla said. Her finger traced the faded ink on the map, the ghost-line of the spur drawn in a draftsman's hand that Wren recognized with a jolt he hadn't yet told her about. "Officially because of rockslides. Unofficially—" she tapped the blank space past the end of the line, the space where the headwaters should have been named and wasn't, "—because of whatever this is."

"My father helped close it," Wren said.

The words came out before he'd decided to say them. Isla looked up. In the lamplight her face was all angles and patience.

"He was a bridge engineer for Meridian. Twenty-two years. Fences, gates, culverts — he built the things that kept people out." Wren folded the map along its creases, careful, careful. "I used to think that was honorable work. Building the boundaries. Keeping the wild places safe from being picked apart." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Now I'm about to use everything he taught me to climb over them."

"Good," Isla said.

Just that. No comfort in it, no softness. Just *good*, said like a verdict, and somehow it was the only thing anyone could have said to him that night that would have let him sleep.

* * *

The walker reached the water crane. Turned. Began his slow trundle back toward the coaling tower, lantern swinging, boots crunching cinder.

"Now."

They went through the fence where the rust had eaten a gap wide enough for a body turned sideways, Wren first, then Isla, then across forty feet of open ballast that felt like forty miles. Every stone under his boots seemed to announce itself. Somewhere a locomotive sighed in the yards, and a coupler clanged, and Wren used the noise to cover the rattle of the padlock key.

The lock opened. The tarp came off in a sheet of gray rot. And there she was — the old handcar, *Kestrel 3* stenciled on her frame in paint gone chalky, her handles waiting like the arms of something that had been sleeping and knew it.

"In," Wren breathed. "Front side. When we clear the yard buildings, pump like the devil's holding your coat."

Isla swung aboard. She was lighter than she looked, or maybe the car just remembered Wren's weight and was glad of the change. He threw his shoulder against the bar, rocked it once to break the brake shoe free of the rail — a trick his father had taught him, *never fight a stuck machine, persuade it* — and the wheels rolled.

The first hundred yards were agony. Every joint in the track spoke under them, every fishplate rang its small iron bell, and Wren pumped with short, maddening strokes, keeping the speed down until the yard buildings slid past and the dark opened up ahead of them like a door.

Then he said, "All of it now," and gave the bar everything he had.

The handcar leapt. Isla matched him stroke for stroke — she was stronger than her wrists suggested, telegraphers' arms, all tendon — and the old spur unspooled beneath them, mile-eating, singing. The city fell away behind: the gaslight glow, the smoke columns, the great black hulk of the roundhouse where Wren had sold twelve years of his life for wages that never caught up with his debts. A wind came off the river smelling of willows and coming rain, and it hit his face and he heard himself laugh — actually laugh, a raw, ridiculous sound torn out of him — and Isla laughed too, high and startled, as if the sound had escaped her without permission.

They were doing it. They were actually doing it.

The rails curved north along the river bluff, and the last light of Meridian sank behind them, and Wren Fairweather — bankrupt, hunted, owner of nothing in the world but a dead man's map and a stolen handcar — flew west into the dark at a full fifteen miles an hour, and it was the finest thing that had ever happened to him.

* * *

The fourth milepost came and went, and with it the last of the company's patrol territory. Wren slowed them to a walk-pace pump and finally a coast, letting the car breathe, letting their arms stop burning. The country here was different already — the yards' coal grime giving way to sage and pine, the air cleaner, colder, carrying the mineral smell of high water somewhere upstream.

"We'll make the junction at Hollow Creek by dawn if we keep a steady eight," Wren said. "There's a maintenance shed there. Dry, locked, and off every schedule the company runs. We can sleep till noon and nobody alive will know we passed through."

Isla sat sideways on the frame, legs dangling, face tipped up to a sky just beginning to show its first hard stars. "You know these lines like other men know their own houses."

"I surveyed half of them." He said it flatly, and then, because the night was big and honest-feeling, he added the rest. "With my father. Summers, when I was fourteen, fifteen. He'd take me along on the inspection runs. Teach me grades and curves, how to read ballast for drainage problems, how to listen to a rail." He put his palm flat against the steel beside him. Warm still, faintly, from friction. "A rail talks, he used to say. You just have to shut up long enough to hear what it's saying."

"And then the company closed them all."

"And then the company closed them all." Wren watched the rails run together into the dark ahead, two threads stitching the wilderness to a pattern only he could see. "He never told me why. Not really. He'd come home from the western surveys looking like a man who'd seen something in a well, and Mother would ask, and he'd say *rockslides*, and go quiet for a week." His jaw tightened. "I thought he was tired. I thought it was just the work wearing on him. Now I think—"

He didn't finish. Ahead, out of the darkness, the rail spoke.

Not the ordinary speech of joints and grade changes. Something else. A wrongness in the rhythm, a hitch in the song, and Wren's whole body understood it before his mind did, the way a sailor understands weather in his teeth.

"Get down!" He grabbed Isla's arm and hauled, and they both dropped flat on the frame as the handcar screamed over the broken place—

It wasn't a broken place. That was the horror of it, the thing that turned his blood to ice water even as the wheels cleared it and the car swayed on, untouched. It was a *cut*. A clean gap in the rail, both rails, four inches wide, the severed ends bright as silver coins in the starlight. Freshly cut. Hours old at most.

Wren brought the car to a halt fifty yards on, hands shaking so badly he had to lean on the bar to stop them.

"That wasn't sabotage," Isla said behind him. Her voice was very controlled, which told him more than screaming would have. "Sabotage tears. That was cut. Deliberate. Measured."

"To stop a train." Wren walked back along the ties with a lantern hooded to a slit, and knelt at the gap, and touched the bright metal. Still sharp-edged. Still cold at the core. "Or to stop whoever followed them up this line." He looked at the cut ends, then down the dark track ahead, and the exhilaration of the escape curdled in his chest and turned to something colder and more awake.

Because a cut rail meant tools. Tools meant time. Time meant somebody had stood on this exact stretch of empty, decommissioned, forgotten track within the last day, calmly sawing through steel, and they had done it facing east — facing *back* down the line, toward the city.

Toward anyone coming after them.

"Somebody else knows about the source," he said quietly.

Isla came to stand beside him. She didn't touch the rail. She was looking west, up the line, into the black throat of the hills, and when she spoke her voice had gone flat and fast, the voice of a woman reading an urgent dispatch.

"The prospector didn't make that map alone. And Vell isn't the only one who wants what's at the end of this line." She turned to Wren, and the starlight showed him her expression — not fear, exactly. Recalculation. "We're not ahead of everyone, Wren. We're behind someone. And they've got a head start."

Wren stood a long moment in the middle of the ruined track, listening. Wind in the pines. Water far off, talking to itself in the dark. And underneath it all, so faint he might have imagined it — though he didn't think he did, and he would swear to the end of his life that he didn't — the sound of an engine, far up the valley, working hard against the grade.

Someone was climbing toward the headwaters tonight. And the rails ahead of Wren and Isla ended in a four-inch gap and thirty miles of empty country beyond it.

"We walk," he said.
