## 5. Marn of the Shallows

The rails ended the way a sentence ends — abruptly, with no promise of another.

Wren stood at the lip of the cut and looked at where the Meridian Rail Company's ambition had simply stopped. The grade ran out of the hills and flattened into a shelf of gravel above the Sable River, ties laid but never spiked, iron rails stacked in rusting pyramids like felled trees. Beyond them, the river widened into a brown-green reach dotted with gravel bars, and the mountains rose on either side, closing the valley to anything that moved on wheels.

"End of track," Isla said flatly. She stood beside him with her carpetbag clutched to her chest and her braid coming loose from a day of ducking patrols. "Sixty miles of water between here and the Gorge of Bells, if my geography holds."

"Your geography holds." Wren crouched and pressed his palm to the nearest rail. Cold. Untouched by morning traffic. Nobody had rolled this line in months, maybe a year. He'd known it would be like this — the map showed the spur dying here, swallowed back by the country it had been cut through — but knowing a thing and standing in front of it were different animals.

They had come forty miles on a handcar through the night, pumping in shifts, hiding in a culvert when the patrol lanterns swept past at Grayling Junction. Wren's arms still trembled with the effort. His shoulders burned. And now there was nothing left to pump.

"So we walk," he said.

"Along the river? With every company man between here and the headwaters looking for two people who fled the city on stolen railway equipment?" Isla set her bag down with more force than necessary. "We might as well wear signs."

"We didn't steal it. I signed it out under maintenance authorization."

"You forged maintenance authorization."

"I *adapted* existing documentation." He heard how it sounded and gave up. "Fine. We walk carefully."

Isla looked at him for a long moment, and then she laughed — a short, exhausted bark with no humor in it, the laugh of someone who had been awake too long and trusted too little and had decided, against all evidence, to keep going anyway. "You're the worst criminal I've ever thrown my lot in with, Fairweather."

"I'm not a criminal."

"You're a debt-ridden signalman carrying a dead man's map into forbidden territory while the most powerful company in the territory hunts you. That's a criminal with extra steps."

He couldn't argue with that either. He picked up his tool roll and slung it over his shoulder, and they started down the embankment toward the water, because the water was the only road left.

* * *

The settlement at the end of track wasn't on any map Wren had ever seen, which meant it was exactly the kind of place the Meridian Rail Company preferred not to acknowledge. A dozen structures leaned along the bank where the rails died: tar-paper shanties, a boat shed, a drying rack hung with fish that glittered like coins in the late light. A saloon built from an old boxcar sat on blocks, its name painted freehand across the side — THE LAST TIE — in letters that had faded to the color of old blood.

The people watched them come. That was the first thing Wren noticed. Not with curiosity — with assessment. A woman mending a net didn't look up from her work, but her hands slowed. Two men on a porch tracked them with the stillness of herons. This was a place that had learned what strangers cost.

"We need a boat," Wren said quietly.

"We need a pilot," Isla corrected. "A boat we could maybe steal. A pilot who knows this water is worth more than everything in my bag."

"And what's in your bag?"

"A telegraph key, a change of clothes, and a forged letter of transit that got us past one checkpoint and won't survive a second." She lifted her chin toward the boxcar saloon. "Pilots drink. Let's go find one."

Inside, the Last Tie smelled of lamp oil, river mud, and cheap whiskey. A tin stove radiated heat against the damp, and half a dozen patrons turned as one when the door swung shut behind them. Wren felt their eyes move over his engineer's coat, over Isla's city boots, cataloguing everything wrong about them.

The barkeep was a heavy woman with forearms like dock rope. She didn't ask what they were having; she poured two whiskeys, set them down, and said, "Passing through or staying?"

"Passing through," Wren said.

"Upstream?"

He hesitated a half-second too long, and the barkeep's eyes narrowed. Around the room, the stillness sharpened. Isla stepped smoothly into the gap.

"Upstream," she said, "as far as the Gorge of Bells. We're told there's still a pilot on this stretch who'll run it."

"There was." The barkeep wiped the counter with a rag that had given up long ago. "There was a pilot could read this river blindfolded. Best on the whole Sable. Company took her license."

"*Her*?" Isla asked.

"Marn Oduya." One of the porch men had followed them in and now sat hunched at the end of the bar, close enough to hear. He was old, weathered down to sinew and suspicion, with a boatman's knuckles scarred white. "Barred from the trade three years back. Refused a contract."

"What contract?" Wren asked.

The old man looked at him with something like pity. "Vell wanted cargo moved at night. Crates that didn't get logged. Past the toll stations without inspection." He turned his glass slowly on the counter. "She said no. Said she'd carry anything on this river except whatever Vell needed hidden. So they took her papers. Barred her from every licensed berth on the Sable. Now she runs gravel and fish when the mood takes her, and drinks here when it doesn't."

"Where do we find her?" Isla said.

The old man studied them both, and Wren watched him weigh something — whether these two were trouble worth having or trouble worth turning away. Finally he jerked his chin toward the door. "Boat shed. Where else would a barred pilot be? Only place left where the river can't hear the company telling her no."

* * *

The boat shed stood on pilings at the river's edge, its doors open to the evening, lamplight spilling gold onto the black water. Wren heard the work before he saw it: the ring of a hammer on wood, steady as a heartbeat.

Marn Oduya was caulking the seam of a flat-bottomed skiff, driving oakum into the planking with a mallet and an iron, and she did not stop when they appeared in the doorway. She was perhaps forty, broad-shouldered and dark-skinned, with gray threading tight black curls cropped close to her skull. Her hands moved with the certainty of someone who had done ten thousand things ten thousand times. She finished the seam, drove the last strand home, and only then straightened and looked at them.

"No," she said.

"We haven't asked you anything yet," Isla said.

"You want passage upstream. To the Gorge of Bells, if I had to guess, because that's where everyone who doesn't belong on this river wants to go, and none of them know why." Marn wiped her palms on her trousers. "Answer's no."

"The company took your license," Wren said. "For refusing to smuggle."

Something flickered behind her eyes — quick, quickly buried. "You've been talking to Hollis. Hollis talks to everybody." She picked up her mallet again. "My license is my business. Good evening."

"They'll take more than your license," Wren said. "That's how it works. First they take the papers. Then the berth. Then the customers. Then the boat. It never stops at the first thing."

The mallet stopped mid-swing.

Marn turned fully around, and now she looked at him properly, the way she'd probably look at a snag in the channel — reading the shape of it, judging how deep it went. "And what would a railroad man know about how the company takes things?"

"My name's Wren Fairweather. I work — worked — signals and closed lines for Meridian Rail. I owe them more money than I'll earn in six years. They own my house, my wages, and very nearly my name." He held her gaze. "So I know exactly how it works. I'm living inside the machine they use to take things."

"And her?"

"Isla Voss. Telegraph operator. She read orders she wasn't supposed to read, and now the company wants her the way they want me." He reached into his coat — slowly, letting her see — and drew out the oilskin packet. He didn't open it. Just let it sit in his hand, visible. "This is why we need the Gorge of Bells. And this is why Corliss will be behind us before the week is out."

Marn went very still.

"Corliss," she repeated.

"You know the name."

"Everybody on this river knows Corliss." She set the mallet down with exaggerated care, the way a person does when the alternative is throwing it. "Corliss burned the Kestrel Bend fish camps two summers ago looking for a runaway. Corliss drowned a man in the shallows at Miller's Eddy for running toll-free cargo. Corliss doesn't chase people, railroad man. Corliss *erases* them." She stepped closer, and up close Wren could see the fatigue carved deep under her eyes, the kind that sleep doesn't touch. "So I'll ask once, plainly. What's in the packet that puts Corliss on the water?"

Wren glanced at Isla. She gave the smallest nod.

"It's a map," he said. "To the source of the Sable. The real source. Behind the fences, past the closed lines, under the mountain. A prospector carried it out of there and died putting it in my hands, and before he died he made me promise to carry it upstream." He paused. "There's something under those mountains that Ambrose Vell has spent years and a great deal of blood making sure nobody finds. And I think it's bigger than gold."

The lamp hissed. Out on the river, a loon called once, long and mournful, and the sound seemed to fill the whole valley.

Marn looked at the oilskin packet for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — lower, rougher, like water over stone.

"My brother went up there," she said. "Four years ago. Teo. He was curious the way you are — the way that gets people killed. He kept writing to me about the fences. About the towns the dams drowned. His letters stopped coming the same month the company posted the reward for a missing surveyor." Her jaw tightened. "I have spent four years not knowing. And every day of those four years, I have known exactly whose fault the not-knowing was."

"Vell's," Isla said quietly.

"Vell's," Marn agreed. "But also mine. Because I stayed downstream. Because I ran safe cargo and told myself the river above the gorge wasn't my river." She picked up the mallet again, turned it over in her hands, and set it down for good. "Here's what I'll give you, Fairweather. I'll take you upstream as far as the Gorge of Bells. That's it. The gorge itself has killed better pilots than me, and I don't run it, and I don't pretend to. Past the gorge, you're on your own."

"That's all we're asking," Wren said.

"It isn't all you're asking." Her eyes were sharp as flint. "You're asking me to point my boat at the place my brother disappeared, with Corliss somewhere behind us, for two strangers I met an hour ago. Don't tell me what you're asking. I already know." She shouldered past them both to the doorway and looked out at the darkening river, at the far shore dissolving into blue shadow, at the mountains upstream that even now held whatever secret the map described.

"Why?" Wren asked. "Why say yes?"

Marn was quiet for a moment. When she answered, she didn't turn around.

"Because for four years I've been waiting for somebody to come down that bank heading upstream instead of running from it," she said. "And because if Teo's up there — if anything of him is up there — I'd rather find it moving than waiting." She looked back at them, and something in her face had settled, some long argument finally concluded. "Be at the shed an hour before dawn. Bring nothing you can't swim with. And eat something, both of you — the river takes its fee out of anybody who boards it hungry."

She went back to her skiff and her caulking, and the hammer began again, steady as a heartbeat, and Wren understood that the conversation was over.

* * *

They found a bench outside the Last Tie and ate smoked fish and hard bread bought with the last of Isla's coin. The settlement settled into night around them: lamps guttering, dogs muttering, the river talking to itself in the dark.

"She lost her brother up there," Isla said at last. "And she's going back. Just like that."

"Not just like that." Wren turned the oilskin packet over in his hands. "Four years isn't just like that. Four years is a person deciding every single day whether to hope or to bury it. She just stopped deciding." He looked up at the mountains, black against the stars. "I know what that costs."

"Do you?" Isla's voice was gentle, which somehow made it worse. "You've known your father built those fences for what — a week? You're carrying his sin in your pocket next to a dead man's map, and you haven't decided yet whether you're running from the company or toward whatever he was hiding."

Wren didn't answer. The fish tasted like smoke and salt and nothing else.

"One more thing," Isla said. She nodded toward the boat shed, where the lamplight still burned and the hammer had gone quiet. "Did you notice she never asked what we'd pay her?"

He had noticed. He'd been trying not to think about what it meant.

"Pilots always ask about payment," Isla went on. "It's how you tell a pilot from a drowning person grabbing a plank. She didn't ask." Isla pulled her coat tighter and looked out at the black water. "Which means whatever she thinks is up there is worth more to her than money. Which means we're not passengers, Wren. We're ballast. And if it comes to choosing between us and what she's looking for—"

"It won't," Wren said.

"You can't know that."

"No," he admitted. "But I signed on to a crew tonight, and I'm going to choose to believe in it until I'm given a reason not to. That's all any of us can do."

Isla snorted softly, but she didn't argue, and after a while her breathing slowed and her head tipped against the post beside her, and she slept sitting up, the way people sleep when they've learned not to expect beds.

Wren stayed awake. Upstream, the mountains waited, and somewhere among them a spring that fed an empire ran dark behind his father's fences, and somewhere on the water ahead a woman named Marn steered toward the place her brother vanished. Behind them, he knew — the way you know weather coming in your knees — Corliss was already moving.

An hour before dawn, the hammering from the boat shed woke him. He rose, stiff and cold, and shook Isla's shoulder.

"Time to board," he said. "The river's taking us upstream."
