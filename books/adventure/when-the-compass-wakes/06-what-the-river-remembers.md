## 6. What the River Remembers

Marn had piloted the Sable for nineteen years, and she knew its moods the way a widow knows a photograph — every crease, every faded corner, every place where time had torn something away.

The river was low and sullen this morning, running brown-green through the willow flats below the rail terminus, and it carried the smell of turned earth and old iron. She poled the flatboat out from the reeds before full light, while Wren Fairweather and his telegraph girl were still stumbling down the bank with their bundles, and she watched them come with the particular patience of a woman who had already decided to take them but wanted them to sweat a little first.

"You said as far as the Gorge of Bells," Wren called, hopping the last few feet onto the gunwale. The boat rocked. He grabbed the mast like a man grabbing a stranger's coat.

"I said I'd take you upriver," Marn corrected. "Whether we get there depends on whether you learn to sit down when I tell you to."

Isla came aboard more carefully, her carpetbag clutched to her chest, her eyes already cataloguing everything — the patched hull, the coiled lines, the long iron pole Marn leaned on like a lance. Marn had seen that look before, on company inspectors and revenue men. But where they catalogued to take, the girl catalogued to understand. That was different. That might even be useful.

"Push us off," Marn said.

Wren shoved against the bank with his boot, and the current took them, and the Sable began to speak in its low constant voice beneath the hull.

* * *

By midmorning the flats gave way to bluffs, and the bluffs to wooded hills, and the channel narrowed until the water ran quick and glassy over drowned meadows. Marn worked the pole in silence, reading the bottom by feel, letting the two of them settle into the rhythm of the boat. It was better that way. People talked too soon, and then they lied.

It was Isla who broke first, naturally. She'd been watching the shoreline with increasing confusion for an hour, and finally she unfolded a strip of paper — a telegraph flimsy, Marn noted, kept folded soft as cloth — and held it out.

"There's supposed to be a town here," she said. "Kestrel's Landing. My route book lists a relay office at Kestrel's Landing. There's nothing. Not even a foundation."

Marn didn't stop poling. "There's a foundation," she said. "You're looking at it."

Wren frowned. "What do you mean?"

"Look down."

They looked. The water was clear enough here, over the old meadow ground, and the morning sun lay flat on it, and beneath the surface — pale as bones under linen — were the shapes of streets. A grid of them, faintly greened with weed, running off toward deeper water. Here and there a fence post stood up out of the shallows like a drowned man's finger. A church bell lay half-buried in silt, its mouth open, ringing nothing.

Isla went very still. Wren stood up so fast the boat lurched, and Marn snapped, "Sit *down*, engineer, unless you want to swim with them."

"What is this?" Wren's voice had gone strange. "What happened here?"

Marn shipped the pole and let the boat drift. Some things you couldn't say standing up.

"Twelve years ago there were three towns on this stretch of the Sable," she said. "Kestrel's Landing. Harrow Bend. And Bellhaven, up past the gorge. Good towns. Mill towns, farm towns, a landing town. Four thousand people, give or take." She looked at the drowned street grid sliding past beneath them. "Then Meridian Rail bought up the headwaters, and the director — Vell — decided the river flowed wrong. Too much water reaching the valley in spring, not enough in August. He wanted it steady. Controllable. A river you could put on a ledger."

"So they built dams," Wren said slowly.

"They built dams," Marn agreed. "And to build the reservoirs, they flooded the valley. Condemned the towns. Paid people a fraction of what their land was worth, and when folks wouldn't sell, they condemned them anyway and moved them out at gunpoint. Then they closed the gates and let the water come up." Her voice stayed level. Nineteen years of practice, keeping it level. "Some of those people are still down there. The ones who wouldn't leave. Old man Kestrel chained himself to his own porch."

Isla's hand had gone to her mouth. Wren was staring at the water as if he could see all the way down through twelve years to the porches and the parlors and the bell.

"The company calls it the Reservoir Improvement Act," Marn went on. "Filed documents, proper seals, everything legal as a hymn. They erased the towns from the maps afterward. New surveys, new route books. As far as the railroad world knows, there was never anything here but empty land." She picked up the pole again. "Rivers remember, though. Rivers don't sign anything."

She pushed off from a submerged fence line and steered them up the drowned main street of Kestrel's Landing, between the ghost-rows of what had been elms, and nobody spoke for a long time.

* * *

They made camp that night on a gravel bar where the channel split, under a stand of cottonwoods gone gold at the edges. Marn built a small fire, small because fires drew eyes on this river, and put on coffee that tasted like bark. It was after dark, with the fire down to coals, that she took the oilcloth packet from inside her coat.

She hadn't planned to show them. But the engineer had spent the whole day looking at the water like a man reading his own sentence, and the girl hadn't stopped watching Marn with those quick dark eyes, and grief shared was grief halved — or doubled, depending. Marn had never learned which. She was tired of carrying it alone either way.

"My brother Teo piloted this river with me," she said. "Before. After the drownings he couldn't let it go. He'd run supplies to the families scattered upcountry, and he started asking questions. Where did the condemnation money really go. Why Bellhaven got erased twice as thorough as the others. What was up at the source worth three towns and four thousand people." She turned the packet over in her hands. "Four years ago he went up past the Gorge of Bells to find out. He sent letters for a while. Then he sent one letter. Then nothing."

"And you've been looking ever since," Isla said quietly.

"Every season. Every channel. Every name on every manifest going upriver." Marn untied the oilcloth. Inside were papers — soft with handling, some water-stained, a bundle of letters tied with wire. "These are what he left me. Company documents he copied before he went. Condemnation orders. Payment schedules. The paperwork of drowning."

She handed them across the fire to Wren, because he was the one who'd asked the sharpest questions, and because engineers read documents the way pilots read water.

He read fast, lips moving slightly, and Marn watched his face change by degrees — puzzlement, then attention, then something colder moving underneath, like a big fish turning in deep water.

"This order," he said. His voice had dropped. "The condemnation survey for Harrow Bend. Filed under the Reservoir Improvement Act, countersigned by the district engineering office." He looked up. "Where did Teo get these?"

"Copied them from a company clerk who owed him money. Why?"

Wren didn't answer right away. He turned the page toward the firelight, and Marn saw his thumb trembling slightly against the paper, and she thought: *there it is.* Whatever it was. There it was.

"Because I know this signature," he said. "I've known it my whole life. It was on the shop drawings pinned above my bed growing up. Bridge trusses, culverts, switch stands. My father signed everything twice — once for the record, once slanted, like he was in a hurry to be done with lying." He traced the ink without touching it. "Elias Fairweather. District engineer, Meridian Rail. That's my father."

The fire popped. Downstream, the Sable ran on over its drowned streets, saying nothing, saying everything.

"He countersigned the condemnations," Isla said. It wasn't a question. She'd read the page upside down; Marn was beginning to suspect the girl read everything upside down, sideways, and through.

"All three towns," Wren said. His face was gray in the coal-light. "Harrow Bend. Kestrel's Landing. Bellhaven. He stamped them approved." He laughed, one dry note with no humor anywhere in it. "I used to ask him about the fences. When I was a boy, riding the maintenance cars with him along the closed lines — there were these fences going up around the headwaters, miles of them, and I asked him why, and he said—" Wren stopped. Swallowed. "He said he was protecting the land. Keeping it safe. I believed him. God help me, I sat on his knee and believed him."

"Protecting it from whom?" Marn asked. She heard her own voice go flat and hard, the voice she used on drunks and revenue men. "From the people whose houses he helped drown?"

"I don't know." Wren set the papers down on the gravel between them, careful, the way you set down something that might go off. "I don't know what he knew or what he told himself. He's dead six years now, and I can't ask him, and maybe that's a mercy for him and a curse for me." He looked at his hands. "I inherited his debts. Turns out I inherited worse than that."

Marn should have felt satisfaction. She'd imagined this moment for years, in a hundred cold camps — some company man's kin confronted with the paper trail, forced to see. She'd imagined hating him for it.

Instead she watched him stare at the drowned towns' paperwork with his father's own hands — she could see Elias Fairweather in them now, the same broad knuckles, the same engineer's care — and what she felt was something closer to recognition. Two people standing on opposite banks of the same flood.

"Teo wrote about your father," she said.

Wren's head came up. "What?"

"In his letters. The early ones, before he went upstream." She untied the wire bundle with fingers that suddenly didn't want to work. "He'd found an older set of documents. Drafts. Objections filed with the district office — memos arguing against the flooding plans, alternate designs, proposals to route the water without drowning anyone. All rejected. All signed off against by the director's office." She found the letter she wanted, third from the top, worn soft as cloth. "Teo thought whoever wrote them was the only honest man in the district office. He never got a name. Just initials. E.F."

Wren took the letter like it might burn him. He read it once, then again, and Marn watched something move through his face that had no easy name — hope and shame tearing at each other like dogs over a bone.

"He fought it," Wren said hoarsely. "He fought it and lost, and then he signed the thing he'd fought, and then he built the fences, and then he never said a word to me. Not one word, his whole life." He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "Why wouldn't he say a word?"

"Because men like your father don't lose and talk," Marn said. "They lose and keep working for the thing that beat them. That's how companies win — not by beating the honest ones, but by making them carry the paperwork after." She took back the letter gently. "Or," she added, and the words came slower, "they wait. Twenty years, thirty. They wait for somebody downstream to pick up what they couldn't finish."

"That's a story," Wren said. "That's a story you tell yourself at night."

"It's the only one I've got left." Marn looked at him across the coals, and let him see all of it — nineteen years of the river, four years of searching, the whole weight of a missing brother. "So here's where we stand, engineer. You're carrying a dead prospector's map to the source my brother died trying to reach. Your father signed the drownings and maybe fought them too. And I'm the only pilot on this river who'll take you past the tolls. I don't need you to be innocent. I need you to be useful, and to keep your promise to that dying man, because I think his map and my brother's letters point at the same truth."

"And if they don't?" Isla asked softly.

"Then we'll know that too." Marn banked the fire with her boot. "Either way, nobody on this boat is going back downriver."

Wren was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed — steadier, stripped down, the way timber looks after the bark's off.

"My father built things that lasted," he said. "Whatever else he was. If there's something of his at that source — something he designed, something he meant to finish — I want to see it finished."

Marn nodded once. That would do. That would have to do.

She was spreading her bedroll when Isla appeared beside her in the dark, silent as her namesake element, and held out the telegraph flimsy she'd been carrying all day.

"You should see this," the girl said. "I pulled it off the wire the night before I found Wren. It's a company dispatch, priority-sealed, Vell's own office to the district enforcers." Her eyes were wide and steady in the last of the firelight. "It's not about the prospector's map at all. It says *recover surveyor's effects and silence all parties.* And it's addressed to a Corliss." She swallowed. "Marn — the dispatch is dated the day after Teo's last letter. They're not hunting the map. They're finishing a hunt they started four years ago."

Out on the black water, the Sable slid past the gravel bar, cold and patient, carrying its drowned bells toward the sea — and somewhere upstream, in the dark above the gorge, something waited that had already cost this river three towns and one brother, and was not done costing yet.
