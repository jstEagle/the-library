## 7. The Toll at Kestrel Bend

I saw the toll station before Marn did — or rather, I saw the wire.

It ran along the riverbank on crooked cedar poles, sagging between the trees like a clothesline someone had strung in the dark, and even from a quarter mile downstream I could read its rhythm in the way it swayed. A line that idle hums differently than a line carrying traffic. This one was talking. Somewhere ahead of us, a key was clicking out messages to Meridian Junction, and every one of those messages would be about us.

"Kestrel Bend," Marn said, and spat over the gunwale. "Company toll. They'll want papers."

"We don't have papers," Wren said.

"We have a boat, a debt-ridden engineer, and me." Marn eased the tiller and the flat-bottomed skiff swung toward the inside of the bend, where the current went lazy and brown-green. "That's not nothing. But it isn't papers either."

I kept my eyes on the wire. Old habit. Three years running the night board at Halloway Junction taught you to hear a line the way a farrier hears a horse's breathing — something wrong in the cadence before anything shows on the surface. The Kestrel Bend operator was sending in short bursts, then long silences. Queries. Waiting on answers. Somebody upstream was asking questions and somebody at the Bend didn't like them.

"They know we're coming," I said. "Or they suspect. There's traffic on the toll wire — short transmissions, gaps between. That's an inquiry pattern."

Marn glanced back at me with something new in her face. Not warmth, exactly. Recalculation. "You can read that from here?"

"I can read that from across a canyon if the wind's quiet." I pulled my collar up against the spray. "What I can't do is make us invisible."

"No," Wren said slowly. He'd gone quiet in the way he did when his mind started working — jaw set, eyes fixed on some middle distance where the problem assembled itself. "But maybe you can make us *legitimate*."

* * *

We put ashore two miles below the station, in a cut of willow and broken shale where the boat couldn't be seen from the water. Marn tied off under an overhang while Wren spread the map's oilskin on a flat stone, weighted at the corners, and stared at it like it might confess something.

"The inspector at these stations is always a company man, but he's never a *good* company man," Wren said. "They post the ambitious ones at junctions and the comfortable ones at tolls. Kestrel Bend will be somebody who hasn't been promoted in nine years and doesn't want trouble. Trouble means reports. Reports mean Vell reads your name."

"And Corliss reads your name after that," Marn said grimly.

"So we give him no trouble. We give him paperwork." Wren looked at me. "You said you worked telegraph. Can you write like a company clerk?"

I almost laughed. "Wren Fairweather. I spent three years forging time-sheets for a station master who drank his wages. I can write like a company clerk, a district superintendent, or God Almighty if you get me the right letterhead."

He grinned — the first real grin I'd seen on him since the handcar, since the tracks, since all of this began — and it did something unfair to my chest that I decided, firmly, to ignore.

The problem was materials. Company transit passes weren't handwritten; they were printed forms, serial-numbered, stamped in violet ink by the district office at Graywater. A forgery without the right paper was a confession. But Marn, who seemed to keep a lifetime of grudges in her boat, reached beneath the stern thwart and came up with a tin box wrapped in oilcloth.

Inside: three genuine transit forms, water-stained but real, taken off a company barge she'd piloted the year she still had a license. And a violet ink pencil, half worn, the kind inspectors used for cancellations.

"You stole these?" Wren asked.

"I *salvaged* them," Marn said, "from a company that stole a river. Let's call it even."

The forms were for freight, though — timber consignments, stamped for the Gorge route. Passenger transit needed different language, a supervisor's countersignature, and a destination code. I sat on the shale with the forms on my knee and built our fiction the way I'd been taught to build a clean transmission: short, boring, unremarkable. Boring things don't get read twice.

Wren Fairweather became F. Warder, maintenance engineer, Meridian Rail, traveling upstream on a signal-line inspection with authority from the Graywater division. Isla — I gave myself a name I hadn't used since I was twelve, my mother's name, Sera Quill, clerk attached. Marn refused a false name entirely.

"I've been Marn on this river since before Vell owned it," she said. "If they want me, they know where I am anyway."

"That's suicide," Wren said.

"It's *consistency*. Inspectors smell invention. Give them one true thing and they'll swallow the rest." She looked at me. "Make her countersignature good, girl. Graywater's division supervisor signs with a heavy downstroke and a loop on the R. I carried his mail long enough to know."

I forged Aldous Renner's signature four times on birch bark before I touched the form. On the fifth try, Marn nodded once, which from her was a parade.

The last thing was the cancellation stamp — the violet mark that proved the form had passed through a legitimate office. We had the pencil but no stamp. So I carved one. A cork float from Marn's tackle box, a nail, a knife, twenty minutes of careful work by firelight: MRD — GW — 114, block letters reversed into the cork. It wouldn't survive close inspection. But close inspection was exactly what we were betting a comfortable man wouldn't give.

I pressed the stamp to the form and held my breath as the violet bloomed.

"There," I said, and heard my own voice shake. "Three souls, one inspection party, bound for the headwaters spur. Signed, sealed, and lying."

Wren took the form and read it twice, and when he looked up at me there was something in his face I couldn't name — respect, maybe, or the beginning of it. "You're wasted on telegraphy, Sera Quill."

"Don't call me that unless they're listening," I said. But I smiled anyway.

* * *

Kestrel Bend sat where the river pinched between two shoulders of granite, and the company had built its toll station on the north bank like a fist around the throat of the water. A plank dock. A long low building of gray weatherboard with the Meridian star painted on its face, faded to a ghost. A signal mast with a chain hoist for raising the barrier boom across the channel. And beside the building, a telegraph shed with its window lit yellow against the early dusk.

One inspector. One deckhand tending the boom winch. Two rifles leaning against the wall, unattended.

"One true thing," Marn murmured as we came alongside. "Let me talk."

She talked. She brought the skiff in with the easy arrogance of a woman who'd run this bend a hundred times, hailed the dock in a voice like gravel, and complained — loudly, wonderfully — about the current above the narrows, the price of tar, the state of the company's mooring cleats. The inspector came out wiping his hands on his apron, a soft-bodied man with a waxed mustache and the eyes of someone who had stopped being curious years ago.

"Papers," he said.

Marn handed him the skiff's registration — genuine, her own name, her own disgrace written into it in some district ledger — and Wren stepped up behind her with the transit form held just wrong enough to look routine. I hung back on the dock, playing the clerk: bored, cold, hugging a canvas satchel to my chest like it contained nothing but sandwiches.

The inspector read. His lips moved. My heart counted seconds.

"F. Warder," he said. "Signal inspection?"

"Line survey," Wren said, in a voice pitched perfectly between authority and tedium. "Graywater wants the old spur relays assessed before freeze-up. You've got a crossarm down two miles south, by the way — I marked it. Your wire's hanging within a foot of the water at high stage."

That was the genius of it, and I could have kissed him. He gave the man a *task*. A small, flattering, official task that made the inspector feel like part of the machine instead of a cog being inspected himself.

"Huh," the inspector said, mollified. He turned the form toward the lantern light, squinting at my violet stamp —

And that was when the door of the telegraph shed opened, and a tall man stepped out into the lamplight, and every plan I had died quietly in my chest.

I knew him from the wanted circulars that passed through every station on the line. Not his face — the circulars never showed faces, only names and descriptions — but I knew the coat. Long, gray, military-cut, buttoned to the throat. I knew the way the deckhand straightened when he appeared, the way the inspector's mustache suddenly looked ridiculous to him, the way he stood as if the ground were furniture arranged for his convenience.

Corliss. Vell's chief enforcer. The man whose name made even station masters lower their voices.

He crossed the dock without hurry. "Papers," he said again, to the inspector, not to us. "All of them."

"Mr. Corliss — this is a routine transit, sir, fully in order—"

"*All* of them."

His voice wasn't loud. That was the worst of it. It was quiet and complete, like a lid closing. He took the transit form from the inspector's hand and read it top to bottom, and I watched his eyes stop on the violet stamp, and I watched him tilt the paper so the light caught the impression of the cork — the faint texture of a stamp that had never touched an office desk in Graywater.

My mouth went dry. Beside me, Wren had gone very still, the stillness of a man measuring the distance to the water.

"MRD-GW-114," Corliss said softly. "That's a freight-cancellation die. Retired in the spring." He looked up. His eyes found mine first — of course they did; guilt has a scent and liars sweat it. "Where did this form originate?"

"Graywater division office," Wren said. Steady. Damn him, steady. "Supervisor Renner's section."

"Renner signed this?"

"With a heavy downstroke," Wren said, "and a loop on the R."

For one endless second, Corliss simply looked at him. Then, distantly, from the telegraph shed, the sounder began to chatter — a message coming through, urgent by its spacing — and the operator leaned out the door and called, "Sir? Junction for you. Says it's priority."

Corliss held the form a moment longer. Then he set it down on the inspector's clipboard, precisely squared, and said, "Wait here," and walked toward the shed.

We waited perhaps four full seconds, which felt like four years.

Then Marn moved. No word, no signal — she simply stepped off the dock's edge into the skiff, and the sudden rock of it was all the alarm we needed. Wren shoved the inspector — gently, almost apologetically, a man tripping over a cleat — and the clipboard, form and all, went spinning into the river. I was already moving, satchel clutched, boots hammering planks, and then the world tilted and we were all three in the boat and Marn had the bow line in her teeth and the knife in her hand and the rope fell free.

"BOOM!" the inspector screamed, finding his voice at last. "DROP THE BOOM! STOP THAT BOAT!"

Above us the chain hoist rattled, the great timber barrier groaning down across the channel — but Marn had already angled for the gap on the far side, the narrow slot where the boom didn't reach, the slot she'd clearly measured with her eyes the moment we docked because of course she had. The skiff shot through with a hand's width to spare, and the current took us like a hand closing.

Behind us, Corliss emerged from the telegraph shed. He didn't shout. He didn't run. He stood on the dock in his gray coat and watched us go, and somehow that was more terrifying than gunfire — the patience of it, the arithmetic. Then he turned and spoke to the operator, and I knew, with sick certainty, that every station on the river was about to learn our names.

* * *

The rapids below Kestrel Bend were called the Washerwomen, Marn shouted over the roar, because the water beat everything white. I believed her. The river dropped through a stair of ledges, and the skiff went down each one sideways, and the world became noise and cold and the taste of the Sable in my throat.

We lost the spare oars at the second ledge. We lost the tin of hardtack and half the tar pot at the third. At the fourth, the skiff struck a submerged shelf and spun, and I went over the side into water so cold it felt like a decision rather than a temperature — and then Wren's arm hooked my collar and hauled me back over the gunwale, both of us gasping, soaked, alive.

Marn threaded the rest of it like a woman reading scripture, calling the rocks before we saw them — "left, LEFT, now ease—" — until suddenly the river widened, gentled, and the Washerwomen were behind us, foaming in the dark.

We grounded on a gravel bar a mile down, shaking too hard to speak. Marn got a fire going with hands that trembled only slightly, which I chose to find heroic. Our supplies lay scattered and ruined on the thwarts. The map, thank God, rode dry inside Wren's shirt, against his skin, where he'd put it days ago and never said a word.

"He knew," Wren said finally. He was staring into the fire, hair plastered to his forehead. "Corliss. He knew the stamp was wrong. He let us run."

"Why would he?" I said.

"Because he doesn't need to catch us at a toll station." Wren's voice was flat and tired. "He needs to catch us at the source. Everything we do from here, he lets us do — until it leads him where Vell wants to go."

I looked at Marn. Marn looked at the fire.

"Well," she said at last, feeding a stick to the flames. "Then we'd best be worth following."

And despite the cold, despite the lost supplies, despite the gray coat waiting somewhere behind us on the darkening river — I laughed. It startled all three of us. But sitting there on that gravel bar, soaked and starving and hunted, with two people I'd known less than a week standing between me and the whole weight of the Meridian Rail Company, I realized I wasn't afraid the way I'd been afraid in Halloway Junction, alone at a night board with a stolen message burning in my pocket.

Because the girl on the wire had found her crew.

Upstream, faint on the night air, I heard it — a sound I knew better than my own heartbeat. A telegraph key, chattering from some distant station, sending our description down the line.

Isla Sera Quill, I thought, whatever name I'm wearing tomorrow — you'd better be faster than the wire.

"Wren," I said. "How much farther to the Gorge of Bells?"

He glanced at the map's outline through his wet shirt, as if he could feel it. "Two days," he said. "If the river lets us."

Behind us, far off, a single rifle shot cracked across the water — not aimed at anything. Just a promise.

Marn stood up and began righting the boat.
