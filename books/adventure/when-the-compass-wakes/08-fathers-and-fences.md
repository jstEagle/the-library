## 8. Fathers and Fences

The fire was small on purpose. Marn had built it in a hollow of stones beneath an overhang of granite, where the flames would throw their light no farther than arm's reach, and she'd fed it with dead alder that burned hot and nearly smokeless. Above them the sky was a spill of cold stars, and below, somewhere in the dark, the Sable River talked to itself in a low continuous murmur, like a man muttering over a ledger he couldn't balance.

They had made eleven miles since Kestrel Bend, most of it dragging the boat through shallows after dark because Marn refused to stop while Corliss might still be behind them. Now her hands were shaking when she lifted the kettle off the fire, and Wren pretended not to notice, the way she had pretended not to notice him limping for the last three hours.

Isla sat closest to the light, her knees drawn up, the telegraph key from her satchel resting on top of them like something holy. She hadn't let go of it since the rapids. It was dented now — a new dent, acquired when she'd thrown herself flat in the bottom of the boat and taken the key's weight against her ribs rather than let it go into the water. Wren had watched her do it. He kept thinking about it. A woman who would bruise herself to keep a piece of brass.

"Tea," Marn said, and poured three tin cups without asking whether anyone wanted any. It wasn't tea, exactly. It was willow bark and something piney she'd stripped from a tree at noon, and it tasted like punishment, but it was hot, and heat counted for more than flavor out here.

Wren wrapped his hands around his cup and watched the steam climb.

For two days he had been carrying something besides the map. He'd been carrying it since Kestrel Bend, actually — since Isla had forged those papers with a steady hand while his own heart hammered so loud he was sure the inspector could hear it through the counter. Since Corliss had walked into that toll station wearing company gray like it had been cut for him, and looked at Wren with eyes that catalogued him the way a clerk catalogues freight: weight, value, destination.

But it was older than that too. Older than Kestrel Bend. It went back to a kitchen table in a house that didn't exist anymore, and a father who had smelled of creosote and iron filings, and a question Wren had asked when he was nine years old: *Pa, why does the company need fences way up there? Nobody lives up there.*

He hadn't thought of that question in twenty years. Now it wouldn't leave him alone.

"You're doing it again," Isla said.

Wren blinked. "Doing what?"

"That thing where you stare into the fire like it owes you money." She blew across her cup. "You've done it four times today. I counted."

"She counts everything," Marn said. "It's the wire in her. You spend enough years listening for clicks, you start hearing them everywhere."

"I heard *you* clicking all afternoon," Isla said. "That knee of yours is going to swell up like a melon if you don't wrap it."

"It's wrapped."

"It's *loosely* wrapped."

Marn grunted, which Wren had learned was either agreement or surrender, depending on the angle of her jaw. Right now the jaw said surrender. She shifted against the rock, stretched the bad leg out toward the fire's warmth, and for a while there was only the river and the small sounds of the night — wind moving through the pines high above, a stone ticking as it cooled, the far-off complaint of some bird neither of them could name.

It was Marn who broke the quiet, though Wren suspected later that Isla had engineered it — that the girl had seen what was sitting on him and simply waited, patient as a spider, until someone else pulled the thread.

"You've been quiet since the Bend," Marn said. "Quieter than usual, which is saying something, because you've got the natural gift of a fence post."

"Thank you."

"Don't mention it." She turned her cup slowly in her fingers. "But here's my experience of men carrying something heavy. They either set it down or they wait until it falls on somebody else. And I'd rather hear it now than have it land on me mid-rapids."

Wren looked at her. In the firelight her face was all planes and weather, the kind of face the river carved and then kept carving just to see how much it could take away before nothing was left. Her eyes were steady. Not unkind. Steady the way a dock is steady — something you can step onto in the dark and trust to hold.

Isla had gone very still. She did that when she was listening — really listening — the whole of her attention narrowing down to a point, the way it must narrow onto a wire.

Wren looked into the fire instead of at either of them.

"When I was a boy," he said, "my father worked for Meridian."

He felt both of them go still. Not dramatically. Just a settling, the way water settles when a stone goes in.

"He was a civil engineer. Structures, mostly — bridges, culverts, retaining walls. But his specialty, the thing they brought him in for, was containment. Anything that had to hold back pressure. Water, earth, whatever." The words came easier than he expected, which frightened him, because it meant they'd been waiting a long time. "He was good. He was *very* good. There are bridges between here and the coast still standing on his drawings, and they'll stand another hundred years, because my father believed that a thing you build should be built so well that nobody ever has to think about it again."

"That sounds like a good man," Isla said carefully.

"He was a good man. That's the part I keep coming back to." Wren turned the cup. "He wasn't cruel. He wasn't greedy. He paid his debts, he went to church, he taught me to read blueprints before he taught me to read novels. When the company sent him upriver — this was before I was born, mostly, but he told me about it — he came home and told my mother it was the finest work of his career."

Marn's cup had stopped turning.

"There were fences," Wren said. "Up at the headwaters. My father designed them. Not just the wire — the whole system. The boundary posts, the patrol roads, the gates with the company locks. There's a stretch of the upper Sable where the valley narrows to a throat, and the company wanted it sealed, and my father built the seal. He used to say—" Wren stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "He used to say the land up there was fragile. That the spring country couldn't take grazing, couldn't take settlement, that men trampling it would ruin it for everyone. He said the fences were protection. He said it so many times I believed it before I could read."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them a branch cracked under snow-weight or age, and the sound fell down the mountainside and died.

"And now?" Marn said.

"Now I've seen the map. Now I know what's behind those fences. Now I know the company bought every parcel around the source and posted it forbidden and drowned three towns downstream to control what comes *out* of it." He made himself look at her. "And I keep telling myself he didn't know. I keep telling myself he was a builder, not a schemer — Vell drew the line, my father just built along it. That's what I want to believe. That's what I've believed for two days straight, and tonight I can't make it hold anymore, because—"

"Because," Marn said quietly, "a man doesn't design a fence system by accident."

"No," Wren said. "He doesn't."

* * *

The silence afterward was long, but it wasn't empty. Wren could feel the other two in it, feel them deciding whether to press or let him breathe, and it was Marn — blunt, unsentimental Marn — who chose to press, because she was the one with the wound.

"My brother Tomas had a word for men like your father," she said. "He called them *good hands*. Men whose hands were so good at building that they never once asked what they were building *for*. The company loved them. Couldn't get enough of them. Paid them double what the honest work paid, and the honest work paid well, because a good hand is worth ten soldiers to a man like Vell. A soldier can shoot a farmer off his land. But it takes a good hand to make the drowning look like engineering."

"Marn," Isla said softly.

"No. Let me finish, because I've carried this up and down this river for six years and I'm tired of carrying it politely." Marn set her cup down on the stone. "Three towns, Fairweather. Harrow Bend. Little Sable. Caudell's Landing. Eleven hundred people, give or take, and every one of them got a paper telling them their land was condemned for flood control, and every one of them got paid maybe a tenth of what it was worth, and every one of them watched the water come up over their roofs because the company had closed the gates upstream and held the spring rain back until the valleys filled. That wasn't weather. That was *design*. Somebody sat in a warm office and calculated how long it would take to drown a church. And the fences your father built—" she tapped the ground between them, once, hard, "—those fences weren't keeping people *out* of the headwaters. They were keeping people from ever going up there and seeing where the water really came from. Seeing the pipes. Seeing what the company was taking."

"I don't know that," Wren said. "I can't know that."

"You're right. You can't." Marn leaned forward, and the firelight moved on her face like water. "So answer me one question, engineer. One. If your father believed the fences were protection — if he truly believed the land was fragile and needed guarding — why did the company pay him to build them? Why does a man like Vell, who wouldn't spend a dollar on a widow's claim, spend thousands on wire and posts and patrol roads to protect ground he'll never graze and never sell?" She shook her head slowly. "Protection is free, Fairweather. Nobody pays for protection. People pay for *concealment*, and concealment costs exactly what your father charged."

Wren opened his mouth. Closed it.

Because there was no answer. Because he had spent two days building the same argument over and over in his head — *he didn't know, he couldn't have known, he was a builder* — and every time he got it finished, some part of him, the part that had inherited his father's own mind, took a red pencil to it and marked the flaw. His father had not been a fool. His father had been the sharpest mind Wren had ever known, a man who could look at a hillside and tell you where the water ran underneath it. A man like that did not build forty miles of fence without asking himself what he was fencing.

And if he'd asked — and Wren knew, deep down, in the place where sons know their fathers — if he'd asked, then he'd known. And if he'd known, then he'd chosen. And if he'd chosen—

"Maybe he found out too late," Isla said. Her voice was careful, the voice of a woman walking across ice. "Maybe he built it believing, and then—"

"Then what?" Marn said, but gently now. "Then he saw the towns drown and said nothing? Then he collected his pension and raised his boy and never once wrote a letter, never once left a drawing, never once told anyone where the bodies of three towns were buried under his beautiful reservoirs?" She exhaled. "I'm not saying he was a monster. Monsters are easy. Monsters you can hate and be done with. What I'm saying is worse. I'm saying he was probably a decent man who did a terrible thing for decent reasons, and then spent the rest of his life making sure he never had to look at it. And that, Fairweather, is the most common sin on this river. It's the sin the whole territory is built on."

Wren stared into the fire. His eyes were burning and he refused to let it show, because these two had lost more than he had — Marn a brother, Isla apparently everything — and he would not sit here weeping about a father who had, at worst, built fences.

But the grief came anyway, and it wasn't for his father. It was for the boy he'd been, the nine-year-old at the kitchen table, asking *why does the company need fences way up there,* and getting a smile and a ruffle of the hair and *because some things need looking after, son.* Twenty years that answer had stood in him like one of his father's bridges, load-bearing, unquestioned. And now Marn had put six pounds of river ice against it and shown him the crack running clean through the span.

"Here's what I know," he said finally. His voice came out rough. He cleared his throat and started again. "Here's what I know. My father died owing nothing to anybody. Clean books, clean name, a plot in the company cemetery with a brass marker. And I always thought that was the measure of him — that he left the world owing nothing." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "But debts aren't only money. Are they. He owed something to Harrow Bend. To Little Sable. To Caudell's Landing. And he died without paying it, and the debt didn't die with him, because debts like that don't. They pass down."

"To you," Isla said quietly.

"To me." Wren looked up, and met Marn's eyes across the fire. "You want to know why I'm really on this river, Marn? It's not the map. It's not even the collectors, though God knows they're reason enough. I'm on this river because I owe eleven hundred people something and I didn't even know it until this week. My father built the lock on the cage. Maybe the least I can do is find the door."

Marn studied him for a long moment. The firelight caught the lines around her eyes, and something in her expression shifted — not softening, exactly. Recalibrating. The way she looked at water before committing the boat to it.

"Tomas wrote letters," she said at last. "After he disappeared, I got a packet of them, forwarded through a cousin. I've read them a hundred times. In the last one he says he's found a man inside the company — an engineer, old, near retirement — who was asking questions about the headwaters. Asking *dangerous* questions. Trying to find out what happened to the original survey records." Her eyes didn't leave Wren's. "He never gave the man's name. Said it wasn't his to give. Said the man had children and deserved whatever protection anonymity allowed."

The fire crackled. The river murmured. Wren felt the ground tilt very slightly beneath him, the way it did when a bridge flexed under load.

"That could be anyone," he said.

"It could," Marn agreed. "Old engineer. Company man. Conscience catching up with him late in life. Children." She picked her cup back up. "My brother vanished the same year your father retired."

* * *

Later — much later, when Isla had rolled into her blanket and Marn had taken first watch at the mouth of the overhang — Wren lay awake with the map's oilskin case under his head like a stone pillow, and listened to the river.

His father had owned a compass. Brass-cased, hinged lid, the needle worn nearly white from decades of thumbs. Wren had it still; it lived in his toolbox, wrapped in a rag, and he hadn't opened the case in years because opening it meant looking at the engraving on the inner lid, the one line his father had ever written him anything by hand:

*A true bearing holds in any weather.*

All his life Wren had read that as an engineer's maxim. Tonight, lying under alien stars with three towns' worth of drowned dead between him and sleep, he understood it differently. His father had known his bearing. Had known it late, maybe — too late to act, or too much of a coward, or too bound by pension and family and the long habit of obedience — but he had *known*, and he had engraved the knowledge where only his son would find it, and then he had died and left the needle pointing.

*Asking dangerous questions.* An old engineer, near retirement, the year Tomas Marn disappeared.

Tomorrow they would reach the Gorge of Bells. Tomorrow the river would try to kill them, honestly and openly, which was more than the company had ever done. And somewhere upstream, behind his father's fences, at the end of his father's sealed spur line, was the source — and possibly, buried in whatever records the prospector's map led to, an answer to a question a nine-year-old boy had asked at a kitchen table twenty years ago.

Wren closed his eyes.

*Why does the company need fences, Pa?*

This time, he was going upstream to ask it himself.
