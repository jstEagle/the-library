## 9. The Gorge of Bells

The river told us what was coming long before we saw it.

Marn heard it first — she'd been hearing it for an hour, she admitted later, a low percussion under the water's voice like a drum wrapped in wool. By midmorning the sound had grown teeth. It came up through the hull of the *Kestrel's Mercy* and into our boots and our bones, a vibration that made the lantern swing on its hook without any wind to move it.

"That's the Gorge of Bells," Marn said. She stood at the sweep oar with her sleeves rolled to the shoulder, forearms corded, eyes fixed on the water ahead as if she could read it like a telegraph tape. "I said I'd take you as far as the Gorge. I didn't say I'd take you through it."

"You said the narrows were passable in high water," Isla reminded her from the cargo well, where she sat with the journal from the prospector's kit open on her knees, keeping it dry under an oilcloth despite everything else getting soaked.

"I said they were passable *once*, in high water, by a pilot who knew the channel." Marn's jaw worked. "That was eleven years ago. The flood of '79 rearranged every rock between here and the falls. Whatever I knew is at the bottom now."

"Then we portage," I said.

Marn laughed — one short, hard note with no humor in it. "Portage the Gorge? Look up, engineer."

I looked up. The walls rose sheer on either side, granite streaked black with two hundred years of runoff, so close together in places that their crowns seemed to lean toward one another like conspirators. There was no bank. There was no trail. There was the river, green and fast in the calmer stretches and white as boiling milk where it broke over hidden stone, and there was us, committed, carried.

The current had already made our decision. That's the thing about rivers nobody tells you until you're on one: past a certain point, you don't choose anymore. You only steer.

"We can line her through," I said. "Rope off the bow, walk her along the wall."

"With what rope?" Marn jerked her chin at the stern, where our last coil had gone into the rapids below Kestrel Bend along with half our supplies, Corliss's name on all of it. What remained was forty feet of patched hemp I wouldn't trust to hang laundry.

Isla closed the journal. "How fast?"

"Faster than you want," Marn said. "Slower than it feels."

She turned the boat toward the sound.

* * *

I have ridden locomotives through rockslides. I have stood on a trestle in a lightning storm with my hand on a live signal box, feeling the current hum through my gloves. None of it prepared me for the Gorge of Bells.

It began gently enough, which was the cruelest part. The water smoothed, the walls widened, and for perhaps three hundred yards we glided between cliffs so tall the sky became a ribbon of pale blue overhead, and the only sound was our own breathing and the drip of water somewhere in the dark. Then the ribbon of sky narrowed, the walls pinched in, and the river dropped.

Not fell — *dropped*, the way a stair drops, again and again, a staircase of white water descending into thunder. And the thunder had voices. That was why they called it the Gorge of Bells: the water found hollows in the rock, chambers and flutes carved by ten thousand years of spray, and every wave that struck them rang them like struck bronze. The whole canyon tolled. Deep notes rolled up from below, high ones shrieked down from above, and between them the roar of the water itself filled every gap, so that the noise wasn't around us but *inside* us, in the chest, behind the eyes. Isla screamed something at me and I watched her mouth move and understood nothing. Language died at the gorge's mouth.

Marn steered.

I have thought about those nine minutes many times since, and each time I come back to the same wonder: that a human being could do what she did. She read the water the way Isla read wire — not the surface but the meaning beneath it. A smooth green tongue of current meant speed; she put us on it. A V of white pointing downstream meant rocks; she split it. Where the river folded against the left wall she held us in the seam, six inches of decision between safety and being ground to pulp against granite, and when the seam failed she threw the boat across the current with nothing but her shoulders and her will.

Twice we hit. The first time was a glancing blow that spun us and filled the bilge with a hand's depth of freezing water. The second time I remember in pieces: a wall of white standing up ahead where no wall should be, Marn hauling the sweep with a sound torn out of her, Isla grabbing my collar, the world going vertical —

— and then the boat struck something that wasn't water, and the world went to pieces.

* * *

Cold is a strange thing. It doesn't hurt at first. It erases.

I came back to myself face-down on a gravel bar, coughing up the Sable River, with the bells tolling somewhere upstream and the light gone amber and low. Someone was kneeling on my back, pounding between my shoulders. It took me a long moment to understand that the someone was Isla, and that she was crying, and that the words coming out of her between sobs were my name.

"M'fine," I managed, which was a lie. Every rib felt cracked. My hands were shredded — I'd put them out against the rocks without deciding to, the body's dumb animal wisdom — and the cold had gotten into me so deep that my thoughts moved like molasses.

"The boat—" I started.

"Gone." Marn crouched at the edge of the bar, wringing out her hair, staring upstream at the narrows with an expression I couldn't read. "Went to kindling on the second drop. I saw the pieces go by while I was swimming."

"Supplies?"

"Some of it washed down with us. Most of it didn't." She nodded at a scatter of debris along the bar: one crate of hardtack, sodden; the coffee pot, dented; a single oar; Isla's carpetbag, miraculously, snagged in a root wad. Of the rest — the tent, the rope, the tools, most of the food — nothing.

"And the map?" The question came out of me before I'd finished thinking it, and the moment it did I understood why Isla had been crying. Not because I might die. Because if I died, the promise died with me.

Isla already had it. She'd lashed the oilskin bundle inside her coat before the first drop — I hadn't known that, hadn't thought to ask, and looking at her dripping and shivering on that gravel bar with the map held safe against her heart, I understood that I had misjudged her from the beginning. I'd taken her for a runaway. She was a courier. She'd understood the mission better than I had.

"It's dry," she said. "Every page."

Marn looked at the two of us, at the map, at the darkening gorge, and let out a breath that was almost a laugh. "Well then. We're rich. We've got a map, a crate of wet crackers, and an oar. Men have built empires on less."

We weren't laughing, any of us, but something passed between the three of us on that bar — something that hadn't existed at Kestrel Bend or on the water before it. Fear does that, if it doesn't kill you. It burns away everything except what's true.

* * *

We climbed.

There was no path, but there was a seam in the eastern wall where a slide had left a ramp of broken stone, and Marn — who had shot this gorge once upon a time and walked out of it once upon a time too, though she didn't say so until later — found its foot by instinct. We went up single file, Isla first because she was lightest, then me, then Marn carrying the crate and the oar lashed to her back like a rifle. The bells faded below us as we climbed, the roar softening from thunder to murmur, and by late afternoon we stood on the gorge rim with the whole broken country spread out before us: forest to the horizon, mountains beyond it going blue and then white, and nowhere, anywhere, a road.

We made camp in the lee of a granite shelf, out of the wind. Marn got a fire going with damp wood and considerable blasphemy. We ate wet hardtack and drank creek water and didn't complain, because complaining cost calories none of us had.

It was Isla who spread the map out by firelight. "While we still have light," she said. "And while we still have hands steady enough to read it."

The oilskin had done its work. The prospector's map lay flat and crisp, its ink lines fine as spider silk, and by the fire's glow I studied it properly for the first time since the night a dying man had pressed it into mine.

I'm an engineer. I look at drawings the way a priest looks at scripture, and what I saw made the hair rise on my arms.

The upper Sable wasn't drawn as a river at all. It was drawn as a *system*. Springs marked with small circles, dozens of them, scattered through the high country like coins spilled on green cloth — and every circle connected by faint dotted lines running downslope, converging, gathering, funneling toward a single point in the flank of Mount Cael. At that point the draftsman had drawn a cross-section, cutaway style, the way you'd draw a tunnel or a mine. And inside the mountain, rendered in careful perspective, was a cavern — vast, cathedral-vast — with a gate across its throat. A floodgate. Engineered. Built.

Beneath it, in the old surveyor's shorthand I knew from my father's own field books, ran a notation:

*Spur Line 7 — sealed 18—. Access via switch junction, mile 41. Gate mechanism: Fairweather patent.*

The fire popped. Somewhere far off, a wolf spoke and was answered.

"Fairweather," Isla said quietly. She was watching my face, not the map.

"My father's name," I said. "On the gate mechanism." I traced the dotted lines with a finger that shook, and not from cold. All those springs. All that water, gathered and gated and piped away — and my father's engineering holding the door shut on it. "He didn't just build fences. He built the lock."

"Or he built the key," Marn said.

I looked up.

"Think about it," she said, poking the fire so sparks climbed toward the stars. "A man builds a gate. He knows how it opens. He knows how it *fails*. If your father wanted that water free someday, the smartest thing he could do was make sure he was the one who built the door." She shrugged. "Maybe he was a jailer. Maybe he was waiting for somebody to come collect the key."

"Switch junction, mile forty-one," Isla read aloud. "That's the old spur. The decommissioned line." Her eyes came up, bright in the firelight. "Wren — that's your line. The one we took out of the city on the handcar. It runs up the valley toward the mountain."

"It was never just a maintenance spur," I said slowly, and the pieces began to turn over in my mind like machinery warming. "Closed lines. Sealed records. A spur that goes nowhere — except it goes *somewhere*, it goes to mile forty-one, and the company fenced the whole headwaters and posted men to keep everyone out. They're not guarding empty wilderness." I looked at the cross-section again, at the cavern drawn beneath the mountain, at the river born in the dark and piped away to fill Ambrose Vell's reservoirs. "They're guarding the tap."

Silence, except the fire and the far-off water.

"So we walk," Marn said at last. She said it simply, the way she steered — reading the water, finding the seam. "Forty-one miles of dead track. Three days, maybe four, on what's left of this crate." She kicked it lightly. "We'll be hungry. We'll be cold. And when we get there, whatever's behind that gate, there'll be men with guns in front of it, because Corliss isn't stupid and neither is Vell."

"Yes," I said.

They both looked at me.

"When we get there," I said, "there'll be a gate with my father's name on it, and I'm going to open it."

Isla reached over and put her hand flat on the map, over the little drawn cavern, over the spring-fed dark. Marn laid hers on top of Isla's, calloused and river-cold. After a moment I added mine, three hands stacked on a dead man's drawing, and none of us said anything, because there was nothing to say that the gesture didn't say better.

Down in the gorge, the bells kept tolling — but softer now, almost gentle, like something ringing us onward.

We slept in shifts. In the gray hour before dawn, I woke to find Isla already up, repacking the map, and Marn standing at the edge of the firelight facing north, where the old rail grade showed as a faint scar through the trees, climbing toward the mountain.

"There's smoke," Marn said quietly, when she saw me watching. "Way up the line. Mile thirty, maybe thirty-five." She turned, and the firelight caught the grim set of her mouth. "Somebody beat us to the tracks, Wren. And they're moving toward forty-one faster than we are."
