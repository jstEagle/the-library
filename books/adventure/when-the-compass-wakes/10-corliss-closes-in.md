## 10. Corliss Closes In

The river told Corliss everything he needed to know, if a man knew how to listen to it.

He stood on the bank at dawn, boots in the mud, watching the water move. Most men saw only water. Corliss saw freight. Every gallon that slid past him was cargo moving without a bill of lading, without a toll, without the company's permission — and that was the whole disease of the territory in a single image. The Sable ran free because no one had ever had the will or the means to put it on the books. Vell had both. Vell had the pipes and the pumps and the paper. What Vell did not have was the map, and somewhere upstream of this mudbank, three people were carrying it toward the one place in the world where it could still do damage.

Corliss crouched and touched the water. Cold. Snowmelt cold, though the season was wrong for it. That meant the source was running high behind whatever gates his father's generation had built up there in the dark. He straightened, wiped his hand on his coat, and turned to the two men waiting by the skiff.

"Which house first?"

"The Hollis place," said Dree, the younger of them. He was a big man with soft hands and a habit of swallowing before he spoke. "Half mile up. They keep goats."

Goats. Corliss filed that away. Livestock meant water rights, and water rights meant someone in that house had signed something once, or refused to.

They walked. The morning fog lay along the valley like spilled milk, and the homestead rose out of it gradually — a fence, a lean-to, a cabin with a stone chimney, and a woman already standing in the yard with a shotgun broken open over her arm, which was as good as an admission she'd been expecting trouble for years.

"Mrs. Hollis," Corliss said, stopping at a respectful distance. He tipped his hat. He always tipped his hat. It cost nothing and it unsettled people more than shouting did. "Meridian Rail Company. I'm looking for travelers. Three of them. A man who looks like a railroad man, a young woman, and a river pilot."

"We don't see travelers. River's closed to traffic since the company bought the licenses."

"The river is closed to *unlicensed* traffic, ma'am." He let the correction sit there, gentle as a laid hand. "These three wouldn't have papers. I'd take it kindly if you'd think back over the last few days."

"I think back fine. Nobody came."

"Your dogs didn't bark at anything in the night?"

"No dogs."

"No dogs." Corliss looked past her, at the goat pen, at the water trough fed by a pipe that ran down from the hillside spring. Clean water, piped, un-metered. "That's company pipe, Mrs. Hollis. Laid in '71. You've been drawing off it eleven years free."

"It was promised when they took the low fields. It's in the agreement—"

"Agreements are between parties, ma'am, and the party that signed yours is dead." He said it without heat, the way a man reports weather. Then he smiled, and it reached his eyes, which made it worse. "Here's my difficulty. Somebody passed this stretch of river inside the last two days in a boat that doesn't belong to any licensed pilot. Somebody fed them, or sold them flour, or mended a sail, or simply kept their mouth shut. And Mr. Vell has instructed me to find out who. Now — you can help me, or you can be a closed door. But doors get opened, Mrs. Hollis. One way or another."

She didn't lower the gun, but her knuckles whitened on it, and Corliss read her like a gauge. She knew something. Not the travelers themselves — she'd have lied worse — but something adjacent. A boat seen. A voice heard in the fog.

"You saw a skiff," he said.

"I saw nothing."

"You saw a skiff going upstream two nights ago, and you didn't report it because reporting things to the company hasn't done this valley any good in eleven years." He took a slow step forward. "I'm not here to punish you for the skiff. The skiff is nothing. I want to know who rowed it."

"Then you'll want to ask the Shallows," she said, and the bitterness in it was real. "Ask Marn Oduya why her boat was out at night. Ask her why her brother went upriver and never came down."

Corliss went very still, in the way that made other men go still too.

"Marn Oduya," he repeated.

"Pilot. Or she was. Barred for refusing a contract." Mrs. Hollis finally lowered the gun, not from surrender but from contempt; she wanted her hands free to gesture. "You people barred her and then you come asking me about her boat. Go ask her."

"Oh, I intend to." Corliss replaced his hat. "Thank you for your time, ma'am. You've been more helpful than you know."

He turned to go, then paused, and looked back at the trough, at the clear water spilling endlessly into the mud.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I believe you saw nothing. I'll note it that way."

He noted it exactly that way, in his book, in his own shorthand: *Hollis — knows Oduya. Knows more. Later.*

* * *

By noon they were at the Shallows, where the Sable braided itself into a dozen silver threads through gravel bars and deadfalls, and where a stilted boathouse leaned out over the water like a man listening for something under the surface. Corliss had been here before, three years ago, the day the company struck Marn Oduya's name from the pilot rolls. He remembered her face that day. She hadn't begged. She hadn't even argued. She had looked at the company man reading the revocation — some clerk, not Corliss himself — with an expression Corliss had thought about more than once since, though he'd never admit it: not hatred. *Assessment.* As if she were measuring him for something.

Today the boathouse stood open and empty. Tools on the bench, tarps folded, a cold stove. But the big boat was gone from its cradle, and the cradle's grease was smeared fresh.

"She launched heavy," Dree said, crouching at the mud below the stilts. "Three aboard, maybe four. Draft's deep."

"And headed upstream." Corliss walked the bank, reading the mud the way Wren Fairweather read track ballast — though he'd have denied the comparison with violence. "Toward Kestrel Bend. There's a toll station there."

"There's also the Gorge beyond it."

"There's a toll station *at* it." Corliss stopped. Somewhere under the gravel under his boots, water was whispering through channels no surveyor had ever charted, feeding springs in three counties, filling wells in towns whose names appeared on no company ledger. He could feel it, almost — the territory's whole hidden circulatory system, and every artery of it leading back to one mountain. "Wire ahead to Kestrel Bend. Tell them to hold all traffic and inspect everything. If Oduya's boat comes through, they're to detain the passengers quietly and wire me before they so much as offer them coffee."

Dree hesitated. "Quietly, sir? Vell said—"

"Vell said bring him the map and the man who carries it. He didn't say drag them through town in chains and give every gossip from here to the Gorge a story to tell. Quietly, Dree. Fear travels faster than we do. We don't need help spreading it."

Dree went to send the wire. Corliss stayed by the water a while longer.

He had been Vell's enforcer for nine years, and in nine years he had learned that most men misunderstood the job. They thought it was about violence. Violence was the smallest tool in the kit, the one you used when every better tool had failed, and using it meant you'd failed too. No — the job was about *certainty*. About being the one man in any room who never doubted, because doubt was the crack everyone else poured themselves into. Farmers doubted. Pilots doubted. Even directors doubted — Vell doubted constantly, that was the engine of him, doubt wearing a good suit. Corliss did not doubt. That was why Vell kept him, and why Vell feared him slightly, and Corliss counted both facts among his professional satisfactions.

But standing at the Shallows, he permitted himself a single unprofessional thought: that the water under his feet had been running since before the company, before the territory, before any signature on any document, and would run long after. And that a man who built his life on controlling a thing like that was building on the river's sufferance, not his own.

He put the thought away where he kept all the others, in the locked room at the back of himself that had no windows. Then he went to work.

* * *

The next homestead was not a homestead anymore by evening.

It sat on a bench of land above a side creek — the Pell place, a sod-roofed cabin and a half-cleared field, and a man named Aldous Pell who had come out onto his porch with a rifle when he saw them coming and stood there with it, not aiming it, just holding it, the way a man holds a thing he hopes he won't have to remember how to use.

"Company property crossed your land," Corliss said from the yard. "A boat. Two nights back."

"Nobody crossed my land."

"A boat on the creek, then. The creek crosses your land. Everything crosses somebody's land, Mr. Pell. That's what land is *for*."

Pell's jaw worked. Behind him, in the doorway, a woman held a child, and the child held a rag doll, and Corliss registered all of it the way he registered everything — completely, without mercy, and without any particular pleasure.

"I know you're hiding them," Corliss continued. "Or you know who is. Here's what I know, so we can save the evening. I know a prospector named Edegar Tull died on the tracks outside Meridian City six days ago with a map in his possession. I know that map shows the headwaters of the Sable. I know a signal engineer named Wren Fairweather vanished from his boarding house the same night, along with a telegraph girl who shouldn't have known he existed. And I know — this is the part I'd like you to appreciate, Mr. Pell — I know that every single person who helps them is committing a crime against the company that employs half this valley. Which makes every person in this valley either my witness or my suspect, and I would genuinely prefer you be the former."

"They're just people," Pell said. "Running. Same as anyone would."

"Everyone is just people, Mr. Pell. That has never once stopped me."

What happened next happened fast, and afterward each man who was there told it differently, and none of them told it right. Dree said Pell raised the rifle. The other man, Sorrel, said Pell only shifted his grip. Corliss himself never said what happened at all, because what mattered was not the trigger but the lantern.

The lantern was sitting on the porch rail. In the scuffle — brief, ugly, over almost before it began, Pell facedown in his own yard with Dree's knee between his shoulder blades — the lantern went over. Autumn had been dry. The sod roof caught like a wick.

Corliss did not order it. Corliss also did not put it out.

He stood in the yard with Pell pinned under Dree's knee and watched the fire take the roof, and the woman came out screaming and the child was crying, and Corliss said, in a voice pitched to carry no farther than the man beneath him:

"Your roof is burning, Mr. Pell. I can have my men put it out. All it costs is a name. Where does Marn Oduya's boat put in above the Shallows? Who feeds them? Where do they sleep?"

"I don't *know*—" Pell sobbed into the dirt.

"That's an answer. It's just not the answer." Corliss crouched, unhurried, firelight crawling orange across his face. "Let me tell you what you're really looking at, Mr. Pell, because I don't think you understand your own situation. You think this is about a map. It isn't. Maps are paper. Do you know what the headwaters actually are?"

Pell said nothing. The roof beams cracked. The woman was dragging the child toward the creek.

"The spring under that mountain feeds every aquifer in this territory," Corliss said. "Every well from Meridian City to the border settlements. Every stock tank, every orchard, every town that ever gets built in the next hundred years. The company owns the pipes. The company owns the reservoirs. The only thing the company doesn't own is the source itself — and if that map opens the source, if those gates ever open, then the water goes where it pleases instead of where it's sold, and every farm in this valley learns what thirst costs. Your well, Mr. Pell. Your creek. Your child's water. That's what's burning on your roof tonight. Not wood. Certainty. The certainty that tomorrow the tap turns the way it turned today."

"You're insane," Pell whispered.

"I'm *employed*," Corliss said. "There's a difference, and it's smaller than you'd hope."

Then he stood, and nodded to Dree, and Dree and Sorrel put the fire out — mostly. The cabin was ruined, the roof gone, the family's winter stacked under wet ash. Corliss left a company scrip chit on the fence post for damages, twenty dollars, which was an insult dressed as compensation, and he knew it, and he did it anyway, because the insult was the message: *this is what a closed door costs, and we can afford to pay it forever.*

Before they walked out, he crouched by Pell one last time. The man was kneeling in his ruined yard, and he flinched when Corliss neared, and Corliss spoke quietly, almost kindly.

"One more thing. Free of charge. The telegraph girl — Isla, they call her. She worked the Meridian exchange. Clever with wires. If she's rewiring anything on your side of the river — signals, toll lines, anything — you'll know before anyone else, because the wires will start telling lies. When that happens, Mr. Pell, you'll send word to me. Not because you love the company." He glanced at the smoking ruin, at the woman and child huddled by the creek. "Because next time I won't leave a chit."

* * *

They made camp that night on a gravel bar downstream of the burned place, and Corliss sat apart from his men with the fire low and his notebook open on his knee, writing by its light in his small exact hand.

*Hollis — knows Oduya. Withholding.*
*Pell — nothing. Example made. Chit left.*
*Kestrel Bend wired. Hold all traffic.*
*Trio: Fairweather (engineer), Isla (telegraph), Oduya (pilot). Boat, upstream, 2 days ahead. Slowing at tolls.*

Two days ahead. On the water, with a pilot who knew every channel, that was a real lead but not a comfortable one. The Gorge would slow them. The Gorge might kill them, which would solve the map problem and lose the source problem, and Vell wanted the source problem solved his way — quietly, legally, with the waters still owned. Corliss understood that. What he understood less well, in the locked room with no windows, was why the thought of the gates opening produced in him not only professional alarm but something adjacent to relief.

He shut the notebook.

Across the fire, Dree asked, "If they shoot the Gorge, sir — the boat won't make it, odds are. Do we wait for wreckage?"

"We follow the banks," Corliss said. "And if we find wreckage, we search it. And if we find bodies—" he turned the page, "—we search those too. The map is oilskin, Dree. Oilskin floats."

He looked upstream, into the dark where the river came from, where the mountains stood around the one locked room in the world bigger than his own. Somewhere in that dark, an engineer was fixing to open a gate his father had built shut, and a telegraph girl was probably already lying to a wire, and a barred pilot was reading water like scripture.

"Sleep," Corliss said to his men. "At first light we take the old haul road. There's a tunnel on the spur line above the Gorge, and tunnels don't care about rapids."

He banked the fire himself, precisely, leaving nothing burning — a habit, or a philosophy — and lay back with his hat over his eyes.

Two days ahead. Maybe less, now. The river carried everything eventually — boats, secrets, bodies, truth. Corliss intended to be waiting downstream of all of it, with dry boots and a steady hand, when it arrived.
