## 11. The Town That Wasn't There

The map had lied to us.

Not the prospector's map — that one had been honest in its way, all careful ink and patient surveyor's hands. I meant the company map, the one every telegraph office in the territory kept pinned behind the operator's chair, the one I'd copied out a hundred times for dispatch riders and land agents and men with clipboards who thought a girl at a key couldn't read what she was sending. The company map showed the upper Sable as empty country: timber, scree, a tributary or two drawn thin as thread, and nothing else for forty miles.

Nothing else except, as it turned out on the third morning after the gorge, a town.

Marn smelled it before any of us did — woodsmoke and river mud and something green, garden-green, impossible this far into the high country. She stood up in the bow of our battered skiff, borrowed from a Bellhaven man we hadn't met yet without knowing it, and lifted her chin like a hound catching scent.

"Bank ahead," she said. "Left side. Kill the lantern."

Wren doused it. We drifted through the last of the dawn fog with our oars shipped, and the fog peeled back from the bank the way a curtain peels back, and there it was.

A town. A real one — not a camp, not a squatter's scatter of lean-tos, but streets laid out in a grid, plank sidewalks, houses with painted trim, a church with a proper steeple, a schoolhouse bell hanging silent in its frame. Laundry lines strung between porches. Smoke rising from two dozen chimneys. A water wheel turning slow and brown in an irrigation channel that someone had cut by hand, by shovel, by years of backs bent to the work.

And not one line of it on any map I had ever keyed out over a telegraph wire.

"That's Bellhaven," Marn said quietly. "Or it was."

"It *is*," Wren said.

"It was drowned." Marn's voice had gone flat, the voice she used when she was holding something down hard. "Company survey called it a flood basin. Moved everyone out, burned what wouldn't move, put the reservoir right over the top of it. My brother wrote me about it. Said they gave folks ninety days and then gave them nothing." She paused, working the current with one oar. "This isn't Bellhaven. This is what Bellhaven became when the maps forgot it."

Isla, I told myself. You're the one who reads wires. You should have known. Somewhere in three years of traffic I must have carried an order about it — a condemnation notice, a relocation directive, a name on a list. But the company didn't send things like that in plain language. They sent them in code, and the code books lived in the director's office, and girls like me were trusted to move the words without ever being meant to understand them.

I understood them now. The fog finished lifting, and so did I.

* * *

They saw us coming, of course. A town that survives by being invisible doesn't survive by being careless. By the time Marn ran the skiff up against the landing — a rough timber dock built where the old steamboat pier had been, according to a faded sign nailed to a post — there were a dozen people along the bank watching us, and at least four of them had guns held in the easy, ready way of people who had practiced.

"Names," said a broad man with a blacksmith's forearms and a shotgun broken open over his arm, casual as a handshake.

"Marn Oduya," Marn said. "Pilot. These are Wren Fairweather and Isla Voss. We came down off the gorge. Our boat didn't make it."

"Nobody's does." The man looked at the three of us — soaked, half-starved, Wren still favoring the ribs he'd cracked on the rocks — and something in his face shifted from suspicion toward a kind of tired recognition. "Gorge spits you out looking like death's laundry. Come up. There's soup."

That was how it started. That was how it always starts in places like this, I learned — not with trust but with soup. We sat in a long low hall that had been a freight depot in another life, at tables made from door planks, and ate barley broth with wild onions in it while the whole town seemed to drift through in ones and twos to look at us. Children first, because children are always sent first; then women with work-roughened hands; then men who stood at the edges and said nothing.

An old woman brought us bread. She moved slowly, one hand pressed to her hip, and her eyes went over each of us in turn — Marn, whose name meant something here, I could see it in the way heads turned when she said it; me, unremarkable, a wet bird of a girl; and then Wren.

She stopped. Her whole body stopped, the way a clock stops.

"Fairweather," she said.

Wren looked up, bread halfway to his mouth. "Ma'am?"

"Your father's name was Aldous Fairweather." It wasn't a question. "Chief engineer of record for the Meridian Land and Water Company. Signed the condemnation orders. Signed the easements. Signed the blasting permits." She recited it like scripture, like a litany she'd been saying to herself for twenty years in the dark. "I've seen his name more times than I've seen my own husband's face, and my husband is under sixty feet of that reservoir."

The hall went quiet. Not hostile-quiet — worse. Listening-quiet. Forty people waiting to hear what the son would say.

"I never—" Wren started, and stopped, and I watched him reach for the truth the way a drowning man reaches for anything. "I knew he worked for the company. I didn't know what he signed. I swear I didn't know."

"You swear." The old woman nodded slowly. Then she leaned down, gathered, and spat at his feet — a thin, deliberate arc that landed on the floorboards an inch from his boot, precise as a telegrapher's fist. "My name is Odile Brannock. I had a house on Kestrel Street. I had a garden. I had a granddaughter buried in the churchyard behind you, and they flooded the churchyard too, Mr. Fairweather, and they sent your father's signature to tell me it was legal."

Then she straightened up and walked away, slow, one hand on her hip, and left the silence sitting on us like a stone.

Wren didn't touch the bread again. He sat with his hands flat on the table, staring at the spot on the floor, and I wanted to say something — I, who had carried the orders myself, who had keyed out requisitions and patrol schedules and God knew what else without once asking what the words weighed — but there was nothing in my mouth that wasn't also an apology from inside the machine. So I put my hand over his instead, and he let it stay there, and that was the whole conversation.

* * *

They let us stay. That surprised me until Marn explained it, sitting with me later on the porch of the house they'd given us — a real house, with a real bed, the first roof over us since the wreck.

"They're not forgiving him," she said. "Don't mistake it. They're keeping him close. If the son of Aldous Fairweather turns out to be worth something, that's worth more than any grudge. And if he turns out to be like his father—" She shrugged. "Well. They know where he sleeps."

"That's not mercy," I said.

"No," Marn agreed. "It's better than mercy. It's a chance."

I watched the town through the porch rail as the evening came on. Lamplighters — actual lamplighters, a boy of maybe ten going post to post with a taper — and somewhere a fiddle tuning up, and the smell of that same woodsmoke and river mud and green growing things. A town erased from every map, living its whole life in the blank space where the company had written *nothing*. I thought about all the blank spaces I'd transmitted in my time. All the coded traffic I'd moved without reading. All the towns like this one that might be living in the margins of the wires I'd carried, and me with my finger on the key, sending the darkness along.

"Wren's taking it hard," I said.

"He should take it hard." Marn said it without cruelty. "Hard is the beginning of useful. My brother took it soft. He got angry, and anger without use just burns you up and leaves the world exactly as it was." She looked at me sidelong. "You took it soft too, telegraph girl. I've seen you flinch every time someone says the word *company*."

I didn't answer. There wasn't an answer yet. But I wrote the sentence down inside myself, the way I used to copy messages into my logbook, and I knew I'd be reading it back for a long time.

* * *

The town council convened after supper — seven people around a table in the old freight hall, plus the whole population standing along the walls, because in Bellhaven apparently everything was everybody's business. They questioned us for two hours. Where we'd come from, why, what we carried. Wren laid the oilskin map on the table and told them the truth, all of it: the dying prospector, the promise, the collectors, the flight upriver, the gorge. He told them about the tunnel on the old spur, and the riddle in the map's margin, and the thing the prospector's notes hinted at beneath the mountain.

He did not tell them his plan, because he didn't have one yet. I could see that. He stood there in front of forty strangers with his father's sin hanging off him like wet wool, and he said, "I don't know what I'm going to do. I only know I'm going upstream, and I'd rather not lie to the people I meet on the way."

Old Brannock spoke from the back. "His father said pretty things too."

"His father," said a younger woman near the front — the blacksmith's wife, I'd learn, a sharp-eyed woman named Tally — "is dead, and this one is here, and here is where things get decided. I say we hear him out."

"We don't even know if the spring story is true," said a man beside her.

"The company thinks it's true," I said.

Every head turned. I hadn't meant to speak. But it was true, and I was the one who could prove it, and suddenly three years of intercepted traffic arranged themselves in my head like a message finally decoded. I stood up. My knees were shaking and I stood up anyway.

"I worked the relay at Corven Junction. Before I ran. I pulled intercepts off the trunk line — orders that weren't addressed to our station, traffic I was supposed to pass blind. There was a file designation that kept recurring: SOURCE-PRIORITY. Requisitions going upcountry. Cement. Steel gate stock. Pump machinery, heavy gauge. Survey crews rotated every six weeks so nobody stayed long enough to ask questions. And a standing order from Director Vell's own office — I copied it myself — that any unauthorized party approaching the headwaters was to be *detained indefinitely*, and I want you to hear those words, because indefinite detention is not a phrase you use for protecting scenery." I looked around the hall. "Whatever's under that mountain, Vell has spent a fortune walling it in and more money than that keeping it secret. Towns don't get erased from maps over nothing. I carried some of the erasing myself. I know how much ink it takes."

Silence. Then Old Brannock, from the back, in a voice like dry leaves:

"The girl reads wires. The pilot knows the river. And the engineer—" she paused, and I braced for the spit again, but it didn't come; what came was worse and better both, something almost like hope worn down to a nub— "the engineer has his father's hands. Maybe that means something. Maybe it means nothing. Put it to the room."

They voted by standing. One by one, then in groups, then nearly all together, the people of Bellhaven stood up — the families the dams had scattered, the farmers the fences had starved, the children born in a town that officially did not exist. They stood up for us. For him. For the son of the man who had signed their valley away, they stood up, and I watched Wren Fairweather receive it like a blow, because forgiveness offered too soon is its own kind of wound, and he knew he hadn't earned a syllable of it yet.

Tally the smith's wife summed it up at the door as we filed out. "You sleep under our roofs tonight. Tomorrow you tell us what you need. And Mr. Fairweather—" she caught his sleeve, and her voice dropped to something fierce and quiet— "whatever you do up that mountain, do it loud enough that the whole territory hears the name of this town. We've been *nothing* long enough."

* * *

I couldn't sleep. Around midnight I gave up and walked out to the landing, and found Wren already there, sitting on the dock edge with his boots off and his feet in the cold water, the oilskin map across his knees though there wasn't light enough to read it.

"Couldn't sleep either?" he said, without turning.

"How'd you know it was me?"

"You walk like you're still counting pulses on a wire." He scooted over. I sat down. The river slid past below us, black and patient, carrying snowmelt down from the mountain we were meant to enter. "They spat at my feet today, Isla."

"One woman did."

"She was right to." He turned the map over in his hands. "You know what the worst part is? When she said his name, for half a second I felt *proud*. That's the reflex they build into you. Your father's name comes up and you stand a little straighter before you remember what it was attached to." He laughed, short and awful. "Half a second. Then I saw her granddaughter's grave under sixty feet of water."

I pulled my knees up. Out on the dark water, a lantern bobbed — night fishermen, or a watch, or just the town refusing to go fully dark, the way it had refused for twenty years.

"For what it's worth," I said, "when I ran from Corven Junction, I didn't run because I'd grown a conscience. I ran because Corliss's men shot a station keeper in Coldbrook for passing one wrong message, and I realized I was next in line no matter how clean my fists were. That's not courage. That's arithmetic." I looked at him. "But sitting in that hall tonight, hearing you tell them the truth with nothing to gain from it — that did something to my arithmetic. So maybe it works backward too. Maybe you start by running and end up standing."

Wren was quiet a long moment. Then he said, "Vell's agents will find this place. Towns like this can't hide forever, not with Corliss tracking us. And when he comes asking about us, every soul on this bank is in danger because of my name."

"Then we'd best give him something else to chase," I said. "Something louder."

He looked at me then, really looked, and I saw the idea take hold behind his eyes — the same look I imagined his father had worn over blueprints, the engineer's look, the look of a man seeing a structure where other people see only rock. Whatever he was building in his head, it had gates and levers and a river in it.

"Go wake Marn," Wren said, standing, pulling his boots on. "And bring your code book, Isla. I think it's time somebody sent a message on Vell's own lines — and I think you're the only one alive who can send it without getting caught."

Far up the valley, past the sleeping town, past the fences, thunder rolled off the mountain — or something very like thunder, deep and rhythmic, like great doors being tested from the other side.

The water under the dock shivered against my feet.

It was starting.
