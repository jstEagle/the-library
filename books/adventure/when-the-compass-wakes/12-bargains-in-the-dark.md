## 12. Bargains in the Dark

The agents came at dusk, riding three mules down the goat track from the pass, and Bellhaven knew what they were before they said a word. You could tell by the way they sat their animals — straight-backed, unhurried, the way men sit when they've never once been afraid of the road behind them. Their coats were too clean for the mountains. Their boots were too good. And one of them carried a lantern with the Meridian Rail Company's sigil stamped on the glass: a black line running through a circle, the way a rail runs through a tunnel, the way a knife runs through a belly.

I was on the roof of the old washhouse, patching a hole with salvaged tin, when the word came up the ladder in a child's whisper. *Company men. Three of them. At the gate.*

I put down the hammer. Below me, the town had gone quiet the way a river goes quiet before it drops — that held-breath stillness, every soul listening. Isla appeared at the top of the ladder, her dark hair tied back with a strip of telegraph wire, and she didn't say anything either. She just looked at me, and her look said everything: *They're here for you. They're here because of us.*

"They can't have the map," I said.

"I know." Her jaw was set. "But they don't have to have it, Wren. They just have to make the town want to give it to them."

That was the truth of it, and we both knew it. Bellhaven wasn't a fortress. It was a hundred and forty starving people living in a town that didn't exist, eating fish from a river the company claimed and growing gardens on land the company had condemned. A hundred and forty people who had spent years learning that the company's mercy was a door that only opened inward.

I climbed down and went to find Marn.

* * *

Marn was in her brother's old room at the end of the long house, sitting with the letters spread out on the blanket the way she did most nights — reading them again, though she knew every word by heart. Her arm was still bound from the tunnel, the wound Corliss's collapsing charges had given her, and she held the paper awkwardly with her good hand. When I came in she didn't look up.

"They'll offer amnesty," she said. "That's how Vell works. He never sends soldiers first. He sends offers."

"You've seen it before."

"Kestrel Bend. Before the toll station." Now she looked up, and her eyes were flat and gray as river water in November. "A man named Odell was running contraband for them. Vell's people offered him a pardon and a pension to name the other runners. He named six. The company hanged four of the six anyway — said their crimes predated the pardon. Odell got his pension. He drank himself to death on it inside two years." She gathered the letters into their oilcloth sleeve. "An offer from Ambrose Vell is a hook with the barb filed off. It still goes in clean. It's the coming out that kills you."

Down in the square, a voice was rising — one of the agents, amplified by the bowl of the valley, by the silence of a hundred and forty people afraid to cough. I caught fragments as I moved to the window. *...full amnesty... passage to Meridian City... five hundred dollars... no questions asked... the man Fairweather is not a criminal, merely misled... the company bears him no ill will...*

"They're being gentle," I said.

"Of course they are." Marn stood, wincing at her arm. "Gentle is worse. Gentle makes people think."

* * *

They held the vote in the meetinghouse that night, and I stood at the back of it and let them look at me.

That was the hardest part — standing there while a hundred and forty faces turned my way, and knowing that every one of those faces had a reason to hate what I came from. I had learned the town's history in fragments over the past days: the dam at Hollow Creek that drowned the orchards, the dam at Two Rivers that drowned the church, the condemnation papers signed in Meridian City by men who never saw the water rise. Bellhaven itself had been erased from the maps because it sat on the survey route to the source — a town that was never supposed to be found again, and so became a town that could not ask anything of the world, because as far as the world knew, it wasn't there.

The eldest of them was a woman called Mother Cass, silver-haired and straight as a fencepost, who had been a girl when the first dam went in. She stood at the front with the company's offer written out fair on a sheet of paper, and she read it aloud in a voice like a bell tolling.

"Full amnesty. Five hundred dollars. And — " her lip curled — "a guarantee that the town of Bellhaven, quote, *will be restored to the company's register of settlements and accorded all legal protections thereof.*"

A murmur went through the room. That was the barb under the barb. Legal protections. Recognition. A hundred and forty people who had spent a decade as ghosts being offered the chance to exist again — on paper, in ink, in the company's own ledger — if they gave up one man and one map.

"They'll never honor it," said a voice from the crowd — Tam Weller, the tracker who had guided us to the spur, a lean man with a face like weathered rope. "You think Vell writes a town back into existence because it did him one favor? The moment he has the map, we're a loose end. Loose ends get cut."

"Maybe," said another voice — a younger one, and I recognized it with a sinking feeling. It was Dree, the ferryman's daughter, whose mother had died the winter the fish stopped running. "Maybe. But maybe not. Maybe it's real. We don't know these people. We know *him*." Her finger found me across the room. "We know his father built the fences. We know the Fairweather name is on the condemnation papers. And now his son comes up the river carrying a map to the one thing that could save us, and we're supposed to just — trust him? Because he says so?"

"I never asked you to trust me," I said. My voice came out lower than I intended, and I had to lift it. "I asked you for a guide and a night's shelter. I'll be gone by morning either way. The map goes where it goes — upstream, to the source — and what happens there won't need your permission or your forgiveness."

"Bold words," Dree said. "From a company man's son."

"I'm not a company man's son. I'm a company *debtor's* son. There's a difference, and it's the only inheritance I ever got."

That got a rough sound from somewhere — not quite a laugh. Mother Cass raised her hand and the room settled.

"We vote," she said. "All who would take the company's offer, and give up the man and the map — stand."

For a long moment, nobody moved. The lamplight swung. Somewhere outside, wind worried the loose tin on the washhouse roof, the same tin I'd been hammering that afternoon, and the sound was very small and very loud.

Then Dree stood. And her father stood with her. And then three more — the Hollis brothers, who ran the still up the draw; a woman whose name I didn't know, holding a child who was too young to understand what her mother's standing body meant.

Seven.

"Those opposed," Mother Cass said, "remain seated."

And the rest of the room stayed where they were — but staying seated wasn't the same as standing *for* me, and I could feel the difference in the air, in the eyes that slid away from mine, in the way the silence had a grain to it, a direction, like a current pulling. A hundred and thirty-three people who hadn't decided to betray me. A hundred and thirty-three people who hadn't decided anything at all.

Mother Cass looked at the count, and her mouth was a thin line. "It's not enough to turn him out," she said. "But it's not enough to call it settled, either. We'll vote again at dawn. Sleep on it." Her eyes found mine, and there was no cruelty in them, which somehow made it worse. "I'm sorry, boy. But a town that's been drowned once learns to count the water before it drinks."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat in the long house with the map across my knees and the oilskin warm from my hands, and I listened to the town breathe around me — doors, footsteps, a baby crying and being hushed, the creak of someone keeping watch on the gate. Isla came and sat with me for a while, her shoulder against mine, saying nothing, which was its own kind of speech. Then she went to the telegraph shack — the town had one, salvaged wire and a battery built out of pickle jars — because she said if the agents sent a report downriver, she wanted to read it first.

It was near midnight when Marn came in.

She didn't sit down. She stood in the doorway with her bound arm cradled against her chest and her brother's letters in her good hand, and she looked at me for a long moment with an expression I couldn't read — something between anger and grief and a decision being made behind her eyes.

"You should know what you cost them," she said. "Not the map. You. Your name."

"I know what my name costs."

"You don't." She came forward and sat, finally, on the bench across from me, and laid the oilcloth packet on the table between us. "You know what the papers say. You don't know what the letters say. There's a difference, and it's the difference between a fence and the man who cuts it."

She untied the cord. The letters were soft as cloth from handling, years of handling, the ink faded to the color of old bruises. She didn't hand them to me. She read.

"*Dear Marn,*" she read, and her voice changed — it went quieter, younger, a voice reading from a voice. "*They've posted the condemnation order for Two Rivers. I got a look at it through a friend in the land office. There's one signature on it that shouldn't be there. One name that fought them every step for a month and then signed anyway. A surveyor's engineer. Fairweather. W. Fairweather.*"

My hands went still on the map.

"I thought he'd sold out," Marn went on, still reading, still in that borrowed voice. "*I went to find him. Found him at the Two Rivers crossing, up to his knees in the water with his instruments, measuring the flood line like it mattered, like measuring it could stop it. I asked him how a man could sign a thing and then weep over it. He said — *"

She stopped. Her throat worked.

"He said, *'Because the order was signed before I ever saw it, and the only choice left to me was what got written in the margin.'*" Marn looked up. "Your father wrote in the margin, Wren. Every page of the condemnation order for Two Rivers, he wrote in the margin. Flow rates. Flood lines. Where the water would go, and how fast, and which houses would stand longest if the gates were ever opened again. He wrote it like a man leaving a trail. Like a man who expected someone to come looking."

The room was very quiet. I could hear my own blood in my ears.

"He couldn't stop the drownings," Marn said. "One engineer against Vell's whole machine — no. But he signed, and then he spent the rest of his life making sure the signing could be undone. The floodgate at the source — the one in your map's final riddle — that's *his* design. My brother figured that out before he vanished. He wrote to me: *'The gate was built to be reopened, Marn. Built that way on purpose. Fairweather made sure of it. The company thinks it's a lock. It's a promise.'*"

I looked down at the map on my knees — the dead prospector's map, the oilskin, the careful survey lines — and I saw it differently now. Not a treasure. Not a weapon. A margin. A whole life written in the margin of an order he'd been powerless to refuse, waiting for a hand to read it.

"Why are you telling me this now?" I asked. My voice was rough. "Before the dawn vote. You could let them turn me over. You'd be rid of me, and the letters would still be true."

"Because Dree's going to stand up at dawn and say the Fairweather name means betrayal," Marn said, "and someone in this room has to stand up and say it doesn't. Not because you've earned it. You haven't. Not yet. But because your father spent twenty years earning it, and dead men can't vouch for themselves, and somebody has to." She gathered the letters back into their oilcloth, and her gray eyes fixed on mine, and there was no softness in them at all — only a faith so hard and clean it was almost a blade. "I lost a brother to this river's truth. I'm not losing his truth to a town's fear on the same night. At dawn, when they vote, I'm going to stand up and read these letters out loud. Every word. And then they can decide what a Fairweather is worth."

"Marn —"

"Don't thank me." She stood, wincing, and tucked the packet inside her coat. "Thank him. By finishing it."

* * *

Dawn came gray and cold, and the town gathered in the meetinghouse again, and Mother Cass called the vote a second time.

Dree stood first this time, faster than before, her chin up, her father's hand on her shoulder. The Hollis brothers stood. The woman with the child stood. And then Marn stood, and her voice carried to the back rafters, and she read my father's name into the air of that drowned town — *W. Fairweather, who measured the flood line, who wrote the margins, who built the gate to be opened* — and I watched the current of the room change direction. I watched it the way I'd learned to read water on Marn's boat: not all at once, but grain by grain, eddy by eddy, until the whole slow body of it was moving somewhere new.

When she finished, the silence lasted ten heartbeats. Then Tam Weller stood. Then two more. Then a dozen, then half the room, and Dree sat down slowly, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand, furious at herself for crying and crying anyway.

Mother Cass let it run its course. Then she raised her hand.

"No more votes," she said. "The town has spoken." Her gaze came to me at the back, and this time there was something in it that hadn't been there before — not forgiveness, not yet, but a door unlatched. "Fairweather. Your father's margin is in our keeping now too. You'll finish what he wrote, or you'll answer to us as well as to Vell. Understood?"

"Understood," I said.

It was Isla who came in from the telegraph shack as the meeting broke, moving fast, her face white under its windburn, a paper strip of intercepted message clenched in her fist. She pushed through the crowd and thrust it at me, and her voice dropped to almost nothing, meant for me and Marn alone.

"It's Corliss. He's not waiting on the agents' report. He's coming up the old spur — with a demolition crew." She swallowed. "Wren — he's going to seal the tunnel from the outside. With us inside it, if we're still in there. We have maybe a day. Maybe less."

I looked at Marn. Marn looked at me. Somewhere beyond the meetinghouse walls, the Sable River ran cold and patient through the gorge, carrying my father's promise toward the sea.

"Then we go now," I said. "While the gate's still there to open."
