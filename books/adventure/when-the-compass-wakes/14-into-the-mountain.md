## 14. Into the Mountain

The tunnel mouth breathed cold air against my face, and I thought, absurdly, of the telegraph office in Meridian — how the key clicked and hummed all night, carrying voices I was never meant to hear, and how I had learned to read silence between the signals. This mountain had that same quality. A held breath. A message waiting.

Wren went first with the lantern, because he said his father's hands knew this rock even if his father's mouth never had. Marn came behind him, her arm bound tight where the gorge had tried to keep her, moving with a stubbornness that frightened me more than any wound should have. I took the rear with the map, folded into quarters inside my coat, close over my heart like a second pulse.

The old spur line ran straight into the dark for a hundred yards before the rails simply stopped — not rusted away, not torn up, but *stopped*, sheared clean as if the mountain had decided mid-sentence it was done talking. Wren knelt at the cut end and ran his thumb along the edge.

"Torched," he said. "Company torch. They didn't abandon this line, Isla. They amputated it."

"Then what are we walking into?"

He looked back at me, lantern light carving his face out of the blackness, and for once he didn't hedge or apologize or weigh both sides of a thing the way he'd been doing since Kestrel Bend. "The truth," he said. "Or whatever's left of it after they got through burning."

We walked.

* * *

The tunnel narrowed and then opened again, and the air changed — went sweet, almost, mineral-cold, tasting of deep stone and something older than stone. My ears popped twice. Marn swore softly and said the pressure reminded her of the drowned towns, of standing on the reservoir shore and knowing there were church steeples under her boat.

Then Wren stopped so suddenly I walked into his back.

Ahead, the lantern found a camp.

It was small and pitiful and unmistakably human: a bedroll gone gray with dust, a tin plate with a spoon still resting in it, a crate turned into a table, and pinned to the tunnel wall by four rusted nails, a survey chart so large it covered the stone from floor to shoulder height. Someone had lived here. Someone had worked here, alone, in the belly of a mountain, drawing lines and numbers by lamplight while the world above forgot him.

And someone had died here. The bedroll told us that much without cruelty — the way it was arranged, the boots set neatly beside it, toes pointing toward the tunnel mouth. Toward home.

Wren lifted the lantern higher. The chart resolved out of shadow: contour lines, elevations, flow rates written in a cramped, beautiful hand. And across the top, a title block in the same hand:

**SABLE HEADWATERS SURVEY — FINAL REVISION — A. VELL, DIR. / FIELD SURVEYOR: E. HOLLIS**

Marn made a sound low in her throat. "Hollis," she said. "That's the name from the reward notice. That's the man whose effects Vell wants burned."

"He wasn't hiding from Vell," Wren said slowly. He'd moved along the wall, reading the chart the way I'd seen him read signal diagrams — whole systems at a glance. "Look at these revisions. Dates going back six years. He was working *for* them. He surveyed this whole system for them."

"Then why is his camp in their tunnel instead of their office?"

Wren didn't answer. He was looking at a smaller paper tacked beside the chart — pages of a journal, actually, sewn into an oilskin cover, hanging from its nail like a confession waiting to be read. He took it down with both hands, careful as a man lifting something out of a fire.

I lit the second lamp we'd salvaged from the wrecked boat, and we three crowded together, and Wren opened the journal of Elias Hollis.

* * *

The first entries were ordinary enough — a surveyor's log, weather notes, mule feed, instrument calibrations. Hollis wrote in a dry, precise voice that reminded me of every company man I'd ever tapped a wire near. But two years in, the voice changed. It changed the way a telegraph line changes when someone starts sending in cipher — same clicks, different meaning.

*The spring is larger than the reports state. Larger than Vell has been told. I have measured the artesian pressure myself and the figures do not lie: if the natural channels were reopened, this water would irrigate the entire eastern valley. Every dry farm from Bellhaven to the Kestrel. Not piped. Not rationed. Free.*

*I brought this to Vell expecting commendation. Instead he asked me how certain I was that no one else could reach the figures. I did not understand the question then. I understand it now.*

*They are not buying the headwaters to develop them. They are buying the headwaters to bury them. As long as the spring feeds only their reservoirs, every settlement in the territory drinks at Vell's pleasure and pays at Vell's price. If the valley knew what runs beneath these mountains, his empire of pipes would be worth nothing overnight.*

*He has asked me to falsify the final revision. To record the spring as seasonal, unreliable, marginal. I have refused. God forgive me, I spent eleven years making maps for men like him and calling it honest work. I will not make this one.*

*They have dismissed me. They have also, I note, begun assigning men to "escort" me between towns. I have come back to my old camp to finish the true survey in secret. If anything happens to me, the figures are here, and the gate mechanism is here, and the man who designed the floodgate — F. Fairweather — left drawings that show exactly how it may be reopened. Vell thinks Fairweather built the fences. Fairweather built the door too. He just never told Vell which one mattered.*

I heard Wren's breath catch. In the lamplight his face had gone strange — stripped down to something young and raw.

"My father," he whispered. "Hollis knew my father. They worked together."

"And your father built the way in," Marn said quietly. "Not just the fence, Wren. The *door*. He left you a lock he already knew how to open."

* * *

I kept reading aloud after that, because Wren seemed unable to, and because someone needed to give the words air. The last entries thinned. Hollis's handwriting deteriorated — the cramped precision loosening, letters drifting, ink blotted where his hand must have shaken.

*They found my supply cache at Kestrel Bend. I have perhaps three weeks of food. It does not matter. The survey is complete. Every figure cross-checked twice. Let them hunt me; the numbers are already true, and truth does not starve.*

*I think often of the valley towns. Of what water would mean to them. I was part of the machine that took it from them. Perhaps this is the only amends available to a man of my trade: to measure honestly, at the end, what the others measured falsely.*

*To whoever finds this: the gate is real. The mechanism is sound. Fairweather's design holds. Open it, and let the river go home.*

The last entry was a single line, undated:

*The lamps are low. I am not afraid. I only wish I had walked upstream sooner.*

Silence took the tunnel then — the real kind, the kind I used to hear between transmissions at 3 a.m., when the wire went dead and you could feel the whole country holding still. Marn sat down slowly on the crate, cradling her bound arm, and stared at the bedroll with its neat boots.

"He died alone," she said, "with the answer in his hands, and nobody came."

"Not nobody." Wren touched the oilskin cover. "Us. We came. Late, but we came."

I wanted to say something kind. I had the words ready — I always have the words ready, that's the operator's curse — but before I could speak, the mountain spoke first.

A boom rolled through the rock. Distant, muffled, felt in the soles of my boots before it reached my ears. Dust sifted from the ceiling of the tunnel in a fine gray veil, settling on Hollis's chart, on his tin plate, on the three of us.

Marn was on her feet instantly, wounded arm and all. "That's blasting powder."

Another boom. Closer. Or maybe the same distance but deeper in — it was hard to tell direction underground; the rock carried sound the way a wire carries current, everywhere at once.

"They're collapsing the tunnel," Wren said. His voice had gone flat and fast, the engineer's voice, the one that did arithmetic while everyone else panicked. "Corliss found the entrance. He's sealing it behind us — no. Ahead of us. Sealing us *in* with the evidence."

"The map shows a way through," I said, and hated how thin my voice sounded. "Doesn't it? There's a route past the gate, up to the source—"

"There's a route." Wren was already rolling Hollis's journal closed, already stuffing it inside his coat next to the map. "But Isla — the gate comes first. Everything past the floodgate is gallery and cistern, and the galleries only open if the gate mechanism is released. My father designed it that way. Fail-safe. No water, no passage."

"So we fix the gate."

"So we fix the gate," he agreed, "while a man who burns homesteads blows up the only road home."

A third explosion, and this time the tunnel behind us groaned — a long, grinding exhalation, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. Dust poured thicker now. Somewhere far back, rock was coming down where we had walked an hour ago, erasing our footprints, erasing Hollis's camp, erasing the way out.

Marn caught my eye across the lamplight. She didn't smile — she wasn't a woman who smiled in tunnels — but she nodded once, slow and steady, the way she did at the wheel when the rapids turned bad and there was nothing left but to run them.

"Well," she said. "No way back. Only way through. Story of this whole damn river."

Wren took up the lantern. His hand was shaking, I saw — but his voice wasn't.

"Hollis waited two years for someone to walk upstream," he said. "Let's not keep the water waiting too."

And we turned our backs on the dying tunnel, and walked deeper into the mountain, toward the sound of our own names being buried — and ahead of us, faint through the stone, I swear I could hear it: water. Moving. Waiting for someone to turn the key.
