## 15. Time Running Out

The mountain groaned.

Not the settling creak of old timber or the distant complaint of a train taking a curve — this was deeper, older, a sound that came up through Wren's boot soles and into his teeth, as if the whole mass of stone above them had drawn a breath and decided to let it out. Dust sifted from the gallery ceiling in pale curtains, catching the light of Isla's lantern like snow falling upward in reverse.

"Move!" Marn's voice, raw from the gorge, from the climb, from everything. "Don't look at it — *move!*"

They ran. The prospector's journal was still clutched against Wren's chest where he'd shoved it inside his coat, its oilcloth cover slick with his sweat. Behind them, back along the tunnel they'd just traversed, came a second sound beneath the groaning: a crack like a rifle shot, then another, then a rolling thunder that he felt rather than heard, and the lantern light behind them died all at once as the passage they'd come through ceased to exist.

Wren didn't stop. Stopping meant thinking about it. Thinking about it meant imagining several hundred tons of rock where his legs had been thirty seconds ago.

The gallery bent left, following some ancient survey line, and the rails appeared underfoot — the old spur, rusted to lace but real, running straight into the dark ahead of them. His father's rails. His father's grade, his father's tunnels, cut through this mountain twenty-five years ago for reasons that had been buried along with everything else.

Another explosion. Closer. The floor jumped beneath them and Isla stumbled, her lantern swinging wild, shadows lurching across the timbered walls like drowning men.

"I've got you—" Marn caught her by the collar and hauled her upright without breaking stride, shoving her ahead of her. "Keep the light up! We need to see the ground!"

"The map said the junction—" Isla gasped between strides. "The journal said the floodgate chamber was past the third junction—"

"Then we find three junctions before the mountain finds us!"

* * *

They found the first one ninety seconds later, and Wren knew it instantly, the way he'd known the gauge of the rails, the spacing of the tie bolts, the particular hand-cut notches in the support timbers. He'd grown up on drawings like this. He'd fallen asleep on his father's drafting table while the man inked profiles of grades and chambers, and somewhere in his skull those lines had never faded.

A switch stand rose out of the rubble-dusted ballast, its lever frozen with corrosion, its target plate long since painted over. But beneath the paint, when Isla held the lantern close, the old company stencil was still legible: **MERIDIAN RAIL & WATER — JCT 1**.

*Rail & Water.* Not Rail Company. Rail **and Water**. He'd seen that name a hundred times on his father's documents and never once asked himself what water had to do with a railroad.

"Second junction should be maybe four hundred yards," he said. "If the grade holds."

"And if it doesn't?" Marn asked.

"Then we're standing in the wrong place when the wrong place comes down."

She grunted — her version of agreement — and they ran on.

The explosions were coming in a rhythm now. Wren counted the intervals without meaning to; it was what he did, what he'd always done, turning chaos into numbers because numbers could be solved. Forty seconds. Then thirty-five. Corliss wasn't collapsing the tunnel randomly. He was working forward from the entrance in measured charges, walking the mountain down behind them, herding them deeper. Which meant Corliss knew exactly how deep the tunnel went. Which meant Corliss had the same survey maps Wren's father had drawn.

Which meant there might be no way out at all except the way the journal promised.

*Through the gate,* the dead surveyor had written, in a hand gone shaky near the end. *Vell sealed it because it was the only door. What he sealed shut can be opened. The engineer who built the lock built it honest.*

The engineer who built the lock. Wren had read that line a dozen times since the gorge and every reading had landed a little harder. He hadn't told the others yet. He didn't have words for it yet — for the thing that was forming in him like a blister, equal parts grief and fury and something he couldn't name.

The second junction came with a sound like the world ending.

* * *

It happened at the mouth of the side gallery. One moment the passage ahead stood open, timbered and dark and whole; the next, the air pressure changed — Wren felt it pop in his ears — and the ceiling two hundred yards ahead folded inward with a roar, stone swallowing stone, and a wall of dust rolled toward them thick as river fog.

"Off the rails! Into the siding!" Marn grabbed Isla again, but this time the dust hit them mid-stride, a hot choking wall that turned the lantern light brown and took the world away. Wren dropped to his knees, coat over his face, breathing through wool, listening to the mountain grind its teeth somewhere above.

When the air thinned enough to see, Isla was coughing against Marn's shoulder, and the second junction — JCT 2, half-buried already — stood to their right, its side gallery intact. Barely. Cracks spiderwebbed across the ceiling plates, and fine grit still rained down in whispers.

"That's not going to hold," Isla wheezed.

"No," Wren agreed. "But it's going to hold longer than the main line." He got his feet under him. "Third junction. How far does the journal say?"

Isla pulled the notebook she'd copied key pages into, her hands shaking so badly she had to brace the paper against her knee. "'Past the third junction, descend by the stair cut, forty-one steps, to the gate chamber.'" She looked up at him, dust streaking her face, eyes red-rimmed and steady anyway. "Forty-one steps, Wren."

"Then we take forty-one steps."

Marn laughed — one short, hard bark with no humor in it at all. "You Fairweathers and your arithmetic."

And that was when the charge went off directly above them.

* * *

There was no thunder this time, no warning groan. Just a single concussive *crack* and the ceiling of the side gallery let go.

Wren saw it in fragments: the timber header splitting, the first slab tipping, Isla frozen in the open with her face turned up, lantern still raised as if the light itself could hold the stone back —

— and Marn hitting her like a pilot diving for a man overboard.

They went down together behind the buried switch stand as the roof came in. The crash was total, absolute, a sound that erased every other sound. Wren was thrown flat, ears ringing, and when he scrambled up through the swirling grit the world had been redrawn: where the gallery had run onward there was now a slope of broken stone, and on top of it, half-buried to the waist, lay Marn.

"MARN!" He was moving before he knew it, digging with bare hands, stones biting his fingers. "Isla — her shoulders — *lift!*"

Isla threw herself at the rock pile, hauling at Marn's arm, her voice cracking on the woman's name. Marn's face was gray under the dust, her lips moving. When Wren cleared enough stone to hear her, what she said was: "Leg. Don't — don't pull the leg. Dig *around* it."

So they dug around it, the three of them and the mountain pressing down, the next charge already rumbling somewhere far off like an approaching storm. It took minutes that lasted hours. When they finally freed her, Marn's left leg hung wrong below the knee, and her breath came in shallow, controlled pulls that Wren recognized from men he'd pulled out of wrecks — the breathing of someone spending pain like coins they couldn't afford.

"Can you walk?" he asked, knowing the answer.

"On a good day," Marn said through her teeth, "with a good leg." She looked at the rubble slope, then at Wren, and something passed over her face that he'd only seen once before, on the river, when she'd talked about her brother. "Get her to the gate. Both of you go."

"We're not leaving you."

"You're not *carrying* me either, boy, not up a collapse slope with the roof coming down. I'll follow. I'm slow, not dead." She gripped his wrist, hard, dust and blood in the creases of her palm. "Wren. Listen to me. Whatever your father built down there — whatever he left unfinished — finish it. That's the only prayer I've got left in me, so make it count."

He wanted to argue. There was no time to argue. Another charge, closer now, and pebbles began to dance on the rubble around them like rain on a drumhead.

Isla took Marn's arm over her shoulders. Between them they got the pilot moving, one hop and drag at a time, up and over the fall, and Wren went ahead with the lantern, testing each stone before he trusted it, hating every second that his back was turned to them and more to the mountain both.

* * *

The stair cut was where the journal promised, behind a false wall of fitted stone that swung when Wren pressed the loose block the surveyor had marked. Forty-one steps, cut clean into living rock, descending in a tight spiral. Wren counted them aloud, Isla echoing him, Marn counting through clenched teeth as they lowered her step by step, and when Wren's boot struck the last one the air changed — cooler, wetter, tasting of mineral and deep water.

And quieter. The explosions had stopped.

For a long moment none of them spoke. The silence was worse than the noise. Wren had worked rail yards his whole adult life; he knew the difference between a storm that had passed and a storm that was reloading. Corliss hadn't given up. Corliss had simply reached the point where the mountain was too solid for quick charges — which meant he'd be bringing up the big ones, the slow ones, the patient ones. Or he'd be finding another way in. There was always another way in. Vell had built this place on the principle that nothing stayed closed forever unless someone kept paying to keep it shut.

The gate chamber opened before them like the inside of a bell.

It was round, vaulted, perhaps sixty feet across, and every surface was dressed stone fitted so precisely that Wren couldn't have slid a knife blade between the blocks. His father's work. He knew it the way he knew his own handwriting — the slight oversize of the keystone, the decorative chamfer on the springer course, habits he'd absorbed at a drafting table before he could write his own name. In the center of the floor, a circular hatch of riveted iron, eight feet across, banded like a barrel top. And set into the wall opposite, a mechanism.

Wren crossed to it slowly, the lantern held high, and stopped breathing.

It was a floodgate control — but unlike any he'd ever touched. A great horizontal wheel of iron and brass, geared through three reductions to a vertical shaft that plunged through the floor toward the hatch below. Around the hub, cast into a brass plate gone green with age, ran an inscription:

**FAIRWEATHER GATE WORKS — PATENT PENDING — E. FAIRWEATHER, ENG'R**

His father's name. His father's gears. His father's careful, honest, overbuilt craftsmanship, the same hand that had drafted the fences and signed the condemnation orders and come home every night smelling of lamp oil and guilt he'd never once spoken aloud.

"Wren?" Isla's voice, very small in the great stone room. "What is it? What does it say?"

He couldn't answer. His throat wouldn't work. Because now he understood — all of it, in one blinding instant, the way a signal drops and the whole line makes sense at once. His father hadn't just built the fence around the headwaters. He'd built the *lock*. And the surveyor's dying words came back to him not as print but as a voice: *the engineer who built the lock built it honest.* Built it honest — meaning built it to be reopened. Meaning every gear, every reduction, every weld in this machine was designed to turn, to *open*, no matter how many years of rust and silence sat on it. His father had spent his life building cages, and somewhere in the middle of it, quietly, without telling anyone, he'd built one door — and put his own name on it like a confession.

"He left it for me," Wren said aloud. His voice cracked on the second word. "He left the whole thing for me. The map, the journal, the compass — it was never Vell's secret to keep and it was never mine to sell. It was a job. An unfinished job, handed down the line like freight." He turned to Isla, and the terror in his chest was still there, still enormous, but it had changed shape somehow, cooled into something with edges, something he could grip. "Help me clear the wheel."

Isla didn't ask questions. She set down the lantern, spat on her palms, and attacked the crusted corrosion with her belt knife while Wren worked the grease cups along the shaft — still threaded, still capped, his father's maintenance schedule stamped inside each lid even after twenty-five years. *Grease quarterly. Exercise annually. Trust the gearing.* Trust the gearing. As if the man had known exactly who would someday be kneeling here, swearing softly in the dark, doing what he was told.

Behind them, at the foot of the stairs, Marn had propped herself against the wall with her ruined leg stretched out, and she was watching Wren work with an expression he'd never seen on her face before. Hope, maybe. Or the memory of hope, worn smooth like river stone.

Above them, faint but unmistakable, came a new sound: boots on stone. Many boots. And a voice, muffled by rock but carrying anyway, cold and unhurried as a ledger line:

"Seal the stair. Bring the long charges. They're not going anywhere — and neither is the water."

Wren looked at the wheel. At his father's name. At Isla's hands already bleeding on the rust.

"Grab hold," he said. "We're opening the river."

Far overhead, muffled through forty-one steps of stone, the first long charge went down the bore with a sound like the end of the world beginning.
