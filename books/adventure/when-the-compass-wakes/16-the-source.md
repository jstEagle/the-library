## 16. The Source

The tunnel ended in a wall of sound.

Wren felt it before he saw it — a pressure in his chest, a tremor climbing through the soles of his boots and up through the bones of his legs, as if the mountain itself had a pulse. The lantern light stretched ahead of them down a gallery of dressed stone, and from somewhere beyond it came a roar like a hundred freight trains held forever at full steam.

"Is that—" Isla began, and her voice was swallowed whole.

Marn, limping on the leg she'd wrenched shielding Isla from the falling rock, put her mouth close to Wren's ear. "The river," she said. "It has to be."

They came around the last bend together, three figures leaning into one another like drunks, and the gallery opened — opened the way a cathedral opens, the way a wound opens — into a cavern so vast that Wren's lantern could not find its ceiling. The light simply gave up, climbing and climbing along walls streaked with mineral white, until the dark above swallowed it. And below, filling the floor of the world, lay the water.

Not moving. That was the horror of it. Wren had spent his life beside rivers; he knew their moods the way he knew timetables. This water did not move. It lay black and glass-still beneath the lantern light, a lake the size of a town, silent except for the thunder pouring down from somewhere to the left — a waterfall the height of a church steeple, plunging out of a fissure in the rock and striking the still surface without so much as a ripple reaching the far shore. The Sable River, being born. Being born and immediately imprisoned.

"There." Marn pointed with her chin.

Along the cavern's eastern wall ran a structure that had no business existing under a mountain: a dam face, concrete and cut granite, eighty feet high if it was an inch, studded with iron walkways and riveted pipe collars thick enough to drive a locomotive through. Great pipes — six of them, seven, Wren lost count — emerged from the dam's flank and bored away into the rock, vanishing toward the north, toward Vell's reservoirs, toward every tap and pump station in the territory. The river fell into its prison pool and was siphoned off pipe by pipe, measured and metered, and whatever meager trickle escaped did so through a grate at the dam's base no wider than a wagon.

The source of the Sable River, behind bars.

Isla stood at the edge of the stone quay with her hand pressed flat against a surveyor's benchmark bolted into the rock, reading the stamped numbers by lantern light. "Meridian Rail Company," she said. "Property mark. There are dozens of them." Her voice had gone strange and flat. "This isn't a secret they kept. It's an asset they inventory."

Wren walked to the dam face and put his palm against the cold concrete. Somewhere inside the structure, water moved under pressure — he could feel it, a hum in the stone like a telegraph wire carrying a message too fast to read. His father had built things like this. His father had built *this*, maybe; the map's final riddle had led them to a floodgate mechanism bearing initials too familiar to be coincidence. E.F. Fairweather, Chief Engineer, Condemnation Survey. He'd seen the signature on Marn's documents a hundred miles downstream, and he'd seen the same hand in the gate housing drawings folded in the prospector's oilskin.

*He built the cage,* Wren thought, *and then he drew the map that leads to the key.*

The prospector — Aldous Reeve, Vell's own surveyor, hunted for what he'd found here — had known. Had died on a night track outside Meridian City pressing that knowledge into a stranger's hands. *Carry it upstream.* Wren had promised a dying man a thing he hadn't understood, and now he stood inside the understanding, and it was enormous, and it was terrible, and it was his.

"We should keep moving," Isla said. "Corliss collapsed the tunnel behind us. He'll have another way in — there's always another way in for men like him."

She was right, and they all knew it, and none of them moved. It was hard to hurry in a place like this. The stillness of the water pulled at them like deep sleep.

Then the lamps came on.

Not all at once — in sequence, bank after bank of electric globes strung along the dam walkways and the cavern ceiling's lower scaffolds, each one flaring with a soft pop, until the whole buried sea lay lit in a harsh white glare that erased every shadow and left the three of them blinking like moles. Wren's hand went to the wrench at his belt before his mind caught up. Useless. Against this, everything was useless.

A figure stood at the center of the main catwalk, eighty feet up, silhouetted against the lights behind him. He didn't shout. He didn't need to. In a cavern that quiet, a normal voice carried like a bell.

"Mr. Fairweather." Corliss. "You're standing on company property."

* * *

They gathered at the base of the access stair because Corliss asked them to, and because Marn's leg was bad and Isla's hands were shaking and Wren's own knees felt borrowed. Corliss descended alone. Behind him on the catwalk, four company men in gray stood with carbines cradled easy and professional, making no show of aiming, which was somehow worse than aiming.

Up close, Corliss looked tired. Wren had imagined him a hundred times since Kestrel Bend — had cast him in nightmares as something huge and pitiless — and the reality was a lean man of fifty-odd with dust on his coat and a healing cut across his knuckles and eyes the color of the water in the pool: dark, still, and very deep.

"You collapsed the tunnel," Wren said.

"I sealed a route I didn't want used twice." Corliss glanced back the way they'd come. "The charge was set to warn, not to bury. If I wanted you dead, Mr. Fairweather, you'd have died in Bellhaven, or on the river, or in the gorge. You've cost me three weeks and two good boats." He said it without heat, the way a man reports weather. "I'd prefer not to spend more."

"You burned the Halloran homestead," Marn said. Her voice cracked on it. "The Hallorans had nothing. A girl of nine was in that house."

"The Hallorans told me where you crossed the shallows, and they are alive and compensated, and the fire was contained to the barn." Corliss turned those still eyes on her. "I don't enjoy that work, Mrs.—pilot Marn. I do it because the alternative is worse. That is the entire truth of my life, and I offer it to you freely, because you've earned honesty if anyone has."

Marn spat at his feet. Corliss accepted it without expression.

He turned back to Wren, and from inside his coat he drew not a weapon but a ledger — a slim, leather-bound book that Wren recognized with a lurch in his stomach, because he'd seen its twin on every collector's desk in Meridian City. The debt books. Red ink. Names and sums and the interest that grew while a man slept.

"Wren Fairweather," Corliss read, though Wren was certain he knew it by heart. "Principal outstanding to the Meridian Rail Company: four hundred and twelve dollars, nine cents, as of the first of the month. Accruing at six percent. Secured against your tools, your lodging bond, and your father's estate — such as it is." He closed the book. "I'm authorized to strike that entry. All of it. Tonight."

The words went through Wren like current through a wire.

Four hundred dollars. He had carried that number so long it had worn a groove in him, the way water wears stone. Four hundred dollars meant the collectors' fists, the eviction notices, the shame of walking past the company office with his hat down. Four hundred dollars was the reason he'd been working the night shift on dead lines when a bleeding man stumbled out of the dark and handed him a map. Every bad road of the last year bent back to that number.

And Corliss was offering to erase it with a word.

"For the map," Wren said. Not a question.

"For the map, and for silence going forward. You take your crew, you go back down the valley, you open a shop or take a pension or drink yourself stupid, I don't much care. You never speak of this place. In exchange: clean slate, cash passage, and my personal word — which, whatever else you think of me, has never been sold." Corliss paused. "There's more."

Of course there was more. Wren waited.

"Vell wants the map. Vell does not want *you*. But I've watched you work since Kestrel Bend — the switch at the spur, the way you read water, the way people follow you without deciding to. That's rarer than you know, and worth more than you imagine." Corliss's voice dropped half a register, and the seduction in it was all the more potent for being quiet. "Clear your debts, take a salary that would embarrass a bank president, and build. Gates, dams, pump stations, the whole circulatory system of the territory. Your father helped design this place, Mr. Fairweather. There's a desk upstairs with his name still penciled on the back of the chair. I could have it carved instead."

Behind Wren, Isla made a small sound — not a word, just a note of alarm, a telegrapher's tick.

Wren looked at the desk that wasn't there. He looked at the still black water, and the pipes drinking it, and the grate at the dam's base letting through a thread of river thin as a lie.

*Order,* Corliss would say. *Someone must hold the pen.* And God help him, standing in the glare of all those electric lights with his debts humming in his ears like a live rail, some exhausted corner of Wren wanted to believe it. Wanted to believe that a man could hold the pen and write gently. That his father had believed exactly that, once, standing in this same cavern with these same lights — and look what the believing had built. A drowned valley. Three towns under the reservoirs. Marn's brother gone into the dark looking for answers and never coming home.

His father had taken the desk. His father had carved his initials into the gate housing and signed the condemnation papers and gone home every night telling himself he was protecting the land, and the land had drowned anyway.

"No," Wren said.

Corliss didn't move. "Think—"

"I've thought about nothing else since the night a dying man bled on my tracks." Wren heard his own voice come out steady, and was grateful for it, because inside he was shaking like a signal lamp in wind. "You're offering me my father's chair. I know how it feels to sit in. I decline."

Something flickered across Corliss's face — regret, Wren thought, actual regret, which was the most frightening thing yet. "That's a pity," Corliss said. "Because the offer expires when I leave this stair, and I am not authorized to make it twice." He raised two fingers toward the catwalk, and the four carbines came up in one smooth motion, no longer casual at all. "So we arrive at the other conversation. The map, Mr. Fairweather. Or the water finds your crew the way it found the towns."

Marn shifted her weight off her bad leg and Wren saw her hand drift toward the boat hook lashed to her pack. Isla had gone very pale but very still, the stillness of someone calculating.

And Wren — Wren felt something settle in him, calm and cold and clear as the pool itself. Because he'd been cornered his whole adult life, and cornered men grab for exits. But he wasn't cornered. He was standing in front of the largest body of pressurized water in the territory, next to a floodgate his father had designed, holding a map whose final riddle he'd already solved on the climb through the gorge.

Corliss thought the map was the treasure. Corliss thought paper was power.

"Corliss," Wren said pleasantly, "do you know what happens to a headgate when the counterweight linkage fails?"

The enforcer's eyes narrowed. "What?"

"It doesn't fail," Wren said. "It falls. Eighty feet of iron, straight down into the valve housing. My father built the safety into it — a dead-man's release, in case anyone ever tried to hold the water hostage. He couldn't stop the drownings, so he left a door." Wren took the oilskin map from inside his coat, slowly, and watched every carbine track it. "You want the map. Here's the trouble. The map isn't the key. The map is just instructions for the key — and I've had three weeks and a very long river to memorize it."

He let the oilskin fall open in his hands, lantern light glinting off the old ink, and smiled the first honest smile he'd smiled in a year.

"So," Wren said. "Let's talk about what *you're* willing to trade."
