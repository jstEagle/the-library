## 18. Let the Water Speak

The cavern opened around them like the throat of the world.

Wren had imagined the source a hundred times since the prospector died in his arms — imagined a spring, maybe, or a mountain lake, something small and secret. What he saw instead stole the breath from his lungs and left him gripping the rusted rail of the tunnel's last gallery like a man holding onto a dream he was afraid to wake from.

The Sable River was born in a cathedral of stone. A vaulted ceiling arched a hundred feet overhead, hung with flowstone that caught the light of their lanterns and threw it back in veins of gold and copper. And beneath that ceiling, filling half the cavern floor, lay water — black, still, and deep beyond reckoning, so calm it mirrored the stone above until Wren could not tell which way was down. The cistern. Vell's cistern. Every drop the mountains gave, gathered here behind a wall of dressed granite and iron, held prisoner in the dark.

Pipes as wide as rail tunnels ran from the dam's face into the eastern rock — Vell's arteries, carrying the river's blood away to his reservoirs while the valley towns downstream drank dust and paid for every cupful.

"God," Isla whispered beside him. Her hand found his sleeve without her seeming to notice she'd reached for it. "It's been sitting here all this time. All this water, just — *sitting*."

"Not sitting," Marn said. She leaned against the gallery wall, one arm pressed hard across her ribs where the falling timber had caught her. Her face was gray with pain, but her eyes were bright and terrible. "Waiting."

Ahead, a lattice of iron catwalks spanned the cistern toward the gatehouse built into the dam itself — a squat block of masonry with a wheel-house on top, and above it, cut into the living rock in letters meant to be read by lantern light:

**MERIDIAN RAIL & WATER CO. — PRIVATE WORKS — TRESPASSERS WILL BE PROSECUTED**

Below the company's letters, older and shallower, someone had chiseled four words and then, apparently, tried to chisel them out again. Wren squinted through the gloom. He knew that hand. He'd know it anywhere — he'd watched it write out his lessons at the kitchen table, steady and careful, an engineer's hand.

*LET THE WATER SPEAK.*

His father's words. Half-erased by his father's own chisel, or someone else's order. Wren's throat closed.

"Wren." Isla touched his arm again, sharper this time. "Listen."

He heard it. Boots on stone, echoing up from the gallery behind them — not running, but coming, unhurried, the walk of a man who believed the outcome was already settled. Corliss. He'd been herding them since the first collapse, letting them do the work of finding the way in, and now they had led him to the very heart of everything.

Marn pushed off the wall and stood straight despite what it cost her. "The winch," she said. "In the gatehouse. If the gates are anything like the lockworks on the lower river, there'll be a manual windlass — counterweights, chain drive. It'll take two people and it will fight you the whole way."

"And the mechanism?" Wren asked.

Isla was already digging in her satchel, pulling out wire, pliers, the little brass contact-blocks she carried the way other women carried hairpins. "If your father designed the floodgate, he didn't design it for company men. Engineers who build things they're ashamed of leave themselves a back door." She looked up at him, and in the lantern light her eyes were fierce. "You told me he taught you everything. So did he teach you this?"

Wren looked at the catwalks, at the gatehouse, at his father's half-murdered inscription. Somewhere below them, the boots came on.

"He taught me," Wren said, "that a machine is a promise you make to whoever comes after you. You build it so it can be understood." He slung the map case across his chest and buckled it tight. "Isla — the wiring runs along the east wall to the wheel-house. I saw the conduit when we came in. Old cloth-insulated line, probably dead for twenty years, but the copper will still be copper."

"I can make it sing," she said.

"Marn—"

"Don't." Marn's voice cracked like a whip. "Don't tell me to stay behind. I've spent eleven years watching that river run thin past towns that used to have mills. I'm holding that winch if I have to hold it with my teeth."

For a moment none of them moved. Then Wren nodded, once, and it was done — three people, one promise, no more words needed.

They went out onto the catwalks together.

* * *

The iron rang under their boots, a low tolling note that rolled out over the black water and came back changed. Halfway across, Wren risked a look down and immediately wished he hadn't: the cistern was deeper than it had seemed from the shore, fathomless, cold breathing up between the grating bars like the exhalation of something sleeping.

Then the far gallery lit with lantern-glow, and Corliss stepped onto the near end of the catwalk.

He was alone. Of course he was alone; men like Corliss traveled light because they never expected to need help. His coat was dusty from the climb, his hat square on his head, and in his right hand — unhurried, almost casual — hung a revolver the size of a sermon.

"Mr. Fairweather." His voice carried easily across the water, flat and reasonable. "You've saved me a great deal of walking. I want you to know I appreciate that."

Wren stopped, ten yards out on the swaying iron, and put himself between Corliss and the others. Behind him he heard Isla's soft footsteps break away toward the east conduit, heard Marn's heavier tread continue toward the gatehouse. Good. Let him look at me.

"You followed us through a collapsing tunnel," Wren called back. "That's appreciation?"

"I followed the plan." Corliss began to walk forward, and the catwalk swung gently with his weight, pendulum-slow. "Mr. Vell is a thorough man. He assumed from the beginning that the prospector would pass his map to someone before he died — they always do, dying men, they always want to hand their burden to somebody younger. So we simply waited to see who would pick it up." He stopped, ten feet away now, close enough that Wren could see the powder-burn freckling his cheek. "And here you are. An engineer. In debt to the company. Do you understand how neatly that fits? It's almost as though the map chose you on purpose."

"It chose wrong."

"No," Corliss said, and there was something almost gentle in it, which was the worst thing yet. "It chose exactly right. Because you're going to give it to me, Mr. Fairweather. Not because I'll shoot you — shooting you creates paperwork, and I dislike paperwork. But because I'm going to stand here and explain to you, calmly, the way one professional explains things to another, why giving me that map is the only sane thing left to do. And then you're going to weigh it. Men always weigh it."

Behind Wren, faintly, he heard metal on metal — Marn reaching the winch, testing the chain. Corliss heard it too. His eyes flicked past Wren's shoulder, and for the first time something moved behind the flat calm of his face.

"The woman is injured," he said. "She'll tire. The girl is playing with wires that haven't carried current since before she was born. And you—" his gaze came back, "—you're standing on my bridge, over my water, inside my mountain. Whatever you think you're about to do, Mr. Fairweather, the arithmetic doesn't favor it."

"Arithmetic," Wren repeated. His hand found the lever housing at the catwalk's rail — a maintenance brake, his fingers reading it blind the way his father had taught him to read any machine, by feel, by logic, by trust. "My father did the arithmetic on this place. Did you know that? He surveyed these galleries. He helped seal them. And then he spent the rest of his life teaching his son that water belongs to the ground it falls on." He tightened his grip. "You want to talk about what's sane? Ask the valley towns whether it's sane to pipe a river into one man's reservoir while their wells go dry."

"The valley towns exist," Corliss said, "because the company permits them to exist. Order isn't cruelty, Fairweather. Order is the only thing standing between ten thousand people and each other's throats. One spring, controlled, means everyone gets a measured share. One spring, freed, means the strong take everything and the rest fight over mud. I don't enjoy what I do. I do what keeps the ledger balanced." He raised the revolver slightly. "Now. The map. Or we find out which of us floats."

Wren smiled, and was surprised to find the smile was real.

"You're too late," he said. "I already gave it away."

Corliss's brow creased — one heartbeat of genuine confusion — and in that heartbeat Wren slammed the maintenance brake open, dropped his whole weight onto the catwalk, and charged.

* * *

The bridge fought both of them. Iron plates slick with condensation, sway and rebound, the black water waiting below like a held breath. Corliss fired once and the shot went wide, screaming off the cavern wall, and then they closed and it was fists and boots and the terrible ringing of the rails.

Corliss was stronger. Wren felt it at once, in the grip that seized his collar and drove him back along the catwalk, in the knee that hammered into his thigh. But Corliss fought like a man executing a procedure, and Wren — Wren had spent ten years wrestling handcars, couplers, and runaway freight on night shifts when nobody else would take them. He knew how to fall. He knew how to let a machine's own weight become his weapon.

He went down deliberately when Corliss threw him, caught the rail, and swung both boots into the back of the enforcer's knee. Corliss staggered. Wren came up under him and drove him against the railing, and for one suspended moment they were chest to chest, eye to eye, the revolver pinned useless between them, the cistern breathing cold beneath their feet.

"Your ledger," Wren gasped, "is about to flood."

Across the cavern, a spark leapt.

He saw it in the corner of his eye — a blue-white kiss of light where the east conduit met the wheel-house, then another, then a stuttering string of them marching along the old line like signal lamps being lit down a dark track. Isla. She was making the dead wire sing. And from the gatehouse came a sound Wren felt in his teeth before he heard it: chain, taking load. Groaning link by groaning link. Marn, wounded, alone, hauling against twenty years of rust and silence.

Corliss heard it too. Something broke loose in his face then — not fear exactly, but the fury of a man watching order come apart at the seams. He twisted free of Wren's grip and brought the revolver around, and Wren did the only thing left: he grabbed the enforcer's gun-arm with both hands and threw them both sideways off the catwalk's centerline, out over the water, hanging by the rail while the shot cracked past his ear and the world tilted.

They wrestled on the edge. Below them the black mirror waited. Corliss's boot heel skidded on wet iron; Wren's shoulder screamed; somewhere far away Isla was shouting his name, and Marn was shouting something else entirely, wordless, the raw sound of a woman pulling a river's weight through her ruined ribs.

"*Let go!*" Isla's voice, cracking across the water. "Wren — the contacts are live — throw it NOW!"

The lever. His father's lever. It stood in its housing six feet away, a plain iron handle worn smooth at the grip by a hand twenty years gone, and between Wren and that handle lay Corliss, and between Corliss and the water lay nothing at all.

Time went strange. Wren saw it all in one long instant: the prospector bleeding out on the midnight tracks, pressing oilskin into his hands — *carry it upstream, carry it upstream*; his father at the kitchen table, drawing gate mechanisms in the margins of his letters, always in the margins, always hiding the truth in plain sight; Marn's brother's name spoken over a grave that wasn't there; Bellhaven's old woman spitting at his feet; and Isla, on the first night, saying *we have the same enemies*, and meaning *we could have the same future*.

Corliss saw where he was looking. "Fairweather," he said, and for the first time the flat voice bent, "don't. You don't know what you'll drown. The records, the reservoirs, the towns below — you'll loose it all at once, there's no controlling—"

"That," Wren said, "is rather the point."

He wrenched free of Corliss's grip, dove the six feet, and threw the lever his father built.

* * *

Nothing happened.

One full second of nothing — the longest second of Wren's life — and then, deep in the dam's bones, something vast turned over in its sleep. A counterweight fell. A chain woke. And the gates began to move.

The sound was not loud at first. It was a grinding, a groaning, a complaint of iron that had waited twenty years to speak — and then the seals broke, and the first water came through in a rope as thick as a house, black in the lantern light, hitting the old channel below the dam with a concussion that knocked Wren flat on the catwalk. The whole structure shuddered. Dust rained from the ceiling. And the rope of water became a torrent became a *river*, roaring down the ancient channels his father had mapped and sealed, tearing the silence apart after twenty years, going where it had always meant to go.

Downstream. Toward Kestrel Bend. Toward the drowned towns' dry beds. Toward Bellhaven. Toward the valley.

Wren pulled himself up the catwalk rail and stood in the spray, soaked to the skin, laughing and weeping and unable to tell which was which. The cistern was dropping — he could see it, the black mirror sinking inch by inch against the dam face, uncovering the drowned foundations of the old natural channel, the river's true bed emerging into the light for the first time in a generation.

Movement at the edge of his vision. Corliss, on his feet at the far end of the catwalk, revolver raised, water spraying around his boots.

Wren didn't move. There was nowhere to move to, and nothing left worth protecting with a lie. "Go ahead," he said. "It's done. You'd be shooting a man who already got what he came for."

Corliss's finger whitened on the trigger. The roar of the river filled the world.

And then the enforcer lowered the gun — slowly, mechanically, like a machine whose instruction set had failed — and stood looking out over the rising flood spilling down the old channels, and his face, for the first time, was not flat at all. It was empty. A man watching the ledger burn and finding, perhaps, that he could not remember why the numbers had ever mattered.

"The towns," he said, barely audible under the water. "There's no record of the water rights now. No survey. No claim. Do you understand what you've done? There's no *order* anymore. Only weather."

"Only weather," Wren agreed. "And rain falls on everybody."

Corliss looked at him a moment longer. Then he turned, coat streaming, and walked away up the far gallery into the dark, and the river's voice swallowed the sound of his footsteps whole.

* * *

They met at the gatehouse door — the three of them, drenched, battered, alive. Marn was leaning on the winch frame with both hands, her side dark with fresh blood, her face lit like a girl's. Isla threw her arms around Wren before he'd fully cleared the doorway, and he held on, feeling her laugh shake through her, feeling his own ribs ache with it.

"You rewired a dead line," he said into her hair, "under fire, in the dark."

"You distracted a murderer," she said, "by getting thrown off a bridge."

"Team effort," Marn rasped, and slid down the winch frame to sit, laughing until she winced.

Above them, through the wheel-house window, the cavern blazed with reflected light as the last of the cistern gave itself to the channels below. Wren looked out at the water — *his* water, *their* water, the valley's water — running free at last down the lines his father had drawn and hidden and, in the end, left unlocked for his son to find.

*Carry it upstream,* the prospector had said. Well. He'd carried it further than upstream. He'd carried it home.

Then Isla stiffened in his arms, her ear tilting toward the tunnel mouth, toward the distant thunder rolling up from the valley floor — and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, another sound. Bells. Church bells, miles away, ringing wild and unbroken.

Bellhaven had heard the river coming.

"They'll be at the washhouse by dawn," Isla breathed. "The flood's carrying everything out of Vell's records vault — every claim, every deed, every lie — washing it all down into the light where anyone can read it."

Wren took the oilskin map case from his chest, held it a moment, and handed it to Marn — the journal inside, the proof, the truth his father had buried and the prospector had died carrying. "Then we'd better get it downstream," he said, "before the water beats us there."

Marn grinned through the blood on her lip, and shouldered the case, and the three of them turned toward the draining tunnels and the long road down into the valley — as the Sable River, born again, roared on ahead of them through the dark, going where it would, speaking at last to everyone who had forgotten its voice.
