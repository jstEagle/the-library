## 20. Vell's Reckoning

Marn had spent her whole life learning to read water, and so she was the first in Bellhaven to understand what the flood meant.

The others heard it as noise — the roar coming down the valley two days after Wren threw the lever, a sound like the mountain clearing its throat. They came out of their houses and stood in the lanes with their faces turned toward the old channels, listening the way you listen to thunder. But Marn stood on the porch of the washhouse with her arm still bound tight against her ribs and felt it through the soles of her boots, through the ground itself, and she knew: that was not floodwater. That was *return*. The river coming home by every road it had ever been made to forget.

The Sable had run thin through Bellhaven for eleven years. Thin enough that the town's wells had gone brackish, that the gardens on the east slope had been abandoned, that children born there had never seen the river stand higher than a man's knee. Now it came down the old channel past the tannery ruins in a brown-and-green wall, carrying branches and silt and the smell of snowmelt from high country no one in Bellhaven had touched in a decade, and it filled the channel to its old banks in an hour, and then it kept rising, gentle and inexorable, until it lapped at the stones of the bridge that hadn't seen water under it since Marn was a girl.

Old Tam Voss waded into it up to his thighs and just stood there, hatless, letting it push against him. Nobody laughed at him. Half the town followed him in.

Marn stayed dry. She had her reasons. Her shoulder was a ruin of bruise and pulled tendon where the falling timber had caught her, and besides — someone had to think about what came next, and it was becoming clear that in this company of three, thinking about what came next was going to be her job.

* * *

The journal changed everything, but not all at once, and not the way Isla had hoped.

Isla wanted to read it aloud in the hall tower, page by page, to the whole town — Vell's own surveyor, writing in his own hand, describing the spring system, the aquifers, the plan to pipe the water south and sell it back to the people it belonged to. She'd sat up half the night copying pages in her careful telegraphist's script while Wren slept like a man felled and Marn dozed in a chair with her bad arm propped on a cushion, waking every time the fire popped.

But when Isla brought the copies to the town council — six elders who met in the back of the grange because Bellhaven officially did not exist and therefore could not officially govern itself — they read in silence for a long time, and then the eldest, a woman named Petra Halloway with hands like tree roots, set the pages down and said:

"You're asking us to rise up."

"I'm asking you to know," Isla said. "What you do with knowing is yours."

"It's not ours," Petra said. "That's the trouble with knowing things. It makes them yours whether you want them or not." She looked around the table. "The pump stations. There are four between here and Kestrel Bend. Company men at each one, armed, with orders we can only guess at. And now the river's come back and every one of those stations is sitting on water we can hear from our doorsteps."

"They'll try to close the gates again," Marn said from the doorway. She hadn't been invited in; she came in anyway. "You should assume that. Vell didn't build an empire of pipes to let it drain out because a town full of squatters got sentimental. He'll have men moving tonight, if he's got any sense."

"And if he hasn't?"

"Then he'll hire some."

Petra looked at her a long moment. "You're the pilot. Marn. The one they barred."

"I'm the one they barred."

"My husband ran cargo with you, before. He said you were the only honest man on the river, and that you'd have starved honest too if your brother hadn't fed you." Something moved behind her eyes. "He said your brother went upriver and never came down."

"He did," Marn said. "And I've stopped waiting for him to come down. I've started finishing what he went up to do."

The council talked until the lamp burned low. In the end it wasn't the journal that decided them — it was the sound of the river, audible through the grange walls, running full and loud and free, a sound none of them had heard in eleven years and none of them could bear to lose twice.

By dawn, Bellhaven had stopped being a town that wasn't there.

* * *

They took the first pump station without a shot fired, which surprised everyone including the men inside it.

It was Marn's idea, and it was simple: don't come as a mob, come as a work crew. Forty settlers with tools on their shoulders, walking up the valley road in the gray light like they were going to clear brush. The station was a squat stone building astride the new channel, with a pipe head the size of a wagon wheel and a company flag nobody had ever bothered to take down. Two guards on the door, young, bored, badly paid.

Wren walked at the front of the crew, and Marn watched him do something she wouldn't have believed of him a month ago: he smiled at the guards. Friendly. Tired. A man with a wrench over his shoulder and a grievance about a jammed gate.

"Morning," he said. "We've come about the intake. It's fouled with debris from the flood — whole town's drinking silty water. You'll want to open the maintenance hatch before it clogs the works entirely."

The younger guard actually started to nod. The older one squinted. "You're not company."

"No," Wren agreed pleasantly. "We're the people whose water runs through your pipes. Which makes us, by my arithmetic, your employers."

There was a long moment where everything could have gone wrong. Marn had her hand inside her coat the whole time, on the grip of the pistol she'd carried upriver and never once had to use, and she watched the older guard weigh it — two of them, forty of the townsfolk, and behind the townsfolk the sound of a river that had already decided whose side it was on.

The older guard unslung his rifle, leaned it against the doorframe, and held out his hand for the wrench.

"Show me this hatch," he said.

After that it went fast. The second station surrendered when word reached it; the third locked its doors and the settlers simply built a bypass around it in a day, Wren directing the pipe work with a chalk stub and a fury of competence that made Marn grin despite her ribs. The fourth fought — one enforcer, a hard man named Grieves whom Marn remembered from the smuggling years, put two rounds through a door and grazed a boy's ear before the crowd took the building off him almost gently, which somehow was worse than if they hadn't. They tied him to a chair in his own station and left him a jug of water drawn straight from the reopened channel, and told him to drink deep, because it was the last thing in the territory he'd control.

Four stations. Four days. No dead.

Marn kept waiting for the other thing. The spectacle. The battle. She'd grown up on stories where justice arrived with gunfire and a burning building, and part of her — the part that had spent eleven years nursing a grief shaped exactly like a bullet hole — had expected to pay for her brother in blood.

Instead she stood on the catwalk of the third station watching Wren and a dozen farmers sweat over a valve wheel, arguing cheerfully about thread pitch, and understood that this was what justice looked like when it actually won: not a blaze, but a workday. Men turning wheels. Water going where it should. The ordinary world, repaired.

Her brother would have hated how undramatic it was. He'd have loved that it worked.

* * *

Word came down the wire on the sixth day.

Isla had strung a line from the grange to the first station within forty-eight hours of taking it — of course she had; give that girl a spool of copper and a free afternoon and she'd have the whole valley talking to itself by dark — and it was Isla who sat at the key when the message clicked through from a sympathetic operator in Meridian City, a woman she'd trained herself before she ran, sending in the clear because there was no longer anyone with the power to punish her for it.

Marn was in the grange yard, splitting kindling one-handed, when Isla came out. She knew before she saw Isla's face. You learned to read people the way you read water, and Isla's face was running high and fast in every direction at once.

"It's Vell," Isla said. "He's gone."

"Gone where?"

"Gone. Fled. Last night, ahead of the news — he must have known what was coming before we did. His house in Meridian is empty. His office stripped. The board of directors convened an emergency session this morning and found nothing to direct, because the man who owned the majority shares has resigned by telegram from somewhere they can't trace." Isla laughed, a strange breathless sound. "Marn, they're saying he crossed the southern pass with two wagons and a hired escort. They're saying he left his own chief engineer holding unpaid wages. They're saying—"

She stopped. Marn set down the axe.

"They're saying what?"

"That the empire's worthless." Isla's voice dropped, went wondering. "All those pipes, all those reservoirs, all that paper — deeds and claims and water rights filed in six counties — and none of it means anything without the source. He didn't just lose the spring. He lost the only thing that made the rest of it real. The company's still standing, technically. It's just standing over nothing. Like a fence around a field that turns out never to have been his field at all."

Marn looked north, up the valley, toward the mountain she could not see from here but could feel, the way you feel weather coming. Somewhere up there was the cavern where Wren had thrown his father's lever, where Corliss had — but that was Wren's story to tell, and he told it badly and briefly and only when asked directly, and she'd learned not to press. What mattered was what came down from it: the water, and the silence where Vell's name used to be.

Eleven years she had imagined this. In the worst imaginings, Vell died badly. In the best ones, he was dragged before the valley in chains and made to look at every drowned steeple in the reservoirs, made to say the names of the towns he'd flooded — Lowmere, Ashford, and the little one whose name Marn's brother had written at the top of every letter home until the letters stopped.

None of her imaginings had included him simply running. Packing his wagons in the night like a thief leaving a rented room, and the great machine of Meridian Rail grinding on without him, its directors suddenly very interested in negotiating settlements, in compensating displaced families, in *moving forward*, as though they hadn't spent a decade doing precisely what he'd told them to.

But standing there in the yard with wood chips on her boots, Marn found she didn't need the chains. Justice as spectacle would have been for her — for her rage, her hunger, the eleven hollow years. This was something else. This was consequence: a man discovering that everything he'd built rested on stolen water, and that water, given back, takes everything else with it when it goes.

"He'll be back," she said finally. "Men like that always think the story isn't finished unless they get the last line."

"Let him come back," Isla said, and there was steel in it that hadn't been there on the night she'd knocked on Wren's door with a warning and a price. "There's a whole valley here now that knows what he took and knows its own strength. Let him come back to *this*."

* * *

That night, Marn climbed to the roof of the washhouse.

It hurt to climb. Everything hurt; the doctor Petra sent for had pronounced her shoulder bruised to the bone and her ribs cracked and had prescribed rest with the weary tone of a man who could see she wouldn't take it. But some things you do hurt, and this was one.

She carried the lantern up with her good arm. It was a river lantern, brass and glass, built to burn in wind — her brother's, recovered from his kit along the journey, the one object of his she'd carried for eleven years without ever finding the right place or time to light it. She'd meant to light it at the source. Then she'd meant to light it at the gorge where the boat broke. Then at the tunnel mouth. There had always been a reason to wait, and she understood now that the reasons had been fear wearing practicality's clothes: as long as the lantern stayed dark, her brother's story stayed unfinished, and unfinished stories are easier to carry than ended ones.

But it was finished now. Not the way she'd written it — he hadn't come down the river, and she hadn't dragged the truth out of the mountain in a single blazing act of vengeance. It had finished the way rivers finish things: slowly, sideways, by wearing away what could not be worn away forever.

Below her, Bellhaven glowed. Every window lit — the town had never dared waste oil like this before, but tonight nobody counted. Down the valley, the four pump stations showed as small steady points, manned now by settler crews, their pipes running open. The river ran loud enough to hear from the roof, a sound Marn thought she could listen to for the rest of her life and die unsatisfied, wanting more of it.

She struck the match. Cupped it. Touched it to the wick.

The lantern bloomed warm yellow behind its glass, and Marn held it up, out over the valley, the way you'd hold a light for someone coming home late across dark water.

"Ashford," she said — the drowned town, his town, the name at the top of every letter. "Lowmere. All of them. Listen." The river roared below, patient and immense. "It remembers you. It never forgot. We just couldn't hear it over the machinery." Her voice caught, and she let it. "You were right, Tam. About all of it. About the spring, about Vell, about the letters. You were right, and it cost you everything, and today it cost *him* everything instead, and I don't know if that's justice but it's what happened, and I lit the lantern, brother. I promised, and I lit it."

Wind off the river made the flame lean and steady itself. Marn sat down on the ridge of the roof with the lantern beside her, and for a long time she didn't say anything at all, and it was the best silence of her life.

Footsteps on the ladder — careful ones, favoring a bad shoulder of their own. Wren's head came up over the eaves, then Isla's behind him, neither of them saying anything about how obvious it was that they'd followed her, both of them knowing better than to speak first.

Wren settled onto the shingles beside her. In his hands, wrapped in cloth, he carried the compass — his father's compass, the one from the prospector's kit, the needle that had spun useless for weeks and finally, in the last days, settled.

"It's pointing somewhere," he said quietly. "Steady, now. Northeast, up-valley. Toward the source." He turned it so she could see the needle trembling faintly, holding true. "I keep thinking — maybe it always pointed there. Maybe it was just waiting for the rest of us."

Marn looked at the needle, and at the lantern burning between them, and at the valley running with light and water all the way to the horizon.

"Spring's coming," she said. "High country will open in six weeks. There are towns up there that haven't heard the news. There are records in Meridian that need reading, names that need saying, and a company full of directors who'll try to negotiate their way back in if we let them." She reached over and steadied the compass in Wren's palm, closing his fingers around it. "So. Where the lines end — where do we go next?"

Isla laughed from her other side, and the lantern burned, and far up the dark valley the river answered, running free toward morning.
