## 22. Homecoming Water

The spring came up the valley like a rumor that refused to die.

Wren Fairweather stood on the rebuilt platform at Bellhaven and watched it arrive — not in the trains or the wagons, but in the ground itself. The willows along the Sable's new course had gone from gray to green almost overnight, as if they'd been holding their breath all winter and had finally been given permission to exhale. The river ran high and clean past the town, loud enough to hear through the walls of the depot, carrying snowmelt down channels that had been dry for twenty years. Down where the drowned towns used to be, the water was finding its old beds again, pooling in cellar-holes and orchard furrows, filling the world back in.

He'd been an engineer long enough to know that water always found the lowest place. He just hadn't known, until this year, that people did too — that given time and a little honesty, they drained toward each other.

"Fairweather." Isla's voice came over his shoulder, bright with the particular satisfaction she reserved for finished work. "Come see what I built."

She led him along the platform to the new pole line — fresh-cut cedar, tarred wire, glass insulators still white with sawdust — strung from Bellhaven east toward the pass and west toward Kestrel Bend, where the toll station stood empty now, its inspector's desk repurposed as a schoolhouse. Isla walked beneath her own handiwork the way other people walked under cathedral ceilings.

"Forty-one miles," she said. "Every pole set by hand. Mrs. Aldous fed me for a week and I never want to eat another of her biscuits as long as I live, but the line is *true*, Wren. First message went out this morning."

"What'd you send?"

She grinned, and there was still something of the girl on the wire about her — the operator who'd tapped out warnings in the dark because someone had to. "Weather report. To nobody. Just because I could." She tapped the nearest pole twice, affectionately, like patting a horse. "First free wire in the territory. No company cipher, no Vell routing codes. If somebody downstream wants to say the river's rising, they can say it plain."

"And if somebody upstream wants to lie?"

"Then everybody gets to hear them do it." She shrugged. "That's the whole trick of it. Sunlight on the wire."

Down at the landing, a crowd had gathered, and Wren could hear Marn's voice cutting through it — that low river-pilot's drawl that made even instructions sound like a dare. He and Isla drifted down the slope with the rest, and there she was: Marn of the Shallows, standing on the deck of a flat-bottomed ferry that hadn't existed three months ago, wearing a pilot's coat with the company brand cut off the shoulder and the raw edge left visible on purpose.

The ferry was called the *Lantern*. Everyone in Bellhaven knew why. Nobody said it out loud, which somehow made it louder.

"She draws eighteen inches empty," Marn was telling the crowd, one boot up on the rail. "Twenty-two loaded. She'll run Bellhaven to the Bend twice a day once the spring surge settles, and she'll run it whether or not anybody signs my papers, because as far as I'm concerned the river already signed them." She spat over the side, ceremonially, into the current. "Fares are whatever you've got. If what you've got is nothing, then nothing it is. All aboard means all aboard."

A cheer went up, and Marn caught Wren's eye across the heads of the crowd and rolled her eyes at the sentiment of it all, but she was smiling when she did it — the crooked smile he'd first seen on the shingle where the rails ended, when she'd told him she'd take them upstream as far as the Gorge and not one mile further, and had then taken them every mile after.

Her arm was healed now. The scar where the rockfall had caught her in the tunnel ran from elbow to wrist, and she'd stopped hiding it somewhere around the time the settlers took the pump stations. She wore it like a route map.

"You two going to stand there admiring the view," she called, "or are you getting aboard? We're making the morning run."

"We're not going anywhere yet," Wren said.

Marn's eyebrows went up. Isla looked at him too, and Wren realized both of them had noticed what he'd only just admitted to himself: he was dressed for travel, pack on his shoulder, and he was pointed upstream.

"The source," he said. "I promised myself I'd go back when the ice let go. There's—" He stopped, started again. "There's work up there. The gate mechanism needs proper maintenance, not just whatever I could rig in the dark with Corliss shooting at us. And the surveyor's camp should be brought down respectful. And I want—" He exhaled. "I want to see it in spring. I've only seen it dying and I've only seen it breaking loose. I want to see it just *being*."

Marn looked at Isla. Isla looked at Marn. It was the look of two people conducting an entire conversation in half a second, a skill they'd perfected somewhere between the rapids and the burning homesteads, and Wren had learned to recognize it and mostly stop being offended by it.

"Well," Marn said. "Somebody's got to hold the winch."

"Somebody's got to check the relay at the gorge," Isla said, already pulling her gloves on. "Purely professional interest."

"That's the spirit."

They took the *Lantern* upriver, because the water went there now. That was the thing Wren kept having to relearn: the old channels were open, the dams were down, and the Sable ran the whole length of the valley again, so the journey that had taken them weeks of sabotage and forged papers and near-death now took four days of easy current and Marn narrating landmarks like a tour guide who'd spent years memorizing a country no one was allowed to visit.

"There," she said, pointing at a flooded meadow where roof beams showed just under the surface, silvered with age. "That was Harrow. My brother fished off that ridge." The water lay quiet over it, and she looked at it a long moment before turning the wheel. "It's better with water on it. I don't know how to explain that. It just is."

"I think I know," Wren said. And he did. His father's fences were gone too — pulled apart by settlers in the first week of the revolt, the posts split for firewood, the wire wound onto reels and hung on the depot wall as a monument nobody had to explain. But Wren had gone out to the head of the old fence line in March, alone, and stood where the gate used to be, and found that grief and relief could occupy the same ground without canceling each other out. You didn't get to choose which feelings came up the channel. You just got to decide what you built on the floodplain after.

At the Gorge of Bells, the water ran white and thunderous through the narrows, and Marn took the *Lantern* through with the casual precision of a woman threading a needle she'd threaded a hundred times in her dreams. The bells themselves — the chimes of spray against stone that gave the gorge its name — rang higher in spring, and Isla stood at the bow with her face turned into the mist, laughing at nothing, at everything, at being alive on a river that belonged to everyone.

Beyond the gorge, they beached the boat and climbed the old spur line on foot. The track was rusted but sound; Wren checked the ties out of habit, and found that Bellhaven's tracker, Odo, had already been through with a crew, clearing deadfall, marking washouts. Word traveled ahead of them even without wires. People knew the engineer was coming back to the source, and people had opinions about what he should fix while he was up there.

The tunnel mouth stood open to the daylight, the way they'd blasted it in their escape, and beyond it the mountain galleries ran cool and dripping, lit now by lanterns Odo's crew had staged at intervals. Wren walked the main gallery slowly, running his lamp along the walls, and everywhere he looked he found his father.

Not the man — the man was eleven years dead and had died believing he'd protected something beautiful. But the man's hands. The cut-stone facing on the aqueduct arches, laid in courses so true you couldn't slip a knife between them. The brass fittings, machined to tolerances no company contract had ever specified, because Elias Fairweather had never been able to build a thing badly even when he was building a sin. The floodgate itself, that great wheel-and-rack assembly Wren had thrown in the dark while the world ended around him — it sat open now, locked open with a pin Odo's people had forged, and the water came through it in a broad silver sheet, singing.

His father had built the cage. His father had also, in the end, built the key, and hidden it inside the cage, and left the map to find it in the hands of a dying stranger. Wren had stopped trying to make those two facts agree. They didn't have to. People were bigger than their worst blueprint.

"He was good," Marn said quietly, beside him. She'd come up the gallery without him hearing, which for a woman her size was its own kind of gift. "The work, I mean. You can tell."

"Yeah."

"My brother's letters said the same. Said the company man who argued against the drownings — said he wasn't a hero, he was just an engineer who couldn't pretend the math worked." She shrugged. "Sometimes that's what a hero is. Somebody who won't pretend."

Wren nodded, because his throat had closed, and Marn, who understood silence better than anyone he'd ever known, let it stay closed and just stood there with him while the water sang.

They brought the surveyor's kit down from the final camp together — the journal wrapped in oilskin, the instruments cleaned and oiled, and the compass. His father's compass, recovered from the prospector's effects, its needle jeweled and true. Wren had carried it since the tunnels without quite knowing what he was waiting for it to do.

Now, standing at the mouth of the birth-cavern with the light falling in from the blast-open ceiling and the whole hidden heart of the mountain laid bare — the cistern empty, the pipes cut and capped, the great chamber given back to echo and drip — he held the compass out flat on his palm and watched the needle swing.

It swung past north. Past every bearing he'd ever steered by. It settled, trembling slightly, pointing straight down the length of the cavern, straight out the opened gate, straight down the old channels toward the valley — toward Bellhaven, toward the drowned towns filling with water, toward the ferry landings and the new telegraph poles and the schoolhouse in the old toll station, toward everything they'd built out of what had been broken.

Toward home, he supposed. If a compass could be said to point at such a thing.

"It's just magnetism," Isla said, coming to stand at his elbow. "Probably iron in the rock. There's a geological explanation for everything."

"Probably."

"But you're going to pretend it means something anyway."

"Absolutely."

She smiled and bumped her shoulder against his, and below them the source of the Sable River poured out of the mountain into the light, going wherever it would.

* * *

They stayed three days at the source, because some things shouldn't be hurried.

Wren serviced the gate mechanism properly this time — greased the rack, trued the wheel, replaced the pin with a better one he machined himself on a portable lathe Marn had apparently acquired through means she declined to specify. He taught Isla the maintenance sequence, and she wrote it out in telegraph shorthand and pinned it inside the relay box Odo's crew had installed at the cavern mouth, so that any operator in the valley could call up the source and ask after it, and someone could answer. A wire to the top of the world. She was insufferably pleased about it for days.

Marn fished. She claimed the pools above the cistern had never been touched, and she proved it by catching more trout in an afternoon than the three of them could eat, and cooking them badly over a driftwood fire, and daring anyone to complain. They complained. She cooked worse the next night, on principle. It was, Wren thought, the happiest either of them had ever seen her, and none of them said so, because saying so would have ruined it, and because Marn lit the lantern she'd carried up from the boat and set it on a stone at the water's edge and let it burn all night, and that said enough.

On the last evening, they sat together on the catwalk over the empty cistern — the same catwalk where Corliss had drawn on him, though the scorch of that day had weathered off the iron — and passed a bottle of Mrs. Aldous's elderberry wine between them, and watched the light go gold and then blue over the water.

"Do you ever think about him?" Isla asked. She didn't have to say who. "Corliss."

"Every day," Wren said. "Less every week, maybe. He chose the current instead of the report. I keep trying to decide if that was the one honest thing he ever did or just the last lie." He turned the bottle in his hands. "Vell I understand. Vell wanted to own the water. That's simple greed, and simple greed I can build a bridge over. But Corliss believed it. Believed order needed a fist around the river's throat, or everything downstream would tear itself apart." He shook his head. "And here we are, six months later, and nobody's torn anything apart. The pump stations run fairer than they ever did under the company. The towns are talking to each other on Isla's wire instead of shooting at each other. Turns out the fist was never holding anything together. It was just a fist."

"People are bigger than their worst blueprint," Marn said, echoing his own words back at him with a pilot's timing, and he laughed, and the sound went up into the mountain and came back changed, the way sounds do in places that have been listening a long time.

The wine was nearly gone when Isla stood up, brushed off her trousers, and announced that she had an announcement.

"Mrs. Aldous has offered me the depot office," she said. "Permanent. Full wire rights. She says Bellhaven deserves an operator who isn't on anybody's payroll, and she's right, and I said yes."

Marn raised the bottle in salute. "About time. You've been living out of a carpetbag since the day I met you."

"And Marn—"

"Don't." Marn held up a hand. "Whatever it is, don't make it a speech."

"The ferry needs a schedule keeper," Isla said. "Someone who can read a wire and read a river. Someone the landings trust. I'm not making it a speech, I'm making it a job offer."

Marn looked at her for a long moment. Then she took the last swallow of wine, stood, and stuck out her scarred hand, and Isla shook it, and that was the whole ceremony, and it was enough.

They both looked at Wren.

"I know," he said. "I know what you're going to say. The valley survey. Paid in gratitude instead of wages. I've heard the pitch from six different settlements."

"And?"

"And I'm taking it." He looked out at the water, silver-dark now under the first stars. "But I'm keeping a room at Mrs. Aldous's. And I'm keeping my tools at the depot. And whenever the survey brings me back down the valley — and it will, constantly, there's forty miles of reopened channel and every mile of it needs an engineer — I'm coming home to Bellhaven to sleep in my own bed and eat biscuits I'll complain about and argue with you two about whose turn it is to haul water." He paused. "If that's—"

"If you say *if that's acceptable*," Marn began dangerously, "I will throw you off this catwalk."

"—then it's settled," Wren finished. "It's settled."

Below them, the river went on speaking in the dark, patient and endless, saying the only thing it had ever needed to say.

* * *

Spring deepened. The valley greened from the rims down, following the water, and the water followed nothing but gravity and its own old memory, and both were enough.

Wren walked the survey all summer. He staked ferry landings at Harrow and Ashford and the third drowned town whose name the old folks still argued about, and at each one the settlers came out to meet him, and at each one somebody eventually recognized his surname, and at each one the recognition landed differently than it used to. At Harrow, an old orchard-keeper named Tull took his hand and said, "Your father argued for us. Your map freed us. Whatever else your name carries, it carries that," and Wren carried the words with him the way he once carried debt — except these weighed nothing, and multiplied instead of accruing interest.

In the fall he came back down the valley on the *Lantern*, riding the deck with a season's field notes in oilskin and a beard he'd stopped apologizing for, and Bellhaven rose out of the river haze the way it never had in the company maps: real, smoke-threaded, doubled in the water. The new depot. The wire poles marching away east and west. The school bell ringing in the old toll station, audible clear across the water, and children on the landing shouting the ferry's name before they shouted his.

Isla met him at the gangplank with a telegram in her hand and didn't give it to him, just read it aloud, grinning: *"FAIRWEATHER STOP SETTLERS AT ASHFORD REPORT SPRING RUNNING CLEAR AND STRONG STOP REQUEST ENGINEER FOR MILL SITE STOP REPLY WHEN CONVENIENT STOP."*

"When convenient," Wren repeated. "Imagine that. The whole valley asking permission now. Sending messages. Waiting for answers."

"Revolutionary," Isla agreed. "Wait till they discover voting."

Marn came up from the boat behind them, hauling the day's catch, and the three of them walked up from the landing together through the evening streets, arguing comfortably about the mill site and the winter schedule and whether Mrs. Aldous had improved her biscuits or merely lowered everyone's standards, and the lamps came on along the wire poles one by one as the dark came down, and the river ran clean and loud beside them all the way home, going where it would, which was everywhere.
