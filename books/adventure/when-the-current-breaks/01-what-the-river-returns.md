## 1. What the River Returns

The Rimewater ran low and green that morning, the color of bottle glass held up to a weak sun, and Ines Montrose read it the way other people read a face—looking for the lie in it.

"Keep your weight centered," they said, not turning around. "The current does the work. You just stay out of its way."

Behind them, in the second seat of the battered cedar-strip boat, Dario Hult clutched his paddle like a rosary. He was a tax attorney from Corvane's interior provinces, soft-handed and soft-bellied, and he had paid Ines an obscene sum in unmarked bills for three days on a river that officially did not have guides anymore. His permit—if you could call the folded paper wedged behind Ines's water flask a permit—bore a name that wasn't theirs and a stamp that had been forged by a man in Port Lassery who charged by the letter. It was good enough for the fish wardens. It would not be good enough for anyone who looked twice.

Ines had been looking twice at everything for six years. It was the only habit exile had left them.

"Is it always this quiet?" Dario asked. His voice had the strained brightness of a man trying to convince himself he was having fun.

"Not always." Ines let the boat drift through a seam of slack water where the current braided around a gravel bar. "Sometimes it's loud."

They did not elaborate. The client didn't need to know what *loud* meant on the Rimewater—the way the gorge sections could swallow a boat whole, the way the flood season turned the tributaries into brown arteries pumping debris from the high country. Dario had wanted wilderness. He was getting the tame version: herons standing in the shallows like patient clerks, kingfishers stitching blue lines over the water, the long green corridors of alder closing overhead until the sky was a rumor.

It was the third day. Tomorrow they'd take out at the old ferry landing below Tarnish Bend, and Dario would go back to his ledgers and Ines would go back to being no one, which was a job they'd gotten reasonably good at.

Then the river gave them something.

* * *

Ines saw it first as a color that didn't belong—a dull iron-orange against the silvered gray of a flood-scoured snag, half a mile down on the outside of the bend. They marked it without letting their eyes linger, the way you mark anything unusual when you're not sure yet whether it's trouble. A snag like this one was a whole drowned tree, root ball torn loose in some spring flood years ago, now lodged in the bank with its branches combed downstream like hair. The floods had stripped it bare and bleached it bone-white, and every year the high water rearranged what hung in it: fishing nets gone feral, a child's wagon once, an entire rowboat impaled like a specimen in a case.

This was different. This was square.

"Ease left," Ines said quietly. "Give the snag room."

Dario overcorrected, because timid clients always overcorrected, and the boat swung wide toward midstream before Ines caught it with two hard draws. The snag slid past on the right, close enough to touch, and Ines got a full look at the thing wedged in the fork of its largest branch.

A chest. Iron-bound, maybe chest-high on a standing man, crusted with silt and rust but unmistakably a chest—and sealed. Not broken open, not spilling its contents downriver the way drowned luggage always eventually did. Sealed, wedged deep in the snag's grip, sitting above the current summer line, which meant it hadn't come down with this year's flood. It had been hanging there since spring. Maybe longer.

Ines's hands went cold on the paddle, and it had nothing to do with the spray.

"Everything all right?" Dario asked.

"Fine. There's a good eddy below the point. We'll camp there."

It wasn't a lie, exactly. The eddy *was* good—deep, sheltered, walled off from the main channel by a spine of gravel. But Ines chose it the way a spider chooses a web site, and they knew it, and some small honest part of them flinched at knowing it.

They made camp in the last hour of light. Dario, exhausted and grateful, ate his rehydrated stew and drank two fingers of the whiskey Ines carried for exactly this purpose, and was asleep in his tent by dark, snoring with the profound unconsciousness of a man who trusted his guide completely. That trust sat in Ines's chest like a swallowed stone. Six years ago they had been the kind of guide people trusted with their lives, and then a mountain had taken eleven of those lives, and the registries of two countries had struck their name from the rolls so cleanly it was as if the ink had never dried there at all. Trust was the one currency Ines couldn't hold anymore without it burning straight through their pockets.

They waited until the moon rose—half-full, enough light to work by—and paddled the empty boat back up along the bank, ferrying across the slack water with slow, silent strokes.

* * *

The snag was bigger up close than it had looked from the channel, a cathedral of dead wood, and the chest sat in it like a relic in a niche. Ines climbed out onto the lower branches, boots finding the wet grain of the wood, and worked their way up to it. The river talked underneath the whole time, a low continuous mutter through the gravel bar, covering whatever sound they made.

Up close, the chest told stories. The iron bands were hand-forged, not milled—old work, older than anything Ines had ever pulled out of this river. The wood beneath the crust of silt was dense and dark, some hardwood that hadn't swollen or rotted despite years in the water, which meant it had been treated, sealed, meant to survive exactly this. And on the front panel, under a skin of mud that came away under Ines's thumb like wet paper, there was a stamp.

A crest. Pressed into a lead seal the size of a coin, set into the wood itself.

Ines knew crests. They'd grown up on heraldry the way other kids grew up on team logos—every salvage family on the Rimewater traded in the past, and the past wore coats of arms the way fish wore scales. This one they didn't know. A tower flanked by two currents of water, crossed by a single star above. Stylized, formal, official.

And beneath it, worn nearly smooth but still legible if you tilted it against the moonlight, a word in a script Ines had seen exactly twice in their life, both times on smuggled documents in Port Lassery, both times handled like contraband:

*Vessia.*

There were places on the maps sold in Corvane bookshops where Vessia should have been, and those places were labeled with other names now, or with nothing at all. There were old people in the river towns who would go quiet if you said the word, and change the subject to weather. A nation that had drowned—not just its cities, though the cities had drowned, everyone agreed on that much—but its own existence, erased so thoroughly that mentioning it in certain company was less like speaking history and more like speaking a curse. Ines had always assumed the erasure was grief. Standing on a dead tree in the middle of the night with a lead seal under their thumb, they felt the first cold suspicion that it might have been policy.

They should have left it. Every instinct honed over six years of careful, invisible living said *leave it*: unknown provenance, official seal, the kind of object that attracted attention the way a wound attracts flies. Ines Montrose survived by being unremarkable. A man who pried open sealed chests on river snags at midnight became, very quickly, a man people asked questions about.

But the river had given it to *them*. Not to the fleet, not to the wardens, not to the next guide who happened by. To Ines, on a trip they almost hadn't taken, for a client they almost hadn't accepted, on a line of water they knew better than anyone still permitted to know it. Six years of taking nothing from the world—no credit, no credit, no credit—and here, finally, was a gift.

And Ines knew, the way river people know, that gifts from rivers are never free. The river gives with one hand and keeps a ledger with the other. Everything it delivers—a body, a boat, a chest—it delivers because something upstream went wrong. Somewhere above Tarnish Bend, something had broken loose or been let go, and this chest was the receipt.

They worked the seal loose with their belt knife, slowly, respecting it. The lead cracked clean. The lid lifted on a breath of air that smelled of tar and old paper and something faintly sweet, like wax.

Inside: oilcloth bundles, tied with cord that still held its knots. A map, folded into eighths, its edges charred as if it had been near a fire that never quite touched it. And letters—dozens of them, bundled by year, the ink faded to the brown of dried blood but legible, the handwriting formal and slanted.

Ines lifted the top letter toward the moon.

They read four words before their hands started shaking too badly to hold it steady.

Four words, and a signature. The signature belonged to a name Ines had known their whole life, had carried like a scar, had spoken aloud at two funerals and never once since. Their grandmother's name. Written in her hand—or a hand pretending to be hers—in the closing of a letter discussing, in the cool language of logistics, the sinking of a country.

The river muttered under the snag. Somewhere upstream, miles back in the dark, Dario slept on, trusting.

Ines sat on the branch for a long time, the letter folded closed against their chest, feeling the ground tilt under them in a way that had nothing to do with water. The identity they had built after the mountain—the careful, blank, blameless nobody—suddenly rested on foundations they couldn't see, over a nation history insisted had never existed, tied by blood to a crime they had never heard named.

Gifts from rivers are never free. Ines had known that since childhood. What they hadn't known, until the moon climbed higher and the cold came up out of the water and soaked through their coat, was what the price might turn out to be.

They resealed the chest, lashed it into the boat under the tarp with the tent poles, and paddled back to camp with strokes as quiet as breath.

Tomorrow they'd run Dario to the takeout and be done with him. Tomorrow they'd start counting things: days, tides, enemies.

Because whoever had sent this chest down the Rimewater—or lost it—would want it back. And Ines had already opened the seal.
