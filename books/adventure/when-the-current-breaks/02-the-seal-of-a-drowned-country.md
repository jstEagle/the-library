## 2. The Seal of a Drowned Country

Ines waited until Dario was asleep.

Not because there was any law against a guide opening a chest the river had given them—the Rimewater's salvage customs were older than any law and simpler: what the current delivers to your line, your hands, your camp, is yours. But because Dario was the kind of man who talked. He'd talk at the takeout, at the fuel dock, at the noodle stand in Harrow Bend where the charter pilots ate. *My guide pulled a chest out of a snag, sealed, heavy, stamped with something old.* Ines had survived eight years on this river by being the least interesting person on it. That was over now, and they knew it, and there was no sense hurrying the funeral.

So they sat in the boat, tied off to a willow at the camp's downstream edge, with a headlamp hooded under a jacket to kill the glow, and worked at the seal with a flat pry bar and more patience than they felt.

The chest itself was the first wrong thing. It wasn't river salvage. River salvage is dented, scoured, wearing its drowning like a coat. This chest had been *protected*—packed in tarred canvas, cradled in straw that had gone to black mush, and the seal on its face was wax over lead, the way you'd seal something meant to survive a specific catastrophe rather than an accidental one. The wax was the color of dried blood. The stamp in it showed a tower rising out of waves, and around the tower's base, a ring of small letters Ines could not read but recognized the shape of: Vessian. Old Vessian, the angular script from the few coins and the one museum plaque that still dared to use the word.

Vessia. The country that had drowned, and then, more thoroughly, had been drowned *again*—in the archives, in the atlases, in the mouths of anyone who wanted to keep their permits. Sixty years ago the Corvane government had broken the dikes at the Vessian lowlands during the war, and the water had taken four cities and a hundred thousand people, and afterward the victors had done what victors do: they had renamed the seabed. The maps said the Sallow Reach now. The history books said *the Vessian question* and then, increasingly, said nothing at all. Ines had met a man once, drunk, who claimed his grandmother had been born a Vessian citizen, and the bar had gone quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone says a dead man's name at the wrong table.

Ines had never given the country much thought. You couldn't afford to, working Corvane waters. Vessia was a superstition now, a story guides told to spook clients—*and below the third bend, they say, the old bell tower still stands in twelve fathoms, and on quiet nights—* The kind of story that was safe precisely because it wasn't true.

The seal cracked with a sound like a knuckle.

Inside, under the ruined straw: oilcloth bundles, tied with cord gone soft as sinew. Ines lifted the first one out and unwrapped it over their knees, and the smell that came up was ink and mildew and something older, a mineral smell, like the inside of a flooded house.

Documents. Dozens of them. Dispatches, in that same angular hand. Ledgers with columns of figures. A sheaf of telegraph flimsies, the paper gone the brown of tea, and Ines tilted the headlamp and read the top one because their hands were already moving and their mind hadn't caught up.

*TO THE HARBOR OFFICE, SALLOWMOUTH. DIKE STATIONS REPORT PRESSURE BEYOND RATING. REQUEST AUTHORITY TO OPEN THE EASTERN CULVERTS BEFORE THE—*

The flimsy ended there, torn. Ines sat with it in their lap and listened to the river move against the hull, and felt the first cold thread of it, the thing they would spend the rest of this story trying not to drown in.

These weren't souvenirs. These were the *records*. The paperwork of the drowning. The thing that had never existed because the victors had burned every copy—except someone, sixty years ago, had packed a chest full of it, sealed it in tar and lead, and put it on a boat. And the boat had gone down. And the river had held it. And now the river, in its own time, had decided to give it back.

*Gifts from rivers are never free.*

Ines set the flimsy down with exaggerated care, the way you set down a thing that might be a trap, and opened the second bundle.

A map. Half of it—the lower half—was fused into a single welded block by water and time, the paper melted into a lump you could have used as a doorstop. But the upper half had survived, and it was a chart of the Rimewater's lower system: the channels Ines ran for a living, drawn in a hand from before the renaming. The names on it stopped Ines's breath for a moment. Not Sallow Reach. *Vessa's Throat.* Not the Harrow Channels. *The Meridian Approach.* And at the center of the chart, in the drowned basin where the old lake had been—drained now, these past decades, by the irrigation cuts, lying naked under the dry season sun like a picked carcass—someone had inked a symbol: a tower, the tower from the seal, and beside it in careful script:

*MERIDIAN STAR — ARCHIVE BELOW WATERLINE — AFT MAGAZINE VAULT.*

The flagship. Ines had heard the name the way you hear all Vessian names, as decoration, as ghost story. The pride of the Vessian fleet, scuttled in the last days when the water came in, sunk deliberately in the deep pool below the lake so the victors couldn't take her. Everyone knew the story. Nobody believed there was anything down there but mud and old iron, because believing otherwise would have meant believing Vessia had had a fleet, a government, an archive worth sealing—had been *real*.

Ines's hands had gone cold. Not river-cold. A different cold, coming from the inside.

They should have stopped there. They knew that even then, some part of them standing back with its arms crossed, saying *put it back, lash it down, run it out to the deep channel at flood tide and let the current take it where it took everything else.* Eight years ago, on the mountain, Ines had made a decision in ninety seconds that had cost them their name in two countries, and the lesson they'd carved out of it—the only lesson, the one they lived by—was *don't touch.* Don't touch the wreck, don't touch the politics, don't touch the truth of other people's wars. Guide the river. Keep your head down. Be no one.

They opened the third bundle.

Letters. Personal correspondence, tied in a ribbon that had once been blue. Ines unfolded the top sheet and the handwriting was small and slanted and fierce, and the signature at the bottom was a name Ines had not seen written down in twenty years, because the last person who wrote it had been their mother, on the envelope of the letter that told them their grandmother was dead.

*Halloran.*

Their grandmother's maiden name. Before the marriage, before the move across the border, before the family had quietly, completely, without ever saying a word about it, stopped being from anywhere at all. Ines had grown up on stories of a village that "wasn't on the maps anymore," and had assumed—children assume—that meant the village was small. It had never occurred to them that it might mean the village was *underwater*, or that the maps had been made to forget it on purpose.

Ines read the letter. Then, because the first one didn't make sense alone, they read the next, and the next, working down through the bundle while the moon crossed the sky and the willow leaves brushed the water and Dario's snoring went on unchanged in the tent, mercifully, obliviously. The letters were from their grandmother—*Mireille Halloran, born Mireille Vessa-Korran*, and Ines said the name silently and felt the ground tilt—to a cousin who had stayed. The cousin had been a harbor clerk in Sallowmouth. The letters spanned eleven years, and in them, in the oblique way people write when they know letters get read, Mireille Halloran had documented everything. The family's shipping business. The dike contracts. The Corvane engineers who had come, in the last year, not to inspect the dikes but to *price* them. And in the final letters, the ones written in a hand that shook:

*They are going to open the culverts, Tomas. I have seen the orders. I have copied what I could. If the water comes, it will not be the war that drowned us. It will be arithmetic. Someone must keep the proof. Someone must keep the names.*

*They say the Meridian Star will hold. They say the vault is rated for fifty years. I am putting my papers with the others. If you receive this, I am already—*

The last letter was unfinished. The sentence simply stopped, and the paper was stained in a way Ines chose not to examine.

They sat in the boat for a long time afterward, the headlamp hooded, the letters squared back into their bundles with hands that were almost steady. The river talked its one long sentence against the hull. Somewhere downstream an owl asked its question and got no answer.

Ines tried to do the thing they were good at: the cold arithmetic. A chest of Vessian state records, surfacing on the Rimewater, sixty years after the drowning, in the same season the lakebed had gone dry enough to expose a hull. That was not chance. That was a search. Someone was looking for the Meridian Star, and the chest was either their map or their mistake—and either way, whoever they were, they would come down the river after it. Ines could feel the shape of the next two weeks the way you feel weather in a scar.

But under the arithmetic, under the guide's cold counting, was the vertigo, and the vertigo was worse.

Because Ines Montrose—false permit, false surname, false everything, the person they had built plank by plank out of silence after the mountain took their name away—that person had been built on one solid fact: *I am from nowhere. I owe nothing to anyone's history. I have no country, no flag, no grave, no debt.* It had been the one comfort of exile, that they had nothing to be loyal *to*.

And now the river had put a chest in their arms full of proof that they were from the one country in the world that had been not only destroyed but *erased*—that their blood came from the drowned places, that their grandmother had copied dike orders with shaking hands while the arithmetic closed over her cities, that the family that raised them had been, all along, a family of survivors who had bought their safety with silence.

Ines had spent eight years refusing to speak about one disaster. The river had just handed them a nation's worth.

They looked down at the seal, broken on the thwart, the tower-and-waves crest staring up at them, and they understood—with the flat, sick clarity of a guide reading water they've never run—that there was no putting this back. You couldn't reseal a thing like this. You couldn't un-open your own name.

The chest went back under the tarp. Ines sat until the sky went grey at the edges, doing the only thing left to do, the thing they'd promised themselves they'd start at dawn.

Counting. Tides. Days. Enemies.

And one more thing now, a new line in the ledger, written in their grandmother's slanted hand: *someone must keep the proof.*

Ines didn't yet know if that someone was going to be them. But they knew, watching the first light come up the Rimewater and touch the broken seal, that by the time the dry season closed the channels, someone was going to reach the Meridian Star—and that Ines Montrose, from nowhere, owing nothing, was already lying to themselves about staying out of it.

Two bends downstream, at first light, a charter boat with Corvane registry papers would pass their camp, running the channel fast, its pilot sweeping the banks with glasses.

Ines didn't see it. The river did.
