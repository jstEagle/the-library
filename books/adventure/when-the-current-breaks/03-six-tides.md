## 3. Six Tides

The harbor at Lowmark smelled of tar and old fish and the particular desperation of a town that lived one bad season from ruin. Ines tied the *Kestrel* at the third piling from the seawall—the loose one, the one that gave under pressure instead of holding fast—and went looking for Doss.

They found him where they always found him, in the back of the chandlery, counting bolts of canvas with the concentration of a man who had never once in his life been surprised by anything. He was seventy if he was a day, built like a knot of driftwood, and he had known Ines's face back when it still appeared on permits.

"You're not here," Doss said without looking up. "I haven't seen you."

"Good. Then you can tell me things."

"I can tell you things to a man I haven't seen." He set down his tally slate. His eyes, when they came up, were not friendly. They were worse than unfriendly—they were afraid, and Doss had not been afraid of anything since the storm that took his left hand, and possibly not even then. "You should not have come to Lowmark this season, Montrose."

Ines leaned against the shelf of rope coils. "That's a fine greeting."

"It's not a greeting. It's a weather report." He glanced toward the front of the shop, where the owner's nephew sat behind the counter doing nothing with great diligence. "There are charter papers posted at the harbormaster's office. Corvane Salvage Consortium. Six vessels, licensed for survey and recovery work on the middle Rimewater." He let the word sit there. *Middle Rimewater.* The exact stretch Ines had run for three years without ever once writing down.

"Salvage," Ines said. "Of what? There's nothing out there but drowned orchards and my patience."

"There's whatever's in a sealed chest stamped with the Vessian crest, which is what the harbormaster says washed up near the Sedge Narrows, because the harbormaster talks, because the harbormaster has been paid to talk." Doss picked up his slate again, then put it down, as if the pretense of work had become too heavy. "Corvane doesn't buy six ships and a government charter for an empty box. Someone told them what was in it before it ever reached your hands. Or someone is guessing very well."

The shop felt smaller than it had a moment ago. Ines became aware of their own breathing, slow and deliberate, the way they breathed on scree slopes when the rock started talking.

"And the dry season?" they asked.

Doss almost smiled. Almost. "You've seen the gauge marks."

"I've seen the gauge marks."

"Then you know what I'm going to say. The snowpack failed upcountry. The reservoirs are holding back everything they can for the irrigation contracts. The river will drop below navigable depth on the middle stretch in—" he counted on his nine fingers, an old habit performed with bitter theater— "twelve days. Maybe eleven, if the wind stays out of the north. After that, no boat draws less than a foot of water through the Narrows, and nothing bigger than a canoe makes it past the Gorge of Names until the rains come back. Which won't be for months."

Twelve days. Ines turned the number over the way they'd turn a stone on the trail, checking what lived underneath it.

* * *

They walked the seawall after dark, because Ines did their best thinking in motion and their worst thinking indoors. The tide was going out, peeling back from the pilings in long brown sheets, and the mudflats beyond the wall gave off a smell like something ancient exhaling. Out past the breakwater, the Rimewater ran wide and shallow and silvered with moonlight, and somewhere upriver of all that silver lay a chest full of a country that had never officially existed, sitting in the hold of the *Kestrel* under a false floor Ines had built for smuggling brandy in a former life.

They counted.

It was what they did. On the mountain, before, they had counted anchors and rope lengths and hours of daylight and the weight of every client's pack, and the counting had kept people alive right up until the day it hadn't. The habit had survived the disgrace. Everything else had burned away, but the arithmetic stayed, cold and faithful as a compass needle.

So: twelve days of navigable water. Six tides, roughly, if you counted the way rivermen counted—a tide being the twice-daily pulse that lifted the shallows enough to move a loaded boat through the sandbars. Six chances to get from Lowmark to wherever the map led, and the map was half-melted, which meant Ines didn't know exactly where *wherever* was, only that it lay somewhere below the drained lakebeds of the old Vessian country, upstream against the current, through water that was leaving faster every hour.

Supplies next. The *Kestrel* carried food for ten days if Ines ate like a monk. Water for five unless it rained, and it would not rain; the sky over Lowmark had the flat, bruised clarity of a sky that had forgotten how. Fuel for the engine: four days' running, then sail and sweep and muscle. Rope, adequate. First aid, adequate. Ammunition—one old flare gun and a boat hook, which was to say none.

Enemies last, because enemies were the part of the equation Ines always wanted to skip and could never afford to.

Six Corvane vessels, chartered, meaning armed escort was probably folded into the contract—salvage fleets working contested waters always bought a gunboat or two, and governments that erased guides from their registries did not hesitate to erase other things. Against that: one riverboat, one disgraced guide, and a chest of paper that would burn as easily as any other paper.

And underneath the arithmetic, in the margin where Ines kept the numbers they didn't write down: someone had told Corvane about the chest. Which meant someone knew Ines had it. Which meant the fleet wasn't searching the middle Rimewater—they were hunting it, and they knew exactly whose wake to follow.

Ines stopped at the end of the seawall and watched the last of the tide slide out.

Here was the thing about rivers that mountain people learned too late: you could not outrun them and you could not argue with them. You could only read them and move first. Every hour Ines spent in Lowmark was an hour the fleet spent closing the gap between its charter papers and its guns. Every tide that ebbed took water Ines would need later. Waiting was not caution. Waiting was drowning slowly, in installments.

But running meant the chest came along, and the chest was not just paper. Ines had opened it again that afternoon, alone in the cabin with the lamp turned low, and read the letters bearing their grandmother's name—*Mirela Halloran-Vess*—in a hand Ines recognized from childhood recipe cards and birthday notes. Their grandmother had signed manifests. Had countersigned orders. Had written, in one letter, a phrase Ines could still see printed on the inside of their eyelids: *the ballast must be placed by morning or the whole arrangement fails.*

*The whole arrangement.* As if a nation were a cargo, loaded and lashed and sent to the bottom on schedule.

Ines had closed the chest and sat in the dark for a long time, listening to the hull tick against the pilings, feeling the vertigo rise like floodwater. Their name was false. Their permit was false. Their entire post-exile existence was a small sturdy boat built to float on the assumption that Vessia had never existed, that the family name meant nothing, that the disaster on the mountain was the worst thing that had ever touched them. And now the river had gone and dredged up proof that the family name meant everything—that Ines Montrose was the last living heir to a country their own blood had helped drown.

Run or stay. Sell or burn. Flee or fight. The equations stacked up in the dark, and every one of them balanced wrong.

What finally tipped it was not courage. Ines wanted to be honest about that, at least with themselves. What tipped it was simpler and colder: if the fleet reached the archive first, Corvane would sell Vessia's bones piece by piece to collectors and ministries, and the truth would be locked in private vaults forever, and Ines's family would remain exactly what history said they were. But if Ines got there first—

If Ines got there first, Ines could decide. That was all. Not salvation. Not redemption. Just the choice, held in their own two hands, for the first time since the mountain.

Ines turned back up the seawall toward the harbor lights, and the arithmetic finished itself without them: depart on tomorrow's morning tide, ahead of the fleet's survey schedule, light and fast, running the channels while they still held water. Six tides. One chance.

* * *

The knock came at the cabin door an hour before dawn, three soft raps, the rhythm of someone who knew boats and didn't want to wake the neighbors.

Ines opened it with the boat hook in reach and found a young man standing on the dock in the gray light, dressed too well for Lowmark and trying to correct for it—a waxed jacket over city clothes, river boots that had never seen a river, a single travel bag held with the grip of someone who expected it to be taken from him. He had dark hair going gray early at the temples and eyes that did the thing Ines hated most in clients: they assessed. Measured the boat, the guide, the exits.

"You're taking on charter work," he said. Not a question.

"I'm not."

"The harbormaster says otherwise. Says the *Kestrel*'s master is running the middle stretch this week, and takes passengers, if the price is right." He produced a fold of bills—not ostentatious, but more than the passage was worth, deliberately so—and held it out. "Upstream. All the way upstream, as far as the water goes."

Ines looked at the money, then at him. "The water doesn't go far. Twelve days, maybe less. You know that?"

"I know that better than you do." Something moved behind the assessment then, something quick and hungry. "I need to be on that river before it closes. I don't care how thin it gets. I'll help pull the boat over gravel myself if I have to."

"No one runs the middle stretch this season," Ines said. "Not alone, not loaded, not with a passenger who dresses like a funeral director on holiday."

The man smiled, and it transformed his face into something younger and more reckless. "My name is Teo Vasq. And you're going to take me anyway, Master Montrose, because we both know the harbormaster sold you out an hour ago, and the men asking after your berth weren't asking politely."

Down the dock, in the direction of the harbormaster's office, lanterns were moving. More than two. Moving with purpose.

Teo Vasq stepped aboard without waiting to be invited, and Ines, cursing in three languages, cast off the lines and took the *Kestrel* out on the ebb, into the last of the deep water, with six tides left and the river already pulling away beneath the hull.
