## 4. The Passenger Who Knew Too Much

The harbor at Cindermill smelled of tar and rotting pilings, and Ines had chosen it for exactly that reason. Nobody came to Cindermill to be seen. They came to disappear, or to buy things that made other people disappear, and both trades ran on the same principle: don't ask.

Ines had posted the notice anyway. *Licensed guide, Rimewater run, one berth, references available.* The lie of it sat in their chest like a swallowed stone. The permit they'd copied was real enough to pass a squinting glance; the name on it belonged to a man who'd drowned two seasons back in the upper gorge, and whose handwriting Ines had practiced for three nights until the letters stopped shaking.

Money was the problem. The chest rode wrapped in oilcloth beneath the false floor of the boat's stern locker, and every day it stayed in Cindermill was a day someone might talk about it. But running the river alone meant no second pair of hands when the channels narrowed, and Ines's left shoulder still sang from hauling the chest out of the snag. A passenger meant coin, and coin meant fuel, food, and bribes—three things the next twelve days would demand like toll collectors.

Three inquiries came. Two were merchants' sons wanting adventure, and Ines turned them away with a price so high it insulted them politely. The third arrived at dusk, when the harbor lamps were just lit and the water between the pilings went black as poured ink.

He was slight, dark-haired, with the careful posture of a man who had learned young that taking up space got you noticed. He wore a surveyor's coat too clean for a surveyor and carried a leather case strapped across his body like a child.

"You're Montrose," he said. Not a question.

"I'm whoever's on the permit."

"The permit says Aldous Wren." The man smiled without warmth. "Aldous Wren died in the Gorge of Names. I checked."

Ines kept their hands loose at their sides and their voice bored. "Then you've wasted a walk. Berth's taken."

"It isn't." He set a purse on the bollard between them. It landed with the heavy, dense sound of real money. "I'm not buying a guide. I'm buying silence about where you're going, and I already know where you're going, which makes me the cheapest passenger you'll ever have."

The lamps hissed. Somewhere down the quay a winch groaned, loading something nobody would log.

"And where's that?" Ines asked.

"Downriver," he said. "Toward the dry channels. Toward whatever the Corvane fleet is chartering six boats to find." He held out a hand. "Teo Vasq."

*Vasq.* The name landed strangely, an old coin from a dead country. Ines didn't take the hand. "That's not a Rimewater name."

"No," Teo said, and let it hang there, in the space where Vessia used to be. "It isn't."

* * *

**Teo** counted the boat's flaws as Montrose cast off before dawn: patched hull seam, jury-rigged rudder yoke, a guide who moved like someone relearning their own body after a long illness. None of it mattered. What mattered was the shape under the stern locker floor that Teo had clocked within ten minutes of boarding—the way Montrose checked its strap twice in the first hour and never touched anything else on the boat.

Grandmother Ilse's letters had described the archive's route downriver in fragments, coded into recipes and hymnals, and every fragment ended the same way: *the current keeps what we could not.* Teo had spent nine years and most of an inheritance following those fragments through archives that officially contained nothing, talking to descendants who officially descended from no one. And now the river itself had apparently delivered a piece of it to a blacklisted mountain guide with a stolen name.

The irony was almost elegant. Almost.

They made the first confluence by midmorning, and Montrose took the eastern channel without consulting any chart Teo could see—which meant there was no chart, which meant Montrose knew these waters the way Teo knew the letters: by heart, by obsession, by need.

"You've run this stretch before," Teo said, settling on the thwart amidships where he could watch both the water and the guide.

"Once."

"In low water season?"

Montrose's jaw tightened. "No."

Teo waited. It was a skill he'd refined over years of interviewing people who wanted to say nothing: he simply existed in the silence until it became more expensive than speech.

"The channels drop four feet by tide five," Montrose said finally. "Six if the rains hold off upcountry. After that, this boat is furniture."

"So we have until tide five."

"*We* have nothing. You have a berth and a fare. That's all."

"Of course." Teo looked out at the water sliding past, brown-green and patient. "And in the locker?"

The oar stroke hitched—a half-beat stutter, quickly corrected. Montrose didn't answer, but the silence had changed texture, and Teo pressed gently, the way you press a bruise to confirm it's yours.

"My family came out of Vessia on the last barges," Teo said. "Before the water took the terraces. My grandmother carried a recipe book across the border, and half the recipes are coordinates. Everyone thinks the sinking was an accident of war, some tragic engineering failure. It wasn't. Someone opened the sluices on purpose, and my family spent seventy years pretending not to know because knowing got people disappeared." He turned back. "I'm not selling anything, Montrose. I'm going to print it. All of it. Every name."

For a long moment the only sound was the river worrying the hull.

"That's a fine story," Montrose said at last, flat as slate. "Here's mine: I take paying passengers down rivers. Whatever you think is in my locker, it's gear."

"You checked the strap four times since dawn."

"Good gear is worth checking."

* * *

**Ines** made camp that night on a gravel bar shaped like a sleeping animal, and lay awake listening to the passenger breathe two boat-lengths away.

*Every name,* Teo had said. As if names were a gift. As if the right names, printed in the right order, wouldn't burn down everything Ines had spent seven years building out of ash and false permits. Because Ines knew how those documents worked now—they'd read enough of them by lamplight to understand the architecture of the thing. The archive wasn't just proof that Vessia was murdered. It was a ledger. Signatures. Families who'd been paid to look away, families whose fortunes still stood on the drowned terraces, and threaded through the middle pages like a vein of ore—

*Halloran. M. Halloran. M. Halloran.*

Their grandmother's name, again and again, in the margins of manifests. Payment received. Passage arranged. Sluice schedules confirmed.

Ines had told themselves, prying open the chest, that the letters might be forged—that history's cruelest joke would be a forgery. But the handwriting matched the recipe cards Ines had grown up watching their grandmother annotate, the same cramped certainty, the same way she crossed her sevens. She hadn't just known. She'd helped. And somewhere downstream, in a vault welded into a flagship's hull, was the rest of the file—including, probably, whatever she'd written in her own hand about why.

Ines did not want to know why. That was the truth they kept returning to, turning over like a stone with something wet underneath. Some part of them wanted the whole archive at the bottom of the channel, unread, unprintable, unprovable—wanted their grandmother to remain a stern woman who taught them knots and weather-reading, rather than whatever the pages said she was.

And the passenger wanted to hand all of it to the world.

Ines watched the embers of the fire dim and thought about the arithmetic of it. Teo Vasq was either exactly what he claimed—a descendant with a conscience and a printing plan—or he was Corvane's cleverest instrument yet, a soft-spoken way to make Ines carry the chest directly to the fleet. The purse he'd paid with was real. So were most traps.

Sleep came thin and late.

* * *

**Teo** woke before first light to the sound of Montrose burning something.

He kept still, eyes shut, breath even, and listened. Paper catching, paper being fed to flames in small pieces, deliberate, unhurried. When he finally stretched and sat up, Montrose was crouched at the fire's edge, stirring ash with a stick, face unreadable in the gray light.

"Cold morning," Teo offered.

"Warmer soon." Montrose kicked dirt over the fire pit. "We make the narrows by noon. After that you keep your hands inside the gunwale and do what I say when I say it."

"Understood." Teo shouldered his pack, then, casually: "What was the paper?"

Montrose paused, one boot on the gunwale. For a heartbeat something moved behind their eyes—calculation, or maybe exhaustion—and then it settled into the flat professional calm Teo was beginning to recognize as the guide's true native language.

"Kindling," Montrose said.

It was a lie. Teo knew it was a lie the way you know rain coming—not from any single sign but from the whole pressure of the air. And he understood, suddenly, with a cold clarity that had nothing to do with the morning chill: Montrose wasn't planning to sell the archive or share it. Montrose was deciding, page by page, whether it should ever see light at all.

Which meant Teo had perhaps five tides to change their mind—or to get the chest away from them.

Which meant, he admitted, shrugging into the surveyor's coat that fooled no one, that he was lying too, had been lying since the bollard in Cindermill. The purse was real but the story of how he'd found Montrose was not. He hadn't followed rumors downriver. He'd followed a letter—one of his grandmother's coded recipes, decoded last spring, naming a mountain guide by description and by the disaster that had ruined them: *the survivor who carries the guilt like ballast. Find them. The river will find them first.*

His grandmother had known about Montrose's family. Had known what they did. Had written, in the last line of the last recipe, a warning Teo had chosen not to mention:

*If the Halloran blood finds the archive first, count your fingers after you shake its hand.*

Teo boarded, took his thwart, and watched the guide push them off the gravel bar into the brown current, toward the narrows, toward tide two, toward whatever each of them was really carrying.

"Fair winds," he said pleasantly.

Montrose grunted and did not look back, and neither of them mentioned the smoke smell still clinging faintly to the morning air, or the fact that both of them had noticed it, and both of them were counting.

By noon the banks began to close in, the water quickened, and far behind them—too far to see, close enough to feel—something with six hulls and a government's patience started down the same channel.
