## 5. Port of Small Mercies

The port announced itself by smell before sight—tar and frying fish and the sweet rot of fruit left too long in the sun. Port of Small Mercies, the charts called it, though no chart had been updated since before Ines was born. The locals called it Mercies, and they called it that with a smirk, because mercy there was a commodity like anything else: weighed, haggled over, sold to whoever paid last.

Ines brought the boat in on the morning tide, running dark past the customs shed where a lamp burned even at this hour. Teo crouched low among the cargo netting, playing freight, as agreed. The chest rode beneath the false floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth, its weight a presence Ines felt through the soles of their boots the way you feel a storm cell building beyond a ridge.

"Two hours," Ines said, tying off at a mooring post scarred by a hundred ropes. "Buy what's on the list, ask nothing, look at no one."

"I've done this before," Teo said.

"That's what worries me."

* * *

Mercies woke slowly, the way ports do when the tide is their clock. Fishermen mending nets under the fish-market awning nodded to Ines without warmth—a guide under a false permit was known here for what it was, and respected accordingly, which was to say barely. Ines moved through the lanes buying hardtack, lamp oil, a new bilge pump to replace the one that had begun to wheeze, and rope, always rope. The dry season had thinned the market as it would thin the river; prices were up, tempers short.

It was at the chandler's, counting out coin for the pump, that Ines saw the notice.

It hung behind the counter on a nail, official paper in an unofficial place—the crest of Corvane Salvage Consortium stamped in blue wax, the ink still sharp enough to smell. A charter notice. Exclusive salvage rights, Rimewater lower reaches, all finds to be registered with the fleet's survey office. And beneath it, in smaller print: *Information regarding unregistered vessels or cargo may be reported to the harbormaster's office. Reward commensurate.*

"New," Ines said.

The chandler, a woman named Brakka with forearms like dock pilings, didn't look up from her ledger. "Came in on yesterday's packet boat. Fleet's got six hulls anchored off the point. Bought the harbormaster Tuesday." She said it the way she'd report weather. "Bought him cheap, too. Man never could hold out for a fair price."

Ines kept their hands still on the counter. "Anyone else selling?"

Brakka's eyes came up then, flat and shrewd. "Everyone sells something in Mercies. Question's whether you're buying or being bought." She slid the pump across. "You want my advice, guide—and you don't, but here it is—don't sleep aboard tonight. There are men who walk the docks after midnight with a list of boats and owners, and your boat's on it now. I'd bet the pump on it."

"How?"

"Harbormaster keeps a register. Your false permit's good enough for the river, not good enough for a man who's been paid to read closely." She snapped the ledger shut. "That'll be eleven marks."

Ines paid. Outside, the morning had brightened into something almost kind, gulls wheeling over the fish stalls, and none of it meant anything anymore. Six hulls. A bought harbormaster. Their name on a list.

And one other thing Brakka had said, or half-said, that Ines turned over now like a stone looking for what lived underneath it. *Fleet's got a new pilot. Came recommended.*

* * *

Teo was waiting at the mooring with flour and salt pork and a face that asked questions. Ines gave him the news in a low voice while rigging the pump, and watched him do his own arithmetic—the same cold arithmetic Ines had learned to do years ago, tides against days, supplies against enemies.

"The harbormaster will sell us by nightfall," Teo said.

"By midday if he's eager."

"Then we leave now. Tide's still with us."

"Tide turns in three hours and the channel east of the point shoals fast. We'd be grounding in full view of six gunboats." Ines cinched the last knot. "We wait for dark, run on the ebb, and until then we're two travelers resupplying. Boring ones."

"Boring," Teo repeated, without conviction.

They were carrying the second load of stores down the lane when Ines saw her.

She stood at the end of the fish-market row, talking to a man in Corvane blue, and for a moment the whole port went silent—not actually, the gulls still screamed and the fishwives still shouted prices, but inside Ines's skull, where a door slammed and everything after was ringing.

Sefa Ilic.

Older. Thinner, with a new scar splitting one eyebrow, hair cropped short in a way that spoke of money spent rather than style chosen. But Ines would have known the set of her shoulders anywhere, the way she stood slightly pigeon-toed when relaxed, the habit of tapping two fingers against her thigh when she was thinking. They had crossed a hundred ridgelines together. They had shared a tent through a whiteout on the Kessarin col. They had—

They had walked down off Mount Veyra with fourteen clients between them and only nine alive, and everything after that had been paperwork and tribunals and the slow public flaying of Ines Montrose's name while Sefa's was quietly cleared, quietly pardoned, quietly erased from the story entirely.

Sefa laughed at something the Corvane man said. The sound carried across the market, familiar as a voice heard through a wall for years.

"She's the pilot," Teo murmured. He'd followed Ines's stare. "Isn't she."

"Yes."

"You know her."

"I knew her." Ines set down the crate of salt pork with exaggerated care, because their hands wanted to do something else entirely. "We guided together. Seven years. Before Veyra."

Teo was quiet a moment. "The disaster. The one that—"

"There was no disaster." The words came out harder than intended, and a fishwife glanced over. Ines lowered their voice. "There was a decision. Made badly, in bad weather, by two people who were both supposed to know better. One of them was me."

"And the other one is about to pilot six gunboats up our wake."

"Yes."

Teo studied them with those too-attentive eyes. "Will she sell you out?"

"She already has, most likely. She just doesn't know it's me yet." Ines picked the crate back up. "Come on. We have a boat to make boring."

* * *

They spent the afternoon performing ordinariness. Ines repaired the pump fitting in plain view on deck, spat on their hands, swore at the knots, did all the small theater of a working guide with nowhere to be. Teo napped below, or appeared to. The chest lay under the floorboards like a held breath.

At dusk Ines went back into the lanes alone, hood up, and found the tavern Brakka had named with a tilt of her head—the Drowned Bell, where information changed hands along with the ale. Ines ordered nothing stronger than tea and took a corner table with a view of the door, and waited, because in Mercies waiting was how you bought things cheaply.

Sefa came in at the turn of the tide.

She sat at the bar, ordered, drank half of it staring at nothing, and Ines watched her across the smoky room and felt the old guilt rise like floodwater. Because here was the thing Ines had never said aloud, not to the tribunal, not to anyone: the route up Veyra that day had been Sefa's objection and Ines's insistence. Sefa had wanted to turn back at the col. Ines had promised the clients the summit—they'd paid for the summit, they'd dreamed of it—and Ines had been young and hungry for reputation and had overridden the one person whose judgment they trusted more than their own. Fourteen went up. Nine came down. And when the inquiry came, Sefa—broken, grieving, loyal to a fault—had told the tribunal the call was shared, had tried to split the blame evenly, and the tribunal, needing a single throat to cut, had cut Ines's because Ines's family name was already suspect, already foreign, already convenient.

Sefa had spent seven years believing she'd abandoned Ines to the blade. Ines had spent seven years knowing the blade should have found them both.

Now she was piloting for Corvane, and Ines understood that too, better than they wanted to. Corvane held debts. Sefa's guiding business had died with Veyra's shadow over it; the tribunals had seen to both of them, one publicly, one privately. People ruined by the same lie tended to drift toward whoever offered them a rope, and Corvane's ropes always came with hooks.

Ines rose, crossed the room, and sat on the stool beside her before courage could fail.

"Sefa."

She turned, and Ines watched recognition arrive in stages—confusion, disbelief, and then something worse than either: fear. Her hand went flat on the bar. "No," she said quietly. "No, you can't be here. Ines. You cannot be here."

"Heard you're flying Corvane's colors these days."

"Don't." Her voice cracked low and urgent. "Don't do that. Don't stand there judging me. Do you know what they hold over me? Do you know what I owe, and to whom?" She stopped herself, glanced around, dragged Ines by the sleeve toward the far end of the bar, into the shadow of a support post. Up close she smelled of engine oil and river water, and there were new lines around her eyes that looked carved rather than grown. "Why are you in Mercies? Why is your boat on the harbormaster's register with a permit that's three weeks forged?"

"So you've read it."

"I wrote the fleet's watch-list, Ines. I described every boat that might run this stretch ahead of us." She closed her eyes. "I described yours. Beam, freeboard, the patched jib. I didn't know it was yours. God help me, I hoped it wasn't."

"And now?"

"Now—" She opened her eyes, and Ines saw the arithmetic running behind them, same as ever, Sefa always counting angles. "Now I'm asking you what you're carrying that six gunboats and a consortium charter are hunting down a dying river. Because whatever it is, it isn't worth your life. It isn't worth—" She caught herself. "It isn't worth what Veyra cost already."

"It's worth more than you think."

"Ines—"

"It's proof, Sefa." Ines kept their voice beneath the tavern noise. "About Vessia. About who sank it, and why, and who was paid. Real proof. Sealed documents."

Something moved through Sefa's face—hunger, briefly, naked and terrible, the hunger of a woman who had spent seven years drowning in someone else's lie and knew exactly what truth was worth. Then it shuttered. "You should burn it."

"You don't mean that."

"I mean it more than I've ever meant anything." She gripped Ines's wrist, hard, her scarred eyebrow drawn tight. "Listen to me. Tomorrow at first light the fleet moves upriver. All six hulls. The captain wants the pilot's opinion on the eastern channels, and I'm going to give it, and I can give it wrong—I can send them aground, buy you a day, maybe two. That's what I can do. That's the price I can pay without Corvane taking everything I have left." Her grip tightened. "But if you're caught with that chest aboard, they won't tribunal you this time. There's no process for this. You'll disappear the way Vessia disappeared. Do you understand me? Disappear."

Ines held her gaze. Seven years of unsaid things sat between them, heavy as ballast. "You could come with us."

Sefa let go as if burned. She laughed once, without any humor in it at all. "There it is. Still the same. Still handing out absolution like it costs you nothing." She stepped back, drained the rest of her drink, and set the glass down with terrible gentleness. "First light, Ines. I'll mischart the eastern channel. After that, you're on your own, and I never saw you."

She walked out into the dark, and Ines stayed on the stool a long moment, hands steady only because they were gripping the bar.

* * *

Back at the mooring, the boat was dark, and Teo was awake, sitting up in the black of the cabin.

"Well?" he said.

"We run on the ebb. Midnight." Ines cast off the lines and stowed them. "Sefa's going to foul the fleet's charts for us. Buy us a day, maybe two."

Teo was quiet. "Can you trust her?"

"No." Ines checked the chest's lashings, then checked them again. "But I trust what she owes. That's different, and it's the only currency that spends in this port."

Below the dock, the ebb began to tug at the pilings, patient as everything the river did. Somewhere out past the point, six hulls rode at anchor with riding lamps lit, and one of them held a woman who had once taken half a mountain's blame to spare Ines the whole of it, and was now spending the rest of her ruin to buy them time.

Ines took the tiller as the boat slid out past the harbor lights, and did not look back at Mercies, because looking back was a luxury the next six tides would not allow—and because in the darkness ahead, where the channel narrowed toward the gorge country, something was already waiting that neither charts nor Sefa's conscience had accounted for.
