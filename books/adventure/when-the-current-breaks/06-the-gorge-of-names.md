## 6. The Gorge of Names

The gorge had a name once. Every rock on the Rimewater had a name once, before the maps were redrawn and the old ones burned. The boatmen who worked the lower reaches called it the Cut, or nothing at all, because no one ran it and therefore no one needed to say it aloud. On Ines's grandmother's chart—the half-melted one curled now in the chest beneath the thwart—it appeared in faded Vessian script: *Gorja e Nomave*. The Gorge of Names.

Ines studied it by the last grey light, holding the paper low so the flame of the lantern wouldn't show above the gunwale.

"You're not serious," Teo said.

Ines didn't answer. The chart showed the gorge as a tight black stitch between two ridgelines, maybe three kilometers of channel, with depth soundings that might have been guesses even when they were drawn—forty years ago, before the dams upstream changed everything about how the river breathed. There was no modern survey. There was no portage around it; the canyon walls went straight up into scrub and shadow. And behind them, six kilometers back at the last bend, the Corvane fleet's running lights were strung across the dark like a carnival that had come to town for an execution.

"They'll expect us to stop," Ines said finally. "Portage at the fish weir, camp, run it at first light. That's what any sane guide does."

"And you're not going to do that."

"I'm going to run it now."

Teo looked at the water. It was already too dark to read it properly—the river had gone from brown to a moving absence, marked only by the white noise of its own current. "You can't see the lines."

"I don't need to see them. I need to hear them." Ines killed the lantern. "Rocks sound different from water. Holes sound different from rocks. I've been listening to rivers since before you were born."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be." Ines wedged the chest under the center thwart and lashed it with the spare painter, double loops, then checked Teo's lifejacket buckles twice without asking permission. "If we flip, you swim left. Left is the eddy wall. Right is the sieve."

"What's a sieve?"

"Water that goes through and people that don't. Swim left."

* * *

The entrance came faster than Ines remembered from the chart's proportions—a narrowing like a throat, walls rising on both sides until the stars were cut down to a ragged seam overhead. The sound changed first. The river's steady murmur broke apart into distinct voices: a long roar ahead-left, a hiss on the right, something underneath both that Ines's spine translated before their mind did. Drop. Big one. Followed by a constriction.

"Paddle when I say," Ines said. "Stop when I say. Don't think."

The bow lifted. For two heartbeats they were paddling downhill through the dark, and then the world became noise and cold and the taste of the river slamming up through the floorboards. Something huge slid past on the right—a rock the size of a chapel, invisible except as a pressure, a parting of the current that tried to suck the stern toward it—and Ines dug hard left, counting strokes, feeling for the tongue of clean water the way a blind person feels for a doorframe.

Then the left voice got louder.

That was wrong. The chart's soundings put the deep channel left. But charts were forty years dead and rivers rewrite themselves every season, and what opened on the left was not a channel but a hole—a reversal, a standing wave folding back on itself, eating its own foam. Ines hauled right. Too late. The stern swung, caught the seam between the two currents, and the river picked the boat up like a thing it had decided to keep.

They went over sideways.

The water took Ines the way water takes everything—completely, without malice, without interest. Cold closed over their head and the universe became tumbling brown chaos, a washing machine of stone and pressure. Their shoulder hit something and spun them. Their boot snagged—only for a half-second, but a half-second is all a river needs—and Ines felt the old familiar arithmetic begin, the calm terrible counting of a drowning person: *how long can I hold, how much air, which way is up.* They'd done this math before, on the mountain, in the white room of an avalanche, and they knew exactly how it ended if you lost count.

Their hand found rock. Held. The current peeled at their fingers like it was peeling fruit.

And then there was weight at the other end of them—a line, taut, jerking—not pulling them out, exactly, but giving them something to pull against, an argument against the river, and Ines realized distantly that someone had rigged a throw rope off the overturned boat, or off themselves, in the dark, in seconds, while the world was still ending. Ines climbed the line hand over hand, lungs burning, and broke the surface in the eddy behind the chapel-rock, gasping, spitting, alive.

Teo was kneeling on the hull of the swamped boat, which had wrapped itself mercifully against the same rock, hauling the rope in with both fists and no technique whatsoever—just fury. His face when Ines hauled themselves onto the rock beside him was white and shaking and absolutely furious too.

"You went left," he shouted over the roar.

"The chart said left."

"The chart is forty years old!"

"Yes," Ines said. "I know."

They looked at each other. The gorge roared. Somewhere downstream the fleet's lights would be closing, patient as tide. And Ines started laughing—helplessly, horribly, the laugh of someone whose body has just remembered it wants to live and doesn't know where to put all that wanting. Teo stared, then laughed too, ugly and cracked, both of them clinging to a wet rock in a drowned country's gorge while their boat drowned politely beside them.

* * *

It took two hours to get the boat upright and bailed enough to limp down the last kilometer of the gorge, walking it through the shallows where they could, lining it with ropes where they couldn't. Twice more the river tested them. Twice more it let them pass. When they finally slid out into the wide slow water below the Cut, the stars had come back whole overhead, and the fleet's lights were nowhere—swallowed, Ines hoped, by the gorge they hadn't dared enter in the dark.

They made camp on a gravel bar, no fire, sharing a blanket against the cold because neither of them had stopped shivering enough to pretend otherwise. The chest sat between them, damp but sealed. Teo wrung out his sleeves with methodical, exhausted hands.

"You rigged that rope," Ines said. "Off the boat. Upside down. In the dark."

"It was either that or watch you die, and then I'd be alone in a gorge with a boat full of documents I can't read." He paused. "I read some of them. Since we left the harbor. Your grandmother's letters."

Ines went very still.

"She wrote to a man named Aldous Ferrin," Teo said quietly. "Fourteen letters. The last ones are dated eleven days before Vessia flooded. She says—" He stopped. Started again. "She says she told him the charges were being moved early. She says she begged him to delay the commission vote."

The river murmured past the gravel bar, indifferent, carrying names none of them would ever hear again.

"My family's name is on the scuttling orders," Ines said. It wasn't a question. They had known since the night they pried open the chest; they had simply been carrying it the way you carry a stone in your boot, refusing to take the shoe off.

"On the orders, yes. But these letters—" Teo turned toward them in the starlight. "These letters say she fought it. Do you understand? Everyone who survived Vessia has spent forty years being told their country deserved to drown, that it was a corrupt little state that sank itself. And here's a Montrose, in writing, trying to stop it, and getting buried with everyone else."

Ines looked at the black water. And the mountain rose up in them—the way it always did, unbidden, the white silence after the slide, Sefa's voice on the radio saying *say it, Ines, say what happened, say it and we both walk away*—and Ines had said nothing, had signed the report that blamed the route, had watched Sefa carry the lie alone until it crushed her career and spared theirs, and had told themselves for nine years that silence was survival.

"There was an avalanche," Ines heard themself say. Almost aloud. The words came out small, worn smooth by disuse. "On the Dorelle traverse. Four clients. I chose the line. I knew the snowpack was wrong—I could feel it, the way you feel weather in your teeth—and I chose it anyway because the clients had paid for the summit and because turning back would have meant admitting I'd misread the season, and my name was—" A breath. "My name was worth something then. So I kept it. And four people went into the white, and Sefa pulled me out, and I let her hang for it."

Teo said nothing. The river said nothing.

"Sefa thinks I'm a coward," Ines said. "She's right. She just doesn't know how right."

"And the chest?" Teo asked softly. "When we find the archive. What are you going to do?"

Ines looked at the chest, at the drowned nation sleeping inside it, at the grandmother they had never met who had apparently been braver on paper than Ines had ever been on a mountain.

"I don't know," they said honestly. "But ask me again at the wreck."

Downstream, faint as a scar of light on the horizon, a searchlight swept the far bank—and held, and began to move toward them.
