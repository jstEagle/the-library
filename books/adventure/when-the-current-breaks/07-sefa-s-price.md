## 7. Sefa's Price

The deadfall camp sat in the elbow of the river where the Rimewater bent north and forgot itself, a shelf of gravel and grey driftwood beneath a wall of leaning pines. Sefa had chosen it because she knew it—had slept here twice in another life, when she and Ines still ran clients together under permits that bore their real names. She arrived an hour before dark, tied her skiff in the same rotted willow Ines had once sworn would outlive them both, and built no fire.

She didn't need light to find the camp. She needed only to wait.

The fleet was four miles downstream, anchored in the last deep pool before the shallows began, its running lights strung across the black water like a carnival that had come to the wrong town. Sefa had eaten at the captain's table for nine days now. She had watched Corvane's men count fuel drums and argue about tide tables, and she had listened to the way they said *Montrose*—flatly, like a number on a ledger, not a name. Nine days of that had done something to her chest she refused to examine.

Corvane held her debts. That was the whole of it, the clean ugly truth: eleven years of salvage work bought on credit against a house she'd lost anyway, against a boat she'd lost too, against a sister's medical bills that had outlived the sister. The company owned her the way the river owned its drowned things—not cruelly, just completely.

And now the river had given Montrose a chest stamped with Vessia, and Corvane wanted it, and Sefa was the only person alive who could walk into a deadfall camp and be heard.

She heard them coming at dusk—the low knock of a pole against stone, the particular care of someone moving through water they didn't trust. Then the skiff slid around the bend, riding high and light, and there was Ines in the stern with the hood back, thinner than the last time Sefa had seen them across a crowded harbor in a country that had since stopped answering her letters. Teo Vasq sat forward, hand resting near the gunwale in a way that pretended not to be ready.

Sefa stood up slowly, palms open.

"Ines."

The pole stopped. For three full seconds the only sound was water worrying the gravel, and then Ines said, "You're piloting for them," and it wasn't a question, and it wasn't surprised either, and somehow that was worse than shouting.

"I'm navigating for them." Sefa kept her voice level. "There's a difference. It matters to me that there's a difference."

"It doesn't matter to the river."

"No," she agreed. "It doesn't."

* * *

They drank tea because tea was what you did, because ritual was the last thing either of them still owned outright. Teo stayed by the boats, watching the treeline with the coiled patience of a man who had learned that history ambushes people. Sefa sat on a driftwood log with her hands around a tin cup gone soft with age, and looked at the person she had once trusted with her life on a mountain, and understood that she was about to do the hardest thing she'd done since the descent from the Col de Verre with half a rope team and no good options.

"I can falsify the fleet's charts," she said.

Ines's cup paused halfway to their mouth.

"Soundings, channel marks, the gorge approaches. I've been correcting them by hand for nine days—small errors, honest-looking ones. By tomorrow night I could have them hunting six feet of water in a channel that runs fourteen. They'd lose two days. Maybe three. Enough to reach the wreck first." She set the cup down. "In exchange, you give me the chest."

The silence stretched long enough that somewhere upstream an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"You'd give it to them," Ines said finally. "You'd hand Vessia to Corvane."

"I'd hand it to Corvane so that it stops being your problem, and becomes mine, and I survive it." Sefa heard her own voice crack on *survive* and hated it. "That's the trade. That's the only trade I came to make."

"And what happens to the documents? To the proof?"

"What proof?" Sefa laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Ines. Look at us. You're a ghost in two registries. I'm a line item in a salvage company's receivables. Whatever's in that chest, whatever's in that wreck—it doesn't come back to people like us. It goes to whoever can afford lawyers in a capital we can't enter. You think publishing it saves anyone? You think *truth* floats?"

"I think drowning it sinks everyone else instead."

"That's not—" Sefa stopped. Breathed. Started again, slower, because this was the thing she had carried down the mountain eleven years ago and had never once set down. "That's not how it works. I know exactly how it works. On the Col de Verre, you made a call. A bad call, maybe—but a call, made fast, in weather that kills slow thinkers. And the report that followed wasn't your report. It was written by people who needed a guide to blame, and they picked you because you were the cheapest thing to bury. I signed my name to a statement I didn't believe because they told me if I didn't, both of us would go down." Her hands had gone white around nothing. "I chose wrong. I know I chose wrong. I've had eleven years to know it. But I chose it because I had a sister dying in a ward that cost more per week than I made in a season, and you—you chose the river, Ines. You got to disappear into something beautiful. I got to stay visible and be owned."

"Is that what you think I did?" Ines's voice was very quiet. "Disappear into something beautiful?"

"Sefa." Teo had drifted closer without either of them marking it, and his voice had a strange gentleness, like a man handling old glass. "What does Corvane actually want from the archive? Have they told you? Or have they told you a version?"

"They want the wreck documented and stripped before the dry season closes the channels. That's all any of us are told."

"And after?" Teo pressed. "After the archive is aboard, after the fleet is paid—what happens to the two guides who found it? One ghost and one debtor?"

Sefa opened her mouth and found nothing there. Because she had asked herself that question every night for nine days, lying in a bunk that smelled of diesel, and every night the answer came back the same shape: *people who know where bodies are buried get buried with them.*

"They'll need the loose ends tidied," she said at last. "Yes. I've done the arithmetic."

"Then give me the chest," Ines said, "and I'll still falsify nothing, and you'll still be owned, and in a month we'll both be stories nobody tells. Or take my counter-offer."

"There is no counter-offer. There's no version of this where you keep it and I keep breathing."

"There is one." Ines leaned forward, firelight—or rather the last grey light off the water—moving on their face. "Falsify the charts. Buy me the time. And when the fleet reaches the wreck, be standing next to the captain when he opens whatever he finds. Be the witness, Sefa. Not the accomplice. You've been the accomplice once already. You know the weight of it. This is the chance to put it down."

"You're asking me to bet my life on your plan, and I don't even know your plan."

"No," Ines said. "You don't. And I'm sorry for that. But I'll tell you one true thing, the thing I should have told you on the mountain, the thing I let you carry alone because carrying it together would have meant saying it out loud." They paused, and Sefa saw—for the first time in eleven years—that Ines Montrose was afraid. Not of the fleet, not of the river. Of the sentence. "The report was right about one thing. It was my call. I called the turn late because I wanted the summit more than I wanted to be careful, and Kessler died because of what I wanted. Everything after—the blacklist, the erasure—I earned the beginning of it. What they did with it after wasn't fair. But the seed was mine."

The words landed in the camp like stones into still water, and the ripples went out and out, and Sefa sat with them, and found that the weight she had been carrying shifted—not lighter, but differently distributed, the way a pack sits right when it's packed right.

"You let me believe it was all them," she said hoarsely.

"You needed it to be all them. So did I."

* * *

Sefa walked back to her skiff in the dark, and Teo fell into step beside her without asking, which she allowed because she was too tired to refuse.

"She's telling the truth," Teo said quietly. "About the mountain. I've read the original incident file. It was scrubbed, but not well."

"Then why tell me?"

"Because you're deciding right now which side of the lie you live on, and lies hold better when both sides are load-bearing." He stopped at the waterline. "Falsify the charts or don't. But ask yourself what Corvane pays witnesses."

Sefa stood a long moment looking at the black water, at the distant string of the fleet's lights, at the eleven years of debt stacked behind her like ballast.

Then she untied her skiff, pushed off into the current, and did not look back—and did not yet know, as the river took her toward the fleet and its waiting ledgers, whether she was rowing toward betrayal or rescue, only that by morning tide, one of those words would fit.

Behind her, in the deadfall camp, a small flame finally caught—and Ines fed it, and watched it, and began, in silence, to count hours.
