## 8. Low Water

Teo felt the river die beneath them before he saw it.

The *Kestrel's Mercy* had been running shallow all morning—her hull whispering over sandbars that should have swallowed a hand's depth more water—but it was the sound that told him. The Rimewater had stopped singing. For four days it had roared and muttered and argued with itself around every bend, and now, in the long straight reach below the Sablefen, it had gone quiet, sliding past the banks like something trying not to be noticed.

Ines stood at the bow with the sounding pole, and Teo watched their shoulders read the water the way other people read letters. Three casts. Four. On the fifth, the pole went down and did not come back soft. It struck bottom with a knock Teo felt in his own teeth.

"Half a meter," Ines said. "Maybe less."

"That can't be right." Teo unfolded the chart they'd bought at Port of Small Mercies, the smuggler's copy with its grease-pencil corrections. "The tide tables say we have another full day of draft. The channel was dredged to two meters after the last dry season—"

"The channel was dredged to two meters by people who assumed the river would cooperate." Ines poled again, harder, and the pole came up streaked with pale silt. "The fen is drinking it. Look at the banks."

Teo looked. What he saw made his stomach tighten: the waterline had dropped a full arm's length since dawn, leaving a glistening band of mud along both shores, stitched with the tracks of wading birds walking where fish had swum yesterday. Stranded reeds lay flat as combed hair. The river wasn't dropping. It was retreating.

Behind them, far back down the reach, a smudge of smoke sat against the sky where no village was marked on any chart.

"Gunboats draw more than we do," Teo said carefully.

"They do. Which means they'll stop at the fen mouth and come overland." Ines shipped the pole and turned, and for a moment Teo saw something he'd been learning to recognize—the guide's face going still and flat, all weather pushed behind glass. "We beach her here. Now, while there's still mud to land on instead of rock."

"You want to abandon the boat?"

"I want to stop pretending she floats." Ines was already moving, kicking open the deck locker, hauling out rope and the canvas sling they'd rigged for the chest back at the deadfall camp. "Boat stays. Chest comes. We walk the old towpath along the east bank—it runs high ground all the way to the lakebed. Two days' march if the mud lets us."

"Two days." Teo did the arithmetic aloud because saying it made it real. "The fleet reaches the fen mouth tonight. Overland by tomorrow afternoon. The wreck drains fully on tomorrow's low tide—" He stopped. "They'll beat us there."

"Not if the mud argues with them the way it's about to argue with us." Ines glanced up, and there was something almost like humor in it, dry as the banks. "I've carried loads through worse. You've walked mountain trails with me for six days, Vasq. This is just a trail with opinions."

* * *

They beached the *Kestrel's Mercy* in a cut of willows, keel grinding into silt that sucked at the hull like a mouth. Teo stood knee-deep in the shallows helping haul the chest onto the sling, and the weight of it astonished him every time—a sealed box of drowned paper, heavier than lead, heavier than grief. Ines took the front carry strap without discussion. Teo took the rear.

The first hour was almost easy. The towpath ran along the crest of the old levee, packed earth underfoot, the dying river glinting gray-green below through gaps in the willows. Teo set his feet down in Ines's prints, learning the rhythm of the load, and let himself think—for perhaps the last time—that this might still end the way he'd planned it in the archive rooms of three cities: documents recovered, authenticated, published, the name Vessia restored to the maps and the ledgers and the mouths of people who had been taught to say *there was never any such country.*

He was still thinking it when Ines stopped so abruptly he walked into the chest.

Ahead, the levee had failed. A whole section of the old towpath had slumped into the fen, torn loose by some flood years ago and never repaired, and beyond the gap the path simply ended in a tangle of drowned brush and standing water that gleamed like hammered tin.

"There's a crossing," Ines said. "Fen people used to run it with planks. The planks are gone or rotten, but the pilings hold. We go light—one at a time, chest passed hand to hand."

"And if someone goes in?"

"Then the others pull them out." Ines said it the way they said everything about danger: as a fact already priced. They shrugged off their pack, stripped it to essentials, and began rigging a rope line between two piling stumps with hands that moved like they'd done this a thousand times, which Teo supposed they had—in another life, on another mountain, before the registries erased them.

Teo held the rope while Ines crossed, light and exact, each step placed like a signature. Then Ines anchored on the far side and Teo came across with the chest strapped to his back, the water sucking at his shins, the whole fen breathing cold up around him, and when he reached the far bank and turned to look back at the little boat shrinking behind them in the willows, he understood—with a clarity that arrived all at once, like ice giving way—that he was no longer on an expedition.

He was on a retreat. And retreats have destinations.

* * *

They made camp that night on a shelf of high ground above a cutoff oxbow, a fire small enough to hide in a coffee tin. The chest sat between them like a third traveler. Ines ate in silence, eyes on the dark stretch of river, where the smoke-smudge from afternoon had thickened into a faint orange glow at the fen mouth. Lanterns. Or searchlights.

Teo waited until the fire burned down to coals. Then he said, "Show me one page."

Ines didn't move. "No."

"One page. One document. I've hauled this box sixty kilometers on my back and I haven't seen a single sheet of what we're carrying. I'm not asking to keep anything. I'm asking to know what I'm bleeding for."

The silence stretched. Somewhere out in the fen, a bird cried once and thought better of it.

Then Ines reached into their coat—not toward the chest, but toward an inner pocket—and drew out a single folded sheet, handled with a care that told Teo it had traveled separately from the beginning. They held it out across the coals without letting go.

It was a shipping manifest, half the ink blurred to ghosts, but the header survived: *Meridian Star — final loading order — Vessia Crest Line.* And in the margin, in a different hand, a note in faded brown-black ink:

*Third hold sealed against orders. Cargo manifests falsified at source. If the board proceeds, the river itself will bury the evidence. There is no clean version of this. — E.M.*

E.M. Teo looked up. "Your grandmother."

"Elena Montrose. Assistant purser, Meridian Star." Ines took the page back, folded it along creases gone soft as cloth. "She signed the falsified manifests. She also wrote that note and hid it inside the seal lining, which is the only reason anyone alive knows the official record is a lie. Both things are true. That's the part nobody wants."

Teo turned this over, and then he asked the question he'd been circling for six days, the one that had started as suspicion and hardened somewhere on the towpath into certainty. "When we get to the wreck. When you open the vault. What exactly is your plan for what's inside?"

Ines fed a stick to the coals and watched it catch.

"Ines."

"You won't like it."

"I already don't like it. Say it anyway."

Ines looked at him then, fully, across the small red light, and their voice came out level and quiet and absolutely certain. "The charges are already set, Teo. I set them at the deadfall camp, in the hull seams, while you slept. Scuttling pattern. When the vault is open and I've seen what's in it, I bring the Meridian Star down, and the channel floods the grave, and Corvane gets mud, and the government gets mud, and my family's name stays buried with the country it helped drown."

The words landed one by one, and Teo felt each of them. He thought of the archive rooms, the microfilm, the three cities of scholars who had argued for forty years about whether Vessia had ever existed. He thought of his grandmother's stories, told in a kitchen in exile, in a language the neighbors were taught not to hear.

"No," he said.

"It's not a vote."

"You're right, it isn't." Teo leaned forward, and the firelight put his shadow tall against the willows. "Because destroying it isn't truth, Ines. It's just a second sinking. Your grandmother hid that note so the lie couldn't stand unchallenged—and you're going to make sure nothing ever challenges anything. Corvane wins either way. The registries win either way. Everyone who says Vessia never existed gets to be right forever, because you'll have made it true."

"They sold my name to buy their silence—"

"And mine!" It came out louder than he meant, cracking the night open. He lowered his voice, but not the heat in it. "You think you're the only one the drowning touched? My family lost a country. Not a permit—a *country*. And your plan is to drown it twice."

Ines said nothing. The coals ticked. Far downstream, the orange glow at the fen mouth had brightened, and Teo realized with a cold drop in his stomach that it wasn't lanterns.

It was engines running their searchlights across the water, closer than the arithmetic allowed.

Ines rose without a word, kicked dirt over the fire, and shouldered the front strap of the sling. But at the edge of the dark they paused, half-turned, and said quietly:

"Dawn tide drains the lakebed. After that, the hull stands open to the sky for six hours. Whatever happens there—happens once." A beat. "Sleep fast, Vasq."

And Teo sat alone in the dark with the chest between his knees and the searchlights crawling nearer down the dying river, doing the only arithmetic left to him: how to reach the vault first, and what to do with his hands when he got there.
