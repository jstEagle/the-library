## 9. The Wreck of the Meridian Star

The lakebed smelled of old salt and dead fish, a mile of cracked gray mud stretching to the horizon like the skin of something flayed. Ines came down the last slope with the chest strapped across their back and Teo half a step behind, both of them sinking to the ankle in mud that sucked at every stride. Above them, the dry season sky was white and pitiless. Behind them, far back along the haul route, dust hung in a thin brown column where the gunboats' shore party was moving.

They had beaten them by hours. Maybe less.

"There," Teo said.

The Meridian Star lay on her side in the center of the basin, and she was enormous—four hundred feet of blackened hull exposed to the air for the first time in sixty years, her barnacled flank rising out of the mud like the ribcage of a beached god. The drained lake had taken everything else: the pilings, the dock towns, the drowned orchards. But it had saved her for last, as if the water knew what she carried and couldn't quite bring itself to give her up.

Ines stood at the edge of the hull shadow, chest heaving, and felt the strangest thing: recognition. Their grandmother's letters had described this ship—the brass fittings, the chapel below decks, the archive vault built into the keel line "so that even if Vessia drowns, its memory will keep." Ines had read those words in a flood-scoured snag two hundred miles upriver, and now here was the sentence made solid, rusting under an open sky.

"Vault access is through the engine trunk," they said. "Grandmother's map shows a hatch on the underside. We'll have to go in through the bilge."

"The fleet's twenty minutes behind us. Maybe fifteen."

"Then we climb fast."

* * *

Inside, the ship was a cathedral of rust. They moved through corridors furred with mineral bloom, past doors fused shut, their boots ringing on deck plates that hadn't heard footsteps since before either of them was born. Teo's headlamp swept across a wall plaque still legible beneath the corrosion: MERIDIAN STAR — PRIDE OF THE VESSIAN FEDERATED TIDES. He stopped and put his palm flat against it, just for a second, the way you'd touch a grave marker.

"My great-grandfather was a purser on this run," he said quietly. "He went down with her. My family never said his name again. That's what losing a country does—it doesn't just drown the place. It drowns the grief."

Ines didn't answer. They were counting hatchways against the map burned into their memory, and what they were feeling was not grief but dread, because the letters in the chest had told them exactly why the Meridian Star went down, and who had signed the orders, and one of the signatures belonged to a Montrose.

The vault hatch was where the map promised: a circular door set into the keel trunk, sealed with a wheel-lock crusted green. Ines braced both feet against the frame and hauled. Nothing. Again. The wheel gave a shriek like a living thing and turned a quarter-inch.

"Together," Teo said, and put his hands over theirs.

On the third pull the seal broke with a wet pop, and cold air breathed out of the vault—air older than both their families, air that had been waiting.

* * *

The vault was small, a steel room no bigger than a chapel, and it was full.

Shelves ran floor to ceiling, and on them sat lead-lined document cases, hundreds of them, each stamped with the crest of Vessia—the wave and the star. Shipping manifests. Naval ledgers. Parliamentary minutes. The entire administrative memory of a nation that six governments had agreed, in treaty after treaty, to call a cartographic error.

Teo walked the aisle slowly, touching nothing, and Ines watched him come apart and reassemble himself, watched him understand that this was real, that his family's erased grief had a body, that it had been down here all along holding its breath.

"It could rebuild a country," he whispered. "Or at least a history. This is everything they took."

"It's also everything Corvane wants to sell," Ines said. "Manifests prove the sinking was ordered from inside. By my grandmother's firm. By my family." They pulled the chest off their shoulders and set it on the floor between them. "These letters name names. The rest of it—" they gestured at the shelves "—is leverage. Leverage gets bought, buried, or fought over. It doesn't get read."

"So we take it out. Publish it. All of it."

"And when Corvane's lawyers get hold of the originals? When the government that erased me buys the printing plates?" Ines shook their head. "I've spent ten years being a rumor, Teo. I know how erasure works. You don't beat it by handing people the original. You beat it by making copies faster than they can burn them."

Above them, faint through sixty feet of dead ship, came a sound that was not the wind: engines. Many engines, on the lakebed road.

"They're here," Teo said.

* * *

Ines had planned for this hour on every sleepless night since tide three, and the plan had always ended the same way, in the same terrible arithmetic. The scuttling charges were still in the hull—sixty-year-old demolition blocks wired into the keel frames, installed by the Vessian navy so the flagship could never be captured. Grandmother's letters had included the detonator codes, because apparently the Montroses had been trusted with the keys to everything, including the drowning.

Ines found the charge housing aft, pried the corroded plate, and there they were: wax-sealed blocks in their cradles, patient as seeds. The primer wires were green with age but intact. The detonator socket took the code drum from the chest, and the drum fit like it had been machined yesterday.

"You're going to blow the ship," Teo said. Not a question. He'd known since the gorge; Ines had seen it in his face for days.

"I'm going to drop the vault section into the channel cut. The dry season breaks in three days—the lake refills, forty feet of water over the wreck. Anyone who wants this archive can learn to dive."

"And the truth?"

"Is already in my head, and yours, and in these—" Ines tapped the chest. "I'm keeping the letters. The rest stays with the ship."

"That's destruction, Ines. That's your family winning twice."

"No." Their voice came out low and steady, and it surprised them. "My family won when they sank her and everyone agreed to forget. This is me refusing the inheritance."

Boots on the hull above. Voices, amplified by the ship's throat. Then a voice Ines knew better than their own, calling down the corridor:

"Ines! I can see your light! Don't do anything stupid!"

Sefa.

* * *

She came down the ladder first, rifle slung, hands open the way she'd held them at the deadfall camp, and behind her came the Corvane captain—a broad, sunburnt woman named Ilse Vant, with a salvage fleet's worth of calm and a pistol that stayed holstered only because the situation hadn't required it yet.

Four more of Vant's crew filled the corridor mouth. The math was bad and getting worse.

"You made good time," Sefa said. Her eyes went to the open vault, to the shelves, and Ines watched ten years of debt and guilt move across her face like weather. "We both did. So let's end it clean. Vant gets the archive, Corvane clears my paper, and you walk away with your boat and your passenger and whatever page you want to keep for yourself. Nobody has to die in a hole in the ground."

"That's the same offer you made at the camp."

"And you're standing in the same place, Ines. Out of options." Sefa stepped closer, and her voice dropped to something only Ines could hear. "I was there too. On the mountain. I carried the same lie you did, except nobody blacklisted me—they just made me useful. There's a difference between falling and being pushed, and you got to be the one who fell. Don't make me the villain for surviving differently."

"You falsified the survey," Ines said. "You signed the report that cleared the route."

"And you saw the cornice crack and said nothing, because saying something meant admitting we'd brought them up the wrong side!" It cracked out of her before she could stop it, and the corridor went very quiet. Even Vant had gone still. "Ten years, Ines. Ten years we've each been carrying half the disaster, waiting for the other one to confess first."

Ines looked at her—really looked—and felt the mountain open under them both, all over again, the white roar of it, the eleven names. And underneath the horror, almost gently, came the thought they'd been running from since the snag: *it wasn't all the lie. Some of it was the mountain. Some of it was weather. But some of it was us, and I have to say which parts.*

"Not here," Ines said. "Not in front of them. But yes. First. I'll say my half, Sefa. All of it. To whoever will print it."

Something moved through Sefa's face—relief, or its funeral.

Vant cleared her throat. "Touching. The archive, please. My crews are cutting the shore road behind you and the tide chart says you have no other exit. Hand over the cases and the chest, and this stays a salvage operation instead of a salvage incident."

Ines's hand was already inside the charge housing, on the code drum.

"Sefa," they said. "The charges are live. Sixty years old, Vessian navy, keel frames. If I turn this drum, the vault section drops into the channel cut and takes everything with it—including us, if we're slow."

"Don't," Vant said, and the pistol came out, and four rifles came up behind her.

"You'll shoot, and the concussion sets it off anyway," Ines said. "Your own engineers flagged the instability—I read your tender documents at Port of Small Mercies. You didn't buy the harbormaster, Captain. You bought a bluff."

And then Sefa moved—not toward Ines, but toward Vant, shoulder checking the woman's gun arm so the shot went wide and high and struck a shelf in a spark of lead and flame. Ines turned the drum.

The sound was not an explosion so much as a decision the ship had been waiting sixty years to make. The keel frames let go in sequence, deep below, a rolling thunder like the earth clearing its throat, and the whole cathedral of rust groaned, tilted, and began to go. Shelves toppled. Lead cases slid like coins into the dark. Teo grabbed the chest strap and Ines grabbed Teo and they ran up the tilting corridor as the deck became a ramp, as the vault swallowed itself, as the Meridian Star folded along the lines her builders had drawn for exactly this moment and slid, stern-first, into the channel cut with a impact that came up through the mud and into their teeth.

Water followed—black, sudden, boiling up through the broken hull as the channel claimed its own. Ines and Teo and Sefa came out of the engine trunk into daylight and ran, really ran, across the cracking mud while behind them the wreck settled into the flooding cut and the lake, patient, began the long work of taking her back.

Vant's crew stood in a stunned line at the basin's edge. Vant herself was screaming into a radio about tonnage and tide windows and lawyers, and none of it mattered, because in three days there would be forty feet of water over the archive and no fleet on earth equipped to argue with it.

Sefa dropped to her knees in the mud, laughing or sobbing, Ines couldn't tell. "You absolute bastard," she said. "You blew up your inheritance."

"I kept a page," Ines said.

They opened the chest there in the shadow of the drowning ship, and took out the letter—the one bearing their grandmother's signature, dated three days before the sinking, addressed to the Vessian admiralty: *I withdraw my firm's consent. I will not be party to this. Find another hand.* A letter that proved the Montrose name had once, briefly, tried to say no.

Not innocence. Testimony. It would have to be enough.

Far downstream, at the mouth of the channel, the first fingers of the returning tide were already moving inland—and Ines, watching the water come, understood that the river that had given them the chest intended to take something back as well.

It was coming for the story. And this time, Ines meant to meet it standing.
