## 10. One Page

The water came back the way it always did—without apology.

We heard it before we saw it, a long low exhale rolling up the drained channel from the sea gates, and then the Rimewater arrived, gray and patient, filling the scar it had spent six tides carving. It climbed the mud in silver sheets. It found the collapsed throat of the wreck and poured itself in without ceremony, closing over the Meridian Star and everything the Meridian Star had carried, and it did not pause, did not grieve, did not ask anyone's permission. Rivers never do. That was the first lesson the mountains ever taught me, and the last one the river confirmed.

Teo and I stood on the high bank with the water rising past our boots, and we watched the place where the archive had been drown for the second time in a hundred years.

"You could have sold it," Teo said. Not accusing. Just setting the fact down between us like a stone, the way he set everything down, flat and honest, a man who had spent his whole life collecting other people's lies and had finally run out of his own.

"I could have," I said.

"You could have given it to me. The whole archive. Every page."

"You'd have printed it."

"That was the idea."

I looked at him. Salt spray, river mud to the knees, a rope burn across one palm from the gorge that he'd never once complained about. A man who had crossed half a country chasing a nation that officially did not exist, armed with nothing but a family name and a printer's stubbornness. He deserved the whole truth and I had given him a wreck and a flood instead.

"Paper burns, Teo," I said. "Governments burn it. Companies burn it. Corvane would have burned it in a warehouse fire by spring, and the registries would have stayed clean, and my name would have stayed dirty, and Vessia would have stayed a rumor. I've watched what happens to truth when it's kept. It doesn't stay truth. It turns into leverage."

"And one page is different how?"

"One page isn't kept," I said. "One page is sent."

I took it out of the oilskin then, because he'd earned the seeing of it. My grandmother's hand, the same hand I'd known from the margin notes in her mountain ledgers, the same slanted, impatient script that had taught me to read contours and current lines. Dated nine days before the sinking. Addressed to the Vessian admiralty, copied in triplicate, one copy kept—the one she'd sewn into her coat when she fled the country that her own family was helping to drown.

*They are telling you the gates will fail on their own. They will not fail on their own. I am a Montrose and I am telling you we built the charges ourselves.*

She had signed it. She had named the others. She had known, when she wrote it, that naming them would cost her everything, and she had written it anyway, and then she had spent the rest of her life in exile raising a granddaughter on the edge of a different river, teaching her to guide, teaching her to read water, never once saying why the water owed her nothing and she owed the water everything.

I had spent nine years believing my family's silence was shame. It wasn't. It was penance, paid in advance, by a woman who had tried.

"She tried to stop it," Teo said quietly. "And they sank it anyway."

"And then they erased her for trying. Both countries. Same pen." I folded the oilskin closed. "That's the inheritance, Teo. Not the chest. Not the wreck. That."

* * *

The last tide carried us down the lower Rimewater in a borrowed skiff, the fleet's gunboats anchored astern of us in the widening estuary like dogs that had been called off and didn't believe it. Captain Verrane of Corvane had watched us drift past the salvage barges at dawn, and she had not fired, and she had not followed, because there was nothing left to take. I had watched her face as the water closed over the coordinates. It was not rage. It was the look of a woman doing arithmetic and arriving at zero. She'd have her own accounting to make in Corvane's offices, and it would not be a kind one, and I found I could not make myself care.

Sefa was not on the barges. I looked for her. I looked the way you look for a scar in a mirror—automatically, expecting it, needing to know it's still there.

Teo noticed. "She could have stopped us at the deadfall," he said. "She had the falsified charts in her hand. She could have handed them to Verrane and been out of debt by now."

"She gave us the wrong channel markers and the right ones both," I said. "She made sure we'd live and made sure she could deny it. That's Sefa. She's been surviving the same disaster I have, just from the other side of it." I watched the estuary birds lift off the mudflats in gray sheets. "Maybe someday I'll tell her she's forgiven. Maybe someday I'll believe it myself."

The dry season was doing its work all around us. The side channels we'd run at flood tide on the way up were braided ribbons now, then strings of pools, then memory. The Rimewater was closing behind us mile by mile, shutting its doors for the season, and by the time we reached the sea port at Grayharrow the river we'd escaped down would not exist in any navigable sense until the rains returned. No one could follow what we'd done. No one could verify it either. Which was, I understood suddenly, the entire point of the page in my coat.

* * *

Grayharrow smelled of tar, drying nets, and the particular desperation of a port at the end of a dry season, full of boats that couldn't leave and crews that couldn't be paid. We tied up at the public quay because neither of us was hiding anymore, and that felt like walking out of a dark room into a bright one with your eyes still shut.

The post office was a single room with a clerk who'd seen everything and cared about none of it, which is the finest quality a clerk can have. There was a wire desk, a scale, a rack of envelopes, and a board of pinned notices for ships that had stopped sailing years ago.

I bought one envelope. Heavy paper, the kind that doesn't tear in weather. I addressed it in block letters, because my hand was not steady enough for anything finer.

*Meridian Courier, Attn: J. Okonkwo, Investigations Desk.*

Okonkwo had been writing about Vessia for eleven years, the only journalist in two countries who refused to use the past tense, the one the registries called a crank and the salvage companies called a problem. Teo had her name tattooed on his plans the way sailors tattoo ports. He'd given it to me at the deadfall camp, in the dark, when we were both too tired to lie.

I put the letter inside. My grandmother's hand, naming names, including her own. Including mine, by blood, by the line that ran from her exile to my blacklisting like a single unbroken rope.

Teo stood beside me at the desk, and I felt him not looking at the envelope, which was its own kind of discipline.

"You understand what this does," he said. "Once Okonkwo prints it, you're not invisible anymore. The blacklist becomes a story. The story becomes a case. There will be inquiries. Corvane's lawyers. Hearings where people say your name out loud in rooms you're not in. You spent nine years building a life small enough to hide in, and this burns it down."

"I know."

"You could still keep it. Bury it. The river's closed. Nobody could prove—"

"That's exactly why I can't keep it." I weighed the envelope in my hand. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed nine years and one drowned country and a grandmother's courage and a partner's betrayal and a mountain I still couldn't say the name of without my chest closing. "Invisibility was the sentence, Teo. I served it. But I never committed the crime. And a person who accepts a sentence for a crime they didn't commit isn't humble. They're just buried alive and calling the grave a bed."

I looked at the clerk. "Wire and post. Fastest service to the capital."

The clerk took the envelope with two fingers, weighed it, and named a price that was frankly extortionate, and I paid it gladly, the most expensive piece of paper I would ever buy, and worth every coin.

The clerk dropped it in the outgoing bin. Just that. A small sound, paper on paper. Nine years of silence ending in a sound you could barely hear.

* * *

We walked back down to the quay as the sun came up properly, burning the mist off the estuary, and the Rimewater's mouth lay open to the sea, wide and glittering and already shallowing, the sandbars surfacing like the backs of sleeping animals. Behind us the river was closing for the season, gate by gate, pool by pool. Ahead of us the sea, and after the sea, whatever came next.

Teo sat on the bollard beside me and produced, of all things, a battered notebook, and began writing, and I realized he'd been writing the whole time—since the gorge, maybe since the chest, maybe since long before he ever stepped aboard my boat with his forged papers and his careful lies. A descendant's account. A testimony of his own.

"What will you do?" he asked. "When it prints. When they come looking for the Montrose who found the archive and drowned it."

"Guide work," I said. "There's always river work. And after the rains, the Rimewater opens again, and someone will need to be shown what's at the bottom of it." I turned my face to the light. "I spent nine years being no one from nowhere. I think I can survive being someone from somewhere, even if the somewhere is a scandal."

"And the mountain?" he said, gently, because he was the only person alive who knew its name, the one I'd finally said out loud in the dark of the gorge with the water trying to kill us both. "The disaster they erased you for. Will you tell that too? Okonkwo will ask. The whole truth or none of it—that's how she works."

I watched the last of the mist burn off the water. Somewhere upcountry, in channels that no longer existed, the Meridian Star sat under new water with its secrets, and my grandmother's letter was riding east in a mail sack toward the light, and Sefa was somewhere paying her debts or not, and the dry season was closing the river behind us like a book.

"I'll tell it," I said. "All of it. What I did, what I didn't do, what really broke loose that day on the ice. Not because it clears me. Because testimony is the only inheritance worth passing down."

Teo nodded and wrote that down too.

The tide turned. I felt it before I saw it—the long pull of the estuary reversing, the water beginning to run out to sea, carrying everything it had taken and everything it had given back. I cast off the skiff's line, and the current took us, and the Rimewater, which had started all of this by dropping a sealed chest at my feet, carried us out past the last sandbar and let us go.

Behind us, the river closed.

Ahead of us, the story began.
