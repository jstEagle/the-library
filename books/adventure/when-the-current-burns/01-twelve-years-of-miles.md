## 1. Twelve Years of Miles

The storm came off the Kestrel Range the way storms always did there—sideways, mean, and without apology.

Orsen Nystrom felt it before she saw it, a pressure change in her back teeth and a sourness in the wind that smelled like iron and old snow. She had been reading weather for twenty-six years, first as a girl trailing her father's traplines through the birch flats below the range, later as the most sought-after high-country guide in three provinces, and now—these last twelve years—as something with no official name at all. An unlicensed guide. A ghost with a compass. The kind of woman wealthy men paid in cash and never mentioned to their wives.

"Miss Nystrom." Aldous Vey's voice carried thin over the wind, pitched with the particular whine of a man whose boots had cost more than her cabin and were performing exactly as badly as they deserved. "You said we'd be below the tree line by noon."

"I said we'd be below the tree line by noon if the sky held," Orsen called back, not turning around. "The sky didn't hold."

She stood at the shoulder of the ridge with her hood down because hoods stole peripheral vision, letting the rain needle her face while she read the terrain the way other people read letters from home. The Kestrel Range unspooled northward in gray serrations, its flanks stitched with scree chutes and hanging snowfields she knew the way a priest knows scripture. That notch to the northwest—Kolbein's Saddle—would funnel the wind into something that could peel a man off the rock. The drainage below it, the one the maps called nothing at all because the cartographers had never bothered, dropped into a spruce hollow where they could wait out the worst of it under stone overhangs older than any government that claimed the land.

Twelve years since anyone had let her guide here legally. Twelve years, and the mountains had not once pretended not to know her.

"We're going down into the drainage," she said, turning at last. Vey stood ten meters back, clutching his ice axe like a man holding a dead bird, his expedition parka beaded silver with rain. Behind him, his two hired porters—quiet brothers out of Brannvik who took her money and asked no questions—waited with the patience of people who had grown up under this same sky. "Now. Before the wind finds the saddle."

"You assured me—"

"Aldous." She kept her voice level. It was a skill, the level voice; she'd built it plank by plank across twelve years of clients who thought money purchased certainty. "In forty minutes, that saddle will produce gusts above a hundred kilometers an hour. In fifty, visibility will drop to arm's length. If we're still on this ridge when either happens, I will not be able to save you, and I would like very much for you to understand that I am not being dramatic. I am being arithmetic."

Something in her face must have landed, because Vey's mouth closed. He was sixty-one, soft in the way of men who bought their altitude rather than earned it, but he wasn't stupid—that was the exhausting part. Stupid clients she could simply steer. Smart ones wanted to know things. Why she wouldn't take them into certain valleys. Why she flinched at the word *blacklist* the way other people flinched at gunfire. Why she counted her miles aloud some nights around the stove, ticking them off like beads, as if the sum might eventually come to something.

They descended. Orsen went first, always first, her boots finding purchase on wet granite where there appeared to be none, her body remembering every hold on this face the way a tongue remembers a lost tooth. Below the ridge the drainage opened like a green throat, spruce closing overhead, and the storm's roar dulled to a pressure against the canopy. Rain came through in cold coins. She found the overhang she remembered—a slab of gneiss tilted against the hillside, floored with dry duff—and got Vey beneath it, got the stove lit, got hot water moving toward coffee, all in the time it took him to complain twice.

"You've been here before," he said. Not a question. He watched her stake the tarp with hands that moved without hesitation, each motion worn smooth as river stone. "Many times."

"Once or twice."

"The maps show no trail into this drainage."

"The maps show a lot of things." She handed him the cup. Her fingers, she noticed distantly, had a fine tremor—not fear, never fear, just the low electrical hum of a body that had been running on competence and caffeine since dawn. "Drink that. We move when the front passes, roughly two hours. You'll want to sleep."

He didn't sleep. They never did, the first night, the rich ones. They sat wrapped in gear worth more than her truck and watched her work, and what they saw seemed to fascinate them: the way she checked anchors twice without looking at them, the way she laid out rope in coils so precise they looked machined, the way she glanced at the sky every ninety seconds even inside the shelter, some interior instrument always running. Competence, Orsen had learned, was a kind of performance. You performed it long enough and it became load-bearing. Take it away and you found out what was underneath, which in her case was mostly exhaustion and a ledger of miles.

She sat at the edge of the overhang with her back to the rock and let the arithmetic run, because it always ran. Eleven point four kilometers today. Two hundred and six this month. Somewhere past four thousand this year, though she'd stopped trusting that figure—the early years were blurred, walked half-mad through country that didn't care whether she ate. Four thousand kilometers of ground covered since the day she'd walked out of the Survey Office in Brannvik with her license revoked and her name struck from the rolls, and what did the sum buy? Nothing. That was the trick of counting miles: it was a currency that spent only forward. You couldn't redeem them for the year you lost. You couldn't trade them back for the client who died on the Halden Icefield, or for the partner whose testimony had ended her career in a hearing room that smelled like floor wax, or for the version of herself who had still believed that truth, patiently told, would eventually win.

Miles didn't fail you, though. That was why she counted them. Years failed you. People failed you. Governments failed you with paperwork. But a kilometer was a kilometer, honest as gravity, and if you stacked enough of them end to end you could pretend you were going somewhere instead of just going.

"Miss Nystrom." Vey again, quieter now, the wind dropping outside so his voice didn't have to fight. "May I ask you something indelicate?"

"You may ask."

"Why does a guide of your caliber work without credentials? My office tried to hire you through proper channels, four years ago. You weren't in the registry. The man I spoke with went pale when I said your name and advised me, quite strongly, to hire someone else."

Orsen fed a stick of spruce to the stove and watched the flame take it. Outside, the front was passing; she could hear the storm's center grinding away northward, dragging its weight over the high peaks, and in an hour the air would come clean and cold behind it.

"Because I'm good at my work," she said, "and bad at politics."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one for sale."

He laughed—a short, surprised bark—and for a moment he looked younger, less like a portfolio and more like a man. "Fair enough. Get me off this mountain alive and I won't press."

"I'll get you off this mountain alive regardless," Orsen said. "That's the part people keep misunderstanding about my situation."

* * *

They came down out of the high country two days later, ahead of schedule, Vey's frostbitten vanity intact and his awe carefully concealed beneath fresh complaints about the food. The trail bottomed out through black spruce and bog myrtle, then along the shingle flats of the Vaskr tributary—cold, quick, the color of hammered tin—and Orsen felt the familiar tightening in her chest as the water slid past beside them, heading south toward country the maps refused to finish drawing.

Every river in these highlands had a name except that one. Official charts showed the tributary rising from the Kestrel Glacier and terminating—just ending, a blue line dissolving into blank paper—somewhere above the gorge country. Orsen had stood on its banks in a dozen seasons and watched it run, patient and purposeful, toward a destination no cartographer would admit to. Rivers didn't end. Rivers were erased. There was a difference, and she had come to suspect the difference mattered, though she'd never said so aloud to anyone.

The checkpoint sat where the trail met the old mining road: a timber hut, a striped pole, a stovepipe leaking woodsmoke into the drizzle. Provincial boundary control, staffed usually by bored conscripts who waved through trappers and fined tourists for unpunched permits. Orsen slowed, as she always did, and put on the face she wore for checkpoints—neutral, tired, forgettable.

The guard who stepped out was not bored. He was maybe fifty, heavyset, with a surveyor's brass pin on his collar and eyes that went straight past Vey's expensive gear and fixed on her face like a man recognizing a photograph.

"Nystrom," he said.

Her hand did not go to her axe, though everything in her wanted to. "Morning, officer. Client transport, upper Kestrel loop, permit's in the—"

"I know who you are." He said it without heat, which was somehow worse. He came closer, boots crunching the shingle, and lowered his voice so Vey and the porters couldn't hear. "Orsen Nystrom. Halden Icefield. Twelve years ago. I was stationed at Brannvik then. I remember the hearing."

The rain ticked against her hood. Downstream, the unnamed river ran on toward its secret, indifferent as ever.

"Then you know I'm not licensed for this range," she said evenly. "And you know what happens next. Write your citation. I'll pay the fine. My client will be on his way and none of this needs to take longer than it already has."

The guard looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes—not cruelty, not quite pity either. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man seeing a monument to something he'd only heard described.

"My sister lives in Brannvik," he said finally. "Her boy got lost on the moor two winters back. Three days in the wet cold. Every volunteer they had out looking." He paused. "They say it was a woman with no name on her permit who found him. Carried him nine kilometers to the road. Never left a name. Never filed a report. The family's been trying to thank her for two years and the province can't tell them who she is."

Orsen said nothing. The rain filled the silence.

"So here's what happens next," the guard said. "I write in my log: *client transport, routine, no violations.* And you pass. And you don't come through here again this season, because the man replacing me on the next rotation isn't sentimental." He stepped back and lifted the pole. "Go on, then."

Vey caught up to her a hundred meters down the road, breathless with indignation on her behalf. "What was that? Did he fine you? I'll cover any—"

"He gave us directions," Orsen said.

"To where?"

"Away." She lengthened her stride, wanting the checkpoint behind her, wanting the whole day behind her, wanting—though she'd have denied it under torture—to be home, in her cabin, with the stove lit and the mile-ledger open and the numbers marching obediently across the page. "Two days' walk south, Mr. Vey, there's a road, and at the end of the road there's a town, and at the end of the town there's a train, and you'll be on it. After that, our contract is complete."

"And after that for you?"

She almost laughed. Rich men and their questions. "After that, more miles."

But the guard's words rode with her the rest of the day, unwelcome as a stone in a boot. *Halden Icefield. Twelve years ago.* Twelve years of walking, and the world had apparently spent them memorizing her name anyway. She had erased herself as thoroughly as the cartographers had erased that river—struck her own blue line from the record, dissolved into blank paper—and yet a stranger at a timber hut had looked at her and seen the whole catastrophe at once, the hearing room, the floor wax, the testimony that had hung her.

Names, she thought bitterly, crossing the shingle flats as the light went copper and low. You couldn't burn them out. They grew back like heather.

* * *

Her cabin sat in a fold of the foothills two days' walk beyond the checkpoint, deliberately unremarkable: sod roof, turf walls, a woodpile ranked like a regiment. She paid her taxes through a Brannvik accountant who never asked questions, received mail at a box registered to a name that was almost hers, and lived, by any government's accounting, nowhere at all.

She was unpacking wet rope in the yard when she saw the figure coming up the track.

Not a client. Clients arrived by truck or on foot with too much luggage and a rehearsed smile. This figure came alone, on foot, in a provincial survey officer's gray-green field coat, and walked the way people walk when they've been walking for a long time and intend to keep walking until someone answers a question. A leather document case hung at her side, rain-darkened, held close to her ribs like something stolen.

Orsen set down the rope coil slowly and did not reach for the axe leaning against the woodpile, though she noted its position the way she noted everything—automatically, professionally, the way she noted loose rock and changing wind.

The officer stopped at the edge of the yard. Younger than Orsen expected—thirty perhaps, maybe thirty-two—with a square jaw chapped raw by weather and eyes the flat gray of the river that wasn't on maps. She looked exhausted in a way Orsen recognized intimately: not tired from the walk, but tired from before the walk, tired from something that had happened to her somewhere else and followed her here.

"Orsen Nystrom?" the woman said.

"Depends who's asking."

"Dagny Holt. Regional Survey Office, Brannvik district." She didn't offer a hand. Instead she lifted the document case slightly, and there was something in her grip—white-knuckled, desperate—that made Orsen's stomach drop before a single word was spoken. "Six days ago, a government survey team went missing near the headwaters of the Kestrel tributary. Six people. No tracks leaving camp. Gear intact." Holt's jaw tightened. "And I need the only guide in this province who knows that country better than the people who drew the maps."

The wind moved through the spruce behind the cabin, carrying the smell of rain and distance. Somewhere far south, the unnamed river ran on toward whatever the maps refused to name.

"I think you've made a mistake," Orsen said. "I don't guide anymore."

"No," Dagny Holt agreed quietly. Her eyes went to the rope in Orsen's hands, still dripping, still coiled with machine precision. "You just count the miles."
