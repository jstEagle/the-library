## 2. The Officer Who Wouldn't Leave

The knock came at dusk, three measured raps, the kind that belonged to someone who had rehearsed it on other doors.

Orsen Nystrom set down the whetstone and listened. Her cabin sat eleven miles past the last legal trailhead, at the end of a track that officially did not exist, which meant anyone standing on her porch had already broken a rule or two to get there. That narrowed the possibilities considerably. Debt collectors didn't come this far. Neither did friends, because she hadn't kept any.

She opened the door with the chain still latched.

A woman stood in the blue half-light, snow gathering on the shoulders of a survey officer's grey coat, official and wrong for this altitude. She was maybe thirty-five, wind-scoured across the cheekbones, with the particular posture of someone who had been sitting in vehicles all day and hated every minute of it. A canvas satchel hung from one shoulder, heavy with whatever she'd carried up eleven miles of bad track rather than trust to a pack animal.

"Orsen Nystrom," the woman said. Not a question.

"You've got the wrong cabin."

"I don't." She held up a hand, palm out—*I'm not armed, I'm not here for trouble*—and Orsen noted the calluses on it anyway, the rope-wear across the fingers. "My name is Dagny Holt. Survey Division, Northern District. I need to talk to you about the headwaters."

"The headwaters are closed to survey work. Have been since the Kestrel Accord."

"I know what they are, Ms. Nystrom. I wrote half the closure orders myself." Snow slid off her brim. "May I come in? It's eleven miles back down and I'd rather not make them in the dark arguing on your porch."

There was something in the voice—not pleading, nothing so soft—but a kind of flat, banked stubbornness that Orsen recognized the way she recognized weather. The kind that didn't blow through. The kind that settled in.

She unlatched the chain. It was a mistake or it wasn't; she wouldn't know until later, and later turned out to be most of a year.

* * *

The woman took off her boots at the door without being asked, which annoyed Orsen more than anything else she could have done. She set them neatly beside Orsen's own pair, side by side, toes aligned, and then stood dripping in the middle of the room like a heron that had wandered into the wrong marsh.

"Sit," Orsen said, because the alternative was watching her stand there all night.

Dagny Holt sat on the bench by the stove, spine straight, satchel across her knees, and looked around the cabin with an expression Orsen had learned to hate over twelve years: the inventory glance. The single cot. The shelves of hand-drawn maps, none of them stamped, all of them illegal in their precision. The wall where gear hung in ranked order, each item in its place, because when you lived alone at the edge of the world, order was the only conversation you had.

"So," Orsen said. "You climbed eleven miles to look at my bookshelves."

"I climbed eleven miles because you don't answer letters." Dagny unbuckled the satchel. "Fourteen letters, Ms. Nystrom. Over five months. All returned unopened—I checked the post office logs. You return everything official. So eventually I stopped writing and started walking."

"That's usually how people learn to leave me alone."

"It's usually how people learn I'm serious." From the satchel she drew a folder, grey, government-issue, and laid it on the table between them without opening it. Orsen's eyes went to it and stayed there against her will. She knew that shade of grey. Every guide who'd ever been ruined knew that shade of grey. "Before we go any further, I want to be honest with you about what I'm holding. This is your file. Blacklist file 4471-K. Twelve years old. I pulled it myself, three weeks ago, and I've read every page."

The stove ticked. Outside, the wind came down off the shoulder of the mountain and leaned against the cabin like a drunk against a fence.

"Then you know why I don't talk to survey officers," Orsen said.

"I know why you *don't*." Dagny's voice didn't rise. That was the unsettling thing about her so far—everything stayed level, pitched low, like water finding its own surface. "I know about the Vinter Pass descent. I know a client named Aldous Rehm died in a whiteout on a route you'd flagged as marginal twice in your own logbook. I know your expedition partner testified that you ignored his warnings and pushed on. And I know—" she paused, and for the first time something moved behind the level voice, something almost like distaste, "—that the inquiry never once asked why a guide with your record would ignore her own flags. They asked six questions. None of them were that one."

Orsen kept her face still. It was a skill, stillness. She'd had twelve years of practice.

"What do you want?"

"A team is missing." Dagny opened the folder—not Orsen's file, but a second one beneath it, thinner, newer, its edges already soft from handling. "Six people. Government survey unit, Northern District, working the headwaters under a mineral assessment permit. They went in on the third of the month. Their resupply check-in was due on the nineteenth. It didn't come. A search flight on the twenty-second found their camp."

"And?"

"And the camp was intact." Dagny laid photographs on the table, one by one, like a fortune-teller dealing cards. "Tents pitched. Stove cold but clean. Food cached and covered against animals. Instruments wrapped and stowed. Boots—six pairs of boots, Ms. Nystrom—lined up outside the tents, laces tucked in. No tracks leaving camp. No tracks anywhere. Fresh snowfall two days before the flight, and not one footprint in it. Six people walked out of a camp above the tree line into subzero temperatures without their boots, and left no sign."

Orsen looked at the photographs longer than she wanted to. She told herself it was professional interest. The camp was pitched well—too well, actually, windward orientation correct, drainage trench cut, the kind of layout you only got from someone who'd wintered high before. But the boots were what caught her. Laces tucked in. That wasn't abandonment. Abandonment was scattered, torn, desperate. Laces tucked in was *tidying*. That was someone putting things right before they went somewhere.

People died in mountains. She had carried two of them down herself, and remembered the weight of both. People did not fold their lives neatly and step out of them.

"Search teams?" she asked.

"Two ground parties, nine days combined. Nothing. The flight crew is calling it exposure, bodies buried by weather. The report's already drafted." Dagny's jaw tightened. "It's a lie. There's no blood, no scatter of gear, no signs of hypothermic paradoxical undressing—which you'd expect, if you know cold like I think you do. Six experienced people don't walk barefoot into a blizzard and vanish cleanly. Something else happened."

"Then find the something else. You have a whole division."

"My division found nothing in nine days because my division searches places that exist on maps." She tapped the photograph of the camp, and Orsen saw her finger stop just short of the river visible beyond the tents—a thin white seam of ice running down toward the tree line. "They were camped on a tributary feeding the Brannvik system. Except when we pulled the hydrographic charts to trace it upstream, the tributary doesn't end where it should. The official map says it rises from the glacier tongue at grid mark 44-something. Our own field crews say otherwise. Two of them, independently, have reported the channel continuing past the glacier line into terrain marked 'unsurveyed.' Terrain nobody has entered since the Kestrel Accord closed it."

Orsen said nothing. But her memory said plenty. She knew that country. She had guided through the edge of it in another life, before the blacklist, before the file with her name in it—knew the way the valley bent north where the maps insisted it ended, knew the sound the water made coming out of that hidden gorge, a sound like breath drawn through teeth. Officially, that river simply stopped. Rivers did not stop. She had known that even at nineteen, reading charts by lamplight, and had filed the knowledge away the way you file away all contradictions between the world and its paperwork: under *not my problem anymore*.

"You want me to go in there," she said.

"I want you to do what you're apparently still the best in the district at doing." Dagny met her eyes. "Track them. Find out what happened to six of my people. In exchange—" she slid the grey folder an inch closer, "—file 4471-K comes out of the registry. Expunged. Your name cleared. You can guide legally again. You can walk into any range office in the north and be handed work instead of shown the door."

The words landed in Orsen's chest like stones dropped down a well. She heard them fall a long way before they hit bottom.

Twelve years. Twelve years of guiding rich clients through legal corridors of wilderness because it was all she was allowed, of knowing ranges she couldn't enter better than the wardens who patrolled them, of counting miles because years had failed her—one mile, then ten, then a season's worth, arithmetic instead of a life. And here was a stranger offering it all back in exchange for walking into the one place she had sworn, quietly, privately, with the whole force of her bitterness behind the vow, never to go again.

"No," she said.

Dagny nodded slowly, as if Orsen had confirmed something rather than refused it. "May I ask why?"

"You may not."

"Because I've read your file," Dagny said, "and I don't believe you did what they said you did. And I think you don't believe it either, and I think that's why you live eleven miles past the end of a road that doesn't exist, surrounded by maps of places you're forbidden to enter. You didn't retire, Ms. Nystrom. You were exiled, and you built a life inside the exile because it was the only shape anyone would let you have." She stood, gathered the photographs with precise, unhurried movements, and repacked the folder. "I'm not going to beg. I'm going to stay the night in the woodshed—you have a woodshed, I checked on the way in—and tomorrow I'm going to walk back down those eleven miles, and next week I'll come back, and the week after that. I have the leave accrued. I have the stubbornness. What I don't have is time, because every day that team stays missing is a day colder and a day deader, and I will not write 'exposure' in a report I know is false."

"There are other guides."

"Not like you. And you know it, which is why you're angry." Dagny slung the satchel. At the door she paused, one boot on, and looked back. "For what it's worth—the inquiry was wrong. Whoever testified against you chose the lie that was easiest to survive. That's not the same as the truth. I've watched enough reports get written to know the difference."

The door shut behind her. Orsen sat by the stove a long while, listening to the wind lean on the walls, looking at the place on the table where the grey folder had been.

She should have felt relief. Refusal was supposed to feel like a wall holding. Instead it felt like a door she'd locked herself—and the terrible suspicion, settling in her gut colder than the night outside, that the woman in the woodshed had read her more accurately in one evening than anyone had managed in twelve years.

* * *

She didn't sleep. Around midnight she lit the lamp, pulled out her own charts—the illegal ones, the good ones—and found the tributary without having to look hard, because she had always known exactly where it was. The channel ran up past the glacier line into blank paper. Blank paper, and beyond it, in her memory, the sound of water where no water should be.

At some hour past two, she dressed, took down her pack, and stepped into the cold.

Dagny Holt was awake in the woodshed. Of course she was. She sat up in her bedroll with her coat already on, and when Orsen held out a tin cup of coffee, black and strong enough to strip varnish, the officer took it without a word and drank like someone who had been waiting to be offered exactly that.

"I'm not doing it for the file," Orsen said.

"I know."

"And I'm not doing it for you."

"I know that too."

"Six pairs of boots," Orsen said, "laces tucked in. Somebody put their camp in order before they left. That's not dying. That's deciding." She drained her own cup and looked north, toward the blank space on the map where the river kept running whether the government believed in it or not. "We leave at first light. And Holt—if you slow me down on the ice, I leave you."

Dagny set down the cup. In the lamplight her face showed something that wasn't quite a smile, but was the first thing resembling warmth Orsen had seen on it.

"Fair terms," she said. "I've been told I'm hard to leave behind."

Outside, the sky was clearing off the northwest, stars sharp as chips of flint, and the wind had gone quiet in the way it does before it changes its mind. Orsen stood a moment in the yard, breath smoking, and felt the old arithmetic stir in her chest—miles to the headwaters, miles to the glacier line, miles into the blank.

But underneath the miles, for the first time in twelve years, something else was moving. Not hope. She didn't have a word for it yet. Only the sense that somewhere upstream, past the edge of every chart, six people had folded their lives neatly and walked away—and that the answer to why might also be the answer to her.
