## 3. Gear With No Grave

The storm had scrubbed the sky clean by morning, and what it left behind was a blue so hard and high it looked like the inside of a bell. Orsen climbed into it with her breath steaming and her boots crunching on a crust of ice that had formed overnight over yesterday's slush. The Kestrel Range stood off to the west, white-capped and indifferent, and she did not look at it. She had spent twelve years learning not to look at things.

Dagny Holt walked a half-step behind her, which was the correct place for a client who didn't know she was a client, and asked questions in the manner of someone who had been told not to.

"How much farther?"

"Two hours if the snow holds. Three if it doesn't."

"And you're sure of the route?"

Orsen stopped. Below them the valley opened like a cupped hand, the unnamed tributary threading through it, silver-bright in the sun. "Officer Holt. I have been walking this country since before your government decided I wasn't allowed to. I know where every rock has rolled and every river bends. What I don't know is why six trained surveyors would leave their camp standing." She started walking again. "So let's go find out."

They climbed through birch scrub that gave way to pine, then to pine bent sideways by wind, then to nothing at all—just stone and snow and the long silence of altitude. Dagny kept up better than Orsen expected. City shoulders, but a walker's stride underneath, and a stubbornness that showed itself in the set of her jaw whenever the grade steepened. Twice she slipped on ice-glazed rock and twice she caught herself without calling out, and Orsen noted that too. People revealed themselves on mountains faster than anywhere else. It was one of the reasons she loved them and one of the reasons she'd left them.

The campsite sat in a shallow bowl below the headwater cirque, sheltered from the north wind by a ridge of granite, close enough to water to be sensible and far enough to be safe. A good site. Orsen had picked sites like it herself for twenty years, and she felt the familiar professional assessment begin before she could stop it—drainage, avalanche angle, exposure—and then the assessment curdled into something else entirely.

Because the tents were still standing.

All three of them. Pitched taut, guy-lines dressed and staked, flys cinched down against weather that hadn't come. Snow lay on them in an even skin, undisturbed, as though the camp had been waiting all winter for owners who meant to come back.

"Nobody struck camp," Dagny said quietly. She had gone very still, the way people do when a room goes wrong.

"No," Orsen said. "Nobody struck camp."

She circled the perimeter slowly, reading the ground the way other people read letters. The snow had melted and refrozen and melted again since the team vanished—Dagny's timeline put it at eleven weeks—but the deeper signs were still legible. Boot tracks everywhere inside the bowl, packed into paths between tents and fire ring and cache. No tracks leaving. Not toward the pass, not down-valley, not toward the glacier at the head of the cirque. The snow beyond the camp's edge was smooth as fresh paper, wind-textured, unbroken except by ptarmigan and once, strangely, by a fox that had come and gone in a straight line, uninterested.

"Show me the cache," Orsen said.

It was a rock hollow lined with the team's spare rope, and it was full. Ration packs stacked in neat columns, foil wrappers glinting. Fuel canisters. A medical kit, sealed. Chocolate, which no expedition leaves behind—not ever, not dying, not fleeing. Orsen crouched and counted without touching. Six weeks of food for six people, give or take.

"They didn't starve," she said. "They didn't freeze. They didn't run out of anything."

"Then what happened?"

Orsen stood and turned a slow circle, and the answer assembled itself in her chest with the slow cold weight of a stone rolling downhill. "That's what we're going to find out."

* * *

The boots were what finally did it.

She found them outside the largest tent—the leader's, by the size of it—six pairs lined up against the granite wall with their laces tucked inside, toes pointing away from camp. Pointing outward. Toward the glacier, toward the source of the river, toward everything the maps refused to name. They were arranged with a care that made her skin crawl: not dropped, not kicked off in panic or haste, but *placed*. Each pair aligned. Each lace coiled. Someone had knelt here in the cold and done this deliberately, and then those someones had walked out into deep snow in their socks.

Orsen crouched beside them for a long time without saying anything. Her breath came out in small white ghosts and dissolved.

"You've seen this before," Dagny said. "Something like it."

"I've seen everything the mountain does to people." Orsen's voice came out flatter than she intended. "I've dug clients out of avalanches. I've carried a man's body nine miles on my back because his family wanted him buried in the churchyard and the helicopter couldn't fly in the weather. I've found people who wandered off a trail and sat down under a tree because they were hypothermic and the tree felt warm, God help them. The mountain kills people in maybe ten ways and I can read every one of them like handwriting." She touched the nearest boot. The leather was stiff with cold. "This isn't any of them. Nobody died here. Nobody was taken. There's no blood, no drag marks, no scuff of struggle. Six people took off their boots, folded their laces, and walked barefoot into the snow like they were going somewhere better."

"Suicide," Dagny said, but she said it the way you test ice—one foot, ready to pull back.

"Six at once? In formation?" Orsen shook her head. "And they cached their food first. Suicides don't pack rations for a return trip."

Dagny crouched beside her. Up close, in the flat white light, Orsen could see how young she actually was beneath the officer's posture—young enough that this might be the first wrong thing she'd ever found in the world, old enough that she'd know exactly what it cost to report it. "Then what are we looking at?"

"We're looking at a choice." Orsen rose, knees cracking. "People die in mountains, Officer. That's ordinary. People choosing to leave—that's not a mountain story. That's something else."

* * *

Inside the leader's tent, the cold had preserved everything with terrible fidelity. Sleeping bags rolled. A small stove, dry and disassembled for travel. A tin cup set upside down on a crate, the way careful people store cups. On top of the crate, weighted under a carabiner against the wind, lay a journal with a soft leather cover gone gray at the corners from handling.

Orsen looked at it for a long moment before she picked it up. Some part of her understood that she was crossing a line—reading the dead's private words—but nobody here was dead, and the journal had been left out, weighted, facing the tent door. Facing whoever came next. Left out like a note pinned to a door.

"Dagny," she said. "You'll want to see this."

They read it together by the light of the open tent flap, sitting on their heels in the trampled snow, passing pages back and forth with numb fingers. The hand was small and precise, an engineer's hand. Sela Halvard, said the first page, in letters so neat they might have been printed. Survey Team Four. Day One.

The early entries were ordinary and almost painful in their ordinariness. Elevations. Barometric readings. Notes on moraine composition and melt rates. Jokes about the freeze-dried dinners. A complaint about a teammate's snoring rendered with dry affection. Sela Halvard had led her team the way good leaders do—attentively, humorously, keeping the human ledger alongside the scientific one. Orsen found herself liking her across the distance of paper and weeks, the way you like a stranger whose footprints you follow on a trail. Steady. Careful. Someone who counted her people before she counted her data.

Then, near the end, the entries changed.

*Day 31. The tributary doesn't behave like a meltwater stream. Flow is constant regardless of diurnal temperature cycle. Water temperature at the source approach measured 41°C. Forty-one degrees. Re-sampled four times. Same result.*

*Day 33. Found the vent field. Steam fissures in the bedrock above the glacier toe, mineral staining, sulfur smell. This is geothermal, not volcanic surface activity. Something is heating the headwaters from below. Logged coordinates. Will report.*

*Day 35. Received response from the Directorate. They want us to hold publication pending review. Review by whom? The mining consortium's consultants were copied on my report. Why?*

*Day 37. Held a team meeting. Read them the Directorate message aloud. Voss says we're being asked to sit on a hazard assessment because the numbers are inconvenient. He's right. If that vent destabilizes, everything downstream of the gorge is in the flood path. Brannvik. The mill towns. Thousands of people.*

*Day 38. We voted. All six. I won't write here what we decided. If anyone reads this, follow the river up. Follow it all the way.*

The last entry was dated two days later. It was short. It ended mid-word.

*Day 40. We are going up to the source tonight. Whatever is there, it is more honest than the people who pay us. We will leave the gear for whoever comes, and we will leave our names with*

Nothing. The sentence simply stopped, the ink trailing slightly at the end as though the pen had lifted, or the hand had. No signature. No date of return. Just the words *we will leave our names with*, hanging in the cold air of the tent like breath that never fell.

Orsen became aware that her hands were shaking. Not from the cold.

"Follow the river up," Dagny repeated softly. She was staring past the page, up the valley, toward the glacier and whatever lay beyond it. "That's where they went. That's the only direction that makes sense of any of this."

"It's also a direction that doesn't exist," Orsen said. "Officially. Your maps show the tributary terminating at the glacier toe. Dead end. Nothing above it worth surveying."

"Maps have been wrong before."

"Not government maps. Not survey maps made by the same office that sent this team." Orsen looked down at the journal in her hands. "Someone drew that map knowing there was something above the glacier they didn't want found. And then they sent six people up here anyway. And now six people have walked off the end of the map in their stocking feet, and their leader left a note telling whoever came next to follow the current."

The wind moved through the bowl, lifting fine snow off the tent flys in little ghosts. Somewhere above them, ice calved off the glacier with a sound like a distant door closing.

Dagny pulled out her radio, thumbed it on, and got only the long empty hiss of dead air. She tried twice, then shut it off. "Out of range until we drop lower. Which means right now, officially, I've found nothing yet." She met Orsen's eyes, and Orsen saw the calculation running behind them—the career arithmetic, the orders, the word *quietly* that had been attached to this mission like a tag on merchandise. "What are you thinking?"

Orsen looked at the boots lined up against the wall. At the food cached for a return. At the journal ending mid-word, mid-promise, *we will leave our names with*—with whom? With what?

She thought about rivers. About how a river that isn't on a map still runs, still cuts its valley, still reaches the sea whether anyone records it or not. She had spent twelve years being a river like that—flowing through country that officially contained no trace of her, carving her own course below the notice of the offices that had erased her name. And now here was a whole team of people who had done it deliberately, walked off the page together, and left instructions.

*I won't write here what we decided.*

But she knew. She knew the way you know a mountain's mood from the shape of its clouds. Six people had looked at the truth and at the men who wanted it buried, and they had chosen the truth, and the truth lived upstream, past the end of the map, beside a river that burned.

"I'm thinking," Orsen said, standing, shouldering her pack, "that I'm going to follow the river up. All the way."

"To the source."

"To wherever the maps stop lying."

Dagny scrambled up after her. "Wait. You're not going alone. My orders—"

"Your orders are to bury this. Mine are mine own." Orsen took the journal, wrapped it in oilcloth, and stowed it inside her jacket, against her chest, where it rode like a second heartbeat. "You can radio your directorate from the low pass and tell them what we found. Boots and blankets and a note. They'll send men who'll file it in a drawer. Or you can walk upstream with me and find out what six people thought was worth more than their names."

The wind gusted, snapping the tent flys like flags of surrender—or like flags of invitation.

Dagny stood in the trampled snow of the abandoned camp, a government officer holding a dead radio, and for a long moment she said nothing at all. Above her the glacier gleamed, and beyond the glacier the valley bent upward into country no chart acknowledged, and from somewhere in that direction, faint and impossible, the air carried a smell Orsen knew from twenty years of mountains and had never smelled here before.

Sulfur. Warm stone. Smoke where nothing should burn.

Orsen turned uphill and began to walk, and behind her, after a moment, she heard Dagny's boots crunching through the crust, following.
