## 4. The River That Isn't on Maps

The survey charts came out of Dagny Holt's pack the way evidence comes out of a courtroom—flat, official, smelling faintly of toner and government ink. She'd spread them across my table that first night, weighted at the corners with a tin cup, a coil of rope, a stone from my own hearth, and I had looked at them the way a man looks at a photograph of his own funeral: recognizing everything, belonging to nothing.

Now she was gone—down to the valley for resupply, she'd said, three days there and three back—and I had the cabin and the charts and the long grey silence of the highlands to myself.

I should have slept. Instead I lit the lamp and unrolled the sheets again, because there was a thing on them that had been itching at me since the checkpoint, since the campsite, since the journal with its sentence cut off mid-word like a throat closed by a hand.

The tributary.

On the official chart it began at the Skarv Glacier—a neat blue line, thin as a vein, running down out of the icefield and joining the Vaskrelva maybe eleven kilometers below the tree line. The cartographer had labeled it with a number, not a name. *Tributary 7-C.* As if the mountain itself had filed paperwork.

I knew that river.

I had drunk from it. I had crossed it in spate with a client's life roped to mine. And I knew something about it that no government chart admitted: it did not begin at the Skarv Glacier. It began higher. Far higher. It came down out of a hanging valley above the icefall where the glacier calved into grey slush, and it ran warm.

I sat with that knowledge the way you sit with an old injury on a cold night—noticing it, testing it, hating how well you know its shape.

Twelve years ago, before the blacklist, before the hearing where Eirik Halvard's voice went flat and reasonable while mine shook, I had guided a geological survey party into the headwaters. Not the government men with their clipboards—the real ones, two academics out of the university in Brannvik, a man named Torvik who smelled of pipe smoke and a younger woman whose name I have honestly lost, which shames me more than any of the rest of it. They'd wanted samples from the hot springs above the icefall. Everyone knew about the hot springs; the old trappers' accounts mentioned *the burning water* going back a hundred years. Steam rising off snow. A river you could warm your hands in in January.

Torvik had stood at the edge of the source pool with his boots steaming and said, half laughing, "This water's been climbing out of the earth since before there were maps, Miss Nystrom. Whatever name they give it, the river won't answer to it."

I'd laughed too, then. I'd been twenty-six and invincible and my name meant something in the valleys.

I looked at the chart now. Tributary 7-C ended at the glacier. Full stop. The cartographers had drawn the icefield as the head of the water, because ice is simple and heat is not, because a glacier can be measured and a vent cannot, because somewhere in an office a man decided that what he could not conveniently survey did not need to exist.

And six people had walked out of their boots beside that non-existent river and gone up into the unnamed country beyond it.

I turned to Sela Halvard's journal again—I had taken to thinking of her by her name now, though Dagny had only given me the file designation. The last entry still stopped my breath.

* * *

*—and the water is wrong, that's the only way I can put it. Wrong the way a room is wrong when someone has moved the furniture in the dark. Rasmussen says I'm imagining it, that the gauge readings are within tolerance, but Rasmussen has never watched a river steam.*

*I grew up on my father's stories of this country. He used to say the mountains keep their honest places hidden and show you only the ones they've dressed up for visitors. I thought it was a saying. I think now it was a warning.*

*The pool at the source has risen four centimeters since Tuesday. Four centimeters of meltwater that has no melt to account for it. There is no sun warm enough up here. There is no wind carrying heat into a basin walled in by ice. Something under the glacier is breathing, and the breathing is getting faster, and when I radioed it in they told me—*

*They told me to log it as seasonal variation.*

*Seasonal variation. In October. Above the frost line. I am sitting on a rock writing this with a pen that is too cold to hold and the water beside me is giving off steam like a kettle left on a stove, and a man in an office four hundred kilometers away has decided that what I am seeing is a season.*

*Ketil asked me tonight what we do if they keep refusing the data. He didn't mean it as a joke. None of us laughed.*

*Tomorrow we go up past the icefall to place the last gauges. After that—*

That was all. *After that.* Two words and then the paper went blank, and somewhere in the archives a clerk had stamped the file CLOSED — PERSONNEL UNACCOUNTED FOR, as if people were inventory that had wandered off a shelf.

I set the journal down. My hands were not steady, and I noticed that the way you notice weather—as fact, not feeling.

Here was the arithmetic I couldn't escape. Six trained surveyors had abandoned their gear in perfect order. Boots lined up. Tents pitched. Food cached against a winter they plainly hadn't planned to spend. People who die in the mountains leave wreckage. People who are taken leave signs. These people had left *tidiness*, which is the signature of a choice.

And the only direction a choice like that leads is up. Past the icefall. Into the basin at the head of a river that officially did not exist, toward a source that breathed steam and rose four centimeters between Tuesdays.

Dagny would say wait. Dagny would say protocol, coordination, don't go alone into terrain that ate a full team. Dagny would be right, in the way that officials are always right—right the way a chart is right, accurate and dead and missing the one thing that matters.

But Dagny had also shown me her orders, or the shape of them, and I had read between the lines well enough. Find the team. Establish whether they defected. Handle it quietly. *Quietly* was doing a lot of work in that sentence, and none of it was work I wanted to be part of. If those six had walked away from their government the way I had once walked away from mine—if they'd found some fold in the mountains to disappear into—then every hour Dagny spent with me was an hour the machinery spent winding itself around them.

I had been the thing the machinery caught. I knew what it felt like when the gears got hold of your name.

Outside, the wind had come around to the north and was working at the eaves with its long fingers. I banked the fire, then built it up again, then stood there like a fool unable to decide what kind of night I wanted, because both kinds led to the same morning.

I took down my pack. It stayed packed through winter anyway—twelve years of habit, twelve years of being ready to move before anyone asked me to—and it took me less than twenty minutes to shift it from ready-to-flee to ready-to-climb. Rope, rack, the good ice axe, the spare axe lashed flat. Stove, bivouac sack, four days of food stretched to seven by arithmetic I didn't enjoy. The medical kit. The flare gun, though flares were for people who expected to be found, and I wasn't sure anymore which side of that line I stood on.

Last, from the bottom of the chest beneath the window, I took the thing I almost never touched: my old guide's notebook, the real one, the one that predated every file the ministry kept on me. Water-stained, swollen, its spine held together with waxed thread I'd sewn myself. Every route I'd ever run was in it, including the ones that weren't supposed to exist—including the headwaters, drawn in my own hand at twenty-six, confident as scripture, in an ink that had faded to the brown of old blood.

There it was. My map of the river with no name. The glacier marked as a waypoint, not a source. Beyond it, the hanging valley, the terraces of hot ground where snow refused to lie, the basin above the icefall where Torvik had filled his sample vials and laughed about names.

I had drawn that country when I still believed countries could be known. Then I'd spent twelve years walking ridgelines I was legally forbidden to walk, memorizing terrain I was erased from, counting miles because miles were the only unit left to me. Every mile had been a way of not measuring the years. You can't grieve properly and cover forty kilometers a day; the body won't permit both. So I'd chosen the body's arithmetic. Kilometers in, kilometers out. Client safe, client paid, cabin dark, repeat.

And now here was a river that had done what I'd done. Erased from the record. Renumbered. Refused a name so that no one would come looking for what lived along it. Somewhere upstream, in country the charts pretended was solid ice, six people—or whatever remained of six people—had followed that same current home.

I understood the pull of it more than I wanted to. That was the dangerous part. Not the climb, not the icefall, not the crevasses under the fresh snow. The dangerous part was that some quiet cellar in me had heard the words *unnamed tributary* and felt *recognized*.

You don't follow a river like that because it's there. You follow it because part of you has always wanted to be nowhere on any map.

I blew out the lamp and let the firelight take the room. Through the window, the Kestrel Range stood black against a sky rinsed clean by the storm, and above it, faint as breath on glass, hung the plume of cloud that never moved—the steam of the burning water, visible from my door on clear nights for twelve years, which I had looked at ten thousand times and never once let myself wonder about.

I wondered about it now.

Three days until Dagny returned. The icefall was a day and a half hard travel if the weather held, and the weather, for once, seemed inclined to hold. I could be at the glacier by tomorrow night, up past the icefall by the second evening, and back or not back before she ever knew I'd gone. If I waited for her, we'd go together, officially, with her radio and her protocols and her orders folded in her breast pocket like a warrant. And whatever we found up there would belong to the machinery by sundown.

If I went alone, it would belong—to whoever had walked barefoot into the snow. To Sela Halvard and her pen too cold to hold. Maybe to me.

I'm not a romantic about solitude. Solitude is what you're left with, not what you choose. But I knew the difference between a duty and a debt, and this one had my name on it in ink older than the ministry's. Twelve years ago I had stood in a hearing room and said nothing while a lie was told about me, and the silence had cost me everything, and I had told myself ever since that silence was safety. Silence was the whole architecture of my life. Silence was the cabin and the off-seasons and the miles.

Sela Halvard had written *they told me to log it as seasonal variation* and then she had picked up her team and walked into the unnamed country rather than log a lie. Whatever else she was—missing, dead, defected, fooled—she had done the thing I never did. She had made noise.

The least I owed her was to read the rest of her sentence.

I sewed the notebook into the inner pocket of my jacket, where it rode against my ribs like a second heartbeat. I wrote no note. Notes are for people who expect to be found, and besides, what would it say? *Gone upstream. Back when the current tells me.* Dagny Holt would stand in my doorway and read the absence of me the way she read everything—precisely, furiously, taking it personally—and some small uncharitable part of me wanted her to feel that absence. The larger part just didn't want to argue. Arguments take hours, and I had a river to catch.

At first light the sky was the color of a blade's edge, cold and clean and without mercy, which is the best sky for travel. I crossed the frozen yard, and at the treeline I stopped and did the thing I always did and had never examined: I counted. Eleven kilometers to the glacier. Four more to the icefall. Numbers laid over the land like a grid, the old arithmetic clicking into place, miles stacking up ahead of me the way they always had, the only currency I'd trusted since the hearing.

Then I made myself stop. Made myself look instead—at the plume of steam standing off the high basin, straight and white in the still air, like a signal fire no one had ever answered.

*After that,* Sela had written. *After that—*

I turned uphill and started walking, and for the first time in twelve years I did not count the miles.
