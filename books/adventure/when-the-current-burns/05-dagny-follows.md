## 5. Dagny Follows

### DAGNY

Dagny Holt had been walking for two days on a trail that officially did not exist.

That was the thing about the northern highlands—half the map was fiction and the other half was apology. The survey office's charts showed contour lines marching confidently across terrain that no one had measured in forty years, rivers drawn where rivers had died, glaciers named for men who'd never seen them. Dagny had spent six years learning to read between those lines, and what she'd learned was this: the government didn't map the highlands because mapping meant admitting they were there.

She crested the moraine ridge at first light and saw the smoke.

Thin, gray, deliberate—a single cook fire burning low in the lee of a granite shoulder, exactly where a person with Orsen Nystrom's instincts would put one. Invisible from below. Visible only to someone who knew to look up instead of down.

Dagny stood in the wind and let herself feel the small, bitter satisfaction of being right. Nystrom had left the abandoned camp before dawn on the third day, alone, without reporting back. She hadn't gone home. She'd gone upstream—toward the tributary that ended at nothing on every official chart, toward whatever it was she'd seen in Sela Halvard's journal that made her lie with her face so carefully blank.

*The river isn't on the maps,* Dagny thought. *And neither is she.*

She adjusted her pack straps and started down the scree. Her boots found the rhythm of descent, heel-first, weight low, the way her father had taught her on the fells above Brannvik when she was nine years old and already asking too many questions about why the valley towns paid taxes to a capital that couldn't find them on a globe.

Two days of pursuit had given her time to think about what she would say when she caught up. She had rehearsed authority: *Survey Officer Holt, you are instructed to return.* She had rehearsed honesty: *I need your help and I'm not supposed to admit it.* She had even rehearsed silence—the option of simply falling into step behind the woman and letting the trail do the talking.

What she had not rehearsed was the sight of Orsen Nystrom herself, crouched at the fire's edge, pouring boiling water over oats with the unhurried precision of someone who had been expecting company since yesterday.

"You're louder than you think," Nystrom said, without looking up. "Your left boot drags on the push-off. I've been tracking you since the second col."

Dagny stopped at the edge of the firelight. Up close, the guide looked worse than her file photograph—thinner, grayer, with the particular weathering of someone who lived outdoors by choice rather than assignment. But her eyes were steady, and steady eyes were rarer in the highlands than clean water.

"Officer Holt," Dagny said. "We should talk."

"We're talking."

"Not here." Dagny glanced at the ridgeline, the open sky, the long bare flank of the valley where any lens or long-ear could reach them. "And not as a chase. Sit down with me. Both of us. Like people who might actually need each other."

Nystrom finally looked up. For a long moment she studied Dagny the way she probably studied weather—reading pressure, reading intent, calculating how much warning she'd get before the front arrived.

"There's a flat rock," she said at last. "You can have the side facing the view. Consider it hospitality."

* * *

### ORSEN

The officer ate like a soldier—fast, efficient, grateful in a way she clearly resented having to feel. Orsen watched her over the rim of her tin cup and did the arithmetic she'd been doing since dawn.

Holt had made the second col in under three days carrying full kit. That was fast for anyone; for a desk officer it was remarkable. Which meant either the woman was tougher than her paperwork suggested, or something behind her scared her more than the terrain ahead.

Orsen suspected both.

"You followed me," Orsen said. "Against orders, I'd guess, since your orders were to bring me back to the campsite and keep me there."

"My orders were to secure your cooperation." Holt set down her spoon. "Securing cooperation doesn't require arresting the cooperators."

"That's a generous reading."

"I'm a generous reader." The officer's mouth twitched—not quite a smile, more like a door opening an inch and then thinking better of it. "You read the journal. All of it?"

"Every page."

"Then you know it ends mid-sentence."

"I know it ends mid-*word*," Orsen said. "*North-by-northwest along the—* and then nothing. No date. No signature. People who die in the mountains finish their sentences. They're writing to whoever finds them. It's the last courtesy the cold allows."

Holt was quiet for a moment. Wind moved through the dwarf birch below the rock, a sound like whispering in an empty room.

"And people who walk away?" the officer asked. "What do they write?"

Orsen set down her cup. Here it was, then—the question underneath the question, the one Holt had carried two days through frozen ground to ask.

"Why don't you tell me what your office thinks happened," Orsen said, "and I'll tell you if it matches what I know about mountains. Fair trade."

Holt looked out at the valley for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—lower, flatter, the voice of someone reciting something she'd been ordered never to recite aloud.

"The working theory is defection. Six team members, coordinated disappearance, equipment deliberately abandoned to simulate misadventure. The ministry wants it handled quietly. No rescue operation. No press. A closed file and a line in next year's budget noting the loss of personnel to—" she paused, tasting the phrase like something spoiled— "'unrecoverable circumstances.'"

"Defection," Orsen repeated. "Six trained surveyors walked away from their posts, into the highlands, in autumn, barefoot."

"The boots were lined up at the camp. You saw them."

"I saw them. And I've spent thirty years watching what people leave behind when they die, Officer, and I'll tell you what I've never once seen: order. Death is chaos. Avalanche, exposure, a fall into whitewater—it scatters people like a dog shakes a rat. Tents stay pitched? Food gets cached? Boots get *lined up*?" She shook her head slowly. "That's not a grave. That's a doorway. Somebody wanted the scene to say *we chose this*, and somebody else in your ministry wants it to say *they ran*. Those aren't the same story."

"No," Holt said quietly. "They're not."

"So which one does your career depend on telling?"

The question landed the way Orsen had intended—clean, center-mass. Holt's jaw tightened. For a moment Orsen thought the woman would stand up, gather her pride, and march back down the mountain to report that the disgraced guide was uncooperative and possibly hostile.

Instead, Dagny Holt did something Orsen had almost forgotten people in uniform could do.

She told the truth.

### DAGNY

"My career depends on this mission closing," Dagny said. "Not resolving. *Closing.* There's a difference, and I learned it the hard way."

The words came out steadier than she'd expected. Maybe because she'd said them to herself so many times on the trail, in the dark, in the wind, that they'd worn smooth as river stones.

"I came up through the Brannvik district office. Flood surveys, bridge load reports, culvert inspections. Work nobody thanks you for and everybody needs. Four years ago I flagged a levee assessment on the lower Vaskr basin—structural deficiency, recommended evacuation-zone revision. My supervisor forwarded it up the chain. Somewhere up that chain, someone decided the finding was inconvenient for a mining lease renewal, and the assessment came back to me marked *revised per ministry guidance*, which is a polite way of saying *falsified per instruction*. I signed it anyway." She heard her own voice go dry on the last word. "Because I had a promotion cycle coming. Because refusing would have ended my career and signing only ended my integrity, and I told myself those weren't the same thing."

Orsen said nothing. She just watched, with those glacial eyes, letting the silence do its work.

"The Halvard team's disappearance is my chance to un-sign that signature," Dagny went on. "If I bring this in clean—if I find the truth and put it in a report that can't be revised—I get transferred to the independent review board. Real authority. The kind that says no and is allowed to mean it." She laughed, short and humorless. "And if I fail, I go back to culverts knowing exactly what my name is worth. Which is whatever the ministry writes under it."

"That's why you chased me," Orsen said. "Not duty. Redemption."

"Duty is just redemption with better posture." Dagny met her stare. "Don't tell me you don't know that. I've read your file, Nystrom. All of it. Twelve years ago you were the most decorated guide in the Kestrel service. You had standing, a reputation, a future. One disaster, one scapegoat, and you lost all of it—and instead of fighting, you walked into the hills and erased yourself. You've been living off the grid so long I half expected your cabin to be off the maps too." She paused. "You measure your life in miles now because miles don't ask you who you are. I measure mine in reports filed correctly because reports don't ask me who I am. We're not so different, you and I. We just picked different units."

The wind gusted, flattening the birch, carrying the smell of snow from the high country. Orsen's expression didn't change, but something behind it shifted—a door moving on its hinges, not opening yet, but no longer locked.

"You want the truth about the team," Orsen said at last. "The real truth, not the version your ministry will accept."

"I want the truth I can live with. If those are the same thing, wonderful. If they're not—" Dagny exhaled. "Then I suppose I'll have to decide which one I can carry."

"That's the first honest sentence I've heard from a government officer in twelve years."

"Don't romanticize it. I'm still going to report everything I learn."

"I'd expect nothing less." Orsen rose, brushing grit from her knees, and looked north up the valley—to where the tributary ran silver through the gray rock, climbing toward a source that appeared on no chart Dagny had ever filed. "But understand something, Officer Holt. If we go up that river together, you follow my rules. My pace, my crossings, my calls. The moment you start giving orders based on maps that lie, one of us dies, and it won't be me."

"Those are steep terms for someone whose cooperation I technically command."

"Technically." Orsen almost smiled. It transformed her whole face for a heartbeat—younger, sharper, someone Dagny might have liked in another life. "Everything up there is technical until it kills you."

### DAGNY

They broke camp within the hour, and Dagny learned quickly what following Orsen Nystrom meant.

It meant walking in the guide's footprints, literally—stepping where she stepped, because Orsen read the ground the way Dagny read documents, catching the instability hidden in a slope of talus, the meltwater hollowed beneath a crust of snow. It meant silence on the move, broken only by Orsen's occasional murmured observations: *rockfall zone, we cross at dawn when it's frozen; that band of shale sheds in afternoon sun; see the lichen line—that's your flood mark, the river's been higher than it looks.*

It meant, somewhere past midday, admitting out loud: "I couldn't have done this alone."

"No," Orsen agreed, without cruelty or kindness. Just fact. "You'd have made the gorge by tomorrow and died in it Tuesday."

They climbed through the afternoon into country that grew stranger with every mile. The tributary widened instead of narrowing, which was wrong—rivers thin as they rise, everyone knows that—but this one gathered strength from springs that seamed the canyon walls like veins, warm to the touch when Dagny trailed her fingers through one. Warm water, at altitude, in October. She filed the observation where she filed everything, in the part of her mind that never stopped drafting reports.

Near dusk, Orsen called a halt beneath an overhang and studied the river with an expression Dagny hadn't seen before. Not the professional calm. Something closer to recognition.

"What is it?" Dagny asked.

"When I guided the Kestrel Range, before—" Orsen waved a hand, dismissing the rest of the sentence, though they both knew what it held. "There was a legend among the old guides. A hot spring country above the headwaters. Steam in winter, green ground in the passes, a valley where nothing froze. Everyone agreed it existed. Nobody agreed on where. And every survey that went looking for it came back with blank pages and bad dreams." She turned to look at Dagny, firelight catching the scar tissue at her hairline—old burn, Dagny noted, old story. "I always assumed it was a tale guides told clients to explain why they wouldn't take certain routes. Now I'm standing in it."

"Steam means geothermal. Geothermal means the survey team had a reason to be here that isn't in their mission brief."

"It means a lot of things." Orsen fed a branch to the fire. "It means the water downstream is warmer than it should be, which means the ice above us is melting from underneath, which means—" she stopped.

"Means what?"

Orsen was quiet long enough that Dagny thought she wouldn't answer. Then, softly:

"Twelve years ago I stopped believing mountains keep secrets. They don't. They keep *debts*. Everything a mountain hides, it hides temporarily, and it always presents the bill at once." She looked upriver, into the dark. "Get some sleep, Officer. Tomorrow we find out what the interest rate is."

* * *

### ORSEN

Orsen woke once in the black hours and lay listening to the river.

It sounded different than it had at dusk. Fuller. More insistent. As if something far above them had opened a tap.

She sat up, checked the temperature of the air against her cheek—warmer than it should be, faintly mineral-smelling—and felt the old dread settle into her chest, familiar as an old coat. Thirty years of mountains had taught her the grammar of disaster, and the river was speaking it now, syllable by syllable, in the dark.

Beside the dead fire, Dagny slept sitting up, her back against stone, her radio cradled in her hands even in sleep like a child holds a toy—or a soldier holds a weapon. Even unconscious, the woman wouldn't put it down. Duty, Orsen thought. It's the last thing they take off you.

She looked at the sleeping officer and felt the strange, unwelcome beginning of something she hadn't felt in twelve years: the possibility that she might not have to carry the next few days alone.

Then the radio in Dagny's hands crackled—once, twice—and Orsen saw the officer's eyes snap open, instantly awake, instantly guarded, and understood that whatever alliance they were building, it still had a fault line running straight through the middle of it.

"What did it say?" Orsen asked quietly.

Dagny stared at the dark screen. When she answered, her voice was careful as a foot placed on rotten ice.

"Nothing. Static." A pause. "Go back to sleep, Nystrom. Tomorrow we climb."

But Orsen had spent thirty years reading what mountains tried to hide, and she recognized concealment when she heard it—in a voice, in a silence, in a river growing louder in the night.

Tomorrow they would climb. And tomorrow, one way or another, she intended to find out what Dagny Holt wasn't telling her.
