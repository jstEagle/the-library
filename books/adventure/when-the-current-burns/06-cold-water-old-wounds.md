## 6. Cold Water, Old Wounds

The river announced itself before we saw it—a low grinding roar beneath the morning fog, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. I'd been listening to it for an hour without saying so, and I could tell from the set of Dagny's shoulders that she'd been listening too.

We came down through a stand of black spruce, the trees so dense and frost-rimed they seemed carved from smoke, and there it was: the unnamed tributary, running high with snowmelt, a hundred feet of churning gray-green water shouldering between granite banks. Ice sheets the size of dinner plates rode the current, colliding and spinning, ticking against each other like crockery in a quake.

"Officially," Dagny said, "this doesn't exist."

"Officially," I said, "neither do we."

She almost smiled. Almost. She'd been almost-smiling at me for two days now, ever since I'd found her on the trail with her government-issue pack and her government-issue boots and that look of a woman who had volunteered for something she hadn't been ordered to do—and couldn't admit which it was.

I crouched at the bank and studied the water the way I used to study clients' faces back when reading people mattered as much as reading terrain. The crossing point I remembered from twelve years ago was gone; the flood had eaten the gravel bar. What remained was a braided channel forty yards downstream where the river split around a midstream boulder, dropping maybe eighteen inches over the run. Doable. Barely. In another month it would be suicide.

"We cross there," I said. "Rope, staggered stance, you first or me first?"

"You're the guide."

"I'm the disgraced guide. You're the officer with the rope." I held out my hand anyway. She gave me the coil, and our fingers touched longer than the handoff required, and neither of us mentioned it.

* * *

The water took Dagny on the third step.

I heard it before I saw it—the change in the roar, a deepening, a hunger—and looked up from my footing to see her swept off the submerged shelf she'd been trusting because I'd told her to trust it. The river didn't grab her. It simply decided she belonged to it now, the way rivers decide everything, and folded her under.

I have watched people die in mountains. I have stood close enough to touch it happening. There is a specific silence inside a rescuer's skull when it starts—not calm, not panic, but a terrible clarity, every irrelevant thing burned away until only the arithmetic remains: distance, current, rope, rock, seconds.

I ran the bank. The rope paid out behind me, still hitched to my harness, dragging through the shallows. She surfaced twenty yards down, arms windmilling, one boot gone, and I saw her try to swim against the current the way land people always do—fighting the river instead of asking it where it was willing to let her go.

"Dagny!" My voice cracked across the water. "Feet up! Feet UP!"

She heard me. God help me, she heard me—rolled onto her back, boots lifting toward the rapids, arms wide, riding it. That bought us both time. I ran harder, lungs full of broken glass, and drove myself out onto the gravel spit below the bend where the current slackened against an eddy line. I waded in to my thighs before the water started trying to peel me off the bottom, and threw the rope.

First throw: short. The coil slapped the water a body-length ahead of her and the current carried it past her reaching hand.

Second throw: I didn't aim at her. I aimed at where she would be, because that is the whole secret of moving water—you never rescue the person, you rescue the future position of the person—and the loop dropped over her shoulder like a blessing.

"Roll into it! Don't grab the rope—let it catch your chest!"

She rolled. The line went taut, sang, bit into my harness, and nearly took me off my feet. I leaned back into the fall line, boots gouging gravel, and hauled hand over hand while the river argued with me the whole way, sullen and immense, giving her up grudgingly, an inch at a time.

When her knees finally found bottom I dragged her the last few feet by the collar of her jacket and we collapsed together on the gravel bar, side by side, gasping like landed fish. The cold came up through the stones and into our spines. Somewhere upstream the river went on deciding things about other people.

Dagny turned her head. Water streamed off her hair into the rocks. Her lips were gray.

"That," she managed, "was not on the survey plan."

"No," I said. "It rarely is."

Then, absurdly, horribly, she laughed—a short, shocked bark of a laugh, the kind that comes out of people when the alternative is screaming—and I felt something crack open in my own chest that had been sealed for twelve years.

* * *

We made camp early, in a hollow above the bank where the spruce broke the wind. I got her out of the wet layers and into the spare wool I carry for exactly this kind of stupidity, mine or anyone else's, and got a fire going with wood so damp it complained the whole time. She sat wrapped in the sleeping bag like a wounded bird, hands around a tin cup, watching me work.

"You knew that crossing," she said. Not accusing. Just noting, the way she noted everything, filing it in whatever drawer officers keep.

"I crossed it twelve years ago."

"With your team. Before."

I fed a stick to the fire. Watched it catch. "Before," I said.

The silence stretched. She didn't push. That was the thing about Dagny Holt I was beginning to understand—she could wait like weather. And maybe it was the near-drowning, the way death shakes loose what life has nailed down, or maybe it was just that I was tired, tired in the marrow, tired of carrying a story that only got heavier the longer nobody else touched it.

"There was a client," I said.

Her eyes came up.

"His name was Tomas Vey. Forty-one years old. A banker who wanted to feel small in front of something honest." I heard my own voice go flat, recitation-flat, the voice of someone reading a report about a stranger. "Late-season ascent on the Kestrel Range. Weather window closed faster than forecast. We were descending, and he fell on the traverse below the col. Roped to me."

The fire popped. Dagny didn't move.

"The rope held. That was the problem. He hung there over the drop and I couldn't raise him, and the anchor was failing, and I had maybe four minutes to make a decision that had no good version. I tried to build a second anchor. I was thirty seconds slow." I turned the tin cup in my hands though it wasn't mine. "The anchor let go. We both went. I caught a ledge. He didn't."

I had not said any of this aloud in twelve years. I hadn't known that until the words were already out, already in the air between us, and I realized my hands were shaking and it wasn't the cold.

"They needed someone to blame," I went on, because now the door was open and it wouldn't shut. "The outfitter needed their insurance. The family needed a reason that wasn't 'the mountain.' And Eirik Halvard—my partner on that expedition, the man who taught me half of what I know—he testified that I'd ignored protocol. That I'd pushed past the turnaround time. That I'd chosen the summit over my client." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "I didn't even fight it. That's the part nobody understands. I just... signed the findings. Let them take the certification. Walked north and stayed walked."

"Why?" Dagny asked quietly. "Why not fight?"

I looked at her—at this woman who had nearly died in my river three hours ago and was already sitting straighter, already reassembling herself into something official—and I told her the truest thing I knew.

"Because some part of me believed it. Because if I'd made a different choice in those four minutes, Tomas Vey would have gone home to his wife. And no hearing, no appeal, no clean record was ever going to make those four minutes unhappen. So I let them have the blame. It fit. It was almost true. And out here—" I gestured at the dark trees, the unseen river, the whole unmapped country around us, "—out here nobody asks your name. You can be nobody. Nobody doesn't lose anybody."

Dagny was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost its officer's edge entirely.

"Eirik Halvard," she said. "That name's in the file. The missing team leader—Sela Halvard. Her father is listed as next of kin. Eirik Halvard."

The fire cracked. Somewhere far off, the river ground its stones.

I stared at her and felt the twelve years tilt sideways.

"He's up here," I said. "Isn't he. Wherever Sela went—wherever this river goes—he's there."

"I don't know," Dagny said. "But the journal entries reference a supply contact. Initials only. E.H."

I put my face in my hands and breathed, once, twice, the way you breathe when the ground moves under you and you can't tell yet whether it's settling or opening.

Twelve years I had built my exile on the story of that man's testimony. Twelve years of miles, each one a small stone laid on his grave in my head. And now the trail I was walking—walking toward the truth of six vanished people, toward a river that maps refused to name—was walking me straight back to him.

"Sleep," I told Dagny, because my voice would not hold anything else. "We move at first light."

She lay down. She did not sleep, and neither did I. Above the black line of the spruce the stars wheeled, indifferent and ancient, and the river kept talking to itself in the dark, saying the same thing over and over, the only thing it has ever said:

Everything runs downhill. Everything comes back around.
